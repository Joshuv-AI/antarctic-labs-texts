Artificial Intelligence Literacy Levels and Attitudes Toward Artificial Intelligence Technology Among Intensive Care Nurses: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Vahide Semerci Çakmak, Cansu Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe artificial intelligence (AI) literacy of intensive care nurses and their behaviors in interacting with AI is crucial for evaluating the impact of AI on patient care and treatment. The aim of this study is to determine the AI literacy levels of intensive care nurses, their attitudes toward AI technologies, and the factors associated with these attitudes.MethodsThe sample for this cross‐sectional study consisted of 280 nurses working in the intensive care units of two hospitals. Data were collected face‐to‐face using a Personal Information Form, an Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale (AILS), and an Artificial Intelligence Attitude Scale (AIAS‐4). Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, independent samples t‐test, one‐way ANOVA, and multiple linear regression analyses were used to analyze the data.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 33.47 ± 7.12. 63.9% of the nurses were female, 48.9% were married, and 72.1% had a bachelor's degree or higher. The vast majority of nurses (96.4%) had not received any training in AI, and 50% had no experience in this field. The mean AIAS‐4 score of intensive care nurses was 6.26 ± 2.74 (min = 1, max = 10) and the mean AILS score was 39.87 ± 6.69 (min = 16, max = 60). The results indicated a strong, positive, and statistically significant correlation between AIAS‐4 and AILS (r = 0.72, p < 0.05). Among the variables included in the model, AILS score, age, gender, marital status, and education level were found to be statistically significant predictors (p < 0.05), explaining 64% of the variance (Adj. R 2 = 0.640; F = 100.383; p < 0.001).ConclusionIn conclusion, the regression model revealed that age was a significant predictor, and individuals aged 36 and over had more negative attitudes toward AI technologies. In contrast, higher levels of AI literacy, male gender, being single, and having a bachelor's degree or higher were found to be significant positive predictors of attitudes toward AI technologies. It is recommended that practice‐based training programs emphasizing clinical benefits, aimed at improving AI literacy and attitudes toward AI technologies among intensive care nurses, be expanded.Keywords: artificial intelligence literacy, attitudes, intensive care, nurses

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to software (and, in some cases, hardware) systems designed by humans that can perceive environmental data to achieve complex goals, interpret this data in structured or unstructured forms to transform it into information, and make logical decisions based on this information by taking action in digital or physical environments [1]. AI is defined as systems that mimic human intelligence to perform tasks that generally require human cognition, such as problem‐solving, decision‐making, and pattern recognition [2]. This technology refers to systems capable of making logical and mathematically grounded decisions [3]. With rapid technological advancements in recent decades, the use of AI technologies, particularly in the healthcare sector, has increased remarkably [4, 5].

AI technologies offer numerous advantages in healthcare settings [6]. Current research shows that the integration of AI into healthcare improves the quality of patient care, increases efficiency, and reduces costs within the healthcare system [5, 7, 8]. These developments enable healthcare services to be delivered in a smarter, faster, more accurate, and more effective way. AI is considered an emerging field in the nursing profession. It is emphasized that nursing, as a professional healthcare discipline, must adapt to these technological advancements and effectively utilize AI applications [4]. Nurses play a central role in patient care, and the increasing demand for evidence‐based practices makes it imperative for nurses to be proficient in current technologies. AI applications optimize care processes by enabling nurses to enhance their skills, deliver higher‐quality and more personalized care, and reduce repetitive workloads [2, 4].

AI literacy is defined as a set of competencies that enable individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies, establish effective communication and collaboration with these systems, and use them as tools in different areas of life (home, work, online environments) [9]. Within this framework, AI literacy encompasses individuals' abilities to understand, use, and interact with AI systems [10]. The increasing use of AI technologies in healthcare necessitates that nurses develop their knowledge and skills accordingly. Increasing nurses' knowledge of AI facilitates the adoption and broader use of these technologies in healthcare services [11].

Enhancing nurses' attitudes toward AI critically depends on increasing their AI literacy. Higher levels of AI literacy positively influence nurses' attitudes toward AI technologies. Increased AI literacy, together with the accompanying positive attitudes, contributes to improving patient monitoring and treatment processes, reducing error rates, enhancing workflow efficiency, and strengthening data analysis. It also facilitates the early detection of patient conditions, thereby improving the quality of care. This process supports nurses' professional satisfaction while promoting the ethical and responsible use of AI tools to improve patient outcomes [12, 13]. In intensive care units (ICUs), where patient data are complex and urgent, AI technologies offer advantages such as advanced monitoring systems and clinical decision support [14].

In intensive care nursing, the potential of AI to facilitate workflows, enhance data analysis, and support the early detection of patient deterioration is widely recognized. However, increased workload, information overload, and difficulties in interpreting AI outputs are major concerns among nurses [15]. Although nurses' levels of AI literacy are reported to be moderate, the rate of AI technology use remains quite low [16]. One study reported that more than half of intensive care nurses had never used AI‐supported applications, while only a small proportion had advanced knowledge and experience in this area [17]. In this context, it is essential for intensive care nurses to receive structured, practice‐oriented training on AI technologies to ensure the safe and effective integration of these systems into healthcare services [12, 13]. Furthermore, improving AI literacy and attitudes among intensive care nurses is important for strengthening the future workforce and enhancing digital readiness in clinical care. To the best of our knowledge, no study in the literature has examined both AI literacy and attitudes toward AI technologies among intensive care nurses simultaneously. In this context, the present study was conducted to determine the AI literacy levels of intensive care nurses, their attitudes toward AI technologies, and the factors associated with these attitudes.

Methods

Type of Research

This study is a cross‐sectional study.

Population and Sample of the Study

The study population consisted of a total of 321 intensive care nurses working in the ICUs of two hospitals located in a province in the Central Black Sea Region, one of which is a public hospital and the other a training and research hospital. Hospitals include internal medicine ICUs, surgical ICUs, general ICUs, coronary ICUs, and pediatric ICUs. No sampling method was applied in the study, and it was aimed to reach the entire population. Nurses who had been working in the ICU for at least 6 months and who volunteered to participate in the study were included. Those on leave (annual leave/maternity leave/unpaid leave) during the study period were excluded. The study was completed with 280 participants. This study reached 87% of the population.

Data Collection Tools

The data collection tools are the Personal Information Form, the AILS, and the AIAS‐4.

Personal Information Form

This form was prepared by the researchers based on the literature. The form contains 16 questions regarding the demographic characteristics of the participants.

Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale (AILS)

This scale was developed by Wang et al. (2023) [18]. The scale was adapted into Turkish by Polatgil and Güler (2023) to assess AI literacy among adults in the general population. The scale consists of 12 questions and 4 sub‐dimensions: awareness, use, evaluation, and ethics. The questions are Likert‐type questions ranging from 1 to 5. Questions 2, 5, and 11 require reverse scoring. Questions 2, 5, and 11 require reverse scoring. The Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient of the scale is 0.93 [10]. In this study, the Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.77.

Artificial Intelligence Attitude Scale (AIAS‐4)

This scale is a short self‐report tool designed to assess individuals' attitudes toward AI technology. It was developed by Grassini (2023) [19]. The scale was adapted into Turkish by Arslan et al. (2023) to assess AI literacy among adults in the general population. The scale is unidimensional and consists of 4 questions. Participants score their agreement with each item using a 10‐point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 10 = Strongly Agree). The total score is calculated by averaging all item scores (total score divided by 4). The higher the score, the higher the individuals' attitudes and acceptance levels toward AI technology. The Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.93 [20]. In this study, the Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.97.

Data Collection

The study data were collected between June 1 and July 15, 2025. The data collection process was carried out through face‐to‐face administration of scales and questionnaires by the researchers. Data were obtained from nurses working in ICUs during periods when the clinical workload was relatively low and in an appropriate environment where distractions were minimized. This approach was preferred to ensure that participants evaluated the items more carefully and to enhance the quality of responses. Before administering the data collection forms, participants were informed about the study, and nurses who voluntarily agreed to participate were included in the sample. The data collection process took an average of 16 min per participant. Approximately 87% of the target population was reached within the scope of the study, while the remaining 13% could not be included in the sample due to annual leave or unwillingness to participate.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS 25 software package. A statistical significance level of p < 0.05 was accepted. Normality of the data distribution was determined by evaluating skewness and kurtosis values within ±2. Descriptive statistics such as number, percentage, mean (X̄) ± standard deviation (SD), and minimum–maximum values for continuous variables, as well as independent samples t‐test and one‐way ANOVA (Tukey test for post hoc analysis), were used in the analysis of the data. In addition, multiple linear regression (Enter method) analysis was used to determine the factors influencing participants' attitudes towards AI technology. For the multiple linear regression analysis, multicollinearity and autocorrelation were tested in the dataset (VIF < 10; tolerance > 0.1; Durbin–Watson = 1.470). Multivariate normality assumptions were tested (Cook's distance < 1). Categorical variables were transformed into dummy variables to be included in the regression analysis; for example, variables such as gender (male = 0, female = 1) and education level were coded appropriately. In this study, the dependent variable was the AIAS‐4 score, and the independent variables were age, gender, marital status, education level, and AILS score. Although many demographic and AI‐related variables were found to be statistically significant in the bivariate analyses, not all of these variables were included in the multivariate model. During model construction, variable selection was performed to reduce the risk of overfitting, prevent multicollinearity issues, and improve model parsimony. In this context, variables that exhibited a high degree of multicollinearity, represented similar constructs, or were considered not to provide additional explanatory value to the model were excluded. As a result, the final model was constructed to include a limited number of variables that showed an independent and statistically significant association with the dependent variable.

Ethical Considerations

Prior to the study, approval was obtained from the ethics committee of a university (May 29, 2025; Decision No: 01‐121). Participants were informed, and those who volunteered were included in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration.

Results

Regarding years of experience, 50.4% of the nurses had 1–10 years of experience. When considering years of experience in intensive care, 67.1% of the participants had 1–10 years of experience. 79.6% of the nurses worked both day and night shifts. Furthermore, 70.4% of the nurses worked in the internal medicine ICU (Table 1).

Discussion

Nurses are a key professional group that plays a critical role in the integration of AI into nursing practice [21]. In the context of intensive care nursing, although the potential of AI to facilitate workflows, enhance data analysis, and support the early detection of patient conditions is widely recognized, increased workload, information overload, and difficulties in interpreting AI outputs have emerged as major areas of concern among nurses [15]. This study investigated the AI literacy levels of intensive care nurses, their attitudes toward AI technologies, and the factors associated with these attitudes.

One of the main determinants in improving nurses' attitudes toward AI is AI literacy. It has been shown that increases in AI literacy positively influence nurses' attitudes toward these technologies. Nurses with higher levels of AI literacy are reported to contribute to the quality of care by demonstrating more positive attitudes in patient monitoring and treatment processes; this also supports professional satisfaction and promotes the ethical and responsible use of AI tools [12, 13]. In addition, it is reported that the intention to adopt AI among intensive care nurses positively influences their readiness to use this technology. Perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, external advantages, and the presence of facilitating conditions are important factors that increase both readiness and adoption of AI [17]. In this study, a strong, positive, and statistically significant relationship was found between AIAS‐4 and AILS; multivariate analysis revealed that increases in AI literacy levels significantly and positively predicted attitudes toward AI. The findings are consistent with the existing literature.

Nurses generally appear to exhibit positive attitudes toward AI technologies. However, there is a need to further investigate the factors associated with attitudes toward AI [22]. Nurses' attitudes toward AI may be associated with factors such as age, gender, and educational level. Previous studies have reported that nursing students exhibit positive attitudes toward AI [23, 24]. In another study, younger nurses were found to demonstrate more positive attitudes toward AI compared to their older counterparts [25]. In the present study, age was identified as a significant predictor, and intensive care nurses aged 35 and under exhibited more positive attitudes toward AI technologies. Consistent with the literature, younger participants may display more positive attitudes toward AI technologies.

The literature also indicates a significant relationship between nurses' education level and their attitudes toward AI technologies [23, 24, 26, 27, 28]. One study reported that nurses with postgraduate education exhibited more positive attitudes toward AI [27]. Studies conducted with nursing students have shown that participants generally exhibit positive attitudes toward AI, while variables such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are influenced by factors such as academic level and prior AI training [23, 24]. Similarly, in the present study, intensive care nurses with a bachelor's degree or higher were found to have more positive attitudes toward AI technologies. This suggests that education level is an important factor in the development of positive attitudes toward AI. This finding indicates that as education level increases, awareness, understanding, and acceptance of AI may also improve. Accordingly, it is important to integrate AI‐related content into nursing education curricula at an early stage in a structured manner. Positioning AI as a supportive tool in clinical decision‐making processes may contribute to the development of both critical thinking skills and digital competencies [23, 28].

It has also been reported that attitudes toward AI technologies may be associated with gender [21, 24]. Gender‐based differences have been observed in the literature, with male nurses generally reported to exhibit more positive attitudes toward AI [29]. In the present study, male intensive care nurses were also found to have more positive attitudes toward AI technologies, and gender was identified as a significant positive predictor of these attitudes. However, some studies have reported that female nursing students have higher overall attitude scores toward AI in healthcare. This suggests that findings may be influenced by sample characteristics and the sociocultural context. The effect of gender on attitudes toward AI should be evaluated not as a sole determinant but in interaction with factors such as cultural background, educational level, and professional experience [24].

The relationship between nurses' attitudes toward AI and certain demographic factors appears to be heterogeneous. The literature indicates that the effects of some demographic variables, such as marital status, are limited and inconsistent [29]. Some studies have reported that there is no significant difference in nurses' attitudes toward AI technologies based on marital status [27]. In the present study, being single was found to be a significant positive predictor of attitudes toward AI technologies. This difference may be explained by the influence of potential confounding variables such as sample characteristics, sociocultural context, age, or familiarity with technology. In particular, single individuals may be more open to innovation or may use technology more extensively, which may have contributed to this finding. Therefore, it is recommended that the relationship between marital status and attitudes toward AI be examined using more comprehensive and multivariate models.

In this study, AILS and AIAS‐4 were used together to evaluate the cognitive and attitudinal characteristics of intensive care nurses regarding AI from a holistic perspective. The intensive care environment provides a clinical context in which advanced technological systems and decision support mechanisms are widely used, and where nurses interact directly with digital and AI‐based applications. Therefore, it is critical to assess not only attitudes toward AI but also the knowledge, understanding, and evaluation skills related to this technology. In this context, AILS enables the assessment of the multidimensional structure of AI literacy, while AIAS‐4 provides an important complement by measuring attitudes toward AI in a brief and practical manner. Moreover, the combined use of both scales allowed for the examination of the relationship between intensive care nurses' level of understanding of AI and their attitudes toward this technology, thereby strengthening the interpretation of the findings. However, it should be considered that the use of these instruments in a specific and high‐technology clinical environment such as intensive care may be influenced by contextual factors. In this study, the internal consistency coefficient of AILS (α = 0.77) was found to be lower than the value reported in the Turkish adaptation study (α = 0.93), indicating the sensitivity of the measurement to the sample and setting. This may be related to the heterogeneity in participants' levels of experience with AI and the specific characteristics of the clinical context. The findings indicate that the scale demonstrates acceptable reliability in the current sample; however, they also suggest that its psychometric properties should be re‐evaluated in different samples.

In the present study, AILS score, age, gender, marital status, and education level were found to be statistically significant predictors among the variables included in the model, and the model explained 64% of the variance in attitudes toward AI technologies. The adjusted R 2 value of 0.640 obtained in this study indicates that nurses' AI literacy and demographic characteristics have a high explanatory power for attitudes toward AI. Consistent with the literature, this result supports the view that AI literacy is an important antecedent in the development of attitudes; as nurses' knowledge and competency levels regarding technology increase, their confidence in the safe and effective integration of these technologies is also strengthened. As emphasized by Atalla et al. and Nashwan et al., increasing literacy levels is an essential step for improving nurses' attitudes and ensuring a successful implementation process [12, 13]. In this context, the high explanatory power of the model theoretically confirms the determining role of literacy on attitudes.

However, an explained variance of 64% in a cross‐sectional attitude model also raises certain methodological considerations. This high value may partially reflect a conceptual overlap [30]. It can be argued that the boundary between nurses' capacity to understand AI tools (literacy) and their tendency to accept and adopt these tools in their professional roles (attitude) is highly permeable. In particular, the inclusion of both technical knowledge and ethical guidelines in AI literacy programs may have led to a strong reciprocal reinforcement between participants' knowledge and attitudes toward technology.

Moreover, such a high value raises the possibility that it may reflect common method variance among self‐report measures [30]. The fact that the data were collected from the same group of participants, within the same time frame, and using similar Likert‐type scales may have led participants to respond to both AI literacy and attitude variables in a similar manner. Hassan et al. reported that while nurses acknowledge the potential of AI, they also have concerns regarding workload and complexity [15]. This indicates that participants' attitudes are multidimensional and that the measurement tools used may assess this complexity within a similar cognitive framework. In conclusion, although the high explanatory power of the model highlights the central role of AI literacy in shaping attitudes, future studies are recommended to employ longitudinal designs or multiple data sources to confirm the causality of this relationship and to minimize method variance.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, due to its cross‐sectional design, the relationships between variables cannot be evaluated over time, and causal inferences cannot be made. The use of self‐report scales for data collection increases the risk of measurement bias, as participants' responses are based on their own perceptions. In addition, the face‐to‐face data collection process may have led to social desirability bias, particularly in items related to AI and technology, as participants may have tended to present themselves as more competent or more positive. Additionally, measuring both AI literacy and attitudes toward AI using self‐report instruments introduces the risk of common method bias.

Another limitation of the study is that the internal consistency coefficient of AILS (α = 0.77) was found to be lower than that reported in the original study. This may indicate that the measurement was influenced by sample‐specific characteristics and may limit the generalizability of the findings. In particular, the relatively homogeneous structure of the sample and the varying levels of participants' experience with AI may have affected the internal consistency of the scale. Therefore, future research should re‐evaluate the scale in different and more diverse samples.

The fact that the study was conducted in only two hospitals located in a single province limits the representativeness of the sample and restricts the generalizability of the findings to different regions, institutions, and working conditions. Although some diversity was achieved by including internal medicine, surgical, general, coronary, and pediatric intensive care units, this scope may not fully reflect all types of ICUs and the variability of practices across different institutional settings. Considering these limitations, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies incorporating larger, multicenter, longitudinal designs and diverse data collection methods are recommended.

Conclusion and Recommendations

In this study, the AI literacy levels of intensive care nurses, their attitudes toward AI technologies, and the factors predicting these attitudes were examined. According to the results of the regression analysis, age was identified as a significant predictor, and nurses aged 36 and over were found to have more negative attitudes toward AI technologies. In contrast, higher levels of AI literacy, male gender, being single, and having a bachelor's degree or higher were found to be significant positive predictors of attitudes toward AI technologies.

Based on these findings, it is recommended to plan structured training programs aimed at improving AI literacy and fostering positive attitudes toward AI technologies among intensive care nurses. In addition, it is recommended to integrate AI‐related content into nursing education curricula and to conduct multicenter studies with larger samples, particularly longitudinal studies on this topic.
