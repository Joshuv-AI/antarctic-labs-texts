Tick bite prevention and management: integrating patient perspectives to shape patient education
Authors: Katherine Duong, Melanie Burk, David Boettiger, Bronte Cross, Ayesha Owen, Jordan Symons, Andrew Ratchford, Sheryl van Nunen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The rise in tick anaphylaxis (TA) in Australia and worldwide underscores the need for effective tick bite prevention and management strategies.Objectives:This retrospective survey of a clinical TA cohort aimed to determine prevention and management strategies employed by patients who had received education following diagnosis of TA and identify areas for improvement in patient education.Methods:Patients diagnosed with TA by a consultant immunologist between 2005 and 2021 in NSW, Australia, completed a survey in 2021 assessing tick bite recurrence, life stage identification, removal methods, need for medical assistance, prevention strategies, and geographic tick bite locations. Relationships between tick bite occurrence and prevention methods, such as the use of permethrin-treated clothing and backyard pest control, were analyzed with Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests.Results:Despite receiving education at the time of their previous TA, 59% of patients experienced further tick bites, and 50% struggled to identify the tick life stage. Preferred prevention methods included avoidance, untreated clothing, and repellents, with some using backyard insecticides. We observed a trend towards reduced tick bite recurrence among patients who treated their backyards and wore permethrin-treated clothing. Almost 46% of bites occurred in patients’ backyards. Adherence to tick removal advice was highly variable. Only 30% of patients sought medical help when bitten again.Conclusion:The recurrence of tick bite in TA patients despite education at first diagnosis indicates a need for clearer and more frequent education to encourage adherence to prevention strategies, targeting high-risk groups, to prevent further fatalities.

1. Introduction

Ticks are well known globally to cause an array of tick-borne diseases and allergies. Diseases such as tick typhus, tick paralysis, and more rarely, Q fever and babesiosis have been reported in Australia, while other diseases have been found overseas, such as borreliosis, tick-borne encephalitis, and Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever [1–4]. Tick-induced allergies range from localized skin reactions to tick anaphylaxis (TA) and the increasingly prevalent mammalian meat allergy (MMA) [5].

Many TA cases are reported in Australia, likely due to the culprit tick species, the Australian paralysis tick (Ixodes holocyclus), [6–8] having a longer hypostome, which allows passage of larger amounts of allergen into its host [9]. However, TA has been witnessed in other countries [10–13], and fatalities have been reported in both Australia and Japan [14, 15]. Similarly, MMA cases are increasing globally [5]. It is acquired through the transmission of the alpha-gal carbohydrate (galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose) from tick saliva into the host and a resulting IgE-mediated cross-reactivity to mammalian meats [5]. While severe allergic reactions to mammalian meats are concerning, MMA also poses the risk of cross-reactivity to other foods and therapeutic goods that contain alpha-gal, and brings forth the issue of drug anaphylaxis [16–18], invoking the use of the term alpha-gal syndrome (AGS). More recent evidence has also demonstrated an association between alpha-gal-sensitized patients and higher rates of obstructive coronary artery disease [19, 20].

With increasing presentations of TA and MMA, appropriate tick bite management and prevention are crucial for reducing the acquisition and exacerbation of these conditions. Such measures have been explored in the literature but remain somewhat inconsistent internationally. Some current recommendations for tick bite prevention include wearing appropriate clothing such as permethrin-treated clothing, using topical insect repellent, vigilance outdoors during warmer months, and showering after returning from a tick-infested area [1, 21–24]. Research has also supported killing adult ticks in situ with a freezing agent before careful removal with fine-tipped forceps to reduce the risk of allergic reaction to the tick bite; however, further studies are required to support this [23]. Actual methods for both tick bite prevention and management used by patients vary greatly, and most evidence is low quality due to inconsistencies in sample sizes, variability in study methods, and paucity of data [15, 23, 25]. Because incorrect removal or disturbance of a tick can lead it to inject allergen into its host, resulting in potentially fatal tick-induced allergies [5], it is necessary to continue investigating the techniques used and whether they assist in the reduction of allergic reactions.

Thus, this study aimed to determine the rates of recurrence of tick bites and tick bite prevention and management strategies employed by patients diagnosed with TA after receiving education. Using the data, recommendations for future patient education on tick bite prevention and management will be made, targeted towards clinicians, municipal authorities, and groups that have responsibilities in patient education, in hopes of reducing the harmful consequences of tick bites.

2. Methods

This study examined the responses from a follow-up survey of patients with known TA.

2.1. Patient data

Patients who presented with TA and were managed in emergency departments in NSW, particularly the Northern Beaches area and other tick-endemic areas of coastal NSW and southeast Queensland, Australia, were referred to and reviewed by a consultant immunologist and allergist between 2005 and 2021. During this review, patients were provided verbal and written education regarding ticks and tick bites, including recognizing tick life stages, strategies to avoid future tick bites, and how they should manage further tick bites. They were also provided with recommendations for further online educative resources.

Information collected at the time of review included patient postcode, date of tick bite, life stage of the culprit tick, location on their body of the tick bite/s, and any reaction to the tick bite/s.

A SurveyMonkey form was sent by email or post in June 2021 to these patients, asking about any further tick bites, tick bite location (geographic as opposed to site of the tick bite on the body), removal methods, management, outcomes, and whether family members had had tick bites. These responses were recorded in a separate database. Patients who provided incomplete survey responses or where their address, month, and year of tick bite were not recorded were also excluded as their data were considered incomplete for analysis.

2.2. Data analysis

Responses from the survey were grouped into tick bite recurrence, tick life stage, removal method at home, whether medical assistance was sought or required, tick bite prevention methods, and location of the tick bite/s on the patient’s body. Additionally, Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to investigate the relationship between tick bite recurrence and the use of permethrin-treated clothing and backyard pest control treatment as preventative strategies. These prevention strategies were chosen for further analysis as they tended to be less employed compared with avoidance of tick-prone areas or wearing insect repellent, making them a potential area for further research.

3. Results

A total of 377 patients diagnosed with TA were reviewed and sent surveys. After applying the relevant exclusion criteria listed above, 169 appropriate patient survey responses were included. Median age of participants was 58 years. Median interval between diagnosis and completion of survey was 6 years.

3.1. Further tick bites

Despite education on tick bite prevention and management, 59% of patients still had further tick bites, and 50% of patients were unable to identify the life stage of the culprit tick (Fig. 1). Of the 84 patients who were unsure of the tick’s life stage, 18% attended a hospital emergency department, and 82% did not. 3.2. Tick bite prevention strategies

Patients preferred to use avoidance, normal clothing (not treated with permethrin), repellent, or a combination of the 3 as their main forms of tick bite prevention. A considerable number of patients also treated their backyards with pesticide in conjunction with other strategies. We observed a trend towards reduced tick bite recurrence when patients used both permethrin-treated clothing and treated their backyards, in comparison to employing either strategy independently or employing neither, although this was not statistically significant (log-rank = 0.2531) (Fig. 2).

However, 46% of patients recall being bitten in their own backyard, but 30% of patients could not recall where they were bitten. Some patients (24%) were bitten in parks or bushland.

3.3. Tick bite management strategies

Many patients adhered to the advice of leaving the tick undisturbed, while some patients had access to freezing the tick or using 5% permethrin cream (Fig. 3) to kill the tick in situ. However, many patients either did not seek medical assistance (41%) or could not recall how they managed the tick bite at the time (30%). Of the patients who did seek medical assistance, many patients presented to a hospital emergency department (62%) or presented to their general practitioner (16%) (Fig. 4).

4. Discussion

Tick-induced allergies have become increasingly common along the Eastern seaboard of NSW, Australia, particularly this century [26], with TA resulting in 4 recorded deaths between 1997 and 2013 [14]. In Australia, tick and ant bites have the third-highest fatality rate relative to hospitalizations, after snake bites and hornet, wasp, and bee stings. Interestingly, many of those deaths occurred at home and in major city areas or inner regional areas with access to healthcare [27]. Thus, it is vital for those who reside in tick-prone areas to be well educated on how to prevent and manage tick bites, including when to seek medical assistance, should they occur. This study aimed at determining whether awareness of these measures reduced the likelihood of further tick bites.

4.1. Recurrence of tick bites

Most patients, despite receiving verbal and written education, had a further tick bite after diagnosis of TA (59% of patients), and 50% were unable to identify the life stage of the culprit tick. Previous studies have indicated that only adult female tick saliva contains the allergens that trigger TA, suggesting that the adult tick is more likely to trigger an episode of anaphylaxis than a nymph or larval tick [6, 26]. It may also be postulated that nymph and larval ticks produce less saliva, hence they are less likely to cause TA. Recognizing the life stage of a tick should thus help to prevent unnecessary presentations to hospital emergency departments and assist clinicians in patient triage. For patients who struggle to identify life stage by examining the tick itself, it may be useful to identify life stage based on the location of the tick bite, as there is evidence in humans and animals that adult ticks preferentially attach to the host’s head and neck [28–31]. Excluding cases where the tick may have detached, been unnoticed, or the life stage could not be recalled at the time of completing the survey, the number of patients who could not identify the tick life stage demonstrates a need for ongoing education and informative resources outside of verbal education from a clinician.

4.2. Tick bite prevention strategies

Current advice for tick bite prevention is inconsistent between Australasian and international organizations, such as the Australasian Society for Clinical Immunology and Allergy (ASCIA), compared with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Information provided to Australasian hospitals and medical professionals recommends that patients use strategies including: avoidance of tick-prone areas, light colored protective clothing, permethrin-treated gear and clothing [21, 24], insect repellent, elevated camping beds, checking pets, clothing, and one’s own body for ticks after returning from a tick-prone area, treatment of backyards with insecticide, and removal of loose foliage which may house ticks. Despite this information being available online and also advised to patients during a consultation, the majority of patients reported avoidance of tick-prone areas, while wearing repellent (with normal clothing) as their main form of prevention, with some using multiple strategies at once and others opting for 1. The tendency for people to use limited strategies and have a relaxed attitude towards tick bite prevention reflects a previous study in Tennessee [32]. This study found that less than one-fourth of respondents to a survey consistently used insect repellent before entering known tick-infested areas or performed body checks after leaving the area, despite knowledge of risks of tick-borne disease [32]. Possible barriers to effectively employing prevention strategies may include lack of accessibility and convenience of the strategy or tool, or difficulty justifying the need to use multiple strategies, which may be related to a current lack of continuing public education on tick bites. In the prevention of tick bites and borreliosis, research indicates that those who understand their personal risk, have knowledge of borreliosis (“Lyme disease”), and personally know a person affected by borreliosis are more likely to engage in prevention practices [33]. Future studies could investigate whether having a previous TA episode, which is arguably distressing for a patient, changes attitudes towards prevention at all.

Several patients also had their backyards treated with insecticide, likely because they had previously been bitten in their own yards. This study showed a trend towards reduced recurrent tick bites when patients wore permethrin-treated clothing and treated their backyards. A larger study would be required to confirm this association and reinforce the need to use multiple prevention strategies together. It may be beneficial for patient resources to recommend treating backyards annually in spring and after heavy rain seasons, as this would best align with the peak of TA presentations and the tick life cycle, and thus should provide the best protection [34]. Alternatives to insecticides should also be explored, such as grazing animals for those with larger estates, to reduce the growth of shrubbery and thus produce less suitable habitats for ticks [35, 36]. Overall, because many patients indicated they were unsure of their geographical location when bitten, consistent and regular use of multiple preventative measures is recommended in all locations.

4.3. Tick removal methods

The current global consensus on tick removal is in favor of using fine-tipped forceps to remove the tick as close to the skin’s surface as possible, to prevent crushing the tick and further injection of toxin into the skin [37, 38]. However, more recently, evidence has highlighted that tick killing in situ with an ether-containing spray (adult ticks) or permethrin cream (nymph and larval ticks) before removal with fine-tipped forceps in the hands of an expert may decrease the risk of patients having anaphylaxis or an allergic reaction [23]. In another retrospective study of TA patients, patients who used a freezing method only made up 5.1% of the patients who developed anaphylaxis, whereas those who used a mechanical method, such as tweezers, digital, or other tools, made up 84% of the cohort [39]. The patients in the present study were given ASCIA’s current recommendation of “Freeze don’t Squeeze,” and of the patients who did attempt a method at home, many adhered to using either the freezing spray or permethrin cream or both. Furthermore, many patients did not seek medical assistance, whether that be in the form of a paramedic, hospital emergency department, or primary care physician. These results may indicate that patients felt they could confidently manage tick bites on their own with the new information they were provided. It appears that patients in this study were better able to follow advice relating to management of tick bites over advice on prevention strategies, suggesting a possible gap in patient knowledge or motivation to use preventative strategies.

4.4. Limitations

The patient survey was sent to all patients who were identified as having a TA diagnosis sometime between 2005 and 2021. However, the median interval between diagnosis and completion of survey was 6 years; thus, it is highly likely that patients who were diagnosed earlier had lower retention of verbal education than those diagnosed later. Therefore, it could be argued that the results of the survey indicate a need for reeducation, as opposed to a need for differing education strategies. The issue of the effectiveness of education is further complicated, as it is unknown whether patients independently sought further information to reinforce their learning after being reviewed. Number of subsequent tick bites following TA diagnosis was reported; however, it is likely that data is missing or inaccurate with delays between diagnosis and completion of survey. While it provides some insight into whether patients retained and used information related to tick bite prevention strategies, it may have been useful to also record the number of subsequent TA episodes following diagnosis. A lower number of subsequent TA episodes may suggest that patients are successfully using tick bite management strategies, for example, leaving ticks undisturbed rather than attempting removal with tweezers, to prevent transmission of allergen and thus anaphylaxis. In performing Kaplan–Meier analyses to investigate whether tick bite incidence is related to the use of specific prevention strategies, such as a treated backyard, it is rare that any prevention strategy is used on its own; hence, it is difficult to test the effectiveness of strategies in reducing tick bite occurrence independently.

4.5. Future directions

Currently, there are various authoritative sources that provide information to the Australian public about tick bites, including government fact sheets, allergy or tick information websites, and information from health or hospital websites. While these resources may be helpful for patients and clinicians who are specifically looking for information, such as how to manage a bite, more accessible and ongoing education is required. In particular, tick bite prevention should be prioritized over that of tick bite management, including more detail about backyard treatment and upkeep, as this is less used as a strategy than others and has the potential to be highly effective [40].

With the majority of TA patients being older females between the ages of 61 and 80 [39], it is important to use strategies targeting this demographic in educational campaigns. For this group of patients who have less screen time, it may be useful to have tangible resources, such as posters in public areas, education sessions, or speakers at community events, or handouts with infographics to keep. It is also vital to have well-trained clinicians in tick-infested regions to educate elderly patients on the topic, as it has been previously demonstrated that patients can benefit from this form of verbal education [41].

Despite an older demographic in TA, the increasing role of digital media in health promotion and education should not be overlooked, as its broad uses have been summarized in previous literature [42, 43]. Primarily, it could be used to widely disseminate correct information about tick bite prevention, such as through advertisements or accessible QR codes [40]. Further development of interactive applications that aid patients in managing tick bites in real time, similar to the Tick Bite Bot available in the United States [38], can occur.

The impact of tick bite prevention extends beyond preventing TA; MMA and its sequelae, including AGS, may also be avoided. MMA resulting from tick bites has been recorded in many countries worldwide and is increasing in prevalence [5, 44]. Its diagnosis may be made by correlating the patient’s clinical history, such as a recent tick bite and symptoms of an allergic reaction upon consuming mammalian meat, with elevated serum alpha-gal specific IgE levels [5]. Without further tick bites and injection of tick saliva, however, the IgE levels will decrease, eventually to a range that allows the patient to safely consume mammalian meat again [45]. While MMA forces individuals to avoid specific foods for up to several years, a more critical consequence of MMA is potential allergy to medications and hospital products containing alpha-gal carbohydrate, such as cetuximab, heparin, resuscitation fluids, vaccines, and antivenoms [16–18]. Drug anaphylaxis is a leading cause of anaphylaxis fatalities in Australia and worldwide [15, 46], and without awareness of MMA, tick bites may inherently contribute further to these statistics. Unfortunately, immunotherapy is not currently available for MMA, nor will it likely become widely available, due to lack of commercial viability [47–50]. Hence, nonsensitized and sensitized individuals must turn to tick bite prevention strategies as their form of MMA prevention.

Thus, the rationale for public education of primary and secondary prevention of tick bites is manifold: (1) reduce episodes of TA in those who have suffered TA, (2) reduce the chance of an individual developing TA, (3) potentially reduce fatalities from TA, (4) prevent recurrence, increased alpha-gal sensitization levels and acquisition of MMA from increases in IgE levels, (5) potentially reduce the number of cases of MMA, and (6) reduce possible fatalities due to drug anaphylaxis.

5. Conclusion

This study has demonstrated that despite face-to-face patient education, tick bites still occur in patients who have had previous TA. The survey has revealed areas for future focus: noting the bodily location of tick bite and identifying tick life stage, as well as emphasis on tick bite prevention strategies. Campaigns should be ongoing and primarily targeted towards the demographics of those commonly presenting with TA. Ultimately, with further research and refinement of tick bite prevention and management strategies, clinicians, educators, employers in tick-exposed industries, and environmental experts may be better equipped to provide education to the public. With this, a reduction in tick bites and their sequelae may be seen, with fewer resulting fatalities.
