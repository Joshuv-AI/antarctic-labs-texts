Serous maculopathy in congenital cavitary optic disc anomaly: A case series
Authors: Jenna Krivit, Gregor S Reiter, Run Zhou Ye, Kahlen Darr, Lisa A Schimmenti, Raymond Iezzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to describe an adult child and parent with cavitary optic disc anomalies (CODA) with associated serous maculopathy and a non-coding triplication upstream of MMP19.DesignRetrospective, single-institution, observational case series.Subjects4 eyes of 2 related patients.MethodsThis retrospective chart review used electronic medical records to summarize patient medical history, ophthalmology consultations, whole genome analysis, and imaging.Main outcome measuresDescriptive analysis of multimodal imaging and genomic sequencing.ResultsPatient 1, a 23-year-old male, was referred for visual changes in the setting of paraganglioma. Introduction

Cavitary optic disc anomalies (CODA) refer to inherited cavitary excavations of the optic nerve. The term “cavitary optic disc anomaly” was first used by Slusher et al. to describe an autosomal dominant pedigree with a range of optic disc abnormalities including features of optic pit, optic nerve coloboma, and morning glory disc anomaly.1 This study reviewed 35 members of a family over 5 generations and concluded the spectrum of morphological variants, ranging from large anomalous discs to typical pits and colobomas, suggested variable expression of a single autosomal dominant defect.1

Fingert and associates mapped familial autosomal dominant CODA to a novel genetic locus on chromosome 12q in 2007.2 Expanded in 2016, this team reported all 17 affected family members had a 6 kilobase (kb) heterozygous triplication upstream of the matrix metalloproteinase 19 gene (MMP19).3 This variant was not present in unaffected members of the family or control subjects. MMPs are zinc-binding endoproteases that maintain tissue architecture and degrade basement membranes and extracellular matrix. They hypothesized that increased MMP19 activity in the optic nerve head may undermine the architecture of the lamina cribrosa and lead to a collapse and malformation of the optic nerve head that is characteristic of CODA phenotypes.1, 2, 3

CODA is a heterogenous phenotypic spectrum including features of optic pits, optic nerve colobomas and morning glory anomaly. Histopathologic specimens of optic pit, optic nerve coloboma and morning glory anomaly demonstrate posterior herniation of dysplastic retina through a defect in the lamina cribrosa and/or juxtapapillary sclera.4 It is hypothesized that the CODA spectrum, associated with abnormal MMP19 activity, shares an embryologic mechanism related to embryonic fissure closure with these distinct clinical entities. Failure of this fissure to close is hypothesized to result in the diversity of CODA phenotypes.4

Optic pits are focal depressions of the optic nerve head, often small and located in the temporal optic disc.5 Most optic pits are sporadic, though autosomal dominant inheritance of this condition has been reported.6 Serous maculopathy develops in more than 50 % of optic pit cases, particularly with large excavations.7 Coloboma of the optic nerve is typically a unilateral excavation in the inferonasal disc.8 Colobomas are typically sporadic and serous maculopathy may develop.9 Morning glory disc anomaly is a unilateral funnel-shaped excavation involving the optic disc and peripapillary retina with an enlarged optic papilla, abnormal vasculature and may be associated with rhegmatogenous retinal detachment.8

The association between the MMP19 CODA genotype and serous maculopathy has not been specifically described, whereas optic pit maculopathy has been well studied. Optic pit maculopathy describes an accumulation of intraretinal fluid (IRF) and/or subretinal fluid (SRF) contiguous with the optic pit depression sometimes progressing to serous detachment of the macula.8 Multiple studies described optic pit maculopathy on optical coherence tomography (OCT), specifically the retinoschisis-like pattern, and hypothesize the fluid emanates from the pit itself.10, 11, 12, 13, 14

We report an adult child and parent with bilateral CODA who each have the MMP19 upstream triplications who presented with serous maculopathy. CODA maculopathy may represent a distinct mechanism of serous fluid accumulation that differs from the mechanism seen in optic pit maculopathy.

Methods

This retrospective case series of patients at Mayo Clinic Rochester, MN, USA includes medical record data and retinal imaging. Approval for this study was obtained from the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board, and the study followed the tenets set by the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed patient consent was waived due to retrospective nature. Information was gathered and secured in a manner compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

A 23-year-old male (patient 1) and his mother (patient 2) were selected for this case series given the CODA presentation, confirmed with whole genome analysis, with concurrent or resolved serous maculopathy. The electronic medical record was used to gather patient demographics, medical history, ophthalmology evaluations, and medical genetics evaluations. Imaging including color fundus photography (CFP), fluorescein angiography (FA), fundus autofluorescence (FAF) at 488 nm and spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT, Spectralis HRA + OCT, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) were analyzed and included.

Results

Case 1

Patient 1 is a 23-year-old Caucasian male with significant medical history of right-sided jugular paraganglioma diagnosed after presenting with sudden unilateral hearing loss. One month after diagnosis, he underwent right transtemporal resection. Genetic testing for hereditary paraganglioma-pheochromocytoma syndrome was positive for heterozygous pathogenic variant of the SDHC gene. Two years later the patient presented with visual changes prompting MRI which noted growth of paraganglioma, and the patient underwent repeat surgical resection. Notably, he was prescribed 4 mg dexamethasone taper post-operatively. Following re-resection, he underwent proton beam radiation.

The patient was initially evaluated by an optometrist for reported vision changes, OD 20/25-1, OS 20/50-2. Patient 1 is a college student with self-reported stress and “type A″ personality. Further evaluation noted optic nerve asymmetry and serous retinal detachment. He was referred for retinal evaluation for possible optic pit maculopathy.

At time of retina consultation best-corrected visual acuity was OD 20/20 and OS 20/25. Optical coherence tomography of the disc showed that the optic nerve cavitation appeared to be full thickness in the left eye (Fig. 1a) and incomplete in the right eye (Fig. 1a). SD-OCT showed SRF adjacent to the optic nerve in the left eye without direct extension to optic nerve head (Fig. 1d). There was one miniscule cystoid pocket of IRF within the inner nuclear layer (Fig. 1b). Central choroidal thickness (Fig. 1c and d) was 301.6μm (OD) and 362μm (OS). Fluorescein angiography (FA) showed intense staining of the optic nerve in the left eye without pooling or leakage into the serous elevation (Fig. 2).

Case 2

Patient 2 is a 46-year-old Caucasian female with no significant medical history. The patient reported bilateral optic pits were first mentioned during routine optometry visit. The patient also reported developing “retinal fluid” during pregnancy. The patient did not recall investigations into either finding (optic pit or fluid) at the time.

Retina consultation and genetic counselling was recommended following congenital CODA diagnosis in patient 1. At consultation, best-corrected visual acuity was OD 20/25 + 2 and OS 20/25-2. Profound cavitary disc anomaly was noted bilaterally, with temporal RPE changes on color fundus photographs (Fig. 3a and b) consistent with resolved and possibly recurrent serous fluid accumulation within the macula. SD-OCT of the optic disc shows significantly deep cavitary anomaly, bilaterally. (Fig. 4a and b). Central choroidal thickness (Fig. 4c and d) was 657.6μm (OD) and 398.8μm (OS). RPE changes on FAF were consistent with recurrent, resolved fluid within the macula as is commonly seen in central serous chorioretinopathy. This finding was evident on bilateral FAF (Fig. 3c and d). Given the lack of active fluid in patient 2 at the time of consultation, FA was not performed.

Follow-up

Both patients were encouraged to follow up in the retina clinic in 3–6 months. Genetic counselling for other family members was encouraged.

Genomic sequencing

Whole genome sequencing was performed as a trio with the adult son, his mother and father. Genetic testing was performed by Variantyx (Framingham, Massachusetts, USA), a CLIA-certified laboratory. Testing identified a 7.53 kb triplication on chromosome 12q13.2 in the mother and son, but not the unaffected father. Using genomic consensus NC_00012.12 the triplication spanning g.55844723_55852252 upstream of MMP19. This region contains an enhancer of MMP19 expression and is triplicated on one copy of chromosome 12 while the other copy of chromosome 12 harbored a single copy of this region. This pathogenic variant was originally described by Hazelwood et al. in a large family and segregated with the CODA phenotype in 17 affected family members as well as an affected unrelated individual with CODA.3 Functional studies with this triplication identified this triplication increased MMP19 expression 8 fold. This triplication was also identified by Hwang et al.15 in 2019 in additional patients with CODA. This variant meets American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMGG) criteria for being classified as pathogenic (PS3, PP1).16

Unrelated to this condition, both the mother and son have a second genetic condition caused by a pathogenic variant in SDHC, c.397C > T (p.Arg133Ter), located on chromosome 1. This is associated with Hereditary Paraganglioma-Pheochromocytoma Syndrome.

Discussion

This case series describes two related patients with pathogenic triplication on chromosome 12q13.2 in MMP19, resulting in bilateral CODA. Patient 1 was initially referred for evaluation of decreased vision in the context of optic disc anomaly and SRF. He presented with his mother (patient 2), who endorsed a history of optic pits and subretinal fluid. On examination both patients had deep cavitary anomalies of both optic discs thus prompting genetic evaluation for inherited CODA.

Optic pit maculopathy was first described in 1980, presenting with IRF accumulation with a schisis-like pattern of retinal edema.10 Notably, in cases of optic nerve pits, the IRF seems to emanate from the disc anomaly itself.10 The IRF then percolates into the subretinal space and can lead to serous sensory retinal detachments (SSRD). A review of 75 eyes with congenital optic pits, found 50 % developed serous maculopathy, however, it is unclear whether the heterogenous congenital CODA genotype has the same prevalence of serous maculopathy.7

The pathogenesis of maculopathy in cavitary disc anomalies is unclear. It is hypothesized that the defects in the sclera or lamina cribrosa permits irregular communication between intraocular and extraocular spaces.8,17,18 Fluctuating pressure gradients between the spaces are thought to induce migration of cerebrospinal fluid and/or liquid vitreous into the adjacent retinal tissue.8,17 One study of 11 eyes with cavitary disc maculopathy used juxtapapillary laser photocoagulation with vitrectomy and gas tamponade to create an intraretinal barrier to fluid migration.18 In support of the pressure gradient hypothesis, all 11 patients had complete resolution of macular fluid and only one patient had recurrence of macular fluid.18

The cases presented herein both have congenital CODA and features of serous maculopathy; patient 1 with active SRF, and patient 2 with evidence of recurrent or chronic, now resolved, SRF. These cases demonstrate interesting phenotypes of CODA-associated serous maculopathy. In patient 1, the OCT lacks the typical “schisis-like” IRF pattern seen in optic pit maculopathy, in fact there is little, if any, IRF visible on OCT. Inamura et al. reports accumulation of SRF directly from the optic disc anomaly is rare in the absence of IRF accumulation.14 In patient 2, the SRF had obviously resolved at the time of evaluation. She reported the “fluid” was present during her pregnancy and there is evidence of resolved edema on color fundus photography and FAF.

Notably, both patients do not present with “typical” maculopathy associated with cavitary disc anomalies. In patient 1, a young male with self-reported “type A” personality, high stress, and recent steroid exposure, the symmetrical sub-foveal SRF with simultaneous central choroidal thickness >300μm could be attributed to central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR). In patient 2, the resolved SRF and increased central choroidal thickness of 657.6μm (OD) and 398.8μm (OS) could also be attributed to CSCR. Cavitary optic disc maculopathy would not typically fluctuate with pregnancy and is unlikely to resolve without intervention.19

CSCR is an idiopathic disease process typically seen in young people involving choroidal stroma thickening, choroidal vascular dilation, retinal pigment epithelial abnormalities and SRF accumulation leading to serous retinal detachment or retinal pigment epithelium detachments.20, 21, 22 CSCR is an idiopathic condition that has been associated with steroid use,23,24 increased stress/sympathetic activity25 and pregnancy,19,26 however, its exact mechanism is not fully understood.

Patient 1 has risk factors for CSCR, however, the FA was more indicative of CODA associated serous maculopathy, in CSCR, pooling of dye within the SRF collection would be expected.20 However, the gross appearance of the OCT is typical of CSCR; absence of IRF, thick choroid, and no apparent connection of SRF with cavitary optic disc. Patient 2 had evidence of resolved SRF which could have been CSCR or CODA associated serous maculopathy. Notably, on SD-OCT her choroid did appear thickened and the OCT shows signs of RPE changes (also visible on the near-infrared reflectance imaging).

We believe these cases may demonstrate an overlapping phenotype between CSCR and CODA-associated serous maculopathy in patients with the MMP19 genotype. In both cases, it is not clear if the subretinal fluid originated from CSCR or from the cavitation itself, and the distinct clinical features could suggest CODA-associated serous maculopathy may be associated with multiple mechanisms.

This study was limited by the retrospective nature, small sample size, and lack of a control group. Future research should focus on studying the relationship between the MMP19 genotype and the serous maculopathy phenotype described in these cases.
