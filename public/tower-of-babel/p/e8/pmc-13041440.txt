FBR-NDs: a ferroptosis-inducing nanocomplex for targeted breast cancer therapy via immune modulation and redox-responsive drug delivery
Authors: Jinjin Chen, Liang Ma, Ming Yin, Jian Sun, Yunhao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to develop and characterize a novel ferroptosis-inducing nanocomplex, FBR-NDs (Fe³⁺-BMS-1-R848 Nanodrugs), and to evaluate its anti-tumor effects in breast cancer cells through induction of ferroptosis and modulation of immune responses.MethodsFBR-NDs were synthesized by nanoprecipitation, followed by characterization using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy to confirm their structure and composition. Drug loading, release, and redox-responsive properties were assessed using ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy, and the drug release profile was evaluated under acidic pH and glutathione (GSH) conditions. In vitro growth inhibition of breast cancer cells (BT549 and MDA-MB-231) was assessed by cell viability, colony formation, and apoptosis assays. Ferroptosis was evaluated by measuring reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial morphology via electron microscopy, and expression of ferroptosis-related markers. In vivo anti-tumor efficacy was assessed in a 4T1 breast cancer mouse model, and immune responses were evaluated by flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays.ResultsFBR-NDs demonstrated high drug loading efficiency and exhibited redox-responsive release behavior under acidic pH and GSH conditions. In vitro, FBR-NDs significantly inhibited breast cancer cell proliferation and induced ferroptosis, as evidenced by elevated ROS levels, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial damage. In vivo, FBR-NDs significantly reduced tumor growth, enhanced apoptosis, and induced ferroptosis in tumor tissues. Additionally, FBR-NDs treatment increased the infiltration of immune cells, including dendritic cells (DCs) and M1 macrophages, and promoted T-cell activation. Immunogenic cell death was observed, as evidenced by increased levels of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and calreticulin in tumor tissues.ConclusionFBR-NDs are a promising therapeutic strategy for breast cancer, combining ferroptosis induction with immune modulation. The ability to induce ferroptosis, enhance immune responses, and promote anti-tumor immunity makes FBR-NDs a potential candidate for cancer immunotherapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02247-2.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women, with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounting for approximately 15% to 20% of all breast cancer cases [1, 2]. The 5-year survival rate for TNBC remains below 30% [3, 4]. In recent years, with the deepening of research into tumor molecular mechanisms, immune checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for TNBC [5–7]. Although TNBC is characterized by a high tumor mutation burden, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, and substantial infiltration of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors as monotherapy remains limited, with objective response rates ranging from 5% in unselected populations to 23% in PD-L1-positive first-line patients [8–11]. A critical challenge in improving immune therapy outcomes lies in effectively activating antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to recognize tumor antigens, which is essential for initiating an immune response [12, 13]. For patients to mount a successful anti-tumor immune response, APCs must recognize tumor-specific antigens and present them to T lymphocytes, thereby activating immune responses and generating immune memory [14, 15]. However, tumor cells often “hide” antigens, reducing their immunogenicity and making this initial recognition process difficult to trigger [16]. Furthermore, the accumulation of immune-suppressive cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) impedes the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells into tumor sites, thereby promoting immune evasion and facilitating tumor progression [17]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies to overcome the low immunogenicity of tumor cells and reverse the immunosuppressive TME.

In recent years, ferroptosis has been shown to be closely associated with cancer immunology [18]. Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, leading to membrane rupture [19]. First proposed by Dixon in 2012, ferroptosis has garnered significant attention due to its distinct mechanism from classical cell death modalities such as apoptosis, autophagy, and necrosis [20]. This pathway avoids the acquired or intrinsic resistance mechanisms that tumors often develop in response to conventional chemotherapy, making it an attractive target for therapeutic development [21]. Current research indicates that ferroptosis can be triggered through three main metabolic pathways: iron metabolism, lipid metabolism, and glutathione metabolism [22, 23]. The glutathione pathway primarily involves the regulation of reduced glutathione (GSH) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). GPX4 plays a critical role in defending against ferroptosis [24]. Additionally, iron metabolism plays an instrumental role in the ferroptotic process [25]. Specifically, iron is reduced to ferrous ions upon entering the cell, accumulating to form an unstable iron pool [26]. The free ferrous ions participate in the Fenton reaction, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), which accumulate and induce lipid peroxidation, ultimately leading to cellular dysfunction and death [27]. Furthermore, tumor cells undergoing ferroptosis release immunostimulatory signals along with damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These factors promote the maturation of dendritic cells, improve antigen presentation, and facilitate the recruitment and activation of CD8⁺ T cells [28]. In addition, ferroptotic cells can secrete chemokines and lipid mediators while simultaneously decreasing the production of immunosuppressive cytokines. This shift helps establish an immune-stimulatory microenvironment that supports CD8⁺ T cell infiltration [29]. Therefore, inducing ferroptosis also represents a promising strategy to prime immune response in cancer management. With the growing understanding of ferroptosis and the discovery of potent inducers of this process, leveraging ferroptosis as a therapeutic mechanism for cancer treatment holds great potential [30].

Despite these advantages, ferroptosis-inducing therapies face several important limitations that hinder their clinical application. Many small-molecule ferroptosis inducers suffer from poor water solubility, low bioavailability, off-target toxicity, and insufficient tumor selectivity, which can lead to systemic side effects or suboptimal efficacy in vivo [31]. Moreover, ferroptosis induction can exert context-dependent effects within the tumor microenvironment: while it often promotes immunogenic cell death and enhances anti-tumor immunity, excessive or dysregulated ferroptosis may impair the function of immune effector cells, promote inflammation that favors tumor progression in certain settings, or contribute to therapy resistance due to tumor heterogeneity and adaptive responses [29, 32, 33]. The lack of reliable, specific biomarkers for ferroptosis susceptibility further complicates patient selection and monitoring [34]. These challenges underscore the need for advanced delivery systems that improve targeting, control release, minimize off-target effects, and synergistically combine ferroptosis induction with immunomodulation to maximize therapeutic benefit while mitigating risks. Recent advancements in nanoparticle-based drug delivery for cancer have emphasized multifunctional, stimuli-responsive systems to enhance precision and efficacy [35–37]. Key trends include active targeting, redox/pH-sensitive release mechanisms, and integration of immunotherapy agents or theranostic functions [38]. These platforms improve bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs, overcome multidrug resistance, and modulate the immunosuppressive TME [39]. Despite progress, clinical translation faces major bottlenecks, such as heterogeneous enhanced permeability and retention effect leading to inconsistent tumor accumulation, rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, and stringent regulatory requirements for long-term safety [40, 41]. Nanoparticles also interact dynamically with the immune system. They can serve as adjuvants to stimulate innate immunity, promote immunogenic cell death (ICD), and drive pro-inflammatory shifts [35]. However, unintended pro-inflammatory or immunosuppressive effects must be carefully managed to avoid adverse outcomes [42].

To address these limitations and gaps, the present study hypothesizes that a multifunctional iron-based nanodrug combining ferroptosis induction with targeted immunomodulation (via PD-1/PD-L1 blockade and TLR7/8 agonism) can overcome the shortcomings of standalone ferroptosis inducers or immunotherapy by simultaneously triggering immunogenic ferroptotic cell death, enhancing dendritic cell activation, reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME, and boosting durable anti-tumor immunity in breast cancer.

In response to this, we have developed a strategy that combines iron-based nanoparticles with GPX4 inhibitors to induce ferroptosis in breast cancer cells. The iron-based nanocomposite induces ferroptosis by generating ROS, inhibiting GPX4 activity, and promoting the accumulation of lipid peroxides (LPO). This approach represents a promising opportunity to enhance the efficacy of ferroptosis-based cancer therapies. In this study, we employed a supramolecular self-assembly strategy to construct a multifunctional nanocomplex, Fe³⁺-BMS-1-R848 Nanodrugs, designated as FBR-NDs. This nanotechnology platform serves as a precise delivery vehicle that responds to the TME. Upon internalization by tumor cells, the nanocomplex disassembles to release its cargo: iron ions Fe3+ and immunomodulators. The released iron acts as the primary driver of cytotoxicity by fueling the Fenton reaction, generating lethal levels of ROS to trigger ferroptosis, which subsequently induces ICD. The goals are to achieve: (1) favorable biocompatibility and responsiveness to the tumor microenvironment; (2) depletion of GSH by Fe3+ to induce ROS generation, synergizing with BMS-1 to enhance ferroptosis; (3) activation of dendritic cell (DC) maturation by introducing the immune adjuvant R848; and (4) the creation of a ferroptosis-immune therapy cascade to enhance anti-tumor efficacy.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of FBR-NDs

FBR-NDs were synthesized using a nanoprecipitation method. In this process, Fe³⁺ (from FeCl₃), BMS-1 [PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor, purity 98%, Cat. No. HY-19991, MedChemExpress (MCE)], and R848 (Resiquimod, purity 98%, Cat. No. HY-13740, MCE) were mixed in appropriate molar ratios. FeCl₃ was dissolved in water to obtain a light-yellow solution, followed by the addition of BMS-1 and R848. FBR-NDs were synthesized via a one-step self-assembly nanoprecipitation method FeCl3 (Iron (III) chloride) served as the iron source and coordination center, while BMS-1 (PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor) and R848 (TLR7/8 agonist) served as the therapeutic cargo. Briefly, 10 mg of FeCl3 was dissolved in 5 mL of deionized water to obtain a light-yellow aqueous solution. Separately, BMS-1 (2 mg) and R848 (2 mg) were prepared (typically in a water-miscible organic solvent like DMSO and added dropwise to the iron solution. The mixture was magnetically stirred at room temperature for 30 min to facilitate the coordination and self-assembly of the nanoparticles. During this process, the solution color visibly shifted from the initial light yellow to an orange-yellow, indicating the successful formation of the nanocomplex. The resulting FBR-NDs were purified by centrifugation (4,000 g for 15 min) to remove unencapsulated drugs and free ions. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with deionized water. The final purified nanoparticles were resuspended in PBS and stored at 4℃ for further characterization and application. To serve as experimental controls, the binary nanocomplexes Fe3+-R848 and Fe3+-BMS-1 were synthesized using the same nanoprecipitation protocol. Briefly, FeCl3 solution was mixed with either R848 or BMS-1 solution alone, under identical stirring and temperature conditions. Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis confirmed that these binary mixtures successfully self-assembled into stable nanoparticles with hydrodynamic diameters comparable to FBR-NDs, ensuring the validity of the comparative efficacy studies.

Characterization of FBR-NDs

Morphological and size analysis of FBR-NDs was performed using TEM. The nanoparticles were drop-cast onto carbon-coated copper grids, stained with uranyl acetate, and imaged using a Hitachi H-7650 TEM operating at 100 kV. The hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) of FBR-NDs were determined using DLS with a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was employed to analyze the elemental composition of FBR-NDs. The oxidation states of Fe and functional groups were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and the amorphous structure of the nanoparticles was confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD). The presence of functional groups on the surface of FBR-NDs was further validated using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy.

Quantification of iron release by ICP-OES

To validate the iron release profiles, 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn from the release medium at predetermined time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h). The samples were digested with 65% nitric acid (HNO3) at 90℃ for 2 h to ensure complete ionization. The iron concentration in each sample was determined using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Agilent 5110).

Drug loading and release studies

To assess the loading efficiency of BMS-1 and R848, FBR-NDs were incubated with excess amounts of each drug for 24 h. Unbound drugs were removed via centrifugal filtration, and the concentration of drugs in the supernatant was quantified using ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy. The release behavior of BMS-1 and R848 from FBR-NDs was evaluated under acidic (pH 5.6) and neutral (pH 7.4) conditions. For GSH-mediated drug release, the release profiles were measured in the presence of 2 mM and 10 mM GSH. To quantify Fe²⁺ release, FBR-NDs were incubated with GSH, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured. The stability of FBR-NDs was assessed by monitoring size and PDI changes over a period of 10 days in water and PBS. Zeta potential measurements were conducted to evaluate the stability and surface charge of FBR-NDs. Finally, dissociation studies were carried out in the presence of ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA), Tween 80, urea, and NaCl to investigate the dissociation and stability of FBR-NDs.

In vitro growth inhibition assay

The human TNBC cell lines BT549 (VCH00164) and MDA-MB-231 (VCH00019), and the murine mammary carcinoma cell line 4T1 (VCM00006), were obtained from ViCell Biotechnology (Zhengzhou, China). All cells underwent short tandem repeat authentication and were routinely confirmed free of mycoplasma contamination. BT549 and MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute − 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 for cell viability assays, in 6-well plates at 1 × 103 for colony formation assays. After 24 h, the cells were treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), R848, BMS-1, R848 + BMS-1, Fe2+-R848, Fe2+-BMS-1, or FBR-NDs for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay kit (C0009S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). For colony formation, the cells were stained with crystal violet (SangonBiotech, Shanghai, China), and the number of colonies was counted. DNA replication capacity was assessed by 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) labeling assays (C0071S, Beyotime, Guangzhou, China), and Caspase-3/7 activity was measured using the Caspase-Glo 3/7 Assay kit (Promega, USA). Senescence was determined by β-galactosidase staining (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US A), and apoptosis was evaluated using Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assays (C1086, Beyotime). Flow cytometry was used to analyze apoptosis and cell cycle progression.

Ferroptosis induction and analysis

To evaluate ferroptosis, the reduced glutathione/oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (oxidized/reduced forms) (NADP+/NADPH) ratios were measured using specific assay kits, following the manufacturer’s protocol. ROS production was detected using the 2′,7′-Dichlorofluorescin Diacetate (DCFDA) assay (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), and lipid peroxidation was assessed by the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific. TEM analysis was performed to assess cellular morphology, focusing on mitochondrial changes such as swelling, membrane rupture, and vesicular structures associated with ferroptosis.

In vivo tumor growth inhibition assays

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China)] and housed under specific pathogen-freeconditions with free access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Yancheng City (Approval No. XIUCC-2022-849) and conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. In vivo tumor growth inhibition was evaluated using a C57BL/6 mouse model. 4T1 breast cancer cells (1 × 10⁶ cells) were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of mice. Tumors were allowed to grow to a size of 100–150 mm³, at which point the mice were randomly assigned to one of the seven treatment groups. Mice were treated with PBS, R848, BMS-1, R848 + BMS-1, Fe³+-R848, Fe³+-BMS-1, or FBR-NDs (10 mg/kg) every two days for six doses. Tumor size and mouse body weight were monitored throughout the study. At the endpoint (Day 35), tumors were collected for histological analysis. Ki67 and cleaved-caspase-3 expression were assessed by immunohistochemistry. Ferroptosis was evaluated by detecting the lipid peroxidation marker 4-HNE and analyzing ferroptosis-related proteins (GPX4 and TLR4) in tumor tissue sections.

Hemolysis assays

Fresh blood was collected from C57BL/6 mice in heparinized tubes. Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated by centrifugation (400 g, 10 min) and washed three times with PBS. The RBCs were resuspended in PBS (2% v/v). FBR-NDs were added to the RBC suspension to achieve final concentrations of 10, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL. PBS and distilled water were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h, followed by centrifugation at 1000g for 5 min. The absorbance of the supernatant (hemoglobin release) was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader.

Immune cell infiltration and immune response analysis

To assess immune cell infiltration, flow cytometry was used to analyze the proportion of activated DCs (CD80+), M1 (CD11b+CD86+), and M2 (CD11b+CD206+) macrophages in the spleens and tumors of treated mice. For immunohistochemical analysis, tumor sections were stained with antibodies against CD80, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, M1 macrophage marker), and Arginase 1 (Arg1, M2 macrophage marker). T-cell infiltration was analyzed by flow cytometry to assess the proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to measure the secretion of DC-related cytokines such as interleukin (IL-12), C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 10 (CXCL10), and IL-23 from the spleen and tumor tissues.

ICD assessment

To evaluate ICD, tumor tissues were analyzed for the expression of DAMPs, including high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and calreticulin (CRT), by immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry. The intensity of staining for these markers was quantified to assess the extent of ICD. The expression levels of immune checkpoint molecules PD-L1 and GAL9 were also analyzed by immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry in tumor sections. The effects of FBR-NDs on T-cell activation were further examined by assessing the frequency of CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues and correlating this with ferroptosis markers.

Immunohistochemistry was performed to assess the expression of various markers in tumor tissues, including Ki67 (cell proliferation), Cleaved-Caspase-3 (apoptosis), 4-HNE (lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis), CD80 (activated DCs), iNOS (M1 macrophage marker), Arg1 (M2 macrophage marker), and immune checkpoint markers PD-L1 and GAL9. Tumor tissues were harvested from mice at the study endpoint (Day 35), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4℃, and then embedded in paraffin. Tissue Sect. (5 μm) were cut using a microtome and placed on glass slides.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was performed to assess the infiltration and activation of immune cells, including DCs, macrophages, and T cells, in tumor tissues and spleens. Tumor tissues and spleens were harvested from mice at the study endpoint (Day 35), immediately processed, and single-cell suspensions were prepared using mechanical dissociation and enzymatic digestion with collagenase IV (1 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and DNase I (100 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C. The resulting cell suspensions were passed through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove debris. For surface marker staining, the cell suspensions were resuspended in PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated with Fc receptor blocking antibody (anti-CD16/32, 1:100, BioLegend) for 15 min at 4 °C to prevent nonspecific antibody binding. Cells were then stained with specific antibodies for the identification of immune cell subsets: CD80 (anti-CD80, 1:100, BioLegend) for activated DCs, CD86 (anti-CD86, 1:100, BioLegend) for M1 macrophages, CD206 (anti-CD206, 1:100, BioLegend) for M2 macrophages, and CD4 (anti-CD4, 1:100, BioLegend) and CD8 (anti-CD8, 1:100, BioLegend) for T cells. For intracellular staining of cytokines or markers, cells were first stained for surface markers, followed by fixation and permeabilization using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular staining for interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (anti-IFN-γ, 1:100, BioLegend) was performed after permeabilization. Staining was performed for 30 min at 4℃ in the dark. After staining, cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 300 µL of PBS for flow cytometric analysis. Flow cytometry was performed using a BD LSRFortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences), and at least 100,000 events were acquired per sample. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10 software (TreeStar). The gating strategy for immune cell subsets was as follows: DCs were identified as CD80+ cells, M1 macrophages as CD11b+CD86+, M2 macrophages as CD11b+CD206+, CD4+ T cells as CD4+ cells, and CD8+ T cells as CD8+ cells. The proportions of each immune cell subset were quantified based on the percentage of the respective positive cells among the total viable cell population. Data are presented as the percentage of positive cells in the tumor or spleen tissues relative to the total cell population.

Immunohistochemistry

For antigen retrieval, slides were incubated in a citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and heated in a microwave for 10 min to unmask epitopes. After cooling, the sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum (for CD80, PD-L1, and GAL9) or 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (for iNOS, Arg1, and Cleaved-Caspase-3) for 30 min at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies: anti-Ki67 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-Cleaved-Caspase-3 (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-4-HNE (1:100, Abcam), anti-CD80 (1:200, BioLegend), anti-iNOS (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-Arg1 (1:200, Abcam), anti-PD-L1 (1:200, BioLegend), and anti-GAL9 (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) overnight at 4 °C. After washing the sections with PBS, the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1,000) was applied for 1 h at room temperature. The color reaction was developed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine solution (Dako), and the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min. The slides were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with coverslips. The stained slides were analyzed under a light microscope (Leica DM4000B). For quantification, at least five fields of view were randomly selected per section, and the positive staining intensity for each marker was evaluated. For cell markers like Ki67 and Cleaved-Caspase-3, the percentage of positive cells was calculated. For lipid peroxidation and immune cell markers, the intensity of staining was scored on a scale of 0–3 (0 = no staining, 1 = weak staining, 2 = moderate staining, 3 = strong staining) by two independent researchers, with the mean score used for statistical analysis.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was conducted to assess the infiltration and activation of these immune cells in tumor tissues. Tumor tissues were harvested from mice at the endpoint of the study (Day 35), fixed in 4%PFA for 24 h at 4°C, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned into 5 µm slices using a microtome and mounted on glass slides. For antigen retrieval, the slides were placed in a citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and heated in a microwave for 10 min to unmask the target epitopes. After cooling at room temperature for 20 min, the slides were incubated in blocking buffer containing 5% normal goat serum (for both CD4 and CD8 antibodies) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1 h at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. Following blocking, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies targeting CD4 (anti-CD4, 1:200, BioLegend) or CD8 (anti-CD8, 1:200, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (for CD4, 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Alexa Fluor 594 (for CD8, 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark to prevent photobleaching. To counterstain the cell nuclei, the slides were incubated with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, 1:1,000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature. After final washes with PBS, the slides were mounted with antifade mounting medium (ProLong Gold, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and coverslipped. The slides were analyzed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6000B) equipped with appropriate filters for Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 594. The infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells into the tumor tissues was quantified by counting the number of positive cells in five random fields per section at 20× magnification. The percentage of positively stained cells was calculated by dividing the number of CD4+ or CD8+ cells by the total number of DAPI-positive cells in each field.

Results

Synthesis, morphology, and structural characterization of FBR-NDs

The multifunctional nanocomplex FBR-NDs was synthesized via a one-time nanoprecipitation method by mixing Fe3+, BMS-1, and R848 at optimized molar ratios. As shown in Fig. 1a, by comparing the changes in the color of the solution, a clear observation can be made that after self-assembly, the color of the solution deepens from the light yellow of FeCl₃ to orange-yellow, which indirectly indicates the successful construction of the nanocomplex (Fig. 1a, b). TEM images (Fig. 1c) demonstrated a uniform spherical morphology of FBR-NDs. Statistical analysis of 100 particles from TEM images revealed an average core diameter of 32.5 ± 4.2 nm. This is slightly smaller than the hydrodynamic diameter (50.43 ± 21.8 nm) measured by DLS (Fig. 1d), a difference attributed to the hydration shell present in the aqueous state. Additionally, we imaged the binary complex Fe3+-BMS-1 (FB-NDs) as a structural control. The similarity in morphology between FB-NDs and FBR-NDs confirms that the encapsulation of R848 does not destabilize the nanocomplex assembly. Compared to base FB-NDs, FBR-NDs showed improved dispersion and reduced aggregation, confirming successful functionalization. TEM mapping (Fig. 1e) revealed the homogeneous distribution of C, N, O, and Fe within FBR-NDs. High-resolution XPS spectra (Fig. 1f-g) further validated the presence of Fe³⁺ oxidation states (Fe 2p peaks at ~ 710 eV and ~ 724 eV) and functional groups, including Fe-O bonds and organic C =O and COOH/OH groups, confirming the incorporation of FeCl₃, BMS-1, and R848.Compared to base FB-NDs, FBR-NDs showed improved dispersion and reduced aggregation, confirming successful functionalization. XRD analysis (Fig. 1h) showed a broad peak at 20–30°, indicating the amorphous structure of FBR-NDs. Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to verify the molecular interactions within the FBR-NDs (Fig. 1i). Compared to the spectra of free BMS-1 and R848, the spectrum of FBR-NDs revealed a new characteristic absorption peak at ~ 580 cm− 1, corresponding to the Fe-O stretching vibration, which indicates the successful coordination of iron ions with the oxygen-containing functional groups of the cargo molecules. Furthermore, the characteristic C = O stretching peak (originally at ~ 1650 cm− 1 in the free drugs) exhibited a redshift to ~ 1635 cm− 1 in the nanocomplex. This shift suggests that the carbonyl oxygen atoms participate in the coordination with the Fe3+ center. Similarly, the broadening of the absorption band around 3400 cm− 1 (O-H/N-H stretching) further confirms the formation of a supramolecular coordination network involving hydrogen bonding and metal-ligand interactions, ensuring the structural stability of the FBR-NDs.

Drug loading, release, and redox-responsive properties

UV-Vis spectra (Fig. 2a) confirmed the successful loading of BMS-1 and R848, showing strong optical absorbance. The release profiles (Fig. 2b) demonstrated pH-responsive behavior, where ~ 90% of BMS-1 and R848 were released under acidic conditions (pH 5.6) within 10 h, while slower release occurred at neutral pH (pH 7.4). GSH-responsive release (Fig. 2c) showed faster drug release at 2 mM GSH compared to 10 mM, indicating GSH-mediated regulation. The GSH-responsive release of iron was initially monitored by colorimetric assay and further validated using ICP-OES. As shown in Fig. 2d, the cumulative iron release measured by ICP-OES was highly consistent with the UV-Vis data, confirming that the iron cargo is released within 48 h under high-GSH conditions (10 mM), compared to negligible release in the absence of GSH. GSH-induced Fe²⁺ release was quantified (Fig. 2d), showing cumulative release increasing with GSH concentration (10 mM > 2 mM). CDT analysis (Fig. 2e) confirmed the production of Fe²⁺ and ROS under GSH conditions, with a decline in absorbance reflecting Fe³⁺ reduction via the Fenton reaction. FBR-NDs remained stable over 10 days in PBS and 10% FBS, maintaining a size of 30–40 nm and low PDI (~ 0.2) (Fig. 2f). Zeta potential measurements confirmed moderate surface charge contributing to stability. Dissociation studies (Fig. 2g) showed that FBR-NDs were highly sensitive to EDTA, which caused effective dissociation due to Fe chelation. Tween 80, urea, and NaCl had minimal effects, indicating robust stability under mild conditions. Furthermore, the hemocompatibility of FBR-NDs was evaluated using a hemolysis assay. As shown in Fig. S1, the hemolysis rate remained below 5% across all tested concentrations (up to 200 µg/mL), indicating that FBR-NDs do not cause significant damage to red blood cells and are safe for blood circulation. As presented in Fig. S2, no noticeable pathological abnormalities, inflammatory infiltration, or tissue damage were observed in the FBR-NDs-treated mice.

In vitro growth inhibition of breast cancer cells by FBR-NDs

To further investigate the impact of FBR-NDs on breast cancer cell growth, BT549 and MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with PBS, R848, BMS-1, R848 + BMS-1, Fe3+-R848, Fe3+-BMS-1, and FBR-NDs. We observed that R848 or BMS-1 alone, or in combination, did not significantly affect cell viability and replication, likely due to their roles as TLR7/8 agonists and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, which might not exert a substantial effect on breast cancer cells by themselves. However, after the addition of Fe3+, a pronounced inhibitory effect on cell growth was evident, manifesting as reduced cell viability (Fig. 3a), a significant decrease in colony formation (Fig. 3b), and a marked reduction in DNA replication capacity (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, treatment led to a notable increase in Caspase-3/7 activity (Fig. 3d). To further verify the induction of senescence, we performed SA-$\beta$-gal staining (Fig. 3e). Cells in the PBS and single-drug control groups maintained their normal spindle-shaped morphology with negligible staining. In contrast, cells treated with FBR-NDs exhibited a distinct senescence-associated phenotype: they appeared significantly enlarged and flattened with an irregular shape. Crucially, these cells showed intense blue-green cytoplasmic staining, indicating high levels of senescence-associated $\beta$-galactosidase activity. This morphological transition is consistent with the cell cycle arrest data, confirming that FBR-NDs effectively drive tumor cells into a senescent state. Since Caspase-3/7 is a well-established marker for apoptosis, we further assessed apoptosis levels using TUNEL staining. We found that Fe3+-R848 and Fe3+-BMS-1 treatment notably increased the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells, with FBR-NDs exhibiting the most significant promotion of apoptosis (Fig. 3f).

FBR-NDs induce ferroptosis in breast cancer cells

Given that FBR-NDs contain Fe3+, which is known to play a role in iron metabolism and is typically associated with ferroptosis, we hypothesized that the growth inhibition observed in breast cancer cells might be due to the exacerbation of ferroptosis. To test this, we first measured the GSH/GSSG and NADP+/NADPH ratios in the treated cells. After treatment with Fe3+-R848, Fe3+-BMS-1, and FBR-NDs, we observed a significant decrease in both the GSH/GSSG and NADP+/NADPH ratios, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the FBR-NDs-treated cells (Fig. 4a-b), indicating that FBR-NDs effectively suppressed GSH metabolism, thereby promoting oxidative stress. Next, we assessed reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and lipid peroxidation. We found a significant increase in ROS and lipid peroxidation levels in BT549 and MDA-MB-231 cells treated with Fe3+, with FBR-NDs inducing the most substantial elevation in both ROS (Fig. 4c). Lipid peroxidation, a hallmark of ferroptosis, was assessed using the C11-BODIPY 581/591 probe (Fig. 4d). In the PBS and drug-only groups (R848, BMS-1, R + B), cells exhibited predominantly red fluorescence, indicating intact non-oxidized membranes. This lack of signal shift confirms that without an exogenous iron source, oxidative stress remains insufficient to drive ferroptosis. In sharp contrast, cells treated with iron-containing formulations (Fe-R, Fe-B, and FBR-NDs) displayed a robust emergence of green fluorescence. This drastic change is attributed to the intracellular release of iron, which catalyzes the Fenton reaction to generate hydroxyl radicals, thereby driving the widespread oxidation of membrane lipids. Notably, the FBR-NDs group showed the strongest green signal, consistent with its extraordinary ability to deplete GSH and amplify oxidative stress. Additionally, TEM analysis revealed mitochondrial swelling, blurred inner membrane structure, increased membrane folding, and rupture of the outer mitochondrial membrane in cells treated with Fe3+ and FBR-NDs. We also observed vesicular structures in the cell membrane and endoplasmic reticulum, indicative of ferroptosis (Fig. 4e). Further investigation of ferroptosis-related markers, including GPX4, TLR4, ACXL4, and FSP1, showed no significant change in marker expression following treatment with PBS, R848, BMS-1, or R848 + BMS-1. However, following Fe3+-R848, Fe3+-BMS-1, and FBR-NDs treatment, we observed a significant decrease in GPX4 and FSP1 levels, alongside a marked increase in TLR4 and ACXL4 expression, with FBR-NDs showing the most potent promotion of ferroptosis (Fig. 4f, Fig. S3).

In vivo inhibition of tumor growth by FBR-NDs

To further confirm the effect of the synthesized materials on breast cancer cell growth in vivo, we inoculated 4T1 breast cancer cells into the right flanks of C57BL/6 mice. Once the tumors reached 100–150 mm³, the mice were randomly divided into seven groups and treated for six doses, with weight and tumor size measured every other day. By the study endpoint (Day 35), the tumor growth rate in all groups was significantly reduced. The combination of Fe3+-R848 and Fe3+-BMS-1 showed more significant tumor growth inhibition compared to single treatments with R848 or BMS-1. Notably, FBR-NDs exhibited more pronounced anti-tumor effects than the R848 + BMS-1 combination (Fig. 5a-d). We then assessed the expression levels of Ki67 and Cleaved-Caspase-3 in tumor tissues as markers of cell proliferation and apoptosis. FBR-NDs-treated tumors displayed a significant reduction in Ki67 positivity, while Cleaved-Caspase-3 positivity increased (Fig. 5e, f). Given the ferroptosis-promoting effects of FBR-NDs, we also investigated the expression of 4-HNE, a marker of lipid peroxidation. The 4-HNE staining intensity was highest in FBR-NDs-treated tumors, indicating substantial induction of ferroptosis (Fig. 5g). Further analysis of ferroptosis-related markers, such as GPX4 and TLR4, revealed that R848, BMS-1, or R848 + BMS-1 treatments had negligible effects on ferroptosis. However, Fe3+-R848, Fe3+-BMS-1, and FBR-NDs treatments significantly decreased GPX4 expression while increasing TLR4 expression (Fig. 5h, i). Furthermore, we evaluated the impact of FBR-NDs on the overall survival of tumor-bearing mice over an extended observation period of 60 days. As shown in (Fig. 5j), mice in the PBS and single-agent groups exhibited rapid mortality due to excessive tumor burden. In contrast, the FBR-NDs treatment group showed a significantly prolonged lifespan (P < 0.0001), with a median survival time extending beyond 50 days. This survival advantage underscores the therapeutic potential of the synergistic ferroptosis-immunotherapy strategy.

FBR-NDs promote DC and macrophage infiltration

Ferroptosis is often associated with oxidative stress and immune responses, which can lead to the secretion of various immune mediators such as cytokines, chemokines, and lipid mediators, thereby activating immune cell infiltration. Notably, DCs can be attracted to the tumor microenvironment by 4-HNE, a product of ferroptosis. We first assessed the proportion of activated DCs (CD80+) in the spleens and tumors of treated mice using flow cytometry. After FBR-NDs treatment, the proportion of activated DCs in both spleen and tumor tissues was significantly higher (Fig. 6a,b). Immunohistochemical staining of tumor tissues also revealed an increased number of CD80 + DCs in FBR-NDs-treated mice (Fig. 6c). Moreover, ELISA analysis showed that FBR-NDs treatment led to increased secretion of DC-related cytokines such as IL-12, CXCL10, and IL-23 (Fig. 6d).

FBR-NDs enhance T-cell activation and immunogenic cell death in breast cancer cells

Given the potential of FBR-NDs to activate T cells and modulate immune responses, we investigated the proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the tumor tissues. We observed that FBR-NDs treatment significantly increased the infiltration of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells compared to other treatment groups (Fig. 7A-B), with similar results obtained from immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 7C-D). Moreover, we assessed the expression levels of immune evasion markers PD-L1 and GAL9 in tumor tissues. Following treatment, the levels of these immune checkpoint markers were significantly reduced, with the lowest expression observed in tumors exhibiting more pronounced ferroptosis (Fig. 7E-F). Given the increased activation of DCs and M1 macrophages, as well as enhanced T-cell infiltration, we hypothesize that FBR-NDs treatment enhances the immunogenicity of breast cancer cells. ICD has been shown to release danger signals, including HMGB1, CRT, and ATP, which break immune tolerance and promote DC-mediated antigen presentation, leading to CD8+ T cell activation and initiating anti-tumor immune responses. Accordingly, we observed significantly higher expression of HMGB1 and CRT in FBR-NDs-treated tumors, confirming that FBR-NDs promote the immunogenic cell death of breast cancer cells (Fig. 7G-H).

Discussion

In this study, we explored the potential of FBR-NDs as a therapeutic agent for breast cancer by inducing ferroptosis and modulating the immune microenvironment. Our results demonstrate that FBR-NDs not only effectively induce ferroptosis in breast cancer cells but also enhance immune responses through increased infiltration of DCs, macrophages, and T cells, leading to improved anti-tumor efficacy. The unique combination of ferroptosis induction and immune modulation presented by FBR-NDs is a promising approach for enhancing cancer therapy, particularly for overcoming the challenges posed by immune evasion and drug resistance in solid tumors, aligning with recent trends in cancer management that emphasize multifunctional theranostic nanoparticles for targeted delivery and immunotherapy [43, 44].

The structural characterization of FBR-NDs revealed their uniform spherical morphology and the successful functionalization with BMS-1 and R848. This is consistent with earlier reports on the use of nanoparticles for drug delivery and immune modulation. For example, Hsieh CH et al. developed iron oxide nanoparticles functionalized with immunostimulatory agents, showing that such nanoparticles could effectively modulate the immune system and induce ferroptosis [45]. Our findings extend these results by showing that FBR-NDs promote ferroptosis specifically through the Fenton reaction, where iron catalyzes the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), exacerbating oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in breast cancer cells. This mechanism aligns with the study of Bae C et al., who demonstrated that iron-based nanoparticles could induce ferroptosis by increasing ROS production and depleting cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms, such as glutathione (GSH), a key indicator of ferroptosis in our study as well [46].

Our study’s findings on ferroptosis induction are supported by the works of Feng Q and Wu Q et al., who showed that iron-based nanoparticles could trigger ferroptosis through the modulation of iron metabolism [47, 48]. In this study, the authors observed that the iron-induced ferroptosis significantly suppressed tumor growth and enhanced immune responses, a finding that closely mirrors our own results. We further showed that FBR-NDs induced a substantial reduction in GSH levels and NADPH/NADP+ ratios, corroborating the observations by Zhou C and Zhang W et al., who linked the depletion of GSH to the exacerbation of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in cancer cells [49, 50]. These findings provide strong evidence that FBR-NDs, by exploiting iron’s role in promoting oxidative damage, could be an effective strategy for targeting cancer cells via ferroptosis.

The combination of R848 (a TLR7/8 agonist) and BMS-1 (a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor) with FBR-NDs further potentiated tumor growth inhibition, suggesting a synergistic effect. This combination approach is consistent with studies exploring the use of immunotherapies alongside ferroptosis inducers. For instance, some investigated the combined use of immune checkpoint inhibitors and ferroptosis inducers, reporting a synergistic effect that enhanced both direct tumor cell death and anti-tumor immunity [51]. Similarly, the work of Zhang et al. demonstrated that the combination of immune checkpoint blockade with ferroptosis induction could promote immune responses, leading to improved anti-tumor effects in colorectal and pancreatic cancer models [52–54]. Our study builds on these findings by demonstrating that the combination of ferroptosis and immune modulation can enhance immune cell infiltration, particularly the activation of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, and reduce immune evasion markers such as PD-L1, an effect that has been observed in other immune-related studies as well [55–57].

Our in vivo data further supports these conclusions, as treatment with FBR-NDs significantly reduced tumor growth and enhanced immune responses compared to single treatments. The tumor tissue analysis revealed a marked decrease in Ki67 positivity, indicating reduced cell proliferation, and an increase in cleaved-caspase-3 positivity, suggesting enhanced apoptosis. This is in line with findings from previous studies, such as that of Caverzan MD and Zhao Y, who observed similar results in their study of iron nanoparticle-based therapies, which induced apoptosis and suppressed tumor growth in vivo [58, 59]. Additionally, the increased expression of 4-HNE, a marker of lipid peroxidation, in tumors treated with FBR-NDs further confirmed the induction of ferroptosis, a phenomenon observed in other ferroptosis-inducing therapies [60].

Importantly, our findings regarding immune cell infiltration align with previous studies linking ferroptosis with the modulation of the TME. For example, Feng Q et al. demonstrated that ferroptosis induction could lead to a shift in macrophage polarization from the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype to the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [47]. Our results similarly showed an increase in M1 macrophage infiltration and a decrease in M2 macrophages in tumors treated with FBR-NDs, suggesting that ferroptosis may play a crucial role in reprogramming the TME. Moreover, FBR-NDs significantly increased the secretion of DC-related cytokines such as IL-12 and CXCL10, further supporting the idea that ferroptosis can activate DCs and enhance anti-tumor immunity. This concept was also discussed by Wiernicki B and Xie L et al., who found that ferroptosis inducers could stimulate immune responses by promoting the secretion of cytokines that recruit immune cells to the tumor site [61, 62].

Our findings suggest that FBR-NDs drive anti-tumor immunity through a specific ‘Dual-Hit’ mechanism. First, the induction of ferroptosis acts as an endogenous vaccine. The accumulating lipid peroxides (4-HNE) and subsequent membrane rupture release DAMPs, specifically HMGB1 and CRT, into the tumor microenvironment. These DAMPs are known ligands for TLR4, expression of which was significantly elevated in our treated tumors. Crucially, this endogenous TLR4 stimulation synergizes with the exogenous delivery of R848. By simultaneously triggering TLR4 (via ferroptotic debris) and TLR7/8 (via R848), FBR-NDs induce a hyper-activated state in Dendritic Cells, leading to superior antigen presentation compared to single-pathway stimulation. Finally, the co-delivery of BMS-1 ensures that the infiltrated CD8+ T cells remain active by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitory axis, thereby sustaining the cytotoxic response.”

One of the novel aspects of our study is the demonstration that FBR-NDs promote immunogenic cell death (ICD), evidenced by the increased expression of HMGB1 and calreticulin (CRT) in tumor tissues [63]. ICD is an emerging concept in cancer immunotherapy, where cancer cells undergo cell death in a manner that stimulates immune responses. This mechanism has been explored in the context of several therapies, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and has been shown to enhance DC-mediated antigen presentation and T-cell priming [64, 65]. Our findings are consistent with this body of work, as we observed that FBR-NDs-treated tumors exhibited significantly higher expression of HMGB1 and CRT, which are hallmark signals of ICD. Furthermore, FBR-NDs treatment led to reduced expression of immune checkpoint markers, suggesting that FBR-NDs could overcome immune suppression and promote T-cell activation, a finding supported by similar studies in lung cancer models [66, 67]. To evaluate the distinct advantages of FBR-NDs, we compared our system with other recently reported ferroptosis-inducing nanoplatforms (Table S1). While traditional iron-based nanoparticles or small-molecule inducers effectively trigger lipid peroxidation, they often fail to mount a sustained anti-tumor immune response due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [68]. Even systems that combine ferroptosis with immune checkpoint blockade often lack a specific DC activator [69]. FBR-NDs overcome these limitations by incorporating R848, a potent TLR7/8 agonist. This ensures that the antigens released by ferroptosis are efficiently processed by activated DCs, while the simultaneous delivery of BMS-1 prevents T-cell exhaustion, offering a comprehensive ‘all-in-one’ immunotherapeutic strategy.

Ferroptosis induced by FBR-NDs may intersect with cellular senescence pathways through shared mechanisms (e.g., ROS accumulation, GPX4 modulation), potentially contributing to the observed reduction in Ki67 positivity via cell cycle arrest. Senescent cells can secrete pro-inflammatory factors that recruit immune cells and amplify ICD signals, aligning with our increased DC/macrophage/T-cell infiltration and cytokine secretion. While this crosstalk may enhance anti-tumor effects, chronic SASP can be context-dependent and potentially pro-tumorigenic in some settings [70]. Direct assessment of senescence markers would clarify this contribution in future work.

Despite the promising preclinical results, several limitations and challenges must be acknowledged. This study was conducted using in vitro cell lines and a subcutaneous mouse model of breast cancer, which may not fully recapitulate the complex heterogeneity, hypoxic regions, and metastatic potential of human tumors. Potential confounding factors include variability in nanoparticle biodistribution, off-target accumulation in normal tissues (particularly iron-sensitive organs such as the liver and spleen), and possible induction of ferroptosis in non-malignant cells, which could contribute to systemic toxicity. The combination of ferroptosis induction with TLR7/8 agonism and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, while synergistic in our model, may face acquired resistance mechanisms in vivo, such as compensatory immunosuppressive pathways or differential responses across tumor subtypes. Moreover, translating FBR-NDs to clinical settings presents significant hurdles common to ferroptosis-inducing nanomedicines, including poor long-term stability, scalability of GMP-grade production, precise tumor targeting in humans, potential immunogenicity of the nanoparticle platform, and the need for comprehensive pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic and toxicology studies. Addressing these issues through advanced delivery strategies, patient stratification based on ferroptosis sensitivity, and rigorous preclinical safety profiling will be essential for future clinical development. Furthermore, it should be acknowledged that this study primarily focused on elucidating the synergistic mechanism of FBR-NDs compared to their individual components. We did not perform a direct head-to-head comparison with clinical standard-of-care chemotherapeutics (e.g., paclitaxel or doxorubicin) or small-molecule ferroptosis inducers (e.g., Erastin). Future studies will be dedicated to rigorously evaluating the therapeutic index of FBR-NDs relative to standard chemotherapy regimens or ferroptosis inducers and investigating their potential in combination with established clinical protocols.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that FBR-NDs represent a promising therapeutic strategy for breast cancer, combining ferroptosis induction with immune modulation. The ability of FBR-NDs to promote ferroptosis while simultaneously enhancing immune cell infiltration and T-cell activation could offer significant advantages over traditional therapies, especially in overcoming immune evasion. The synergy observed between ferroptosis and immune checkpoint inhibition highlights the potential of FBR-NDs as a powerful tool in cancer immunotherapy. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the safety and efficacy of FBR-NDs in clinical settings and to explore their application in other cancer types.
