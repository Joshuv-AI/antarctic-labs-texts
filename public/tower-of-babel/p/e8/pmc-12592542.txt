Temporal integration window of sensory processing as a neuropsychological marker for clinical high risk of psychosis
Authors: Shuo Lin, Lu Tian, Wei-hao Tan, Li-na Wang, Wei-gang Pan, Li Li, Yi Liu, Chao-meng Liu, Han Wu, Wen Wang, Wen-qing Jin, Yi-lang Tang, Yan-ping Ren, Xin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Early identification of individuals with clinical high risk (CHR) for psychosis relies heavily on subjective assessments, necessitating the exploration of objective markers to enhance diagnostic accuracy. This study aimed to evaluate the temporal integration window (TIW) of sensory processing in individuals with CHR, first-episode schizophrenia (FES), and healthy controls (HC) to assess its utility as an objective neuropsychological marker for identifying CHR individuals. Ninety participants (30 CHR, 30 FES, 30 HC) completed temporal order judgment of visual and auditory(TOJV and TOJA) and simultaneity judgment of the flash-beep (SJF) tasks. Unisensory (visual and auditory) thresholds and TIW were measured and compared across groups. Cognitive function was assessed using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB). The associations between cognition with unisensory thresholds and TIW were analyzed. The TIW was significantly prolonged in both the CHR (336.95 ± 108.30 ms) and FES (413.15 ± 121.27 ms) groups compared to the HC group (229.95 ± 54.53 ms) (P < 0.05). Visual and auditory thresholds and TIW were negatively correlated with information processing speed and attention (both P < 0.05). Notably, TIW effectively discriminates across three groups. Compared to HC, each 50 ms increase in TIW was associated with a 2.3-fold increased risk of CHR (95% CI 1.27–4.28, P = 0.01; AUC = 0.81, 95% BCa CI 0.65–0.91). Similarly, it was associated with a 3.3-fold increased risk of FES (95% CI 1.78–6.34, P < 0.01; AUC = 0.94, 95% BCa CI 0.81–0.99). No correlations were observed between TIW and symptom severity in FES and CHR. Prolonged TIW in multisensory integration may serve as a promising objective neuropsychological marker for identifying individuals at CHR for psychosis.

Introduction

Individuals at clinical high-risk (CHR) for psychosis face a significantly higher lifetime risk of developing schizophrenia1, with 20%-30% transitioning to psychosis within a few years2. Early identification and intervention are critical to reducing conversion rates, preserving cognitive function and improving long-term outcomes3. CHR individuals often experience attenuated psychotic symptoms, such as brief hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking, alongside with impaired social functioning4 and cognitive deficits in domains like attention, memory, and executive functioning5. These cognitive impairments can be used as early indicators of psychosis risk, with studies6 demonstrating that cognitive performance in CHR individuals is intermediate between healthy controls (HC) and those with first-episode schizophrenia (FES). Longitudinal studies7 further revealed varied trajectories for cognitive deficits in CHR, with some individuals showing improvement, while others experience progressive declines, particularly in domains like attention and memory8,9, or progress to other mental disorders. Despite the importance of early detection, current neuropsychological assessment for CHR and early psychosis largely relies on subjective scales, such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST)10, and the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB)11. These measures, while valuable, are limited by their reliance on self-reports and subjective responses, which can be influenced by factors such as motivation, fatigue, or symptom variability. This reliance on subjective assessments highlights a critical research gap: the need for objective, reliable marker to enhance the precision of early psychosis identification and facilitate timely intervention.

Audiovisual integration, which spans multiple domains of cognitive function such as attention and memory12, is one promising area for objective assessment. It measures the capacity of brain to process and integrate diverse sensory inputs in complex environments, a function often impaired in individuals with cognitive impairments13. The temporal integration window (TIW), a key indicator of audiovisual integration, reflects the time frame with which distinct sensory stimuli are perceived as a single event14,15. A prolonged TIW reflects impaired temporal acuity in audiovisual integration, where longer intervals of stimulus asynchrony are perceived as simultaneous; this deficit is associated with slower reaction times, increased perceptual errors, and reduced cognitive adaptability, and further widens with age-related cognitive decline16–19. Notably, TIW can be modulated through cross-modal cognitive training15,20 suggesting its potential as a malleable marker for intervention21. Unisensory thresholds, defined as the minimal detectable interval between successive stimuli of the same modality (e.g., visual or auditory), further complement TIW by providing insights into sensory processing precision22.

Prior research suggested that TIW was significantly prolonged in individuals with chronic schizophrenia23, and correlated with symptom severity, particularly hallucinations24, independent of antipsychotic medication effects25. However, research on TIW and unisensory thresholds in early psychosis, including CHR populations, remain limited. This gap is significant, as identifying objective markers in the prodromal phase could enable earlier and more targeted interventions to prevent or delay psychosis onset. Furthermore, most published studies have focused on chronic schizophrenia, leaving the applicability of TIW as a marker for early-stage psychosis underexplored. The absence of research on TIW in CHR populations, combined with the lack of objective neuropsychological measures, underscores the need for studies that investigate sensory integration as a potential marker for early psychosis.

In this study, we employed the temporal order judgment (TOJ) and simultaneity judgment of the flash-beep (SJF) tasks to assess unisensory thresholds and TIW in patients with FES, CHR, and HC. The primary aim was to explore whether TIW and unisensory thresholds could serve as objective neuropsychological markers for distinguishing these three groups, and to investigate their underlying implications. We hypothesized that TIW and visual or auditory threshold would be significantly enlarged in both CHR and FES groups compared to the HC group, thus enabling their differentiation and potentially serving as objective neuropsychological indicators to identify CHR individuals.

Methods

Participants

The samples in this study included three groups: FES, CHR, and HC. 30 FES patients and 30 CHR individuals were recruited from Beijing Anding Hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University in Beijing, China between December 2022 and September 2024. 30 HC participants were recruited from social recruitment. The inclusion criteria for all three groups were: age 16-40 years, at least a middle school education, right-handed, with normal vision and hearing, no music background, and no visual or auditory impairments. FES26 patients met the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia and had limited treatment ( ≤ 2 weeks of regular or ≤1 month of irregular antipsychotic medication), with illness duration under 2 years. CHR27 was defined as individuals who met the criteria for Brief Intermittent Psychotic Syndrome (BIPS), or Attenuated Positive Symptom Syndrome (APSS), or Genetic Risk and Deterioration Syndrome (GRDS) as per the Structured Interview for Prodromal Syndromes (SIPS)28.

Among the 30 FES participants, 14 (46.7%) were medication-naïve at study entry. The remaining 16 (53.3%) had prior psychiatric medication exposure, including benzodiazepines and antipsychotics (paliperidone, aripiprazole, olanzapine, haloperidol; 4 received combination therapy). For the 12 participants with available antipsychotic dosages (excluding 1 on benzodiazepines only and 3 with unavailable data), medication exposure was quantified as Haloperidol Equivalents29 (HALeq; mean ± SD: 5.42 ± 1.99 mg/day). In the CHR group, 18 participants were taking medications, primarily antipsychotics (e.g., amisulpride, risperidone, aripiprazole, olanzapine, blonanserin, perospirone, paliperidone), with 6 receiving combination therapy. The mean HALeq dose among medicated CHR individuals was 6.81 ± 4.28 mg/day. Of the 12 medication-naïve CHR participants, one was received psychotherapy.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anding Hospital affiliated with Capital Medical University [number: (2018) Scientific Research No. (133)]. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200058124).

Procedure and materials

All participants underwent screening with relevant questionnaires by trained raters, followed by assessments using MCCB and the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-Revised in Chinese (WAIS-RC), including information, similarities, picture completion, and block design. CHR participants were assessed using SIPS, the syndromes were constructed and confirmed by experienced clinicians. FES patients underwent the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) interview and the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). The evaluation was conducted by three raters, all of whom completed training for inter-rater reliability on the scales, intra-class correlation coefficient(ICC) = 0.95. HC participants were assessed using the MINI interview to confirm eligibility.

Stimuli and tasks

The experiment consists of TOJ and SJF tasks. All task parameters mainly refer to the previously existing studies30.

TOJV task

Participants observed two hollow circles on the screen at fixed intervals. Participants were asked to determine which circle appeared first. The two white circles (outer diameter = 10 cm; thickness = 1.5 cm; duration = 13.3 ms) appeared sequentially against a black background, with one circle positioned above and the other below, maintaining a fixed cross arrangement. The centers of the two circles were spaced 20 cm apart. Each pair of circles was separated by varying stimulus onset asynchronies (SOA) of 13.3, 26.7, 40.0, 53.3, 66.7, 80.0, or 106.7 ms (screen refresh rate = 75 Hz). Each SOA condition was repeated 20 times in a random order, resulting in a total of 140 trials.

TOJA task

Participants heard two sounds continuously through headphones at fixed intervals, after which they were asked to judge which sound occurred first and respond accordingly. Two pure tones (one high tone at 2200 Hz and one low tone at 500 Hz; duration = 7 ms) were presented in binaural mode through the headphones, with the sound intensity controlled at 80 dB. The SOA was set at 13.3, 26.7, 53.3, 80.0, 106.7, 146.7, 200.0, or 253.3 ms (player sample rate = 44100 Hz). Participants pressed a button to indicate which stimulus they perceived to occur first. Each SOA condition was repeated 20 times in a random order, resulting in a total of 160 trials.

SJF Task

The experiment involved presenting a visual and auditory stimulus either simultaneously or with a specific interval. The visual stimulus was a white circle (duration = 13 ms), and the auditory stimulus was a pure tone (1000 Hz, duration = 13 ms). Participants were then required to determine whether the sound and the visual stimulus were presented synchronously or asynchronously, responding accordingly. The SOA between the visual and auditory stimuli ranged from 0 to ± 600 ms, with intervals of 100 ms (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600). Each SOA condition was repeated 10 times in a pseudo-random order, resulting in a total of 130 trials. In this context, “+” indicates asynchronous stimuli led by the visual stimulus, while “-” indicates asynchronous stimuli led by the auditory stimulus.

The experimental paradigm was implemented using MATLAB 2018b software with the Psychtoolbox for stimulus presentation. All three paradigms included a practice session consisting of 10 trials before the formal experiments. Participants were required to achieve an accuracy of 80% to proceed to the main experiment. They could practice multiple times to ensure a full understanding of the experimental tasks. This is similar to previous studies22. The number of distinct practice trials completed was not recorded, as the goal was task comprehension. Data collection of TOJ and SJF tasks was conducted in a soundproof environment to minimize external noise interference. Participants were instructed to sit comfortably in a chair positioned 0.8 meters from the display screen. After adjusting the volume to 80 dB, they were equipped with headphones. During the experiment, participants were guided to maintain focus and minimize unnecessary movements to ensure the accuracy of data collection. The screen used was a 17-inch LCD monitor from Dell, with a resolution of 1024 × 768 and a refresh rate of 75 Hz. The sound card had a sampling rate of 44,100 Hz, and sound playback was achieved through external headphones. Sound intensity was controlled using a precision acoustic testing device (Model 824) produced by Larson Davis, adjusting the system volume to maintain an ear-level intensity of approximately 80 dB to meet experimental requirements.

The visual threshold can be calculated through the TOJV task and the auditory threshold can be calculated through the TOJA task. TIW was calculated through the SJF task. For each participant, the TOJ accuracy at each SOA was fitted with a sigmoid psychometric function, and the unisensory temporal threshold was defined as the SOA yielding 75% correct responses. Similarly, in the SJF task, the proportion of simultaneity responses at each SOA was fitted with a Gaussian function on a participant-by-participant basis. The amplitude, mean, and standard deviation were freely estimated. The mean corresponds to the point of subjective simultaneity (PSS), and TIW was determined by the standard deviation of the fit, representing the SOA range within which a participant was most likely to tolerate temporal offsets and still perceive the stimuli as synchronous23, 31. A more detailed calculation process can be found in the Supplementary Materials Figs. 1 and 2. These models simulated the calculation of several key temporal processing metrics, including unisensory thresholds and TIW, derived from individual data fitting.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26, MATLAB 2018b and Python 3.11. Demographic data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Group differences in demographic characteristics were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\chi }^{2}$$\end{document}χ2 tests for categorical variables. Post-hoc analysis to assess differences between groups. After controlling for covariates of gender, age, educational attainment, and IQ, Linear Mixed Model (LMM) variance analysis was performed to evaluate unisensory thresholds and TIW across the three groups. Post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction was subsequently conducted to further investigate intergroup difference. Additionally, logistic regression analysis and ROC analysis were employed to compare the differential roles of unisensory thresholds and TIW among the groups. To quantify and correct for potential overfitting, we applied bootstrap optimism-correction procedure (2000 resamples) to the logistic regression models and calculated Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve(AUC). The apparent AUC were then adjusted by subtracting the mean optimism estimate, yielding AUC with 95% Bias-Corrected and Accelerated Bootstrap Confidence Interval (95% BCa CI). To explore the relationship between audiovisual integration, cognitive function, and symptom severity, we used forward stepwise regression to assess the associations between unisensory thresholds and TIW, with various cognitive domains and symptom severity after controlling for gender, age, education, and IQ as covariates.

All statistical tests of group differences and correlations were two-sided, with the significance level set at P < 0.05. Tests involving multiple comparisons were adjusted via Bonferroni correction.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Significant differences were observed in education level (P < 0.01), IQ (P < 0.01), and partial cognitive abilities (P < 0.05). Post-hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction revealed that HC demonstrated superior performance relative to FES patients in information processing speed, attention, reasoning and problem solving, and total scores (P < 0.05), while also outperforming CHR individuals in information processing speed, attention, working memory, and total scores (P < 0.05). Notably, no significant differences emerged between FES and CHR groups across all assessed domains. No significant differences in age and gender were observed among the three groups(P > 0.05). Comparison of unisensory thresholds and TIW among CHR, FES, and HC groups

Comparison of the visual threshold of TOJV Task among CHR, FES, and HC groups

During data quality control whose accuracy rates at the longest SOA condition were >2 SDs below their respective group mean30 were excluded (CHR: n = 7; FES: n = 6; HC: n = 5). Detailed information on exclusions and post-exclusion demographic analyses is provided in Supplementary Material Note 1. Data of the TOJV task of 72 participants (CHR = 23, FES = 24, HC = 25) were analyzed. We conducted an LMM analysis of visual threshold across the three groups, controlling age, gender, education level and IQ as covariates. Results revealed that there was a significant difference in visual threshold (F[2,71] = 5.18, P = 0.01, partial η2 = 0.14). Post-hoc analysis revealed a significant difference between the FES and CHR groups (P = 0.01, Cohen’s d = −0.60), while no significant differences were observed between the HC group compared with either CHR or FES. Complementing these analyses, ROC curves assessments based on the TOJV threshold revealed only modest discrimination capacity. For FES vs HC comparison, the AUC was 0.61 (95% BCa CI=[0.40–0.77]). For CHR vs HC comparison, the AUC was 0.57 (95% BCa CI=[0.39–0.73]), and for FES vs CHR comparison, the AUC was 0.58 (95% BCa CI=[0.39–0.74]). Bootstrap correction adjusted the AUC estimates, indicating limited overfitting. Logistic regression, with a 10-ms scaling to match the observed range, showed a robust association between TOJV threshold and diagnosis (OR = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.053–2.036, p = 0.02); specifically, each 10-ms prolongation was associated with a 46% increase in the offs of an FES diagnosis. The subgroup analysis of CHR did not find statistically significant differences in subgroup types. Comparison of the auditory threshold of the TOJA task among CHR, FES, and HC groups

During data quality control, participants whose accuracy rates at the longest SOA condition were >2 SDs below their respective group mean were excluded (CHR: n = 2; FES: n = 9; HC: n = 8). Detailed information on exclusion and post-exclusion demographic analyses is provided in Supplementary Material Note 1. Data of 71 participants (CHR = 28, FES = 21, HC = 22) were analyzed. LMM analysis revealed significant intergroup differences in TOJA threshold (F[2,70] = 3.35, P = 0.04, partial η2 = 0.10). Post-hoc comparisons indicated that FES exhibited significantly higher TOJA threshold compared to HC (P = 0.05, Cohen’s d = −0.89). Logistic regression analysis demonstrated no statistically distinguishable patterns between TOJA thresholds and diagnostic classification (P > 0.05). To account for temporal resolution variations across the three groups, the analysis interval was optimized to 10-ms increments in regression modeling. Consistent with these findings, ROC analyses showed only modest discrimination: For the comparison between FES and HC, the AUC was 0.70 (95% BCa CI 0.51–0.84), for CHR vs HC, the AUC was 0.70 (95% BCa CI 0.54–0.83), and for FES vs CHR, the AUC was 0.57 (95% BCa CI 0.38–0.74); none remained significant after Bonferroni correction. The subgroup analysis of CHR did not find statistically significant differences in subgroup types. See Tables 2 and 3 and Fig. 2B.

Comparison of TIW of SJF task among CHR, FES, and HC groups

During quality control, participants who demonstrated simultaneity judgment rates exceeding >50% at maximum SOA and had poor data fit (R² < 0.3)30, 31were excluded (CHR: n = 4; FES: n = 8; HC: n = 6). Detailed information on exclusion and post-exclusion demographic analyses is provided in Supplementary Material Note 1. Data of SJF task of 72 participants (CHR = 26, FES = 22, HC = 24) were analyzed and the TIW was calculated. LMM analysis revealed significant group differences in TIW(F[2,71] = 12.21, P < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.27). Post-hoc analysis showed FES group had a significantly enlargement of TIW compared to HC groups (P < 0.01, Cohen’s d = −1.98) and CHR groups (P = 0.02, Cohen’s d = −0.68), respectively. There was significant statistical difference between the CHR and HC groups (P = 0.05, Cohen’s d = −1.23). The logistic regression revealed significant discriminative capacity of the TIW metric across populations. Notably, CHR individuals showed 2.33 times greater odds of TIW deviation relative to HC (OR = 2.33, 95% CI [1.271–4.279], P = 0.01). FES patients demonstrated a pronounced 3.36-fold elevation in TIW abnormality risk compared to HC (OR = 3.36, 95% CI [1.780–6.342], P < 0.01), suggesting strong diagnostic validity. Additionally, FES patients maintained a significant 1.44 fold increased TIW impairment risk versus CHR counterparts (OR = 1.44, 95% CI [1.060-1.957], P = 0.02). Consistent with these differences, ROC analyses demonstrated strong discriminative ability for TIW: for the comparison between FES and HC, the AUC was excellent (0.94) (95% BCa CI 0.81–0.99; sensitivity=0.82, specificity=1.00; P = 0.04), For CHR vs HC, the AUC was 0.81 (0.65–0.91; sensitivity=0.65, specificity=0.96; P = 0.05). For FES vs CHR, the differentiation was moderate, with an AUC of 0.68(0.51–0.82; sensitivity=0.82, specificity=0.50; P = 0.06). Bootstrap optimism was <0.03 for all contrasts, indicating minimal overfitting. See Tables 2 and 3 and Fig. 2C. The ROC classification results are shown in Fig. 3. To address potential temporal resolution differences between groups, the analytical intervals were systematically adjusted in 50-ms increments during model construction, ensuring equivalent temporal sampling precision across all comparisons. The subgroup analysis of CHR did not reveal statistically significant differences across subgroup types. See Supplementary Material Table 5. Discussion

This study is the first to apply the TOJ and SJF tasks to assess TIW in individuals at CHR and FES patients, providing novel insights into the early detection of psychosis. Our findings reveal a few key observations: First, we found significantly prolonged TIW in both CHR (336.95 ± 108.30 ms) and FES (413.15 ± 121.27 ms) groups compared to HC (229.95 ± 54.53 ms), with CHR showing intermediate values. The TIW demonstrated excellent discriminability across all three groups. These results suggest that TIW prolongation may serve as an objective neuropsychological marker for early psychosis detection. Second, correlation analysis revealed that visual threshold were negatively correlated with information processing speed, while auditory threshold and TIW showed negative correlations with both information processing speed and working memory. In addition, no significant correlations were observed between unisensory thresholds, TIW and clinical symptoms in CHR and FES patients.

Unisensory processing in early psychosis

Our study found significant statistical differences in visual threshold between the CHR and the FES groups, with visual threshold significantly elevated in FES patients. Regression analysis revealed that compared to the CHR group, every 10 ms increase in visual threshold in the FES group corresponded to a 46% increase in the risk of developing psychosis. Although regression analysis did not indicate significant differences between groups for auditory threshold, significant differences in TOJA threshold were observed between HC and FES groups. IN contrast, the differences in the TOJV threshold between these two groups reached borderline significance between these two groups. Notably, no significant differences were detected between HC and CHR groups for either auditory or visual thresholds, which aligns with findings from previous studies31,32 indicating subtle sensory processing deficits and heterogeneity during the clinical high-risk state.

Unisensory thresholds have been extensively investigated in the context of psychiatric disorders. Previous research33 highlighted a relatively high incidence of unisensory sensitivity issues among patients with schizophrenia, revealing a low-level temporal coding deficit in FES25. Later studies34 suggested that the unisensory temporal coding deficits in schizophrenia may arise from impairments in predictive mechanisms at the implicit processing level. In other words, individuals with schizophrenia tend to rely more on direct sensory input rather than processing information based on expectations or predictions. Similar sensory processing anomalies have also been observed in CHR individuals, though there is yet to be a unified understanding of these phenomena. A follow-up study35 conducted on the CHR population showed that early visual abnormality scores and auditory abnormality scores had opposite predictive effects on the transition outcomes in CHR individuals. Kadivar et al.32 conducted a multi-pathway study on visual contrast sensitivity and found that both CHR and FES groups exhibited abnormalities in luminance processing mediated by the magnocellular pathway, while no significant differences were observed in chromatic processing compared to the HC group.

The discrepancies in findings regarding single sensory thresholds in CHR populations across different studies may stem from variations in research objectives and experimental paradigms. However, another very important factor is the considerable heterogeneity inherent in the clinical characteristics of the CHR population itself. Based on our research findings, we believe that distinguishing between CHR and FES populations solely through unisensory thresholds measurements is highly challenging. In fact, other studies36 suggested that temporal precision in patients with schizophrenia is not impaired, and they can still perform normally in certain visual processing tasks. However, their temporal integration ability is significantly disrupted, which in turn affects clinical symptoms.

TIW as an objective marker for early psychosis

Most previous studies24, 37 indicated a prolonged TIW in patients with chronic schizophrenia compared to HC. Stevenson23 reported the TIW of 550 ± 240 ms in patients with chronic schizophrenia compared to that of 240 ± 114 ms in healthy individuals, aligning with our findings. It is important to note that a previous study suggested both unisensory thresholds and TIW were associated with the stage of the illness38, reflecting different manifestations at different disease phases. However, these studies included individuals in both the early stages of psychosis and chronic disease course, characterized by high heterogeneity. Our study primarily targets early psychosis, so caution should be exercised when comparing results across studies with differing sample characteristics.

In the present study, regression analysis and ROC evaluation (FES vs HC: AUC = 0.94; CHR vs HC: AUC = 0.81; FES vs CHR: AUC = 0.68) demonstrated that TIW effectively discriminates among the three groups. Compared to HC, every 50 ms increase in TIW was associated with a twofold increase in the risk of being diagnosed as CHR and a threefold increase in the risk of FES diagnosis. According to the results of our cross-sectional study, audiovisual integration dysfunction becomes evident during the early progression of psychotic disorders. In schizophrenia research, Noel et al.39 proposed that patients may have deficits in processing sensory information, leading to an impaired ability of their sensory systems to accurately reflect the temporal characteristics of external stimuli. Their altered decision-making processes, influenced by prior expectations, were attributed to functional abnormalities in the superior temporal sulcus and inferior frontal cortex40, 41. Additionally, Foucher et al.24 suggested that audiovisual integration impairment in schizophrenia reflects a functional connectivity disturbance. At the neuro-electrophysiological level, they hypothesized that demyelination causes delayed action potentials, resulting in conduction and connection errors that contribute to the observed widening of the TIW.

From a predictive coding perspective42, these findings can be further interpreted as a reduction in the precision (reliability) of temporal priors in schizophrenia. When temporal priors are weak, the brain is unable to effectively pre-activate sensory cortices at expected time points, necessitating greater reliance on inherently noisy single-trial sensory inputs. This increased reliance on noisy inputs lowers the precision of the posterior estimate, requiring larger physical asynchronies before two events are discriminated as non-simultaneous. This phenomenon manifests behaviorally as elevated unisensory thresholds and an expanded TIW. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, prior studies consistently indicate that an expanded TIW in schizophrenia populations is accompanied by severe structural and functional brain impairments20,22. It is generally believed that individuals at CHR also exhibit early brain structural and functional changes43, although these alterations tend to be milder compared to those observed in patients with schizophrenia.

Cognitive function was correlated with TIW

We conducted correlation analyses between TIW and various cognitive domains, revealing a significant negative relationship between the expansion of the TIW and both information processing speed and attention. Notably, a recent study has suggested that deficits in processing speed and alertness/attention may serve as potent neurocognitive markers for identifying individuals at risk for psychosis44, aligning with our current findings. We hypothesized that as cognitive impairments worsen progressively, alterations in brain structure and function exert a detrimental impact on audiovisual integration. Although previous studies have reported heterogeneous results across different disorders and experimental paradigms45, and the underlying neural mechanisms may be more complex46, both unisensory sensitivity and TIW partially reflect cognitive functioning. The TIW represents a dynamic and complex system composed of multiple cognitive dimensions. Prior research has demonstrated close associations between TIW and working memory47, executive control48, attention48, and perceptual integration. Attention plays a critical role in cognitive processing by enabling the brain to filter relevant information49. When attentional capacity declines, the speed of information processing and integration slows, necessitating longer duration to capture stimuli. This compensatory mechanism may underlie the observed enlargement of the TIW. However, confounding factors such as disease state, symptom severity, and medication use may influence these findings50, warranting cautious interpretation. Beyond diagnostic status, age has a pronounced impact on both cognitive function and TIW; this aligns with previous reports16 and underscores the need to stratify by age in between-group comparisons.

In summary, the increased TIW observed in CHR individuals may serve as an early indicator of cognitive impairment. Among those with favorable prognoses, TIW may normalize alongside cognitive improvements, whereas in individuals who transition to schizophrenia, TIW might further expand7. Our findings underscore the strong translational potential of TIW. Developing portable TIW-based devices or software for clinical neuropsychological testing could facilitate efficient early psychosis screening in both community and hospital settings.

TIW was lack of relationship with clinical symptomatology

The size of the TIW was closely related to the precision of time perception51. A smaller TIW indicated higher perceptual accuracy and a more efficient coordination of sensory processing, which was essential for adapting to rapidly changing environments52. The prefrontal cortex played a key role in this process53,54, and inter-individual differences in TIW width were often attributed to variations in cortical function. In patients with cognitive impairments, abnormal expansion of the TIW and reduced time perception accuracy may lead to distorted sensory processing and psychotic symptoms55. Abnormalities in multisensory temporal encoding were closely associated with positive symptoms of schizophrenia, such as hallucinations23. However, we did not find a significant correlation between TIW and disease severity, consistent with findings from another study30. In addition, no significant differences were found among the CHR subgroup analyses.

Several factors may explain this lack of correlation. First, TIW appears to reflect a trait-like multisensory timing dysfunction that is only weakly associated with state-dependent symptom fluctuations56. Second, fundamental differences in the construct and timescale of TIW (measuring millisecond-level temporal binding in the lab) and PANSS (summarizing current clinical phenomenology), along with symptom heterogeneity, can obscure cross-sectional associations. Third, TIW can be modulated by multiple influences (e.g., cognitive domains and affective states)22, while clinical severity reflects broader considerations (e.g., symptom profiles and psychosocial functioning)57,58. Finally, the influence of antipsychotic exposure on cognitive function and TIW merits attention. Given the limited and heterogeneous exposure in this cohort, the effect on TIW remains insufficiently characterized. Future work could explicitly assess medication effects using standardized indices. While TIW may not serve as a direct index of concurrent symptom severity, it could possess prognostic significance. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether baseline TIW predicts transition in CHR and informs trajectories of symptoms and functioning in early psychosis.

Limitations

While this study provided novel findings and insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional study, we were unable to track cognitive and TIW changes over time, particularly in CHR individuals who may convert to schizophrenia. Second, the relatively small sample size may have affected the statistical power and the ability to fully characterize potential subgroups within the CHR population. Third, although antipsychotic medications were not known to directly affect TIW, their potential effects on cognitive function remained uncertain, possibly introducing confounding factors in FES participants. Fourth, this study focused on the audiovisual integration functions in the CHR population, primarily using behavioral data, which may limit the depth of our findings. Fifth, another limitation of this study is the lack of detailed information on family history of the psychosis and neurodevelopmental disorders within the FES group, and comorbid diagnoses. These factors, known to influence sensory processing and TIW, were not assessed and represent potential confounding variables. Finally, our results may disproportionally represent participants capable of maintaining stable task performance; therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to individuals with more severe perceptual or attentional instability This exploratory findings were interesting and promising, but follow-up confirmatory studies were needed to validate these findings and established the utility of TIW as an objective marker for disease identification.

Conclusions and future directions

This study highlighted the potential of the TIW as a novel neuropsychological marker for early identification and diagnosis of schizophrenia spectrum disorders. By demonstrating significant differences in TIW among HC, CHR and FES groups, our findings suggested that TIW may reflect a transitional stage in the progression of psychosis. Moreover, the significant correlations between TIW and cognitive function underlined its relevance in understanding the complex relationships between sensory integration and cognitive impairments. As a promising objective neuropsychological marker, TIW held the potential for early identification and diagnosis of mental disorders. Future research should consider cohort-based longitudinal studies to further validate its effectiveness and clarify its relationship with disease transformation and assessing intervention outcomes, thereby providing stronger evidence for clinical application.
