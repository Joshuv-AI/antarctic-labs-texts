Decolonising global health through implementation science: defining the path to equity and structural transformation
Authors: Deena Mehjabeen, Kyle Patel, Rahul M Jindal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDecolonising global health requires rethinking how evidence is generated, valued and applied to address colonial legacies and power imbalances in systems, practices and research.MethodsThis analysis draws on findings from a scoping review and synthesises existing literature to examine how decolonisation principles can be integrated into implementation science.ResultsWe propose three conceptual shifts: (1) Embedding contextually and locally driven intervention approaches, (2) Centring participatory, equity-focused methodologies that redistribute authority and (3) Expanding metrics to capture power, authorship equity, adaptability and legitimacy. These shifts reposition implementation science as a transformative practice supporting structural change.ConclusionOperationalising these shifts through policy-aligned, locally led research, equitable mentorship and technologies co-designed with communities provides a practical framework for researchers, funders and policymakers to embed decolonisation into implementation science, ensuring interventions are contextually relevant, equitable and socially just.

Introduction

Global health encompasses collaborative, cross-border research initiatives aimed at improving health outcomes for all people.1 It builds on national public health strategies and spans both population-level interventions and individual healthcare. Despite considerable progress, global health remains shaped by colonial legacies that sustain biases, unjust power dynamics and inequitable resource distribution, all reinforcing structural disparities.2 Recent cutbacks and the dismantling of USAID, driven in part by growing opposition to international aid in the US,3 highlight how global health remains vulnerable to political shifts in the Global North. Funding and policy priorities are often determined by the changing agendas of domestic leaders and philanthropists, leaving countries in the Global South exposed to uncertainty despite their reliance on sustained support for health programmes and development. The rise of populist right-wing movements worldwide has intensified anti-aid sentiment, further destabilising international health efforts. These dynamics underscore the urgency of decolonising global health by reducing reliance on volatile donor politics and strengthening locally led, sustainable systems of governance and financing.4 As Kumar et al5 argue, many existing guidelines for equity in research partnerships fall short because they do not shift decision-making power, redistribute resources or hold funders accountable to the Global South.5 Similarly, McCoy et al6 emphasise that colonial and financialised systems of global health continue to extract value via wealth, knowledge, and epistemic privilege, reinforcing structural inequalities.6 Against this backdrop, decolonising global health is not only a moral imperative but also a practical necessity for achieving equitable and accountable health systems, particularly for marginalised populations.

Early work in decolonising research, particularly led by Indigenous scholars, has adapted implementation science to support community partnerships7 and to co-produce governance tools for local contexts.8 9 Other studies have focused on tailoring interventions to Indigenous communities and addressing structural inequities.10 11 These efforts demonstrate the value of participatory and contextually driven approaches, yet they often do not fully embed local authority or equity-focused evaluation throughout the research process. Addressing these limitations requires strategies that systematically redistribute power, engage communities meaningfully and incorporate locally defined metrics of success. These principles are central to the framework proposed here. These initiatives build on decades of research and scholarship emphasising Indigenous and local knowledge systems as central to ethical, relevant and effective health interventions.

While this paper focuses on colonial legacies, racism operates as a closely related and reinforcing system that shapes inequities in global health. Emerging work highlights the need to explicitly address structural racism within implementation frameworks, including through equity-focused design and antiracist approaches.1214 Other studies emphasise the importance of prioritising underserved populations and integrating equity considerations from the outset.1517 Addressing both colonialism and racism is therefore essential to advancing more equitable and just implementation practices.

Implementation science, a key mechanism for translating evidence into policy and practice, has also been influenced by structural dynamics.18 19 This paper focuses on decolonisation within the discipline of implementation science because it offers a valuable opportunity to interrogate the underlying assumptions used in global health and scientific practice worldwide. Methodologies, analyses and conclusions are validated based on innate assumptions of what constitutes evidence and how it should be gathered. These methods produce new evidence and methodologies built on those original assumptions of what is knowledge and validity, historically favouring those in power, colonisers of the past and present. It is particularly well positioned to contribute to decolonisation efforts because of its focus on how interventions are adapted, implemented and sustained in real-world contexts. Unlike fields that primarily generate evidence, it engages directly with questions of context, power and decision-making in practice, creating opportunities to centre local knowledge, redistribute authority and support locally led solutions. Its emphasis on adaptation, participatory engagement and systems thinking provides a practical pathway for translating decolonising principles into action within health systems.14 20

Recent commentaries have highlighted the need to decolonise implementation science by addressing power imbalances, epistemic injustice and the dominance of Western-centric methodologies,21 22 and scholars have proposed practical guiding principles rooted in Indigenous leadership to reshape the foundations of this field.20 While these works provide a critical conceptual foundation, they offer limited guidance on how decolonisation principles can be operationalised across diverse contexts. Building on this discourse, our analysis synthesises findings from our scoping review23 to propose concrete conceptual shifts and practical frameworks for embedding decolonisation into implementation science, ensuring interventions are contextually relevant, equitable and socially just. At the same time, implementation science emerged from the evidence-based medicine movement, which initially focused on closing the gap between research evidence and routine practice by promoting systematic uptake of interventions. This origin can shape the field toward an emphasis on standardised methods, quantifiable outcomes and generalisable evidence, even as newer frameworks increasingly balance these approaches with contextually driven and equity-oriented perspectives.24 The methodologies, notions of rigour and evaluation frameworks often privilege Global North epistemologies, marginalising local expertise and reducing relevance for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to differences in context, available resources or health system structures. The decolonisation movement urges global health institutions, funders and researchers to confront and transform systemic inequities rooted in colonial ideologies.25 Tools such as Intervention and Research Readiness Engagement and Assessment of Community Healthcare (I-RREACH) provide practical frameworks for assessing and improving implementation readiness among researchers, organisations and communities, supporting more equitable and contextually responsive research.26 Central to this effort are principles of social justice, human rights and universal health coverage, with an emphasis on primary healthcare and task-sharing approaches that are developed with active community input to ensure relevance and responsiveness.27 28 However, the movement still lacks a unified definition and clear goals.29

Building on key dimensions identified in our scoping review, we propose the following working definition: ‘Decolonising global health is the process of dismantling colonial legacies and power imbalances embedded in global health systems, practices and research. It involves shifting from hierarchical, Global North dominated models toward equitable and inclusive practices that equip scholars, practitioners and communities from the Global South by redistributing resources, decentralising research and valuing Indigenous and local knowledge’.

This definition reflects key structural issues identified in the literature. Among them is the disproportionate influence of Global North institutions in selectively funding priorities, determining research agendas and shaping publishing outputs. Scholars and practitioners from LMICs often face systemic barriers to accessing research funding, securing lead authorship and participating in high-level global health governance.30 These dynamics echo historical patterns of knowledge extraction and exploitation, as highlighted in Raewyn Connell’s Southern Theory,31 which critiques the dominance of Global North perspectives in producing and legitimising knowledge. Connell’s framework underscores how knowledge generated in the Global South is often appropriated, marginalised or framed through Northern priorities, reinforcing structural inequities and highlighting the need for approaches that genuinely centre local voices and expertise. Crucially, decolonising implementation science must go beyond language and intention. It requires changes in who defines problems, who designs solutions and who is deemed an expert. It also demands structural shifts in governance, funding allocation and authorship norms. Without these fundamental shifts, efforts to make implementation science more participatory may end up reinforcing the same hierarchies they aim to break down.32

Traditional notions of rigour in implementation science, which often rely on narrow trial designs and strict adherence to the original protocol,33 can unintentionally reinforce the very hierarchies they aim to challenge. If implementation research is to support decolonisation, we must rethink what counts as “robust” evidence and whose knowledge is valued. We propose three interrelated shifts to reposition implementation science as a more equitable and transformative practice: (1) Contextual and locally driven approaches, (2) Participatory and equity-centred methodologies and (3) Expanded metrics of success. These shifts can be operationalised through locally led, policy-relevant research and learning-oriented adaptation, inclusive leadership, equitable mentorship, participatory practice, co-designed technologies, equity-focused metrics and systems for adaptation. Together, they move implementation science from a technical toolkit to a process that redistributes authority, centres lived experience and reflects local priorities. Drawing on alternative epistemologies historically marginalised in global health, including Qiné from Ethiopia and Ubuntu as a pan-African philosophy,34 35 these shifts are grounded in relationality, collective responsibility and contextually informed knowledge.

Contextual and locally driven approaches

We argue that implementation frameworks need to move beyond one-size-fits-all models.36 In implementation science, this means designing interventions that are directly grounded in local health systems, policy priorities and community realities, drawing on local knowledge and lived experience. Such locally led, policy-relevant research enhances contextual fit, supports sustainability, and embeds findings within real-world health and social systems.

Participatory and equity-centred methodologies

Equity is not a neutral concept, and questions of whose equity is prioritised are central to how agendas are set and resources allocated. Equity concerns translate into concrete strategies for ensuring methodological fairness, such as co-produced governance structures, shared decision-making and equitable mentorship. Inclusive leadership and participatory practice strengthen engagement, centring local knowledge and lived experience at every stage. If not intentionally addressed, attempts to advance equity may inadvertently reproduce existing hierarchies, prioritising dominant perspectives over locally defined priorities. Practical approaches may include co-produced governance structures with shared decision-making authority, funding models that allocate resources directly to local institutions and accountability mechanisms such as equitable authorship practices, transparent priority-setting processes, community-led evaluation frameworks and regular reflection to monitor power dynamics and guide adjustments throughout the research cycle.39 40 In the absence of formal enforcement mechanisms, guiding frameworks such as I-RREACH can support meaningful redistribution of power by systematically assessing readiness, structuring engagement and embedding accountability mechanisms, providing researchers concrete ways to advance equity in practice. We advocate for co-producing research questions, methods, and outcomes with policymakers, global health practitioners and community members to ensure relevance and legitimacy. Genuine participation requires time, trust-building and shared decision-making. Research teams must remain responsive to evolving priorities and avoid extractive or tokenistic practices.41

Inclusive and decentralised leadership

Inclusive and decentralised leadership

We emphasise the need to redefine expertise to include Indigenous knowledge, lived experience and community insight. Research consortia should adopt co-leadership models where LMIC investigators are involved from design to dissemination, with equitable authorship and shared decision-making. We believe that mentorship is a critical enabler of systemic change and power-sharing in global health. Early-career researchers from underrepresented regions, particularly LMICs, must be supported not only through training, but through opportunities to lead, access funding and shape research agendas. Effective mentorship must move beyond technical guidance to promote equitable authorship, leadership development and shared decision-making.42 This involves dismantling gatekeeping practices and fostering collaboration where knowledge is co-produced rather than imposed. Importantly, the mentor-mentee relationship should not be viewed as hierarchical.

Embedding participatory practice

Embedding participatory practice

We assert that communities must be engaged as co-producers of knowledge, not simply sources of data. This requires humility, reciprocity and long-term relationship-building. Researchers should create inclusive spaces such as town halls or community forums46 where diverse participants can help define problems, interpret findings and co-produce actions, particularly in contexts shaped by historical marginalisation.47 We use co-production to refer to a distinct methodological approach that entails shared authority across all stages of the research process, including problem definition, study design, implementation, interpretation and dissemination. This goes beyond consultation or advisory input, which often involve engagement on predefined agendas without redistribution of decision-making power. In contrast, co-production requires meaningful power-sharing and mutual accountability between researchers and community members, aligning with participatory action research traditions. We caution against the superficial use of co-production language where decision-making remains centralised, as this risks reproducing the extractive practices that decolonising approaches seek to challenge.48

Expanded metrics of success

We recommend measuring equity alongside effectiveness and cost. In the context of implementation science, indicators should capture outcomes that matter locally, including the proportion of local co-leads and authors, community ownership, intervention adaptability and real-world translation of research findings. Mixed methods such as realist evaluation, systems mapping and narrative inquiry can help identify not only what works but for whom and why.36 By incorporating equity-focused metrics alongside traditional performance measures, implementation science can operationalise decolonising principles while generating actionable insights for practice. While equity metrics continue to evolve and face debate, it is critical that measurement tools reflect local contexts and power dynamics to truly advance fair and effective implementation. One possible avenue could be bibliometric analyses across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus or even Google Trends, potentially enhanced by artificial intelligence (AI) tools, to explore how decolonisation is reflected in scholarship, practice and discourse.49 Similarly, scorecards such as those recently proposed for global surgery to assess individual contributions could be adapted to evaluate engagement with decolonising principles in global health research and practice.50 Such approaches, while experimental, offer a structured way to monitor progress, highlight gaps and ensure accountability in advancing equity and justice.

Equity and technology in implementation science

Emerging technologies offer powerful tools for implementation science but without an equity lens they risk reinforcing existing disparities. We highlight three priorities for ensuring technologies serve rather than sideline marginalised communities.

Co-design and contextual relevanceAs part of the broader co-production approach, co-design focuses on the collaborative development of interventions and implementation strategies. This involves co-producing technologies including mobile health apps, AI tools and large language models with communities and embedding them within existing systems such as health services or social care. Social media and AI tools have transformative potential in global health but carry significant risks if left unmonitored. Many AI systems are trained predominantly on datasets from Western or high-income country contexts, embedding cultural, linguistic and structural biases. If deployed without adaptation, these tools may produce inaccurate predictions, exclude marginalised populations or reinforce existing inequities. Co-design with local communities ensures technologies reflect local realities, supports equitable access and safeguards against misuse or data exploitation, making interventions both contextually relevant and socially just.51 However, to ensure marginalised communities can benefit, these tools must be designed with cultural, linguistic and infrastructural realities in mind, supporting local expertise and practice rather than bypassing them. Equally important is addressing access barriers including finances and technology resources so that communities have the means to use these tools effectively. In such contexts, addressing inequities must also involve closing the so-called “digital divide” that leaves rural and remote communities in LMICs with far less access to digital infrastructure compared with urban areas, further limiting their participation and voice in global health.52 Furthermore, these models must be trained in equitable ways that protect people from data exploitation and ensure the source data reflects the communities where the model will be applied.

Implementation science holds significant potential to support the decolonisation of global health if reimagined around equity and shared authority. By grounding research in local contexts, strengthening participatory approaches and transforming leadership structures, it can contribute not only to improved health outcomes but also to sustained structural change.

Decisions rooted in justice and human dignity elevate global health efforts, ensuring focus on marginalised communities. Moral principles are foundational in health systems, shaping priorities, decision-making and accountability.53 In contrast, global health often emphasises operational efficiency, measurable outcomes and standardised approaches, which can inadvertently sideline ethics and deepen inequities. While global health promotes health equity and population well-being, embedding a clear moral framework remains aspirational. Integrating ethics into global health education can prepare future leaders to balance technical expertise with ethical responsibility, ensuring interventions are both effective and responsive to structural inequities. Moral determinants of health are long acknowledged but rarely operationalised in global health, highlighting the need for frameworks that combine internal ethical guidance, formal directives and institutional accountability.53 Indigenous knowledge systems, which underscore trust, inclusivity and collective responsibility, illustrate how contextually grounded moral frameworks can guide decolonised practice. By integrating ethical principles with local knowledge and participatory approaches, global health interventions can be technically sound, socially responsible and aligned with justice.54

Our working definition, informed by our recent scoping review, highlights the need to dismantle colonial structures, centre marginalised voices and promote inclusive, context-driven collaboration. A decolonised framework must remain adaptable, grounded in community needs and guided by long-term commitment to justice and shared responsibility. A growing body of scholarship and advocacy continues to call for a fundamental rethinking of implementation research, one that prioritises equity, shifts power and actively disrupts colonial hierarchies embedded in global health practice. This includes reimagining how implementation science can be timely and relevant for policymakers in LMICs; how rigour can be redefined through participatory and culturally grounded approaches; and how theories, methods and evaluation frameworks can support co-production, real-world impact and sustainable change. Within this, diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) has been a cornerstone of advancing equity and, by extension, decolonising practices. Yet, while many Global North countries continue to embed DEI principles, its dismantling in the US risks creating divergent meanings and applications of the term across contexts, potentially complicating international collaborations.5 These recommendations are informed by epistemologies such as Qiné35 from Ethiopia and Ubuntu, emphasising relational, participatory and locally grounded approaches.34 These long-held but historically deprioritised intellectual traditions within mainstream global health have the potential to inform knowledge and practice rooted in epistemological diversity.

Discussion and conclusions

The recommendations presented in this paper are directed to two primary audiences. First, policymakers and funding bodies in both LMICs and the Global North, who can determine research agendas, allocate resources and support equitable partnerships. Second, researchers in LMICs and the Global North, who are responsible for conducting studies and implementing interventions in ways that operationalise equity, such as through co-designed governance, participatory methods and community engagement. While these audiences share the goal of advancing equity, the ways the recommendations are applied differ. Policymakers can relinquish traditional roles of power and incorporate an equity- and justice-centred framework into funding and policy decisions. Researchers, in turn, can focus on collaborative processes and ethical engagement to critically examine the epistemologies and frameworks they use to produce knowledge, which may perpetuate the colonial structures currently in place.

Building on prior efforts in participatory and contextually tailored approaches, the shifts proposed here emphasise more equitable and locally grounded practices in global health. Operationalising these shifts requires coordinated action across policy and practice, including reforms to funding structures, co-designed governance models and sustained investment in locally led research and capacity strengthening. Deeply embedded institutional norms and funding structures may limit the extent to which power and resources can be redistributed, and meaningful participation requires sustained time, trust-building and commitment that may not align with short-term project timelines. There is also a risk of tokenistic engagement if participatory processes are not accompanied by genuine shifts in decision-making authority. Importantly, persistent power asymmetries in global health continue to silence critical voices, and addressing these inequities is essential for achieving sustainable, contextually relevant and ethically grounded interventions. Our hope is that by offering approaches to implementation science with a framework that centres local and Indigenous knowledge and epistemologies, new methodologies and evidence will emerge that help drive forward the decolonising agenda. The future of global health must be built on inclusive systems where all voices are heard, knowledge flows in multiple directions and justice guides action. Ultimately, prioritising local leadership and sustained investment in locally led systems is central to fostering lasting and equitable change.
