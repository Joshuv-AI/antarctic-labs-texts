Support from New York City’s office of chief medical examiner for people bereaved by drug poisoning: development and implementation of the family support program
Authors: Karli R Hochstatter, Hannah Johnson, Megan Brown, Jan Gryczynski, Talia Nadel, Jason Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBereavement from loss related to drug poisoning, the leading cause of injury-related death in the United States, is associated with elevated risk for prolonged grief disorder, substance use, and poor mental health outcomes. Despite this, few interventions specifically target survivors of overdose loss. The Family Support Program (FSP) is a novel, social worker-delivered bereavement support service provided by the Drug Intelligence and Intervention Group of the New York City Office of Chief Medical Examiner for family, friends, and other close social network members bereaved by drug poisoning. The FSP integrates evidence-based components including motivational interviewing, psychoeducation, social support, and linkage to care to reduce grief-related distress and improve psychosocial functioning. This paper describes the development, implementation, and pilot-phase adaptations of the FSP, including program design, staffing strategies, data infrastructure, and lessons learned. This paper can guide medical examiner and coroner offices in developing responsive, trauma-informed services for individuals affected by drug poisoning loss.Keywords: Overdose loss, Grief, Bereavement intervention, Substance use, Medical examiner services

Background

Overdose bereavement

Drug poisoning (often referred to as overdose) is the leading cause of injury-related death in the U.S., with mortality rates remaining historically elevated despite recent declines [1]. As the opioid epidemic continues, experiencing loss to drug poisoning has become increasingly common. A nationally representative survey from 2023 found that over 40% of adults in the U.S., or approximately 125 million Americans, personally knew someone who had died from an overdose [2]. Furthermore, it was estimated that over 300,000 children lost a parent to drug overdose between 2011 and 2021 [3]. Among those who reported ever knowing someone who died by overdose, the mean number of overdose losses was 2.9 [2].

Impact of overdose bereavement on grief and functioning

Several studies show that sudden or unexpected loss increases risk for mental health problems and poor functioning compared to loss from natural causes [4–9]. The unexpected death of a loved one is associated with the development of depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, physical illness, social isolation, substance use, and other psychiatric disorders [4, 10–13]. People bereaved by overdose are almost three times more likely to meet criteria for prolonged grief disorder (PGD), post-traumatic stress disorder, and major depressive disorder [14]. Family members bereaved by overdose perceive a heavier emotional burden and less social support than those bereaved by other types of deaths [5]. Those bereaved by overdose struggle in accessing support from both formal and informal circles, [15] and may face challenges with housing, finances, social isolation, and legal issues that often accompany loss and contribute to suffering [16, 17]. Bereavement experiences may also vary depending on the relationship to the decedent and the developmental stage of the bereaved individual. While parents who lose a child to overdose have demonstrated higher risk of hospitalization for mental illness [18] and subsequent mortality [19], partners have reported severe financial issues, housing insecurity, and caregiver burden [16], and children who lose a parent have demonstrated increased risk for functional impairment and long-term mental health challenges [3, 12, 20]. Furthermore, structural vulnerability and social inequity related to gender, class, age, sexual identity, and race/ethnicity may contribute to poor bereavement outcomes [17].

Due to the difficult circumstances surrounding a drug poisoning death, including the stigma associated with drug use and feelings of guilt and self-blame, researchers have coined the terms ‘special death’ and ‘special grief’ to describe the particular challenges associated with drug-related loss [7, 8, 15, 21]. Overdose bereavement is also associated with ‘disenfranchised grief,’ which is grief following a loss that is not openly acknowledged, depriving the bereaved of the opportunity to share their experiences [8, 22–24]. Research shows that bereaved persons who lost someone to overdose one to two years ago still have high levels of grief symptoms [25]. In recognition that symptoms of persistent and intense grief differ from symptoms of general anxiety and depression [26], prolonged grief disorder (PGD) – defined as experiencing several symptoms of grief at least 6 months after the loss – was added to the DSM-5 in March 2022 [27]. High levels of stress, feelings of shame, and social isolation increase the risk of developing PGD [28]. Research has found that those bereaved by overdose death are at greater risk for PGD than those who are bereaved from other deaths [5, 6, 29]. Meta analyses found a pooled prevalence rate of PGD of 49% among individuals bereaved by unnatural deaths [9], compared to 10% of those bereaved by natural deaths [30]. Furthermore, a cross-sectional study of overdose-bereaved family members found that 26% suffered from PGD [25, 31]. Witnessing an overdose is associated with a significantly more severe grief response [32]. Importantly, several of these qualitative and epidemiological studies documenting elevated grief severity and poor health outcomes among individuals bereaved by drug-related deaths have been conducted internationally, including in Norway, Scotland, England, and beyond [8, 15, 19, 21, 24, 25, 31, 33], underscoring the global relevance of overdose bereavement.

Bereavement and substance use

There is strong evidence of a positive relationship between bereavement and substance use. Stress-coping theory posits that individuals may engage in substance use as a way of coping with loss by staving off negative emotions [34, 35]. A large cohort study found that parents who lost a child were at significantly higher risk for hospitalization for substance use than parents who had not lost a child [18]. The likelihood of developing a substance use disorder is nearly doubled among young adults who experience multiple deaths in a short period of time [36]. In a study of 196 bereaved people who use drugs, 83% reported increasing drug consumption after suffering the loss [37]. Overdose-bereaved individuals who already use drugs have also reported using more types of substances than usual to manage feelings of grief [38]. Qualitative research also demonstrates the use of drugs to help cope with loss from unnatural causes [33, 38, 39]. In a Norwegian registry study, overdose-bereaved parents had a higher risk of mortality, including death by substance use, than non-bereaved parents and parents bereaved by other causes of death [19].

Bereavement interventions

Social support is often described as an important factor in coping after loss [40, 41]. Several studies have found that social and emotional support following the death of a loved one predicts better grief adaptation and less symptoms of PGD [42–45]. Additionally, motivational interviewing, psychotherapy, and other counseling approaches have been shown to help bereaved individuals cope with loss [46]. Motivational interviewing encourages clients to explore and resolve ambivalence regarding the loss, and is a key component of treatment for individuals with PGD [47]. Support offered by trained professionals has also been shown to help facilitate the grieving process and reduce the negative consequences of bereavement [48–52]. Social workers are particularly well-equipped to support bereaved individuals by providing information and education, social and emotional support, clinical recognition of abnormal bereavement reactions, case management, counseling, psychotherapy, and referral to resources/services [53–55].

In addition to clinical and professionally delivered bereavement interventions, grassroots organizations led by bereaved parents, family members, and people with lived experience of substance use have played a critical role in providing bereavement support. These groups often provide informal peer support, community-based grieving spaces, education, advocacy for expanded services, and drug policy reform [56–60]. Although many of these initiatives operate outside formal funding or evaluation structures, they represent an important and growing component of the response to drug poisoning-related bereavement. The bereavement support program described in this paper operates within a governmental setting and is intended to complement existing community-led and peer-driven efforts.

Bereavement services in medical examiner and coroner (ME/C) settings

Medical examiners and coroners (ME/Cs) are officials responsible for investigating deaths, specifically those that are sudden, suspicious, or unexplained. Deaths referred to ME/Cs are often violent or unexpected (e.g., drug poisonings, homicides, suicides, accidents). Given the traumatic nature of these deaths, ME/C offices are well suited to interact with and deliver services to individuals who are at high risk of more severe grief reactions and maladaptive coping [61, 62]. ME/C offices in the U.S. have begun to recognize their unique positioning to support decedents’ family, friends, and other close peers during bereavement with psychotherapy and support services [63–65], such as by embedding social workers and peer grief support [62, 66, 67]. While one study has documented high utilization of social work support services [62], there are no known published data describing their impact on health outcomes. Moreover, there have been no studies on services offered specifically to people bereaved by drug poisoning.

Theoretical frameworks

The bereavement support program described in this paper is guided by the Social-Ecological Model (SEM), which posits that health and behavioral outcomes are shaped by interactions across multiple levels of influence: individual, interpersonal, community, and societal [68, 69]. At the individual level, people bereaved by drug poisoning may experience intense grief, trauma, mental health challenges, and disruptions to daily functioning that increase vulnerability to poor health outcomes. At the interpersonal level, bereavement occurs within close relationships and social networks; the loss of a loved one may alter family dynamics, reduce sources of emotional or material support, and affect shared environments where drug use or other stressors are present. At the community level, bereaved individuals may face barriers or have limited access to health services, harm reduction resources, or culturally appropriate support systems, particularly in communities affected by economic marginalization or concentrated drug-related harms. At the societal level, structural factors such as stigma surrounding drug use, criminalization, and broader socioeconomic inequalities may further shape how individuals experience and cope with drug poisoning loss. These shared risk environments may contribute not only to the decedent’s drug poisoning but also to the bereaved individual's own vulnerability to poor health outcomes [70]. The SEM informed the design of the bereavement program described in this paper by emphasizing the need for supports that address not only individual grief reactions but also interpersonal relationships, resource access within communities, and structural barriers such as stigma.

Objective

In 2022, the Drug Intelligence and Intervention Group (DIIG) of the New York City (NYC) Office of Chief Medical Examiner (OCME) implemented the Family Support Program (FSP) to address the needs of overdose decedents’ social network members. The FSP incorporates evidence-based intervention components, including motivational interviewing, social support, and linkage to care, to help adults reduce grief symptoms, prevent substance use risks, and improve mental health and other psychosocial functioning following loss to drug poisoning. The purpose of this paper is to describe the development and implementation of the FSP and lessons learned from its pilot phase. This information can be used by other ME/C offices to facilitate the implementation of similar services for the family and friends of the overdose decedents in their jurisdictions.

Methods

Setting

The NYC OCME is the largest medical examiner’s office in the U.S [71] and investigates all suspected overdose deaths in the city. In 2024, there were 2,192 unintentional drug poisoning (overdose) deaths in NYC, down from 3,056 deaths in 2023 [72]. Opioids were involved in 78% of overdose deaths; 73% involved fentanyl [72]. The OCME encounters numerous bereaved individuals during overdose death investigations. In addition to investigators connecting with family and friends at the death scene, the OCME works closely with next-of-kin, emergency contacts, and other social network members of overdose decedents to better understand the circumstances of the death, collect additional information, coordinate burial logistics, and share medical examiners’ findings. Through these efforts, the OCME develops relationships with decedents’ social network members. These points of contact in the aftermath of loss present a unique opportunity to offer support services for people with drug poisoning-related bereavement.

Population

The OCME uses a tool, the Suspected Potential Overdose Tracker (SPOT), to identify suspected overdose deaths in near real-time [73]. The SPOT tool identifies approximately 77% of drug overdose fatalities (i.e., sensitivity) within 72 h of the death. This approach allows the OCME to reach out to social network members soon after the death occurs, eliminating the need to wait for cause and manner of death to be determined to target overdose bereaved individuals. From the casefiles of suspected overdose decedents identified by SPOT, the FSP extracts contact information and reaches out to next-of-kin, emergency contacts, individuals present at the death scene, or others for whom the OCME has obtained contact information. These ‘social network members’ include individuals closely connected to the decedent, including biological family members, chosen family, partners, friends, and others who had personal relationships with the decedent. The FSP does not conduct field-based outreach or attempt to locate individuals beyond those identified in case files. Outreach is conducted via telephone using available contact information. As a result, the program may not reach individuals who lack reliable phone access, potentially limiting engagement with the most structurally vulnerable persons affected by overdose loss. Additionally, the FSP does not reach out to individuals who served in the decedent’s life in a professional capacity (e.g., service providers, employers, landlords, etc.). They also do not reach out to children (persons under 18 years of age), but will work with their parents or guardians to ensure minors are connected to appropriate supports.

Random selection of clients

Given the scale of loss to drug poisoning in NYC, it has not been feasible to offer the FSP to all bereaved individuals without compromising the quality of care provided to network members. To address this, and in anticipation of the need to evaluate the impact of FSP in the future, the OCME implemented a randomized selection process for client outreach. The random selection is done using SAS 9.4 from a weekly pool of decedents who had passed away from suspected overdoses. This strategic decision, atypical for public sector programming, reflects a forward-looking commitment to evaluating the FSP’s impact through rigorous research. Specifically, it enables a comparison of individuals who were selected for the FSP with a Control group selected at random. The use of randomization in client selection enhances the internal validity of effectiveness studies by reducing selection bias and strengthening causal inferences. This random selection of clients will be leveraged in a research study that will be embedded into OCME to evaluate the FSP (described below).

FSP staffing model and caseload structure

The FSP is delivered by a team of licensed social workers who work with overdose-bereaved individuals to identify needs, deliver resources, and connect them to needed services. FSP team members also receive additional training in harm reduction, substance use, and grief support. The FSP team includes a Supervising Social Worker and English- and Spanish-speaking social workers who are well-versed in NYC’s local resources and referral organizations, particularly as they relate to grief, substance use, physical and mental health, housing, child welfare, and social services. The social workers use this knowledge to shepherd clients through these service systems.

Each social worker is assigned seven suspected overdose cases per week. On average, each case involves contact with approximately 1.9 bereaved individuals (see Preliminary Data below), resulting in an estimated 14 new clients per week per social worker. This caseload structure was informed by existing literature on trauma-exposed professionals, which emphasizes the importance of manageable workloads and autonomy in mitigating burnout and promoting staff well-being. The program's scheduling model is designed to offer flexibility and support individualized work styles. Social workers are granted discretion in organizing their weekly schedules, such as by concentrating client outreach to certain days or times of day or distributing calls and documentation evenly across the week. This autonomy fosters a sense of control and adaptability, which is particularly important in emotionally demanding roles such as post-overdose bereavement support.

FSP common areas of need

FSP social workers assume a client-centered approach to outreach, engaging in unstructured, client-led needs assessments. Topics addressed by the FSP include but are not limited to OCME sudden death information and logistics, supportive counseling provided by the social work team, grief and mental health resources or referrals, and social services resources or referrals (Table 1). Although these are some of the most common areas of need, the client-centered approach results in a broad range of topics for assessment, counseling, education, and linkages to services in the community.

FSP procedures

The FSP social workers typically make their initial contact with overdose decedents’ social network members approximately three weeks after a suspected overdose death occurs, most often before cause and manner of death is finalized. The social workers first introduce themselves and explain their role as a social worker at OCME and then turn their focus to the client’s needs and state of mind. FSP social workers take an unstructured approach to assessing client needs, often starting with open-ended questions like “How are you feeling today?” Then, when appropriate, the social workers will use probing, motivational interviewing techniques, and various rapid screening tools to explore the client’s needs and challenges. As needed, social worker will engage in informal, short-term counseling to facilitate emotion processing, reduce situational avoidance, assist with coming to terms with the loss, encourage healthy coping mechanisms, and resolve ambivalence to behavior change. The social worker works with their clients to identify resources and strategies to resolve personal barriers, taking a strengths-based approach as appropriate, to identify their existing supports. As necessary, the social workers make referrals to help meet client needs, as described in Table 1. The social workers use a person-centered approach making sure they prioritize the concerns that are most urgent and important to their client. This person-centered approach emphasizes meeting clients where they are at and comprehensively addressing a wide range of challenges they may be facing, both materially and emotionally. At the conclusion of each call, the social workers and client determine collectively whether additional follow-up calls are necessary. The social worker will continue to conduct follow-up calls as necessary to ensure all needs within their purview are met. As necessary, the social workers also use text messaging and e-mail to reach out and/or follow-up with clients. There is no limit to the number of contacts or the length of time a client may remain engaged in the program.

Because OCME is a governmental agency and operates within the medico-legal death investigation system, some individuals may perceive the FSP as being connected to law enforcement or criminal investigation processes. Thus, some individuals may have concerns about interacting with government-affiliated professionals for fear of potential involvement with the criminal legal system, child protection services, or other authorities. To mitigate these concerns, FSP staff introduce themselves explicitly as social workers during outreach calls and explain that the purpose of the call is supportive rather than investigative. The social workers emphasize that conversations are confidential within the limits of the law and that individuals are free to share only what they feel comfortable discussing. Staff do not attempt to elicit information about criminalized or stigmatized behaviors (e.g., immigration status, active drug use) and instead focus on assessing support needs and providing resources. Despite these efforts, some individuals may remain hesitant to engage or disclose sensitive information with FSP staff.

FSP data collection

The social workers systematically record multiple datapoints during and after each call, including administrative information (e.g., encounter date/time and encounter type such as phone call, text message, or e-mail), information about what services or referrals were provided (if any), and clinical notes from the social worker. Outreach for unsuccessful contact attempts are also recorded. These data are captured in an internal application developed and implemented with data security safeguards by the OCME IT team.

Preliminary data

The OCME began piloting the FSP in September 2022 with one social worker. The program expanded to a team of 7 social workers in September 2023. From September 2023 through April 2025, the team of social workers reached out to 4432 network members. The 4432 network members were identified among 2393 overdose decedents randomly selected for the FSP, averaging 1.9 network members per decedent. At least one network member was reached in 83.6% of deaths. The relationships of the 4432 network members to overdose decedents included siblings (n = 1350, 30.5%), parents (n = 715, 16.1%), children (n = 764, 17.2%), extended family (n = 584, 13.2%), partners or ex-partners (n = 565, 12.8%), and friends or roommates (n = 402, 9.1%). During these 20 months, 6836 calls in total were made between the social workers and the 4432 network members (ingoing and outgoing) and 3054 (68.9%) network members were successfully reached. Of the 3054 network members reached, 2315 (75.8%) received some level of service or referral from the team. Specific services provided by the social workers include but are not limited to supportive counseling (n = 1600, 52.4%), grief or mental health referrals or resources (n = 396,13.0%), social services referrals or resources (n = 288, 9.4%), and OCME sudden death information and logistics (n = 1406, 46.0%). Lessons learned

Early implementation of the program has yielded several key insights and informed iterative refinements to the service design. One notable adaptation involved the timing of outreach to decedents’ network members. Initially, outreach calls were placed approximately two weeks following the overdose death. However, this timeline often coincided with funeral services and other arrangements being made, which limited families’ capacity to engage. As a result, the outreach protocol was adjusted to initiate contact three weeks postmortem, allowing for more appropriate timing and improved receptivity.

Another significant modification pertained to follow-up procedures. The original design included a standardized follow-up call approximately one month after the first outreach. Over time, it became clear that the need for follow-up could be more appropriately determined by the social worker during the initial interaction. Although grief responses may evolve over time, the FSP utilizes a person-centered, needs-based follow-up approach. During the initial interaction, the social worker assesses both immediate needs and potential stressors that may emerge later, and participants are informed that they may re-engage with the program at any time. Rather than implementing automatic follow-ups for all individuals, subsequent contact is determined collaboratively based on expressed needs and preferences. This model aligns with the SEM and stress-coping theory, which emphasize the variability in bereavement trajectories. This approach aims to provide responsive support while respecting autonomy and minimizing potentially intrusive outreach. This change also streamlined service delivery and allowed for more efficient allocation of staff resources.

The pilot phase also highlighted important considerations related to data collection and management. Early experience with the initial data capture system revealed that several variables were not being recorded consistently across social workers because they relied on subjective interpretation or were primarily documented in free-text notes. For example, categories related to client needs (e.g., emotional support, safety concerns) and counseling topics were sometimes interpreted differently by different social workers, leading to variability in how these data were recorded. In addition, some service elements (e.g., referrals provided, counseling topics discussed, follow-up actions) were captured only in narrative fields, which limited the ability to systematically analyze the data. In response, the data management system was revised to include more structured fields, including standardized categories for common client needs and services provided. Radio buttons, checkboxes, and skip logic were incorporated to improve consistency in data entry while still allowing space for narrative notes when appropriate. These changes improved the reliability of data captured across social workers and enhanced the program’s ability to monitor service delivery and evaluate program outcomes. Getting input from the social workers at each stage of the technical design was essential to this process to ensure that data collection systems also met their case management needs.

Early implementation of the program has demonstrated that client reactions to outreach have been overwhelmingly positive. Many individuals are pleasantly surprised that a government agency is proactively offering bereavement support and frequently convey appreciation and gratitude for the call. Negative reactions or concerns about engaging with social workers are relatively rare. While some individuals may initially be cautious, most appear receptive once the purpose of the outreach is explained. This response is likely attributable to the program’s person-centered, trauma-informed approach, in which social workers emphasize empathy, respect, and voluntary engagement, and meet individuals where they are in their bereavement process.

Embedded research study

To assess the impact of the FSP on people bereaved by drug poisoning, we have initiated a research study that aims to (1) characterize the mental health and substance use patterns among drug poisoning-bereaved individuals, and (2) examine the effect of the FSP on their grief severity, substance use, service utilization, and bereavement-related functioning.

The study uses a mixed-methods design combining quantitative surveys and qualitative interviews. Potential participants are identified in OCME investigation records and recruited approximately six months after the drug poisoning death. The study will compare outcomes among individuals who were randomly selected to receive outreach from the FSP (intervention arm) with those who were not selected for the program (control arm). Survey measures include validated instruments assessing grief severity, substance use, depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, coping, and service utilization. We hypothesize that FSP participants will have better health outcomes than control participants.

A subset of participants will also complete in-depth qualitative interviews to better understand bereavement experiences, barriers and facilitators to accessing support, and perspectives on the FSP outreach model. These interviews will provide additional context regarding coping processes and service needs among individuals affected by drug poisoning loss.

The findings from this study will help inform future program refinement and provide evidence on the potential impact of bereavement outreach programs embedded into medical examiner offices. A detailed description of the study protocol will be reported separately.

Discussion

Future directions

At the conclusion of the embedded research study that leverages the control group selected at random, the OCME will be expanding the FSP to reach the social networks of all overdose decedents. By documenting the impact of the FSP, the research study will offer critical data to inform OCME’s service expansion, as well as other ME/C offices interested in adopting this service model.

Although the FSP was developed within the operational context of a medical examiner’s office and did not include formal peer advisory involvement, we recognize that engagement of people with lived and living experience of drug use and drug poisoning bereavement is considered best practice in harm reduction research and service design. The qualitative component of the proposed study is designed to center the voices of bereaved individuals, including those with lived and living experience of drug use, to inform program refinement. Future phases of this work will prioritize the development of a peer advisory structure involving people with lived experience of overdose bereavement and substance use to guide program adaptation, implementation strategies, and interpretation of findings.

Impact

The NYC OCME is at the forefront of reimagining the role of ME/C offices in responding to the ongoing drug poisoning crisis. Embedding supportive services for individuals bereaved by drug poisoning within ME/C offices is a highly innovative approach that has already generated considerable interest in the field, as the OCME has received inquiries about their service model from ME/Cs in several other jurisdictions. Medical examiner offices considering similar bereavement support services should consider the operational and methodological lessons from the FSP experience thus far when designing and implementing their own programs.
