Aortic Valve Calcium Score Spectrum Identifies Bimodal Outcomes for Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation: Insights From the AMTRAC Registry

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDegenerative calcification is the primary mechanism underlying severe aortic stenosis; however, some patients with hemodynamically severe aortic stenosis have low aortic valve calcium scores (AVCS), suggesting heterogeneous disease processes. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics and outcomes across the spectrum of AVCS.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis of the AMTRAC (Aortic+Mitral Transcatheter Valve) registry, including 3766 patients with severe aortic stenosis who underwent transcatheter aortic valve implantation across 16 centers mainly in Europe (2013–2024). Patients were stratified into three groups of low, high, or very high based on their AVCS. The primary end point was 3‐year mortality. Secondary end points included periprocedural outcomes (Valve Academic Research Consortium‐3) and posttranscatheter aortic valve implantation hemodynamics.ResultsPatients with low AVCS more frequently presented with low‐gradient aortic stenosis and advanced New York Heart Association classes. At 3 years, mortality displayed a bimodal pattern in association with AVCS spectrum: 30.5% in the low, 26.5% in the high, and 31.3% in the very‐high AVCS group (P<0.05). Paravalvular leak greater than moderate increased with AVCS severity (0.5%, 0.9%, 1.9%, for low, high, and very‐high AVCS, respectively; P<0.01). The risk for stroke and permanent pacemaker implantation were also higher in the very‐high AVCS group compared with the low/high AVCS groups (stroke: 4.1%, 1.4%, 2.4%; permanent pacemaker: 21.2%, 14.7%, 14.2%; for very‐high, low, high, respectively; P<0.05 for both).ConclusionsLow AVCS defines a unique phenotype marked by predominant fibrosis, advanced symptoms, and increased mortality despite fewer procedural complications. In contrast, very‐high AVCS is linked to higher procedural risk, more bicuspid valves, and increased mortality. These insights challenge calcification‐based criteria and support incorporating fibrosis assessment into treatment strategies.

Severe aortic stenosis (AS) is a progressive valvular disease characterized by left ventricular outflow obstruction, leading to heart failure and increased mortality if left untreated. Traditionally, surgical aortic valve (AV) replacement was the standard treatment; however, transcatheter AV implantation (TAVI) has emerged as a less invasive, safe, and efficacious alternative, particularly in patients at high or intermediate surgical risk. 1 , 2

AV calcification is a key pathological hallmark of AS and plays a crucial role in procedural planning for TAVI. AV calcium burden has been assessed using computed tomography (CT)‐derived Agatston units (AU) scores for the past 2 decades, 3 which correlate with the severity of AS and procedural success. 4 , 5 AV calcification is a hallmark of degenerative AS and is deeply rooted in the pathophysiology of stenosis formation. 6 Robust data are available regarding the association of AV calcium score (AVCS), valve hemodynamics, and prognosis of patients. 7 However, a subset of patients present with severe AS despite a low AVCS, a condition that poses unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. 8 , 9 These patients often exhibit leaflet fibrosis rather than calcification as the principal mechanism of stenosis, leading to underestimation of disease severity using traditional imaging criteria. 10 , 11

Furthermore, patients with low AVCS tend to have distinct clinical characteristics compared with those with heavily calcified valves. They are often older, more frequently female, and exhibit a higher prevalence of concomitant conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease. 11 , 12 The hemodynamic presentation of those patients may also differ, with a tendency toward lower mean transvalvular gradients despite symptomatic severe AS, making diagnosis more complex. The underlying cause in these patients may involve systemic metabolic disorders, inflammatory pathways, and genetic predisposition influencing valve remodeling, rather than the conventional calcific degeneration seen in most AS cases. 9 , 10 , 11

TAVI outcomes in low AVCS remain a subject of ongoing investigation. 11 , 12 Alternative imaging techniques such as contrast‐enhanced CT or advanced echocardiographic parameters have been proposed to better characterize leaflet morphology and improve patient selection for TAVI in this subset. 9 , 10 , 11

The current study aims to evaluate the outcomes of TAVI across different ranges of AVCS, stratified into 3 groups: low, high (classic), and very high (top 25 percentiles). We compared these groups to characterize their distinct clinical profiles and to assess procedural success, hemodynamic improvement, and complication rates. Our goal is to better understand how AVCS influences TAVI outcomes and to inform strategies for optimizing patient selection and procedural planning.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study will be shared upon reasonable request to the corresponding authors.

Study Population

The AMTRAC (Aortic+Mitral Transcatheter Valve) registry is an investigator‐initiated, multicenter registry involving 16 TAVI centers across Europe and Israel. The design and data collection methodology of the registry have been previously described. 10 , 11 For this study, we included consecutive patients who underwent TAVI at the participating centers from January 2013 until October 2024.

All patients with available AVCS measurements were included. Due to the retrospective nature of this registry, the Agatston AVCS method that was available at the registry's conception was chosen rather than more recent AV calcium volume or calcium density. 13 , 14

The patients with previous AV interventions (valve in valve procedure) or those with no postdischarge follow‐up data were excluded from the study.

Standard Protocol Approvals and Registrations

The registry protocol was approved by the local institutional review board as required at each participating center, the registry is listed in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04031274).

CT Imaging and Calcium Score Assessment

Gated‐CT imaging was performed according to the local practice at each center, adhering to standardized TAVI planning protocols. Calcium score scan was performed before the injection of contrast media, using a 3 mm slice thickness. AVCS was assessed using the AU method. The software used for the analysis of Agatston AVCS varied between sites.

Patients were stratified into a low AVCS group based on previously validated and widely accepted thresholds of <1200 AU for women, and <2000 AU for men, recognized by international guidelines. 15 , 16 , 17 Those exceeding these thresholds were classified as having high AVCS (“classic” reference range). Within this group, very high AVCS was defined as sex‐specific upper quartile of the high ACVS group (top 25 percentiles, chosen arbitrarily to examine the hypothesis that AVCS‐related outcomes behave as a spectrum, >3241 AU for women, and >4981 AU for men).

Study End Points

The primary end point was all‐cause mortality at 3 years. Secondary end points included periprocedural outcomes defined by the Valve Academic Research Consortium‐3 criteria, as well as postprocedural valve hemodynamics at discharge.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline and procedural characteristics were summarized as mean±SD or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Comparisons between AVCS groups were performed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and ANOVA with post hoc Bonferroni correction for continuous variables. Cumulative 3‐year all‐cause mortality was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, compared with the log‐rank test.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards modeling was used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for mortality.

For periprocedural complications, logistic regression model was used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for binary outcomes variables.

Ethical Approval and Study Registration

The study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers. The AMTRAC registry is listed on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04031274).

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, and influential observations and potential nonlinearity in covariate effects were assessed using Martingale residuals and their log‐transformed (deviance) residuals, respectively.

RESULTS

A total of 3766 patients were included in the analysis. Patients were categorized into 3 groups based on sex‐specific AVCS thresholds: low AVCS (n=665; mean 1110±504 AU), high AVCS (n=2328; mean 2772±927 AU), and very high AVCS (n=773; mean 5574±1734 AU) as shown in Figure 1.

Patients with very high AVCS were less likely to have a small annulus (<430 mm2) compared with the low and high AVCS groups (41.3% versus 61.9% and 54.7%, respectively; P<0.001). Bicuspid AV anatomy was more prevalent in the very high AVCS group compared with the high and low AVCS groups (7.3%, 3.1%, and 4.1%; P<0.001). Over time, there was a significant shift in the distribution of AVCS categories among patients undergoing TAVI with a higher fraction of high and very high AVCS in more recent practice (P<0.001).

At 3 years, mortality displayed a bimodal pattern in association with AVCS spectrum: 30.5% in the low AVCS group, 26.5% in the high group, and 31.3% in the very high group (P<0.05). DISCUSSION

In this large international cohort of 3766 patients, we examined the association between AVCS and TAVI outcomes. Unlike previous studies, which focused on AVCS as low or high, or as a continuous variable, 18 we further stratified patients in the high AVCS subgroup into high and very high AVCS based on sex‐specific AVCS thresholds. To the best of our knowledge, this is by far the largest cohort in which the association of AVCS using AU measurement and post‐TAVI outcomes was examined. Our main findings are as follows: We found a bimodal association between the AVCS groups and mortality: patients with “classic” reference range—high AVCS—had the best outcomes following TAVI, whereas those with either low or very high AVCS experienced higher 3‐year mortality (Figure 2).We found a linear association between PVL rates and AVCS. Patients with low AVCS had the lowest rate of more than mild PVL (3.5%), whereas those with very high AVCS had the highest (10.4%) (Figure 3, Table 2).In the very high AVCS group (compared with the low and high AVCS groups), higher rates of periprocedural complications, including stroke and permanent pacemaker implantation were noted (Table 2).

Data on the association between AVCS and TAVI outcomes are limited. Studies by Abramowitz et al. 1 and Juhász et al., 2 which included 470 patients (mean follow‐up 12.6±8.1 months) and 327 patients (median follow‐up 4.9 [2.0–5.8] years), respectively, used a 2‐tier classification of AVCS (low versus high) and reported no significant differences in mortality between low and high AVCS groups.

The present study employed a novel 3‐tier stratification (low, high, and very high AVCS), as we examined the hypothesis that AVCS behaves differently across the AVCS spectrum; therefore, we further separate high AVCS into distinct high and very high categories. Accordingly, the present study analysis revealed significantly worse survival rates among patients with low and with very high AVCS. This granular stratification uncovers differences that are obscured when patients with high and very high AVCS are combined into a single group, as done in earlier studies, where comparatively better prognosis of the high AVCS group may “dilute” the poor outcomes of the very high AVCS subgroup.

Patients with AVCS values in the higher range often exhibit extensive valve calcification, which can increase procedural complexity during TAVI and may contribute to worse clinical outcomes. 19 , 20 The poorer outcomes observed in the very high AVCS group in our study, compared with the classic “reference range” high AVCS group, may be attributed to the higher prevalence of bicuspid AV anatomy and the increased rate of periprocedural complications, including stroke and more than mild PVL. These findings are supported by a previous study by Foley et al., which demonstrated that higher AVCSs were associated with an increased risk of stroke in patients undergoing TAVI. 21 Furthermore, we found that patients in the very high AVCS group had a higher rate of permanent pacemaker implantation post TAVI, compared with the other 2 groups. This finding is supported by a recent study of 761 patients, which demonstrated that an AVCS >4510 AU was significantly more common among those requiring permanent pacemaker implantation after TAVI procedure. 22

At the lower end of the AVCS spectrum, the disease appears to follow a distinct pathophysiological course, driven predominantly by valvular fibrosis rather than calcification. In our study, patients with low AVCS demonstrated a unique clinical profile, more advanced symptoms despite lower transvalvular gradients and, paradoxically, worse outcomes despite fewer procedural complications such as PVL. These findings align with those of Abramowitz et al., 1 who also reported a higher prevalence of diabetes and lower PVL rates in this group, without significant differences in procedural complications. However, to the best of our knowledge, the increased mortality observed in our study in patients with low AVCS has not been previously reported.

Several factors may underlie this observation, including greater myocardial damage, diagnostic and referral delays, and a higher burden of comorbidities such as diabetes. Notably, patients in the low AVCS group had a significantly higher rate of advanced symptoms (New York Heart Association Class III–IV: 65.0%) compared with those with high and very high AVCS (59.5% and 59.0%, respectively; P=0.03), suggesting a possible role of underlying metabolic or inflammatory mechanisms, or diffuse myocardial fibrosis. Delayed referral due to uncertainty regarding AS severity in the setting of low AVCS may also contribute to the higher symptom burden and mortality in this group.

AS is a heterogeneous disease characterized by progressive fibrocalcific remodeling of the AV. This remodeling is driven by genetic factors, lipoprotein deposition and oxidation, chronic inflammation, metabolic abnormalities, and osteoblastic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells. 17 Clinical factors such as sex, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, systemic inflammatory conditions, and rheumatic disease further influence the extent and nature of valve damage. 11 , 23 , 24 Histological studies have identified a more fibrotic phenotype in specific patient subgroups. 11 , 23 Diabetes, in particular, exacerbates valve inflammation that may shift remodeling toward fibrosis over calcification. 25 Consistent with these findings, we found that patients with low AVCS had a higher prevalence of diabetes, suggesting that fibrosis and inflammation may be dominant drivers in this subgroup.

These insights underscore the critical need for refined risk stratification and the development of alternative imaging markers to better characterize disease severity in patients with AS and low AVCS. Current calcium‐based thresholds may inadequately reflect disease burden in this group, leading to underrecognition of severe AS and delayed intervention, which may partly explain their worse outcomes. Alternatively, the observation of lower survival benefit despite a similar rate of procedural success suggests that other factors, beyond AS itself, may determine the clinical course in these patients. Better characterization of the low AVCS group to identify those not likely to benefit from TAVI will optimize patient triage and help avoid futile interventions.

Future studies should explore the role of adjunctive imaging techniques, such as contrast‐enhanced CT 26 or advanced tissue characterization with echocardiography, to improve patient selection within this cohort. Additionally, optimizing the timing of TAVI in patients with low AVCS is crucial. Given their higher symptom burden and increased mortality despite technically successful procedures, early intervention before the onset of irreversible myocardial damage could improve long‐term outcomes. Further research is warranted to determine whether alternative treatment strategies, including earlier TAVI or medical therapies targeting fibrosis, could positively alter the disease trajectory in this unique subset of patients with AS.

Our study has several strengths. First, it is an international, multiethnic registry that includes data from 16 TAVI centers, which enhance its generalizability and provides insights into a diverse patient population. Additionally, it represents the largest cohort to date exploring the clinical significance of severe AS with AV calcium below the threshold typically associated with severe AS (patients with low AVCS), thereby adding valuable and reliable data to the existing smaller studies on this topic. The inclusion of recent procedures up to 2024 further strengthens the relevance of our findings, as it reflects current clinical practice and the latest advancements in TAVI technology. Moreover, our study provides long‐term insights by offering an extended follow‐up period of 3 years post‐TAVI, contributing valuable information on patient outcomes and the durability of the intervention. Finally, by including “all‐comers” patients treated under real‐world clinical conditions, our study offers a more comprehensive and generalizable assessment of TAVI outcomes.

Limitation

Although this study provides novel insights into the interplay between AV calcification, fibrosis, and TAVI outcomes, it has several limitations. The retrospective design, although allowing for a large cohort, limits our ability to control for all potential confounding factors, which could introduce biases in patient selection and data interpretation. Additionally, the absence of a dedicated core laboratory for image and data analysis may lead to variability in measurements and interpretations, particularly regarding AV calcium and AS severity, which could affect the consistency and accuracy of clinical assessments. Although the scan protocol for calcium scoring was standardized, AU measurement was performed on software from numerous vendors, which might cause inconsistent AU reporting. Data regarding baseline ECG and diabetes severity are lacking in the AMTRAC registry. Similarly, data on preballoon aortic valvuloplasty and the use of embolic protection devices were unavailable. However, given that current evidence indicates that embolic protection device use does not significantly reduce periprocedural stroke rates, 27 we do not believe that variability in embolic protection devices use explains the higher rate of periprocedural stroke observed in the very high AVCS group. Hemodynamic data during follow‐up were also only partially available, as many patients were followed in their referring centers rather than at the sites where the TAVI procedure was performed. Therefore, hemodynamic outcomes were reported at discharge only. Furthermore, additional diagnostic tests for myocardial damage or fibrosis were not available and should be further evaluated in future studies. Finally, AVCS is a “basket” index that does not capture the granularity in calcium distribution across the valve. For example, a bulky spotty annular calcium nodule or a heavily calcified bicuspid raphe may have different implications compared with diffuse leaflet calcification.

Conclusions

Our study introduces a novel 3‐tier AVCS stratification in patients undergoing TAVI and highlights a bimodal association between the AVCS spectrum and midterm mortality. Low AVCS identifies a distinct clinical entity characterized predominantly by fibrosis of the valve, more advanced symptoms, and unexpectedly poor outcomes despite less procedural complications such as PVL and stroke.
