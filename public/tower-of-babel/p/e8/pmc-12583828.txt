Calculation and analysis of passive earth pressure under ultimate stress conditions
Authors: Guangxiu Liu, Faning Dang, Xu Wang, Yugen Li, Wen Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper develops a new method for calculating the passive earth pressure (PEP) on retaining walls under ultimate stress conditions. First, it is assumed that when the sliding wedge is in the limit equilibrium state, the soil elements on the slip surface, at the wall-soil interface, and within the wedge all reach the ultimate stress state obeying the Mohr–Coulomb criterion. The trajectory of principal stresses within the wedge takes the form of a circular arc. Subsequently, the PEP calculation equation for the retaining wall under ultimate stress conditions is derived based on the circular arc thin-layer unit method, with the unit obtained by layering along the principal stress trajectory. Furthermore, a formula for calculating the maximum friction angle (δmax) at the wall-soil interface is proposed under passive conditions. The influence of the wall-soil interface friction angle on the distribution form, magnitude, resultant force action point of PEP, and overturning moment at the base of the retaining wall is then analyzed. Additionally, the stress state of soil elements within the sliding wedge is determined according to the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion. Finally, the proposed method was validated against numerical simulations and model test data. The PEP under ultimate stress conditions represents the plastic upper-bound solution, while Coulomb’s earth pressure serves as the plastic lower-bound solution, providing new insights for accurate assessment of PEP. The maximum wall-soil interface friction angle formulation established in this study offers a theoretical basis for determining the interface friction angle under passive conditions, particularly resolving the selection of interface friction angle when the backfill has a large internal friction angle (φ > 30°).Keywords: Retaining walls, Passive earth pressure, Principal stress trajectory, Ultimate stress state, Wall-soil interface friction angleSubject terms: Civil engineering, Natural hazards

Introduction

The calculation of earth pressure (EP) in retaining structures, a classical problem in geotechnical engineering, has attracted considerable attention. Since the famous Coulomb’s theory1 was proposed, EP calculation has remained a hot topic of research for engineers. The classical EP theories of Rankine2 and Coulomb1 are still widely used in engineering design due to their clear mechanical concepts and simple calculation methods. However, both theories have certain drawbacks: (1) Rankine’s theory is only applicable to cases where the retaining wall’s back is vertical and smooth, and the fill surface is horizontal, limiting its applicability; (2) While Coulomb’s EP theory can accommodate more complex boundary conditions, it assumes a linear distribution of EP with depth along the retaining wall. However, numerous indoor tests and field observations show that the EP distribution along the depth is nonlinear3–9.

Terzaghi10 demonstrated the existence of the soil arching effect through a movable gate test, describing it as the deflection and redistribution of stress in the soil in his later work11, and pointing out the nonlinear nature of EP. To explore analytical methods that reflect the nonlinear distribution characteristics of lateral EP on retaining walls, Кarah12 developed the horizontal differential layer analysis method to solve the nonlinear distribution of EP. Handy13 described the soil arching effect by approximating the minor principal stress trajectories as suspended chain lines. Using the horizontal differential layering method, he developed a method to calculate both the lateral EP coefficient and the nonlinear distribution of EP. Wang14 proposed a calculation method for the nonlinear distribution of EP based on Coulomb’s slip surface, using the static equilibrium of differential units. However, this approach does not account for the soil arching effect. Paik et al.15 assumed a circular minor principal stress trajectory and developed a new formula for the nonlinear distribution of active EP. This formula incorporates the soil arching effect and applies the horizontal differential layering method. Additionally, Goel et al.16 and Peng et al.17 assumed a parabolic shape for the soil arch. Based on these assumptions, many researchers have made several improvements to both the horizontal differential layering and soil arching methods18–25. However, there is a common drawback when solving for EP using the horizontal differential layering method. It is challenging to accurately consider the action of tangential forces on horizontal thin-layer units. In practical engineering, the backs of walls are often not smooth, making friction between the wall and the soil unavoidable. According to the shear stress reciprocity theorem, shear stress must exist on the horizontal thin-layer unit. Ignoring the shear stress between soil layers or making approximations can lead to deviations in the distribution of EP from its real behavior and result in inaccurate estimations of the resultant force’s point of application. Therefore, Cao et al.26 and Zhang et al.27 proposed the study method of using circular arc thin-layer units along the principal stress trajectories to eliminate the influence of shear stress on the force analysis of thin-layer units. During the force analysis at the upper and lower interfaces of the thin-layer unit, Cao et al.26 assumed that major principal stresses increase linearly, while Zhang et al.27 assumed that minor principal stresses increase linearly. However, the justification for these assumptions regarding the linear increase of principal stresses with depth lacks sufficient validation.

Additionally, Coulomb’s theory considers the sliding wedge as a rigid body and does not account for the stress state of the soil elements. Rankine’s theory applies only when the back of the wall is vertical and smooth, under which conditions the soil elements behind the wall reach a state of ultimate stress. However, when the back of the retaining wall is rough, there are few definitive conclusions or well-founded arguments in the existing literature regarding the stress state of soil elements within the sliding wedge28–30. Furthermore, the idea that the soil elements within the sliding wedge reach the ultimate stress state in the soil arching method has been questioned in recent years. Liu et al.31 contended that, under active conditions, the soil elements near the wall-soil interface reach their ultimate stress state, while the soil along the sliding plane does not. Wang et al.32 argued that the soil along the sliding plane reaches the ultimate stress state, while the soil within the wedge and behind the wall does not. Moreover, some numerical studies have shown that for a rigid wall under active transfer mode, the soil elements reach the ultimate stress state at both the slip surface and the wall-soil interface, while the soil elements remain in an elastic state within the wedge body25,33. However, based on discrete element simulations of rigid retaining walls, Zhang et al.34 suggest that, under the translational displacement mode, some soil elements within the sliding wedge attain the ultimate stress state, and under the rotation mode around the bottom of the wall, all the soil elements reach the ultimate stress state. Therefore, the unified analysis of the stress state and static equilibrium of the soil behind the retaining wall requires further investigation.

Based on the above analyses, further investigation is needed into the distribution characteristics of PEP on retaining walls and the stress state of the backfill behind the wall. This study proposes a calculation method for PEP on retaining walls under ultimate stress conditions, building on existing research. First, considering the soil arching effect of the sliding wedge, it is assumed that when the sliding wedge reaches the limit equilibrium state, the soil elements on the slip surface, at the wall-soil interface, and within the wedge body all reach the ultimate stress state obeying the Mohr–Coulomb criterion35,36. Additionally, the trajectory of major principal stresses within the wedge takes the form of a circular arc. The sliding wedge is then divided into multiple circular arc thin-layer units along the major principal stress trajectories. Using the static equilibrium principle, the PEP on the retaining wall under ultimate stress conditions is derived through force analysis of the thin-layer units. Additionally, current research indicates that under active limit equilibrium conditions, the maximum wall-soil interface friction angle (δmax) typically approaches the internal friction angle (φ) of the backfill when the wall back is rough. However, under passive conditions, δmax is generally lower than φ. The theoretical rationale for this discrepancy in interface friction behavior between active and passive states remains unclear. To address this, we derived a computational formulation for δmax based on the stress state of the backfill behind retaining walls under passive conditions and the Mohr–Coulomb criterion. Subsequently, the influence of the wall-soil interface friction angle on the magnitude, distribution of PEP, location of the resultant force, and the overturning moment at the wall’s base is analyzed. Moreover, the stress state of soil elements within the sliding wedge is determined using the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion. Finally, the proposed method is validated through numerical simulations and comparison with model test data. Moreover, this study improves the calculation of interface forces on the upper and lower boundaries of the circular arc thin-layer unit, using the gravitational characteristics of the soil behind the wall. This improvement assumes a linear variation of vertical stress magnitude with depth at the layered interface37, contrasting with Zhang et al.27 assumption of a linear increase in minor principal stress at the same interface.

Consequently, these results are not further discussed in Figs. 10, 11, 12.

From Fig. 12, it can be observed that as the wall-soil interface friction angle increases, the overturning moment at the bottom of the wall first increases and then decreases in both this study and the study by Hou et al.18. In contrast, Zhang et al.27 show that the overturning moment at the bottom of the wall initially increases and then gradually levels off. As mentioned earlier, due to the frictional effect of the wall-soil interaction, the PEP exhibits a “concave” non-linear distribution with depth. As δ increases, the “concave” non-linear distribution of σw becomes more pronounced, resulting in a lower application position of the EP resultant force. Despite the increase in the horizontal resultant force of PEP with increasing δ, the effect of the lowered application position of the EP resultant force causes the calculated overturning moment in this study to be significantly smaller than Coulomb’s results.

Discussion on stress state of soil behind wall

According to the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion, the stress state of soil elements within the sliding wedge can be assessed by comparing the EP in this study with Coulomb’s EP: ① If the EP in this study is less than Coulomb’s, it indicates that the stress state within the sliding wedge has reached the plastic failure state in passive limit equilibrium; ② If the EP in this study is equal to (or close to) Coulomb’s, it indicates that the stress state within the sliding wedge is at (or near) the ultimate stress state in passive limit equilibrium; ③ If the EP in this study exceeds Coulomb’s, it indicates that the stress state within the sliding wedge has not yet reached the ultimate stress state in passive limit equilibrium.

According to Fig. 11, Coulomb’s EP resultant force is the outer envelope of the resultant force of the PEP under ultimate stress conditions. Based on the above discriminant criteria, the stress state of soil elements within the sliding wedge in passive limit equilibrium is as follows: when the back of the retaining wall is smooth (δ = 0°), the results of this study align with Coulomb’s EP, indicating that the soil elements are in a passive ultimate stress state, consistent with classical Rankine’s EP theory. When the back is rough (δ > 0°), the EP calculated in this study is less than Coulomb’s, suggesting that the soil elements have reached a plastic failure stress state. Moreover, the degree of plasticity increases with wall roughness.

For further analysis, it is necessary to introduce and elaborate on the passive failure mechanism of the retaining wall. When a retaining wall experiences displacement or rotation due to external forces, the soil behind the wall transitions from a state of rest to a passive state. As this displacement or rotation increases, the EP gradually rises from the initial static earth pressure (E0). Once a failure plane forms within the soil mass, the soil behind the wall reaches a passive limit equilibrium state, and the EP attains its maximum value, referred to as PEP (Ep). According to the passive failure mechanism of retaining walls and the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion, the stress state of soil elements within the sliding wedge transitions from the initial static stress state to the passive limit equilibrium state, ultimately reaching a plastic failure state. The PEP under ultimate stress conditions proposed in this study can be defined as the plastic upper-bound solution, while Coulomb’s EP represents the lower-bound solution.

Using the circular arc thin-layer unit method, an equation for calculating PEP on retaining walls under ultimate stress conditions was established. The proposed method was validated through numerical simulations and comparison with model test data.
