Patient satisfaction and oral health-related quality of life for prefabricated teeth versus CAD-CAM milled acrylic resin denture teeth. A crossover clinical trial
Authors: Heba Wageh Abozaed, Sarah Abdelaal Ali, Aisha Zakaria Mostafa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial teeth replicate the appearance of natural teeth and restore proper alignment, oral function, and speech. Over the years, dental materials and prosthetic design advancements have transformed the creation and effectiveness of complete denture teeth. This evolution showcases a continued commitment to developing materials and methods that enhance the comfort, functionality, and visual appeal for patients without teeth. This study evaluated and compared patient satisfaction and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQL) between prefabricated and computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) milled acrylic resin denture teeth.Materials and methodsThis crossover clinical study included 10 completely edentulous patients rehabilitated with complete dentures (CDs). Two types of acrylic resin teeth were used: prefabricated and CAD-CAM-milled artificial teeth. Crossover design is a research method that minimizes variability among participants by assessing the impact of various treatments on the same individual. This methodological approach facilitates the identification and measurement of the treatment effects. Following six months of complete denture wear, patient satisfaction and oral health-related quality of life were evaluated using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-EDENT-19). Statistical analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test in SPSS software, with statistical significance established at P < 0.05.ResultsThere was no statistical difference between the groups in the total VAS scores and items, except for chewing efficiency, with a P-value of 0.035, where CAD-CAM milled resin teeth had statistically higher scores. In contrast, prefabricated teeth had a statistically higher aesthetic score (p = 0.033). Regarding OHIP-EDENT-19, many items showed no statistical differences between the two groups, except for functional limitations and difficulty chewing, which had a P-value of 0.05 and were statistically higher in prefabricated teeth. In contrast, psychological discomfort (P = 0.034) and social disability (P = 0.046) had significantly higher scores for the CAD-CAM milled teeth.ConclusionThis study suggests that prefabricated and CAD-CAM milled resin teeth are viable treatment options for complete dentures. Prefabricated teeth are advisable when aesthetics is the patient’s primary concern. Conversely, CAD-CAM milled teeth are preferable when chewing efficiency is the primary concern.Clinical trial registrationRegistered at www.clinicaltrials.gov: (NCT06179615) (11-12-2023).

Background

The most common method for treating complete tooth loss is the fabrication of complete dentures. Different therapeutic approaches have been recommended to restore natural appearance and function. Complete denture rehabilitation remains popular for edentulous patients with systemic, anatomical, or economic limitations [1].

Crossover design is an effective research method, especially in clinical trials, in which every participant undergoes all treatments being studied in a different sequence. This approach offers several advantages; it significantly reduces variability between subjects and minimizes the influence of confounding factors like age and gender. As a result, increased precision leads to greater statistical power with fewer participants than parallel-group designs. This makes crossover studies especially useful in situations where recruiting participants is challenging or when focusing on individual treatment effects. For the same sample size, crossover designs usually provide more statistical power to identify a treatment effect than parallel designs, as the variance within individuals is typically less than between subjects [2, 3].

Digital complete dentures are created by scanning definitive impressions or casts along with a maxillomandibular record. This information is then used in the software to design the denture base and arrange the artificial teeth. The denture is then manufactured using additive methods, such as 3D printing, or subtractive methods, like computerized numerical control milling [4, 5].

Computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) technologies provide several benefits in the creation of complete dentures. These technologies reduce the discomfort associated with traditional impression techniques, enhance processing accuracy, and cut down both chairside and laboratory times. Additionally, they lower chair time and clinical appointment costs. The digitized process also improves the visualization and identification of edentulous arches and decreases polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) shrinkage during polymerization [1, 5–8].

Artificial teeth should closely replicate the appearance and anatomical structure of natural teeth. The materials employed must be biocompatible with oral soft tissues, insoluble in saliva, lightweight, and possess robust mechanical properties to endure masticatory forces without fracturing. Various options exist for artificial denture teeth, including traditional and modified acrylic resin, high cross-linked acrylic resin, composite resin teeth, and porcelain artificial teeth [9, 10].

Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) is a widely utilized acrylic material due to its ease of manufacture, cost-effectiveness, low weight, superior biocompatibility, aesthetic attributes, stability within the oral environment, insolubility in bodily fluids, relative ease of manipulation, color stability, characterization, and glossy surface post-polishing. However, PMMA denture resins exhibit certain drawbacks, such as suboptimal surface properties and inadequate mechanical parameters, including flexural strength, hardness, fracture, and impact strength [10, 11].

In contrast to traditional polymerization methods, prefabricated polymeric blanks are polymerized under standardized conditions at elevated temperatures and pressures to enhance their material properties. CAD-CAM PMMA-based polymers have the potential to reduce residual monomer release, improve color stability and optical qualities, and facilitate prosthesis production through straightforward machining processes [12, 13]. When evaluating wear resistance and surface hardness, CAD-CAM milled acrylic resin teeth have demonstrated superiority over prefabricated acrylic resin artificial teeth [14].

Patient satisfaction is a critical objective following complete denture treatment, influenced by factors such as socioeconomic status, age, gender, number of post-delivery appointments, previous experience, oral condition, and patient-dentist interaction [15, 16]. OHRQoL assesses the impact of oral health on an individual’s daily life, living conditions, and social functioning [17]. The OHIP questionnaire is the most widely employed tool for evaluating OHRQoL [18].

Several systematic evaluations comparing CAD-CAM-milled dentures with traditional CDs showed that CAD-CAM CDs are comparable or superior to traditional dentures [19–21]. However, the studies on patient satisfaction between 3D-printed and traditional dentures are inconclusive [22, 23].

Notably, no study to date has directly compared patient-reported outcomes for denture teeth alone using a crossover design. Consequently, our clinical study aimed to assess patient satisfaction and quality of life in prefabricated and CAD-CAM-milled acrylic complete denture artificial teeth. The null hypothesis stated that there would be no correlation between patient satisfaction and quality of life with complete denture artificial teeth, whether prefabricated or CAD-CAM milled.

Materials and methods

This clinical, randomized, prospective, double-blind, crossover study compared two complete dentures constructed with two different types of acrylic resin artificial teeth: prefabricated and CAD-CAM milled teeth, to assess patient satisfaction and oral health-related quality of life. The study procedures were carried out at the prosthodontic department. The Declaration of Helsinki’s ethical guidelines were adhered to in this study. The ethics committee at the Faculty of Dentistry at Mansoura University approved this clinical study (number A11080622). This study was registered as a clinical trial. Gov ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06179615.

All participants signed an informed consent form after being informed of the study methods. Two separate researchers conducted the therapy and follow-up without knowing the groups to which the participants were assigned. Similarly, the participants were unaware of their group assignments. An individual who was unaware of the treatment groups performed the randomization. This process uses randomly generated numbers created in an Excel spreadsheet. The sample size was determined using G*Power Software (version 3.1.9.7) with an α error of 0.05, 80% power, and an effect size of 1.2. Based on prior literature [24], the crossover study design employed a sample size of 10 participants. Following the construction of the two complete dentures, five participants initially received complete dentures with prefabricated teeth (Group I), while the remaining five participants were provided with CAD-CAM-milled artificial teeth (Group II).

The criteria established for all ten patients included a positive cooperative attitude and healthy, firm oral mucosa, devoid of severe mucosal diseases such as candidiasis, hyperplasia, or malignancies. Additionally, the patients did not exhibit medical conditions that could affect treatment, including mental illness, Parkinson’s disease, allergies to denture materials, or an inability to perform self-care [25, 26].

Prosthetic procedure

Patients received their complete dentures in a crossover manner. Half of the patients were initially provided with complete dentures featuring prefabricated teeth for six months, while the other half received a second complete denture with CAD-CAM-milled teeth for an additional six months. Following a scheduled two-week washout period [27], the remaining patients received their complete dentures in the opposite sequence. This methodology was designed to mitigate potential carryover effects between treatments.

Conventional dentures with prefabricated acrylic resin teeth were constructed by obtaining definitive impressions of the maxilla and mandible using a zinc oxide eugenol-free impression material (Cavex Outline; Cavex Co., Haarlem, Netherlands). After pouring the impressions, the resulting master castings were digitized and duplicated for the remaining dentures using CAD-CAM-milled teeth. Maxillomandibular relations were recorded and transferred to a semi-adjustable articulator (Whipmix Corp., Louisville, Kentucky, USA). Prefabricated teeth from Yamahachi Dental Manufacturing (Yamahachi Dental Mfg Company, Japan) were used as conventional dentures. A wax denture was constructed employing a bilateral balanced occlusal scheme. The waxed denture was fitted in the patient’s mouth to verify the correct positioning of the teeth for evaluating occlusion, vertical dimension, phonetics, and aesthetic profile. Technical errors were rectified. The dentures were constructed using compression molding with heat-cured acrylic resin (Acrostone, heat-cured acrylic resin, Egypt). The dentures were then finished and polished (Fig. 2) [14].

For standardization, the denture bases of the two dentures (prefabricated and CAD-CAM-milled teeth) were identical. Complete mandibular and maxillary dentures, as well as master casts, were coated with a thin layer of anti-glare spray (Digi-scan Spray Art.-Nr. 581 − 0300) and scanned using a 3D desk scanner (Auto Scan-DS-EX, Lab Scanner). The CAD-CAM Exo-cad Dental IDB 2.4 plovdiv7290 program (version 2.4 Engine build 72900 was used to import output data (STL files). Complete dentures with CAD-CAM-milled teeth were prepared using these files (Fig. 3A, B).

CAD-CAM denture teeth were created using a milling machine (Model MDX-50, DGSHAPE, Japan) and polymethyl methacrylate blanks (XTCERA, China) with a length of 16 mm and a diameter of 98 mm. The teeth were then polished. Temporary material was printed in 3D (RAS-DENT 3D printer, RASPART.eg) and converted to denture bases using 3D printing resin for dental casting (Ultra resins, D-CAST, EGE dental industry, Turkey) with the desired slice thickness. The printed bases were flasked to fabricate heat-cured acrylic resin denture bases (Acrostone, heat-cured acrylic resin, Egypt). The CAD-CAM-milled teeth were inserted into the corresponding recesses in the heat-cured acrylic resin denture base, and a self-cured bonder (Ivocad Bond, Ivoclar Vivodent, Liechtenstein) was used to attach the teeth to the denture base (Figs. 3C, D, E, and F). Finally, the dentures were finished and polished (Fig. 4) [14].

Evaluation of patient satisfaction (VAS) and oral health impact profile (OHIP-EDENT-19)

Six months after wearing each complete denture, the patients completed the OHIP-19 and a patient satisfaction questionnaire. The questionnaire was divided into two sections: (1) VAS [28, 29].

(2) OHRQoL using the OHIP-EDENT-19 [28, 30, 31]. The questions were translated into Arabic using Google Translate (computer assistance). The questions were administered by an interviewer.

Regarding the VAS, participants were asked eight questions to score their answers related to mandibular overdentures. Responses were evaluated on a Likert scale from 1 to 5 (1 = highly unsatisfied, 2 = practically none satisfied; 3 = indifferent; 4 = satisfied; and 5 = extremely satisfied). The patient’s dental and OHRQoL were assessed using 19 questions divided into seven categories: functional restrictions, physical pain, psychological discomfort, physical problems, psychological restrictions, social disability, and Handicap. Responses were scored from 1 to 5 on a Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = virtually none, 3 = rare, 4 = regular, and 5 = extremely frequent). Lower scores indicated better OHRQoL, whereas higher scores indicated worse OHRQoL [32].

Statistical analysis

Non-parametric data were organized to compare the two complete denture types, and descriptive analysis was conducted using the median (minimum-maximum) and percentage. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed to assess statistically significant differences between the two dependent groups. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 23, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

The study included ten participants, equally divided between males and females, aged 55 to 65 years, with a mean age of 60 ± 5 years. Participants’ educational backgrounds varied, with two having higher education, six having secondary education, and two having primary education. Due to the motivation for this brief evaluation period, all participants attended regular appointments without any withdrawals.

Regarding the VAS, participants were asked to grade their questionnaire responses on a Likert scale of 1–5, with 1 indicating the worst response. This study revealed a significant increase in chewing efficiency with CAD-CAM-milled artificial teeth in complete dentures compared to prefabricated teeth (P = 0.035). However, patients reported significantly higher satisfaction with aesthetics in the prefabricated group than in the CAD-CAM milled group (P = 0.033). The other items in the VAS and total satisfaction showed no significant differences between the two types of artificial teeth (Table 1; Fig. 5).

The OHRQoL and OHIP-EDENT-19 questionnaires involved seven domains: functional limitation (FL), physical pain (PP), physical disability (PD1), psychological discomfort (PD2), psychological disability (PD3), social disability (SD), and handicap (H) (Table 2). Each domain included 2–4 questions (items). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two artificial tooth types used in this study in most of the items, including physical pain (PP), physical disability (PD2), and psychological disability (PD3). In examining functional limitations, specifically the difficulty in masticating food, prefabricated teeth exhibited a statistically significant prevalence over CAD-CAM milled teeth (P = 0.05). Additionally, some psychological discomfort associated with self-consciousness about dentures was statistically greater for CAD-CAM-milled teeth than for prefabricated teeth (P = 0.034). Social disability, where patients were irritable with other people, was statistically more common in the CAD-CAM milled teeth group (P = 0.046). Finally, handicap items, especially felt that life in general was statistically less satisfying in CAD-CAM-milled teeth when compared to prefabricated teeth (P = 0.039).

Discussions

Patient satisfaction has been shown to have a significant influence on the impact of dental management and prosthetics. As indicated by the extant literature, it was asserted that this objective constituted the primary ambition of prosthodontic rehabilitation for edentulism [33].

The design, methodology, and patient selection process of this clinical study aimed to eliminate any potential influences on the study findings. Consequently, a crossover design was chosen, and the dentures were fabricated in the same dental laboratory, reducing variation between patients’ dentures [34].

In contrast to longitudinal study designs, crossover studies allow participants to experience all treatment options, facilitating personal comparisons [35]. Furthermore, crossover trials necessitate fewer participants than parallel group trials [36]. These within-subject crossover studies also contribute to the standardization of factors related to subjects and dentures, thereby enabling more precise comparisons of patient satisfaction with each prosthesis.

The results of this questionnaire-based clinical study showed no significant differences between prefabricated complete denture teeth and CAD-CAM milled teeth in most of the assessed items of patient satisfaction and oral health-related quality of life. This outcome aligns with a previous study that reported no significant difference in satisfaction scores between patients rehabilitated with CAD-CAM-fabricated complete dentures and those with conventional complete dentures. However, the overall satisfaction score for CAD-CAM milled dentures tended to be positive [37].

In this study, the comparison of patient satisfaction (VAS) revealed a significant difference between milled and prefabricated teeth concerning the restoration of chewing efficiency, which was enhanced by improving the mechanical properties of the teeth. This agrees with the results of Srinivasan et al., [38] who observed that CAD-CAM resins demonstrated superior mechanical characteristics, including greater elastic modulus, ultimate strength, and toughness. Dentures constructed using milling machines were milled from resin blocks, and their physical attributes and compatibility are purported to be superior to those of conventional complete dentures. Additionally, in this study, the improved chewing efficiency can be attributed to the superior wear resistance and surface hardness of CAD-CAM milled complete denture acrylic resin teeth compared to prefabricated complete denture acrylic resin artificial teeth [14].

The enhanced chewing efficiency of CAD-CAM-milled teeth can be attributed to their improved mechanical properties. According to Stawarczyk et al., PMMA resin milled with CAD-CAM exhibited less wear than conventionally made PMMA resin. PMMA blocks for CAD-CAM are industrially polymerized in an ideal manufacturing environment, featuring enhanced surface characteristics that resist wear and exhibit good mechanical qualities, including strength [39]. Wear resistance was reported to be higher in milled teeth compared to prefabricated or 3D-printed denture teeth by Kim et al. [24]

In contrast, in this study, the comparison of patient satisfaction (VAS) revealed a significant difference between milled and prefabricated teeth regarding aesthetics. Prefabricated teeth exhibited a superior appearance compared to CAD-CAM-milled teeth. This finding is consistent with a previous study that demonstrated CAD-CAM milled teeth have lower aesthetics than conventional denture teeth, potentially due to differences in the manufacturing process [32]. Although 3D-printed artificial teeth share the same physical properties as resin teeth, differing only in their uniform color, prefabricated artificial teeth more closely resemble natural teeth, exhibiting multiple areas of translucency and distinct optical features [27].

In this study, the comparison of the OHRQoL revealed that there was a significant difference between milled and prefabricated teeth regarding psychological discomfort and irritability with other people, potentially attributable to aesthetic appearance.

The study found that overall patient satisfaction with complete dentures using CAD-CAM-milled denture teeth was not different from that using prefabricated denture teeth. This may be due to the higher aesthetic appeal of prefabricated denture teeth, whereas chewing efficiency was superior in CAD-CAM-milled denture teeth. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating enhanced masticatory efficiency with digitally constructed dentures [7, 40]. However, other studies have indicated that aesthetics is superior to conventional dentures [41–43].

This study has several methodological limitations. The small sample size reduces statistical power, and although the crossover design controls for inter-individual variability, carry-over effects cannot be entirely excluded. Ethical constraints prevented an extended washout period; thus, mitigation strategies such as soft-food dietary guidance, psychosocial support, and ongoing participant engagement were implemented. The short intervention duration limits evaluation of long-term outcomes like prosthetic wear, discoloration, and retention. Reliance on subjective measures, including the Visual Analogue Scale and OHIP-EDENT-19, may introduce bias, while the absence of objective functional assessments and extended follow-up further limits the findings. Finally, the homogeneity of the study population restricts external validity, underscoring the need for larger, more diverse cohorts and longer-term, objective evaluations in future research.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both prefabricated and CAD-CAM milled acrylic resin denture teeth are clinically acceptable for use in complete dentures. CAD-CAM teeth were associated with superior masticatory performance, whereas prefabricated teeth offered improved aesthetic outcomes. These findings should be interpreted with caution due to methodological limitations, including a limited follow-up duration, reliance on subjective assessment instruments, and the potential influence of crossover effects. Future longitudinal studies employing objective evaluation methods and involving larger, more heterogeneous populations are recommended to substantiate and generalize these results.
