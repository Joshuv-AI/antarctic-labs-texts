Response to Kårhus et al. Regarding “Ruminococcus gnavus and Biofilm Markers in Feces From Primary Bile Acid Diarrhea Patients Indicate New Disease Mechanisms and Potential for Diagnostic Testing”
Authors: Evette B.M. Hillman, Julian R.F. Walters, Danielle Carson, Sjoerd Rijpkema
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We thank Kårhus and colleagues1 for their thoughtful comments on our work and for describing their complementary study. This performed detailed enterohepatic, glucometabolic, and microbiome characterization in 12 Selenium Homocholic Acid Taurine (SeHCAT)-verified bile-acid-malabsorption patients matched 1:1 to healthy controls.2 Together with evidence of elevated 7α-OH-4-cholesten-3-one, increased fecal bile-acid content, and impaired insulin sensitivity, their study demonstrated increased abundance in bile-acid-malabsorption of certain taxa including Blautia, Streptococcus, Ruminococcus gnavus (R gnavus), and Akkermansia muciniphila. We were aware of this work and publication, and we regret that we did not include a discussion of their important findings in the final version of our publication.

Our SeHCAT-defined bile acid diarrhea (BAD) cohort demonstrated a strikingly greater prevalence of R gnavus, detected in 88% of patients vs <10% of healthy controls (odds ratio ≈ 73; P < .0001), with a negative correlation between R gnavus and SeHCAT retention (P = −.53; P = .008).3 Thus, both studies independently identify R gnavus as a reproducible microbial hallmark of bile-acid–related diarrheal disorders. Where Kårhus et al applied 16S rRNA sequencing with matched controls, we extended the observation using metagenomic shotgun sequencing which enabled us to not only detect over 100 species but also their functional genes needed for secondary bile acid synthesis and biofilm formation.

Kårhus et al2 conducted a highly controlled, prospective, sex-matched, age-matched, and BMI-matched case-control study, with 12 subjects per group, emphasizing postprandial enterohepatic and gluco-metabolic physiology. In contrast, our work analyzed a larger, well-phenotyped cohort, focused on microbial composition, bile-acid metabolomics, and functional gene profiling.3 Although our design did not include strict matching, multivariate analyses consistently identified disease-linked clustering across 3 orthogonal data layers—taxonomic, gene, and bile-acid profiles—indicating that the observed differences reflect disease biology rather than demographic variation.

We agree with Kårhus et al that matching can reduce confounding in microbiome studies. Their careful control for BMI, however, may also constrain the natural metabolic variability of BAD, since SeHCAT retention has been reported to correlate negatively with BMI and fibroblast growth factor 19 concentrations.4 Despite differences in design, the internal coherence of our results supports a genuine disease-specific signature. Bile-acid-transforming genes (baiA/baiB/hdhA) were enriched. In addition, extended bile-acid profiling showed increased iso- and oxo-forms and elevated UDCA in BAD, while allo-pathway bile acids were lower. These multiomic and metabolite associations, together with correlations between R gnavus abundance and SeHCAT values, argue strongly that the differences observed are driven by pathophysiology rather than artifacts of age, sex, or BMI. Nevertheless, we concur that future studies should prospectively adjust for these variables to refine causal inference.

Our study adds a new dimension by linking bacterial biofilm formation genes and bile-acid functional markers to disease severity and SeHCAT retention. This extends the findings of another recent paper linking R gnavus, biofilms, and fecal bile acids5 supporting a plausible model whereby biofilm-associated microbial communities alter bile-acid biotransformation and ileal reabsorption, contributing to the pathophysiology of BAD and suggesting measurable diagnostic biomarkers. We welcome future collaborative studies.
