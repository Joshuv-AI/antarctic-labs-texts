Patency of the lenticulostriate and Pontine arteries after stenting of the intracranial arteries
Authors: Radosław Rzepliński, Michał Tomaszewski, Michał Kucewicz, Jerzy Małachowski, Bogdan Ciszek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterventional neuroradiology methods have a well-established role in the treatment of many cerebrovascular diseases, including ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, with perforating artery strokes being a complication that limits recovery after intracranial artery stenting. The aim of this study was to investigate the influence of covering the perforating artery origins with stent struts on their patency.Two geometric models of coverage of the perforating artery ostia by stent struts were developed, and calculations were made using data on origin dimensions from a collection of 3D models of twenty-three middle cerebral arteries and ten basilar arteries obtained by scanning a collection of anatomical specimens via microcomputed tomography.Analysis of 323 perforating artery geometries revealed that flow-diverting stents reduced the area of perforator origin from a few to 20–30%, whereas in the case of conventional stents, at least one third of the origin in every second perforator studied was covered. Smaller perforating arteries may be completely closed by the stent struts. In the case of implantation of two stents using telescopic technique, the coverage percentages were approximately 2 times higher.Stenting may significantly reduce the effective cross-sectional area of the perforating artery origin. The influence depends on the type of stent: flow diverting stents exert a mesh-effect, and the effect of classic stents is related to strut width. The effect is exacerbated by the implantation of two stents. Considering the supply areas of the pontine and lenticulostriate arteries, even small perforating artery infarction may lead to devastating neurological complications. To increase the safety of intracranial artery stenting, it is reasonable to design and use stents with the narrowest possible struts.Keywords: 4–6: lenticulostriate arteries, Pontine arteries, Intracranial atherosclerosis, Stents, Flow diverting stents, Perforating artery

Background

Cerebrovascular diseases are the second leading cause of death globally and are related to high clinical and social burdens [1–3]. Neurointerventional radiology methods are increasingly used to treat certain conditions, including ischemic stroke, intracranial atherosclerosis, aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, arteriovenous malformations etc [4–8]. These procedures usually include intracranial artery stenting (using conventional and flow diverting stents) to restore or modify blood flow and reshape the vessel or position embolization material (such as coils). One of the major concerns is postprocedural side-branch patency, which is especially true for the perforating arteries [9, 10]. Because of their small diameter, these vessels are poorly visible on standard imaging studies [11] but are of incredible clinical importance because they supply critical neural structures such as the internal capsule and the hypothalamus [12–14]. Recovery after intracranial intervention may be delayed or prevented by one of the possible complications associated with significant morbidity and mortality: perforating artery infarction [15–18].

The aim of this study was to investigate the influence of cerebral artery stenting on perforator patency by creating a geometric model of the relationship with stent struts and analyzing the geometry of the origins of the lenticulostriate arteries (branches of the middle cerebral artery, MCA) and the pontine arteries (branches of the basilar artery, BA).

Materials and methods

The study consisted of 3 steps: (1) obtaining 3D models of the perforating arteries of the MCA and BA with a focus on the geometry of the perforator ostia, (2) measuring the origins of the perforating arteries and (3) developing a geometric model of the relationship between the stent struts and the ostia of the perforating arteries and performing the calculations.

Study approval

The study was conducted on a collection of anatomical human brain specimens from the Department of Descriptive and Clinical Anatomy, Medical University of Warsaw, Poland and was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Warsaw, Poland (22nd July 2020, number 138/2020).

Three-dimensional models of the perforating arteries

The study group included twenty-three specimens of the MCA with the lenticulostriate arteries and ten specimens of the BA with the pontine arteries. One third of the donors were females (3/10 BA, 8/23 MCA), and the donors were between 21 and 78 years old. In every case, central nervous system-related causes of death and significant intracranial pathology (neurodegeneration, cerebral infarction, traumatic brain injury etc.) were ruled out.

The unfixed specimens of the human brain were prepared microsurgically as described previously [19]. The arteries of the circle of Willis were rinsed clean with saline solution and filled with contrast medium (barium sulfate and gelatin mixture). The specimens were then fixed for 14 days in buffered 10% formalin solution. After fixation, the specimens were scanned with a Nikon/Metris XT H 225 ST micro-computed tomography scanner (Nikon Corporation, Japan, voxel size amounted to about 25 μm). The repeatability and quality of the methodology used were confirmed in a series of our recent publications [11, 19–22].

The results were analyzed with Mimics 23.0 software (Materialise, NV, Leuven, Belgium) with the aim of measuring the dimensions of the ostia of the lenticulostriate arteries (branches of the MCA) and the pontine arteries (branches of the BA). First, three-dimensional reconstruction of the vascular tree of every specimen was performed and the artifacts were cleared. For every perforating artery of every specimen, the perforating artery origins were measured via a semiautomated built-in Area tool that calculates the lengths of the long and perpendicular short axes, as described previously [23]. The shape of the ostia turned out to be ellipsoidal.

Results

We calculated the percentage of coverage by stent struts for 165 lenticulostriate arteries and 158 pontine arteries (323 perforating arteries in total; 11 arteries were excluded because of incomplete filling by the contrast agent). The percentage of perforator ostia coverage depended heavily on the stent strut width. A stent strut covers half of all MCA and BA perforator ostia by 30% or more and every one in four perforators by at least 51% or 37% for MCA and BA perforators, respectively. Narrower struts are characterized by smaller coverage percentages, but one in four perforating arteries is still covered by at least 19% or 14% for MCA and BA perforators, respectively. Discussion

Coverage of a large artery origin by a stent is believed to be neutral to the blood flow and function of the area supplied [5, 27]. However, the situation remains unexplored for the perforating arteries, where the size of the vessel ostium is comparable to the stent strut width. Analysis of 323 perforating artery geometries provided information on the relationship between the stent struts and the perforator origins as well as the rationale for the clinically occurring complications of intracranial artery stenting. The study was made possible by the unique collection of three-dimensional vascular tree models that are of sufficient quality to precisely visualize perforating artery geometries [11, 19–21]. We decided to perform the calculations separately for different stent strut widths, including sizes typical for conventional and flow-diverting stents [24–26].

The calculations revealed that flow-diverters (stents made of a dense network of fine threads with diameters ranging from 30 to 78 𝜇𝑚) reduce the area of the perforator origin from a few to 20–30%, so for most perforators these stenoses are expected to be hemodynamically insignificant. In the case of using the telescopic technique, the coverage percentages were approximately 2 times higher. The degree of stenosis depends, of course, on the size of the perforator, and for small lenticulostriate arteries it can completely cover the ostium. Small perforators supply smaller volumes of nerve tissue, and the resulting lacune would not be large, but owing to the nature of the area supplied, it can be clinically significant (i.e., cause the development of a neurological syndrome) because the supply area of the perforators includes the internal capsule, etc. Generally, the impact of flow-diverting stents on the patency of the perforating arteries depends on the mesh effect rather than the influence of one stent strut.

The situation looks different for stents with struts of greater width: 100–150 𝜇𝑚. Unlike flow-diverting stents, these stents have different functions and therefore different indications. The struts form a not-so-dense network, and their function may be, for example, to support embolization material in an aneurysm. The size of the struts is comparable to the width of the perforator ostia, so they cover at least one-third of the origin of every second perforator studied. When using the telescopic technique, the effect is even more pronounced. The sparser strut network decreases the likelihood of a stent element covering a perforating artery origin, but when it does occur, the impact is more significant than with flow diverters, as reflected in the results of our model: when a stent with a strut width of 150 𝜇𝑚 is used, even every fourth lenticulostriate artery origin may be covered by more than 50%. As the localization of the origins of the lenticulostriate arteries is variable, analyzing the microanatomy and planning the procedure is necessary because it sometimes allows for avoiding covering the ostia.

Cases of both early and late ischemic complications associated with flow-diverter implantation are known in the literature [28–32], and the percentage of acute perforating artery strokes is greater in the vertebrobasilar system, reaching 7% [10, 33]. Similar complications are also observed after stenting for intracranial artery stenosis with conventional stents and the incidence of perforating artery infarction ranges from 2.5 to 10.3% [16, 17, 34]. The literature data on this subject is presented in Table 3. The problem of side branch patency is also considered in interventional cardiology [35–37]; however, diameters of the analyzed coronary arteries vary significantly from the cerebral perforating arteries. The higher complication rate in the posterior part of the circle of Willis appears to be due to the eloquence of the area being supplied and the lack of collateral circulation. It can also be assumed that the technical aspects of neuroradiological interventions have a significant effect on the patency of the perforating arteries: both over- and undersizing [38] as well as overlapping stents [39] may further reduce inflow to the perforating arteries. In the context of late ischemic complications, the effect of stenting on remodeling of the perforator ostia also needs to be studied [28, 40–42]. In the clinical scenario, it should be remembered that mechanical effects analyzed in our study may coexist with additional problems such as atherosclerotic plaque rupture during stenting of stenosis or thrombosis after flow diversion for fusiform aneurysms.

The clinical significance of potential ischemic complications depends on the area supplied by the perforator rather than its diameter. In the case of the pontine arteries (branches of the BA) the areas supplied include many vital neural structures; therefore, pontine artery infarctions are rarely clinically silent and typically include cranial nerve palsy ipsilaterally and contralateral hemiplegia [51]. On the other hand, lenticulostriate infarction may remain unrecognized provided that the supplied area does not include eloquent structures [12]. Hence, to determine the real incidence of ischemic complications after intracranial artery stenting, it is necessary to routinely perform brain MRI (especially diffusion-weighted imaging, DWI, and apparent diffusion coefficient, ADC, sequences), not only when new neurological symptoms develop [42, 52]. Considering the results of the geometric model, it seems reasonable to develop and use stents with narrower struts.

Recent studies revealed that chronic hypoperfusion is sufficient for the development of dementia [53–56]. Considering the results of this study, investigating the influence of blood flow impairment through the perforating arteries after stenting on brain aging and cognitive performance would be valuable. The problem is especially interesting in the context of a recent report that revealed that the origins of the deep perforating arteries are locally narrowed by intimal hyperplasia and that the degree of stenosis may increase with age [23]. Therefore, stenting may further disturb the blood supply to the basal ganglia and exacerbate hemodynamic changes, promoting brain aging.

Limitations of the study include a lack of hemodynamic analysis, parent artery deformation simulation, and simplified assumption. The calculations assumed that the stent strut followed the long axis of the perforating artery ostium. Of course, other settings are possible: both more (ostium covered obliquely or not at all) and less favorable (ostium covered by the junction of the stent struts); however, this does not change the conclusions of the study.

This study also sets directions for further research. As noted above, the impact of intracranial artery stenting on local hemodynamics and perforating artery remodeling, as well as chronic hypoperfusion and cognitive performance, needs to be clarified. In addition, stents with narrower struts should be designed, taking into consideration the balance between the risk of ischemic complications and fulfilling the intended function.
