Throttling characteristics prediction methodology for combined grooves in directional spool valves under variable flow rates
Authors: Yuanliu Chen, Anlin Wang, Xiaotian Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is a contradiction between the calculation accuracy and cost when computing the pressure and flow rate. A throttling characteristics prediction methodology for directional spool valve was proposed to balance the contradiction. The throttling characteristics were represented by the flow-number, defined as the product of the flow coefficient and the orifice area, to reveal simultaneously the mapping relationships of the two versus the spool geometry, stroke and flow rate. The pressure and flow rate characteristics at different strokes were obtained through the computational fluid dynamic (CFD) simulations that were validated through bench testing. The concept of saturated flow-number theory was introduced to describe the effects of the flow rate on the flow-number. Models for the saturated flow-number and critical flow rate were established using the U-shape groove, a typical throttling structure, as an example to illustrate the effects of groove structure and opening. The throttling characteristics of the directional spool valve could be predicted by the flow-numbers of the combined grooves. The simulated and experimental results demonstrate that the prediction methodology achieves high calculation accuracy while incurring minimal costs. This methodology holds significant implications for the forward design of valve spool throttling structures.Keywords: Directional spool valve, Throttling characteristics, Saturated flow-number, Critical flow rate, Computational fluid dynamic

Introduction

Directional spool valves play a crucial role in hydraulic systems and find extensive application in various industrial areas. They control the direction and speed of the fluid by changing the throttling characteristics. The relationship between the pressure and flow rate varies with the valve spool stroke. The throttling characteristics of directional spool valves are directly related to the valve spool geometries which are supposed to be designed carefully to meet the throttling characteristics requirements.

Many researchers have studied the valve spool design through CFD simulations. Gui et al. [1] optimized the valve spool structures to minimize the steady flow force and the mass of the main spool. CFD simulations were carried out to evaluate the flow force. Jian et al. [2] investigated the effects of the spool geometries on flow characters through CFD simulations. Simic et al. [3] employed CFD simulations to analyze the relationship between the geometries of a hydraulic seat valve and its flow forces. Liu et al. [4] researched the influence rules of the structural parameters on the hydrodynamic forces through CFD simulations. The above studies show that the CFD simulations are mostly applied in the analysis of an existing valve or a limited amount of valves.

The CFD simulations can hardly be utilized directly in the design process due to the huge calculation costs. Amirante et al. [5] minimized the flow force by utilizing a genetic algorithm in conjunction with a CFD solver. The experimental validation of this optimization was reported in Ref. [6]. The optimization process took 500 h with only 1000 individuals explored. The calculation costs were impractical for the engineering application. Kong et al. [7] introduced the proper orthogonal decomposition method to simplify the fluid field data. But a CFD simulation was required first to provide the data representing the original filed. Consequently, this approach did not fundamentally reduce the computational costs.

The throttling characteristics are described approximately by the flow coefficient and the orifice area to reduce the calculation costs. The flow coefficient is generally regarded as a constant in engineering application [8], which may cause a large error due to the nonlinear relationship between the flow coefficient and the pressure drop. Borghi et al. [9] carried out experiments to study the discharge characteristics. Results showed that the spool geometries, the spool stroke and the flow rate would influence the flow coefficient. Valdes et al. [10] derived the flow coefficient as a function of the Reynolds number. This function revealed the influence of the flow rate. But the characteristics parameters could only be used for the valves in complete geometric similarity as the studied ones. Zhang et al. [11] and Wang et al. [12] established the mapping relationship between the saturated flow coefficient and the valve spool geometries. The influence of the flow rate could be ignored if it was large enough. However, their proposed models were limited to valves with specific groove combinations. Lisowski et al. [13] demonstrated that considering both the flow rate and spool position enabled more accurate estimation of the flow coefficient. Afatsun et al. [14] proposed the model for the simulation of the flow rate and the pressure, where the flow coefficient depended on both the Reynolds number and the spool geometries. But the studied valve had a simple spool without grooves.

Valdes et al. [15] pointed out that there was a significant difference between the flow coefficients with and without the cavitation. Frosina et al. [16] investigated the gaseous cavitation in spool valves through simulations and experiments. Amirante et al. [17] observed a decrease in the flow coefficient due to the presence of cavitation. Mohan et al. [18] proposed that the flow coefficient would start to decrease at some critical point with an increasing amount of cavitation. The cavitation should be avoided in most hydraulic systems. Therefore, its effects are ignored in this paper.

In addition to the flow coefficient, the orifice area is difficult to estimate accurately as well. Wang et al. [19] derived the function expression for the orifice area of the sloping U-shape notch machined by two different methods. The CFD simulation and the experiment proved that the orifice areas and the throttling characteristics of the sloping U-shape notches were different with an arcuate bottom and a plain bottom. Ye et al. [20] observed changes in throttling surfaces as a result of variations in groove structures and valve spool positions. It was not sufficient to replace the orifice area with just the axial or radial area.

Zhang et al. [21] established an artificial neural network for a directional valve. The inputs were the structural parameters, the valve spool stroke and the flow rate. The model produced the flow coefficient and the orifice area as outputs. This model could be applied in the design process due to its minor calculation costs. However, the designed valve spool must be machined with the same combination of grooves. Chen et al. [22] proposed the flow-number (the product of the flow coefficient and the orifice area) parametric model. The throttling characteristics of the spool valve could be obtained by the simple linear superposition of the flow-numbers of each groove. However, this model disregarded the effect of the flow rate.

This paper proposed a flow-number parametric model under variable flow rates, building upon the study conducted in Ref. [22]. By considering the flow-numbers of combined grooves, the proposed model predicts the throttling characteristics of the spool valve, effectively balancing calculation accuracy and cost constraints.

Materials and methods

Working principle

Fig. 1 shows a schematic diagram of a spool valve. The spool valve depicted is a 6-way 3-position open center type directional control valve. This spool valve represents the swing circuit of a VM30NF06 type multiway valve used in excavators, whose manufacturer is CCHC Hydraulics. The ports A and B are connected to the actuator. The pump is connected to the port P through a check valve. And the port C is also connected to the pump. The ports T and D are connected to the oil tank. The ports As and Bs are connected to the pilot oil. The valve spool compresses the spring to move rightwards when the pilot pressure is exerted on the port As. Then the port A is connected to the port P and the port B to the port T through the grooves in the valve spool. The oil from the pump flows to the actuator through the flow path P-A. And the oil returns to the tank through the flow path B-T. When the pilot pressure is exerted on the port Bs, the flow paths P–B and A-T are conductive.

The pressure drop mainly takes place around the throttling grooves. The throttling characteristics are generally changed by adjusting the grooves in the valve spool due to the easy access to machining. Bench test

A bench test was carried out to obtain the throttling characteristics of the spool valve. The sketch of the test bench is shown in Fig. 2.

Sketch of the test bench.

1-variable pump; 2-relief valve; 3-flow meter; 4-pressure gauge;5-tested valve; 6-proportional relief valve; 7-motor

The flow rate, the inlet and outlet pressure of the tested valve were measured using flow meters and pressure gauges. The flow rate could be adjusted by altering the motor's rotation speed or adjusting the displacement of the variable pump. The outlet pressure was set by the proportional relief valve. The spring of the tested valve depicted in Fig. 1 was eliminated. The valve spool position was controlled by the screw-feeding device, as shown in Fig. 3.

Screw feeding device.

1-screw; 2-end cap; 3-displacement sensor; 4-housing.

The screw was axially connected to the valve spool. The valve spool could stop at any position due to the self-lock thread. The screw converted the rotation to the displacement of the valve spool. The scale value on the end cap displayed the rotation angle, thus the variation of the spool stroke could be calculated. And the actual value could be measured by the displacement sensor. The housing of the screw-feeding device was directly attached to the valve body.

The throttling characteristics of the flow path B-T were tested on the test bench. The valve spool was fixed at a certain stroke x. The inlet flow rate was gradually increased until the relief valve reached its maximum capacity. The pressure drop Δp was calculated by the difference between the inlet and outlet pressure. The outlet pressure was fixed at 0.1 MPa. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 4.

Experimental and simulated throttling characteristics of the flow path B-T.

The pressure drop is approximately a quadratic function of the flow rate. With the increase of the spool stroke, the pressure drop under the same flow rate decreases, namely the flow-number increases.

CFD simulation

The CFD simulation was carried out to have an insight view of the fluid field in the spool valve. The CFD model of the flow path B-T was created based on the spool valve geometry. Fig. 5 is the model at the stroke of 7.5 mm.

The simulation was carried out with a commercial code Pumplinx. The model was meshed with the general mesher. More than 70,000 cells were created, as shown in Fig. 6. Grid independence verification was performed to confirm that the chosen cell quantity was sufficient for obtaining accurate results. The pressure drop was simulated with the number of cells between 20,000 and 190,000, as shown in Fig. 7. All other settings remained unchanged. As can be seen in Fig. 7, after the number of cells reached 70,000, the pressure drop fluctuated around 10 MPa and changed little. The cell quantity was restricted to 70,000 to minimize computation time.

Grid independence verification.

Δp-pressure drop.

The standard k-ε turbulence model is known for its robustness and cost-effectiveness [23]. Filo et al. [24] compared the standard, realizable and RNG k-ε turbulence models. The standard one was 10 % and 11 % faster than the realizable and RNG ones while the differences in results were negligible. Huang et al. [25] and Xie et al. [26] also applied the standard k-ε turbulence model. Therefore, the standard k-ε turbulence model was applied in this study.

The inlet boundary condition was specified as a volumetric flux, while the outlet boundary condition was specified as a pressure outlet. The remaining boundaries were defined as rigid walls. The outlet pressure was set as 0.1 MPa while the inlet flow rate was set as a series of values. The VM30NF06 type multiway valve utilized the ISO VG46 oil, whose working temperature was −20 °C–90 °C and viscosity 10–1000 mm2/s. The fluid was considered as an incompressible Newtonian fluid with a density of 890 kg/m3 and a viscosity of 0.89 Pa·s. The simulated results are shown in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4 demonstrates good agreement between the simulated and experimental results. Thus the CFD simulation and the selection of the turbulence model are verified. The same settings can be applied in the subsequent research.

Throttling characteristics of combined grooves

As mentioned in 3.2, the spool valve usually contains a combination of several grooves. The saturated flow-number and the critical flow rate can be calculated by the models if the structure and the opening of each groove are ascertained. In other words, the models of the saturated flow-number and the critical flow rate can express the effects of the structure and opening of the U-shape groove. The inlet pressure will not be output until the difference between the calculated and actual flow rate is within the convergence criterion. Compared to the flow coefficient models in Refs. [[10], [11], [12]], the prediction methodology in this paper is not limited for a specific combination of grooves. Both the number and the structure of grooves can be changed. Unlike the flow-number model in Ref. [22], this prediction methodology can be applied to both large and small flow rate conditions. However, it must be noticed that this prediction methodology only suits the combination of U-shape grooves. Further researches are required for other types of grooves.

Conclusions

A throttling characteristics prediction methodology for combined grooves in spool valve was proposed in this paper, which could express the effects of the spool geometries, the spool strokes and the flow rate.

The product of the flow coefficient and the orifice area was defined as flow-number to describe the variation of the both. Based on the fluid dynamic theory and experiments, the saturated flow-number theory was proposed. The relationship between the flow rate and the flow-number was explained by introducing the saturated flow-number and critical flow rate. The models of the two parameters were established respectively to express the effects of the spool strokes and geometries.

The calculation accuracy of the prediction methodology was verified by comparing it to the CFD simulation and experimental data. The maximum error to the simulation and experiment was 4.12 % and 15 % respectively. And the calculation time was much less than that of the traditional CFD simulation. This prediction methodology could be applied in the design process of spool valve due to the ideal accuracy and low cost, which contributes to the forward design.
