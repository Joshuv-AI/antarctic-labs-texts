Characteristics Associated With Community Violence Prevention Programs in US Hospitals: A Cross‐Sectional Survey Analysis
Authors: Vivek A Ashok, Katherine Yun, Matthew J Klebanoff, Elinore Kaufman, Jaya Aysola, Therese S Richmond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundViolence is a social determinant of health, and hospitals are well‐positioned to promote patient well‐being by addressing its root causes. Understanding factors associated with hospital engagement in community violence prevention can guide intervention development and capacity building.ObjectiveTo examine hospital and county‐level factors associated with the presence of community violence prevention programs (CVPPs) in U.S. hospitals.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study linked data from the 2022 American Hospital Association Annual Survey with county‐level socioeconomic and demographic data from the US Census Bureau and all‐cause homicide rates from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The sample included general medical and surgical hospitals with Medicare identification numbers. Survey‐adjusted logistic regression assessed associations between hospital CVPP presence and all‐cause homicide. Our response variable was whether or not the hospital had a CVPP, and our explanatory variable was county‐level all‐cause homicide rates. We adjusted for hospital characteristics and county‐level socio‐demographics.ResultsOf 4,374 hospitals, 990 (22.6%) reported having CVPPs. Compared to those without CVPPs, hospitals with CVPPs were more likely to be nonprofit (85.0% vs. 62.9%), large (> 500 beds; 16.9% vs. 4.7%), have more annual ED visits (51,873.9 vs. 26,224.5), and be urban (81.1% vs. 51.8%) (all p < 0.001). They also more frequently offered outpatient psychiatric (86.1% vs. 46.9%), substance use (74.4% vs. 23.2%), and pain management (93.1% vs. 65.2%) services. In adjusted models, homicide rates were not associated with CVPP presence (aOR = 1.01, 95% CI [0.99, 1.04]). CVPP presence was independently associated with nonprofit ownership, larger size, trauma designation, and lower social deprivation in urban counties.ConclusionsHospital and community characteristics, rather than homicide rates, predict CVPP presence. CVPPs are concentrated in larger, urban, well‐resourced hospitals rather than in areas with the highest homicide rates, highlighting potential misalignment between program placement and community need.

Introduction

Violence is a major public health issue and is well‐recognized as a social determinant of health [1, 2, 3]. Both direct exposure to violence, such as assault, and indirect exposure, such as witnessing community violence, are associated with the development of health conditions and poor health outcomes. Survivors of violence experience higher than usual rates of anxiety, depression, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and sleep disturbances [4, 5, 6]. Cumulative exposure to violence potentially increases risk for developing cardiovascular disease [7]. Beyond its health consequences, violence imposes a significant burden on patients and public insurance systems. Child survivors and their caregivers have increased economic burdens, trouble paying medical bills, and increased use of acute medical services [8, 9, 10].

Cases of community violence most often present to hospitals, with the emergency department (ED) serving as the primary point of entry for injured patients. While the ED's immediate responsibility is acute injury care, its unique position at the intersection of clinical care and community harm makes it a critical setting for violence prevention efforts. The ED frequently serves as the only point of healthcare contact for patients who are uninsured, socioeconomically disadvantaged, or living in rural areas, positioning it as a key locus for injury prevention and health equity–oriented interventions that cannot reliably occur elsewhere in the healthcare system [11]. In 2017 alone, 2.3 million emergency department visits were made for violent injuries in the United States (US), representing almost 8% of all injury‐related visits [12]. Of these patients, most are released from the ED, while approximately 16% are hospitalized [12]. Patients, health systems, and payors incurred an estimated $4.3 billion in violence‐related ED costs in 2010 [12].

Consequently, hospitals have taken a variety of approaches to promote the health and well‐being of their patients through community violence prevention. One example is the expansion of hospital‐based violence intervention programs (HVIPs), often situated in emergency departments. HVIPs provide individual psychosocial support and case management services to survivors of violent injury [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. These programs address patient and community‐level structural determinants of violence to prevent an episode of reinjury and improve recovery. They may also incorporate behavioral, psychosocial, and mentorship interventions [11]. Another potential forum for community violence prevention programming is through injury prevention programs, required for trauma center certification for Level 1–3 adult trauma centers and Level 1–2 pediatric trauma centers [20]. Importantly, while injury prevention programs fulfill trauma designation requirements, they are not required to address community violence. Many institutions may not engage in community violence prevention through injury prevention programming alone.

Despite these expanding efforts, the nationwide scope and prevalence of hospital‐led community violence prevention efforts, which may include but are not limited to programs such as HVIPs or injury prevention programs, remain unclear. Further, little is known about the medical and social services provided by hospitals that engage in violence prevention or the socio‐demographic, economic, and violence‐related characteristics of the communities they serve. To address these gaps, we conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of the 2022 American Hospital Association (AHA) Annual Survey to examine characteristics associated with hospitals that report having community violence prevention programs (CVPPs). We also analyzed the local contexts in which these hospitals operate and explored the relationship between homicide rates and the presence of CVPPs.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

We analyzed a cross‐sectional survey of hospitals comparing those with and without community violence prevention programs. We identified hospitals with and without CVPPs using the 2022 AHA Annual Survey, a voluntary survey of over 6193 hospitals across the US and its territories. This survey was conducted from September 30, 2021, to October 1, 2022 [21]. We linked AHA survey data to county‐level socioeconomic and demographic data from the US Census Bureau, as well as county‐level rates of all‐cause homicide from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [22].

The AHA defines a CVPP as “an organized program that attempts to make a positive impact on the type(s) of violence a community is experiencing. For example, it can assist victims of violent crimes (e.g., rape) or incidents (e.g., bullying) by linking individuals to hospital or community services to prevent further victimization or retaliation” (Full definition provided in Table S1). The survey asks respondents to report community violence prevention programming as follows: None, within a hospital, within a health system, or as a joint venture. We compared hospitals reporting any vs. no community violence prevention programs [21]. Importantly, these programs should not be equated with HVIPs. HVIPs undergo rigorous development and evaluation as outlined by the Health Alliance for Violence Intervention (HAVI). The AHA CVPP survey item may capture hospitals with HVIPs; however, survey respondents may also refer to any and all programs their hospital has that qualify under the definition provided.

Data Sources and Variables

AHA survey data were merged with county‐level data from (a) the US Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS) 2022 5‐year estimates, and (b) the 2016–2021 CDC Web‐based Injury Statistics Query and Reporting System (WISQARS), using county‐level Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) codes [22, 23]. Of the 2437 counties represented by the AHA, all were successfully matched with county‐level data from the ACS and WISQARS.

Using AHA survey data, we compared hospitals with and without CVPPs across general hospital characteristics, medical services, trauma center designation, and healthcare utilization, which included the number of outpatient visits and inpatient discharges. We also examined the presence of clinical and social services often required by patients affected by violence, including mental health care, substance use disorder treatment, physical rehabilitative services, housing assistance, education or employment assistance, and legal aid [24, 25, 26, 27].

Using ACS county‐level data, we examined the percentage of county residents from minoritized racial and ethnic groups, specifically Black and Hispanic, given evidence that violence disproportionately impacts these subpopulations [28]. We also examined the county‐level rates of public insurance and uninsurance, as insurance availability and access to healthcare are potentially associated with reductions in violence [29, 30, 31]. To further capture social context, we incorporated the most recent validated Social Deprivation Index (SDI), derived from the ACS 2019 5‐year estimates, to provide composite county‐level estimates of social need. The SDI ranges from −2.1 to +2.1, with higher values indicating greater social deprivation [32].

We obtained county‐level crude rates of all‐cause homicide per 100,000 people from the CDC's WISQARS database for the years 2016–2021. To preserve confidentiality, the CDC suppressed data for 1478 out of 3152 counties (46.8%) due to low death counts (defined as between one and nine deaths). After linking with the American Hospital Association (AHA) survey data, 2437 counties were matched, of which 469 (19.2%) had suppressed homicide data. These 469 counties were excluded from the primary analysis due to the absence of homicide data [33, 34, 35].

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. In the first, we conservatively imputed five deaths for each county with suppressed data and calculated crude homicide rates by dividing by the respective county population. This approach preserved geographic representation while avoiding under‐ or overestimation. In the second, we randomly assigned a value between one and nine deaths to counties with suppressed data. A complete list of variables and definitions is provided in the Supplement.

Statistical Methods

Our analysis included US general medical or surgical hospitals with Medicare identification numbers that responded to the AHA Annual Survey. We included hospitals in counties from the 50 states, the District of Columbia, and territories when comparing hospital characteristics. Hospitals located in counties with missing data from the ACS and WISQARS were excluded when comparing county‐level socio‐demographic data and rates of violence. Non‐responders to the AHA survey question about CVPPs were excluded from the primary analysis. We completed supplemental descriptive comparative analyses to assess whether non‐responders to the CVPP question more closely resembled hospitals with or without CVPPs, thereby allowing for a better understanding of potential nonresponse bias.

First, we calculated unadjusted comparisons of hospitals with and without CVPPs across various hospital and county‐level characteristics. We dichotomized categorical outcome variables from the AHA survey to yes/no. We used chi‐squared tests and survey‐adjusted regression analysis to compare categorical and non‐categorical variables, respectively, between hospitals with and without CVPPs. We applied Bonferroni's correction for multiple comparisons with a significance level of 0.017. We conducted survey‐adjusted regression instead of Kruskal–Wallis tests due to the non‐parametric nature and non‐normal distributions of continuous variables. Additionally, we conducted a subgroup analysis of hospitals with Level 1–3 trauma centers to examine the presence of CVPPs. In this subgroup analysis, we also compared county‐level all‐cause homicide rates to better understand the contextual differences between counties with trauma centers and CVPPs and those without.

Second, we performed a logistic regression to assess the relationship between the presence of a CVPP within a hospital and county‐level crude rates of all‐cause homicide. We used homicide as our explanatory variable because the AHA definition of CVPPs corresponds to interpersonal violence and does not include suicide or unintentional injury. Our response variable was the presence of CVPPs at the hospital level. We adjusted for hospital characteristics, including bed size, hospital ownership, presence of a trauma center, and rural location, to examine institutional capacity and resource availability, which may influence the likelihood of implementing a CVPP. We also adjusted for county‐level demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, including SDI, to account for contextual factors such as county‐level disinvestment and disparities in healthcare access. Lastly, we adjusted for percentages of non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and American Indian/Alaskan Native populations. Race and ethnicity were included in our model as demographic composition may influence the adoption of CVPPs because of disproportionate impacts of violence on these communities.

This study was not considered human subjects research by the definition provided by the Institutional Review Board. We performed the analysis using STATA 18.0 software using the “SVY” code package.

Results

Our analysis of hospital characteristics included 4374 hospitals, of which 990 (22.6%) had CVPPs, and 2241 (51.2%) did not have CVPPs. 1143 (26.1%) did not answer the CVPP question. Hospitals with CVPPs were more likely to be nonprofit (85.0% vs. 62.9%; p < 0.001), larger (> 500 beds) (16.9% vs. 4.7%; p < 0.001), located in urban areas (81.1% vs. 51.8%; p < 0.001), and have more annual ED visits (51,873.9 vs. 26,224.5; p < 0.001) compared to hospitals without CVPPs (Table 1).

A greater percentage of hospitals with CVPPs screened for social needs (95.3% vs. 76.7%; p < 0.001) and had programs addressing housing (78.2.7% vs. 44.0%; p < 0.001) and unemployment (66.4% vs. 40.9%; p < 0.001). Hospitals with CVPPs also provided more medical services aligned with the needs of patients impacted by violence, including outpatient psychiatric services (86.1% vs. 46.9%; p < 0.001), outpatient substance use services (74.4% vs. 23.2%; p < 0.001), and pain management programs (93.1% vs. 65.2%; p < 0.001). Characteristics, such as size, location, and nonprofit status, were similar between non‐responders and hospitals without CVPPs (Table S1).

Our analysis of the relationship between the presence of CVPP and county‐level characteristics (including all‐cause homicide rates) included 989 hospitals with CVPPs and 2228 hospitals without CVPPs (Table 2). Compared to hospitals without CVPPs, hospitals with CVPPs were in counties with higher mean rates of all‐cause homicide (7.1 vs. 6.8 per 100,000; p = 0.029), though the difference was statistically insignificant. Hospitals with CVPPs were in areas with less social deprivation (mean difference = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.16, −0.01]) and higher median household income (mean difference = $12,336.30, 95% CI [10,802.0, 13,870.5]).

In both unadjusted and adjusted models, there was no association between county‐level rates of all‐cause homicide and the presence of CVPPs (OR = 1.01, 95% CI [0.99, 1.02]; aOR = 1.01, [0.99, 1.04]) (Figure 1). Furthermore, two sensitivity analyses affirmed there was no significant association between county‐level homicide rates and the presence of CVPPs, supporting the robustness of our main finding (Supplement). Notably, in the adjusted analysis, the presence of CVPPs was independently associated with several hospital and community characteristics. These were large hospital size, the presence of a trauma center, non‐profit status, lower social deprivation index, and urbanicity (Figure 1).

Discussion

This cross‐sectional study found that hospitals with CVPPs are often large, urban, high‐volume, academic, and well‐resourced, capable of providing comprehensive medical and social services. Additionally, hospitals with CVPPs, compared to hospitals without CVPPs, were also more often located in counties with lower social deprivation, higher educational attainment, higher percentages of insured patients, and lower percentages of unemployment. Notably, after adjusting for hospital and county‐level characteristics, we found that the presence of CVPPs in hospitals was not associated with county‐level rates of all‐cause homicide. While this may suggest that CVPP adoption is not primarily driven by local violence burden, it is possible that CVPPs are effective in reducing homicide at the individual or neighborhood level, but their effects are not detectable at the county level due to their targeted scope, delayed impact, and potential for confounding by indication. This also suggests opportunities for the expansion of these programs into areas with the highest levels of homicide. Specifically, expanding CVPPs to under‐resourced hospitals in counties with elevated violence rates may help reduce rates of violence, highlighting the need for policies that prioritize implementation in these areas.

While only 990 hospitals (22.6%) in the survey reported having CVPPs, this suggests that a growing number of hospitals nationwide are beginning to recognize and address violence as a social determinant of health, and to acknowledge the role of health systems as essential components of the broader community violence intervention ecosystem [15]. For example, the ED often serves as the first entry point into the healthcare system when individuals experience violence and offers an opportunity to reach patients less likely to access routine medical care [11]. EDs located in areas with high rates of violence and in hospitals that are well‐resourced are prime candidates for the implementation of violence prevention programs.

Emergency departments, including those in under‐resourced settings, have a long history of addressing social determinants of health through evidence‐based interventions such as screening and brief intervention for substance use and smoking, linkage to care for uninsured patients, and programs addressing housing instability, food insecurity, and transportation barriers [11, 36, 37, 38, 39]. For example, the SafERteens randomized controlled trial conducted at a Level I trauma center in Michigan enrolled adolescents aged 14–18 years and delivered a brief intervention, via computer or therapist‐assisted computer, to reduce aggression among youth with a history of alcohol use or aggression [36]. Participants receiving the intervention demonstrated significant reductions in peer aggression and victimization at 12 months compared with control groups [36]. Framing violence prevention as part of this existing continuum represents an extension of established ED practice that aligns with hospital and community missions to advance health equity.

Factors other than county‐level homicide rates, such as hospital size, ownership, trauma center presence, and community social deprivation, are stronger predictors of CVPP presence. This suggests that resource availability at the hospital and community levels predicates the establishment of these programs. Well‐resourced, urban institutions often serve as anchor institutions within their surrounding communities and are mission‐driven to address violence as a social determinant of health [2]. These hospitals have the capability, through greater infrastructure, personnel, and finances, to engage in community health efforts. In addition, national trauma leadership may influence program adoption. Recent work led by the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma highlights opportunities to expand hospital‐based violence intervention programs through partnerships with health systems and the Health Alliance for Violence Intervention, potentially contributing to the greater presence of such programs at trauma centers [40]. Taken together, our findings suggest that hospitals serving under‐resourced communities, where the burden of violence may be greatest, are also less likely to have the institutional and financial capacity to establish CVPPs.

Our findings are consistent with existing literature, which emphasizes the importance of community violence prevention programs being situated in institutions that provide robust resources to individuals who have been injured by violence [41]. The development of programs to address violence is often contingent on a local champion, institutional buy‐in, and sustainable funding to pilot these initiatives [41]. Furthermore, funding hospital‐based community violence prevention programs can be challenging in states where Medicaid has not allowed for reimbursement of community violence intervention services, and instead, funding hinges on a mix of institutional largesse, private foundations, and grants [42, 43]. Even in states where Medicaid reimbursement is available, reimbursement levels are often insufficient to fully cover the costs of services provided, representing an additional barrier to program sustainability. Some hospitals may also establish community violence prevention programs as part of efforts to meet nonprofit community benefit requirements.

The disparity in resource availability between hospitals with and without CVPPs is also reflected in the differences between the medical and social services provided. Hospitals with Level 2 and Level 3 trauma centers are more likely to be smaller and located in rural, under‐resourced areas. Because trauma centers are potentially expensive to maintain [44], acute services may take precedence over the establishment of CVPPs. Importantly, hospitals with CVPPs more often address structural determinants of violence such as education and employment opportunities, mental health and substance use care, and housing. Well‐resourced areas typically have more support targeted toward the antecedents of violence, which may help explain the lack of association between the presence of CVPPs and rates of homicide.

We were unable to distinguish between program types, intensity, or alignment with evidence‐based models. Hospitals reporting a CVPP may vary widely in the scope and quality of their efforts, leading to substantial differences in impact. Future research should systematically characterize the types of programs hospitals offer and assess their alignment with established standards, such as those set by the HAVI for hospital‐based violence intervention programs [41]. In addition, further research identifying high‐need geographic areas at the census tract level, particularly those with large nonprofit hospitals lacking CVPPs in regions with high violence, can offer valuable insights into prime candidates for program implementation. Qualitative studies can examine these hospitals to identify barriers and facilitators to CVPP implementation. Lastly, quasi‐experimental studies can help determine whether CVPPs contribute to reductions in violence and improved recovery.

Limitations

Our study has limitations. First, social desirability bias may have led to an over‐reporting of CVPP presence at hospitals. Second, non‐response was another limitation; however, non‐respondents had similar characteristics to hospitals without CVPPs, mitigating this concern. Third, we relied on county‐level homicide rates due to the limited availability of reliable, standardized data on nonfatal violent injuries. Nonfatal violent assaults occur far more frequently than homicides and may better capture the broader burden of community violence that motivates hospitals to establish community violence prevention programs. As a result, using homicide rates alone may underestimate the association between community violence burden and CVPP presence, particularly in settings where nonfatal injury rates are high but homicide rates are relatively low. Fourth, we used county‐level socio‐demographic data rather than census tract or zip code data, which offers less geographic specificity and overlooks the role of systemic inequities in concentrating violence within disinvested areas. Fifth, the AHA definition of a community violence prevention program is broad. As a result, survey respondents may be referring to a range of violence‐related interventions that fit this definition, including programs addressing forms of violence that are more commonly managed within hospital or ED settings. This may lead to an overestimate of the number of programs specifically targeted toward community violence prevention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this cross‐sectional study found that the presence of CVPPs for individual hospitals was not associated with county‐level rates of all‐cause homicide. Rather, factors other than homicide rates, such as hospital size, ownership, trauma center presence, and community resources, were more strongly associated with CVPP presence. These findings suggest an opportunity to better align the location of CVPPs with areas experiencing the highest levels of violence.
