T cell exhaustion in poorly immunogenic HCC is partially rescued by checkpoint blockade but suppressed by oncolytic virotherapy
Authors: Thanich Sangsuwannukul, Benjamin Kendall, Jason Tonne, Olivia Liseth, Jill Thompson, Kamonlapat Supimon, Muriel M Metko, Alex Chen, Maria P Chiriboga Yerovi, Rosa Maria Diaz, Laura Evgin, Mason J Webb, Jacob van Vloten, Satdarshan P Monga, Sheeba Irshad, Mark McNiven, Lewis Roberts, Richard G Vile
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, the benefits of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are restricted to a subset of patients. We hypothesized that co-treatment with the inflammatory oncolytic virus (OV) vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV-IFNβ) would reprogram the highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) to enhance ICB. However, VSV-IFNβ inhibited the efficacy of ICB. To develop better, mechanism-based immunotherapies for HCC, here, we characterized (1) the baseline T cell response to HCC, (2) its enhancement by ICB, (3) the inhibitory effects of VSV, and (4) the antigenic response of HCC to treatment. We show that a slowly developing anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response becomes exhausted, is only initially sensitive to ICB, is outcompeted by a strong anti-viral CD8+ T cell response against VSV-IFNβ, and combination with ICB boosted the anti-viral, as opposed to the anti-tumor, response. We also identify additional molecules (CD39, LAG3) that may be valuable therapeutic targets to combine with ICB. Finally, we show that Sleeping Beauty (SB)-HCC tumors evolve different antigenic/immunogenic profiles to evade different therapies. Therefore, the future immunotherapy of HCC should target multiple molecules/immune checkpoints, avoid attritional competition by immunodominant T cells, and account for the rapidly evolving tumor phenotypes that emerge as a result of treatment-selective pressures.

INTRODUCTION

Current treatments for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are suboptimal, and HCC represents a clear unmet clinical need. The immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment (TME) remains a major challenge to effective immunotherapy. The anti-programmed cell death-ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1) antibody atezolizumab, as an immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), in combination with the vascular endothelial growth factor-targeting antibody bevacizumab, has been approved as standard-of-care (SOC) for HCC.1 However, only a subset of patients responds to ICB therapy. It may be that positive responses to ICB are associated with tumors that already have an immune infiltrated (“hot”) TME, characterized by proinflammatory cytokines and immune cell infiltration. Such tumors may have therefore already induced a weak anti-tumor T cell response that can then be expanded and de-repressed by ICB.2 In this respect, oncolytic viruses (OVs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic approach to convert tumors with very poor immune infiltration (“cold”) into “hot” tumors by inflaming the TME with virus-induced cytokines and immunodominant viral antigens. The rationale is that this inflammation will also lead to the release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and the priming of anti-tumor T cells.3 ,4 However, a major potential drawback to this model is that the immunologic reality is that viral proteins will most likely be a source of immunodominant, non-tolerized antigens against which the immune system will be highly reactive, with high-affinity T cell receptor (TCR) and B cell receptor responses. In contrast, TAAs are, for the most part, likely to be immunosubdominant and, if any non-tolerized T cells exist at all in patients’ T cell repertoire, these are likely to be low-affinity TCR interactions.5 OV have demonstrated potential in various cancer models, including HCC, but their efficacy as monotherapies has been limited.6 Several OVs, such as herpes simplex virus, measles virus, and adenovirus, have advanced to clinical trials, and a pilot study of oncolytic adenovirus combined with PD-1 targeting nivolumab provided overall survival of 15.04 months and reversed ICB resistance.7 ,8 Based on investigational enabling toxicology studies, we conducted a first-in-human clinical study of vesicular stomatitis virus encoding human interferon β (VSV-hIFNβ) administered via intravenous (i.v.) injection using ultrasound guidance into liver tumors (this study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01628640).9 ,10 A total of 15 patients were treated (dose range 105 −1.8 × 107 50% tissue culture infectious dose). Subsequently patients were restricted to <50% hepatic tumor involvement, with no further cytokine release syndrome (CRS). Preliminary evidence of efficacy included one partial response by RECIST version 1.1 criteria and two patients with stable disease.

Therefore, given the use of anti-PD-L1 ICB as SOC therapy for HCC, combined with the hypothesis that OV therapy can reset the TME of HCC from immunosuppressive to immune inflamed, thereby increasing the potential to generate ICB-boostable anti-tumor T cell responses, we tested combination therapy of VSV-IFNβ with anti-PD-L1 ICB in a preclinical model of HCC.11,12 We generated HCC tumors in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice using the h-Met/β-catenin Sleeping Beauty (SB) transposon system.12,13 Critically, we showed that this model is susceptible to treatment with ICB such that ~50% of tumor-bearing mice are reproducibly cured long term (>100 days) of disease, mimicking the human therapy in which not all patients are successfully treated by anti-PD-L1 ICB alone. However, contrary to our expectations, when anti-PD-L1 ICB was combined with VSV-IFNβ OV therapy in the SB model, we observed that VSV-IFNβ inhibited the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-L1 ICB alone.12 Although PD-L1 expression is normally associated with tumors and myeloid cells, PD-L1 expression on CD8 T cells can contribute to immunosuppression through engagement of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and may predict responsiveness to ICB therapy.14,15 Therefore, given the central role of CD8 T cells in the anti-tumor response in our HCC model and that the significance of PD-L1 expression on CD8 T cells in response to ICB remains unclear, we evaluated PD-L1 expression on CD8 T cells and how it is modulated upon ICB treatment. The SB-HCC model recapitulates important features of the human disease both histologically and immunologically in that it is partially but not completely susceptible to anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy.12 Therefore, in the present study, we have exploited this model to understand the basis of how HCC induces a weak T cell response to itself, how anti-PD-L1 ICB can be used to enhance that response, and how OV therapy can immunologically interfere with that therapeutic anti-TAA T cell response. Overall, our data here will guide the rational design of therapies in which ICB is used alone, or is combined with oncolytic therapy, for the treatment of HCC.

RESULTS

Immunophenotyping of the h-Met β-catenin SB-induced model of HCC

We used a murine SB transposon model to co-express h-Met and S45Y-β-catenin to mimic the pathogenesis of human HCC, allowing us to investigate the immune landscape and therapeutic responses to ICB and OV within a controlled, immunocompetent environment.12,13 To characterize the T cell response to the h-Met/β-catenin SB model of HCC, we used mass cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) to identify the phenotypes of T cells induced by the presence of tumor. The frequency of T cells in the total cell population in the normal liver was approximately 50% (~35% CD8+ T cells and 20% CD4+ T cells). The presence of HCC tumor induced significant relative changes in these populations in that CD8+ T cells became progressively enriched in tumor-bearing mice, suggesting development of an anti-tumor T cell response. Conversely, CD4+ T cells were consistently decreased in tumor-bearing mice compared to tumor-free mice at all time points (Figures 1 and S1D). No significant difference in levels of other immune cell types was observed between tumor-free and tumor-bearing mice, and the patterns of modulation were similar across time points (Figure S1). Samples from tumor-free or tumor-bearing livers harvested on days 28, 42, and 56 showed clear separation of data (Figures 1A, 1B, and S2A) and distinct immune cell distributions between the two groups. From a summation of the data at all time points, the presence of tumor induced a significant increase in the relative proportion of CD8+ T cells (69.65% ± 4.18%), with a corresponding decrease in the proportion of CD4+ T cells (30.35% ± 7.67%) compared to tumor-free mice (Figures 1C and 1D). In more detail, the frequency of PD-1 expression in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations of tumor-bearing mice increased significantly from those levels in tumor-free mice (0.315 ± 0.136–0.842 ± 0.270 in CD4+ T cells and 0.150 ± 0.085–3.005 ± 1.003 in CD8+ T cells) (Figures 1E and 1G). Most notably, median levels of CD39 expression in CD8+ cytotoxic T cells was increased to 2.128 ± 0.436 in HCC mice, compared to 0.893 ± 0.213 in tumor-free mice, and LAG3 expression was increased from 0.103 ± 0.122 in tumor-free mice to 1.300 ± 0.666 in SB-HCC mice (all p < 0.001) (Figures 1E and 1G). No significant changes in TIM3 or LAG3 expression were seen in the CD4+ T cell population between tumor-free and tumor-bearing mice (Figures 1E and 1F) and TIM3 was also not altered in the CD8+ T cell population. Despite tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) extracted from tumor-bearing mice showing a trend toward higher granzyme B expression in the CD8+ T cell population, this did not reach significance between HCC-bearing and tumor-free mice (Figures 1E and 1G). The comparative changes of T cell profiles in tumor-free and HCC-bearing mice between days 28, 42, and 56 were largely similar (Figures S2B and S2C). Overall, these data show that the development of SB-HCC was associated with progressive infiltration of CD8+ T cells into livers but that these CD8+ T cells acquired an exhausted/dysfunctional phenotype characterized by significantly higher levels of CD39, PD-1, and LAG3 expression.

Anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy and cytotoxic CD8+ T cell infiltration in the murine SB-HCC model

When SB-HCC-bearing C57BL/6 mice were treated with two cycles of three doses of anti-PD-L1 antibody, we reproducibly observed cure rates of about 50% compared to treatment with immunoglobulin G (IgG) isotype control antibody over multiple different experiments (Figures 2A and 2B; Table S1). Tumor-bearing liver samples harvested after the first or second cycle of treatment on days 28 and 42, respectively, contained reduced numbers of CD4+ T cells compared to tumor-free livers irrespective of whether they were treated with anti-PD-L1 ICB or control IgG (Figure 2C). In contrast, as shown in Figure 1, CD8+ T cells were significantly increased in tumor-bearing livers at day 28 without ICB therapy but were not significantly increased in relative proportion by a single cycle of ICB treatment (Figure 2C). Although there was a trend toward increased levels of CD8+ T cells in tumor-bearing livers treated with two cycles of anti-PD-L1 ICB compared to control IgG in some mice, this did not reach significance across multiple animals (Figure 2C).

Treatment with the first cycle of anti-PD-L1 ICB significantly reduced the levels of PD-L1 on all cells in tumor-bearing livers and specifically on tumor-bearing liver-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, compared to tumor-bearing livers treated with control IgG (Figure 2D). Interestingly, analysis of total liver cells showed a rebound of PD-L1 at day 42 to levels comparable to those in tumor-free livers (Figure 2D). Interestingly, we also observed this rebound of PD-L1 expression in IgG-treated mice (that were not treated with anti-PD-L1 ICB). This effect probably reflects a progressive increase in the immunosuppressive nature of this slowly developing SB-HCC model contributed by the cellular stromal components of the TME as documented in Figures S1 and S3. However, the extent of the PD-L1 rebound was predominant in ICB-treated mice. This rebound of PD-L1 expression was even more marked on infiltrating CD8+ T cells and rose to significantly higher levels than in tumor-free mice at day 42 (Figure 2D). In tumor-bearing livers, this rebound of PD-L1 expression on CD8+ T cells trended toward being increased following ICB therapy compared to control IgG treatment but did not reach significance (Figure 2D). These data suggest that the therapeutic effects of ICB therapy may manifest early after antibody therapy and that sustained ICB may even induce significant tumor escape by inducing high levels of rebound PD-L1 expression on relevant cells.

Treatment of SB-HCC with anti-PD-L1 ICB leads to rebound PD-L1 expression but reduced levels of PD-1 and CD39

In light of the curative response in about 50% of tumor-bearing animals to anti-PD-L1 ICB (Figures 2A and 2B), we investigated the effects of anti-PD-L1 treatment on the baseline T cell profiles that we had observed in tumor-bearing mice (Figures 1 and 2). Liver samples from SB-HCC tumor-bearing mice treated with intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of anti-PD-L1 ICB for two cycles (three doses per cycle) were analyzed at the end of each treatment cycle (days 28 and 42) and 1 week after the completion of treatment (day 56). Multiple predictive markers, including PD-L1 expression, TIL density, and specific CD8+ T cell phenotypes, have been proposed to assess response to ICB therapy.16 Unsupervised clustering was performed on CyTOF data from CD45 immune cell infiltrates, showing a significant modulation of the CD8+ T cell population (cluster 1), which was the most abundant (~40%) upon ICB treatment (Figure S3). The absolute number of CD8+ TILs (filtered by CD45, TCRβ, CD3, CD4, and CD8 T cell markers) was highest on day 28 after the first cycle of anti-PD-L1 ICB injection (Figure 3A), but, consistent with the flow cytometry data in Figure 2, they gradually reduced in level over time (days 42 and 56) (Figures 3A and 3B). Also consistent with the data of Figure 2, after one cycle of ICB (day 28), the frequency of PD-L1 expression was low (0.1022 ± 0.049), indicating effective treatment with the anti-PD-L1 antibody correlated with the therapy observed (Figures 2B and 3C). However, a significant rebound in PD-L1 expression was observed on days 42 (1.170 ± 0.269, p = 0.0011) and 56 (1.215 ± 0.179, p < 0.001) despite ongoing anti-PD-L1 treatment (Figures 3C, 3D and 3E). Serial CyTOF analyses following ICB therapy showed that the PD-L1 rebound effect was not limited to tumor-infiltrating T cells but also to other immune cell types in the TME such as myeloid cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages (Figure S3D). These data are consistent with our previous findings that multiple cell types in addition to tumor cells and T cells are major sources of PD-L1 expression12 and show that the TME can regain a highly immunosuppressive phenotype following ICB treatment. ICB therapy also induced a significant increase in regulatory T cell (Treg) infiltration early after treatment (Figure S4C), as well as trends—which did not reach significance—toward increased levels of IFN-γ, interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-10, IL-12, and CCL5 in ICB-treated livers compared to untreated HCC-bearing livers (Figure S5). Interestingly, although induction of IL-1β and IFN-α were significantly elevated in VSV-treated mice, levels of IFN-γ, a key effector cytokine in anti-tumor activity that was induced by ICB treatment, were significantly reduced in mice treated with the combination of VSV and ICB (Figure S5). Overall, these data suggest that the PD-L1 rebound effect is likely due to multiple mechanisms, including a tumor-intrinsic increase in PD-L1 in response to increasing levels of IFN-γ in the TME, as well as extrinsic effects, including changes in the cytokine milieu, some of which may be mediated by an influx of Treg cells. In contrast to the rebound of PD-L1 expression, sustained anti-PD-L1 therapy was associated with a significant fall off of expression of CD39 and PD-1 between days 42 and 56 (Figures 3C and 3E). Expressions of granzyme B, LAG3, and TIM3 on the total T cell population were not significantly altered between all time points of ongoing ICB therapy (Figures 3C and 3D). Taken together, these data show that anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy initially reduced the expression of PD-L1 on infiltrating CD8+ T cells but that this was followed by a significant rebound, suggesting that time-limited anti-PD-L1 therapy may be therapeutically superior to sustained treatment. CD8+ T cells induced by anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy showed high levels of both CD39 and PD-1, which reduced in levels significantly with persistent anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy.

Interaction of an anti-viral response with the ICB-enhanced anti-tumor T cell response

We have previously shown that the combination of anti-PD-L1 ICB with VSV-IFNβ oncolytic virotherapy was reproducibly unable to improve survival compared to ICB alone and in most cases repeatedly diminished its efficacy irrespective of the timing of the virus treatment relative to ICB administration. Thus, when VSV was administered either with or after ICB, the CD8+ T cell-dependent therapeutic benefit of ICB was diminished.12 Therefore, in the present study, we investigated how the different time points of VSV administration influenced the CD8+ T cell levels both qualitatively and quantitatively. To understand how VSV virotherapy detracts from ICB treatment in this model, single-cell isolation and CyTOF analysis of TIL was performed after treatment with either VSV monotherapy at different schedules or in combination with ICB treatment (Figure 4A). A total of 207,853 liver-infiltrating T cells were categorized into 6 distinct clusters based on conventional T cell markers, including TCRβ, CD3, CD4, and CD8 (Figure 4B). As before (Figure 2), the presence of tumor induced a population of CD8+ T cells that predominantly exhibited highly exhausted and dysfunctional phenotypes characterized by the co-expression of PD-1, CD39, EOMES, and LAG3 (Figure 4B). This population was further expanded by ICB treatment, suggesting that this is the population of anti-tumor CD8+ T cells responsible for therapy with anti-PD-L1 ICB. Upon addition of the immunogenic VSV-IFNβ OV to the system at both early (E) and intermediate (M) time points, the relative proportion of exhausted T cells was increased (Figure 4B). There was a consistent trend toward increased absolute numbers of infiltrating CD8+ T cells following VSV treatment compared to tumor-free, tumor-bearing, or ICB-treated mice, although this did not reach significance (Figure S4A). Interestingly, although the absolute T cell numbers between VSV and VSV+ICB-treated mice were not different (Figure S4A), the proportion of CD8+ T cells between these groups gradually increased both within and across time points (Figure S4C). These data are consistent with a model in which expansion of the viral specific CD8+ T cell population outcompetes a weaker anti-tumor T cell population in an immune compartment of limited capacity. Furthermore, we also observed the induction of three distinct classes of CD8+ T cells—active type 1, type 2, and type 3—by VSV therapy that were not present in the absence of virus (Figures 4C and 4D), and increases in these subsets were predominantly responsible for the immune activity observed during the early (E), intermediate (M), and late (L) time points of VSV treatment, respectively. Thus, administration of VSV-IFNβ at the early time point significantly increased levels of active type 1 CD8+ T cells (CD39INT granzyme B+ ) detected at days 28 and 42 with a fall off at day 56 (Figure 4D). This subset in particular was significantly amplified by the addition of ICB therapy at day 28 (Figure 4D). The VSV-reactive active type 2 CD8+ T cells, characterized by co-expression of CD39INT , PD-1, and granzyme B were amplified when ICB was combined with VSV therapy (Figures 4C and 4D), suggesting their critical role in the deleterious effect on ICB-responsive T cells by distracting them from anti-tumor activity to anti-viral responses. Minimal induction of active type 1 CD8+ T cells was observed on day 42 in ICB monotherapy; however, VSV and VSV+ICB treatments had a greater impact on this population (Figure 4D). Most significantly, while these three viral-responsive T cell subsets expanded upon VSV-IFNβ administration, the putative anti-tumor CD8+ T cells (exhausted phenotype) induced by ICB consistently contracted rapidly after VSV was given at early, intermediate, or late time points (Figure 4D), indicating a depletion of the anti-tumor T cell response by the more immunodominant anti-viral T cell response. All three of these viral-responsive subsets of CD8+ T cells expressed higher levels of granzyme B compared to ICB-responsive CD8+ TILs (Figure 4C), suggesting that both the quality and the quantity of the anti-viral T cell response was superior to that of the anti-tumor T cell response. At day 28 post-VSV treatment, we also observed a modest downregulation of levels of CD45+ CD11c+ dendritic cells in the tumor-bearing livers, which was not significantly different from VSV+ICB therapy. This population also gradually diminished over time (days 28–56) irrespective of treatment (Figures S4B and S4C). Interestingly, however, we also observed that the levels of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) were significantly reduced across all treatment groups (ICB, VSV, and ICB+VSV) compared to untreated HCC-bearing mice (Figure S5). These data suggest that, although the quantitative levels of dendritic cells may not be affected by virotherapy treatment ± ICB, VSV treatment may be functionally inhibiting levels of (tumor) antigen presentation through loss of GM-CSF function. Interestingly, the presence of SB-HCC tumors consistently reduced the proportion of liver-infiltrating CD4+ T cells as seen above, which ICB did not significantly restore (Figures 1, 2, and 4D). The induction of anti-viral CD8+ T cell expansion by VSV therapy, at the expense of the anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response, also generally coincided with a reduced proportion of CD4+ T cells (especially in the presence of added ICB). Taken together, these data show a significant upregulation and dominance of viral-specific T cells in response to VSV therapy that outcompetes and attenuates the anti-tumor T cell response elicited by ICB treatment.

Given that the development of SB-HCC was associated with increased levels of both CD39 and PD-1 on infiltrating CD8+ T cells in the absence of ICB therapy (Figure 1G), we further analyzed the virally induced CD8+ T cell subsets for their expression of CD39. High CD39 expression on T cells is commonly associated with T cell exhaustion resulting from prolonged exposure to tumors,17 whereas intermediate CD39 has been associated with a less dysfunctional anti-tumor phenotype.18 As before, levels of liver-infiltrating CD39Hi CD8 T cells increased significantly in the presence of HCC tumor compared to tumor-free livers (Figure 4E). Consistent with the induction of a more therapeutic phenotype, ICB treatment was associated with a trend toward a higher proportion of CD39INT CD8+ T cells (Figure 4E). Whereas neither early VSV alone nor early VSV+ICB changed the relative proportions of CD39INT CD8+ T cells, when administered at intermediate or late time points, VSV alone induced higher proportions of CD39INT CD8+ T cells than in tumor-bearing mice treated with ICB alone (Figure 4E). The addition of ICB with either intermediate or late VSV treatment increased the proportion of CD39Hi CD8+ T cells, with a corresponding decrease in the proportion of CD39INT CD8+ T cells (Figure 4E). In addition to the effects of ICB on increasing the putatively inhibitory CD39Hi phenotype in combination with VSV at intermediate or late treatment time points at the expense of a more therapeutic CD39INT phenotype, there was also a strong positive correlation between induction of the CD39Hi CD8 T cell phenotype and PD-L1 expression in livers of mice treated with VSV at intermediate and late time points (Figure 4F). Moreover, the putative anti-tumor, exhausted CD8+ T cells associated with ICB-mediated therapy expressed significantly higher levels of CD39 than did any of the active type 1, type 2, or type 3 subsets of anti-viral CD8+ T cells (Figure 4C).

Overall, these data suggest that the CD8+ T cells induced by VSV are both quantitatively and qualitatively (CD39INT /CD39low) fitter than the tumor-induced, exhausted (CD39Hi) CD8 T cells that are the target of ICB therapy.

The antigenic specificity of the T cell response shifts in response to both virus and tumor escape

We confirmed the replacement of anti-tumor CD8+ T cells with anti-viral CD8+ T cells by analyzing the clonality of CD8+ TILs using a panel of antibodies targeting TCR variable β (TCR Vβ) chains. CD8+ TCR usage in livers of tumor-free mice closely resembled that of the naive CD8+ T cell repertoire (Figure 5A). In contrast, tumor progression in SB-HCC significantly shifted the repertoire, favoring increased usage of some TCR chains, such as TCR Vβ8.1/2, while reducing usage of others, such as TCR Vβ5.1/2 (Figures 5A and 5B). Upon anti-PD-L1 ICB treatment, a shift in TCR Vβ clonotype usage was observed, with distraction from the predominant clonotypes of tumor alone (e.g., TCR Vβ8.1/2) to other TCR Vβ clonotypes (e.g., TCR Vβ8.3). Treatment with VSV-IFNβ reproducibly induced a dominant clonotype using TCR Vβ13 (Figures 5A and 5C). Mice treated with VSV monotherapy showed a significant switch in TCR usage to include Vβ13 and four other Vβ chains, and their specificity for the immunodominant VSV-N52–59 peptide was confirmed using tetramer analysis (Figures S6A and S6B), consistent with published reports of TCR usage against the immunodominant N52–59 epitope of the VSV-N protein.19 Similarly, while ICB treatment appeared to focus T cell responses on certain TCR usages (e.g., the Vβ8.3 TCR), the addition of VSV-IFNβ to ICB therapy shifted the T cell response away from T cells expressing Vβ8.3 toward a dominant clonotype using TCR Vβ13 (Figures 5A and 5B). Tumor-bearing mice developed a significantly greater diversity of V(D)J combinations, combined with an increased number of clonotypes compared to tumor-free mice (Figures S7A and S7B). These data suggest the development of an anti-tumor immune response, which is consistent with the susceptibility of these SB tumors to anti-PD-L1 ICB. Addition of ICB shifted the pattern of V(D)J combinations and seemed to focus the T cell response in terms of numbers of dominant clonotypes, suggestive of an augmented, more specific anti-tumor T cell response (Figures S7A and S7B). Treatment with VSV generated a significantly increased diversity of the T cell repertoire compared to tumor-free or tumor-bearing mice ± ICB (Figure S7A) with a corresponding shift in the frequency of different clonotypes (Figure S7B). Addition of ICB therapy to VSV treatment appeared to maintain the high diversity of the putative anti-viral response (Figure S7A) with a moderate focusing of the clonotypes in use (Figure S7B). A similar pattern was reflected by variations in CDR3 length and sequences. Thus, a moderate expansion of the repertoire induced by tumor alone became more focused with the addition of ICB (Figures S7C and S7D), with a significantly increased diversity of CDR3 lengths upon the addition of VSV treatment ± ICB (Figures S7C and S7D). Taken together, these data are consistent with a model in which a moderate anti-tumor T cell response develops in response to HCC and which is expanded by ICB; however, upon treatment with VSV, a much more diverse anti-viral T cell repertoire is induced that replaces, or is at least superimposed upon, the anti-tumor T cell response. Characterization of the TCR α and β chains used in each of these responses will help to identify both dominant anti-tumor and anti-viral T cell receptors. These data reveal an antagonistic effect between the induced anti-viral CD8+ T cell response and the preexisting anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response that was mediated through shifts in CD8+ TIL subtypes and TCR Vβ clonotype usage, with the immunodominant VSV response predominant regardless of treatment timing.

To investigate the antigenic targets/specificity of the T cell responses presented by these TCR usage patterns at a high level, pooled SB-HCC tumors from mice that had not been treated at all (three pooled SB-HCC lines) (no treatment) or that had escaped treatment with VSV-IFNβ (two pooled SB-HCC lines) (VSV ESC), anti-PD-L1 ICB (three pooled SB-HCC lines) (ICB ESC), or combined ICB+VSV treatment (two pooled SB-HCC lines) (ICB+VSV ESC) were used as targets for co-culture ex vivo with splenocytes harvested previously from untreated naive C57BL/6 mice (naive), VSV-IFNβ-treated mice (VSV), or anti-PD-L1 ICB-treated mice (ICB) (Figure 5D). Splenocytes from treatment-naive mice could be stimulated very weakly to secrete IFN-γ by SB-HCC tumors from mice that had received no treatment, confirming that SB-HCC tumors are weakly immunogenic (Figure 5D). However, SB-HCC VSV ESC tumor cells were significantly more immunogenic to splenocytes from naive C57BL/6 mice and induced significant levels of IFN-γ secretion (Figure 5D), indicating that escape from viral oncolysis is associated with the de novo expression of highly immunogenic epitopes.12 This finding is consistent with our reports that HCC escapes VSV treatment through the acquisition of genomic mutations that decrease susceptibility to viral replication/oncolysis and that a subset of those mutations generates immunogenic neo-antigenic epitopes that are de novo targets for T cell recognition.20 ,21 SB-HCC ICB ESC tumors were even less immunogenic than untreated SB-HCC tumors to splenocytes from naive C57BL/6 mice (Figure 5D), evidence that effective immunotherapy with anti-PD-L1 drives a loss of immunogenicity in ESC tumor cells, which allows them to evade the anti-tumor T cell populations induced by the weakly immunogenic SB-HCC. Finally, SB-HCC (ICB+VSV-IFNβ) ESC tumors were significantly more immunogenic than SB-HCC ICB ESC tumors to splenocytes from naive C57BL/6 mice, presumably reflecting the emergence of tumor cell variants expressing immunogenic variants of proteins that reduced their sensitivity to VSV oncolysis.

While SB-HCC tumors from untreated mice were poorly but significantly immunogenic on co-culture with splenocytes from naive C57BL/6 mice, splenocytes from SB-HCC-bearing mice treated with VSV-IFNβ did not recognize SB-HCC tumors from untreated mice at all (no detectable IFN-γ secretion) (Figure 5D). This finding is consistent with our data showing that treatment with VSV generates a shift in T cell clonotypes away from tumor-reactive T cells toward anti-viral T cell populations. Interestingly, SB-HCC VSV ESC tumor cells were very good targets for splenocytes from SB-HCC-bearing mice treated with VSV-IFNβ (Figure 5D). These data confirm that the evolution of a virus escape phenotype by SB-HCC tumors is associated with the evolution of mutations that both reduce viral replication/oncolysis and simultaneously generate neo-antigenic epitopes that are highly immunogenic.20,21 However, the potent immunogenicity of SB-HCC VSV ESC tumor cells to splenocytes from SB-HCC-bearing mice treated with VSV-IFNβ was significantly reduced when ESC tumors from mice treated with both VSV-IFNβ and ICB were the targets (Figure 5D). This may reflect the finding that the combination of ICB with VSV-IFNβ expands and specifically amplifies the anti-viral T cell subsets identified in Figure 4 (active type 1, 2, and 3) and not the weaker anti-tumor T cells, with the result that tumor cells evolving immunogenic mutations associated with escape from viral oncolysis are more readily cleared. Finally, SB-HCC ICB ESC tumor cells were completely immunologically invisible to splenocytes from SB-HCC-bearing mice treated with VSV-IFNβ (Figure 5D), confirming once again that the CD8+ T cell populations induced following viral infection strongly outcompete the weaker populations of preexisting anti-SB-HCC CD8+ T cells.

SB-HCC tumors from untreated mice were highly immunogenic (high levels of IFN-γ) on co-culture with splenocytes from SB-HCC-bearing C57BL/6 mice treated with ICB, reflecting the expansion of anti-tumor T cell populations by the anti-PD-L1 treatment (Figure 5D). However, SB-HCC VSV ESC tumors from mice treated with VSV-IFNβ were completely invisible to splenocytes from SB-HCC-bearing C57BL/6 mice treated with ICB. Thus, anti-PD-L1 ICB treatment expands and amplifies CD8+ T cells reactive to SB-HCC but not to SB-HCC VSV ESC tumors. We propose that this is because, presumably, SB-HCC VSV ESC tumors are antigenically very different from parental SB-HCC because of the acquisition of the mutations needed to escape virus infection/replication/oncolysis. SB-HCC ICB ESC tumors were much less immunogenic than SB-HCC-untreated tumors to splenocytes from SB-HCC-bearing C57BL/6 mice treated with ICB, reflecting the evolution of various mechanisms of immune escape by the escaped tumor cells from the ICB-enhanced anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response (Figure 5D). Finally, SB-HCC (VSV+ICB) ESC tumors were completely non-immunogenic in this assay to splenocytes from SB-HCC-bearing C57BL/6 mice treated with ICB, once again reflecting the antigenic shift from anti-tumor to anti-viral CD8+ T cell responses induced by the presence of immunodominant VSV antigens (Figure 5D).

With respect to the antigenic specificity of the anti-viral and anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses, we confirmed that VSV-IFNβ therapy consistently induced a memory response to a known immunodominant epitope derived from the VSV nucleocapsid protein (VSV-N52–59) (Figure 5E). This anti-VSV T cell response was significantly enhanced when ICB therapy was combined with VSV-IFNβ (Figure 5E), showing that the ICB was focused on anti-viral T cells and away from the therapeutic anti-tumor CD8+ T cells. Splenocytes from mice treated with VSV-IFNβ alone showed a significant reactivity to the negative control SIINFEKL epitope (Figure 5E), consistent with our previous observations that treatment with VSV induces generalized T cell activation.22 Interestingly, this generalized T cell activation was significantly increased in splenocytes from mice treated with both VSV-IFNβ and ICB (Figure 5E).

We have previously shown that both melanoma and HCC tumors escape from VSV treatment in vivo through genomic mutation to avoid viral replication and oncolysis and that a subset of those virus-escape-mediating mutations generate immunogenic neo-antigenic epitopes, which are de novo targets for T cell recognition.19 ,20 We characterized one specific mutation in the wild-type cellular CSDE1 gene (CSDE1WT) that converts a proline to a serine at amino acid 5 (CSDE1P5S), which plays a critical role in HCC escape from VSV-IFNβ virotherapy by reducing viral replication. In addition, this mutation creates a non-tolerized, highly immunogenic epitope (CSDE1P5S 1–8) from the highly tolerized, non-immunogenic self-CSDE1WT 1–8 epitope against which a potent CD8+ T cell response is raised in vivo as tumor cells evolve to escape VSV oncolysis.12,21 As expected, SB-HCC-bearing mice left untreated or treated with either VSV-IFNβ, ICB, or a combination of ICB+VSV-IFNβ had no T cell response against the self-CSDE1WT 1–8 epitope, confirming that oncolytic virotherapy was unable to break tolerance to this important self-protein. However, treatment of SB-HCC with VSV-IFNβ induced a CD8+ T cell response to the mutated CSDE1P5S 1–8 epitope, showing that virotherapy exerted a powerful selective pressure on tumor cells to evolve resistance to VSV oncolysis (Figure 5F). Interestingly, the combination of ICB with VSV-IFNβ significantly diminished the cellular T cell response to CSDE1P5S 1–8 (Figure 5F), which is the opposite effect to that of ICB on the anti-viral (VSV-N52–59) T cell response in Figure 5E. Using an ex vivo functional assay, the data of Figures S6C–S6E show that when anti-tumor CD8+ T cells were incubated in vitro with target antigen-positive tumor cells they proliferated well as evidenced by dilution of CellTrace Violet dye (Figure S6C), secreted high levels of IFN-γ (Figure S6D), and killed the target cells (Figure S6E). However, upon co-culture with increasing numbers of anti-VSV CD8+ T cells, we observed significant reductions in all three parameters (T cell proliferation, IFN-γ secretion, and target cell killing), confirming that competition between T cell populations can be recapitulated in vitro (Figures S6C–S6E). These data are consistent with the observation that the immunodominant anti-viral response outcompetes weaker immunosubdominant anti-tumor T cell responses in vivo and that concurrent ICB therapy acts predominantly on the stronger (anti-viral) response compared to the weaker (anti-tumor) response.

DISCUSSION

We showed previously that the hMet/β-catenin SB model of HCC resembles the human disease in that it is partially but not completely (~50% of mice cured long term >150 days) susceptible to SOC anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy dependent upon CD8+ T cells.12 Our laboratory has previously explored the oncolytic and immunotherapeutic potential of VSV in solid-tumor models of melanoma and colorectal cancer. In the present study, no side effects were observed following VSV administration in the immunocompetent SB-HCC C57BL/6 model. Although VSV is neurotoxic, viral replication is inherently sensitive to the anti-viral action of type I IFN, and so to refine the specificity and safety of the therapeutic virus further, we engineered VSV to express IFN-β. We have performed extensive toxicology and pharmacology studies in rats and rhesus macaques to demonstrate that intrahepatic injection of VSV expressing species-matched IFN-β is well tolerated.9 ,10 Expression of IFN-β from VSV not only provides improved safety of the virus as above but also increases the efficacy of the therapy, as shown by the comparison of VSV encoding species-matched versus incompatible IFN-β and its therapeutic efficacy in xenograft mouse and immunocompetent rat models of HCC.23 We reasoned that this improved safety profile, in combination with increased inflammation induced by the release of virally encoded IFNβ, makes VSV-IFNβ an attractive candidate for combination with ICB therapy. However, the combination of ICB with oncolytic VSV-IFNβ, initially intended to enhance SOC ICB therapy by inducing inflammation of the TME of HCC, inhibited the therapy of ICB alone.12 To develop rational new combination immunotherapies for HCC, here, we sought to understand the mechanism of ICB-mediated therapy of HCC at the T cell level, the reasons for the inability of ICB to cure 100% of mice as is the case clinically, and how OV therapy leads to a significant attritional effect on therapy with ICB alone.

Figure 1 shows that the presence of tumor induced a significant increase in the relative proportion of CD8+ T cells infiltrating into tumor-bearing livers, confirming that this model of HCC can induce a T cell response against itself. However, our data also explain how these tumors become highly immunosuppressive. Thus, tumor progression was associated with a significant relative depletion of CD4+ T cells in the liver, suggesting that the loss of CD4+ T cell help may reduce the activity of the anti-tumor CD8+ T cells generated by the presence of tumor. Levels of PD-L1 also progressively increased in liver-infiltrating CD8+ T cells in tumor-bearing mice, contributing further to an immunosuppressive TME. In addition, the infiltrating CD8+ T cells acquired an exhausted phenotype characterized by very high levels of CD39, PD-1, and LAG3, molecules that could provide alternative targets for ICB/immunotherapy along with anti-PD-L1.

Consistent with increasing levels of PD-L1 in the SB-HCC, treatment with anti-PD-L1 antibody reproducibly cured about 50% of treated mice (Figure 2). Levels of CD8+ T cells in tumor-bearing livers at day 28 after a single cycle of ICB treatment were not increased relative to untreated mice (Figure 2C), and continued treatment did not significantly increase CD8+ T cell infiltration (Figures 2C and 2D), suggesting that ICB was enhancing the activity of anti-tumor T cells rather than their numbers. Most notably, although anti-PD-L1 ICB initially significantly reduced the levels of PD-L1 of both total liver cells and infiltrating CD8+ T cells (day 28), those levels rebounded significantly at day 42 despite continued ICB treatment (Figure 3E). We are currently testing the hypothesis that time-limited anti-PD-L1 therapy may therefore be therapeutically superior to sustained treatment, and we are investigating how sustained anti-PD-L1 ICB treatment increases long-term PD-L1 on infiltrating CD8+ T cells.

Conversely, CD8+ T cells exposed to anti-PD-L1 ICB showed initially high levels of both CD39 and PD-1, characteristic of tumor-bearing livers without ICB treatment (Figure 1G), with a significant reduction of marker expression with persistent anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy (Figure 3D). These data suggest the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 ICB (Figure 2B) against HCC is mediated at least in part by reduction in levels of CD39 and PD-1, and that combination of anti-PD-L1 with anti-CD39 and/or anti-PD-1 ICB could significantly improve immunotherapy against HCC.

Therefore, viewed in the context of the incomplete efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy in both pre-clinical and clinical scenarios, it seems likely that the variation in therapeutic outcome of ICB therapy for HCC can be explained in terms of a finely poised balance between multiple factors. These include the inherent tumor immunogenicity determined by the stochastic development of tumor-associated mutations in addition to common driver mutations, the strength of the resultant anti-tumor T cell response, the ability of anti-PD-L1 antibodies to reduce PD-L1 levels initially after early treatment, and the speed with which PD-L1 levels rebound following initial treatment. Our data suggest that the induction of reciprocal/compensatory inhibitory markers on TILs after ICB administration could be a potential mechanism of secondary resistance that sustains immunotolerance and facilitates tumor evasion. This hypothesis is consistent with previous studies reporting secondary resistance to PD-1 blockade in non-small cell lung cancer, associated with the upregulation of alternative immune checkpoint molecules (LAG-3 and TIM-3).24 In addition, anti-CD39 blockade was reported to rescue anti-PD-1 resistance and improve treatment outcome,25 and the anti-tumor efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 ICB enhanced by CD39 blockade (IPH5201) and CD39 disruption has been used to support an ongoing clinical study (MATISSE).26 ,27 Therefore, given that CD39 and LAG3 contribute to T cell dysfunction and immunosuppression in HCC, dual ICB therapy could be a potential strategy to overcome the adaptive resistance of PD-L1 ICB and synergistically reinvigorate anti-tumor T cell responses, a strategy that is currently being investigated in our laboratory.

We reported previously that the combination of anti-PD-L1 ICB with VSV-IFNβ oncolytic virotherapy could not improve survival compared to ICB alone, irrespective of the relative timings of OV and ICB.12 Our data here provide an immunological explanation for this antagonism between these two different immunotherapies. Thus, although the presence of HCC induced a population of putative anti-tumor but highly exhausted/dysfunctional CD8+ T cells responsible for therapy with anti-PD-L1 ICB (Figures 4C and 4D), the addition of the immunogenic VSV-IFNβ OV induced three distinct classes of CD8+ T cells—active type 1, type 2, and type 3 (Figure 4D)—responsible for the immune activity observed during early, intermediate, and late time points of VSV treatment, respectively. The data in Figure 5 show that the induction of a strong, immunodominant (high-affinity TCR interactions, non-tolerized T cell repertoire) anti-viral CD8+ T cell response rapidly outcompeted the weaker (likely low-affinity TCR interactions, mostly-tolerized T cell repertoire) anti-tumor CD8+ T cells and also led to a further relative depletion of CD4+ T cells. Figure 4 also shows that oncolytic VSV alone induced a strong, immunodominant anti-viral CD39INT / CD39low T cell response, which combined with ICB shifted toward a CD39Hi phenotype associated with greater levels of exhaustion/dysfunction. Therefore, these results further support the testing of therapies aimed at maintaining a CD39INT /CD39low T cell phenotype in combination with anti-PD-L1 ICB to support the persistence of more potent, less exhausted, and dysfunctional anti-tumor CD8+ T cells.

Using TCR profiling, Figure 5 shows that the preferential expansion of viral-responsive CD8+ T cells by VSV therapy failed to both maintain and expand, and even suppressed, the usage of TCRs putatively associated with the anti-tumor response (as seen in ICB treatment). These findings show that high TCR affinity interactions with immunodominant viral antigens generate anti-viral T cells of high fitness (such as those using the Vβ13 TCR), which can outcompete and even replace tumor-reactive T cells with likely low-affinity TCR-TAA binding. These findings underscore the disruptive influence of VSV on ICB efficacy, driven by the expansion of anti-viral CD8+ T cells at the expense of anti-tumor immune responses, and provide a mechanistic explanation for the loss of therapy that we consistently observed when VSV was combined with ICB therapy.12 These findings are also significant in that they show that OV therapy may not necessarily lead to the priming and expansion of anti-tumor T cells recognizing actionable TAA because the predominant T cell response to OV therapy may be anti-viral rather than anti-tumor.12,28–34

We also characterized the antigenic targets/specificity of the T cell responses to HCC at a high level represented by these TCR usage patterns (Figure 5). Thus, SB-HCC tumors express immunogenic determinants through the course of their development in vivo that stimulate a weak CD8+ T cell response to themselves (Figure 5D). Immunotherapy with anti-PD-L1 ICB enhanced anti-tumor T cell responses with specificity for these immunogenic antigens/epitopes (Figures 3 and 4), but it also applied strong selective pressure on tumor cells to escape therapy, resulting in the emergence of escape tumors with antigenic landscapes that are very different from the parental untreated SB-HCC in terms of T cell recognition (Figure 5D). Molecular identification of the antigenic determinants expressed by these ICB-escape tumors will elucidate how tumors evade ICB therapy at the cellular mutational level. We also show here how oncolytic virotherapy drives highly selective evolution of the tumor immunophenotype. Thus, we confirmed that the antigenic profile of virus escape tumors was driven primarily through the evolution of cellular mutations (e.g., CSDE1P5S), which help the cell avoid viral infection/replication/oncolysis (Figure 5F).21 Critically, the combination of OV with ICB shifted the CD8+ T cell response even more in favor of the stronger, immunodominant (anti-viral) (e.g., anti VSV-N52–59) response (Figure 5E) compared to the weaker, immunosubdominant (anti-tumor) response (Figure 5F). This finding has important implications for the development of combination therapies between OV and ICB: our data provide a cautionary warning that using ICB with OV may simply enhance anti-viral T cell responses at the expense of, and even lead to complete attrition of, anti-tumor T cell responses. Our data suggest that potential mechanisms underlying the competitive dynamics between anti-tumor and anti-viral responses are likely to be multi-factorial. In this respect, the data of Figures S4B and S4C suggest that the dominance of the anti-viral immune response over the anti-tumor response cannot be explained by quantitative changes in the levels of dendritic cells infiltrating tumor-bearing livers. However, the loss of GM-CSF from tumor-bearing livers upon virotherapy treatment (Figure S5) suggests that qualitative differences in antigen presenting cell (APC) function and subsets may be important and experiments to test this hypothesis are under way in our laboratory. Other mechanisms that may contribute to the weakening of the anti-tumor response in the presence of the anti-viral response in addition to changes in antigen presentation and cytokine profiles might include shifts in metabolic control of T cell responses. In this respect, we observed high levels of expression of metabolic regulator proteins such as CD39 and CD38 in exhausted T cell subsets (Figure 4C), which were reduced upon virotherapy treatment, consistent with previous studies that high levels of metabolic regulators are associated with immunosuppression and resistance against anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies.35,36 These data suggest that anti-viral T cells may be metabolically fitter (CD39INT /CD39low) than exhausted anti-tumor T cells (CD39Hi) and that the added intervention of ICB on these more active anti-viral T cells may further accentuate the loss of anti-tumor T cell efficacy. Based on these and other data we propose that the balance between anti-viral and anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses can be redressed in favor of anti-tumor immunity by incorporating tumor antigens into the OV such that the anti-viral response is now chimerized with the anti-tumor response.12 ,37–39 Therefore, we propose that it will be possible to develop highly personalized immunotherapies for HCC in which tumor gene expression data are combined with mutational/immunogenicity predictions to engineer VSV expressing multiple tumor-associated antigens, including cDNA libraries, to avoid tumor escape by antigen loss.12 ,37–39

In summary, we show here that a slowly developing anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response against SB-HCC becomes rapidly exhausted but is sensitive to anti-PD-L1 ICB. However, prolonged ICB therapy was associated with a rebound of PD-L1 expression, which may contribute to treatment failure. We also identify additional potential molecules and immune checkpoints, such as CD39 and LAG3, which may be valuable therapeutic targets to combine with anti-PD-L1 ICB in the development of improved immunotherapies for HCC. In addition, we show that the weak, exhausted, and dysfunctional anti-HCC CD8+ T cell response is outcompeted by a strong anti-viral CD8+ T cell response induced by therapy with oncolytic VSV-IFNβ and that combination OV and ICB therapy further detracts from the anti-tumor T cell response by preferentially enhancing the anti-viral T cell response. Finally, we show that SB-HCC tumors evolve their antigenic/immunogenic profiles very effectively in vivo when subjected to ICB, OV, or combination ICB+OV selective pressures to evade either or both of these therapies. We believe that our findings of competition between the anti-tumor and anti-viral T cell responses in the HCC model will be largely generalizable to other tumor types as shown by the data in a murine melanoma model both ex vivo (Figures S6C–S6E) and in vivo (data not shown). However, the extent of the competition between the immunodominant anti-viral response and the anti-tumor response will likely depend upon multiple factors, principal among which will be the immunogenicity of the tumor-associated antigens (purely self, near-self or highly immunogenic neo/mutated-self). which will be reflected in the affinity of the anti-tumor T cells. Therefore, we recommend that future developments for the immunotherapy of HCC should target multiple immune checkpoints expressed on the exhausted anti-tumor T cells, should avoid attritional competition by T cells recognizing more immunodominant antigenic targets compared to the largely tolerized low TCR affinity HCC TAAs, and should account for the rapidly evolving tumor phenotypes that emerge in the patient as a result of treatment-selective pressures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines and vesicular stomatitis viral preparation

Baby hamster kidney (BHK) cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin. All cell lines were cultured at 37 ° C, 5% CO2 , and 95% humidity. Cells were tested for mycoplasma using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza) every 6 months. VSV encoding IFNβ (VSV-IFNβ), wild-type CSDE (VSV-CSDEWT), and mutant CSDE (VSV-CSDEP5S) were rescued from the pXN2 cDNA plasmid and propagated as previously described.12,23,37 Briefly, BHK cells were infected at low multiplicity of infection, and cells were incubated for 48 h. Viral supernatant was collected, and cell debris was removed by centrifugation (3,000 rpm, 5 min, at 4 ° C). Supernatant was clarified through a 0.22-μM filter and subjected to superspeed centrifugation at 27,000 rpm using 10% sucrose cushion. Viral titer was determined using plaque assay, and viral aliquots were stored at −80 °C until used.

Mice models

C57BL/6 mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory and housed in the Animal Facility (Biosafety Level 2) at Mayo Clinic Rochester. All in vivo studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Mayo Clinic. The hMet S45Y β-catenin SB-HCC model was developed as previously described.12 We diluted 20 μg pT3-EF5a-hMet-V5, 20 μg pT3-EF5a-human β-catenin with S45Y mutation, and 1.6 μg pCMV/SB (the hyperactive SB expression vector) in sterile normal saline (0.9% NaCl). Hydrodynamic injection of prepared plasmids was performed at the lateral tail vein of mice in 5–7 s to induce slow-progressive liver tumor.

In vivo experiments

Following hydrodynamic injection on day 0, mice were treated with anti-PD-L1 antibody (BE0101) or control IgG2b isotype control (BE0090) purchased from Bio X Cell i.p. at a concentration of 200 μg per injection for two cycles (six doses total)—first cycle during days 20–25 and second cycle during days 34–39. VSV-IFNβ was delivered via i.v. tail vein injection at 5 × 107 PFU per dose on the same day as the antibody injection. Three different timings of VSV treatment were administered: early time point (days 20–25), intermediate time point (days 34–39), and late time point (days 48–53). Mice were euthanized after the first cycle of ICB injection (day 28), the second cycle of injection (day 42), and 1 week after two cycles of injection (day 56) for single-cell analysis.

Tissue processing and single-cell isolation

Mice were euthanized at indicated time points or when they reached endpoint, livers were harvested, and single cells were isolated. Livers were rinsed with ice-cold PBS and cut into pieces with a scalpel, followed by enzyme digestion using deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) and Liberase TL (Roche) performed at 37 ° C for 30 min, followed by mechanical isolation by pressing through a 70-μM filter. For splenocyte isolation, spleens were harvested and mashed through the filter without enzymatic digestion. Isolated cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and red blood cells were lysed using ACK lysis buffer. All samples were washed three times with cold PBS and resuspended in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum prior to further experimentation. Viable cell number was determined by trypan blue.

Single-cell analysis

Single-cell analysis was performed on freshly isolated single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained in flow cytometry staining buffer with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies specific to mouse CD3 (clone 17A2, dilution 1:500), CD4 (clone RM4–5, dilution 1:500), CD8 (clone 53–6.7, dilution 1:500), CD39 (clone Duha59, dilution 1:200), and PD-L1 (clone 10F.9G2, dilution 1:200) purchased from BioLegend. Viable T cells were gated by using fixable live-dead viability dye (Zombie NIR, dilution 1:1,500). For TCR Vβ study, mouse cells were stained with anti-mouse TCR Vβ screening panel (BD Pharmingen, catalog no. 557004) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Tumor-infiltrating T cells were gated on single cells/lymphocyte size exclusion/live cells/CD3+ . All samples were run on Cytek Aurora at the Mayo Clinic Core facility, Cell Analysis Flow Cytometry Core. Data were analyzed using FlowJo version 10.8.1 software.

For CyTOF, liver-infiltrating cells were stained and conducted by the Immune Monitoring Core and the Department of Immunology, Mayo Clinic (Rochester). Tumor-infiltrating T cells were characterized by 22 T cell markers, including T cell-type markers (TCRβ, CD3ε, CD4, CD8α) and T cell state markers (granzyme B, PD-1, PD-L1, CD39, LAG-3, TIM-3, EOMES, galectin-9, CD69, CD86, Fas, CD38, CD73, P2RX7, GLUT1, GATA-3, CD44, and CD62L). All markers that were used to characterize immunophenotypes and functional states in this study are summarized in Tables S2 and S3. Data clean-up was performed, exported, and analyzed using RStudio packages.

In vitro coculturing and re-stimulation assay

Tumor lines were isolated from the hMet S45Y β-catenin SB-HCC mice. The SB-HCC tumor targets were isolated from three different SB-HCC mice (no treatment) pooled together. VSV ESC were harvested from two pooled together SB-HCC liver tissues that had been treated with but escaped VSV therapy. Anti-PD-L1 ICB ESC were pooled from three different tumor-bearing mice that had been treated with anti-PD-L1 but had developed tumors. ICB+VSV ESC tumors were isolated from two pooled SB-HCC lines that had escaped from combined ICB+VSV therapy. The escaped tumors (1 × 106 cells) were pretreated with IFN-γ (200 U/mL for 24 h) and cocultured with splenocytes harvested from donor mice receiving either no treatment (naive), VSV-IFNβ, or anti-PD-L1 ICB at an effector-to-target ratio (E:T) of 10:1 in ELISpot wells (mouse IFN-γ ELISpot kit, R&D Systems, catalog no. EL485). For in vitro peptide re-stimulation assay, splenocytes (1 × 107) isolated from the indicated condition were cocultured in the presence of immunodominant VSV-N52–58 , ovalbumin-derived SIINFEKL, CSDEWT , or mutant CSDEP5S peptide (1 μg/mL per well) for 72 h. After coculturing, signals were developed, and the number of spots was counted.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.2 software. Single comparisons were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t tests. Multiple comparisons were analyzed using one-way or two-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) with a Tukey’s correction method. Survival study and hazard ratio were calculated using the log rank (Mantel-Cox) test. All data were represented as means ± SDs. All statistical analysis was performed at a 95% confidence interval, and asterisks represent significant differences (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001).
