Papillary fibroelastoma (PFE) is one of the most common primary cardiac tumors.1 Although it is often asymptomatic and typically discovered incidentally, many reports have identified PFE as the cause of cerebrovascular or cardiovascular events. Recognized as a slow-growing tumor, the average growth rate of PFE is approximately 0.5 ± 0.9 mm per year.2 Retrospective studies have also highlighted an association between hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and the development of PFEs, with most PFEs occurring on the aortic valve (47%) or along the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) (42%).3 However, to our knowledge, PFEs associated with the mitral valve in patients with HCM have not been documented in the literature. This paper presents a unique case of an echocardiographic mass on the mitral valve suggestive of PFE in a patient with HCM.

Case Presentation

The patient is a 66-year-old woman with a past medical history of hypertension, obstructive HCM, and NYHA functional class III heart failure secondary to LVOT obstruction on a beta-blocker, who first presented to our clinic for a chief complaint of dyspnea on exertion. She underwent a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) that showed a resting LVOT gradient of 60 mm Hg that increased to 200 mm Hg with Valsalva maneuver. As a result, the patient started treatment with mavacamten. As part of ongoing mavacamten surveillance, a routine TTE was obtained every 3 months. Although her resting and Valsalva LVOT gradient showed improvement (both <30 mm Hg) with medication uptitration, a new mass was incidentally identified. On a routine surveillance TTE, a well-circumscribed, spherical mass, with a diameter of approximately 0.77 cm, was found, which appeared attached to the atrial surface of the posterior mitral leaflet (Figure 1). A subsequent cardiac magnetic resonance revealed a 1.3- × 1.1- × 1.2-cm, well-circumscribed, mobile, rounded lesion in the same location, which is likely representative of PFE; however, myxomas or fibromas could not be ruled out (Figures 2A and 2B). After a thorough discussion with the patient and a cardiac surgeon, the decision was made to defer surgical resection at this time given her lack of underlying comorbidities and symptoms related to the PFE. During subsequent evaluations, the patient has remained asymptomatic, and surveillance TTEs have continued to show the PFE without morphologic changes and an LVOT gradient improvement to <30 mm Hg.

Discussion

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of cardiac PFE remains poorly understood primarily because PFEs are often discovered incidentally without clear indications of when they begin, how they progress, or what risk factors contribute to their development. Some PFEs are congenital, as described in case reports involving neonates and infants. However, most reported lesions are presumed to be acquired. In some studies, PFEs were found incidentally in patients with no prior evidence of the lesion on TTEs4 or during previous cardiac surgeries.5 Several hypotheses have been proposed for acquired PFEs, including hamartomatous growths, neoplastic components potentially triggered by viral infection or genetic factors, microthrombi deposition at sites of endothelial injury, and endothelial responses to hemodynamic or mechanical trauma.1,6,7

In patients with HCM, hemodynamic trauma caused by turbulent flow is suggested to be a primary factor in PFE formation. This association is further supported by findings from a study by Kumar et al.3 Specifically, the study noted that the dynamic LVOT obstruction in patients with HCM increases the risk of PFE formation at sites like the LVOT or aortic valve, where high-velocity blood jets are more likely to cause endothelial damage. In our case, we observed an echocardiographic mass suggestive of PFE in a patient with HCM, but at a different anatomic location—the atrial surface of the posterior mitral valve. This site experiences much less shear force than the LVOT and aortic valve. Additionally, the patient was receiving mavacamten that appropriately reduced the LVOT gradient (<30 mm Hg), as shown in subsequent echocardiogram findings. These factors suggest that hemodynamic stress from LVOT obstruction alone may not fully explain the pathogenesis of PFE in this case.

Complications

Many studies have investigated the association between echocardiographically suspected left-sided PFEs and an increased risk of embolization, including stroke and myocardial infarction.8 Additionally, depending on their size and mobility, mitral valve PFEs can impede left ventricular filling, often mimicking the clinical presentation of mitral stenosis.6 This obstruction can potentially lead to pulmonary hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and congestive heart failure.9 Likewise, a cohort study identified HCM as an independent factor contributing to the risk of stroke and peripheral embolization.10 Although no studies have specifically addressed this risk in HCM patients with concomitant PFE, we hypothesize that the risk is further compounded in this population. Based on the existing literature, close monitoring of echocardiographic PFE progression and timely surgical excision are recommended to help prevent these complications.6

Management

In our case, a shared decision was made to pursue nonsurgical management. Although the literature suggests that surgical resection is the gold standard treatment for symptomatic patients with PFEs, limited data support similar benefits for asymptomatic patients. In their large cohort analysis of patients with echocardiographically suspected PFEs, Tamin et al8 reported an increased risk of cerebrovascular events and mortality in patients who did not undergo surgical removal, compared with those who did. However, most of these patients had PFEs located on the aortic valve, preexisting cerebrovascular or cardiovascular disease, and presented symptoms at the time of diagnosis. Therefore, these data may not be directly applicable to our current case because our patient has no other comorbidities besides HCM (under treatment), presented with a presumptive PFE located on the mitral valve, and was clinically asymptomatic at the time of diagnosis. The case was discussed by a multidisciplinary team, including input from several cardiothoracic surgeons. Given the patient's asymptomatic presentation, absence of other cardiac abnormalities, and stable appearance of the valvular mass on prior TTEs, the shared decision was to continue periodic clinical evaluation and echocardiographic surveillance. Should there be significant changes in the lesion size or mobility, surgical management will be reconsidered. Although antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy is often suggested in high-risk patients who are not surgical candidates, or in asymptomatic patients with high-risk features PFE managed conservatively, no randomized controlled trials currently support this approach1,7

Conclusions

Although our findings reinforce the association between HCM and echocardiographic PFE, they also challenge the idea that hemodynamic trauma from LVOT obstruction is the primary factor in its formation. The location of the suspected PFE points to the possibility of other contributing mechanisms that warrant further investigation. Future research should aim to identify additional factors, including genetic or biochemical influences, that may play a role in the development and progression of PFEs in HCM.

Our case also emphasizes the need for individualized management of an incidental valvular mass suggestive of PFE. Although surgical intervention has traditionally been considered the gold standard for symptomatic patients with underlying comorbidities, some case studies have shown that conservative management can be an effective alternative, with no complications during the follow-up periods. However, there is still no clear consensus regarding the optimal timing for interval TTE, the indications for anticoagulant therapy, or disease progression that warrants surgery. Future research should focus on these areas to further support the role of nonsurgical management in patients with echocardiographic PFE diagnosis.

Study Limitation

One limitation of this case is the absence of a definitive diagnosis of the mitral valve mass at the time of writing. Although the characteristics of PFE on echocardiograms are well documented11 and align with those observed in our case, the differential diagnosis remains broad until confirmed by tissue pathology.
