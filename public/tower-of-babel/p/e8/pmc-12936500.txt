Melittin Acts as a Radiosensitizer for Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer by Inhibiting the DNA Damage Repair System and Inducing Apoptosis
Authors: Eunhui Seo, Hyunkwan Seo, Kyung-Ok Kim, Kyu Chan Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the radiosensitizing effects of melittin, the main active component of bee venom, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells. Given the limitations of radiotherapy due to intrinsic tumor resistance, we investigated whether melittin could enhance the efficacy of radiation by promoting apoptosis and impairing DNA damage repair mechanisms.Materials and MethodsNSCLC cell lines were treated with melittin and exposed to ionizing radiation. Clonogenic assays assessed radiosensitivity, while Western blot analyses examined DNA damage response proteins (DNA-PKcs, ATM, γ-H2AX) and apoptosis markers (Bax, cleaved caspase-3). In vivo, a BALB/c nude mouse xenograft model was used to evaluate the therapeutic effect of combined treatment. Tumor volume, weight, and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining were analyzed.ResultsMelittin significantly enhanced radiosensitivity in NSCLC cells, as indicated by decreased colony formation. It reduced phosphorylation of DNA-PKcs and ATM while increasing γ-H2AX, suggesting impaired DNA double-strand break repair. Apoptotic activity increased with elevated Bax and cleaved caspase-3 levels. In vivo, combination therapy markedly inhibited tumor growth, as supported by IHC evidence of increased apoptosis and DNA damage.ConclusionMelittin is a potent radiosensitizer in NSCLC, impairing DNA repair and promoting apoptosis. These findings support its potential as a novel adjunct to radiotherapy to improve NSCLC treatment outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally in both men and women and ranks as the second most diagnosed cancer.1 Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the predominant type of lung cancer in the United States, constituting approximately 84% of all lung cancer diagnoses.2 Despite advancements in treatment techniques, the prognosis of NSCLC remains unfavorable, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 26% across all disease stages.2 Timely detection of NSCLC is critical, as only 25% of patients are diagnosed at an early stage, making them eligible for surgical intervention.2

Locally advanced NSCLC, for which surgery is not feasible but potential for cure exists, presents a significant challenge in terms of treatment options. In such cases, radiation therapy (RT) plays an important role as a local treatment and the primary modality for local control instead of surgical intervention, and its effectiveness is crucial for achieving optimal outcomes. The current standard of care for patients with stage III NSCLC involves a combination of chemotherapy and RT.3 Recent advances in immunotherapy have led to the incorporation of agents such as IMFINZI® (durvalumab) into treatment regimens, following concurrent chemoradiotherapy.4 A recent therapeutic strategy involving the use of durvalumab demonstrated a reduction in the local recurrence rate following chemoradiotherapy from 42.9% to 29.4%.5 Despite these advancements, the current recurrence rate remains high, emphasizing the critical need to enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy, which is the cornerstone of local control. Therefore, improving local control by overcoming radiation resistance in NSCLC is critical for ongoing research and development.

Bee venom, a natural toxin obtained from honeybees, has traditionally been used in Oriental medicine to treat various diseases.6 The antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties of bee venom have been investigated by analyzing the protein structure of its components.6 Melittin is the major peptide in bee venom and has hydrophobic structural characteristics, except for the Lys-Arg-Lys-Arg sequence at the C-terminus, which shows strong activity on the cell wall.78 Recently, the anticancer effects of melittin have garnered attention, and its efficacy has been reported in various cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma, melanoma, ovarian cancer, gastric cancer, breast cancer, and lung cancer.9

Melittin was shown to exert an antitumor effect in a mouse model of Lewis lung carcinoma by regulating tumor-associated macrophages.4 It also significantly inhibited epidermal growth factor-induced invasion and migration of NSCLC cells and exhibited its antitumor effects by inhibiting the vascular endothelial growth factor signaling pathway and hypoxia-inducible factor 1-α expression in NSCLC cells.10 In addition to these anticancer properties, melittin is recognized as a radiosensitizer, reducing radioresistance in breast, esophageal, and head and neck cancers.1112 We previously demonstrated the radiosensitizing activity of melittin in breast cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo.13 Further research on the role of melittin as a radiation sensitizer is warranted, and to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the radiosensitization activity of melittin in NSCLC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture

The NSCLC cell lines used in the study, NCI-H460 and A549 cells, were purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea). Both NCI-H460 and A549 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium supplemented with 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA). The cells were incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every 2 days, and subcultures were performed every 3 days.

X ray irradiation

The cells were irradiated with a 6 MV photon beam using a Clinac iX linear accelerator (Varian, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a multileaf collimator (width, 5 mm). Physical doses were measured using a diode detector to ensure the delivery of the planned radiation doses. The cells were irradiated with doses of 2, 4, 6, and 8 Gy at a rate of 6 Gy/min. Control cells were not irradiated (0 Gy) and were treated under the same conditions as the irradiated cells. For the in vivo experiments, mice were anesthetized before irradiation and restrained on a mouse bed in a dose-build-up cradle, as previously reported.14 The mice were subjected to a radiation dose of 15 Gy in three fractions at 24-hour intervals.

Cell viability assay

The effect of melittin on the viability of cells was determined using a water-soluble tetrazolium (WST) assay. Cell viability was measured using the EZ-CYTOX Cell Viability Assay Kit (Daeil Lab, Seoul, Korea). Cells were seeded at a density of 5×103 cells/well in 96-well plates. After 24 hours of incubation, the cells were treated with different concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 4.0, and 6.0 µg/Ml) of melittin for 24 and 48 hours. To confirm the combined effect of irradiation and melittin, the cells were irradiated with 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8 Gy and treated with or without 1.0 µg/Ml and 2.0 µg/Ml melittin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). After 24 hours, 10 Μl of EZ-CYTOX Kit reagent was added to each well and the plates were incubated at 37℃ for 0.5-2 hours. The plates were agitated at room temperature for 1 minute before detection. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax ABS Plus; Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Each assay was performed in triplicate.

Clonogenic assay for the radiosensitizing effect of melittin

The radiosensitizing effect of melittin on NCI-H460 cells was evaluated using fixed concentrations of melittin and stepwise increases in irradiation dose. Cells were seeded at 1.0×106 cells per 6-well plate and incubated. The control group was not treated with melittin (0 µg/mL), whereas the other groups were treated with 1.0 µg/mL and 2.0 µg/mL melittin for 24 hours. The cells were then irradiated with 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8 Gy and incubated at 37℃ in 5% CO2 for 10 days. The colonies were fixed and stained with crystal violet to count the number of colonies (>50 cells/colony). The plating efficiency (PE) is the ratio of the number of colonies to the number of seeded cells. The surviving fraction (SF) is the number of colonies formed after irradiation divided by the number of colonies plated, with a correction for PE. The PE and SF for each treatment were calculated using the following equations:15 PE(%)=number of colonies formednumber of cells×100 SF=number of colonies formed after irradiationnumber of cells seeded ×PE

Flow cytometry

To determine the extent of apoptosis in NCI-H460 cells following the combination treatment with melittin and irradiation, an annexin V/PI double-staining assay was performed, and the cells were analyzed using flow cytometry. The assay was performed using double-negative cells (viable cells), annexin V single-positive cells (early apoptotic cells), propidium iodide (PI) single-positive cells (necrotic cells), and double-positive cells (late apoptotic cells). The percentage of cells in each category was plotted into four quadrants using a scatter plot.16 The cells were treated with 2.0 µg/mL melittin or left untreated, and then irradiated with 8 Gy. After treatment, the cells were incubated in dishes for 24 or 48 hours. Cellular apoptosis was detected using a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) annexin V apoptosis detection kit (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Annexin V-FITC binding was analyzed by flow cytometry with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 530 nm using an FITC signal detector (FL1), whereas PI staining was analyzed using a phycoerythrin emission signal detector (FL2). All experiments were repeated three times.

Western blot analysis

To examine the effects of melittin on DNA damage repair and apoptosis following irradiation, Western blot analysis was conducted using NCI-H460 cells. Cells were treated with melittin (1.0 µg/mL or 2.0 µg/mL), either alone or in combination with irradiation, and incubated for 24 hours. Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified, and 20 µg of protein per sample was resolved on 6%–15% SDS-PAGE gels, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dried milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies.

Primary antibodies against phospho-ATM, ATM, phospho-DNA-PKcs, DNA-PKcs, γ-H2AX, H2AX, cleaved caspase-3, phospho-p53, phospho-NF-κB p65, phospho-IκBα, p53, NF-κB p65, IκBα, and β-actin were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), while Bcl-2 and Bax antibodies were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse, 1:5000; goat anti-rabbit, 1:5000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 1 hour at room temperature. Signals were developed using the Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and band intensities were analyzed by densitometry. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

Xenograft mouse model of lung cancer

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of Gachon University Animal Research. This study was approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of the Clinical Medicine Research Institute, Gil Medical Center, Gachon University (MRI-2021-0001, 0008). Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (Orient Bio, Seongnam, Korea) were subcutaneously inoculated with NCI-H460 cells (2×106 cells/mouse) on the left side of the back. When the tumors grew to 100–250 mm3 on day 7 after injection, the animals were randomly divided into four groups (n=10 per group): 1) Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) (control), 2) melittin, 3) irradiation, and 4) melittin and irradiation. Mice in the melittin-alone and combination groups received 2 mg/kg melittin via intraperitoneal injection on days 20, 21, and 22. The 2 mg/kg dose was selected based on previous in vivo studies demonstrating antitumor efficacy and acceptable tolerability of melittin at this concentration in xenograft.1718 Mice in the irradiation-alone and combination groups were irradiated with 5 Gy per day on the same days, receiving a total dose of 15 Gy in three fractions under respiratory anesthesia with 2% isoflurane. Tumor growth was measured 2–3 days per week until day 39 by measuring the length (L) and width (W) of the tumors using electronic calipers. Tumor volumes were determined using the following formula: Tumor volume=L×W×W2

At the time of sacrifice on day 39, all tumor tissues were collected and weighed. Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) and embedded in paraffin for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis.

In addition to the NCI-H460 xenograft mouse model, a similar experimental setup was performed using A549 cells. Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 2×106 A549 cells. When the tumors reached a size of 100–250 mm3, the mice were divided into four groups: 1) PBS (control); 2) melittin alone; 3) irradiation alone; and 4) melittin in combination with irradiation. Mice in the melittin-alone and combination groups were administered melittin intraperitoneally at 2 mg/kg/day for 3 days, while those in the irradiation-alone and combination groups received 5 Gy per day for 3 days, resulting in a total radiation dose of 15 Gy.

IHC staining

Tumor tissue sections were prepared for IHC analysis following standard procedures. Briefly, the sections were deparaffinized using xylene and rehydrated using a graded ethanol series. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating the sections with 0.3% H2O2. Antigen retrieval was performed by immersing the sections in sodium citrate buffer (10 Mm sodium citrate, 0.05% Tween 20, Ph 6.0) and heating at 120℃ for 10 minutes. After antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked with 2% non-fat dried milk for 1 hours at room temperature. Primary antibodies against Bcl-2, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 were applied to the sections, which were then incubated overnight at 4℃. Following primary antibody incubation, the sections were treated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Antibody binding was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine as the chromogenic substrate. For histological analysis, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted on coverslips. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed to assess histopathological changes in the tissue sections.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean±standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons post hoc test in GraphPad Prism 7 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical ignificance was determined at p<0.05.

RESULTS

Effect of melittin on NSCLC cell viability

The cytotoxicity of melittin on NCI-H460 cells was evaluated using various concentrations (0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 4.0, and 6.0 µg/mL) over 24 and 48 hours. Cell viability was measured relative to the control group. At concentrations of 2 µg/mL or lower, cell viability remained above 90%, indicating minimal cytotoxicity at these levels. However, at higher concentrations of 4 µg/mL and 6 µg/mL, there was a significant reduction in cell viability (Fig. 1A and B). Similar results were observed in A549 cells, another NSCLC cell line (Fig. 1C and D). Based on these findings, melittin concentrations of 1.0 µg/mL and 2.0 µg/mL were selected for further in vitro experiments.

Significant inhibitory effect of combined treatment with melittin and irradiation on NCI-H460 cell viability

Cells were treated with melittin in combination with various doses of irradiation. The reduction in cell survival with the combined melittin (1.0 µg/mL and 2.0 µg/mL) and irradiation treatment, compared to irradiation alone, was as follows: 11% for both doses at 2 Gy, 31% and 37% at 4 Gy, 37% and 42% at 6 Gy, and 47% and 53% at 8 Gy, respectively (Fig. 2). The suppressive effect of melittin on irradiated cells remained consistent at 11% for both melittin concentrations at 2 Gy. However, at radiation doses of 4 Gy or higher, the increase in melittin concentration from 1.0 to 2.0 µg/mL resulted in an additional 5%–6% reduction in cell survival.

Melittin enhanced radiosensitivity of NCI-H460 cells: evidence from clonogenic assays

To explore the radiosensitizing potential of melittin in NCI-H460 cells, a clonogenic assay was conducted, and radiation dose-response survival curves were analyzed. As the irradiation dose increased, the number of colonies decreased logarithmically, and survival curves were plotted as the fraction of surviving cells against the irradiation dose (Fig. 3). The combination of melittin and irradiation significantly reduced the percentage of surviving cells. Notably, the cell survival curve showed a clear synergistic effect when melittin was combined with irradiation, resulting in a steeper slope compared to when each treatment was used alone. As the melittin concentration increased from 1.0 to 2.0 µg/mL, the slope of the survival curve became even steeper. At 4 Gy, the survival rates were 0.136 for irradiation alone, and 0.095 and 0.074 for 1.0 µg/mL and 2.0 µg/mL of melittin, respectively (Fig. 3). The synergistic effect was more pronounced at 8 Gy, where the survival rate was 0.019 for irradiation alone and 0.012 and 0.002 for 1.0 µg/mL and 2.0 µg/mL melittin, respectively. These findings suggest that melittin enhances the effect of irradiation-induced cell death, increasing the radiosensitivity of NCI-H460 cells.

Enhanced induction of apoptosis in NCI-H460 cells by combined treatment of melittin and irradiation

In this study, the induction of apoptosis in NCI-H460 cells was evaluated using flow cytometry analysis following combined treatment with melittin and irradiation. After 24 and 48 hours of treatment, a significantly higher percentage of apoptotic cells (19.3% and 29.9%, respectively) were observed in the combination treatment group compared to those treated with radiation alone (11.0% and 14.3%) or melittin alone (0.9% and 2.9%) (Fig. 4). These results demonstrate that the combination treatment significantly enhances cell death compared to radiation alone, indicating that the radiosensitizing effect of melittin on NCI-H460 cells is closely related to the activation of the apoptotic pathway.

Combination treatment with melittin and irradiation inhibited DNA damage repair to induce NCI-H460 cell apoptosis

The expression levels of DNA damage and repair-related proteins in NCI-H460 cells under different experimental conditions are shown in Fig. 5A. Following irradiation at a dose of 8 Gy, the levels of p-DNA-PKcs and p-ATM, which indicate DNA damage, increased (Fig. 5A and B). However, treatment with both melittin and irradiation reduced the phosphorylation of DNA-PKcs and ATM, with more pronounced reductions observed when the melittin concentration increased from 1.0 to 2.0 µg/mL. Notably, the expression of γ-H2AX, a key marker of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) induced by irradiation, was significantly higher in cells treated with the combination of melittin and irradiation compared to irradiation alone. Additionally, as the melittin concentration increased, γ-H2AX expression also increased (Fig. 5A and C). The combination treatment significantly increased the levels of Bax and cleaved caspase-3, which are well-known pro-apoptotic markers, while Bcl-2 levels were notably reduced (Fig. 5C and D). This shift in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, a key determinant of mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis, strongly suggests that melittin enhances radiosensitivity in NCI-H460 cells. The observed changes in apoptosis-related proteins support the conclusion that melittin promotes apoptosis by impairing DNA repair mechanisms and modulating the apoptotic signaling network.

Combined melittin and irradiation treatment induces upstream apoptotic signaling pathway activation in NCI-H460 cells

Considering that p53, NF-κB p65, and IκBα are established upstream regulators of apoptosis and radiation responses, their involvement in the radiosensitizing effect of melittin was examined. Western blot analysis was performed to assess the phosphorylated forms of these proteins, which represent their activated states. As shown in Fig. 6A, the combination treatment of melittin and irradiation markedly increased the expression of phospho-p53, phospho-NF-κB p65, and phospho-IκBα. These findings were further confirmed by densitometric quantification from three independent experiments (Fig. 6B). These results indicate that melittin enhances radiation-induced apoptosis in NCI-H460 cells at least in part by activating upstream apoptotic signaling pathways involving p53 and NF-κB, supporting its role as an effective radiosensitizer.

Combination treatment with melittin and irradiation synergistically inhibited tumor growth in a xenograft mouse model using NSCLC cell lines

In a xenograft mouse model using the NSCLC cell lines, the combination of melittin and irradiation demonstrated a synergistic effect in inhibiting tumor growth. All mice survived until the end of the experiment, and no significant differences in body weight were observed among the four groups (mean weight: 21.4g) (Supplementary Table 1, only online) at the start of treatment. However, by day 5, mice in the irradiation-alone and melittin-irradiation combination groups showed the largest weight decreases, 3.22 g and 3.47 g, respectively. In contrast, mice in the control and melittin-alone groups gained 0.56 g and 0.43 g, respectively. Aside from the weight loss, no significant toxicity was noted from either melittin or irradiation. Over the course of the study, the body weights of the mice in the irradiation-alone and combination groups increased more rapidly than those in the control or melittin-alone groups.

In terms of tumor growth, the time required for the NCI-H460 cells to grow and form tumors of 500 mm3 was 25 days in the control group, 29 days in the melittin-alone group, and 35 days in the irradiation-alone group. However, in the combination group, the tumor volume did not reach 500 mm3 (Fig. 7A). Specifically, on day 3 after treatment began, the tumor volume was 171.3 mm3 and showed no significant increase for the remainder of the study. At the end of the experiment, tumors were excised and weighed. The tumor weights for the control, melittin-alone, irradiation-alone, and combination groups were 1.019±0.24 g, 0.969±0.14 g, 0.409±0.04 g, and 0.245±0.06 g, respectively. Tumor weight and size were significantly reduced in both the irradiation-alone and melittin-irradiation combination groups compared to the control group (p<0.01) (Fig. 7B and C). Notably, the combination treatment showed an even greater reduction in tumor weight (p<0.05), indicating a synergistic inhibitory effect on tumor growth.

Similar results were observed with another NSCLC cell line, A549, where the combination of melittin and irradiation significantly reduced tumor volume compared to either treatment alone, further confirming the synergistic inhibitory effect (Fig. 7D).

Combined treatment with melittin and irradiation reduced tumor mass by promoting the apoptosis of NCI-H460 cells in a mouse model of lung cancer

IHC staining for Bcl-2, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 was conducted on excised tumors from mice treated with melittin alone, irradiation alone, or a combination of both in vivo (Fig. 8). In the combination treatment group, tumor tissues showed a marked increase in the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3, while the expression of Bcl-2 was significantly reduced. These results align with the findings from in vitro experiments, further supporting the role of melittin in enhancing radiosensitivity through the induction of apoptosis.

DISCUSSION

Melittin is a major polypeptide component found in bee venom, particularly in honeybees (Apis mellifera), featuring a small peptide structure devoid of disulfide bridges.7 Its N-terminus is predominantly hydrophobic, whereas its C-terminus is hydrophilic and strongly basic. Scientific inquiry into the protein structure of melittin extends beyond elucidating its conventional mechanisms as an antibacterial or anti-inflammatory agent to unveil its potential as an anticancer agent.6 The primary mode of action of melittin involves the disruption of cell membranes and formation of pores or channels in the lipid bilayer.19 This disruption leads to the leakage of cellular contents, culminating in cell lysis and death. This mechanism is believed to underlie the cytolytic and hemolytic effects of melittin, resulting in cancer cell death and contributing to its anticancer properties. Additionally, melittin interferes with the intracellular signaling pathways crucial for cell survival and proliferation, ultimately leading to apoptotic cancer cell death.20 Furthermore, in addition to directly killing cancer cells, melittin can also interfere with tumor growth and metastasis by modulating processes such as angiogenesis and cell migration.1121

Melittin has demonstrated therapeutic potential across a variety of diseases due to its diverse biological activities. In rheumatoid arthritis, melittin modulates the immune response and reduces inflammation by inhibiting NF-κB activation and decreasing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6.22 In multiple sclerosis (MS), melittin may alleviate symptoms through anti-inflammatory effects, degradation of hyaluronan in inflammatory lesions, and potential inhibition of Borrelia burgdorferi, a bacterium linked to MS.23 For cardiovascular diseases, melittin activates the COX pathway and central alpha7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, leading to notable pressor and tachycardic effects.24 Additionally, melittin exhibits significant antimicrobial activity by disrupting microbial cell membranes, leading to cell lysis and death, and showing potential against bacteria, viruses, and fungi.25 These diverse effects make melittin a potentially versatile therapeutic agent with applications in autoimmune, neurological, cardiovascular, and infectious diseases.

To explore the potential of melittin as a radiosensitizer for NSCLC, rather than its direct anticancer effect through apoptosis or cytolytic cell death, it was necessary to determine the treatment concentration that would not induce significant intrinsic cytotoxicity and would directly cause cancer cell death. Our results demonstrated that even at the highest concentration and duration of treatment (2.0 µg/mL melittin for up to 48 hours), NCI-H460 cell viability was maintained at over 90%. This indicates that melittin does not exhibit significant intrinsic cytotoxicity under these conditions and does not directly induce cancer cell death. These findings are consistent with the results reported by Zhang and Chen,10 who found that melittin at a concentration of 1.0 µg/mL did not induce significant cell death in A549 cells, another human NSCLC cell line. Given the lack of observed direct cytotoxicity at the tested concentrations, we selected concentrations of 1.0 µg/mL and 2.0 µg/mL melittin for subsequent in vitro studies. These chosen concentrations served as a rational starting point, facilitating comparison and validation with results from other reported studies. Furthermore, they allowed the optimization of radiation sensitization effects without inducing excessive cytotoxicity in cells. When melittin was combined with irradiation, a significant dose-dependent synergistic inhibitory effect on cell survival was observed. At 2 Gy, no additional detrimental effect was observed with increased concentrations of melittin from 1.0 to 2.0 µg/mL on irradiated cell survival. However, as radiation doses were increased to 4 Gy or higher, a 5% or 6% increase was noted, corresponding to an elevation in melittin concentration.

The evaluation of a substance’s radiosensitizing effect often employs a clonogenic assay, as cancer cells can proliferate indefinitely, and observing the growth of visible colonies provides convenient evidence that these cells maintain their reproductive integrity.15 In our study, as the irradiation dose and melittin concentration increased, colony counts decreased significantly, indicating a synergistic effect between melittin and irradiation. The cell survival curve demonstrated a robust synergistic effect, whereby the cell survival fraction decreased more significantly with increasing melittin concentration as the radiation dose increased. Combining an 8 Gy irradiation dose with 1.0 µg/mL resulted in a 1.6-fold decrease in the SF, whereas doubling the melittin concentration to 2.0 µg/mL led to an almost 6-fold decrease, corresponding to a decrease in SF from 0.012 to 0.002. These findings suggest that melittin possesses potent radiosensitizing activity in NSCLC cells. It is noteworthy that our results are consistent with findings reported by Yang, et al.12 regarding the sensitization effect of melittin for unresectable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells to radiation with a sensitization enhancement ratio of 1.15–1.42, highlighting the parallel efficacy of melittin in this context. Another study emphasized melittin’s capacity to enhance radiosensitivity in hypoxic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma by inhibiting HIF-1α.12 Our results contribute to this body of evidence by demonstrating the radiosensitivity effect of melittin for the first time in NSCLC, which is consistent with reports in other carcinomas.

The increased expression of cleaved caspase-3 observed in our study with the combination treatment of melittin and radiation further underscores the potential radiosensitizing effects in NSCLC. Flow cytometry can differentiate live, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cells based on fluorescence signals.26 Our flow cytometry results confirmed that combined treatment with melittin and irradiation significantly enhanced the apoptosis of NCI-H460 cells compared to either treatment alone, which was consistently observed at both 24 and 48 hours post-treatment. Considering previous reports showing that melittin induces apoptosis in A549 cells through mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent intrinsic apoptotic pathway activity,27 it is probable that similar mechanisms underlie our findings. During apoptosis, Bcl-2 and Bax play important roles as upstream signals for caspase-3. When the protein levels of Bcl-2 decrease, Bax is activated to form pores in the mitochondrial membrane and release cytochrome C. Cytochrome C then forms apoptosomes in the cytoplasm and finally activates caspase-3 to initiate cell death. Many studies have aimed to overcome radioresistance by targeting caspase-3 in various cancer cell lines. Yang, et al.28 demonstrated the critical role of caspase-3 in radiotherapy-induced apoptosis of irradiated MCF-7 breast cancer cells, suggesting that caspase-3 deficiency could potentially contribute to radioresistance in breast cancers. Mutant-p53 colorectal cancer cells, which are often resistant to RT, exhibited reduced numbers of radioresistant mutant-p53 HT-29 cells after pretreatment with omega-3 docosahexaenoic acid and irradiation.29 This reduction was accompanied by the activation of caspase-3. These studies indicate that substances that increase caspase-3 activation in response to radiation may act as radiosensitizers. In our study, when melittin and radiation were combined, Bcl-2 inhibition and increased Bax and cleaved caspase-3 levels were observed in both cell lines (Fig. 5B) and animal models (Fig. 7). On combining our results with those of other studies, it appears highly likely that melittin acts as a radiosensitizer, enhancing the Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 apoptotic signaling pathway.

Radiation-induced DNA DSBs trigger various forms of cell death, including apoptosis, thereby maximizing the efficacy of RT.30 Consequently, ongoing efforts are focused on the development of pharmacological agents that inhibit the mechanisms involved in DSB repair and enhance the effectiveness of radiation therapy. DSBs are recognized by ATM and DNA-PK, both of which possess kinase and repair activities.3132 Increased levels of γ-H2AX, a phosphorylated form of histones, are observed in response to DSBs, serving as a marker for DNA damage.33 Our findings revealed elevated levels of phosphorylated DNA-PKcs and ATM in response to 8 Gy irradiation, indicating the activation of the DNA damage repair pathway. However, upon the addition of melittin to irradiation, we observed reduced phosphorylation of DNA-PKcs and ATM, suggesting the inhibition of the DSB repair pathway. This inhibitory effect was more pronounced at higher melittin concentrations, indicating that it was dose-dependent. Furthermore, the increased expression of γ-H2AX in cells treated with the combination of radiation and melittin therapy further supports the impairment of DNA repair mechanisms. These results indicate that melittin enhances the sensitivity of NCI-H460 cells to irradiation by interfering with the DNA repair process, presenting another independent mechanism alongside apoptosis, and highlighting its potential as a radiosensitizer in cancer therapy.

In our experiments, protein lysates were collected 24 hours post-irradiation. At this time point, γ-H2AX remained elevated in the melittin plus irradiation group, while p-ATM and p-DNA-PKcs levels were reduced. γ-H2AX foci form within minutes after DNA damage,3334 and are known to persist for over 24 hours in cells with impaired repair.35 ATM and DNA-PKcs phosphorylation peaks early and then declines,32 and DNA–PK inhibition has been shown to prevent DSB repair even at 24 hours post-irradiation.36 Together, our data suggest that melittin disrupts both early kinase activation and late-stage DNA damage resolution, thereby enhancing radiosensitivity.

To validate the radiosensitizing effect of melittin on lung cancer cell lines in vivo, we used a mouse xenograft model with NCI-H460 cells. Our study revealed a significant reduction in tumor weight in both the irradiation-alone and melittin–irradiation combination groups compared to that in the control group, with an even more pronounced decrease in tumor weight observed in the combination group. Notably, the combination treatment prevented the tumors from reaching a volume of 500 mm3, a milestone surpassed by the control and single-treatment groups. In the resected tumors, the decreased expression levels of Bcl-2 and the increase in Bax and cleaved caspase-3 were most profound in the combination treatment group. These findings suggest a promising therapeutic avenue for lung cancer treatment, potentially enhancing the efficacy of irradiation while mitigating toxicity, as indicated by manageable changes in body weight and the absence of serious adverse effects.

However, a limitation of this study is the use of BALB/c nude mice, which lack functional T cells. Therefore, the contribution of the tumor microenvironment and immune-mediated radiation effects, including potential immunogenic cell death, could not be fully addressed. Radiation-induced antitumor efficacy is known to be closely linked with immune activation and tumor–immune interactions,37 and nude mouse models do not adequately recapitulate these processes.38 Future studies using immunocompetent or humanized mouse models are warranted to clarify these immune-related mechanisms.

This favorable safety profile led us to further examine whether the melittin dose used in our study aligns with existing preclinical data. In the present study, we used a melittin dose of 2 mg/kg for in vivo experiments, which was selected based on previously published in vivo studies demonstrating both antitumor efficacy and acceptable safety at this concentration. Wang, et al.17 showed that 2 mg/kg melittin administered intratumorally significantly suppressed colorectal cancer growth and metastasis in a BALB/c xenograft model, without inducing weight loss or systemic toxicity. Similarly, Wang, et al.18 reported that intraperitoneal injection of 2.5 mg/kg melittin in a NSCLC model led to marked tumor regression with minimal toxicity, supporting the biological activity and tolerability of this dose. For in vitro experiments, 1.0 µg/mL and 2.0 µg/mL of melittin were selected based on cell viability screening results showing dose-dependent cytotoxicity in NCI-H460 cells while preserving clonogenic capacity. These concentrations were also consistent with previous reports evaluating melittin’s anticancer effects in vitro without inducing nonspecific cytolysis. Together, these in vivo and in vitro dose selections reflect a balance between antitumor efficacy and tolerability, and ensure consistency with established experimental models. Despite this favorable preclinical safety profile, melittin is well known for its hemolytic activity and systemic toxicity at higher doses.7 Therefore, direct systemic administration may not be feasible in clinical settings. To overcome these limitations, various targeted delivery strategies, such as liposomal encapsulation or nanoparticle formulations (“NanoBees”), have been explored to enhance tumor-specific delivery while minimizing off-target effects.39 Future studies are warranted to investigate whether such delivery systems can be combined with radiotherapy to maximize the clinical applicability of melittin.

This combination treatment strategy of melittin and irradiation may have substantial clinical relevance, offering a means to improve treatment outcomes in patients with lung cancer. Our study adds to a growing body of research supporting the multifaceted anticancer properties of melittin, such as its ability to enhance radiosensitivity12 and suppress HIF-1α/VEGF expression,21 and now, its synergistic effect with irradiation in inhibiting tumor growth. Further investigations into the mechanisms underlying this synergy, along with rigorous clinical studies, are warranted to validate and translate these preclinical findings into effective lung cancer therapies that improve patient outcomes.

In this study, melittin was identified as a potent radiosensitizer for NSCLC cells, targeting critical components of the DNA damage repair and apoptotic signaling pathways. Specifically, melittin was found to reduce the phosphorylation of DNA-PKcs and ATM, which are essential for the repair of DSBs. Additionally, melittin treatment resulted in increased levels of γ-H2AX, a marker of DNA damage, indicating an accumulation of DSBs. Concurrently, melittin modulated apoptotic signaling by decreasing the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 while increasing the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3. These results suggest that melittin enhances radiosensitivity by impairing DNA repair mechanisms and promoting apoptosis. Furthermore, melittin may exert its effects by targeting higher-order regulators involved in the DNA damage response and apoptotic pathways.

In addition to its effects on DNA damage and apoptosis markers, melittin was found to modulate upstream apoptotic signaling pathways relevant to radiation responses. Specifically, the phosphorylation of p53, NF-κB p65, and IκBα was significantly elevated by combination treatment, indicating enhanced activation of pro-apoptotic signals. These molecules are well-established mediators of the DNA damage response and play critical roles in regulating cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and inflammatory signaling following irradiation.4041 The observed upregulation of phosphorylated p53 suggests activation of the p53-mediated apoptotic pathway, whereas increased phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 and IκBα may reflect dynamic regulation of NF-κB signaling. This dual modulation supports a model in which melittin sensitizes NCI-H460 cells to radiation not only by amplifying downstream apoptosis but also by enhancing upstream damage-sensing and pro-death signaling pathways. These findings further strengthen the mechanistic basis for melittin’s radiosensitizing effects and suggest that targeting upstream regulators such as p53 and NF-κB may provide additional therapeutic leverage in overcoming radioresistance in NSCLC.

Another limitation of this study is that only two NSCLC cell lines, A549 and NCL-H460, both harboring wild-type p53, were evaluated. NSCLC, however, is a highly heterogeneous disease characterized by frequent genetic alterations such as KRAS and EGFR mutations, which are known to influence tumor biology and therapeutic responses.4243 Therefore, the radiosensitizing effects of melittin observed here may not be fully generalizable to all NSCLC subtypes. Future studies incorporating KRAS-mutant (e.g., H1299) or EGFR-mutant (e.g., H1975) cell lines, as well as patient-derived xenograft models, are warranted to determine whether melittin’s radiosensitizing activity extends to diverse genetic backgrounds.

Given the significant findings of this study, further research is warranted to elucidate the precise molecular targets and mechanisms by which melittin enhances radiosensitivity and induces apoptosis in NSCLC cells. Specifically, detailed investigations into the modulation of p53 and NF-κB pathways by melittin are recommended. Such studies could provide deeper insights into the potential therapeutic applications of melittin as a radiosensitizer in the treatment of NSCLC.

Despite extensive efforts, NSCLC remains a formidable disease with a grim prognosis and a 5-year survival rate of only 26% across all stages. The results obtained from irradiating NCI-H460 cells in the presence of melittin offer valuable insights into the development of innovative approaches to tackle radiation resistance in lung cancer and enhance the effectiveness of RT. Melittin, a peptide derived from bee venom, exerts potent radiosensitizing effects in NSCLC cells by disrupting DNA DSB repair mechanisms and enhancing the Bcl-2/Bax/caspase-3 apoptotic signaling cascade. Furthermore, in vivo studies revealed a synergistic interaction between melittin and radiation, effectively suppressing the growth of transplanted tumors in a mouse model. Therefore, melittin holds promise as a potential radiosensitizer for improving the effectiveness of radiotherapy in NSCLC. These findings highlight the possibility of exploring novel therapeutic strategies. Future studies should focus on developing techniques to overcome radiotherapy resistance in patients with NSCLC. Further investigations are needed to understand the anticancer properties of melittin and its role in radiosensitization. Such endeavors have the potential to result in the development of more effective treatments and improved outcomes for patients with NSCLC.
