Preclinical carotid atherosclerosis is independently associated with cardiovascular events and mortality in type 1 diabetes
Authors: Clara Viñals, Ignacio Conget, Camila Milad, Montse Granados, Jesús Blanco, Maria Claro, Verónica Perea, Tonet Serés-Noriega, Irene Vinagre, Marga Giménez, Antonio J. Amor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate whether carotid atherosclerosis predicts future cardiovascular events (CVE) and mortality in adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) in primary prevention.MethodsCohort study in adults with T1D participating in a cardiovascular risk assessment program that included carotid ultrasound. Background

People living with type 1 diabetes continue to face a markedly increased likelihood of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and premature mortality relative to individuals without diabetes, although this excess risk has declined over recent decades [1]. Contemporary population data show that adults with type 1 diabetes experience more than four-fold higher incidence of major cardiovascular events (CVE) than those in the general population, a pattern evident even among young adults [2].

The mechanisms contributing to CVD in type 1 diabetes arise from the convergence of established cardiovascular risk factors and diabetes-specific biological processes. Persistent hyperglycemia together with metabolic dysregulation promotes vascular injury and accelerates the development of cardiovascular complications [3, 4].

Population-based cardiovascular risk equations, including those designed for type 2 diabetes, perform poorly in estimating cardiovascular risk in adults with type 1 diabetes [5, 6]. Although type 1 diabetes-specific risk engines have been developed and validated in selected cohorts [7, 8], their clinical utility remains limited, as they have not been sufficiently established nor extensively evaluated across diverse populations with type 1 diabetes.

Atherosclerosis remains the central pathological basis for most cardiovascular outcomes, and its early, clinically silent stages can be reliably detected using non-invasive imaging techniques such as carotid ultrasound [9]. In the general population, the detection of carotid atherosclerosis, and particularly the presence of carotid plaques, is strongly linked to incident CVE and provides prognostic value beyond conventional risk markers [10]. Despite the substantially elevated cardiovascular burden observed in type 1 diabetes, evidence regarding the prognostic significance of preclinical carotid atherosclerosis in this population is limited [11].

The objective of this study was therefore to evaluate whether preclinical carotid atherosclerosis independently predicts future CVE and mortality in adults with type 1 diabetes.

Methods

Study participants

We performed a prospective observational study on individuals living with type 1 diabetes and no previous history of CVD (coronary artery disease, stroke, peripheral artery disease and/or heart failure), who were followed in a Diabetes Unit of a tertiary referral hospital in Catalonia, Spain. Participants were consecutively enrolled between January 2015 and December 2022. The study protocol was approved by the local ethic committee (HCB/2026/0018), and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Participants were included if they had no history of CVD and met one of the following criteria: being older than 40 years old, presence of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) regardless of age and duration of type 1 diabetes, and/or having more than 10 years of duration of type 1 diabetes and other cardiovascular risk factor (CVRF). Additional CVRFs included presence of diabetic retinopathy, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-cholesterol) > 160 mg/dL or statin use, triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-cholesterol; <40/45 mg/dL in men/women), hypertension (defined as blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg or treatment with antihypertensive drugs), premature CVD in first-degree relatives (< 55/65 years in men/women), active smoking habit, history of eclampsia/preeclampsia, hypoglycemia unawareness (Clarke test > 3) [12] or a prior episode of severe hypoglycemia (defined as an episode of confirmed hypoglycemia requiring external assistance for recovery).

Clinical and laboratory parameters

Demographic and clinical information, including microvascular complications, type 1 diabetes duration, family history of premature CVD, lifestyle habits, and medical treatment, was obtained from patient interviews and clinical records, following the published protocol [13]. Anthropometric and blood pressure measurements were collected using standardized methods, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated conventionally. DKD was defined by albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) ≥ 30 mg/g (confirmed in ≥ 2/3 samples) and/or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²; diabetic retinopathy was assessed by ophthalmologic fundus examination. Laboratory evaluations included fasting blood and first-morning urine samples for lipid profile, glucose, creatinine, albumin-to-creatinine (ACR), LDL-cholesterol (Friedewald formula), non-HDL cholesterol, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; assessed with the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration [CKD-EPI] equation), and centrally measured HbA1c (Tosoh G8 Automated HPLC Analyser, Tosoh Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA) aligned with the standards of the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT), with a normal range of 4%–6% (20–42 mmol/mol)). Insulin resistance was estimated using the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), a validated surrogate marker of insulin sensitivity in type 1 diabetes that incorporates information on glycemic control, hypertension status, and adiposity-related variables (waist-to-hip ratio) [14], and cardiovascular risk was assessed through the Steno Type 1 Risk Engine (ST1RE) [7, 15].

Carotid B-mode ultrasound imaging

Bilateral carotid ultrasonography was performed using high-resolution B-mode equipment (5–10 MHz transducers; ACUSON X700 [Siemens Healthineers] or Aplio a450 [Canon]). Imaging acquisition adhered to predefined protocols for carotid intima–media thickness (IMT) and plaque assessment [16, 17]. Mean IMT and maximum IMT were assessed in the common carotid artery, carotid bulb, and internal carotid artery.

Plaques were considered when a focal arterial wall thickening extended into the lumen by at least 50% relative to the adjacent IMT, or when the IMT measured ≥ 1.5 mm from the media–adventitia interface to the intima–lumen boundary. Plaque height was measured in the view (longitudinal or transverse) that provided the most accurate visualization. In participants with carotid plaques, maximum IMT was defined as the height of the largest plaque. Preclinical carotid atherosclerosis was defined by the identification of at least one plaque in any of the carotid segments evaluated. Atherosclerotic burden was quantified as the total number of plaques per patient and categorized into three groups: 0 plaque, 1–2 plaques, or ≥ 3 plaques.

All studies were carried out by two endocrinologists experienced in vascular ultrasonography (AJA and CV), ensuring consistent image acquisition and measurement procedures.

Cardiovascular events and mortality

The primary outcome was the development of the first CVE during the follow-up period. CVE comprised coronary artery disease events (acute myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, or hospitalization for unstable angina), cerebrovascular events (ischemic, haemorrhagic, or lacunar stroke), peripheral artery disease events (PAD), incident heart failure and cardiovascular death.

PAD events were defined as incident PAD, including new-onset disease documented by an ankle–brachial index ≤ 0.9, or clinically overt PAD requiring lower-extremity revascularization, hospitalization, or resulting in critical limb ischemia or amputation [18]. Incident heart failure was defined as a first clinical diagnosis of heart failure occurring during follow-up, supported by typical signs and symptoms together with imaging evidence of either reduced or preserved left ventricular ejection fraction. Cardiovascular death was defined as death attributable to a cardiovascular cause, including fatal myocardial infarction, fatal stroke, heart failure, sudden cardiac death, or other adjudicated cardiovascular causes.

All CVE diagnoses were obtained from electronic medical records during scheduled follow-up visits.

The secondary outcome was the composite of first CVE or all-cause mortality. Vital status and causes of death were ascertained from electronic medical records for patients who did not attend follow-up visits, and causes of death were adjudicated as cardiovascular or non-cardiovascular (e.g., neoplasia or cancer). All events were independently adjudicated by two investigators, with disagreements resolved by a third investigator. Follow-up information, including vital status and outcome ascertainment, was available for all participants included in the study, and no participants were lost to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (first quartile–third quartile) as appropriate; categorical variables as number (percentages). Normality of continuous distributions was assessed using probability plots and descriptive diagnostics; when non-normality was evident, nonparametric tests were used.

Group comparisons

Baseline characteristics were compared according to: (i) presence of preclinical carotid atherosclerosis, (ii) incident CVE during follow-up, and (iii) the composite of “CVE or death”. For continuous variables, we used Student’s t test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test otherwise. Categorical variables were compared with Pearson’s χ² tests.

Time-to-event analyses

Follow-up time accrued from the baseline assessment to the first event of interest or censoring. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to compare event-free survival across carotid atherosclerosis categories, and differences were assessed with the log-rank test. Carotid exposures were evaluated as: (1) presence of any plaque and (2) atherosclerotic burden (0 plaques, 1–2 plaques, ≥ 3 plaques).

Crude incidence rates were calculated as the number of events divided by the total person-time at risk and expressed as events per 1,000 person-years. Person-time was accumulated separately for each outcome (CVE and the composite of CVE plus all-cause mortality) using the corresponding time-to-event variables. Incidence rates were estimated for the overall cohort and stratified by plaque burden categories.

Cox regression

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals for two outcomes: (a) CVE and (b) the composite of CVE or all-cause death. Carotid atherosclerosis was evaluated using two predefined exposure definitions: (1) presence of any carotid plaque (yes/no), and (2) plaque burden categorized as 0 plaques (reference), 1–2 plaques, and ≥ 3 plaques. For each exposure, we applied a hierarchical adjustment strategy with three models: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age and sex), and Model 3 (additionally adjusted for diabetes duration, eGDR, statin use, LDL-cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, smoking status, retinopathy, and DKD). Although the number of outcome events was limited, covariates included in the fully adjusted model were selected a priori based on their established clinical relevance in cardiovascular risk and type 1 diabetes, rather than through data-driven selection procedures. The fully adjusted model was intended to capture complementary pathophysiological domains, including demographic factors, metabolic profile, diabetes-related factors, pharmacological treatment, and microvascular complications, to minimize residual confounding. All covariates were considered as baseline variables and were not modelled as time-dependent exposures.

Finally, an exploratory analysis was performed to assess the association between plaque burden and outcomes after adjustment for ST1RE score (low/moderate/high-risk) and retinopathy. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted using the same multivariable adjustment strategy as the primary models, including: (1) additional adjustment for BMI; (2) replacement of diabetic kidney disease by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinary ACR as continuous variables; and (3) evaluation of carotid intima–media thickness measures as continuous variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 2026.04.0 + 526; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Description of the cohort

A total of 701 adults with type 1 diabetes were included; 51.9% were male, with a mean age of 48.5 ± 10.5 years and a diabetes duration of 26.9 ± 10.5 years. Carotid plaque was detected in 283 participants (40.4%). As shown in Table 1, participants with plaque were older (53.7 ± 9.6 vs. 44.9 ± 9.6 years; P < 0.001), more often male (57.6% vs. 48.1%; P = 0.013), with longer diabetes duration (28.8 ± 11.6 vs. 25.7 ± 9.5 years; P < 0.001), and higher 5-year mean HbA1c (7.72 ± 0.87% vs. 7.57 ± 0.95%; P = 0.020). Further, they showed higher tobacco exposure—both in terms of current smoking and cumulative pack-years, and a greater burden of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, elevated triglycerides, central obesity, and lower eGDR (p < 0.05 for all comparisons). They also had worse kidney function and higher inflammatory markers. Accordingly, the use of statins, antihypertensive agents, and antiplatelet therapy was more common in this group. Notably, high-risk ST1RE scores were substantially more frequent among participants with plaque (50.2 vs. 17.2%; p < 0.001; Table 1).

Follow-up data

During a mean follow-up of 5.85 ± 2.21 years, a total of 50 events occurred in the cohort (Supplemental Fig. 1). Forty-five were CVE, including 41 non-fatal events—coronary artery disease (n = 18), peripheral artery disease (n = 14), cerebrovascular disease (n = 6), and incident heart failure (n = 3), and four fatal CVE (three sudden deaths and one fatal myocardial infarction). In addition, five non-cardiovascular deaths were recorded, all attributed to cancer. Detailed clinical and imaging characteristics of all participants who experienced an event are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

As shown in Table 2, participants who experienced a cardiovascular event during follow-up were older and had a longer duration of type 1 diabetes compared with those without events. They showed a higher burden of chronic diabetes complications, with significantly more retinopathy and DKD. Classical cardiovascular risk factors were also more adverse, including higher rates of hypertension, higher systolic blood pressure, greater tobacco exposure, particularly current smoking, and higher triglyceride and lipoprotein(a) levels. Kidney function and insulin-sensitivity estimates were poorer in the event group (lower eGFR and eGDR). Cardiovascular risk stratification differed markedly, with two-thirds of individuals who developed events classified in the high-risk ST1RE category. Carotid atherosclerosis was also more prevalent and extensive among those with events, and mortality during follow-up was substantially higher in this group. Comparable differences were also observed for the composite endpoint of incident CVE and death (Supplementary Table 2).

In time-to-event analyses, individuals with carotid plaque showed lower event-free survival than those without plaque for both CVE and the composite of CVE plus total mortality. Event-free survival declined progressively across increasing plaque burden categories (0 plaques, 1–2 plaques, ≥ 3 plaques), p < 0.001 (see Fig. 1). The crude incidence rate in the overall cohort was 10.96 events per 1,000 person-years for CVE and 12.17 events per 1,000 person-years for the composite outcome. When stratified by plaque burden, incidence rates increased markedly across categories, from 6.0 to 13.9 and 31.0 events per 1,000 person-years for CVE, and from 6.8 to 15.6 and 33.2 events per 1,000 person-years for the composite outcome in individuals with 0, 1–2, and ≥ 3 plaques, respectively.

In Cox proportional hazards models, any plaque was associated with a three-fold higher unadjusted risk of CVE (Model 1: HR 3.14, 95% CI 1.69–5.84, p < 0.001) and a similarly increased risk for the composite outcome (HR 3.07, 95% CI 1.71–5.51, p < 0.001). After adjustment for sex and age (Model 2), plaque remained associated with higher risk (CVE: HR 1.94, 95% CI 1.00–3.77, p = 0.05; CVE + mortality: HR 1.90, 95% CI 1.02–3.55, p = 0.045). In a more comprehensive model that additionally accounted for diabetes duration, insulin-sensitivity estimate (eGDR), lipids, statin use, tobacco exposure, and microvascular complications (Model 3), plaque presence remained significant, predicting CVE (HR 2.14, 95% CI 1.08–4.26, p = 0.03) and the composite outcome (HR 2.02, 95% CI 1.06–3.87, p = 0.034).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort of more than 700 adults with type 1 diabetes at primary prevention, followed for a mean of nearly six years, we observed 45 fatal/non-fatal incident CVE and 5 non-cardiovascular deaths. Notably, carotid atherosclerosis was already present in 40% of participants at baseline. The presence of plaques was independently associated with both CVE and mortality, and a clear gradient of risk emerged with increasing atherosclerotic burden, even after comprehensive multivariable adjustment. Collectively, these findings support an independent association between preclinical carotid atherosclerosis, higher carotid plaque burden, and the risk of incident CVE and mortality in adults with type 1 diabetes.

In the general population, multiple studies have shown that the presence of atherosclerosis is independently associated with CVE and all-cause mortality and provides incremental predictive value when added to established cardiovascular risk scores [10, 19, 20]. In type 1 diabetes, evidence regarding the value of carotid plaque detection for cardiovascular risk assessment is inconsistent. In the DCCT trial, after adjustment for confounders, CVE were weakly associated only with common carotid intima–media thickness [11] Notably, the trial included a younger population and had a low incidence of CVE compared with our cohort. A recent study conducted in a Mediterranean population with type 1 diabetes evaluated preclinical carotid and femoral atherosclerosis [21] and included participants with characteristics broadly comparable to those of our cohort, including a mean age of 49 years, slightly shorter diabetes duration (23 vs. 27 years), higher mean HbA1c levels (7.95% vs. 7.6%), and a similar ST1RE risk score. The overall prevalence of preclinical atherosclerosis was also comparable (43% vs. 41%), although carotid atherosclerosis was less prevalent in that study (35% vs. 41%). Notably, across published studies [22, 23], the prevalence of carotid atherosclerosis in individuals with type 1 diabetes ranges widely from 11% to 57%, largely reflecting differences in age and diabetes duration of the studied populations. During a median follow-up of 5.8 years, 19 CVE and 13 deaths were recorded in this Mediterranean population [21], yielding markedly high unadjusted hazard ratios for CVE (25.0) and all-cause mortality (7.57). In contrast, although the unadjusted hazard ratios observed in our cohort were more modest (3.14 for CVE and 3.07 for the composite outcome), a key strength of our study is that the association between preclinical atherosclerosis and outcomes remained robust after comprehensive multivariable adjustment (an important limitation of the previous study [21]). Moreover, our analysis demonstrates a clear gradient of risk according to atherosclerotic burden, with individuals presenting ≥ 3 plaques exhibiting a persistently higher risk. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that, beyond the mere presence of carotid plaques, plaque burden itself is independently associated with future CVE as well as the composite outcome of CVE and mortality.

The robustness of our findings is supported by the consistent association between the presence and burden of preclinical carotid atherosclerosis and CVE/mortality, even after adjustment for well-established risk factors in the type 1 diabetes population. In recent years, adiposity- and insulin resistance–related variables have gained increasing attention in this setting. In particular, insulin resistance proxies such as eGDR have previously been associated with atherosclerosis burden in our cohort [24]. Beyond preclinical atherosclerosis, this marker has also been linked to future cardiovascular events and mortality in people with type 1 diabetes [25, 26]. Indeed, obesity was recently identified as a major contributor to CVD in adults with onset of type 1 diabetes [27]. In this context, the persistence of the observed associations after adjustment for these factors (Model 3) is reassuring. Moreover, in a sensitivity analysis including BMI in the fully adjusted model, the association between carotid plaque burden and both cardiovascular events and the composite outcome remained essentially unchanged, with participants presenting ≥ 3 plaques continuing to show a significantly increased risk of adverse outcomes (Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, the association between carotid plaque burden and outcomes remained robust when DKD was replaced by continuous measures of renal function and albuminuria. Notably, albuminuria was independently associated with adverse outcomes, whereas eGFR was not, and the prognostic value of higher plaque burden persisted after accounting for these parameters (Supplementary Table 5). Importantly, the consistency of the observed associations across unadjusted, partially adjusted, and fully adjusted models reduces the likelihood that the findings are explained by model overfitting alone.

Furthermore, our exploratory analyses showed that carotid ultrasonography remained independently associated with cardiovascular events even after accounting for ST1RE. Among the several cardiovascular risk assessment tools available for type 1 diabetes [28], ST1RE is one of the most extensively validated across populations beyond the original derivation cohort [29, 30]. Although cardiovascular risk scores are useful for population-level risk stratification, their ability to predict risk at the individual level is limited, particularly over longer follow-up periods, as they are generally developed for short- to intermediate-term prediction [29]. Therefore, imaging-based assessment of preclinical atherosclerosis may provide a valuable complementary tool for long-term cardiovascular risk evaluation in people with type 1 diabetes.

In line with previous reports [31, 32], the presence of diabetic retinopathy in our cohort was independently associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes, even after adjustment for multiple confounders and for the ST1RE score. Although a trend toward an association between diabetic kidney disease and the risk of CVE and mortality was observed, this relationship did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the low prevalence of DKD in our cohort (less than 10%). These findings support the concept that microvascular complications, particularly retinopathy, may act as markers of cumulative vascular injury and may help refine cardiovascular risk stratification in people with type 1 diabetes.

Previous studies in individuals with type 1 diabetes have also demonstrated that atherosclerotic burden assessed by coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring is independently associated with major adverse CVE [33, 34]. However, despite growing evidence supporting the prognostic value of imaging-based measures of preclinical atherosclerosis, there is currently no standardized imaging modality recommended for cardiovascular risk assessment in type 1 diabetes [35]. In the present study, we assessed preclinical atherosclerosis using carotid plaque detection based on a previously published standardized ultrasound protocol [13]. Carotid ultrasonography offers several advantages, including the absence of ionizing radiation, high reproducibility, and the ability to detect atherosclerosis at earlier stages compared with CAC scoring. In addition, carotid plaque assessment is not influenced by statin therapy, which was common in our cohort (39% of participants); whereas statin use may increase CAC scores and make their interpretation more difficult [36].

Several strengths and limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Among the main strengths is the inclusion of a large, well-characterized cohort of adults with type 1 diabetes and no prior CVD, followed prospectively for clinically relevant cardiovascular outcomes. Importantly, evidence linking preclinical carotid atherosclerosis with hard CVE in individuals with type 1 diabetes remains scarce, with most previous studies being cross-sectional or focused on surrogate endpoints. In this context, our study provides valuable longitudinal evidence on the prognostic significance of carotid atherosclerosis in this population. Preclinical carotid atherosclerosis was systematically assessed using high-resolution carotid ultrasound according to a standardized and previously validated protocol [13], with all examinations performed by experienced operators, ensuring high reproducibility. Moreover, this study goes beyond the mere presence of carotid plaque by evaluating atherosclerotic burden, demonstrating a clear dose–response relationship with CVE and mortality. The robustness of our findings is further supported by comprehensive multivariable adjustment, including traditional cardiovascular risk factors, diabetes-related complications, ST1RE risk score, insulin sensitivity estimates, and renal function.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, the number of CVE, although comparable to other longitudinal studies in type 1 diabetes, was relatively limited and may have reduced statistical power for certain subgroup analyses, particularly for DKD, which had a low prevalence in this cohort. In addition, the number of outcome events relative to the number of covariates included in the fully adjusted models was limited, which may increase the risk of overfitting and reduce the stability of some estimates. However, covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, and the consistency of the associations across crude, partially adjusted, and fully adjusted models supports the robustness of the main findings. Second, the observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded.

Third, participants were recruited from a single tertiary referral center rather than from an unselected population of individuals with type 1 diabetes, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, analyses were based on baseline measurements only, without accounting for potential changes in clinical variables or treatments over time. In particular, statin use was considered only at baseline and was not modelled as a time-dependent variable. Because the detection of carotid plaque may have led to treatment intensification during follow-up, this approach may have attenuated the observed associations by preventing some cardiovascular events. Additionally, the inclusion criteria reflected a prespecified protocol and guideline recommendations available at the time of study design, which may differ from more recent risk-stratification approaches. Nevertheless, the consistency of the associations observed across multiple models and outcomes supports the clinical relevance of carotid plaque burden assessment for long-term cardiovascular risk stratification in adults with type 1 diabetes.

Another limitation is that carotid atherosclerosis was assessed using two-dimensional rather than three-dimensional ultrasound. Although 3D ultrasound enables plaque volume quantification and has recently been evaluated in individuals with type 1 diabetes [37], its use remains limited and is not yet widely implemented in routine clinical practice. Furthermore, 2D carotid plaque burden has been shown to predict future cardiovascular events in the general population [38], and whether 3D plaque volume measurements provide additional prognostic information beyond conventional plaque assessment in type 1 diabetes remains uncertain. Therefore, the incremental clinical value of 3D carotid ultrasound in this population requires further investigation.

In conclusion, in adults with type 1 diabetes and no previous cardiovascular disease, the presence of preclinical carotid atherosclerosis is independently associated with an increased risk of future CVE and the composite of CVE and mortality. Beyond plaque presence, a higher carotid plaque burden identifies individuals at particularly elevated risk, demonstrating a clear dose–response relationship. These associations remained robust after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors, diabetes-related complications, renal parameters, insulin sensitivity, and established type 1 diabetes-specific risk scores. Given the limited availability of validated long-term cardiovascular risk prediction tools in type 1 diabetes, our findings support the potential role of carotid ultrasound as a non-invasive and reproducible tool that may complement cardiovascular risk assessment. Assessment of carotid plaque burden may provide clinically relevant information to help identify individuals at higher cardiovascular risk who could benefit from more intensive preventive strategies.
