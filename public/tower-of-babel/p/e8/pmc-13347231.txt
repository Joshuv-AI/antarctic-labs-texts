Considerations on the risk assessment of genetically modified plants containing transformation events stacked by conventional crossing

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a mandate from EFSA, the EFSA Panel on Genetically Modified Organisms (GMO Panel) addressed the food and feed risk assessment of genetically modified plants containing stacked transformation events (‘stacks’). The GMO Panel examined the experience gained in the assessment of stacks in the last 20 years. Based on this, the GMO Panel concluded that under specific conditions, field trials for comparative analysis may not always be required for the assessment of stacks and a derogation may be considered. The conditions are: all the events combined in the stack should express traits through newly expressed proteins (NEPs) and these should not be metabolic modifiers. Under the same conditions and in the absence of interactions affecting NEP expression levels, a derogation from current requirements for dietary exposure assessment may also be considered. The GMO Panel also proposes improvements for applicants regarding the presentation of molecular characterisation data for stacks.

SUMMARY

EFSA mandated its GMO Panel to issue a scientific statement on the food and feed safety assessment of genetically modified (GM) plants containing stacked transformation events (henceforth, ‘stacked events’ or ‘stacks’) obtained by conventional crossing of GM plants, each with one or several transformation events. This statement addresses three terms of reference (ToR) as requested in the mandate: Summary of experience gathered by the GMO Panel in the assessment of GM plants containing stacked events over the last 20 years, including more recent complex cases. (ToR1).Building on earlier experience, propose improvements in the way data are presented, allowing streamlining of the risk assessment process, with a focus on potential interactions. (ToR2).Building on earlier experience, revise the data requirements for the assessment of interactions between the events combined in stacks and, in particular, propose criteria to define cases where field trial data used for agronomic, phenotypic and compositional analysis (‘comparative field trials’) may not be required. (ToR3).

To address ToR1, the GMO Panel reviewed the 61 applications for authorisation of stacked events for food and feed (‘stack applications’) for which the assessment was completed between 2003 and 2025. It was found that stacks combining single events with specific traits have been extensively assessed, in particular those conferring herbicide tolerance or insect resistance. In addition, the GMO Panel's experience with stacks has concerned mainly events in which the traits were achieved through the expression of newly expressed proteins (NEPs). By contrast, events with other traits, or with traits not achieved through NEPs (e.g. obtained by RNA interference, RNAi), occurred only sporadically in stacks. The GMO Panel also reviewed the outcome of the comparative analysis of the 61 stack applications: none of the results were considered indicative of an unexpected interaction between the events for any of the applications. For a few applications, a targeted comparative analysis (limited to a small number of selected parameters) was used within a hypothesis‐driven approach to test for a potential interaction identified from the characteristics of the single events.

To address ToR2, the GMO Panel provided guidance on how to present the molecular characterisation data in stack applications more clearly, to support a straightforward risk assessment of the stack. Recommendations are given regarding the presentation of the sequencing data of the stacked events, updated bioinformatic analysis and protein expression data. A tabular format is proposed for some of the data, to facilitate the discussion of interactions between the events.

To address ToR3, the GMO Panel considered the review conducted for ToR1 and formulated two criteria to define cases where comparative field trials may not be required: first, the intended traits must be obtained through the expression of NEPs; second, none of these NEPs should be a metabolic modifier. When the two criteria are met by all the single events in a stack, a derogation from the requirement for comparative field trials may be considered by EFSA on a case‐by‐case basis, in accordance with Article 5(2) of Implementing Regulation (EU) 503/2013. The GMO Panel concluded that a derogation may also be considered for the assessment of dietary exposure to NEPs, based on the same criteria and provided that there are no interactions affecting NEP expression levels in the stack. All stack applications should include a structured discussion of potential interactions between the single events. Where comparative field trials or dietary exposure data are not provided, this discussion should be sufficiently detailed to support a derogation.

INTRODUCTION

Background as provided by EFSA

Commission implementing Regulation EU No 503/2013 (Reg. (EU) 503/2013) applies to applications for authorisation of genetically modified (GM) food and feed in accordance with Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003 of the European Parliament and of the Council and amending Commission Regulations (EC) No 641/2004 and (EC) No 1981/2006. Annex II of Reg. (EU) 503/2013 provides the specific scientific requirements for the food and feed risk assessment of GM plants containing stacked transformation events (‘stacked events’ or ‘stacks’): ‘The applicant shall provide a risk assessment of each single transformation event (‘single event’), or refer to already submitted applications, and shall also include an assessment of the following aspects: (a) stability of the transformation events; (b) expression of the transformation events; and (c) potential synergistic or antagonistic effects resulting from the combination of the transformation events shall be subject to an assessment in accordance with Sections 1.4 (Toxicology), 1.5 (Allergenicity) and 1.6 (Nutritional assessment).’

Considerable experience has been gathered by the GMO Panel from assessing 61 applications for authorisation of stacks for food and feed uses, import and processing (‘stack applications’) over the last 20 years. Taking stock of relevant experience, the GMO Panel analysed the current data requirements and the presentation of data in order to identify information which, on a case‐by‐case basis, might not need to be provided for the risk assessment of stacked events, 1 while maintaining the highest possible level of protection for consumers and the environment.

ASSESSMENT

Introduction

Current strategy for the risk assessment of stacks

Reg. (EU) 503/2013 lays down the scientific requirements for all applications for the placing on the market of GM plants for food and feed uses submitted under Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003. Annex II specifies the requirements for the risk assessment of food and feed from GM plants, including those containing stacked events. In the risk assessment of stack applications, applicants must provide (or refer to) a risk assessment for each single transformation event.

According to Reg. (EU) 503/2013, the risk assessment of a stack application must also address the following aspects: ‘(a) stability of the transformation events, (b) expression of the transformation events, (c) potential synergistic or antagonistic effects resulting from the combination of the transformation events shall be subject to an assessment in accordance with Sections 1.4 (Toxicology), 1.5 (Allergenicity) and 1.6 (Nutritional assessment).’ This is done following the general approaches and principles laid down in Reg. (EU) 503/2013, EFSA GMO Panel (2011) and the other applicable EFSA guidance documents,3 as summarised in the following paragraphs for the main risk areas.

To assess the stability of the transformation events in the stack (a), each constituent event should be sequenced in the stack material itself and compared to the previously assessed sequence. To assess the expression of the transformation events (b), NEP levels in the stack should be quantified and compared with the levels quantified in the corresponding single events. This comparison should support the identification of potential interactions between the combined transformation events that could alter NEP expression. Updated bioinformatics analyses should be provided for all the events combined in the stack, including (1) the assessment of similarity to known allergens and toxins for both the NEPs and all open reading frames (ORFs) within the insert and spanning the junctions between the insert and the plant genome, (2) an analysis of the presence of celiac associated epitopes for all NEPs, (3) an evaluation of potential gene interruption and (4) an assessment of the potential for horizontal gene transfer to microorganisms.

To assess whether the combination of transformation events gives rise to synergistic or antagonistic effects (c), a comparative assessment is required to identify differences in agronomic performance, phenotypic characteristics and compositional data between the stack and derived food and feed and its comparator. According to Reg. (EU) 503/2013, the comparative analysis should conclude on the following points (hereafter, ‘conclusions of the comparative analysis’): ‘(a) whether agronomic and phenotypic characteristics of the genetically modified plant are, except for the introduced trait(s), different to the characteristics of its conventional counterpart and/or equivalent to the reference varieties, taking into account natural variation; (b) whether compositional characteristics of the genetically modified food and feed are, taking into account natural variation, different to the characteristics of its conventional counterpart and/or equivalent to the reference varieties, except for the introduced trait(s); (c) characteristics for which the genetically modified plant or the genetically modified food and feed are different to the characteristics of its conventional counterpart and/or not equivalent to the reference varieties taking into account natural variation, which need further investigation; (d) whether, in the case of transformation events stacked by conventional crossing, there are indications of interactions between the combined transformation events.’ Conclusions (a–c) are required both for single and stacked events; conclusion (d) is specific to stacked events. Consequently, the comparative analysis performed for stack applications has so far been used for the identification of potential interactions that would manifest as measurable differences in agronomic, phenotypic or compositional data.

The comparative analysis of composition determines whether the combination of events results in the presence of new constituents or relevant changes in the levels of existing food and feed components. Where differences are identified, their potential impact on toxicological, allergenicity and nutritional endpoints is evaluated to determine whether they raise safety or nutritional concerns. In assessing the NEPs, evaluations of toxicity and allergenicity consider the potential interactions resulting from their combined presence in the stack. The assessment further considers the established biological function and mode of action (MoA) of the individual events, the NEPs themselves and, for the traits conferred, determines whether there is any scientifically plausible hypothesis for interactions. The assessment of NEPs in stack applications also includes estimates of human and animal dietary exposure, accounting for expression levels in the relevant plant tissues.

For segregating crops such as maize and oilseed rape, according to Reg. (EU) 503/2013, the application for a stacked event (‘high stack’) must cover all possible ‘sub‐combinations’ of events (stacks combining fewer of the events of the high stack) ‘independently of their origin’: this includes lower‐order stacks that could be obtained by conventional breeding. The applicant shall provide a scientific rationale justifying that there is no need to provide experimental data for sub‐combinations not yet authorised or, in the absence of such rationale, provide the experimental data. 4 On this basis, the GMO Panel has defined a strategy for the assessment of sub‐combinations, 5 including those for which experimental data are not available, for example because such stacks do not exist yet. According to this strategy, a sub‐combination lacking specific data can be assessed using – together with data on relevant existing sub‐combinations – the outcome of the risk assessment of the high stack and the single events. The applicant is required to provide the relevant information and discuss whether interactions can be hypothesised for the sub‐combination under assessment.

ToR1: Summary of experience gathered by the GMO Panel in the assessment of GM plants containing stacked events over the last 20 years, including more recent complex cases

Summary of the stacked events assessed by the GMO Panel

Between 2003, when the GMO Panel was established, and the end of 2025, the GMO Panel adopted scientific opinions on 136 GM plant applications (excluding renewal applications); of these, 75 concerned single events and 61 concerned stacked events. For stacks of segregating crops, such as maize and oilseed rape, the risk assessments also covered sub‐combinations, i.e. multiple stacked events (Section 3.1). When these sub‐combinations are also included, the GMO Panel assessed 300 stacked events.

Tables 1 and 2 provide a summary of the scientific opinions on stack applications adopted by the GMO Panel, based on the crop and on the characteristics of the single events combined in the stacks. In Table 1, the stack applications are grouped according to the traits expressed by the single events combined in the stacks. The table shows that nearly all stacks, 60 over a total of 61, contain at least one event expressing a herbicide tolerance trait and that a large majority, 46 out of 61, contain at least one event conferring insect resistance. Other traits occur only sporadically in the stacks. In addition, NEPs used as markers in the transformation process, not included in Table 1, were present in 22 out of 61 stack applications.

Outcome of the comparative analysis and identification of interactions

The GMO Panel conducted a review of the 61 scientific opinions on stack applications adopted before the end of 2025 (Tables 1 and 2) focusing on the role of the comparative analysis in the identification of unexpected interactions (Section 3.1: conclusions of the comparative analysis (d)).

The review showed that, across the 61 scientific opinions on stacked events, the comparative analysis did not identify any changes considered indicative of interactions between the combined events. The review also showed that in a limited number of cases, 7 the comparative analysis supported a hypothesis‐driven assessment approach. In those cases, the GMO Panel used the characteristics of the single events to predict potential interactions that could affect specific compositional parameters, and then used information on those parameters to test the interaction hypothesis. For example, the GMO Panel requested the additional, targeted comparative analysis of nitrite and nitrate levels, not included among the mandatory endpoints for maize (OECD, 2002), in tissues of the three‐event stack maize DP202216 × NK603 × DAS‐40278‐9 to investigate a potential interaction between two of the events (EFSA GMO Panel, 2025). 8 Section 3.4 discusses the implications of these findings for the experimental evidence currently required for stacks.

ToR2: Building on earlier experience, propose improvements in the way data are presented, allowing streamlining of the risk assessment process, with a focus on potential interactions

The molecular characterisation data requested for stack applications are primarily intended to confirm the structure and the sequence of the events combined in the stack, as well as to assess the potential for interactions between these events. These interactions may occur at the level of the inserts and their associated genetic elements, throughout the molecular processes from transcription and translation to the expression of NEPs, or at the level of their biological functions and their expected effects.

However, the GMO Panel has noted that the presentation of these data by the applicants does not always support a straightforward risk assessment of the stack. For example, applicants often cite previously submitted information without adequate synthesis, do not provide side‐by‐side comparisons of protein expression levels or do not clearly highlight newly identified information from the bioinformatics analyses. The following considerations are therefore provided to guide a clearer presentation of molecular characterisation data in applications for stacked events.

Integrity of the events

The resequencing of events in the stack is a requirement in Reg. (EU) 503/2013 to confirm their integrity. In accordance with the existing EFSA technical note on DNA sequencing quality (EFSA, 2024) and any future updates, the applicant should clearly highlight all the nucleotide differences observed in the events sequenced in the stack compared to all the sequences of those events previously submitted. Moreover, the applicant should assess the potential relevance for safety of all identified differences.

All genetic elements present in the stack should be described in the context of the stack application (see Table B.1 of Appendix B). In addition, the description of the NEPs, along with their biological functions and intended effects, should be provided (Table B.2). The applicant should discuss whether the combination of events could give rise to interactions affecting the structure, expression or stability of the introduced genetic elements, as compared to the single events. This information should be provided in the relevant section of the main text.

Updated bioinformatics analyses

The updated bioinformatics analyses in GM dossiers include: (i) analysis of the flanking sequences to identify potential interruptions of endogenous genes; (ii) assessment of similarity to known toxins and allergens for the NEPs and for all ORFs within the insert and spanning the junctions between the insert and the plant genome: (iii) analysis of the NEPs for their capacity to trigger celiac disease symptoms; (iv) assessment of the events for their potential to facilitate horizontal gene transfer; (v) RNAi off‐target analysis in case any of the events include a gene silencing construct. These analyses are performed according to Reg. (EU) 503/2013 and the other applicable EFSA guidance documents. 9

The purpose of bioinformatics analyses in stack applications is to update the assessment of the stacked events by using their latest sequence information as determined in the stack, thereby addressing changes, if any, compared to the previously assessed version(s), and employing the most up to date databases available. Accordingly, the updated analyses submitted as part of stack applications should clearly and explicitly highlight the new information identified in the results in comparison with the bioinformatics data previously submitted and previously assessed by the GMO Panel. If the results of the bioinformatics analyses for the events combined in the stack remain unchanged compared to those previously assessed, this should be clearly stated. If any new or different result is obtained, it should be explicitly described and discussed, with particular attention to its relevance for the safety assessment of the stack. A summary of the bioinformatics analysis results for each event in the stack should be provided in the relevant section of the main text of the application. 10

Protein expression

Applicants are expected to provide a comparison of the NEP levels in the stack and in the corresponding single events. For this comparison to be meaningful, the singles and the stack should be grown in the same field trials, under the same conditions and with the same crop management practices, including the same herbicide treatment, following the indications of Reg. (EU) 503/2013.

Expression levels should be quantified in plant tissues from both the single and stacked events using appropriate methodologies, in accordance with the existing explanatory note on the determination of NEP levels (EFSA, 2018) and any future updates. The expression levels measured in the singles and stack shall be presented in the same table. The presentation of the data should enable the identification of differences in expression levels that may indicate possible interactions between the combined events. The applicant should address the biological relevance of any observed difference. Where no relevant differences are identified, this should be clearly stated. The applicant should indicate where this information and the tables can be found.

The recommendations in this section are independent of the discussion in Section 3.4, hence they apply to all stack applications. The applicants are encouraged to implement the improvements described in this section as soon as possible after the publication of the statement.

ToR3: Building on earlier experience, revise the data requirements for the assessment of interactions between the events combined in stack applications and, in particular, propose criteria to define cases where field trial data used for agronomic, phenotypic and compositional analysis may not be required

Based on the experience gained in the risk assessment of stacks, as evaluated in Section 3.2, the GMO Panel analysed the current data requirements to identify information that, on a case‐by‐case basis, may not need to be provided for the risk assessment of stacked events, by way of derogation, in accordance with Article 5(2) of Regulation (EU) No 503/2013. In this section, the conditions under which a derogation may be considered are discussed. In accordance with Article 5(3) of Regulation (EU) No 503/2013, the consideration of a derogation does not preclude EFSA from requesting the relevant data during the risk assessment, where appropriate.

Field trials for comparative analysis

Under the current strategy (Section 3.1.1), applicants are required to provide field trials for agronomic, phenotypic and compositional comparative analysis (‘comparative field trials’) as part of the identification of potential interactions between events. As shown in Section 3.2, the GMO Panel has assessed many combinations of events and did not identify indications of interactions raising safety concerns. This fact does not rule out the occurrence of interactions between new events in future stacks. However, the GMO Panel has extensive experience in assessing stacks combining specific categories of events (Tables 1 and 2). 11 The GMO Panel concluded that, for stacks combining those categories of events, comparative field trials as currently performed provide limited added value for identifying interactions. Where a hypothesis of interaction is defined, the comparative assessment can be applied in a targeted manner to test it, focusing on relevant endpoints (Section 3.2.2), rather than relying on an untargeted, non‐hypothesis‐driven approach.

Criteria to define cases where field trials for comparative analysis may not be required

Based on the considerations in Section 3.4.1, the GMO Panel has identified screening criteria that can be used to determine whether comparative field trials may not be required to conclude the risk assessment of a given stack application. These criteria should be applied to each single event combined in the stack, to support a derogation on a case‐by‐case basis in line with Article 5(2) of Reg. (EU) 503/2013, without anticipating the outcome of the assessment.

Criterion 1: Traits achieved through the expression of NEPs

Most of the new traits combined in the stacks already assessed by the GMO Panel were achieved through NEPs (see Table 2). The experience gained with these events supports the view that their behaviour in stacked combinations is generally well predictable based on the assessment of the single events. Therefore, only events expressing traits obtained through NEPs will be considered for potential derogation from the requirements for comparative field trials.

Criterion 2: None of the events combined in the stack acts as a metabolic modifier

A metabolic modifier is defined here as a NEP that reprograms, redirects or broadly perturbs endogenous metabolic or hormonal pathways of the plant, for example, by introducing a novel catalytic function, or by modifying the regulation of existing metabolic networks, including transcription factors or other regulators of protein activity. For this criterion, NEPs introduced to specifically metabolise herbicides (e.g. PAT 12 ) or to compensate for the function of the herbicide's target enzyme (e.g. CP4 EPSPS 13 ), as well as other previously assessed selectable markers (e.g. PMI 14 ), are not considered metabolic modifiers.

Because metabolic modifiers can affect central metabolic or regulatory processes, the agronomic, phenotypic and compositional analysis is particularly important for GM plants — single events and stacks — expressing these types of NEPs. The GMO Panel considers that field trial data for such stacks provide valuable information for identifying potential synergistic or antagonistic effects resulting from the combination of the transformation events.

Dietary exposure assessment

The GMO Panel considered whether criteria 1 and 2 (Section 3.4.2) may be extended to data other than comparative field trials. This consideration applies to cases in which both criteria are fulfilled, no plausible hypothesis of interaction is defined and the available evidence, including molecular characterisation data, does not indicate any interaction affecting NEP expression. In such cases, the dietary exposure assessment performed for the individual single events would remain applicable to the corresponding stack event. Therefore, on a case‐by‐case basis and in accordance with Article 5(2) of Reg. (EU) 503/2013, EFSA may consider a derogation from data requirements for the dietary exposure assessment for humans and animals for these stack applications.

Comparative analysis of the single events

In cases where no field trial data are provided for the stack (Section 3.4.2), the outcome of the comparative analysis of the single events should then be used to identify potential interactions. Such analysis may help indicate whether unexpected interactions may occur in the stack, even if these are not predicted by the analysis of the MoA or from the safety assessment of the single events. Statistically significant differences between a single event and its conventional counterpart may exist that could be biologically relevant in terms of potential interactions with other single events. Even if such differences did not raise safety concerns for the single event, they could still reflect changes in the plant's metabolism that interact with the other events in the stack. By examining these relevant effects observed in the single events, the applicant should formulate testable hypotheses about possible interactions and assess whether they could raise safety concerns in the stacked event. 15 Differences to be considered relevant should include the changes that were further assessed for safety in the scientific opinion for the single events. 16 The applicant should also consider additional differences that may be relevant for the identification of potential interactions when the single event is combined in the stack. The applicant should clearly describe the criteria (e.g. effect size) used to select such differences.

Evidence needed for the application of the criteria

This section summarises the information the applicant is expected to provide when submitting a stack application without comparative field trials or dietary exposure assessment. The information should be presented and discussed in the main text of the relevant sections of the dossier.

Data and applicability of criteria

The applicant should clearly indicate which information is not being submitted: the comparative analysis, the dietary exposure assessment or both. For EFSA to consider a derogation, for either data set, the applicant should provide a table listing all the events combined in the stack and include, for each event, a justification explaining why the two screening criteria are fulfilled (Table B.3).

Discussion of potential interactions

As described above, the applicant should include an analysis of the potential occurrence of interactions between the events considering all the information available for the single events and the stack (e.g. biological function, MoA, protein expression levels). The analysis should also consider the results of the comparative analysis of the single events (Section 3.4.4), selecting differences relevant for potential interactions with other events in the stack (Table B.4). The criteria for the selection should be described. The outcome of the analysis should clearly indicate (i) whether there are indications of interactions and in this case (ii) whether any testable hypothesis of interaction has been identified.

If there is indication of an interaction altering NEP levels, a dietary exposure assessment should be provided even when the conditions in Section 3.4.2 are fulfilled. Any other hypothesis of interaction should be addressed case‐by‐case, e.g. using further evidence from scientific literature or by generating appropriate, additional experimental data.

CONCLUSIONS

Over the last 20 years, the GMO Panel has accumulated considerable experience in the assessment of stacks. Based on this experience, the GMO Panel considers that, for stacks combining events expressing traits only through NEPs that are not metabolic modifiers, comparative field trials may not always be required and a derogation may be considered by EFSA on a case‐by‐case basis. Under the same conditions and in the absence of interactions affecting NEP expression levels, a derogation from dietary exposure assessment may also be considered. The GMO Panel also proposes improvements to applicants in the presentation of molecular characterisation data in stack applications, to facilitate the analysis of potential interactions between the events.
