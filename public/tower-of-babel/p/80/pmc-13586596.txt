Early Prediction of the Breast Cancer Response to Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy Based on Intratumoral Interstitial Fluid Pressure using 3-D Subharmonic-Aided Pressure Estimation
Authors: Sung In Choi, Basak Dogan, Corinne E. Wessner, Jessica H. Porembka, Priscilla Machado, B. Bersu Ozcan, Nisha Unni, Maysa Abu-Khalaf, Kenneth Hoyt, Flemming Forsberg, Kibo Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Elevated intratumoral interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) may negatively impact the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). This study aimed to predict the breast cancer response to NAC based on intratumoral IFP using 3D contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) with subharmonic-aided pressure estimation (SHAPE). This prospective study enrolled breast cancer patients undergoing NAC from two clinical sites. 3D CEUS was performed at 0%, 10%, and 30% of NAC completion using a GE Logiq E9 with Definity. The pressure gradients between the tumor and adjacent normal tissue were estimated by SHAPE, utilizing the inverse linear relationship between the ultrasound contrast agent’s subharmonic signal amplitude and hydrostatic pressure. The obtained SHAPE gradients were evaluated for the prediction of pathological complete response (pCR) and radiological response (RR, tumor volume reduction ≥90%), using t-test and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Additionally, the effect of incorporating tumor volume into the SHAPE gradient was evaluated. This study included thirty-seven patients. SHAPE gradients were significantly different between pCR and non-pCR at 0% (p = 0.03), 10% (p = 0.03), and 30% (p = 0.001) completion of NAC, and between RR and non-RR at 30% (p = 0.007) completion of NAC. The optimal predictive performance for SHAPE was achieved at 30% NAC completion, yielding area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.85 for pCR and 0.79 for RR. By combining the tumor volume-based parameters and SHAPE gradient, AUCs were 0.98 for pCR and 0.84 for RR predictions at 30% NAC completion. SHAPE gradient, independently or combined with tumor volume may aid in the early prediction of breast cancer NAC response.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most diagnosed cancer among women in the United States, accounting for 32% of all cancer cases [1]. It is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in women as well as the primary contributor to the rise in overall cancer mortality among U.S. women under 50 y old [1]. Localized-stage breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed among U.S. women, with diagnoses increasing by 1.4% per year from 2012 to 2021 [2].

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is the standard treatment for locally advanced breast cancer at an early stage. Its primary purpose is to reduce the tumor size and stage making conservative breast surgery a viable option [3]. A randomized trial for breast cancer patients who underwent NAC found that mastectomy rates were reduced by 7%–13% [3]. Pathologic complete response (pCR) is the most favorable outcome after NAC [4]. The pCR rate among the patients is positively correlated with the long-term survival rate after NAC and is a strong predictive marker of survival with some breast cancer subtypes [4-6]. However, the NAC outcomes are not uniformly positive, and the pCR rate varies widely depending on various factors such as breast cancer subtypes, chemotherapy regimens, patient age, medical history, and so on. Continuing NAC without assessing early treatment response may expose patients to unnecessary toxicity and limited opportunities for treatment adaptation, possibly worsen oncologic outcomes, leading to a higher risk of distant metastasis and inferior survival [7,8]. Therefore, early prediction of the NAC response may help minimize nonbeneficial delays for patients who do not respond to the treatment and paves the way for early switching to a potentially more effective regimen.

Early prediction of breast cancer response to NAC treatment is challenging due to the heterogeneity of tumor characteristics and microenvironment variability. Current practice is lacking, not standardized and involves a combination of clinical factors and imaging modalities. Researchers have investigated many predictive markers of NAC response, including tumor size, chemoresistance, and molecular markers such as estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), Ki-67 protein, and more [9,10]. One important factor affecting chemotherapy effectiveness is the interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) within cancerous tissue. Elevated intratumoral IFP is mechanically associated with tumor stiffness and presents a known barrier to drug delivery [11]. This increased pressure results from an abnormal microenvironment characterized by high vascular permeability, rapid cell proliferation, and functional deficiencies of lymphatic microvessel, along with other changes in cancerous tissue [12-14].

Wagner and Wiig [13] reported that the IFP within cancerous tissue increased to a range of 10– 40 mmHg, whereas it was typically maintained at 0 mmHg in normal skin and muscle tissue. Others reported that the IFP of breast tumors was up to 29 mmHg while the normal breast parenchyma was still close to 0 mmHg [15-17]. This elevation in intratumoral pressure contributes to increased resistance to blood flow and changes in transcapillary pressure. These dynamics create an imbalance in the pressures between the tumor and adjacent tissue, thereby hindering the effective delivery of cancer therapeutics to both tumor cells and surrounding interstitium [14,18,19]. Consequently, monitoring the pressure gradient between the cancerous and normal tissues may be a predictive marker for chemotherapy efficacy.

In our pilot study, we introduced a pressure gradient monitoring technique to predict the breast cancer response to NAC, utilizing subharmonic-aided pressure estimation (SHAPE) with 3D contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) [20]. SHAPE estimates pressure based on a linear inverse relationship between subharmonic signal amplitude from ultrasound contrast agents (UCAs) and hydrostatic pressure [21]. This technique allowed us to estimate a relative pressure inside the tumor compared to the adjacent normal tissue, which forms the SHAPE gradient. The study showed that the SHAPE gradient measured at 10% of NAC completion could effectively distinguish radiological response (RR; p = 0.0011), which is defined as cases achieving a volume reduction after NAC of more than 90% of the initial tumor volume [20].

However, predicting pCR (p = 0.13) using the SHAPE gradient as a prediction marker proved more challenging in the earlier study. It was possibly due to a small sample size and the later study point, i.e., 60% completion of NAC, predicted neither pCR nor RR. Defining the most predictive study time point for assessing NAC response is critical when using the SHAPE gradient as a predictive marker, because the SHAPE gradient is a relative pressure change compared to the reference pressure, which can also be altered by chemotherapy. Chemotherapy drugs possibly affect the vascular condition of tumor and the density and composition of surrounding breast tissue [22,23]. Thus, the SHAPE gradient measured at each study time point was considered as an independent prediction marker to minimize the variation caused by the systemic treatment.

This study aimed to evaluate further the SHAPE gradient as an accurate and early predictor for breast cancer responses to NAC with an expanded population from two clinical sites. To enhance the predictive value of the SHAPE gradient, we substituted the study time point at 60% completion of NAC with a time point at 30% completion of NAC. We hypothesized that SHAPE gradient could predict NAC treatment efficacy at 10% and 30% NAC completion with possibly higher accuracy at 30% NAC completion. Additionally, we investigated whether the predictive value of the SHAPE gradient varies across breast cancer subtypes and assessed whether tumor volume or tumor volume change can be used as an additional predictor, both independently and combined with the SHAPE gradient.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This IRB - approved study (IND:112241, NCT04715958) prospectively enrolled early-stage breast cancer patients who underwent NAC at two sites from April 2021 to June 2024. Patients with other primary cancers requiring systematic treatment were excluded. All participants provided written informed consent. NAC regimens were determined by treating physicians and followed standard of care practice. The study was originally designed based on the sample size of 200, which aims to achieve 90% power if the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis results in an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86 with consideration of a dropout rate of 35%.

Ultrasound examinations

Participants had CEUS exams at three time points during the treatment period, 0% (pretreatment), 10%, and 30% of NAC completion. The percentage was calculated based on each participant’s assigned NAC regimen length. For example, for the 24-wk regimen, the exams at 10% and 30% of NAC completion were set between weeks 2–3 and weeks 7 –8, respectively. After 100% completion of NAC, participants had a conventional ultrasound (US) exam only to evaluate the final tumor size before surgery. All US exams were performed using a GE Logiq E9 scanner and a RSP6-16 probe (3D linear array; GE HealthCare, Waukesha, WI, USA) by a registered research sonographer or a radiologist with extensive CEUS experience.

We first performed conventional US including 3-D tumor size measurements and Doppler imaging. Then, patients received an infusion of Definity (Lantheus Medical Imaging, N Billerica, MA; 3 mL suspended in 50 ml of saline) via a vein using a 22- or 24-gauge angiocatheter at a rate of 4–10 mL/min, titrated to effect. The UCA dosage was determined based on the previous subharmonic imaging study [20]. Before collecting the SHAPE data, the optimal acoustic setting was determined for each participant using the power optimization algorithm, detecting the most pressure-sensitive subharmonic signal from UCA [24,25]. With the optimal acoustic setting, three sets of 3-D SHAPE data were collected with and without the infusion of Definity using the built-in subharmonic imaging mode (transmitting/receiving frequencies of 9 MHz/4.5 MHz; cf., Fig. 1). The SHAPE datasets without UCA were collected to account for any US signals from tissues within the range of the UCA subharmonic frequency.

Data processing and analysis

The acquired 3D SHAPE data was analyzed offline with MATLAB (R2024b, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The example of the SHAPE data processing to extract the UCA subharmonic signal in the regions of interest (ROI) is shown in Figure 2. Each 3D dataset is composed of 100–200 2-D image planes (in the elevational direction), depending on the chosen image quality and the field of view determined by tumor size. The SHAPE gradients were calculated from images that included both tumor and adjacent normal tissue, with 20–30 frames selected equidistantly from each dataset. We selected two ROIs from each image: one in the tumor and another in the adjacent normal tissue (reference ROI). These ROIs were chosen avoiding specular tissue or biopsy clip. Once ROIs were selected, we kept the locations as consistent as possible across elevational frames. Additionally, the tumor ROI size was selected to match the reference ROI size in each frame. The SHAPE gradient resulted from the difference in the mean subharmonic signal intensities of these two ROIs after removing the signals not from UCA, as shown in Figure 2c. This process was applied to all datasets collected at 0%, 10%, and 30% of NAC completion. The calculated gradients for each study time point were assessed independently. We anticipated seeing more positive SHAPE gradient values for a relatively lower IFP elevation within the tumor, as SHAPE utilizes the inverse relationship between UCA signal amplitude and pressure.

For the tumor volume/volume change assessment, the measurements from the study US were utilized. Tumor volume (V) was calculated using the 3-D tumor measurements assuming an ellipsoid. The volume difference ratio (VDR) [26] was defined as, VDR0 = (V0-Vn)/V0, where V0 indicating the baseline volume at 0% NAC completion and n = 10 and 30 indicating 10% and 30% NAC completion, respectively. Three parameters—SHAPE gradient, Vn, and VDRn—at n% of NAC completion were compared to the NAC response outcomes, pCR and RR, using t-tests. pCR is defined as the absence of residual invasive cancer in the complete resected breast specimen and all sampled regional lymph nodes and the outcomes were obtained from the final surgical pathology reports [27]. RR refers to a 90% reduction in volume from the initial tumor volume [20]. Tumor vascularity was assessed using Doppler images (performed by an experienced radiologist [PM]). More than two large, Doppler-detectable vessels within the tumor were considered as highly vascularized tumors.

The prediction performance of the parameters was evaluated with ROC curve analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (Version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and MATLAB. Any p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All prediction models used logistic regression for binary classification with 95% confidence index (CI). The results were internally validated using the bootstrap with 100 replicas. The models combining two parameters included only the subjects who had both parameters, and the models using single versus two parameters were compared using the DeLong test.

Results

The flowchart of study participants is shown in Figure 3. The patient demographics, cancer characteristics, and NAC information are presented in Table 1. There were 15 (41%) pCRs and 22 (59%) non-pCRs. The non-pCRs included 20 partially completed cases and two no response cases. Alternatively, there were 21 (57%) RR and 15 (41%) non-RR cases; one participant did not have any imaging after NAC completion. The analysis included 37 (86%) out of 43 total enrolled cases, and 28 (76%) of them completed all three CEUS exams. Ideally, a SHAPE study from all patients at all exams was preferred, but not all patients could make all exams, 1 (0%), 2 (10%), and 3 (30% NAC completion). In the end, 32 (86%), 32 (86%), and 31 (84%) out of 37 patients could complete exams 1, 2, and 3, respectively, since the patients were newly diagnosed with breast cancer and would encounter various emotional and physical complications during chemotherapy.

The mean age of patients in the pCR group (49 ± 11 y) was younger compared to the non-pCR (53 ± 10 y), but no significant difference was found (p = 0.24). Tumor grade 3 (80%) was more prevalent in pCR than grade 1 and 2 (20%), compared to non-pCR, where fewer tumor grade 3 (32%) were found compared to grade 1 and 2 (68%). In assessing tumor vascularity, Doppler imaging was not available for one non-pCR case. The number of highly vascularized tumors (more than two large vessels within the tumor) was higher in pCR than in the non-pCR group (33% vs. 29%) but not significant (p = 0.77). These results are summarized in Table S1.

Figure 4 presents the results comparing the SHAPE gradients to the NAC responses. The mean SHAPE gradients (unit: a.u.) in the pCRs were significantly higher than those in the non-pCRs at all exam points (2.12 ± 4.17 vs. −0.53 ± 2.14 at 0%, 4.37 ± 9.71 vs. −0.84 ± 2.21 at 10%, and 2.21 ± 3.17 vs. −1.31 ± 1.92 at 30%). Unlike pCR, the SHAPE gradients in the RRs and non-RRs showed a significant difference only at 30% NAC completion (1.32 ± 3.95 vs. −0.32 ± 2.25 at 0%, 3.30 ± 8.57 vs. −1.10 ± 1.72 at 10%, and 1.19 ± 3.05 vs. −1.76 ± 2.04 at 30%). If a Bonferroni correction for the number of tests was made (n = 3, p = 0.05/3 = 0.017), the mean SHAPE gradients were significantly different between pCR and non-pCR (p < 0.01) and between RR and non-RR (p < 0.01) only at 30% NAC completion.

Analysis for breast cancer subtypes was performed for patients with hormone receptor positive (HR+: ER+ and/or PR+) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) subtypes. As shown in Table 2, the SHAPE gradients of the HR+ subtype showed a significant difference between pCRs and non-pCRs (all p-values <0.05), while the SHAPE gradients of the TNBC did not differentiate pCR from non-pCR. The HR+ subtype cancers showed lower treatment efficacy (32% of patients achieved pCR) compared to the TNBC cases (50% of patients achieved pCR), as NAC was primarily designed to focus on aggressive breast cancer types, such as TNBC and HER2-enriched [28].

Tumor volumes were not significantly different between the pCR and non-pCR groups at baseline (11.15 ± 15.27 vs. 11.70 ± 16.51 cm3), or at any other study time point (all p > 0.37). However, we observed a decreasing trend in tumor volumes from the initial volume for the pCR and RR cases. (pCR vs. non-pCR: 8.20 ± 10.37 vs. 5.43 ± 6.91 at 0%; 3.89 ± 5.34 vs. 5.88 ±7.94 at 10%; and 1.44 ± 1.92 vs. 2.47 ± 4.31 at 30%; RR vs. non-RR: 6.82 ± 9.18 vs. 6.77 ± 8.12 at 0%, 3.72 ± 4.88 vs. 7.29 ± 9.35 at 10%, and 1.46 ± 1.71 vs. 3.01 ± 5.28 at 30%; unit in cm3). Nevertheless, the VDR10 and VDR30 differentiated neither pCR nor RR from the other outcomes.

Table 3 shows the ROC curve analysis results for SHAPE gradient, V, and VDR for pCR and RR predictions using a single parameter. The SHAPE gradient achieved the highest AUC at 30% NAC completion for both predictions. Both V and VDR resulted in below 0.75 AUCs for pCR and RR at all study time points. Table 4 presents the ROC curve analysis results for combining SHAPE gradients with V or VDR. Combining V with SHAPE gradients, AUC increased from 0.75 to 0.83 at 10% for pCR and from 0.76 to 0.85 for RR. The combination of SHAPE gradient and VDR improved AUCs from 0.80 and 0.91 to 0.87 and 0.98 at 10% and 30% NAC completion for pCR, respectively. For RR prediction, combining VDR with the SHAPE gradient did not improve AUCs. For all comparison results, no significance was found (p > 0.05). Discussion

The use of NAC has increased over the years due to its benefits in increasing the breast-conserving rate and overall survival rate, despite potential toxicity and delays in better treatment. Thus, it is important to find methods to monitor and predict the NAC response effectively and early. In this study, we demonstrated that SHAPE gradient can differentiate pCR from non-pCR based on intratumoral IFP changes as early as 30% of NAC completion. Furthermore, incorporating tumor volume parameters into the SHAPE gradient improved predictive performance.

Finding an effective, imaging-based monitoring and predictive marker of the treatment response is challenging. In 1994, Nathanson’s group showed that IFP of human breast carcinoma was significantly elevated compared to the normal tissue [15]. Numerous studies have indicated that IFP can serve as an important indicator of chemotherapy response and tumor malignancy [12,13,29-32]. IFP shows significant correlations with various factors, including tumor size, blood vessel formation, and cell proliferation [12,31,33]. However, measuring IFP directly within a tumor is not preferred due to the invasiveness of techniques, which can cause tissue trauma [14,16].

Advanced imaging techniques such as MRI can measure IFP noninvasively. However, evaluating intratumoral IFP remains in challenging due to the affordability and complexity issues using the technologies [14]. Hence, we introduced our method using CEUS with the SHAPE, an affordable, noninvasive, and simple alternative for intratumoral pressure assessment [34]. In this study, the SHAPE gradient, directly related to the intratumoral pressure changes, showed significant differences between pCR and non-pCR as well as between RR and non-RR with reasonable performance for both pCR and RR predictions unlike tumor volume/volume change.

Our previous study demonstrated high potential of using the SHAPE gradient in predicting NAC response. Increasing the sample size and enhancing data quality using an advanced scanning system with improved imaging resolution and signal-to-noise ratio could make pCR prediction possible using the SHAPE gradient in this study. Moreover, adding tumor volume-associated parameters to the SHAPE gradients improved the accuracy of NAC response prediction. It has been proven in other prediction studies that combining various factors, such as clinical, pathological, or radiological parameters, can increase prediction performance compared to single-factor-based prediction accuracy [35,36]. Janssen et al. [35] reported that combining clinical-radiological information could improve pCR prediction: from an AUC of 0.76 for the clinical-only model to 0.84 with the addition of computer-extracted MRI features and 0.85 with the addition of liquid biopsy information [35].

In this study, we also examined the use of the SHAPE gradient in different breast cancer subtypes and found some interesting aspects. The SHAPE gradient overall indicated that prospective responders have lower intratumoral pressure compared to nonresponders, regardless of subtype. At each CEUS exam, the SHAPE gradients for the HR+ subtype followed the overall trend and showed a significant difference between pCRs and non-pCRs (p < 0.05), but not in the TNBC (see Table 2). Moreover, we could observe a correlation between tumor pressure and the aggressiveness of tumor, as Nathanson and Nelson stated. 15 The SHAPE gradients before starting NAC were as low as a mean of −0.25 for TNBC and the value of −2.42 for HER2-enriched. For the HR+ subtype, the mean SHAPE gradient was 1.31, which may indicate lower pressure and less aggressiveness compared to the other subtypes. However, after 30% NAC completion, the mean SHAPE gradients of the TNBC and HER2-enriched increased to 0.07 and 1.51, respectively, while the HR+ subtype decreased to 0.47. It could be interpreted as aggressive subtypes showing a sign of decreasing pressure inside tumor as therapy progresses. The HR+ is the most common subtype of breast cancer, but it shows a lower NAC efficacy compared to the other aggressive subtypes. This may be due to the NAC strategy focusing on aggressive cancer types, such as HER2-enriched or TNBC [28]. Since we have a limited number of patients in each subtype and considering unknown therapeutic effects for different subtypes, no solid conclusion can be drawn in this study. However, these findings may support the potential use of the SHAPE gradient for assessing tumor aggressiveness and developing subtype-specific prediction methods.

There are limitations in this study, indicating a need for further investigation. Firstly, we need more data to build a SHAPE gradient-based prediction marker with improved accuracy. Future increases in the number of subjects can improve prediction accuracy, as demonstrated. More advanced and standardized data processing methods will be explored. Accounting for the US tissue signals within the UCA subharmonic frequency range required us to collect matched SHAPE datasets with and without UCA, which was challenging in hard-to-scan tumor locations and in cases of unexpected patient motion. The SHAPE software will be modified to address this issue in our future studies. An efficient method for selecting ROI location and size in images (currently performed manually) with an objective evaluation of the intra- and inter-observer agreement will be investigated and developed to facilitate the use of the SHAPE gradient as a NAC response predictor. Finally, we will consider expanding the study to include a multidimensional analysis by combining clinical parameters associated with NAC response, such as molecular subtypes, tumors, and patient characteristics. Since these factors play a significant role in predicting the response to NAC, combining these parameters with the SHAPE gradient may enhance prediction in future studies. Moreover, future analysis will include site-based data quality assessment and validation to evaluate the transportability of the data and method.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that SHAPE gradients based on intratumoral IFP using noninvasive and cost-effective 3D CEUS could predict NAC response early. The SHAPE gradient can be an independent predictor and a combinable factor for predicting and monitoring NAC response.
