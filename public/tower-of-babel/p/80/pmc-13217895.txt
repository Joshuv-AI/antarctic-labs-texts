Paeoniflorin: a natural candidate for the treatment of metabolic diseases
Authors: Minghao Ji, Haoyang Gao, Xiaotong Ma, Wei Dai, Jiabin Wu, Weihua Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global prevalence of metabolic diseases (MetDs) is rising annually and poses a major challenge to public health. Paeoniflorin(PF), a naturally occurring phytochemical, considerably improves insulin sensitivity, glucose and lipid metabolism, bile acid metabolism, inflammation and oxidative stress. This article comprehensively reviews recent research on the role of PF in the treatment of six common MetDs: diabetes mellitus and its complications, non-alcoholic fatty liver, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, atherosclerosis, cholestasis, and polycystic ovary syndrome, along with its potential molecular mechanisms. In addition, we summarize the pharmacological and toxicological properties of PF and propose strategies to enhance its bioavailability, opening new avenues for its clinical application. Overall, this study demonstrates that PF exhibits therapeutic potential against various MetDs and may serve as a promising candidate for the treatment of metabolic disorders.

Introduction

Metabolic diseases (MetDs) refer to class of disorders resulting from disturbances in metabolic homeostasis. Common examples include diabetes mellitus (DM), obesity, non-alcoholic fatty liver (NAFL), and gout. In addition, MetDs encompass not only primary metabolic disorders, in which metabolic dysregulation, characterized by insulin resistance(IR), chronic low-grade inflammation and lipotoxicity, serves as the principal pathogenic driver, but also secondary metabolism-related disorders, in which metabolic imbalance critically contributes to disease initiation and progression. The incidence of these diseases continues to rise, affecting individuals across all age groups and imposing a substantial global public health burden [1, 2]. Although numerous medications, such as biguanides, statins, thiazolidinediones, and bile acid(BA)-based drugs, have shown efficacy in treating MetDs, their long-term clinical application is limited by adverse effects such as myopathy, hepatic dysfunction, increased risk of fractures and cardiovascular events, and gastrointestinal disturbances and contraindications [3–6]. Moreover, the chronic and costly nature of treatment further exacerbates patient burden. These limitations highlight the need for safer, multi-target therapeutic agents capable of modulating multiple interconnected metabolic pathways.

The Paeoniaceae family consists of a single genus, Paeonia, which encompasses approximately 35 species that can be divided into herbaceous peonies and woody tree peonies (Paeonia suffruticosa). These plants have long been valued for ornamental and medicinal purposes, with a documented history of therapeutic use exceeding 1,000 years [7]. Modern studies have validated their diverse biological activities, including anti-cancer [8], antidepressant [9], and microcirculation-promoting effects [10].

Total glucosides of peony(TGP)include ethanol-water extracts derived from various parts of Paeoniaceae plants, with the most abundance observed in the roots [11]. More than 15 monoterpene glycosides, including paeoniflorin(PF), albiflorin, oxypaeoniflorin, benzoylpaeoniflorin, and benzoyloxypaeoniflorin, have been identified in TGP. PF is the predominant component, comprising over 34.6% of the total extract, and is considered the principal bioactive constituent [12], First isolated and named by Shibata et al. in 1963 from white-flowered peony [13], PF (chemical formula: C₂₃H₂₈O₁₁; molecular weight: 480.46) is a water-soluble monoterpene with high hydrophilicity and poor lipophilicity, which limit its transmembrane absorption. Its core structure is a pinane derivative [14, 15].Extensive research has confirmed PF’s pharmacological effects, including anti-inflammatory [16], antioxidant [17], antitumor [18], immunomodulatory [19] and neuroprotective [20] properties. These findings suggest that PF exerts considerable therapeutic benefits in the context of MetDs. Previous reviews have primarily focused on the pharmacological effects of PF within individual disease contexts—such as liver diseases, cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, or oxidative stress–related conditions—often emphasizing descriptive summaries of signaling pathways or protective effects on single organs. However, no existing review has systematically integrated PF’s actions across multiple MetDs within a unified framework, nor has PF been comprehensively evaluated from the perspective of MetDs convergence, in which IR, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated lipid or BA metabolism serve as common upstream pathogenic drivers. Consequently, the broader significance of PF as a systemic metabolic modulator, rather than a treatment limited to isolated disease entities, remains insufficiently recognized. In addition, recent advances in the field have substantially expanded our understanding of PF’s molecular targets.

In this review, we summarize the therapeutic mechanisms of PF in improving metabolic dysfunction across a spectrum of MetDs, encompassing primary MetDs and secondary metabolism-related disorders. Accordingly, we conducted a title-based literature search using “paeoniflorin” as the keyword and further screened studies focusing on PF-mediated interventions in MetDs. Based on this strategy, we reviewed and integrated current evidence regarding the therapeutic effects of PF on DM, NAFL, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis(NASH), atherosclerosis(AS), cholestasis(CL), and polycystic ovary syndrome(PCOS). Protective mechanisms of PF against DM and its complications

Protective effects and underlying mechanisms of PF in diabetes management

DM is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by elevated blood glucose levels, and it poses an important global public health challenge. As of 2024, DM is affecting approximately 589 million people worldwide, with projections indicating that this number may rise to 853 million by 2050 [21]. DM is classified into type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and other specific types. T1DM is an autoimmune disease marked by immune-mediated pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and apoptosis, and it typically results in severe endogenous insulin deficiency. By contrast, T2DM is a non-autoimmune condition characterized by IR accompanied with insufficient insulin secretion by pancreatic β-cells [22]. T2DM accounts for more than 90% of all DM cases, which makes it the predominant form of the disease [23]. Over time, chronic hyperglycemia can lead to notable damage to multiple organ systems—including the heart, vasculature, kidneys, feet, nerves, eyes, and liver—resulting in various complications [24]. Importantly, the pathogenesis of DM involves a complex interplay of IR, chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and gut microbiota imbalance. Given this multifactorial nature of DM, therapeutic strategies capable of targeting multiple pathogenic processes are considered advantageous.PF has emerged as a promising multi-target agent in diabetes management.PF enhances insulin sensitivity and promotes glycogen synthesis, while simultaneously exerting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. In addition, PF modulates gut microbiota composition and contributes to immune homeostasis, thereby protecting pancreatic β-cell function and mitigating metabolic dysfunction. Collectively, these actions enable PF to effectively delay the onset and progression of DM.

Insulin resistance

IR represents a central pathological mechanism in the development of T2DM, and improvement of insulin sensitivity can effectively slow disease progression [25]. Elevated levels of free fatty acids (FFAs), such as palmitic acid (PA), impair insulin signaling and trigger IR [26]. In PA-induced HepG2 hepatocytes, PF pretreatment downregulated Rho-associated kinase (ROCK), which resulted in reduced inhibitory serine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) and restored downstream phosphoinositide 3-kinase(PI3K)/Akt signaling [27]. Consistent with improved insulin signaling, PF partially reversed the PA and TNF-α induced reduction in 2-deoxy-D-glucose uptake [27]. Similar molecular changes have also been observed in PF-treated 3T3-L1 adipocytes [28]. After these upstream events, PF further modulated key intermediate regulators of glucose storage. Glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β), a serine/threonine kinase, inhibits glycogen synthase via phosphorylation; insulin normally suppresses GSK3β activity through PI3K/Akt dependent phosphorylation [29]. Accordingly, by restoring Akt activity, PF promotes glycogen synthesis and improves insulin sensitivity in a dose-dependent manner [30]. Notably, PF may also ameliorate maternal fetal glucose metabolism disorders and IR in GDM, potentially through the inhibition of Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway activation [31].

Inflammation and oxidative stress

Chronic low-grade inflammation is closely associated with the pathogenesis of T2DM [32]. Human studies have confirmed that targeting islet inflammation holds promise as a therapeutic strategy for DM [33]. Macrophages promote lipolysis in adipocytes via tumor necrosis factor(TNF-α) secretion, which leads to increased FFA release. These FFAs, in turn, activate macrophages and enhance TNF-α expression, creates a self-reinforcing inflammatory feedback loop [34]. PF inhibits activation of the extracellular signal regulated kinase 1/2(ERK1/2), maintains the expression of the lipid droplet-associated protein perilipin, and reduces FFA release. Furthermore, PF modulates the IκB kinase(IKK)/Nuclear factor-kappa B(NF-κB) signaling pathway, partially suppressing PA-induced TNF-α secretion from macrophages. These actions collectively disrupt the pro-inflammatory feedback loop [27]. This inflammation-disrupting effect was further confirmed in subsequent studies [28]. Moreover, PF intervention increased the abundance of beneficial gut microbiota such as Sutterella, enhanced intestinal barrier function, reduced gut permeability, and decreased the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which contribute to the amelioration of T1DM. PF also promoted immune homeostasis by increasing the proportion of regulatory T cells while suppressing pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 cell responses, potentially through inhibition of the Toll-like receptor 4(TLR4)–MyD88/TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β(TRIF) signaling pathway [35]. Oxidative stress is another critical factor in T2DM, and it results from an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body’s antioxidant defense mechanisms. This imbalance contributes to IR, β-cell dysfunction, and complications in the vascular and nervous systems [36]. PF may attenuate oxidative stress by suppressing the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase(MAPK) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase(JNK) signaling pathways, reducing ROS and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels while enhancing the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) [37] (Fig. 2) (Table 1).

Protective mechanisms of PF against diabetic cardiomyopathy(DCM)

DCM refers to structural and functional abnormalities of the myocardium caused by metabolic disturbances associated with DM; it occurs in the absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. The precise pathogenesis of DCM remains incompletely understood [38]. PF exerts cardioprotective effects on DCM primarily through the regulation of ferroptosis and modulation of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP).

Ferroptosis is a newly identified form of regulated cell death that is dependent on iron and ROS [39]. Recent studies have demonstrated a close association between ferroptosis and DCM [40]. Glutathione peroxidase 4(GPX4), a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, detoxifies lipid peroxides and maintains redox homeostasis, thereby mitigating ferroptotic cell death [41]. In DCM mouse models, PF administration activated the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling pathway, which led to the upregulation of downstream GPX4 expression and a concomitant reduction in iron and MDA accumulation.11,12-Epoxyeicosatrienoic acid (11,12-EET), a key microbial metabolite, may plays a mediating role in PF-induced activation of the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway [42]. In addition, DCM and myocardial infarction(MI) have a bidirectional pathological relationship. DCM exacerbates post-MI heart failure by impairing the compensatory capacity of non-infarcted myocardium, whereas MI can accelerate DCM progression and increase mortality [43].CGRP is a multifunctional peptide with positive inotropic and vasodilatory effects, and it contributes to improved cardiac function following ischemia [44]. Transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1), a non-selective cation channel expressed in sensory neurons, induces calcium influx upon activation, which subsequently activates calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) [45]. cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), a downstream target of CaMKII, mediates CGRP expression. PF treatment considerably upregulated TRPV1 expression and CREB phosphorylation in the myocardium. Notably, the cardioprotective effects of PF were abolished in TRPV1 knockout mice or in the presence of CaMKII inhibitors, which suggests that PF may promotes CGRP release through the TRPV1/CaMKII/CREB signaling cascade to ameliorate MI-related injury [46].

Protective mechanisms of PF against diabetic angiopathy(DA)

DA serves as a major contributor to the high morbidity and mortality associated with diabetes, manifesting in vascular complications such as cardiovascular disease(CVD), diabetic retinopathy(DR), and nephropathy [47].PF mitigates vascular injury in diabetes by modulating oxidative stress and apoptosis.

Network pharmacology analysis identified a high binding affinity between PF and Harvey rat sarcoma.

viral oncogene homolog (HRAS), a member of the RAS family. HRAS, when bound to GTP to form the active RAS-GTP complex, promotes cellular apoptosis [48]. In high-glucose(HG)-induced human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), PF pretreatment significantly increased cell viability and reduced apoptosis. This finding was accompanied with dose-dependent decreases in HRAS and RAS-GTP expression. Conversely, HRAS overexpression reversed the protective effects of PF, which indicates that PF may alleviate HG-induced endothelial injury by suppressing HRAS-mediated RAS signaling and apoptosis [49]. In another study, glycemic variability (GV) was simulated by alternating HUVEC culture between normal and HG conditions with concurrent PF intervention. Following PF intervention, improvements in cellular morphology and viability were observed, accompanied with a reduction in intracellular ROS levels and an increase in glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity, which indicate that PF alleviated GV–induced endothelial injury and oxidative stress [50].

Protective mechanisms of PF in diabetic nephropathy(DN)

DN accounts for approximately 44.5% of end-stage renal disease cases in developed countries. This condition often requires dialysis or kidney transplantation, significantly increases the risk of CVD, and thereby poses a substantial public health burden [51]. The renoprotective effects of PF on diabetic kidney disease primarily involve the modulation of podocyte apoptosis, autophagy, and inflammatory responses.

Necroptosis, a regulated form of cell death, is initiated by pattern recognition receptors or cytokines and mediated by receptor-interacting protein kinases 1 and 3 (RIPK1 and RIPK3), with phosphorylated mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (p-MLKL) acting as the terminal executor. This process leads to plasma membrane permeabilization and rupture [52]. Computational target prediction and molecular docking studies have shown that PF may directly bind to and promote the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of TNF receptor 1 in podocytes, which inhibits the RIPK1/RIPK3 pathway and MLKL phosphorylation, This condition effectively suppresses podocyte necroptosis [53]. Autophagy, a highly conserved lysosomal degradation process, may exert either protective or detrimental effects depending on the context. PF can restore autophagic activity in podocytes and inhibit HG-induced apoptosis by suppressing the endothelial growth factor_receptor 2/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [54]. In addition, PF pretreatment alleviates glomerular mesangial cell dysfunction by partially inhibiting the excessive autophagy triggered by advanced glycation end products (AGEs), via suppression of the receptor of AGE(RAGE)/mTOR signaling pathway [55]. Inflammation is a hallmark pathological feature of DN, with macrophage infiltration occurring early in disease progression. Macrophages can polarize into two distinct phenotypes: pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, which promote ROS generation and nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, which support tissue repair and inhibit fibrosis [56]. Ji et al. [57] were the first to report that PF suppresses the expression of transforming growth factor(TGF-β) and ICAM1, inhibits NF-κB activation, and attenuates macrophage infiltration. Macrophage infiltration and activation show close associations with TLRs [58]. Subsequent studies revealed that PF may suppress macrophage M1 polarization and inflammatory cytokine release by inhibiting the TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway [59, 60] Moreover, PF modulates the MyD88-dependent TLR4 pathway, which reduces M1 macrophage polarization and associated pro-inflammatory responses [61]. Various natural compounds can ameliorate the pathological processes of DN by modulating the TLR4 signaling pathway [62]. Although the MyD88-independent TLR4 pathway may also contribute to PF-mediated anti-inflammatory effects in DN, further research is needed to clarify its role [63]. In addition, PF may prevent M1 polarization and inflammatory infiltration by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling cascade [64]. Collectively, preclinical evidence suggests that PF confers renoprotective effects in DN through coordinated regulation of cell death pathways, autophagy, and inflammation.

Protective mechanisms of PF in diabetic foot ulcers(DFUs)

DFUs are chronic, refractory, and highly recurrent complications of diabetes, with a lifetime prevalence as high as 25%. These ulcers are characterized by impaired progression through the normal wound-healing phases—hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodeling—which lead to delayed healing, accompanied with reduced neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytic activity [65]. Therefore, suppressing local inflammation and oxidative stress may represent effective strategies to accelerate wound healing in DFU patients.

The NOD, LRR, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, a cytosolic multiprotein complex composed of the NOD-like receptor NLRP3, adaptor protein ASC and pro-caspase-1, plays a critical role in diabetic wound healing [66]. PF potentially inhibits inflammasome activation by downregulating CXC chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) expression, which reduce NF-κB transcriptional activity and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [67]. Nrf2 serves as a key regulator of oxidative stress responses. Normally sequestered by Keap1 in the cytoplasm, Nrf2 is released under oxidative stress and translocates to the nucleus, where it induces the expression of antioxidant genes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), this condition contributes to its anti-inflammatory, antioxidative and anti-apoptotic effects [68]. PF activates the Nrf2 pathway, which results in the upregulated expressions of HO-1, VEGF and TGF-β1.This phenomenon reduces oxidative stress, promotes cell proliferation and accelerates wound healing [69]. Furthermore, PF modulates immune responses by suppressing signal transducer and activator of transcription 1(STAT1) expression while enhancing STAT6 expression, thereby promoting macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 phenotype.Building on these findings, PF-loaded hyaluronic acid hydrogel(HA-PF) was developed to enable sustained PF release. The HA-PF hydrogel significantly accelerated wound closure in diabetic mice, enhanced angiogenesis and increased collagen deposition [70]. Collectively, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of PF in the treatment of DFUs.

Protective mechanisms of PF in diabetic cognitive dysfunction(DCD)

DCD, also referred to as diabetic encephalopathy, is a neurophysiological and anatomical remodeling of the brain induced by DM [71]. Regulation of glucose metabolism is a primary strategy for alleviating DCD. In addition, synaptic injury driven by inflammation and oxidative stress represents a key pathological basis of this condition [72, 73].

In terms of glucose metabolism, intraperitoneal(IP) administration of PF enhances the phosphorylation of PI3K and Akt, while reducing aberrant phosphorylation of IRS-1, which indicates that PF may restore impaired insulin signaling in the cortex and hippocampus of streptozotocin (STZ)-injected mouse models via the PI3K/Akt pathway [74]. Tau protein, the most abundant microtubule-associated protein in neurons, becomes hyperphosphorylated under hyperglycemic conditions, which contributes to hippocampal neurodegeneration [75]. PF may reduce the suppressor of cytokine signaling 2 (SOCS2) expression.Such as outcome restores IRS-1 activity and enhances the Akt/GSK-3β signaling pathway, which facilitates Tau dephosphorylation [76].Regarding inflammation and oxidative stress, PF intervention reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress markers, while increasing levels of GSH, SOD and cytochrome c oxidase activity. These effects help mitigate mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation [74, 76]. Functionally, PF substantially improves recognition index and escape latency in behavioral tests, which further confirm its neuroprotective effects on DCD.

Protective mechanisms of PF in diabetic ocular complications

Diabetic eye disease (DED) refers to a spectrum of ocular complications caused by diabetes, including DR, diabetic macular edema, diabetic keratopathy, diabetic cataract and glaucoma. Although these conditions affect different parts of the eye, they all originate from hyperglycemia-induced vascular damage. DEDs often coexist or occur sequentially in the same patient and represent leading causes of visual impairment and blindness [77].

DR is characterized by ischemia, abnormal angiogenesis and breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB) [78]. Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system(CNS), release inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinase-9(MMP-9), which contribute to BRB disruption [79]. Administration of PF potentially alleviates BRB disruption by upregulating the expression of suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3), thereby inhibiting the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway. This condition leads to the reduced expression of MMP-9 and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β(IL-1β). However, the downstream regulatory mechanisms of SOCS3 in this context remain to be fully elucidated [80]. Targeting sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) can ameliorate lens epithelial cell proliferation and apoptosis, which alleviates DC [81]. PF can considerably inhibit the epithelial-mesenchymal transition(EMT), oxidative damage and apoptosis of human lens epithelial cells induced by HG through upregulation of the expression of SIRT1.These protective effects are evidenced by increased levels of E-cadherin, Bcl-2,antioxidant enzymes and decreased levels of N-cadherin and pro-apoptotic proteins [82]. These findings suggest that PF may serve as a promising candidate for the treatment of DED.

Protective mechanisms of PF in other diabetic complications

Beyond the aforementioned complications, PF shows therapeutic potential in the alleviation of diabetic neuropathy through the reduction oxidative stress and apoptosis. Mechanistically, PF activates the Keap1/Nrf2/antioxidant response element signaling pathway, which leads to upregulated antioxidant enzymes, decreased MDA and ROS levels, and enhanced anti-apoptotic to pro-apoptotic gene expression ratio [83]. Thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP), a key regulator of intracellular redox signaling, plays a pivotal role in the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Molecular docking and cellular thermal shift assay experiments demonstrated that PF can directly bind to TXNIP, thereby suppressing the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome-related proteins in the liver of db/db mice. This condition leads to reduced inflammatory cytokine levels and ameliorates diabetic liver injury [84].

In summary, PF has demonstrated protective effects against a wide range of diabetic complications, including DCM, diabetic vascular disease, DN, DFU, DCD, and diabetic ocular complications. Its mechanisms of action involve inhibition of ferroptosis and necroptosis, enhancement of myocardial contractility, attenuation of oxidative stress and inflammation, regulation of autophagy, improvement of hippocampal function and maintenance of BRB integrity (Fig. 3; Table 1).

Protective effects and mechanisms of PF in NAFL

The metabolic abnormalities underlying diabetic complications also drive the initiation and progression of NAFLD.NAFLD is characterized by ectopic lipid accumulation in the liver in the absence of excessive alcohol consumption or other identifiable causes, which ultimately leads to metabolic dysfunction [85]. The nomenclature of NAFLD is gradually being updated, with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) emerging as a more precise term. Marked by hepatic steatosis, NAFL represents the early stage of NAFLD. Several pathological drivers, including IR, abnormalities in lipid and BA metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress, play critical roles in disease progression [86]. Certain natural compounds, such as ginsenoside Rg3, exert multi-target regulatory effects on the prevention and treatment of NAFLD [87]. Similarly, PF can promote fat oxidation, inhibit fat synthesis, and reduce the accumulation of triglycerides(TGs) and cholesterol, while also enhancing insulin signaling and improving hepatic glucose metabolism. In addition, PF exhibits antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, which collectively delay the onset and progression of NAFL.

Hepatic lipid metabolism

Lipid metabolism is essential for hormone biosynthesis, energy production, and fat storage. When lipid utilization is impaired and lipogenesis is upregulated, lipid accumulation occurs, and it contributes to chronic lipid-related diseases, such as NAFLD [88]. In a rat model of fructose-induced hepatic steatosis, PF was found to activate the liver kinase B1/AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling pathway, which inhibited lipogenesis [89]. In addition to these targets, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), a family of ligand-activated transcription factors, play vital roles in lipid metabolism [90]. In PA-stimulated HepG2 cells, PF inhibited the expression of PPARγ in a dose-dependent manner, which alleviated TG accumulation [30]. PF also attenuated TNF-α-induced PPARγ expression and downregulated the mRNA expression of its downstream targets, adipocyte protein2 (aP2) and adiponectin [28]. Moreover, PF inhibited PPARα expression while upregulating the expression of downstream genes such as CPT-1α and acyl-CoA oxidase (ACOX), contributing to improved lipid metabolism [91]. The liver is also a central organ for cholesterol metabolism. Hepatic cholesterol dysregulation not only contributes to NAFLD but also promotes atherosclerotic dyslipidemia [88]. PF potentially reduces the expression of HMGCR and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor, thereby inhibiting cholesterol synthesis, and may suppress cholesterol efflux through the inhibition of the PPARγ/liver X receptor alpha (LXRα)/ATP-binding cassette protein A1 (ABCA1) pathway [92, 93].

Hepatic glucose metabolism

High-fat and high-sugar diets disrupt glucose metabolism and thus promotes the onset of NAFL. In a 24-week feeding study using a high-fat diet(HFD) supplemented with 0.05% PF, the homeostatic model assessment IR index was reduced to 27.3% of that observed in the HFD only group. Moreover, the mRNA levels of key gluconeogenic and glycolytic enzymes—phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase)—were decreased [93]. Oral administration of PF for four weeks significantly reduced hepatic injury markers, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and restored hepatic glycogen content. As previously discussed, the IRS/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is central to insulin sensitivity regulation. In HFD fed rats, this pathway was disrupted, evidenced by the increased serine phosphorylation of IRS-1 and decreased Akt phosphorylation. PF treatment reversed these alterations, enhancing insulin signaling and glucose metabolism and thereby ameliorating NAFL [92].

Hepatic inflammation and oxidative stress

Oxidative stress plays a critical role in the onset and progression of NAFLD [94]. Four weeks of continuous oral administration of PF improved the balance between pro-oxidant and antioxidant factors [95]. Specifically, cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) is a key enzyme involved in ROS generation and the mediation of oxidative stress in NAFLD [96]. Continuous PF administration markedly inhibited hepatic CYP2E1 expression in mice, which resulted in the reduced production of ROS and antioxidative and hepatoprotective effects [92].Moreover, PF downregulated the expressions of inflammation-related genes, which improved the hepatic cellular microenvironment in high-cholesterol diet-fed rats and exerted notable anti-inflammatory effects [95].

In summary, PF can regulate lipid metabolism by inhibiting lipogenesis and improving fatty acid oxidation, enhance hepatic insulin signaling, reduce gluconeogenesis, restore glycogen storage, and improve overall glucose metabolism. In parallel, PF exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, thereby improving the hepatic microenvironment. Collectively, these multi-target actions highlight the potential of PF to delay the onset and progression of NAFL (Fig. 4; Table 1).

Protective mechanisms of PF in NASH

NASH represents a progressive stage of NAFLD; it is pathologically characterized by hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, which may ultimately lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Approximately 41% of NASH patients exhibit significant fibrosis, and the extent of hepatic fibrosis is a primary predictor of mortality in this population [85, 97].Fibrosis itself is a wound-healing response to liver injury, driven by the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), which results in the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins. Quiescent HSCs normally store vitamin A and maintain metabolic homeostasis, but upon activation, they transdifferentiate into myofibroblast-like cells, lose their vitamin A content, upregulate α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and produce pro-inflammatory mediators and collagen, which all contribute to hepatic scar formation [98]. PF can inhibit HSC activation through epigenetic mechanisms and modulation of the TGFβ-Smad signaling pathway, which attenuates the progression of liver fibrosis. In addition, PF alleviates inflammation and oxidative stress, which further enhances its antifibrotic effects and contributes to a comprehensive improvement in the pathological process of NASH.

Inhibition of HSC activation

Epigenetics refers to heritable changes in gene expression that exclude alterations in the DNA sequence, including mechanisms such as DNA methylation, histone modification, and non-coding RNA regulation [99]. Among these, inhibitors of enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2),a histone methyltransferase, shows promise in anti-fibrotic therapies. PF exhibited similar regulatory effects on histone methylation. Wang et al. [100], using molecular docking and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) techniques, demonstrated that PF directly bound to EZH2 and inhibited its methyltransferase activity, which reduced H3K27me3 enrichment at the PPARγ promoter region and restored PPARγ expression. PPARγ participates in the suppression of TGF-β/Smad signaling in HSCs, thereby downregulating fibrotic gene expression and exerting anti-fibrotic effects [101]. Thus, PF may inhibit CCl₄-induced HSC activation and slow down fibrosis progression via an epigenetic mechanism.

TGF-β1 is widely recognized as a central mediator of tissue fibrosis. It activates the downstream Smad signaling cascade, which promotes HSC activation and consequent ECM accumulation and scarring [102]. Smad2, Smad3, and Smad4 are major positive effectors in this pathway, whereas Smad7 functions as a negative feedback regulator that inhibits TGF-β1/Smad signaling and thereby suppresses fibrogenesis. PF inhibits the TGF-β1 signaling pathway by reducing the activation of Smad2/3/4 while upregulating Smad7 expression. PF also substantially reduces serum markers of liver injury and hepatic expressions of collagen type I alpha 1 chain mRNA and protein. Notably, similar protective effects have been observed in radiation-induced fibrosis models, which suggests that PF offers protection against chemical and radiation-induced liver injury. These findings indicate that the TGF-β1/Smad pathway is a key target through which PF mitigates hepatic fibrosis. However, the precise molecular mechanism by which PF regulates Smad7 expression remains to be elucidated [103, 104].

Inflammation and oxidative stress

The progression of hepatic fibrosis is often accompanied with dynamic changes in hepatic macrophage polarization between the pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes. Modulation of macrophage polarization has thus emerged as a critical strategy for the management of NASH-associated fibrosis [105]. PF inhibits aberrant macrophage activation and regulates their distribution within organs, significantly attenuating systemic inflammatory responses [106]. At the molecular level, PF may downregulates the activity of ROCK and inhibits the activation of its downstream NF-κB signaling pathway. This condition includes suppression of phosphorylation levels of MYPT1, IKKβ, IκBα,and NF-κB p65, which collectively reduce hepatic infiltration of CD68⁺ macrophages and the expression of the pro-fibrotic cytokine TGF-β1 [107]. Hypoxia-inducible factor-1α(HIF-1α) also plays a crucial role in regulating the M1/M2 polarization balance of macrophages [108, 109]. PF reduces HIF-1α expression via the inhibition of the NF-κB pathway, thereby suppressing M1 polarization while promoting M2 polarization. This outcome ultimately contributes to the resolution of hepatic inflammation [110]. Moreover, PF inhibits the formation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and enhances antioxidant enzyme activity, which alleviate oxidative stress and inflammation in liver tissue [111]. Collectively, these findings suggest that PF exerts potent anti-fibrotic effects through multi-pathway modulation of macrophage polarization, suppression of inflammatory responses, and attenuation of oxidative stress, which underscore its strong therapeutic potential for the treatment of NASH-related liver fibrosis.

In summary, PF suppresses fibrogenic responses by modulating epigenetic regulation and fibrotic signaling pathways, while concurrently regulating macrophage polarization to reduce pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signals. In parallel, PF enhances antioxidant defenses and inhibits inflammasome activation, which results in improved hepatic inflammatory and redox microenvironment. Collectively, these multi-target actions underscore the potential of PF to delay NASH progression and attenuate fibrosis development (Fig. 5; Table 1).

Protective effects and mechanisms of PF in AS

Metabolic disorders not only promote hepatic injury and fibrosis in NASH but also accelerate endothelial dysfunction and plaque formation in the vasculature.AS has long been classified as a CVD; however, extensive evidence indicates that its initiation and progression are fundamentally driven by metabolic dysregulation. Disturbances in lipid metabolism, IR, and chronic low-grade inflammation collectively promote endothelial dysfunction, foam cell formation, and plaque development. Therefore, AS can be reasonably conceptualized as a metabolism-driven vascular disease and included within the spectrum of secondary metabolic disorders [112–114]. Vascular endothelial cells (VECs) and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) are essential components of the vascular wall, Foam cells constitute a critical cellular component of atherosclerotic plaques. PF has demonstrated cardiovascular protective effects by improving VEC and VSMC dysfunction and by inhibiting foam cell formation, thereby alleviating the progression of AS and related CVD [115, 116].

Inhibition of VSMC proliferation

During the progression of AS, VSMCs undergo phenotypic switching to a synthetic, proliferative state, contributing to ECM production and the formation of the fibrous cap that stabilizes plaques [115]. PF exerts multifaceted protective effects in this context. It enhances the phosphorylation of IκBα and p65,which reduces HG-induced abnormal VSMC proliferation while promoting apoptosis [117]. PF also inhibits cell cycle progression, proliferation, and inflammation in VSMCs, potentially through the suppression of p38 MAPK and ERK1/2 phosphorylation and modulation of the NF-κB pathway. Furthermore, PF upregulates HO-1 and downregulates the proliferation of cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), which reinforces its anti-proliferative effects [118]. In addition, PF considerably attenuates the release of inflammatory cytokines through the_inhibition of the TLR4-MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, which improves the VSMC microenvironment [119].

Protection of VEC function

Endothelial cells (ECs), as integral components of the cardiac and vascular systems, play critical roles in angiogenesis and the regulation of vascular tone. Endothelial injury and overactivation are key pathogenic events in AS, they initiate a cascade of inflammatory responses within the arterial wall [68]. PF may enhances autophagy by upregulating SIRT1 expression, thereby attenuating apoptosis and adhesion molecule expression in HUVECs [120]. PF pretreatment also reduces inflammatory cytokine production in HUVECs, potentially by downregulating the expression and release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and its receptors—RAGE, TLR2, and TLR4—and by suppressing NF-κB pathway activation. These findings suggest that the HMGB1-RAGE/TLR2/TLR4-NF-κB axis may represent a critical target through which PF mitigates AS-related endothelial dysfunction [121]. In human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAECs), PF similarly exhibits anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects. In addition, PF suppresses β-catenin and downstream proliferative genes such as c-Myc and cyclin D1 in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. The combined administration of PF and the Wnt inhibitor XAV939 demonstrated enhanced effects, which indicates that PF may exert endothelial protection through inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling [122]. These findings indicate that PF exerts anti-atherosclerotic effects by protecting endothelial cells.

Inhibition of foam cell formation

Foam cells, formed by macrophages engulfing excess cholesterol, are central to early atherogenesis [123]. Reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) is a critical physiological process that facilitates the efflux of excess cholesterol from peripheral tissues, including macrophages in the vascular wall, to the liver or intestines for excretion, thus preventing foam cell formation [124]. LXRα, a nuclear receptor, plays a pivotal role in regulating RCT-related gene expression [125]. In ApoE⁻/⁻ mice fed with HFD for 12 weeks with PF treatment, PF significantly upregulated the mRNA and protein levels of key RCT-related genes in the aorta, liver, and intestines. Serum levels of total cholesterol (TC), TG, and LDL-cholesterol were reduced and high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol and ApoA-I levels were increased. In RAW264.7 macrophages, PF pretreatment resulted in a marked decrease in foam cell formation and enhanced cholesterol efflux. Notably, the RCT-promoting effects of PF were abolished when LXRα was knocked out, which indicates that PF may regulates RCT via the LXRα/ABCA1/SR-BI signaling pathway. Such a condition promotes cholesterol efflux from aortic macrophages and thereby reduces foam cell burden and plaque formation [126]. Compared with traditional synthetic LXR agonists, such as T0901317, PF—acting as a natural and safer LXR modulator—does not induce side effects including hepatic steatosis, which highlights its potential as an ideal therapeutic candidate for AS.

In summary, PF suppresses abnormal VSMC proliferation and inflammatory activation, while protecting endothelial cell function by attenuating apoptosis, inflammation, and adhesion molecule expression, thereby maintaining vascular homeostasis. In addition, PF promotes RCT and enhances cholesterol efflux from macrophages, which result in inhibited foam cell formation and reduced lipid accumulation within the arterial wall. Collectively, these multi-target actions underscore the potential of PF to attenuate atherosclerotic plaque formation and progression (Fig. 6; Table 1).

Protective mechanisms of PF in CL

Cholestatic liver disease refers to a clinicopathological condition characterized by abnormal bile flow, accompanied with the accumulation of BAs in the intrahepatic and extrahepatic compartments [127]. Its etiological factors include metabolic dysregulation, mechanical obstruction, hepatitis, and drug exposure.Current understanding suggests that the pathogenesis of CL is a complex process involving excessive inflammatory responses, dysregulation of hepatocellular transporter systems, and oxidative stress–induced liver injury [128]. Therefore, from the perspectives of etiological factors and underlying mechanisms, CL may also be considered a metabolic disorder.Current understanding suggests that the pathogenesis of CL is multifactorial, involving hepatocyte apoptosis, dysregulation of BA transport proteins, excessive inflammation, and oxidative stress-induced liver injury [129]. PF alleviates the onset and progression of CL through multiple mechanisms. It not only reduces hepatocyte apoptosis and enhances the integrity of the biliary barrier, but also regulates BA transport. In addition, PF exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, which synergistically mitigate liver injury and effectively improve the cholestatic condition.

Inhibition of hepatocyte apoptosis

BA-induced hepatocyte apoptosis is considered a key mechanism in the progression of CL [130]. Under pathological conditions, the retention of hydrophobic BAs promotes liver injury by triggering hepatocyte apoptosis or necrosis [131]. The apoptosis induced by intrahepatic BAs is regarded as a central pathological event in CL. In vivo studies have demonstrated that oral PF administration downregulated pro-apoptotic proteins and upregulated anti-apoptotic proteins in a dose-dependent manner, which alleviated α-naphthylisothiocyanate(ANIT)-induced CL [132]. Further research revealed that PF may modulate hepatocyte apoptosis through direct interaction with JNK1/2.Network pharmacological analyses revealed that PF can form hydrogen bonds with amino acid residues of JNK1. In vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed elevated p-JNK/JNK ratios in CL models, which were significantly reduced by PF treatment. These findings suggest that PF may alleviates apoptosis by directly binding to and inhibiting JNK1 pathway activation. In addition, PF upregulates factors that contribute to the restoration of the biliary epithelial barrier, which indirectly alleviates hepatocyte apoptosis [133].

Promotion of BA

Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) acts as a primary sensor of BAs and is activated by primary BAs to regulate their synthesis, transport, secretion, and reabsorption through transcriptional mechanisms, thus maintaining BA homeostasis [134]. In a rat model of 17α-ethinylestradiol (EE)-induced CL, administration of PF dose-dependently upregulated the mRNA and protein expression of FXR and its downstream BA efflux transporters, bile salt export pump (BSEP) and multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 (MRP2).Simultaneously, PF downregulated the expression of the BA uptake transporter sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide(NTCP) and the BA synthesis enzyme cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase(CYP7A1), which relieved hepatic bile retention [135]. In ANIT-induced cholestatic rat models, PF was also found to modulate the expression of BA transporters; however, no inhibitory effect on the BA-synthesizing enzyme CYP7A1 was observed [136]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the modeling approach, as ANIT typically induces direct injury to the biliary epithelium, which results in hepatic damage. Therefore, PF probably regulates BA transport primarily through the modulation of inflammatory pathways in this context [137].

Attenuation of inflammation and oxidative stress

CL is commonly accompanied with the activation of inflammatory [138] and oxidative stress responses [139], both of which are not only pathological hallmarks but also crucial contributors to hepatic injury and disease progression.PF significantly inhibited the ANIT-induced upregulation of inflammatory markers in liver tissue [132, 136]. Regarding oxidative stress, ANIT-induced CL elevated hepatic levels of NO, MDA, and ROS, while reducing GSH levels, which indicates impaired antioxidant defenses and increased lipid peroxidation. PF treatment dose-dependently reversed these changes [140]. PF also promoted Akt phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of Nrf2, which suggests that PF may enhances GSH synthesis via activation of the PI3K/Akt/Nrf2 signaling pathway.Such a condition ameliorates ANIT-induced oxidative stress [141]. NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4), a member of the NOX enzyme family, acts as an oxygen sensor and mediates ROS production by catalyzing the electron transfer from NADPH to molecular oxygen, which results in the formation of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [140]. PF treatment was found to downregulate NOX4 protein expression [142].

In summary, PF alleviates CL through coordinated modulation of multiple pathogenic processes, including suppression of hepatocyte apoptosis, restoration of biliary epithelial barrier integrity, and regulation of BA transport. In parallel, PF attenuates inflammatory responses and oxidative stress by enhancing antioxidant defenses and limiting excessive ROS production, which improves the hepatic inflammatory and redox microenvironment. These multi-target actions underscore the potential of PF to mitigate the onset and progression of CL by restoring BA homeostasis and protecting liver function (Fig. 7; Table 1).

Protective mechanisms of PF in PCOS

PCOS is one of the most common metabolic disorders, affecting approximately 11%–13% of women of reproductive age.Although the pathophysiology of PCOS remains incompletely understood, growing evidence indicates a strong association with obesity and related metabolic disturbances. Currently, no approved pharmacological treatments are specifically indicated for PCOS management [143, 144].

In a dehydroepiandrosterone(DHEA)-induced rat model of PCOS, four weeks of continuous oral administration of PF significantly inhibited TGF-β1-induced Smad3 phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner and increased the expression of Smad7 and matrix metalloproteinase-2(MMP2).These results suggest that PF exerts anti-ovarian fibrosis effects by modulating the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway [145]. CYP17A1,a key steroidogenic enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of pregnenolone(P) or progesterone into androgen precursors, is markedly overexpressed in the follicular cells of women with PCOS. PF significantly reversed dexamethasone(DEX)-induced testosterone(T) hypersecretion in primary mouse theca cells(TCs). Mechanistically, this effect is primarily achieved by dose-dependently downregulating the expression of CYP17A1. At higher concentrations(100 µg/mL), PF also suppressed the expression of CYP11A1, an enzyme responsible for pregnenolone synthesis. Altogether, these actions reduce T levels and restore P synthesis, which contribute to the correction of hormonal imbalances in PCOS [146] (Table 1).

Shared mechanisms of PF in MetDs

Although the pathological manifestations of MetDs vary across organs and clinical contexts, accumulating evidence from the present review indicates that PF exerts its protective effects through a set of highly conserved molecular pathways shared among diverse metabolic disorders. These common mechanisms primarily converge on the regulation of inflammation, oxidative stress, glucose and lipid metabolism, and cell fate decisions, which provided a unifying framework for comprehending the broad-spectrum therapeutic potential of PF.

Chronic low-grade inflammation represents a central pathological hallmark across MetDs, including DM, NAFLD, AS, CL, and PCOS. Notably, PF consistently suppresses inflammatory signaling by targeting upstream pattern-recognition receptor pathways, particularly the TLR2/4–MyD88 axis, which leads to the inhibition of downstream IKK/NF-κB activation. This anti-inflammatory action is repeatedly observed across multiple MetDs models and cell types, which results in reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and attenuation of macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization. Oxidative stress is another shared driver of metabolic dysfunction and tissue injury. PF exerts robust antioxidant effects by limiting ROS generation—through inhibition of stress-activated kinases such as JNK and p38 MAPK or suppression of NOX4 activity—and enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses. Activation of the PI3K/Akt/Nrf2 signaling pathway by PF promotes the transcription of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, GPX and enzymes involved in GSH synthesis. This action restores redox homeostasis across hepatic, vascular, renal, neural, and reproductive tissues. In parallel, PF modulates glucose and lipid metabolism through conserved metabolic signaling networks. Restoration of insulin sensitivity via the ROCK–IRS-1–PI3K/Akt–GSK-3β axis constitutes a central mechanism in diabetes, diabetic complications, and NAFL. Meanwhile, PF regulates lipid homeostasis by activating AMPK and modulating PPARα/PPARγ-dependent transcriptional programs, which leads to reduced lipogenesis, enhanced fatty acid oxidation, and improved cholesterol handling. In macrophages and vascular tissues, PF further promotes RCT through activation of the LXRα–ABCA1/ABCG1 pathway, which contributes to anti-atherosclerotic effects. Beyond metabolic regulation, PF influences multiple modes of cell fate determination, including apoptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, and necroptosis. Through coordinated regulation of SIRT1, Bax/Bcl-2, caspase cascades, mTOR-dependent autophagy pathways, and Nrf2-mediated ferroptosis defense, PF confers cytoprotective effects on metabolically stressed organs. Importantly, these mechanisms are not disease-specific but recur across distinct pathological settings, which underscore their role as shared therapeutic nodes (Fig. 8).

Pharmacological and toxicological characteristics of PF

Pharmacology

Pharmacokinetics(PK), the study of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME), is fundamental to preclinical and clinical research. A thorough understanding of PK properties ensures accurate dosage and minimizes adverse effects [147]. Multiple animal studies have demonstrated the extremely low oral bioavailability of PF. In male rats orally administered with PF at doses of 0.5, 2, and 5 mg/kg, the bioavailability was approximately 3%–4% [148]. Similarly, in male rats subjected to a chronic unpredictable mild stress model and treated with 20 mg/kg PF orally, the bioavailability was low at 2.32% [149]. This low bioavailability is closely related to PF’s limited absorption, rapid metabolism, and efficient excretion in vivo.

Absorption

Perfusion experiments using an everted gut sac model in male rats showed that PF is primarily absorbed in the duodenum and jejunum, which suggests that its absorption is localized to the proximal small intestine. However, the overall absorption efficiency remains low (P*eff < 0.4). P-Glycoprotein (P-gp), encoded by multidrug resistance genes and abundant in intestinal tissue, is an ATP-dependent efflux transporter that actively pumps drugs out of cells to maintain low intracellular concentrations. Caco-2 cell transport assays revealed that PF is a substrate of P-gp, as its apical-to-basolateral flux was significantly lower than the reverse direction. In the absence of inhibitors, the BL→AP transport rate is approximately thrice times that of AP→BL, which indicates that PF has poor membrane permeability and is prone to active efflux [150].

Metabolism

Masao Maruno and colleagues first reported, using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) combined with enzyme immunoassay (EIA), that PF has a short plasma half-life (t₁/₂) of 9.9–12.6 min in rats, which indicates rapid systemic metabolism [148].Comparison of area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) values following portal vein (IPV) and intravenous (IV) injection showed near equivalence, which suggests minimal hepatic metabolism, with the primary metabolic site being likely located in the intestine. In vitro everted gut sac experiments further showed that PF’s intestinal permeability after 60 min was 19.4%, which is markedly lower than that of phenobarbital (63.1%), This finding highlights PF’s poor trans-epithelial absorption. Notably, PF’s AUC was significantly higher after IP injection compared with IPV, which suggests that PF is not easily degraded in the intestinal wall. The unabsorbed portion of PF is primarily metabolized by the gut microbiota, though the specific metabolic products are unidentified [151].Subsequent studies cultured anaerobic bacteria isolated from human feces with PF and revealed that they could be extensively degraded via ester hydrolysis, deglycosylation, and oxidation. Bacteroides fragilis and Lactobacillus brevis were particularly effective in degrading PF. Identified metabolites included paeonimetabolin I (PM-I), PM-II, and PM-III, with PM-I considered the primary bioactive metabolite following oral administration of PF [152].

When PF was intravenously administered to male rats at different time points, it rapidly penetrated the blood–brain barrier (BBB), reached peak concentrations in the hippocampus within 10–15 min, and then gradually declined. Nonetheless, low levels of PF could still be detected after 120 min, which suggests some degree of hippocampal retention [153]. Additional studies confirmed that PF crosses the BBB mainly via passive diffusion. However, its brain penetration is limited by P-gp-mediated efflux. Co-administration with the P-gp inhibitor verapamil significantly increased PF’s transmembrane permeability, which supports the view that P-gp restricts its CNS distribution [154]. Interestingly, PF’s antidepressant effect may not depend entirely on its direct action within the brain. PF is metabolized by gut microbiota into benzoic acid, a compound capable of crossing the BBB. Once in the brain, benzoic acid may exert antidepressant effects by modulating N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) activity, which implies a potential role for the gut–brain axis in PF’s mechanism of action [149].

Excretion

Following intravenous administration, PF is primarily excreted in the urine, accounting for approximately 50.5% of the administered dose. Only small amounts are excreted via bile (6.9%) and feces (0.22%) [148]. After oral administration, PF is mainly excreted in the urine in the form of its metabolite, benzoic acid. Within 96 h post-dosing, the cumulative urinary excretion rates of PF and benzoic acid reached 1.66% and 42.13%, respectively. By contrast, the cumulative fecal excretion rates of PF and its metabolite were very low at 0.016% and 0.15%, respectively—and the biliary excretion rate within 60 h was 0.02%. These findings indicate that benzoic acid is the predominant urinary excretion form of PF after oral dosing and suggest that PF is primarily metabolized by intestinal microbiota. Given the minimal excretion via bile and feces and the extensive absence of enterohepatic recirculation, PF is likely cleared from the body mainly through gut microbial metabolism followed by renal excretion [149].

Toxicology

Preclinical studies

Researchers cultured HeLa cells with PF at a concentration of 80 µg/mL (approximately 166.4 µM) and observed no significant inhibition of cell proliferation. Furthermore, cell cycle distribution following PF treatment did not differ notably from that of the untreated control group, which indicates that PF exhibited no evident cytotoxicity at this concentration [155]. In another study involving SGC7901/VCR cells, PF exposure at concentrations ranging from 0 µg/mL to 80 µg/mL showed no significant cytotoxic effects. However, at 160 µg/mL with 48 h of incubation, a marked reduction in cell viability was observed, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was 324 µg/mL [156]. Nonetheless, even at 160 µg/mL, a 30 min treatment with PF did not significantly impair cell viability, which implies that its cytotoxicity may depend on factors such as cell type and incubation duration.In animal studies, a rat single-pass four-segment intestinal perfusion model revealed that PF had minimal effect on water flux across intestinal segments (all < 0.5%/cm), consistent with previous findings, and further supporting the view that PF and its metabolites possess negligible acute toxicity [150]. Altogether, these results demonstrate that the effective concentration range of PF for its bioactivities is exceedingly below its toxic threshold. The current body of evidence consistently suggests that PF has very low toxicity and a favorable safety profile.As a natural compound, PF displays significant therapeutic potential and is considered safe for the treatment of MetDs.

Clinical studies

Although clinical studies on PF remain limited, existing data suggest that intravenous and oral administration of PF are well tolerated, with no serious adverse effects reported. Huoxuetongluo lyophilized powder injection (HTLPI), a traditional Chinese medicine injection composed mainly of Semen Persicae (peach kernel) and Radix Paeoniae Rubra (red peony root), contains active components such as amygdalin and PF. In one study, 12 healthy volunteers underwent a single ascending-dose intravenous injection trial (containing 18.3, 35.8, and 54.1 mg PF per dose), with a 7-day interval between doses. Each dose was infused for over 4 h. In a separate group of 9 healthy volunteers, a multiple-dose regimen was administered intravenously (6 g HTLPI per day for 7 consecutive days, diluted in 250 mL saline). Results show linear increases in the Cmax and AUC of AD and PF within the 3–9 g dose range. Importantly, no subjects withdrew due to adverse events, which indicates good dose-dependent PK behavior and tolerability of PF [157]. In another trial, Tangzhiqing(TZQ) tablets, a proprietary Chinese medicine containing 6.40 mg PF per tablet and currently undergoing Phase II clinical evaluation, were administered to 20 healthy Chinese male volunteers. Subjects were divided into groups and received 2, 3, or 4 TZQ tablets on the second day of a two-day oral intervention period. No adverse events were reported throughout the study [158].

Strategies for the enhanced bioavailability of PF

As previously discussed, PF exhibits poor oral bioavailability. On one hand, its strong polarity and low lipophilicity limit its capability to passively diffuse across biological membranes. On the other hand, hydrolysis by brush-border enzymes in the small intestine converts a substantial portion of PF into metabolites prior to absorption. Meanwhile, PF is further degraded by gut microbiota, which collectively reduces the amount of intact PF that enters systemic circulation. In addition, the high expression of P-gp in the intestinal epithelium further decreases net absorption by actively pumping PF back into the intestinal lumen. Thus, although PF demonstrates multiple beneficial effects on MetDs models, its limited bioavailability compromises its therapeutic efficacy and clinical applicability. To address this issue, scholars proposed three approaches: co-administration with other agents, structural modification, and nanotechnology-based delivery.

Co-administration

Cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4), a major enzyme in the cytochrome P450 family, plays a critical role in drug metabolism, similar to P-gp, by influencing drug absorption, distribution, and elimination [159].Co-administration of PF with compounds such as peimine, glycyrrhizic acid (GA), and sinomenine (SIN) can inhibit P-gp and cytochrome P450 activity, which increases systemic PF exposure in a dose-dependent manner.

In rats pre-treated with peimine (5 mg/kg) for 10 days followed by PF (20 mg/kg), peimine inhibited the activity of key metabolic and efflux enzymes (CYP3A4 and P-gp), which resulted in increased systemic exposure and metabolic stability of PF. PK analysis showed increases in PF’s Cmax and AUC(0–t), an extended half-life (t₁/₂), and a reduced clearance (CL/F) [160]. The effect of GA on PF metabolism is complex. A 2016 study identified 18β-GA as GA’s main metabolite and demonstrated that it influences PF absorption in a concentration- and intestinal-segment-dependent manner. At 100 µM, 18β-GA increased PF absorption in the ileum but reduced it in the jejunum; at 1 mM, it enhanced absorption in both segments. This finding maybe related to differences in P-gp modulation by GA and 18β-GA and their regional expression patterns in the intestine [161]. However, GA itself may reduce PF bioavailability. Pretreatment with GA (100 mg/kg/day for 7 days) prior to PF (20 mg/kg) administration decreased PF’s PK parameters. In vitro studies also confirmed that GA induces CYP450 and P-gp activity [162]. Notably, when PF was incorporated into GA micelles, about 66% was rapidly released within 0.5 h, with sustained release over 10 h.This finding suggest that GA micelles may serve as promising oral carriers for PF [163]. Additional studies have demonstrated that SIN, an alkaloid extracted from Sinomenium acutum, can greatly enhance the oral bioavailability of PF in rats. At concentrations of 16 and 136 µM, SIN increased PF (20 µM) absorption by 1.5- and 2.5-fold, respectively [164]. A dose of 90 mg/kg SIN hydrochloride may represent the optimal in vivo concentration for enhancing PF bioavailability [165]. Single-pass intestinal perfusion studies using 50 µM PF revealed that 100 µM SIN significantly improved absorption in the jejunum (by 45%–55%) and ileum (by 80%–86%), Moreover, the absorption-enhancing effect of SIN on PF is attributable to the inhibition of P-gp mediated efflux, without altering the metabolic pathways leading to paeonimetabolin and benzoic acid formation [150]. Other known P-gp inhibitors such as verapamil, quinidine, and cyclosporine A, also considerably enhance PF absorption. Moreover, several traditional Chinese medicine formulations, including Jing-Zhi-Guan-Xin [166] and Taohong Siwu Decoction [167], when co-administered with PF, improve its bioavailability. These findings suggest that co-administration strategies can effectively enhance PF absorption. However, extended systemic exposure to PF may increase the risk of toxicity, which highlights the need for precise dosage optimization in such combinations.

Structural modification

Paeoniflorin-6′-O-benzenesulfonate(CP-25) is a sulfonated ester derivative synthesized through the esterification of the 6′-hydroxyl group of the glucose moiety in PF with benzenesulfonyl chloride. Compared with PF, CP-25 possesses greater lipophilicity, enhanced biological activity, and improved bioavailability. One study reported absolute bioavailability values of 3.6% for PF and 10.6% for CP-25 [168], Following oral administration of 50 mg/kg PF or CP-25 in rats, tissue concentrations of CP-25 at 0.5, 3, and 8 h were consistently higher—ranging from 1.05- to 11.59-fold—which suggests superior tissue penetration [169]. CP-25 has shown efficacy in improving vascular inflammation [170], Sjögren’s syndrome [171], neointimal formation and angiogenesis [172]. At a dose of 100 mg/kg, CP-25 demonstrated comparable therapeutic effects to first-line drugs such as methotrexate and hydroxychloroquine in models of adjuvant arthritis, collagen-induced arthritis, and experimental Sjögren’s syndrome. Notably, CP-25 exhibits lower toxicity and broad modulatory effects across various cell types. However, this compound also exhibits notable tissue distribution and sex-related differences, which suggests that further research on CP-25 can offer a novel avenue for the enhanced bioavailability of PF [173].

Nanotechnology

Transdermal drug delivery systems offer an alternative to oral administration, particularly for localized treatment. However, the stratum corneum poses the greatest barrier to transdermal absorption.Ethosomes and microneedles represent promising technologies for enhancing PF skin penetration. Microneedles disrupt the stratum corneum and facilitate drug entry via microchannels, which notably improves the delivery of hydrophilic compounds such as PF. Ethosomes, which are lipid vesicles containing ethanol or propylene glycol, can deform and become smaller than conventional liposomes, which allows them to traverse the skin barrier and improve drug retention within the skin.Huang et al. [174] observed that optimal delivery in rats was achieved using microneedles with a length of 500 μm, a pressure of 3 N, and a duration of 3 min. However, Xiao et al. [175] reported that although ethosomes and microneedles independently enhanced PF penetration, their combination did not exhibit synergistic effects, which indicates that the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. In addition, PF nanocrystals(PA-NCs) showed significantly improved cellular uptake and permeability in Calu-3 cells. The intact NCs were internalized by the cells, and elevated PF concentrations in the brain confirmed the efficiency of intranasal NC delivery—a potentially promising approach for the treatment of Parkinson’s disease [176]. Encapsulation of PF in poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles also reduced P-gp-mediated efflux and enhanced lipophilicity, which facilitated intestinal mucosal absorption and systemic transport [176] (Fig. 9).

Summary and translational implications

Overall, the extremely low oral bioavailability of PF represents a major obstacle to its clinical translation. This limitation arises from multiple factors, including poor membrane permeability due to high polarity, extensive intestinal and microbial metabolism, and active efflux mediated by P-gp. Although PF has demonstrated robust therapeutic effects across diverse MetDs models, these PK constraints substantially compromise its systemic exposure and translational feasibility. Importantly, current PK evidence is largely derived from rodent models, and extrapolation to humans should be approached with caution due to interspecies differences in intestinal enzyme expression, transporter activity, and gut microbiota composition. Nevertheless, accumulated experimental data indicate that this limitation may be partially overcome through rational strategies, including the development of PF derivatives with improved lipophilicity, advanced drug delivery systems, and co-administration with other agents.

Discussion and prospects

This study systematically summarized recent advances and mechanistic insights into the role of PF in the intervention of MetDs. Overall, PF exhibited promising therapeutic potential and multi-target regulatory effects across various MetDs models. In DM, PF improves IR by inhibiting the ROCK pathway and downregulating its downstream effector GSK3β.In addition, PF exerts potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, which help alleviate the chronic inflammation and oxidative stress associated with diabetes.In NAFLD, PF modulates hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism by regulating key enzymes such as ACC1 and GSK3β, which results in the interference of interfering with lipogenesis and gluconeogenesis. PF also attenuates hepatic steatosis and inflammation by suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway. In NASH, PF demonstrates anti-fibrotic activity primarily through epigenetic regulation and modulation of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, which inhibits HSC activation while reducing inflammatory responses and oxidative stress.In AS, PF exerts protective effects by inhibiting abnormal proliferation of VSMCs, preserving VECs function, and reducing foam cell formation. In cholestatic liver diseases, PF alleviates hepatic injury by regulating BA efflux via FXR-mediated pathways, suppressing hepatocyte apoptosis, and reducing oxidative stress and NF-κB-mediated inflammation. Moreover, PF has shown promising effects on PCOS, likely through the inhibition of TGF-β/Smad signaling and downregulation of key steroidogenic enzymes involved in androgen biosynthesis.Although the experimental evidence summarized in this review consistently demonstrates the beneficial effects of PF across multiple MetDs models, the overall strength of the available evidence remains predominantly preclinical. The majority of studies were conducted in cell-based systems or rodent models, and often used acute or chemically induced disease paradigms, which may not fully recapitulate the complexity, chronicity, and heterogeneity of human MetDs. In addition, the dosing regimens employed across studies vary considerably, and the relevance of experimental doses to human therapeutic exposure remains uncertain. This lack of standardized dosing and clinical PK data further constrains translational interpretation.Consequently, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to clinical settings.

Compared with current first-line clinical therapies, such as metformin, statins, and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, PF demonstrates several distinctive advantages in addressing the complex and multifactorial pathophysiology of MetDs. A key advantage of PF lies in its broad-spectrum and multi-pathway regulatory properties. Metformin primarily improves glucose metabolism by suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis and activating the AMPK pathway [177]; statins mainly target cholesterol biosynthesis [178]; and GLP-1 receptor agonists exert their effects predominantly by enhancing glucose-dependent insulin secretion and regulating appetite [179]. Notably, co-administration of PF with metformin synergistically suppressed the progression of liver fibrosis in murine models, which highlights the potential of PF as a complementary agent to existing first-line therapies [180].

By contrast, PF simultaneously modulates inflammation, oxidative stress, glucose and lipid metabolism, BA homeostasis, and multiple cell fate–related processes. Notably, across a range of diseases—including DM and its complications, NAFL/NASH, AS, CL, and PCOS—PF consistently targets shared upstream pathogenic drivers, such as NF-κB–mediated inflammation, Nrf2-regulated oxidative stress, and IR. In addition, PF is derived from medicinal plants and has a long history of traditional use, which may suggest a favorable safety profile and long-term tolerability.

Despite these promising features, the clinical translation of PF faces several challenges. The most prominent limitation is its extremely low oral bioavailability, which is largely attributable to its poor intestinal absorption, P-gp–mediated efflux, and extensive metabolism by gut microbiota. Moreover, current evidence supporting PF’s therapeutic efficacy is mainly derived from preclinical animal models and in vitro studies, and large-scale, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trials are still lacking. These limitations hinder definitive conclusions regarding optimal dosing regimens, long-term safety, and clinical effectiveness. Meanwhile, MetDs frequently present as comorbid conditions in clinical settings; however, most existing studies focus on PF’s effects on single-disease models, which leaves its potential to simultaneously modulate multiple metabolic disorders through shared signaling networks insufficiently explored.

Therefore, future research should prioritize strategies aimed at improving the bioavailability and translational feasibility of PF. Current approaches to enhancing PF bioavailability mainly include co-administration with other agents, structural modification, and nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems. Among these approaches, co-administration is considered the most practical strategy due to its relatively well-established research foundation, whereas nanotechnology-based delivery systems show considerable promise by improving drug stability and enabling targeted delivery. Cyclodextrin-based encapsulation may represent another potential strategy to enhance PF bioavailability [181, 182]. Furthermore, network pharmacology and systems biology approaches should be integrated for the systematic identification of common molecular targets of PF and elucidate its multi-disease regulatory mechanisms mediated by shared metabolic pathways. Importantly, rigorously designed clinical trials are urgently needed to determine whether the multi-target effects observed in experimental models can be translated into clinically meaningful benefits. Finally, the therapeutic efficacy of PF may vary depending on the botanical origin, extraction methods, and cultivation duration of different Paeonia species [183].Therefore, rigorous selection of high-quality Paeonia sources is essential before clinical application. Altogether, the evidence discussed in this review supports PF as a potential adjunctive or integrative therapeutic agent for the treatment of MetDs.
