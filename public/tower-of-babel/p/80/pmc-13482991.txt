Navigating chemical-linguistic sharing space with heterogeneous molecular encoding
Authors: Liuzhenghao Lv, Hao Li, Yu Wang, Zijun Chen, Zhiyuan Yan, Zongying Lin, Yuyang Liu, Li Yuan, Yonghong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemical language models are powerful tools for navigating chemical space, but their reliance on linear representations such as molecular strings creates a semantic gap, hindering their ability to bridge natural language with the full complexity of molecular structures. Here we show that chemical language models can gain a comprehensive, multi-modal understanding of molecules through heterogeneous molecular encoding, which integrates one-dimensional sequences, two-dimensional topology, three-dimensional geometry, and statistically derived molecular fragments. We further introduce a query-based module that converts heterogeneous structural information into a unified representation compatible with language models, together with a chain-of-fragment mechanism that guides molecular generation through a hierarchical chemical blueprinting process. To support research in this area, we constructed a million-scale dataset for multi-objective molecular design. Experimentally, the framework enables bidirectional navigation of the chemical-linguistic space, achieving consistent improvements across molecular comprehension and design tasks over strong baselines.

Introduction

In recent years, the exploration of chemical space has become a cornerstone of modern chemistry and molecular science1,2. With the rapid growth of molecular structures and their textual descriptions, a distinct paradigm is emerging: the chemical-linguistic space3,4, which enables more intuitive representations of molecular properties and interactions. To model this space, chemical language models (CLMs) have been developed, typically based on sequence-based representations such as the simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES)5 and the self-referencing embedded strings (SELFIES)6 to treat molecules as linear token sequences, akin to natural language7–9. Through masked or causal language modeling10,11, CLMs can learn the bidirectional dependencies between molecular structures and natural language, which can be used for conditional molecular generation12,13 and molecular comprehension14,15, offering significant potential for drug design and virtual screening16. However, the reliance on a single and linear molecular representation limits the ability of CLMs to capture the full complexity and multidimensional nature of molecular structures, hindering their effectiveness in modeling complex chemical phenomena17.

Advances in molecular representation learning have produced highly effective encoders for two-dimensional (2D) graph structures18,19 and three-dimensional (3D) coordinates20. Integrating these specialist encoders with language models can yield powerful multi-modal models capable of bridging natural language and molecular structure, unlocking broad potential in molecular design and analysis21,22. However, a long-standing challenge is that molecular representations from different perspectives, including one-dimensional (1D) SMILES, 2D graphs, and 3D conformations, each possess inherent limitations. Specifically, (1) SMILES sequences, generated via depth-first traversal, are not permutation-invariant, meaning small molecular changes can cause disproportionate sequence variations23, introducing challenges in molecular design; (2) 2D molecular graphs, while excelling at capturing topology, can be affected by issues such as over-smoothing in graph neural networks (GNNs)24 or an over-emphasis on global interactions in Graph Transformers25; (3) 3D geometric models, focusing on spatial arrangements, may struggle to differentiate molecules with similar geometries but distinct structures or maintain robustness against conformational changes26. Therefore, integrating complementary molecular representations across modalities is crucial for achieving accurate and reliable chemical design and analysis.

Concurrently, the application of general-purpose large language models (LLMs) to scientific domains has marked another significant advance. A prominent and highly successful research direction augments LLMs with external, chemistry-specific tools27,28. This paradigm leverages the LLM as a sophisticated controller to orchestrate specialized software for tasks such as synthesis planning and automated discovery. While exceptionally powerful, this approach relies on specialized functions of the integrated tools, rather than enhancing the LLM’s core, intrinsic understanding of molecular structure. To build a unified and versatile framework for the chemical sciences, it is crucial for the LLM itself to be able to perceive and process rich, multi-modal molecular information directly.

Previous efforts to build such chemically aware models have explored various multi-modal alignment strategies. Some work focused on creating robust molecular-only representations by aligning 2D and 3D views through contrastive learning, though often without incorporating language29–31. A step closer to this goal, methods like CLAMP32 have successfully bridged molecules and text for discriminative tasks by contrastively aligning their respective embeddings33. While this paradigm excels at tasks predicated on similarity, such as property prediction or retrieval, it is not inherently designed for complex, instruction-guided generative tasks. This distinction reveals a critical gap: the need for a unified generative framework that moves beyond alignment for similarity. The objective of such a framework is to directly model the conditional probability of generating a target output (e.g., a molecule or its textual description) given a multi-modal context, thereby enabling a broader spectrum of scientific tasks within a single, end-to-end architecture. However, current explorations34,35 have not systematically addressed the collective limitations of each individual representation.

To address these challenges, we propose Heterogeneous Molecular Encoding (HME), a framework that empowers generative LLMs with a comprehensive, multi-modal understanding of chemical structures. Specifically, we encode molecular input from both sequential and geometric perspectives. For geometric encoding, we adopt specialized 2D and 3D molecular encoders. We introduce the Query Learning module, a modality adapter that bridges these specialist encoders with the LLM. This module employs cross-attention to transform the atomic features from each encoder into a sequence of soft tokens that the language model can directly process. We further develop a fusion module that integrates the 2D and 3D soft tokens to create a synergistic geometric representation.

Furthermore, we introduce molecular fragments as an additional modality for LLMs. By employing a subgraph mining algorithm, we construct a data-driven fragment vocabulary and expand the LLM’s native vocabulary to process fragments as distinct tokens. This strategy enables seamless integration with the LLM’s pre-trained knowledge and allows us to introduce the Chain-of-Fragment (CoF) mechanism. CoF structures molecular generation as a hierarchical chemical blueprinting process: it first generates a sequence of constituent fragments, followed by the final SMILES string based on this structural blueprint. This mechanism improves generative accuracy and enables fine-grained control over the generated structure. To facilitate the training and systematic evaluation of these multi-condition generative capabilities, we also construct a large-scale Multi-Condition Molecular Design (MCMoD) dataset.

In this work, we show the effectiveness and generalizability of the HME framework across a comprehensive suite of molecular tasks. By integrating HME with multiple LLM backbones, including Llama-3, Llama-2, and Qwen-2.5, our enhanced models achieve substantial improvements in molecular comprehension, including molecular captioning and question answering (QA). For molecular generation, evaluations on the MCMoD dataset demonstrate that HME reliably performs molecular design guided by textual descriptions, chemical properties, and fragment constraints. Furthermore, HME shows strong performance in the challenging task of pocket-based ligand generation, demonstrating highly competitive results on a standard benchmark against specialized 3D generative models. Additionally, the HME framework exhibits strong zero-shot generalization capabilities in multi-objective molecular design scenarios, highlighting its broad potential as a versatile tool for chemical discovery and molecular engineering.

Results

Construction of our heterogeneous molecular encoding structure

Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow of our HME framework. HME incorporates features of the same molecule from different perspectives to achieve a deep and comprehensive molecular understanding. As shown in Fig. 1a, HME derives encodings from four molecular tokenizers: (1) the 1D SMILES Tokenizer, which embeds the SMILES string of the molecule into the text feature space, maximizing the use of the pretrained knowledge within the LLM; (2) the 2D Graph Tokenizer, which encodes the molecular topology to provide structural information; (3) the 3D Coordinate Tokenizer, which encodes the Euclidean coordinates of each atom, supplying more fine-grained spatial information; and (4) the Molecular Fragment Tokenizer, which utilizes a vocabulary of substructures as additional tokens to explicitly represent higher-level chemical fragments. Unlike approaches that develop rule-based string representations to replace SMILES, such as Group SELFIES36 and SAFE37, our method treats fragments as an additional information source. We introduce a subgraph mining algorithm38, construct a data-driven fragment vocabulary, and expand the LLM’s native vocabulary to process fragments as distinct tokens. Figure 1b provides further details of HME. A key architectural component is the Query Learning module, which serves as a learnable adapter to bridge the heterogeneous outputs of the structural encoders with the uniform input space required by the LLM. It transforms the feature embeddings from each encoder into a sequence of soft tokens, making them compatible with the language model and addressing the inefficiency caused by varying atom counts. We then employ a fusion mechanism based on cross-attention to facilitate reciprocal information flow between the 2D and 3D soft tokens, creating a synergistically fused geometric representation. The resulting soft tokens from all structural modalities are concatenated with the token embeddings from the SMILES and fragment encoders. This composite sequence is fed into a transformer decoder for unified autoregressive modeling, which enables both molecular comprehension and molecular generation to be framed as next-token probability modeling. This architecture enables a structured technique for hierarchical generation which we term CoF. Acting as a chemical blueprinting process, the model first generates an intermediate sequence of key molecular fragments to establish a structural foundation before generating the final, complete molecule. MCMoD: a large-scale dataset for multi-condition molecular design

Our HME can model the chemical-linguistic space bidirectionally, meaning it can seamlessly unify both molecular comprehension and molecular generation. This dual capability makes it essential to systematically evaluate its performance across these two major domains. In the molecular comprehension field, there are well-established and comprehensive benchmarks for evaluating CLMs35,39,40. In the conditional molecular generation domain, benchmarks exist for evaluating molecular generation with description-based control conditions9; however, we found that these benchmarks are limited by their single-condition focus and small dataset size41, which hampers a comprehensive assessment of a CLM’s conditional molecular generation capabilities. To address these limitations, we constructed the MCMoD dataset.

MCMoD is constructed to facilitate the training and evaluation of models for multi-objective molecular design, integrating diverse control conditions such as textual descriptions, physicochemical properties, and molecular fragments. The dataset comprises over one million molecules, covering a wide range of chemical entities including synthetic molecules, natural products, and protein ligands (see Supplementary Note 2 for a detailed characterization of chemical space, property distributions, and textual statistics). It supports multi-objective joint control, such as using both property values and specific fragments as conditions. Additionally, MCMoD creatively leverages fragment sequences as a bridge between text and molecular modalities to explore the application of the CoF paradigm in CLMs.

Overall, the MCMoD dataset we propose not only enables the systematic evaluation of HME and other CLMs in conditional molecular generation but also promotes practical applications in fields such as drug development and chemical engineering through its diverse control conditions42. The source data for MCMoD were obtained from datasets and databases such as PubChem43, ZINC44, ChEBI45, COCONUT46, and DTP47. We used RDKit to canonicalize the molecules and calculate commonly used property values, and employed our fragment generator to obtain fragment sequences. Following established guidelines for chemical structure curation48, further details on the data standardization protocol and conformation generation are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Evaluation of molecular comprehension tasks

Molecule comprehension aims to model the mapping from chemical space to linguistic space, that is, to capture chemical structural information and express it in human-understandable natural language. This capability is crucial for bridging the gap between computational chemistry and human intuition, facilitating knowledge transfer, and enabling more effective drug design and documentation49. However, existing approaches either lack comprehensive utilization of multi-perspective molecular features7,50 or fail to leverage LLMs, which provide strong interactive capabilities and rich domain knowledge33. Our proposed HME, built upon advanced LLMs, unifies the molecular captioning task, the general QA task, and the property QA task within a natural language dialog framework. Notably, HME explicitly integrates diverse and complementary molecular information, enabling a more holistic understanding and interpretation of molecular structures.

We used the 3D-MoIT dataset35 specifically designed for CLMs in chemistry as the molecular comprehension benchmark. We performed additional processing on the original dataset, such as obtaining graph structures, coordinates, and fragment sequences. We selected a broad range of baselines: MolT57, which focuses on translating between SMILES and text; MoMu50, which leverages both SMILES and graphs to understand molecules; 3D-MoLM35, which extensively utilizes molecular coordinate information; MolCA51, which integrates a pre-trained graph encoder with an LLM; and 3D-MolT552, which encodes 3D structural information into the SELFIES representation.

Molecular captioning

Analogous to image captioning, molecular captioning aims to generate descriptive text for molecules, a crucial task that bridges the semantic gap between chemical structures and human understanding, enabling more informed decision-making in drug design7. We evaluate molecular captioning using standard text-generation metrics, including BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR, which assess the similarity between generated and reference texts with different emphases on precision, recall, and alignment quality53–55.

This result highlights the effectiveness of our heterogeneous encoding in providing a richer structural context for text generation. Molecular general question answering

Recently, LLMs such as ChatGPT have demonstrated the ability to answer a wide range of human queries56. Naturally, this has led to an emerging demand for asking questions about molecules to gain insights into their physicochemical properties, functions, origins, and other aspects. As a result, the molecular general QA task has emerged to meet this need, enabling users to interact with models in a way that provides comprehensive and accessible molecular knowledge. The same metrics used in the molecular captioning task are used to evaluate the correctness of the generated answers.

HME also outperforms the baselines in this task, as shown in Table 2. Against the strong baselines, HME (Llama-3-8B) continues to demonstrate superior performance, achieving a METEOR score of 59.67 compared to 58.97 for MolCA and 56.97 for 3D-MolT5. This consistent advantage underscores the benefits of our heterogeneous encoding approach, which provides the LLM with a richer, multi-modal context for answering complex questions. Furthermore, the strong performance of HME variants based on Llama-2-7B (METEOR 59.27) and Qwen-2.5-7B (METEOR 59.45) confirms that our architecture’s effectiveness is generalizable and not dependent on a single LLM backbone. Molecular property question answering

The objective of molecular property prediction is to infer diverse property values based on molecular structures. Within our HME framework, molecular property prediction is transformed into a molecular property QA paradigm, enabling users to specify properties of interest through natural language queries. This approach gives rise to a more user-friendly and unified model capable of predicting multiple molecular properties simultaneously. Our HME model can predict the property values of a given molecule, including not only conventional properties such as molecular weight, LogP, and TPSA, but also quantum chemical properties like HOMO, LUMO, HOMO-LUMO gap, and SCF energy. To evaluate the model’s performance, we extract the predicted numerical values from the generated answers using regular expressions, and then compare them with the ground-truth values.

As shown in Fig. 2b, we plot the distribution of the absolute difference between the predicted and actual values. We observe that HME significantly outperforms all baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of heterogeneous encoding. Specifically, 2D graphs provide a more stable molecular representation, containing expert knowledge such as bond types and atomic connectivity. Although 3D coordinates offer detailed spatial information, they may carry potential errors and lack stability. By integrating 2D and 3D information, HME gains a robust and comprehensive understanding of molecular geometry. A more detailed quantitative comparison using the mean absolute error (MAE) is provided in Supplementary Note 5.3, where HME achieves leading performance, reducing the error on molecular weight prediction by 46.4% compared to the strong 3D-MolT5 baseline. Furthermore, we demonstrate the robustness of HME on standard community benchmarks. In Supplementary Note 5, we present its performance on six MoleculeNet tasks39 for property prediction and the PDBBind benchmark58 for ligand-affinity prediction, where it achieves results comparable to or exceeding those of specialized models, validating the practical applicability of HME’s learned representations.

Evaluations of conditional molecular generation tasks

Conditional molecular generation aims to design molecules that satisfy specified constraints, which is crucial for drug design and related applications as it reduces blind exploration of the vast chemical space. Previous approaches often lack a unified framework for flexible conditional control. In our HME framework, we unify diverse conditional generation tasks into a next-token prediction problem by expressing various conditions as language. Leveraging the flexibility of natural language, HME demonstrates remarkable zero-shot generalization capabilities. The incorporation of molecular fragment sequences also enables a structured generative process that we term CoF. We used the MCMoD dataset as a benchmark to evaluate the performance of HME across three tasks of conditional molecular generation: description-based molecular generation, protein-ligand design, and multi-objective inverse molecular design. The baselines span a wide range, including simple RNNs59, simple transformers60, MolT57 models of different scales, GPT-3.556 with exceptional zero-shot/few-shot capabilities, and the generative molecular pretrained model MolXPT61. We also include several strong, recently proposed models such as MolFM62, GIT-Mol63, and the diffusion-based TGM-DLM64 to ensure a comprehensive comparison.

Description-based molecular generation

In contrast to molecular captioning, this task aims to generate a molecule from a detailed description. The significance of this task lies in its potential to enable chemists to design molecules by providing precise natural language specifications, thereby bridging human intentions and computational molecular design. While previous works focused on directly mapping textual descriptions to SMILES, our model employs the CoF mechanism to facilitate a hierarchical chemical blueprinting process. By first establishing fragment tokens before determining the precise atomic arrangement, this approach improves generative accuracy. We employed multiple metrics to evaluate the similarity between the molecules generated by the models and the reference molecules, including BLEU, various molecular fingerprint similarities, and the Fréchet ChemNet Distance (FCD)65, which measures the distance between molecules in the chemical space.

As shown in Table 3, HME achieves the best performance on 7 out of 8 metrics, outperforming strong baselines including MolT5-Large and the diffusion-based TGM-DLM. For instance, HME achieves the highest Exact Match rate (0.334) and the lowest Levenshtein distance (14.25), indicating its superior ability to generate precise molecular structures. To directly quantify the contribution of this mechanism, we conducted an ablation study by removing the CoF module. As detailed in the final two rows of Table 3, this resulted in a consistent and significant performance degradation across all key metrics (e.g., a 21.9% relative decrease in Exact Match rate and a 5.3% relative decrease in Morgan FTS), confirming the functional importance of this structured generation approach. Pocket-based ligand generation

To evaluate HME’s capability in a more scientifically rigorous and practical setting, we shift from a simple affinity prediction task to pocket-based ligand generation. The objective is to generate a candidate ligand, conditioned on the 3D geometry of a given protein binding pocket. This task requires the model to learn a generalizable mapping from diverse 3D pocket structures to property-compliant molecules. To this end, we augmented the HME architecture with a dedicated protein structure encoder and a corresponding Query Learning module to process pocket geometries. Furthermore, we use multi-conditional prompting (MCP), which provides explicit instructional guidance to the model. By formatting desired properties, such as a QED of 0.7, an SAS of 4, and an affinity of -9 kcal mol−1, into a textual prompt that is co-conditioned with the pocket geometry, MCP enables fine-grained control over the generative process.

We benchmarked our approach on the standard CrossDocked2020 dataset66, ensuring direct comparability with leading methods. Our results demonstrate that HME consistently generates molecules with more favorable property profiles compared to a range of strong baselines (see Supplementary Note 4.2 for detailed quantitative metrics). Notably, HME achieves a leading average binding affinity of −7.87 kcal mol−1, surpassing strong 3D-native generative models like TargetDiff67. This advantage extends to drug-like properties; for instance, HME achieves an average QED of 0.71, a substantial improvement over all baselines. This combined strength is reflected in a class-leading success rate of 44.4%, outperforming the next-best model TamGen68 by a clear margin. The full property distributions are visualized in Supplementary Note 4.

The multi-conditional prompting endows HME with robust controllability over the generation process. Systematically varying the target property values in the input prompt effectively steers the property distributions of the generated molecules. The generated molecules also exhibit high structural and geometric fidelity, with their bond, distance, and ring system distributions closely aligning with those of reference molecules. This comprehensive evaluation validates HME’s utility as a robust and controllable framework for pocket-based ligand generation. Detailed analyses of controllability and structural fidelity are provided in Supplementary Note 4.3, accompanied by other details of this task.

Conditional generation has been explored based on physicochemical properties using diffusion models69–71, as well as on structural fragments via grammar-based formalisms36,37. Our HME framework contributes to this area by enabling joint conditional generation based on both properties and fragments. By representing statistically prevalent fragments as discrete tokens within an expanded LLM vocabulary, HME allows for the specification of both physicochemical targets and desired structural fragments within a single, flexible natural language prompt.

Molecular generation in a zero-shot manner

Leveraging the flexibility of natural language and next-token prediction, our HME model demonstrates remarkable zero-shot capability. Specifically, in the multi-objective molecular inverse design task, HME can generate desired molecules under the control of four fragments, despite the training data containing at most three fragments. Additionally, although HME is trained with control over only a single property type, it generalizes effectively to multi-property control conditions.

As shown in Fig. 4c (the fourth panel), under the condition of four control fragments (γ = 4), we generated 2000 molecules and evaluated how many of the control fragments were incorporated into the final molecules. We observed that 30.31% of the molecules contained all four control fragments, which is a satisfactory result given the increased difficulty of the task with more fragments. Additionally, 69.82% of the molecules contained at least three fragments, surpassing the 62.59% observed for γ = 3. Furthermore, 93.59% of the molecules contained at least two fragments, significantly higher than the 78.84% observed for γ = 2. These results validate HME’s strong generalization ability with respect to the number of fragments.

As illustrated in Fig. 4d, we evaluated HME’s ability to generalize to unseen combinations of property constraints. The model was trained exclusively on single-property conditions, making any prompt containing multiple properties a zero-shot task. The experiment compares generation under a non-zero-shot, single-property prompt (LogP=2, chosen for its relevance to oral bioavailability) against two zero-shot, dual-property prompts. The first comparison, shown in the left two panels, indicates that adding a zero-shot QED constraint with a target value of 0.9 does not degrade control over the primary LogP property, while the QED distribution becomes concentrated around the target value. The second comparison, shown in the right two panels, indicates a similar outcome when an SAS constraint with a target value of 2 is applied. This demonstrates that HME can effectively perform zero-shot, multi-property conditional generation by successfully controlling for a secondary property without compromising the primary one.

These compelling results underscore HME’s remarkable generalization capabilities in molecular inverse design, potentially accelerating the drug development pipeline and enabling more efficient exploration of the vast chemical space.

Discussion

In this work, we introduced HME, a unified framework designed to enable LLMs with a comprehensive understanding of chemistry for both molecular comprehension and molecular design. A core principle of our approach is the integration of heterogeneous molecular representations, including 1D SMILES, 2D topology, 3D coordinates, and statistically derived fragments, to provide a holistic structural context to the LLM. We developed a modular architecture centered on a Query Learning module, which effectively bridges the gap between specialized molecular encoders and the LLM’s embedding space. For generative tasks, we proposed the CoF mechanism, a structured hierarchical process that first generates a chemical blueprint before assembling the final molecule. While hierarchical, this mechanism operates fundamentally as a structural blueprinting tool grounded in chemical space statistics, which should be distinguished from the abstract logical inference of reasoning models such as GPT-474 or DeepSeek-R175. Unlike symbolic reasoning, CoF optimizes molecular generation by learning probabilistic dependencies between fragments and atomic structures.

To facilitate research in this area, we constructed and released MCMoD, a large-scale, multi-conditional dataset for molecular design. Our extensive experiments demonstrate that HME achieves strong performance across a diverse range of tasks. In molecular comprehension, it surpasses strong baselines in captioning and QA. In conditional generation, HME exhibits precise control over multiple physicochemical properties, demonstrates robust zero-shot generalization to unseen combinations of constraints, and performs well in the challenging task of pocket-based ligand generation by effectively leveraging 3D protein geometry. These results underscore the value of integrating rich, multi-modal structural information into LLMs for chemical discovery.

While our framework demonstrates robust capabilities in generating molecules that satisfy specific constraints, achieving genuine de novo drug design remains a substantial challenge. Specifically, although our framework exhibits exploration at the frontiers of the training chemical space, the generated structures tend to represent evolutionary derivatives rather than the radical scaffold hopping typically required for primary hit discovery. Consequently, while the current framework shows significant potential for tasks such as lead optimization where adherence to structural constraints is crucial, further enhancing the capacity for unconstrained structural innovation remains a critical objective to fully realize its practical utility in broader drug design applications.

Additionally, a limitation of our work is that it does not model molecular dynamics, which is becoming increasingly important due to its crucial role in understanding protein-ligand interactions and conformational changes. In the future, we plan to extend HME to tasks related to chemical reactions and molecular dynamics. Furthermore, we intend to build upon our pocket-based design capabilities to explore pharmacophore modeling and lead optimization, aiming to further contribute to the drug design pipeline.

Methods

Molecules can be characterized from multiple perspectives, including 1D sequences, 2D topological graphs, and 3D spatial coordinates. Our HME framework is designed to construct a comprehensive molecular representation for LLMs by integrating these complementary modalities. This approach aims to empower a unified generative model to address a wide spectrum of tasks in chemistry, from molecular comprehension to molecular design. In the following sections, we detail the architecture and training procedure of HME. The specific prompts used for various tasks are provided in Supplementary Note 9.

Overview of the framework architecture

The HME architecture comprises three main components: (1) a Unified Molecular Tokenizer that provides a multi-faceted molecular representation, (2) Query Learning modules that act as a universal adapter to the LLM, and (3) an autoregressive LLM decoder that supports both molecular comprehension and design.

Query learning as a modality adapter

A central challenge is to align the continuous, variable-length representations produced by structural tokenizers, including 2D, 3D, and protein tokenizers, with the token-based interface of the LLM. Direct projection of atom-level features creates token sequences that are often prohibitively long and informationally sparse, while global pooling operations result in considerable information reduction, discarding critical local structural details. To address this trade-off, we introduce the Query Learning module as a universal interface. This module functions as an information resampler, employing a fixed number of learnable queries to extract molecular features into a sequence of soft tokens, effectively decoupling the resolution of the physical input from the context length of the language model.

Multi-modal fusion and integration

To create a synergistically fused geometric representation, we facilitate reciprocal information flow between the 2D and 3D modalities. After fusion, the soft-token sequence from the fused 2D/3D molecular representation is concatenated with the token embeddings derived from the SMILES and fragment tokenizers along the token dimension. For pocket-conditioned tasks, the protein pocket soft-token sequence is appended in the same token-wise manner. The resulting composite token sequence U is then fed into the autoregressive decoder.

Autoregressive decoder

We utilize the 32-layer transformer decoder from Llama-3-8B as a universal decoder to model high-level semantic relationships among all input tokens. A subsequent linear layer serves as the generation head. During inference, we sample from this distribution to perform next-token generation. Since we have expanded the vocabulary, the generated tokens can belong to text, SMILES, or fragments.

Training procedure

Our training process consists of two stages: modality alignment pre-training and task-specific instruction tuning. To improve training efficiency, all molecular encoder embeddings are pre-computed offline and stored, avoiding the overhead of running multiple large encoders during training. Detailed hyperparameters for training are provided in Supplementary Note 1. During the modality alignment stage, training primarily focuses on adapting the molecular tokenizers and the Query Learning module to produce representations that are aligned with the LLM’s latent space. In this stage, the parameters of the autoregressive decoder are kept largely frozen, while the other parameters (Θ) are updated.
