Severe, refractory paraneoplastic aplastic anemia secondary to primary peritoneal carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Kayla Paulosky, Pegah Blustein, Amanda Fader, Matthew Jacob Lankiewicz, Samuel M. Law, Emily MacArthur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This is the first case reported of paraneoplastic aplastic anemia secondary to primary peritoneal carcinoma.•Severe pancytopenia complicated surgery, management of post-operative complications, and chemotherapy selection and duration.•This case highlights the need for multi-disciplinary care in complex gynecologic paraneoplastic syndromes.

Introduction

Paraneoplastic syndromes are manifestations of malignancy not directly caused by the tumor, metastasis, or treatment, but triggered by biological substances, such as hormones, autoantibodies, cytokines, and peptides, secreted by tumors or by cancer-related immune cells (Pelosof and Gerber, 2010 Sep). Approximately 8 % of cancer patients are affected, although true prevalence is uncertain due to rarity (Pelosof and Gerber, 2010 Sep). Paraneoplastic syndromes are classified by the systems they affect. In gynecologic malignancies, they can involve the nervous, ophthalmologic, dermatologic, rheumatologic, endocrine, hematologic, and renal systems (Viau et al., 2017 Sep). In epithelial ovarian cancer, hematologic syndromes reported include thrombocytosis, neutrophilia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and thrombocytopenia (Viau et al., 2017 Sep). Acquired aplastic anemia is a rare paraneoplastic presentation described in lymphoma, thymomas, lung cancer, and one case of myxoid leiomyosarcoma, but to our knowledge has not been reported in the setting of epithelial ovarian or peritoneal cancers (Pedrosa et al., 2021 Feb, Koistinen et al., 1990 Jan, Ritchie and Kumari, 2011). We present a case of paraneoplastic aplastic anemia diagnosed at the time of high-grade serous, primary peritoneal cancer (PPC) diagnosis unrelated to treatment.

Case report

A 63-year-old female with a recent deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) on apixaban, presented with abdominal bloating, fatigue, decreased appetite, and shortness of breath. Imaging revealed a heterogenous pelvic mass, carcinomatosis, and prominent bilateral pelvic lymph nodes. Her serum CA-125 was 7,059 and lymph node biopsy demonstrated high-grade serous carcinoma, of gynecologic origin. Her history included a pathogenic germline BRCA1 mutation, for which she had undergone a total vaginal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy 20 years prior, as well as a risk-reducing bilateral mastectomy. She had no prior history of malignancy.

On admission at the outside hospital, she had pancytopenia; Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC) 0.12 x103cells/µL and Platelets 7.0 x103cells/µL (Table 1). At the time of DVT and PE diagnosis three months prior, her counts had been normal (Table 1). Hematology was consulted and performed a bone marrow biopsy showing hypocellularity (10–20 %) with left shifted maturation and megakaryocytic hyperplasia without evidence of hematologic malignancy. Hematology initiated romiplostim 3.6 mg/kg, GCSF 480mcg, prednisone 1 mg/kg, and platelet transfusions and her counts improved (Table 1). An inferior vena cava filter was placed for management of her venous thromboembolism. She underwent primary cytoreductive surgery, including low anterior resection with rectosigmoid anastomosis, achieving optimal cytoreduction based on operative report. Pathology confirmed Stage IIIC2 PPC. Post-operatively, she developed a colonic anastomotic dehiscence with rectovaginal fistula, requiring return to the operating room on post-operative day 11 for diverting end colostomy and Hartman’s pouch creation. Despite hematologic treatment with romiplostim, GCSF, and prednisone, profound persistent pancytopenia led to transfer to our tertiary care center.

She developed a pelvic abscess and Hartman's pouch necrosis. Surgery was deferred due to persistent pancytopenia; a pelvic drain was placed, and broad-spectrum antibiotics administered. After 38 days, imaging confirmed source control, and the drain was removed. Antibiotics, in addition to prophylaxis against Pneumocystis Jiroveci and Herpes simplex virus was continued given her low absolute neutrophil count (ANC).

Hematology was consulted at time of transfer and repeat bone marrow biopsy showed left shifted, myeloid-predominant trilineage hematopoiesis and erythroid and megakaryocytic hypoplasia, a CD34 + cell count < 0.02 %, and no cytogenetic or molecular abnormalities (Fig. 1). This supported a diagnosis of autoimmune paraneoplastic aplastic anemia associated with her PPC. She received prednisone, romiplostim, and filgrastim; however, due to refractory pancytopenia, she was initiated on an AA specific regimen of ATG, eltrompobag, and cyclosporine (Peffault de Latour et al., 2022). Expected recovery from ATG intervention was known to be 12–16 weeks, thus multidisciplinary communication was essential to support her counts while receiving chemotherapy through the waiting period prior to her expected count recovery (Peffault de Latour et al., 2022).Fig. 1Patients Bone Marrow Biopsy.

The patient remained clinically stable with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 2, and chemotherapy targeting the underlying malignancy was initiated after multidisciplinary discussion. The decision to proceed with chemotherapy in the setting of her pancytopenia was challenging and based heavily on the patient’s stable clinical status despite poor counts. Given evidence supporting single-agent platinum-based therapy in patients with germline BRCA1 mutations, weekly, low-dose single-agent cisplatin 40 mg/m2/dose was selected due to its lower myelosuppressive profile compared to carboplatin (Wiedemeyer et al., 2014, Mangioni et al., 1989 Oct 4). She continued to have significant myelosuppression (see Table 1), requiring weekly packed red blood cells and platelet transfusions. However, she overall tolerated weekly cisplatin well with a marked decrease in her CA-125. Given her clinical stability, low-dose weekly paclitaxel was added with the second chemotherapy cycle. Twenty-one days after cycle 2, she developed hyponatremia and new atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular rate requiring intensive care unit (ICU) care. Work-up revealed bacteremia. Her atypical presentation was attributed to her severe immunocompromised state. The infectious etiology remained broad including possible central-line associated bloodstream infection, catheter-associated infection, or intra-abdominal infection, in addition to concern for typhlitis given computed tomography (CT) findings of increased small volume ascites, diffuse small bowel wall thickening and mucosal edema, and slight enlargement of intra-peritoneal and retro-peritoneal lymphadenopathy.

In the ICU, antibiotics were broadened, peripheral inserted central catheter line was removed, and blood cultures cleared within 7 days. The patient was stabilized and transferred back to the gynecologic oncology unit. Her later course was complicated by a gastrointestinal bleed, continued pancytopenia (Table 1), deconditioning, and decline in her mental and emotional status. After goals-of-care discussions with the patient and her family, she chose to discontinue further therapy, transitioned to home hospice, and died 14 days later.

Discussion

This case is notable for the diagnosis of severe, refractory aplastic anemia (AA) at the time of cancer diagnosis, in the absence of prior chemotherapy or other known etiologies. Clinicians caring for patients with PPC or ovarian cancer should be aware that rare hematologic paraneoplastic syndromes, such as AA, may present concurrently with cancer and significantly complicate management. Recognition of aplastic anemia is critical, as it can profoundly influence treatment decisions, including timing of surgery, selection of chemotherapy, and early engagement of a multi-disciplinary team.

Acquired AA is an immune-mediated disorder characterized by hypocellular bone marrow and pancytopenia, resulting from T-lymphocyte mediated destruction of hematopoietic stem cells (Aplastic, 2018). Our patient met criteria for very severe AA, with marrow cellularity of 10–20 % and marked depression of all three hematopoietic lineages below outlined thresholds (absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 0.2 x 103/L; platelets < 20 x 109/L; reticulocytes < 60 x 109) (DeZern AE, Smith C. Acquired marrow failure syndromes: aplastic anemia, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and myelodysplastic syndromes, American Society of Hematology Self-Assessment Program., 2019, González, et al., 2022).

While, to our knowledge, there are no other known published cases of AA secondary to epithelial ovarian or primary peritoneal carcinoma, a case report from a patient with Hodgkins's lymphoma is available (Pedrosa et al., 2021 Feb). Both our patient and the Hodgkin’s Lymphoma patient report were diagnosed with AA at the time of their initial cancer diagnoses. Our patient had no evidence of spontaneous hemorrhage at initial cancer diagnosis, with her only notable physical exam finding being easy bruising and petechiae on her shins. Her diagnosis prompted initiation of steroids, romiplostim, and GCSF; however, refractory cytopenias required escalation with the addition of ATG and cyclosporine, which were initially deferred due to her infections.. Based on a phase 3 randomized trial, the combined immunosuppressive and marrow stimulating regimen of horse ATG, cyclosporine, and eltrombopag is used for patients with severe or very severe AA, who are not eligible for hematopoietic stem cell transplant (Peffault de Latour et al., 2022). While she was initially treated with romiplostim, often used in critically ill patients due to its subcutaneous administration, once AA was diagnosed, TPO mimetic therapy was changed to eltrombopag based on guidelines.(Peffault de Latour et al., 2022). Although GCSF is not routinely administered in the treatment of acquired AA due to theoretical concerns of precipitating neoplastic myeloid clones, in our case, it was initiated due to the initial diagnostic uncertainty, and subsequently, due to the development of severe infections. By comparison, the lymphoma patient ultimately succumbed to septic shock and multiorgan failure after 59 days of admission (Pedrosa et al., 2021 Feb).

Malignant paraneoplastic syndromes often confer a poor prognosis, partly due to the complexity of treating the syndrome and associated cancer simultaneously (Madariaga, 2019). Traditionally, treatment of a solid tumor malignancy is with both surgery and chemotherapy, which also treats the associated paraneoplastic syndrome (Pelosof and Gerber, 2010 Sep). In our patient, proceeding with upfront surgical management was reasonable given concern for exacerbation of her cytopenias by cytotoxic chemotherapy and count recovery observed immediately prior to surgery. However, given our patient's post-operative course, the decision to proceed with cytoreductive surgery and colonic resection with anastomosis in the setting of aplastic anemia merits discussion. Significant immunosuppression and compromised wound healing secondary to bone marrow dysfunction may have compromised the colonic anastomosis and contributed to infectious post-operative complications. A more conservative approach, such as low-dose neoadjuvant chemotherapy may have preserved physiologic reserve and altered her clinical course. However, we observed that administration and selection of chemotherapy in this setting remains challenging.

Notably, our patient demonstrated marked initial response to cisplatin, with a significant decline in her CA-125. Given her age, performance status, and tolerance of the first cisplatin cycle, the decision was made in concert with the hematology team to proceed with standard platinum doublet chemotherapy, adding a low dose of weekly paclitaxel to the regimen. The medical literature supports the central role of platinum agents in the management of ovarian cancer and PPC, particularly in patients with germline BRCA mutations. These patients experience improved survival outcomes and greater tumor responsiveness to platinum-based chemotherapy compared to those without BRCA mutations (Alsop, et al., 2012). Given this patient’s initial response to cisplatin and her underlying BRCA1 mutation, she may have achieved similar disease control with single-agent platinum therapy, particularly in the setting of significant pancytopenia. While the addition of paclitaxel is standard, single-agent platinum may be a reasonable alternative in select patients, especially when toxicity is a concern.

Additionally, this case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for infection in patients with pancytopenia, as their ability to mount inflammatory or immune responses is impaired. Common signs of infection such as fever, tachycardia, and leukocytosis may be absent. In our patient, early signs of bacteremia in the latter part of her clinical course were limited to hyponatremia and later atrial fibrillation with RVR. Initially, her hyponatremia was attributed to a medication change. However, persistence despite medication discontinuation and new-onset atrial fibrillation with RVR is what ultimately prompted an infectious work-up. While hyponatremia is most commonly associated with infections such as tuberculosis, pneumonia, HIV, bacterial meningitis, leptospirosis, malaria, and leishmaniasis, it can also reflect severity and morbidity in bacterial sepsis (Liamis, 2011). In this case, hyponatremia may have been an early indicator of her clinical deterioration.

Finally, despite upfront cytoreductive surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, we did not observe improvement in this patient’s aplastic anemia with treatment of the underlying malignancy. She continued to require near daily transfusions until discharge. However, it is important to note hospice care was initiated at the patient’s and her family’s request 4 weeks before the expected timeline for count recovery from ATG therapy (Peffault de Latour et al., 2022). Thus, her ultimate bone marrow response to cancer-directed treatment remains unknown.

Conclusion

In summary, we present what is, to our knowledge, the first reported case of paraneoplastic aplastic anemia secondary to high-grade serous primary peritoneal carcinoma. The diagnosis of aplastic anemia was confirmed by bone marrow biopsy, and the patient was managed with a standard triple immunosuppressive regimen, alongside intensive transfusion support with near-daily platelet and red blood cell transfusions. Despite these challenges, she underwent cytoreductive surgery and initiated chemotherapy. However, the development of major post-operative complications, including a colonic anastomotic leak, abdominal abscesses and subsequent surgery in the setting of persistent and profound myelosuppression ultimately necessitated the permanent discontinuation of cytotoxic chemotherapy.

This case underscores the critical importance of a coordinated, multidisciplinary approach involving gynecologic oncology, medical oncology, hematology, and infectious disease specialists to navigate the complex interplay of malignancy, paraneoplastic syndromes, and treatment-related toxicities and complications. It also highlights the unique therapeutic dilemmas and medical complexities encountered in managing paraneoplastic aplastic anemia in advanced gynecologic malignancy, emphasizing the need for collaboration, individualized care strategies, and further research into this rare but challenging clinical scenario.
