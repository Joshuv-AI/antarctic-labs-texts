Exploring therapeutic targets and molecular mechanisms for treating diabetes mellitus-associated heart failure with Qishen Yiqi dropping pills: A network pharmacology and bioinformatics approach
Authors: Yirong Ma, Junyu Lai, Zhengtao Chen, Qiang Wan, Xianlin Shi, Hao Zhou, Jiaming Li, Zurong Yang, Jianguang Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus (DM) and heart failure frequently coexist, presenting significant public health challenges. QiShenYiQi Dropping Pills (QSDP) are widely employed in the treatment of diabetes mellitus concomitant with heart failure (DM-HF). Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying their efficacy have yet to be elucidated. Active ingredients and likely targets of QSDP were retrieved from the TCMSP and UniProt databases. Genes associated with DM-HF were pinpointed through searches in the GeneCards, OMIM, DisGeNET, and TTD databases. Differential genes connected to DM-HF were sourced from the GEO database. Enrichment analyses via gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways, as well as immune infiltration assessments, were conducted using R software. Further analysis involved employing molecular docking strategies to explore the interactions between the identified targets and active substances in QSDP that are pertinent to DM-HF treatment. This investigation effectively discerned 108 active compounds and 257 targets relevant to QSDP. A protein–protein interaction network was constructed, highlighting 6 central targets for DM-HF treatment via QSDP. Gene ontology enrichment analysis predominantly linked these targets with responses to hypoxia, metabolism of reactive oxygen species, and cytokine receptor interactions. Analysis of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways demonstrated that these targets mainly participate in pathways linked to diabetic complications, such as AGE-RAGE signaling, dyslipidemia, arteriosclerosis, the HIF-1 signaling pathway, and the tumor necrosis factor signaling pathway. Further, immune infiltration analysis implied that QSDP’s mechanism in treating DM-HF might involve immune-mediated inflammation and crucial signaling pathways. Additionally, molecular docking studies showed that the active substances in QSDP have strong binding affinities with these identified targets. This research presents a new model for addressing DM-HF through the use of QSDP, providing novel insights into incorporating traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) principles in the clinical treatment of DM-HF. The implications of these findings are substantial for both clinical application and further scientific inquiry.

1. Introduction

Heart failure (HF), characterized by significantly diminished cardiac pumping capacity, leads to reduced circulatory efficiency and manifests in symptoms like difficulty breathing and extreme fatigue, which severely impact patients’ quality of life.[1] HF, a rapidly expanding global health concern, currently affects over 64 million individuals worldwide and significantly strains healthcare systems.[2,3] Diabetes mellitus (DM), an independent risk factor for HF, can increase the risk of HF development 2 to 4-fold. Studies indicate that DM accelerates the deterioration of cardiac structure and function, and exacerbates HF symptoms through mechanisms like cardiomyocyte glucotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses. These findings underscore the importance of managing DM and its complications to prevent and treat HF.[4–6] With the aging population, the prevalence of HF, particularly among diabetic patients, is anticipated to rise, emphasizing the need for targeted treatment and management strategies for this group. Current treatments for diabetes mellitus concomitant with heart failure (DM-HF), including dapagliflozin, liraglutide, and valsartan, often lead to complications such as electrolyte imbalances, renal impairment, and gastrointestinal side effects.[7–9] Consequently, developing treatments with fewer side effects is a critical and urgent need.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) primarily utilizes natural medicinal substances, including herbs, minerals, and components derived from animals. These substances are capable of enhancing patients’ quality of life and modifying the medicinal properties through both traditional and contemporary processing methods, which reduce toxicity and thus improve safety.[10] QiShenYiQi Dropping Pills (QSDP), a traditional Chinese patent medicine based on TCM principles, comprises ingredients like Astragalus, Salvia, Panax notoginseng, and Borneol. This formula is designed to invigorate Qi and promote blood circulation. Extensive clinical research indicates that QSDP effectively enhances blood circulation, dilates coronary arteries, improves myocardial blood supply, and boosts cardiac pumping function. Furthermore, QSDP exhibits antiplatelet aggregation properties, aiding in thrombosis prevention. These pharmacological benefits show substantial potential for treating chronic HF.[11,12] Additionally, Astragalus is reputed to improve pancreatic β-cell function and reduce blood glucose levels, while Salvia is known to enhance microcirculation and mitigate diabetic vascular complications. Together, these properties assist diabetic patients in managing blood glucose levels and reducing complication risks.[13,14] Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms through which QSDP treats DM-HF are yet to be fully understood.

Network pharmacology, an emerging discipline, integrates knowledge from systems biology, pharmacology, computational biology, and bioinformatics. It aims to analyze and manipulate complex biological networks, thereby facilitating the precise development and application of pharmaceuticals. With ongoing advancements in bioinformatics, network pharmacology leverages these tools and databases to explore the extensive interactions between drugs and multiple biological targets. This approach transcends the traditional “one target, one drug” model, advocating a shift towards a “multiple targets, one drug” research paradigm. Consequently, network pharmacology can effectively predict drug efficacy, potential side effects, discover new drug applications, and optimize combination therapies, offering novel insights and tools for tackling complex diseases.[15–17] In this research, network pharmacology and bioinformatics were combined to determine the primary targets and pathways implicated in the use of QSDP for treating DM-HF. Furthermore, the molecular targets of QSDP specifically for DM-HF treatment were identified. The procedural workflow is illustrated in Figure 1. Such analysis deepens our understanding of QSDP’s pharmacological properties, providing both theoretical support and practical guidance for its clinical use. Our findings are poised to offer significant references for developing new treatment strategies, crafting effective medications, and enhancing the quality of life for patients with DM-HF.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Components and targets of QSDP

In the study, the herbs comprising QSDP—Huangqi, Danshen, Jiangxiang, and Sanqi—were identified as search terms in the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database (TCMSP: https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php).[18] This search yielded all components and their respective targets for each herb, following the established criteria of oral bioavailability ≥30% and drug-likeness ≥18%. The names of these targets were then normalized to gene names through the use of the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/).[19]

2.2. Identification of DM-HF targets

Employing “diabetes mellitus” and “heart failure” as keywords, the study identified targets by querying several databases: GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/),[20] Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) (https://omim.org/),[21] DisGENET (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/),[22] and the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD) (http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/).[23] GeneCards was filtered to include only entries with a relevance score of 10 or higher; DisGENET required a minimum score of 0.1. Subsequently, results were standardized to gene names using the UniProt database. Duplicate gene entries were removed, and overlapping targets for DM and HF were consolidated using the R package Venn.

2.3. Selection of DM-HF differential genes

A thorough search was performed in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using the keywords “diabetes combined with heart failure,” which resulted in the retrieval of the gene expression dataset GSE26887. Using the limma package in R, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined. The screening criteria were set at an absolute |log FC|>0.5 and adjusted P-value <.05.

2.4. QSDP targets in DM-HF

The disease-associated targets were integrated with the DEGs. Subsequently, Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html) was used to illustrate the convergence of these overlapping targets with known QSDP targets. This approach aims to identify potential targets through which QSDP may exert therapeutic effects on DM-HF.

2.5. Construction of the “Drug-Component-Target” network

The network was constructed using Cytoscape 3.10.1,[24] featuring diseases, drugs, components, and related targets as nodes. The interrelationships among these nodes were represented as edges, forming the Drug-Component-Target network.

2.6. Construction of protein interaction networks

The intersecting target list was submitted to the STRING database, specifying “Homo sapiens” as the organism and setting the minimum interaction confidence score to 0.4. Additionally, the parameters were adjusted to exclude disconnected nodes. The resultant TSV file was then imported into Cytoscape 3.10.1 software to analyze and visualize the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. Subsequently, the MCODE plugin was utilized to detect highly connected subnetworks. To discern central genes, the cytoHubba plugin was employed to calculate Degree Centrality (DC), Betweenness Centrality (BC), and Closeness Centrality (CC). This approach enabled us to identify key genes within the network, which may play crucial roles in the interaction between QSDP and the human body.

2.7. GO and KEGG analysis

To explore the potential effects of QSDP on DM-HF, we utilized the R package clusterProfiler to conduct gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, identifying pathways with P < .05 as significant. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis was applied to the DEGs in the GSE26887 dataset, and the results were visualized using the “ggplot2” package.

2.8. Immune infiltration analysis

To explore the infiltration of immune cells in DM-HF, the proportions of 22 different immune cell types were determined using the CIBERSORT deconvolution approach, which leverages gene expression data.[25] This evaluation utilized the DEGs in dataset GSE26887. For the visualization of these findings, the R packages “corrplot,” “ggplot2,” and “ggpubr” were employed. Additionally, the relationship between primary biomarkers and the expression levels of infiltrating immune cells was assessed using the Spearman nonparametric correlation coefficient.

2.9. Molecular docking

In this study, utilizing the framework of a herbal-compound-target network, 5 principal constituents of QSDP were discerned and their potential therapeutic targets were ascertained via molecular docking with 8 pivotal genes. PubChem IDs and three-dimensional chemical structures were acquired from the PubChem database; these structures were then transformed into PDB files using the software Open Babel.[26] The crystal structures of proteins, sourced from the Protein Data Bank,[27] were converted into pdbqt files employing PYMOL, and underwent dehydration and hydrogenation using the Autodock tool, paving the way for subsequent molecular docking. Visualization of the molecular docking outcomes was carried out with PYMOL, leading to the identification of the most efficacious QSDP components for combating DM-HF. The selection criterion was predicated on the notion that a lower binding energy suggests more robust ligand–receptor interactions.

3. Results

3.1. Collection of QSDP components and targets

We obtained 108 active ingredients and 257 genes from the TCMSP database (Set 1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N277). We illustrated the distribution of active chemical components in QSDP medicinal materials using a Venn diagram (Fig. 2A). We gathered 475 targets related to DM-HF from GeneCards, 115 from DisGeNET, 7 from OMIM, 7 from TTD and 229 from GEO. After merging and removing duplicates, we identified 743 disease-related targets and isolated 90 intersecting target genes with drugs. We then developed a network of Traditional Chinese Medicine compound-target genes utilizing Cytoscape 3.10.1 software, pinpointing quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol, formononetin, and isorhamnetin as crucial nodes (Fig. 2B).

Differential gene expression analysis was performed to identify genes associated with DM-HF by comparing gene expression profiles between a normal cohort and DM-HF patients using the dataset GSE26884; this analysis revealed 153 upregulated and 76 downregulated genes (Set 2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N277). Furthermore, a volcano plot and a heatmap were constructed to illustrate the top 50 DEGs (Fig. 3A and B).

Volcano plot and Heatmap of the DEGs identified from GSE26887. Volcano map of DEGs from GSE26887 (A). Heatmap of DEGs from GSE26887 (B). DEGs = differentially expressed genes.

We cross-referenced the DEGs with disease targets obtained from the GeneCards, OMIM, TTD, and DisGeNET databases, then compared these targets with those of QSDP. This approach identified 90 intersecting targets (Set 3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N277).

3.2. PPI network

To delve deeper into the mechanisms by which QSDP impacts DM-HF, we integrated 90 intersecting targets into the STING database and constructed a PPI network (Fig. 4A). The resulting network, analyzed using Cytoscape 3.10.1 software, included 90 nodes and 2050 edges, with average DC, BC, and CC values of 45.578, 0.0057, and 0.684, respectively. Utilizing the CytoHubba plugin, we identified the top 10 targets through measures such as DC, BC, and CC, and then intersected these targets (Fig. 4D–F). Using a threshold of 2.5 times the average BC value, we ultimately identified 6 central targets (Fig. 4C). Subsequent cluster analysis conducted with the MCODE plugin yielded a densely interconnected subnetwork (Fig. 4B).

3.3. GO enrichment analysis

In pursuit of a more comprehensive understanding of QSDP’s mechanisms in addressing DM-HF, we performed a GO enrichment analysis on the 90 intersecting targets. This analysis revealed significant enrichment in 2387 biological processes (BP), 61 cellular components, and 147 molecular functions, all statistically significant with a P-value <.05 (Set 4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N277). The enriched terms were visualized using bar graphs created in R, emphasizing the top 10 enriched terms across BP, molecular functions, and cellular components. The GO analysis revealed that genes associated with BP are predominantly enriched in responses to oxidative stress and hypoxia, wound healing, epithelial cell proliferation, and responses to bacteria and lipopolysaccharides. Genes related to cellular components are enriched in membrane rafts and microdomains, extracellular matrix components and the external side of the membrane, as well as vesicle lumens. Meanwhile, molecular function-related genes are involved in transcription factor binding, cytokine activity and receptor binding, and kinase regulator activity (Fig. 5A and B).

3.4. KEGG enrichment analysis

We performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for 90 intersecting targets and 229 DEGs (Set 5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N277, Set 6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N277). The analysis indicated that the intersecting targets are significantly enriched in pathways associated with diabetic complications, including the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 5C). The DEGs are predominantly enriched in pathways linked to Alzheimer disease, motor protein functions, bacterial infections, and the TGF-beta signaling pathway. These results indicate that QSDP potentially achieves its therapeutic outcomes by influencing various pathways associated with metabolic functions and immune-inflammatory responses (Fig. 5D).

3.5. Immune infiltration analysis

To further explore the association between QSDP treatment for DM-HF and the immune microenvironment, we performed an analysis of immune cell infiltration on the DEGs present in the GSE26887 dataset. This investigation employed the LM22 reference matrix and the CIBERSORT deconvolution method, executed with 1000 permutations, using R programming language. This analysis demonstrated that DM-HF patients display increased proportions of memory B cells and naive CD4 T cells, whereas the proportions of activated CD4 memory T cells, activated natural killer cells, M1 macrophages, resting mast cells, and activated mast cells are reduced (Fig. 6A and B).

This technique replicates ligand–receptor interactions, encompassing electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces, to forecast binding modes and affinities between proteins, or between small molecules and proteins. Consequently, it facilitates the identification of active components and the virtual screening of potential drug targets. Such techniques are crucial for predicting active pharmaceutical ingredients and for virtually screening potential drug targets. 4. Discussion

DM serves as an independent risk factor for HF and frequently coexists with HF in clinical settings. Research shows that in individuals with DM, prolonged metabolic disturbances and continuous hyperglycemia induce a range of pathological changes, including endothelial dysfunction and accelerated arteriosclerosis.[28] The primary mechanisms driving these changes are increased oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, which impair endothelial function, disrupting the normal function and structure of blood vessels. Specifically, chronic high blood glucose levels harm endothelial cells, diminishing their capacity to produce nitric oxide, an essential vasodilator that maintains vascular health. This persistent endothelial dysfunction is further exacerbated by oxidative stress, which degrades vascular health and accelerates arteriosclerosis. Consequently, these pathological alterations substantially elevate the risk of HF and other cardiovascular diseases.[28–30] Additionally, DM directly compromises cardiac structure and function, which can lead to myocardial hypertrophy, ventricular dilation, and ventricular dysfunction. These specific changes, independent of coronary artery disease and hypertension, may progress to diabetic-specific cardiomyopathy, thereby aggravating the advancement of HF.[31–33] Inflammation plays a crucial role in the development of HF among individuals with DM. A prolonged state of high blood glucose can trigger a systemic inflammatory response; this chronic inflammation enhances the expression of inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and C-reactive protein, which promote myocardial cell injury and dysfunction. This inflammatory process involves various cell types and signaling pathways, including endothelial cells, macrophages, and myocardial cells. These cells interact through the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and extracellular vesicles, intensifying myocardial disease and fibrosis, and thereby exacerbating HF.[34–36] Consequently, understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms, such as the roles of inflammation pathways and oxidative stress, is crucial for addressing HF induced by DM. Exploring potential pharmacological interventions that address these mechanisms may facilitate the formulation of enhanced treatment approaches.

In the research presented, the network analysis of drug-ingredient-target interactions and molecular docking studies identified quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol, formononetin, and isorhamnetin as the 5 principal active compounds in QSDP. Quercetin, a critical flavonoid, demonstrates multifaceted benefits including the reduction of blood pressure, lipids, and glucose levels, alongside antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial activities, and cardiovascular protection.[37] It reduces blood glucose levels by inhibiting α-glucosidase activity, which slows glucose absorption in the intestines. Additionally, quercetin has been shown to potentially enhance the insulin signaling cascade, augment the uptake of glucose mediated by insulin, and elevate the efficiency of glucose utilization in various tissues.[38,39] Quercetin also suppresses NF-κB activation, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, which mitigates inflammation in myocardial cells and safeguards cardiac function. Moreover, quercetin enhances cellular antioxidant enzyme activity, scavenges free radicals, reduces oxidative damage, and thereby decreases HF risk.[40] Luteolin, another flavonoid, prevalent in various plants, has shown promising effects in animal studies. It activates the AMP-activated protein kinase pathway, which enhances insulin sensitivity and promotes glucose uptake. Furthermore, luteolin aids in protecting pancreatic β-cells from damage and apoptosis—cells critical for insulin production. Additionally, luteolin aids in maintaining normal insulin secretion by mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby helping to regulate blood sugar levels.[41–43] It also regulates the metabolism of myocardial cells and reduces myocardial cell damage through activation of the Akt/Nrf2 signaling pathway.[44,45] Research has shown that kaempferol decreases levels of reactive oxygen species and inflammatory markers, attributing to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.[46] It also curbs sugar absorption by inhibiting intestinal α-glucosidase activity, contributing to blood sugar control. Regarding insulin metabolism, kaempferol potentially enhances insulin secretion and improves insulin sensitivity by influencing key molecules such as AMP-activated protein kinase and PI3K/Akt.[47,48] In the context of HF, kaempferol protects cardiac tissues by preventing cardiomyocyte apoptosis, promoting cardiomyocyte survival, and improving cardiac function through pathways like PI3K/Akt and ERK1/2.[49] Formononetin, an isoflavone derived from medicinal plants and herbs like red clover and Astragalus, is effective in decreasing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. It achieves this by blocking the activation of the NF-κB pathway and the release of histamine.[50,51] Through its anti-inflammatory effects, formononetin not only enhances pancreatic function and insulin sensitivity in diabetic patients but also diminishes myocardial inflammation and fibrosis. Consequently, the comprehensive anti-inflammatory properties of formononetin may hold significant therapeutic value for individuals with DM-HF. Isorhamnetin, prevalent in a variety of plants, demonstrates extensive pharmacological activities. Studies have shown that isorhamnetin stimulates the JAK2/STAT pathway, enhancing glucose uptake by facilitating the translocation of GLUT4 across various signaling pathways in skeletal muscle cells. This action helps in averting hyperglycemia and sustaining glucose homeostasis.[52] Furthermore, isorhamnetin offers cardioprotective effects by inhibiting lactate dehydrogenase-triggered apoptosis via the NF-κB signaling pathway, reducing ischemia–reperfusion injury in ventricular myocytes. Angiotensin II (AngII), a key regulator in cardiac fibroblasts (CFb), attaches to receptors on CFb, prompting their proliferation and the synthesis of extracellular matrix. Isorhamnetin counters AngII-driven CFb proliferation and collagen production in a dose-dependent manner, thus averting myocardial fibrosis linked to HF.[53,54]

This investigation employed protein interaction network analysis to examine the relationships among various potential drug targets and established disease indicators, which is essential for uncovering the molecular bases of complex diseases. We pinpointed 8 central targets—INS, IL6, TNF, ALB, IL1B, PPARG, CCL2, and IGF1—using 3 criteria: DC, BC, and CC. These findings suggest that QSDP could be effective in treating DM-HF by modulating inflammatory responses and metabolic control. IL-6 (interleukin-6), TNF, and IL1B are key cytokines integral to the inflammatory process and the regulation of immune functions. IL-6, recognized for its versatile roles, is critical in immune reactions, inflammation, and hematopoiesis. This cytokine is produced by a range of cells such as T cells, B cells, monocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells, plays a dual role in modulating inflammation and immune responses. While it promotes inflammation to combat infections, its imbalance may result in autoimmune disorders. IL-6 exhibits both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory properties, thus intricately influencing the behavior of other cytokines in immune responses.[55,56] TNF is primarily produced by macrophages and is crucial for regulating immune cells, inducing fever, apoptosis, and inflammation, as well as inhibiting tumor growth and viral replication. Excessive TNF production is associated with various diseases, including autoimmune diseases, insulin resistance, and cardiac injury. TNF is a major mediator of acute phase responses and is part of the body’s reaction to injury or infection.[57,58] Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) is another potent pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays a critical role in the body’s response to infection and injury. It is mainly produced by activated macrophages and can induce cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. IL-1β is key in the development of inflammatory diseases and various cellular activities, including the regulation of immune responses. Similar to TNF, IL-1β is significant in acute phase responses and, if uncontrolled, can drive chronic inflammation, affecting conditions such as DM and HF.[59] AKT1 is an important serine/threonine kinase involved in regulating cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism. It plays a central role in insulin signaling. Upon insulin stimulation, AKT1 is activated via the PI3K/AKT pathway, promoting the translocation of the GLUT4 transporter and thereby increasing cellular glucose uptake.[60] Studies show that cardiac-specific overexpression of AKT1 can induce physiological myocardial hypertrophy, enhancing cardiac function. Additionally, AKT1 maintains normal cardiac function in HF by regulating energy metabolism and fatty acid utilization.[61,62] Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG) is critical for regulating fat metabolism and insulin sensitivity. As a nuclear receptor family member, PPARG is a key regulator of insulin sensitivity. By activating PPARG, the uptake and storage of fatty acids in adipocytes are promoted, plasma free fatty acid concentrations are reduced, insulin signaling is enhanced, and insulin resistance is improved, thereby regulating blood glucose levels.[63] Additionally, PPARG has anti-inflammatory effects and can reduce tissue inflammatory responses by decreasing the production of inflammatory cytokines. In cardiovascular diseases, PPARG slows disease progression by inhibiting inflammatory cell activity within atherosclerotic plaques.[64] JUN is a significant transcription factor widely involved in BP such as cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, inflammation, and metabolism. The JUN gene regulates the expression of target genes by forming dimers or multimers with other proteins and binding to DNA. As a crucial member of the AP-1 transcription factor family, JUN typically forms heterodimers with proteins such as FOS or ATF, recognizing and binding to specific AP-1 binding sites to initiate or inhibit gene transcription activities.[65,66]

According to GO and KEGG enrichment analysis results, QSDP’s therapeutic effects on DM-HF involve multiple pathways. BP genes are primarily enriched in oxidative stress and hypoxia responses, wound healing, epithelial cell proliferation, and responses to bacteria and lipopolysaccharides. The regulation of these processes impacts tissue damage and inflammatory responses in DM and its complications, as well as the survival and function of cardiomyocytes in HF patients. Furthermore, the enrichment of CC genes in cellular components such as membrane rafts, microdomains, extracellular matrix, membrane exterior, and vesicle lumen indicates crucial adjustments in cellular responses to external signals, material transport, and processing. These adjustments play a significant role in cellular stress responses induced by DM and cardiac remodeling in HF. Additionally, we observed significant enrichment of molecular function-related genes in transcription factor binding, cytokine activity, receptor binding, and kinase regulatory activity. Changes in these molecular functions provide a biological basis for inflammatory responses and cellular damage in DM complications and HF. The KEGG enrichment analysis of cross-targets primarily involves the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, pathways related to dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis, and the activation of TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways, demonstrating their close association with metabolic abnormalities in DM, inflammatory responses in HF, and changes in cardiac structure and function. In DM complications, advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) and their receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) play a pivotal role, especially in the development of cardiovascular diseases. The high-glucose environment in diabetic patients promotes an increase in AGEs, which, upon binding to RAGE, activate multiple cellular signaling pathways, leading to oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and cellular dysfunction, thereby exacerbating the pathological progression of DM. Research has shown that levels of the advanced glycation end-product (AGE), Nε-carboxymethyl lysine, are higher in individuals suffering from obesity and DM. The interaction between Nε-carboxymethyl lysine and the RAGE plays a critical role in promoting inflammation and insulin resistance associated with obesity.[67] Another study highlights the crucial role of AGEs in the progression of diabetic complications, where their accumulation in the extracellular matrix leads to structural abnormalities and cellular dysfunction, constituting a critical pathogenic mechanism in both microvascular and macrovascular complications.[68] Dyslipidemia, a common complication in diabetic patients, often characterized by elevated triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, poses major risk factors for atherosclerosis. Atherosclerosis impairs cardiovascular function by reducing vascular elasticity and obstructing blood flow, playing a key role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases such as HF. Research demonstrates that atherosclerosis is primarily a chronic inflammatory condition affecting arteries, characterized by the accumulation of lesions within the subendothelial space. This leads to the narrowing of arterial walls and disruption of normal blood flow. This vascular narrowing and blood flow obstruction directly reduce myocardial perfusion, increasing cardiac workload and thus promoting the development of HF.[69,70] Therefore, managing dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis is crucial in preventing HF. Inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-17 play a key role in the pathogenesis of DM-HF. Specifically, overexpression of TNF-α is closely associated with the development of insulin resistance in DM, potentially due to an intensified inflammatory response. Conversely, IL-17 mainly modulates cardiac inflammation and fibrosis, directly affecting cardiac structure and function, which may accelerate the progression of HF. Therefore, activation of these cytokines not only exacerbates the inflammatory state in DM but may also lead to HF by affecting pathological changes in the heart.[71,72] These findings emphasize that regulating TNF-α and IL-17 could be an important therapeutic approach in treating DM-HF. The aberrant activation of these pathways not only advances the disease but also provides potential molecular targets, offering significant directions for the development of treatment strategies for DM-HF. A detailed study of these key signaling pathways and their gene expressions enables a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of these complex diseases, thus aiding in the formulation of more effective treatment strategies. This research underscores the importance of developing therapies targeting these biomarkers, providing a scientific basis for future treatment strategies.

In this study, we utilized molecular docking techniques to examine the interactions between key active components and core targets. In molecular docking analysis, binding energy is a crucial metric for evaluating the strength of interactions between 2 molecules, with lower binding energies indicating stronger interaction forces and higher binding affinity. Negative binding energies suggest a spontaneous binding tendency between the molecule and the target. Specifically, binding energies below 0 kcal/mol are indicative of spontaneous interactions. A binding energy of <‐5 kcal/mol is often considered biologically significant, signifying a high affinity between the molecule and the target, and suggesting potent drug activity.[73] In our simulations, all observed binding energies were below ‐5 kcal/mol, highlighting the robust interactions and substantial biological activity potential of the studied compounds with their respective targets. Thus, these compounds are highly promising drug candidates, meriting further biological validation and pharmacological research. The findings of this study lay a strong scientific groundwork for further experimental validation and drug development efforts.

Network pharmacology is a field that employs bioinformatics tools and biomedical databases to explore drug-biological organism interactions. Despite its potential to elucidate drug action mechanisms, several challenges persist. Primarily, network pharmacology depends on the quality and accuracy of databases, which can affect the reliability of analytical results. Additionally, while molecular docking techniques commonly validate drug-target interactions, they often neglect the dose–response relationship—the correlation between a drug’s efficacy and its concentration. Moreover, network pharmacology predictions typically rely on static biological networks, omitting the temporal dynamics of drug actions crucial for simulating disease processes and evaluating treatment effects. Additionally, this methodology does not adequately account for cell and tissue specificity, nor does it consider the genetic and physiological variations that are fundamental to personalized medicine. This oversight contradicts the principles of TCM, which prioritize customized diagnosis and treatment based on individual differences. Furthermore, the complexities of combination drug therapies introduce challenges due to their potentially nonlinear interactions, which traditional network analysis methods may not adequately capture. Despite these limitations, advancements in network pharmacology continue. For instance, the precise analysis of TCM components using LC/MS technology and the development of more complex dynamic models through artificial intelligence algorithms are significant strides toward enhancing predictive reliability. Ultimately, these theoretical models must be substantiated through comprehensive experiments to verify their effectiveness and safety for practical use. Consequently, future efforts in this study will concentrate on experimental validation to affirm the practical applicability of these theoretical models, driving technological innovation and optimizing methods to improve research applicability and accuracy.

5. Conclusion

In our research, we determined the primary active constituents and central targets of QSDP for treating DM-HF by building a “Drug-Component-Target” network and a PPI network. Our enrichment analysis indicated that the treatment mechanism of QSDP for DM-HF involves various BP, such as inflammatory responses and metabolic pathways. Additionally, molecular docking studies showed that all central targets have strong binding affinities with QSDP’s key active ingredients, including quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol, formononetin, and isorhamnetin. Our findings illuminate the potential and mechanisms of QSDP in treating DM-HF at the molecular and pathway levels, offering novel insights into multi-target drug therapy for DM-HF.
