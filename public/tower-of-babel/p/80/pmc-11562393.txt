Multifocal malignant perivascular epithelioid cell tumor (PEComa) of the peritoneum: A case report
Authors: Farah Sassi, Nadia Boujelbene, Imen Abbes, Lamia Naija, Nayssem Khessairi, Karima Mrad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceNeoplasms with perivascular epithelioid-cell differentiation (PEComa) are rare. We present a case of multifocal malignant PEComas arising in the peritoneum.Case presentationA 61-year-old woman presented with a painful abdominal mass that had progressed over the past year and recently increased in size. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed two masses: an 11-cm partially solid and cystic tumor, with moderate contrast uptake probably originating from the left ovary, and an 18-cm sized, intraperitoneal abdominal heterogeneous enhancing mass. Two nodules in the mesentery and the bladder peritoneum were discovered intraoperatively. The patient underwent Hudson's intervention with resection of the epiploic mass and the two nodules. Microscopic examination revealed a tumor proliferation of epithelioid clear cells with delicate vascular septae. Immunohistochemistry was positive for HMB45, MelanA and SMA. The final diagnosis of a malignant PEComa was retained. The patient developed two local peritoneal recurrences and died after 26 months of follow-up.Clinical discussionOur case is original by its multifocal presentation being the seventh case of a multifocal malignant PEComa reported in the literature. PEComa's clinical presentation and radiographic appearance can often mimic a soft tissue clear cell sarcoma.ConclusionClinical and pathological correlations are mandatory to make a precise diagnosis and adapt the treatment of PEComas.

Introduction

Since Bonetti et al. [1] first proposed the entity of Perivascular Epithelioid cell (PEComa) in 1992, the category of PEComas has grown in recognition. The term PEComa refers to the cells that make up a group of cancers, most commonly lymphangiomyomatosis, angiomyolipoma, clear cell “sugar” tumors of the lung, hepatic falciform ligament clear cell myomelanocytic tumors, and soft-tissue clear cell myomelanocytic tumors [2,3]. PEComas are rare mesenchymal tumors with a distinctive immunoreactivity for melanocytic and smooth muscle markers [4,5]. Most of these neoplasms behave as benign or indolent lesions with an overall good prognosis, but a minority show variable malignant potential [4]. They have been described in a number of visceral and soft-tissue locations as a unique mass. Multifocal PEComas have only been reported in six cases, to the best of our knowledge [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Herein, we present the seventh case of a multifocal PEComa arising in the peritoneum of a 61-year-old female with overt malignancy. The aim of this study was to review clinicopathological features, emphasize on differential diagnosis and discuss therapeutic modalities and prognosis of this rare entity.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old woman with a history of diabete type II presented with the onset of a painful abdominal mass that had progressed over the past year and had recently increased in size.

Clinical examination was unremarkable without clinical signs of tuberous sclerosis. On abdominal examination, she had right iliac fossa tenderness and a firm and mobile abdominopelvic mass. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed large (25 × 18 × 13 cm), necrotic and highly vascularized abdomino-pelvic masses compressing adjacent structures. The ovaries and uterus could not be identified. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a partially solid and cystic tumor, with moderate contrast uptake thought to originate from the left ovary. Additionally, an 18-cm sized, second intraperitoneal abdominal heterogeneous enhancing mass was observed, suggesting a second localization. The esophagogastroduodenoscopy was normal. Serum levels of CA19–9 were elevated at 98.000 U/L (normal range < 35,000 U/L) and CA-125 level was elevated at 140.600 U/L (normal range < 35,000 U/L). ACE was within normal limits. The diagnosis of an ovarian tumor was suggested. The patient underwent an explorative laparotomy. Intraoperative findings showed a smooth surface mass arising from the omentum. This mass adhered to the last small intestine bowl and to the rectum without a cleavage path. It was associated with two others cystic masses landlocked in the Douglas. The patient underwent an excision of the omental mass as well as radical pelvic cytoreduction (Hudson intervention) with no macroscopic tumor residue left. On gross examination, the omental mass had a smooth surface, measured 23 × 21 × 11 cm and weighed 1.85 kg. The cut surfaces showed large yellowish foci, probably necrotic, and brownish areas. This mass was attached to a 12-cm intestinal segment with a regular mucosa. Pelvic exenteration showed a 10 × 7 × 3 cm mass between the rectum and uterus. Tumor's cut surfaces had cystic and solid areas containing fresh and old hemorrhage. Microscopic examination showed a partially necrotic neoplastic proliferation involving the muscularis propria of the small bowl. It was arranged in sheets and nests (Fig. 1) with richly vascularized stroma composed of thick-walled sometimes hyalinized vessels (Fig. 2). Tumor cells were epithelioid with abundant clear-eosinophilic cytoplasm (Fig. 3). Occasionally, the nuclei showed prominent nucleoli and had pseudo-inclusions with marked anisonucleosis (Fig. 4). Tumors cells showed significant mitotic activity (5 mitoses per 10 High Power Fields (HPF)). On immunohistochemistry, tumor cells were positive for HMB45, MelanA, SMA and focally EMA (Fig. 5). They were negative for AE1/AE3, S100 protein, CD-117 and CD34 ruling out a stromal tumor; Chromogranin and Synaptophysin ruling out a neuroendocrine tumor; Desmin and CD99 ruling out a rhabdomyosarcoma; PHOX2B, TTF1 and PAX8 ruling out an ovarian tumor; MITF ruling out a melanoma. There was no loss of expression of the INI1/SMARCB1 gene product. A weak focal nuclear expression of TFE3 was noted. The proliferation index Ki67 was estimated at 20 %. The second mass shared the same pathological patterns. The tumor adhered to the cervico-isthmic wall, without infiltrating it. It infiltrated the mesocolon, the mesovarium without reaching the ovarian parenchyma and the right parametrium. Two nodules, one in the mesentery and the other under the bladder peritoneum were metastatic. No lymph node metastases were found. The bilateral ovaries were unremarkable. Surgical limits were safe from tumoral invasion. The constellation of these clinical and pathological findings led to the diagnosis of a malignant multifocal PEComa. A thoracic CT scan was done and showed no distant metastases. The postoperative course was uneventful, no further treatment was needed and the patient was discharged 4 days after surgery. At 22 months of follow-up, the patient developed a local recurrence and underwent tumor re-excision. A second recurrence was observed three months later; however, the patient could not undergo surgery due to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Discussion

The World Health Organization of soft tissue tumors (WHO, 2020) defines PEComa as a mesenchymal tumor composed of histologically and immunohistochemically distinctive perivascular epithelioid cells [13]. The molecular basis of most PEComas is primarily associated with alterations in the tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC1) or (TSC2) genes, which are critical components of the mTOR signaling pathway. Around 20 % of PEComas lack TSC1/TSC2 mutations and are instead characterized by translocations involving the TFE3 gene which tends to have a more aggressive behavior [14,15].

PEComas show a marked female predominance with a wide range and a peak in young to middle-aged adults (median age: 45 years) [13]. PEComas growing extra-uterinally on extra-gynaecological tract normally present as a painless mass. When symptoms occur, they are often due to tumor size. Palpable mass and abdominal pain are the most common signs [16,17].

The kidneys, liver, lungs, and uterus are the most often affected organs by PEComa [18]. A few cases have also been observed in the bladder, prostate, ovaries, pancreas, and soft tissues [19,20]. Additional cases of PEComas have been documented in recent research in the cutaneous [21], stomach [22], gastrointestinal tracts [23], orbital [24], omentum [25], scrotum [26], mediastinal [27], and even adrenal glands [28]. Multifocal PEComas is extremely rare. Women in all the six cases reported so far range in age from 35 to 70 years. Most cases are complicated by tuberous sclerosis and impacted the uterus, cervix, and genital system. The pelvic cavity, wide ligament, uterus, and cervix were all involved in a case, although there was no proof of tuberous sclerosis [9]. In the present case, PEComas was diagnosed in a woman, involving the omentum, the douglas, the mesentery and the bladder peritoneum, without clinical sign of tuberous sclerosis.

PEComas appear grossly as a well-circumscribed masses with a solid, fibrous, or fleshy cut surface. Tumors display a wide variety in size (mean: 5-8 cm) [13].

On microscopic examination, these tumors grow into sheets or nests, composed of epithelioid or spindle-shaped cells with clear and granular cytoplasm, in a perivascular distribution. Only a small portion of tumors have predominantly spindle cell morphology. Sometimes the cytoplasm vacuolates giving the appearance of an adipocyte cell. While modest pleomorphism and occasional multinucleated cells may be present in a typical PEComa, mitotic figures are typically uncommon or absent. A combination of mitotic activity, necrosis, and pleomorphism characterizes malignant PEComas [3]. The morphological pattern in our case showed only epithelioid cells, with significant mitotic activity, cell pleomorphism and focal necrosis.

These tumors frequently co-express muscle markers such as SMA, Desmin, Caldesmon, and melanocytic markers such as HMB45 which is the most sensitive, near 50 % stain for melan-A and MITF [13]. In epithelioid tumor cells as opposed to spindled ones, melanocytic marker expression is more prevalent. SMA is frequently more extensively positive than Desmin and Caldesmon. Some tumors don't express muscle-specific markers. S100 nuclear expression is typically not seen in PEComas. TFE3 translocation (detected through either FISH or IHC) has significant nuclear staining in 20 % of cases. Younger patients are more likely to develop PEComas with TFE3 rearrangement which also show a strong alveolar growth pattern and epithelioid morphology and frequently lack the expression of smooth muscle markers [13]. In our case, the tumor was focally positive for TFE3.

In this case, SMA was expressed in tumor cells. Few PEComas may show focal AE1/AE3 expression but they do not show the diffuse cytokeratin expression seen in clear cell carcinomas. Here again, demonstration of melanocytic marker expression is very valuable in distinguishing PEComa from carcinoma [29,30]. Our case was diagnosed as a PEComa due to the histologic and immunohistochemical features.

The modified gynecologic–specific algorithm PEComa classification categorizes tumors as either: benign, uncertain malignant potential or malignant according to the presence of 4 out of 5 statistically significant features such as size >5 cm, high-grade atypia, mitosis >1/50 HPFs, necrosis and vascular invasion. However, we have yet to truly understand the true behavior of these tumors, because some tumors with “benign” appearances exhibit aggressive behavior, while others with “malignant” appearances exhibit indolent one. In our case, PEComas were classified as malignant due to a tumor size >5 cm, high-grade atypia, mitosis >1/50 HPFs and necrosis.

The differential diagnosis of PEComa depends on the site of origin. In the peritoneum, the malignant PEComas shall be differentiated from metastatic melanoma, metastatic renal clear cell carcinoma, paraganglioma, epithelioid leiomyosarcoma, extra-gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), and epithelioid clear cell mesothelioma.

The accurate diagnosis of PEComa in the peritoneum is challenging due to its morphologic and immunohistochemical similarities with many clear cell tumors. It mostly relies on the location of the neoplasm and the pathological approach. Since PEComas typically show immunoreactivity for both melanocytic and smooth muscle marker, its distinction from a metastatic melanoma represents a diagnostic challenge. S100 has been suggested to be a useful marker in the differential as it is expressed in melanomas and absent in PEComas. S100 expression was, however, unexpectedly discovered in up to 33 % of PEComas [31]. Thus, SOX10 which is negative in PEComas can diagnose melanoma [32].

Metastatic renal clear cell carcinoma is HMB45 negative and CK, EMA positive. Paraganglioma cells express NSE, Synaptophysin, Chromogranin A and CD56 but does not express HMB45 [33]. Epithelioid leiomyosarcoma and PEComa have overlapping histologic and immunohistochemical features including expression of smooth muscle markers; however, epithelioid leiomyosarcomas are non-immunoreactive for melanocytic markers. PEComas can also be easily distinguished from epithelioid extra-GIST by positive staining for Melan-A and HMB-45 and negative staining for both CD117, DOG-1, CD34, and pan-cytokeratin (AE1/AE3) [34].

Significant recurrence correlations have also been found for tumor sizes >5 cm. The liver, lymph nodes, lungs, and bone are the most often reported metastatic sites [35].

The most appropriate strategy treatment for PEComa has not yet been determined. When the primary and metastases are resectable, curative or debulking surgery looks to be the cornerstone of treatment. Regarding targeted therapy, the use of mTOR inhibitors in metastatic PEComas with TSC1/2 mutations have been reported in few cases. In contrast, TFE3-rearranged PEComas are generally unresponsive to mTOR inhibitors, highlighting the differences in their underlying molecular mechanisms [15]. Our patient underwent a surgical excision of the masses with no neoadjuvant treatment.

Locally progressed or metastatic tumor typically harbour a poor prognosis. The effect of neo/adjuvant treatment is poorly established and is reported with uneven responses [36]. Among the six cases, five patients were alive with no evidence of disease, with a median follow-up of 21 months. One case had no follow-up data available [36]. Our patient had a local recurrence, which was re-excised at 22 months of follow-up, followed by a second recurrence three months later. However, she could not undergo further surgery and ultimately died due to DIC.

Conclusion

This case report indicates that the diagnosis of primary multiple peritoneal PEComas should be taken into consideration for big and multiple masses. Pathological characteristics and immunohistochemical markers are required for diagnosis and may aid in risk stratification and therapy. With the possibility of multifocality and malignant potential indicated in this report, a thorough search for synchronous PEComas and metastases may be considered.
