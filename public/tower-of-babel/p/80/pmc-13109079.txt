Combined Inhibition of TRPM4/NMDA Receptor Complex and Extrasynaptic NMDA Receptors Is Candidate Therapeutic Target for Suppression of Epileptic Seizures and Improvement of Cognitive Impairments
Authors: Motohiro Okada, Ruri Okubo, Tomoka Oka, Eishi Motomura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCognitive impairments are important therapeutic targets in epilepsy medication; however, effective pharmacological treatments for epilepsy patients with comorbid cognitive impairment have not been established. Effects of chronic administration of MK‐801 (noncompetitive NMDA receptor inhibitor), memantine (low‐affinity extrasynaptic‐NMDA receptor preferential inhibitor), FP802 (TwinF interface inhibitor), and probenecid (pannexin1 containing hemichannel inhibitor) for 14 days on epileptic seizure frequency, sucrose preference, extracellular levels of L‐glutamate and D‐serine, expression of GluN2A and GluN2B, and phosphorylated CREB levels in the frontal cortex (epileptic focus region) were investigated using a genetic rat model of epilepsy with cognitive impairment (S286L‐TG rats). Seizure frequency of S286L‐TG was reduced by chronic administration of probenecid, MK‐801, and memantine + FP802, but not by memantine or FP802 alone. Decreased sucrose preference of S286L‐TG relative to wild‐type littermate rats was restored by memantine and memantine + FP802, but aggravated by MK‐801 without being affected by probenecid or FP802. Downregulated GluN2A/GluN2B expression in S286L‐TG relative to wild‐type was restored by probenecid, memantine, and memantine + FP802, but further decreased by MK‐801, and was unaffected by FP802. Upregulated basal extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in S286L‐TG were restored by probenecid and memantine + FP802, further increased by MK‐801, and unaffected by FP802. In contrast, chronic memantine alone restored the basal D‐serine increasing but did not affect basal L‐glutamate. These results suggest the possibility that a combination of suppressing TRPM4/NMDA receptor complex and extrasynaptic NMDA receptor may contribute to reducing epileptic seizures and ameliorating dysfunction/bias of preference regulation, providing a potential therapeutic strategy for epilepsy patients comorbid with cognitive impairments.

Introduction

Prevalence of neuropsychiatric disorders and/or cognitive impairments is higher in epilepsy patients than general population [1, 2]. Therefore, neuropsychiatric comorbidities, which can interfere with daily life more than the seizures themselves in some individuals, is also fundamental therapeutic target in epilepsy medication [2, 3]. Neuropsychiatric comorbidities in epilepsy are considered to be caused by epileptogenesis/ictogenesis, anti‐seizure medication and cumulative brain damage from seizures [2]. Historically, functional abnormalities in NMDA receptor (NMDAR) have been considered to play opposing roles in seizures and neuropsychiatric/neurodevelopmental disorders. Particularly, hyperactivated glutamatergic transmission is established pathophysiology of seizures (ictogenesis) [4], whereas NMDAR dysfunction contributes to pathophysiology of several neuropsychiatric disorders, including autism and schizophrenia [5, 6, 7]. Several conventional anti‐seizure medications that inhibit glutamatergic transmission carry high‐risk for cognitive deficits [8, 9, 10]. Historically, potent noncompetitive open channel NMDAR inhibitor, MK‐801 showed favorable seizures inhibition, but development for its anti‐seizure medication was unsuccessful due to severe cognitive deficits [11]. These findings highlight limitations of medication for patients suffering epileptic seizures comorbid with cognitive impairments.

To address these important challenges in epilepsy medication, this study determined the functional abnormalities in genetic rat model of autosomal dominant sleep‐related hyperkinetic epilepsy (ADSHE), named S286L‐TG (carrying S286L‐mutant rat Chrna4 corresponding to human S284L‐mutant CHRNA4). ADSHE Patients with S284L‐mutant CHRNA4 have been established to exhibit carbamazepine‐resistant ADSHE seizures and comorbid neurocognitive impairments, such as autism, which are distinct from the phenotypic features of core ADSHE population [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. S286L‐TG is validated model comparable phenotypes to ADSHE patients with S284L‐mutation [17, 18], such as carbamazepine‐resistant ADSHE seizures [19], including three major distinct epileptic phenotypes, nocturnal paroxysmal arousal (responsible circuits: pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus‐thalamus) [19, 20] and nocturnal paroxysmal dystonia (thalamus‐basal ganglia) [21] and nocturnal epileptic wandering (thalamus‐frontal cortex‐basal ganglia) [19]. Identified functional abnormalities, to date, are as follows: Loss‐of‐function of S286L‐mutant α4β2‐nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChRs) in the pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus and its projection regions, including thalamus, basal ganglia and frontal cortex, leads to GABAergic disinhibition, resulting in hyper‐activation of glutamatergic transmission in motor and cognitive pathways [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. These transmission abnormalities are synergistically reinforced by astroglial transmission abnormalities, specifically the age‐dependent upregulation of connexin43 (Cx43) [22] and pannexin1 (Px1) [24] hemichannels induced by enhanced Erk signallings via function α4β2‐nAChRs signaling [21, 26], in the mediodorsal thalamic nuclei, motor and orbitofrontal cortexes, resulting in chronic and excessive nonexocytotic astroglial releases of excitatory gliotransmitters, L‐glutamate and D‐serine [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. These functional abnormalities in S286L‐TG suggest that hyperactivation of tripartite glutamatergic transmission in thalamocortical pathways possibly contribute to not only ictogenesis but also cognitive impairments via disrupting integration inputs to cortex [17, 18], based on the recent pathophysiology of cognitive impairments induced by NMDAR dysfunction [7, 27].

On the other hand, clinical potential for improving seizures, cognitive impairment, and neuroprotection via extrasynaptic‐NMDAR modulation, have been recently reported from several clinical and preclinical studies. Recent clinical trial reported that low‐affinity extrasynaptic‐NMDAR preferential inhibitor, memantine [28, 29] may have the potential to improve both seizures and cognitive impairment (its effectiveness was statistically significant but very weak) in epilepsy patients with neurodevelopmental disorders [30]. The discrepancy in effectiveness between MK‐801 and memantine against seizure and cognitive impairment suggest that efficient extrasynaptic‐NMDARs inhibition may be therapeutic target. Recently, protein–protein interactions between GluN2A/GluN2B containing extrasynaptic‐NMDAR and other proteins contribute to excitotoxicity via NMDAR downstream signaling abnormalities [31, 32, 33]. 57‐amino‐acid‐long cytosolic portion domain in transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M member‐4 (TRPM4), named “TwinF”, interacts with 18‐amino‐acid‐long domain in GluN2A and GluN2B, named “I4” [33]. “TwinF interface inhibitor” (previously termed “NMDAR/TRPM4 interaction interface inhibitor”) disrupt the complex formations of TRPM4 with GluN2A/GluN2B, resulting in suppression of neurotoxic NMDAR/TRPM4 death signaling [33]. Indeed, several preclinical studies have reported that TwinF interface inhibitors suppress NMDAR‐associated neurotoxicity, reduce stroke‐induced brain damage and spinal motor neuron death in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis model, and improve cognitive impairment in Alzheimer's disease model [32, 34, 35].

Based on these findings, extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibition offer potential therapeutic benefits in treating epileptic seizures with cognitive impairment in epilepsy patients. To explore our hypothesis, we investigated the effects of MK‐801 (potent noncompetitive NMDAR inhibitor), memantine (low‐affinity extrasynaptic‐NMDAR preferential inhibitor), FP802 (TwinF interface inhibitor) and probenecid (pannexin1 containing hemichannel inhibitor) on ADSHE seizures and NMDAR‐associated behavioral and transmission abnormalities in S286L‐TG.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

All animal care and experimental procedures described in this report were performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Mie University, Japan (No. 2024–70; April 1, 2024) and the Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines [36].

Based on age‐dependent development of phenotypic features of S286L‐TG [18, 19], in this study, male 4‐weeks of age S286L‐TG (n = 6) and wild‐type littermates (n = 6), male 8‐weeks of age S286L‐TG (n = 72) and wild‐type (n = 72) were used. Both male S286L‐TG and wild‐type rats were housed individually in cages in air‐conditioned rooms (temperature, 22°C ± 2°C) with 12‐h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Although no clear clinical sex differences have been reported in the epileptic phenotypes, estrogen may affect the expression of NMDARs. Therefore, male S286L‐TG and wild‐type littermates were used in this study.

Drug Administration

Based on the phenotypic features of S286L‐TG, that ADSHE seizures onset period is approximately 8‐week of age, but no epileptic events are observed at 4‐weeks of age [17, 18, 19, 20], in this study, male S286L‐TG and wild‐type littermate (6‐weeks of age) were chronically administered target agents for 2‐weeks (from 6‐weeks to 8‐weeks of age). Each rat was weighed before chronic administration. All rats were anesthetized with 1.8% isoflurane (400 Anesthesia Unit, Univentor, Zejtun, Malta), and an osmotic pump (2ML2; Alzet, Cupertino, CA, USA) was inserted subcutaneously (sc) while maintaining body temperature at 37°C (ThermoStar Homeothermic Monitoring System, RWD). Male rats (6 weeks of age) were administered probenecid (100 mg/kg/day) [20, 24, 37], MK‐801 (0.1 mg/kg/day) [27, 38], memantine (MEM: 10 and 15 mg/kg/day) [39, 40], FP802 (40 mg/kg/day) [35, 41], and memantine (10 mg/kg/day) + FP802 (40 mg/kg/day) for 2 weeks (from 6 to 8 weeks of age) [42].

Sucrose Preference Test

S286L‐TG has already demonstrated no general behavior or sensorimotor abnormalities, such as water maze, passive avoidance or forced swim tests, whereas spontaneous locomotor activity and social interaction detected behavioral deficits, indicating the possibility that S286L‐TG may develop autism‐like neurocognition deficits [17, 18]. However, from the perspective of NMDAR dysfunction, spontaneous locomotor activity is not appropriate assessment, since both MK‐801 and memantine increased locomotor activity [43]. Contrary, either increasing or decreasing sucrose preference in several autism models have been reported, indicating that autism has dysfunction or bias of preference regulating system [44, 45]. This finding is rational from the perspective of recent pathomechanisms hypotheses that cognitive impairment in autism involves not only impaired social cognition contextual processes but also significant biases in sensory integration and/or executive processes [46]. Sucrose preference test has traditionally been considered to be established assessment for analyzing anhedonia or depression, since sucrose preference, which is decreased by chronic mild stress depression model, is ameliorated by conventional antidepressants [47]. Indeed, MK‐801 restored sucrose preference impairments induced by chronic mild stress, but conversely decreased sucrose preference in healthy control [47]. Contrary, decreased sucrose preference by MK‐801 were restored by D‐cycloserine, glycine transporter inhibitors and clozapine [27, 34, 37, 48]. Taken together with these findings; therefore, sucrose preference is recently rerecognized as behavior test for assessing cognitive impairment associated with NMDAR dysfunction in thalamocortical glutamatergic pathway [27, 34, 37, 44, 45, 48]. Based on these findings, this study conducted sucrose preference test as indicator of biases in sensory integration and executive processes.

After chronic administration of probenecid (100 mg/kg/day), MK‐801 (0.1 mg/kg/day), memantine (10 or 15 mg/kg/day), FP802 (40 mg/kg/day), memantine (10 mg/kg/day) + FP802 (40 mg/kg/day) for 2 weeks (sc), two identical bottles of equal volume were filled with tap water with or without 1% sucrose, and placed on the two sides of cage for 72‐h. Both bottles were switched three times at 24‐h interval. Sucrose preference was determined by measuring the final volume of each bottle at end of 24‐h for 3 consecutive days. Sucrose preference was calculated as the percentage of sucrose intake to the sum of sucrose and water intake [27].

Microdialysis and Electrocorticograms

Rats (4‐ and 8‐weeks of age) were anesthetized with 1.8% isoflurane and placed on a stereotaxic frame. Concentric direct‐insertion‐type dialysis probes (0.22‐mm diameter, 3‐mm exposed membrane: Eicom, Kyoto, Japan) were implanted in the orbitofrontal cortex (A = +3.2 mm, L = +2.4 mm, V = −6.5 mm, relative to bregma) [19]. Recording and reference screw electrodes were fastened to the skull over the frontal (A = +2.7 mm, L = −1.8 mm, relative to bregma) and occipital (A = −10.0 mm, L = +1.8 mm, relative to bregma) region [19, 20].

Perfusion experiments began 18‐h after recovery from anesthesia at a constant rate of 2 μL/min with modified Ringer solution (MRS: 145 Na+, 2.7 K+, 1.2 Ca2+, 1.0 Mg2+ and 154.4 Cl−, buffered at pH 7.4, with 2 mM phosphate buffer and 1.1 mM Tris buffer) [19, 27, 37]. Extracellular levels of L‐glutamate/D‐serine were measured 8‐h after starting perfusion. Dialysates were collected every 10‐min interval. After collection of basal extracellular levels of L‐glutamate/D‐serine for 60‐min, the perfusate was switched from MRS to MRS containing 100 μM NMDA (NMDA‐evoked stimulation) for 10 min.

Electrocorticograms (ECoG) of freely moving rats were telemetrically recorded (Unimec, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed using PowerLab (AD Instruments, Dunedin, New Zealand). Location of dialysis probe was verified at end of each experiment using 300‐μm thick brain tissue slices (Vibratome 1000, Technical Products International, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Determination of Levels of L‐Glutamate and D‐Serine

Extracellular levels of L‐glutamate/D‐serine were analyzed using ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC; xLC 3185PU; Jasco, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with fluorescence detector (xLC 3120FP; Jasco), and the excitation/emission wavelengths were set at 280/455 nm [27]. Sample was derivatised with isobutyryl‐l‐cysteine and o‐phthalaldehyde [27]. The derivatised samples (5‐μL aliquots) were automatically injected using autosampler (xLC 3059AS; Jasco) and separated analytical column (Triat C18, particle 1.8 μm, 50 × 2.1 mm; YMC, Kyoto, Japan) maintained at 500 μL/min and 45°C. Linear gradient elution program was used for over 10 min with mobile phases: A (0.05 M sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.0) and B (0.05 M acetate buffer containing 30% acetonitrile and methanol, pH 3.5).

Capillary Immunoblotting

During anesthetized with 1.8% isoflurane, the frontal cortex was dissected according to our previous methods [19, 20, 27, 37]. After washout of the dissected frontal cortex, for determination of GluN2A/GluN2B in the plasma membrane and phosphorylated cAMP response element binding protein (pCREB) in nuclear fractions were extracted using Minute Plasma Membrane Protein Isolation Kit (Invent Biotechnologies, Plymouth, MN, USA). To adjust the protein level in the plasma membrane fraction and total lysate to 1 μg/μL, the sample was suspended in RIPA Lysis and Extraction Buffer (FujiFilm‐Wako, Osaka, Japan).

Capillary immunoblotting analyses (12–230 kDa size assay) were performed using the single‐plex method (ProteinSimple, Santa Clara, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The lysates of the frontal cortex were mixed with a master mix (ProteinSimple) to obtain a final concentration of 1× sample buffer, 1× fluorescent molecular weight marker, and 40 mM dithiothreitol; the mixture was heated to 95°C for 5 min. The samples, blocking reagents, primary antibodies, HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies, chemiluminescent substrate (SuperSignal West Femto; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and separation and stacking matrices were dispensed into the designated wells of 25‐well plates. After plate loading, separation, electrophoresis, and immunodetection were performed using a fully automated capillary system. The capillary immunoblotting analysis was conducted at room temperature, using the default settings of the instrument. The capillaries were first filled with a separation matrix, followed by a stacking matrix, with approximately 40‐nL of sample (the protein level was adjusted to 1 μg/μL) used for loading. To reduce errors as much as possible, identical lysates were loaded onto two different plates (for standard and target proteins) were loaded onto separate plates, and then experiments were performed on the same day.

During electrophoresis, proteins were separated by molecular weight through stacking and separation matrices at 250‐V for 50 min, and then immobilized on the capillary wall using proprietary photo‐activated capture chemistry. The matrices were then washed again. Next, the capillaries were incubated with the blocking reagent for 15 min and the target proteins were probed with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies (A00098, 10 μg/mL; GenScript, Piscataway, NJ, USA).

Primary antibodies against GAPDH (NB300‐327, RRID:AB_10001915, 1:300; Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA), GluN2A (PPS012, RRID:AB_2112297, 1:100, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), GluN2B (PPS013, RRID:AB_562667, 1:100, R&D Systems), cAMP response element binding protein (CREB) (#4820, 1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and pCREB (#9198, 1:50, Cell Signaling) were used. Target protein sizes were determined for each sample in comparison to the migration of the size markers of the ladder (EZ Standard Pack 12–230 kDa; ProteinSimple). The heights and areas of all detected peaks were systematically calculated using the Compass for Simple Western software program (ver. 6.1.0, ProteinSimple), using the high dynamic range algorithm in Compass for Simple Western.

Data Analyses

All experiments in this study were designed with equally sized animal groups (n = 6) without formal power analysis, in accordance with previous studies [27, 35, 41, 49]. To randomize and blind data, the sample order in autosampler and capillary immunoblotting for L‐glutamate, D‐serine and protein levels were determined using random number tables. During ECoG monitoring and sucrose performance test, investigators were also blinded to group assignments and treatments. All values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and p‐values < 0.05 (two‐tailed) were considered statistically significant for all tests. In the capillary immunoblotting study, the levels of GluN2A/GluN2B in each sample were calculated relative to level of the loading control (GAPDH). The level of pCREB in each sample was calculated relative to that of CREB. The absolute levels of extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine were used for statistical analyses.

Protein levels, seizure frequency, sucrose preference and basal extracellular levels of L‐glutamate/D‐serine were also assessed using student t‐test, one‐way or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Scheffe's post hoc test. When F‐value of ANOVA was significant (p < 0.05), data were analyzed by Scheffe's post hoc test using BellCurve for Excel (ver. 3.2, Social Survey Research Information Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). NMDA‐evoked astroglial release of L‐glutamate/D‐serine were analyzed using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) with Scheffe's post hoc test using BellCurve for Excel. When data did not violate the assumption of sphericity (p > 0.05), F‐value of MANOVA was analyzed using assumed degrees of freedom of sphericity. When the assumption of sphericity was violated (p < 0.05), F‐value was analyzed using Greenhouse–Geisser corrected degrees of freedom. When F‐value of MANOVA was significant (p < 0.05), data were analyzed by Scheffe's post hoc test using BellCurve for Excel.

The data and statistical analyses complied with recommendations of the British Journal of Pharmacology for experimental design and analysis in pharmacology [50].

Chemical Agents

MK801 maleate (noncompetitive NMDA receptor antagonist), memantine (low‐affinity extrasynaptic‐NMDAR preferential inhibitor), probenecid (pannexin1 containing hemichannel inhibitor) were purchased from Funakoshi (Tokyo, Japan). TwinF interface inhibitor, FP802 [32] was obtained from Enamine (Kyiv, Ukraine). NMDA (NMDAR agonist) and memantine (low‐affinity extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibitor) [49] was obtained from Fujifilm‐Wako Pure Chemical Co (Osaka, Japan). All compounds were prepared on the day of the experiment. MK801, memantine and NMDA were dissolved directly in the experimental medium. Both probenecid and FP802 were initially made up as 10 mM stocks in DMSO.

Results

Age‐Dependent Fluctuation of the GluN2A/GluN2B Expression and Basal Extracellular L‐Glutamate/D‐Serine Levels in the Frontal Cortex

The expression levels of GluN2A in the frontal plasma membrane fraction of both S286L‐TG and wild‐type rats increased from 4‐weeks to 8‐weeks of age, whereas those of GluN2B decreased in an age‐dependent manner (Figure 1). At 4‐weeks of age (before epileptic discharge onset), both the expression of GluN2A and GluN2B in S286L‐TG were almost equivalent to those of the wild‐type, whereas at 8‐weeks of age (ADSHE seizure onset period), the expression of both GluN2A and GluN2B in S286L‐TG was lower (downregulated) than in the wild‐type (Figure 1).

Basal extracellular levels of L‐glutamate and D‐serine in the frontal cortex during wakefulness of both S286L‐TG and wild‐type increased from 4‐weeks to 8‐weeks of age (Figure 1). At 4‐weeks of age, the levels of L‐glutamate and D‐serine in S286L‐TG were also almost equivalent to those in wild‐type, whereas at 8‐weeks of age, the levels of L‐glutamate and D‐serine in S286L‐TG rats were greater than those in wild‐type (Figure 1).

Numerous studies have reported age‐dependent increases in extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels and GluN2A expression and decreased GluN2B expression [17, 18, 51]; therefore, S286L‐TG also show age‐dependent developmental characteristics of glutamatergic transmission. However, S286L‐TG probably already exhibited the downregulation of GluN2A/GluN2B and upregulation of basal releases of L‐glutamate/D‐serine at the onset period of ADSHE seizures.

Impacts of MK‐801, Probenecid, Memantine and FP802

Impacts on Seizure Frequency and Sucrose Preference

Chronic administration of both probenecid (100 mg/kg/day) and MK‐801 (0.1 mg/kg/day) decreased the frequency of ADSHE seizures, whereas neither chronic administration of memantine (10 mg/kg/day) nor FP802 (40 mg/kg/day) affected ADSHE seizures (Figure 2).

Sucrose preference of S286L‐TG was attenuated compared to wild‐type (Figure 2). Chronic administration of MK‐801 further decreased sucrose preference in both wild‐type and S286L‐TG, whereas the chronic administration of probenecid did not affect sucrose preference of them (Figure 2B). Neither memantine nor FP802 affected sucrose preference in wild‐type (Figure 2). Impaired sucrose preference in S286L‐TG was weakly restored by memantine, but not affected by FP802 (Figure 2).

Impacts on Basal Extracellular Levels of L‐Glutamate/D‐Serine in the Orbitofrontal Cortex

To elucidate the mechanisms of the seemingly inconsistent effects of probenecid, MK‐801, memantine and FP802 on seizure frequency and sucrose preference, the effects of chronic administrations of these four agents on basal extracellular levels of L‐glutamate and D‐serine in the focus region, orbitofrontal cortex, were determined.

Chronic administration of probenecid did not affect basal extracellular levels of L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in orbitofrontal cortex of wild‐type, but restored the upregulated L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in S286L‐TG (Figure 3). Contrary, chronic administration of MK‐801 increased basal extracellular levels of L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels of both wild‐type and S286L‐TG (Figure 3A). Memantine did not affect the L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in wild‐type, but ameliorated the increased basal D‐serine levels without affecting L‐glutamate levels (Figure 3). Contrary, FP802 did not affect the levels of L‐glutamate/D‐serine in either wild‐type or S286L‐TG (Figure 3).

Result that chronic probenecid administration decreased basal extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels of S286L‐TG without affecting those of wild‐type, is supported by the previous findings that pannexin1‐hemichannels in the orbitofrontal cortex of S286L‐TG were constitutively activated [17, 24, 27, 37]. Systemic administration of MK‐801 drastically activated thalamocortical glutamatergic transmission via intrathalamic GABAergic disinhibition; however, the local administration of MK‐801 into the frontal cortex did not affect extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels, indicating that presynaptic NMDAR in the frontal cortex does not contribute to the regulation of the release of L‐glutamate/D‐serine [27, 49, 52]. Therefore, the finding that the chronic administration of MK‐801 increased extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in the frontal cortex of both S286L‐TG and wild‐type suggests continuous activation of the thalamocortical pathway by MK‐801. Results that memantine decreased predominantly D‐serine level than L‐glutamate suggests that extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibition by memantine possibly attenuates astroglial release, considering that extracellular D‐serine is mainly astroglial origin but L‐glutamate is derived from both astrocytes and neurons [27]. TwinF interface inhibitors represent an exciting new target for neuroprotective medication; however, in S286L‐TG, NMDAR/TPRM4 may not contribute to upregulation of extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine.

Impacts of Combind Administration of Memantine and FP802

Neither the chronic administration of memantine nor FP802 alone decreased the frequency of ADSHE seizures in S286L‐TG rats, suggesting that extrasynaptic‐NMDAR or NMDAR/TRPM4 complex does not significantly influence ictogenesis. Although further exploration is required to confirm this hypothesis, since the sensitivity to NMDAR inhibitors between NMDAR uncoupled and coupled with TRPM4 is not identical [53]. To determine the effects of uncoupled extrasynaptic‐NMDAR alone (eliminating the effects of the NMDAR/TRPM4 complex), the chronic combined administration of memantine+FP802 was conducted.

Contrary to the administration of memantine or FP802 alone, chronic combined administration of memantine (10 mg/kg/day) plus FP802 (40 mg/kg/day) decreased ADSHE seizures and restored sucrose preference impairments of S286L‐TG, but did not affect sucrose preference of wild‐type (Figure 5). Combined administration of memantine+FP802 also ameliorated the downregulation of GluN2A/GluN2B and upregulation of basal extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in S286L‐TG (Figure 5). These results seemingly support to interpret that FP802 may pharmacokinetically or pharmacodynamically enhance the effects of memantine. However, pharmacokinetic enhancement can be ruled out, since memantine (15 mg/kg/day) further decreased sucrose preference in both wild‐type and S286L‐TG without ADSHE seizure frequency in S286L‐TG (Figure 6). These dose‐dependent biphasic effects of memantine on cognitive function were similar to previous demonstrations [39, 40].

Discussion

His study elucidated the complicated functional abnormalities of glutamatergic transmission associated with NMDAR in the frontal cortex (focus region) of S286L‐TG rats, a genetic rat model of ADSHE with cognitive impairments, and also suggested novel/candidate therapeutic targets that potentially improve epileptic seizures and cognitive impairments. The results in this study are summarized in Table 1. Hyper‐glutamatergic transmission is traditionally established pathophysiology of epilepsy; however, actual functional abnormalities of glutamatergic transmission in the frontal cortex of S286L‐TG were more complicated than our expectations, involving the downregulation of GluN2A/GluN2B and upregulation of the basal release of L‐glutamate/D‐serine in the focus region. Despite the downregulation of GluN2A/GluN2B, MK‐801 (potent noncompetitive open channel NMDAR inhibitor) and probenecid (pannexin1‐hemichannel inhibitor) decreased the seizure frequency of S286L‐TG. Neither extrasynaptic‐NMDAR preferential inhibitor, memantine, nor TwinF interface inhibitor, FP802, alone affected seizure frequency, whereas the combined administration of memantine+FP802 suppressed ADSHE seizures in S286L‐TG. Contrary, sucrose preference, which was reduced in S286L‐TG compared to wild‐type littermate, was further aggravated by MK‐801, and not affected by probenecid or FP802; however, memantine and combined administration of memantine+FP802 restored sucrose preference impairment in S286L‐TG. These results suggest that synaptic‐NMDARs may be potent targets for anti‐seizure medications, but synaptic‐NMDAR inhibition carries high‐risk of cognitive impairments. Contrary, extrasynaptic‐NMDAR preferential inhibitor, memantine, is expected to improve sucrose preference impairment, but it does not appear to be sufficiently effective to suppress ADSHE seizures. However, memantine possibly suppress extrasynaptic‐NMDAR that have been dissociated from the NMDAR/TRPM4 complex by FP802, potentially leading to both suppression of ADSHE seizures and improvement of sucrose preference impairment. In other words, memantine may unaffect NMDAR in NMDAR/TRPM4 complex. These findings suggest that the combination of TwinF interface inhibitors with extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibitors and/or selectively potent inhibitors of NMDAR combine with and without TRPM4 may be applicable to therapeutic strategies for the treatment of not only carbamazepine‐resistant ADSHE seizures, but also focal epilepsy accompanied by cognitive impairment.

Mechanisms of Downregulation of GluN2A/GluN2B in S286L‐TG

The relative enhancement of excitatory transmission compared to inhibitory transmission in epileptic foci or epileptic circuits has been established to play fundamental roles in the pathophysiology of epileptic seizures, such as ictogenesis/epileptogenesis [55, 56]. In particular, the activation of NMDAR via increased glutamatergic transmission contributes to epileptic hyperexcitability and its propagation [55, 56]. Indeed, the upregulation of NMDAR in the brains of patients with hippocampal sclerosis and several rodent epilepsy/seizure models has been revealed [56, 57, 58, 59]. Age‐dependent epileptogenesis and event‐related ictogenesis in S286L‐TG have been demonstrated. Extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in the frontal cortex during wakefulness and slow‐wave sleep (glutamatergic transmission was pronounced during slow‐wave sleep compared to wakefulness) were consistently greater than those of wild‐type [17, 18, 19, 23, 24, 60]. The increasing activated astroglial hemichannels contribute to continuously increasing basal extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in S286L‐TG [17, 18, 20, 21, 24]. Hemichannels are generally activated by plasma membrane depolarization. The voltage‐sensitivity of pannexin1‐hemichannel is the most sensitive among astroglial hemichannels, and its threshold is lower than connexin43‐hemichannel, around the resting membrane potential [24, 61]. Based on these findings, increasing extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels in the frontal cortex during resting stages via activated astroglial pannexin1‐hemichannels is thought to contribute to epileptogenesis/ictogenesis for ADSHE seizures in S286L‐TG [17, 18, 19, 23, 24, 60]. However, in this study, GluN2A/GluN2B was unexpectedly downregulated in the frontal cortex of S286L‐TG.

Expression of GluN2A/GluN2B in the plasma membrane is regulated by various processes, such as transcription, trafficking and recycling. Both Erk and CREB signallings enhance their transcription and trafficking [62, 63, 64]. It has been already revealed that Erk signaling is upregulated in S286L‐TG, leading to upregulation of Cx43 and Pannexin1‐hemichannels [21, 25, 26]. In this study, CREB signaling was decreased in S286L‐TG compared to wild‐type [63]; however, it has been already revealed that Erk signaling is upregulated in S286L‐TG, leading to upregulation of Cx43 and Px1 hemichannels [21, 25, 26]. Therefore, further studies are needed to clarify the impacts of increasing Erk and decreasing CREB signaling on expression of GluN2A/GluN2B.

This study demonstrated the more attractive potential mechanisms of downregulation of GluN2A/GluN2B. The principle function of NMDAR is to regulate neuronal excitability by cation influx through cation channels induced by the coactivation of glutamate and glycine binding sites in NMDAR; however, recent findings regarding downstream signaling of NMDAR revealed that GluN1 and GluN2s subunits possess specific signaling capabilities independent of cationic mobilization [51, 59, 65]. In particular, activation of the glycine‐binding site increases binding to adaptor‐protein‐2, resulting in the enhancement of the internalization/downregulation (endocytosis) of NMDAR [27, 66, 67]. Therefore, based on the functional features of NMDAR, persistently increasing astroglial D‐serine release through activated pannexin1‐hemichannel possibly contributes to the downregulation of NMDAR (GluN2A/GluN2B) in S286L‐TG. Indeed, in this study, the chronic administration of probenecid decreased basal extracellular D‐serine levels and restored the GluN2A/GluN2B downregulation in S286L‐TG, but neither extracellular D‐serine levels nor the GluN2A/GluN2B expression was affected by probenecid in wild‐type. In contrast, the chronic administration of MK‐801 increased extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels and decreased GluN2A/GluN2B expression. Therefore, although both probenecid and MK‐801 suppressed ADSHE seizures in S286L‐TG, decreasing and increasing extracellular D‐serine levels induced by probenecid and MK‐801 probably played important roles in the amelioration and enhancement of the GluN2A/GluN2B downregulation in S286L‐TG, respectively. Therefore, the downregulation of GluN2A/GluN2B in S286L‐TG is involved in sucrose preference impairments rather than the ADSHE seizures in S286L‐TG. In other words, the possibility that sucrose preference impairment in S286L‐TG is induced by increased astroglial transmission during the interictal stage rather than by activated neurotransmission and subsequent receptor response associated with epileptic hyperexcitability, which are the major targets in epilepsy pathophysiology. The reliability of this hypothesis should be clarified through a comprehensive analysis of the transcription, trafficking and recycling process.

Impacts of Extrasynaptic NMDAR Inhibition

The chronic administration of both probenecid (pannexin1‐hemichannel inhibitor) and MK801 (noncompetitive NMDA receptor inhibitor) suppressed ADSHE seizures in S286L‐TG; however, these agents had the opposite effects on basal extracellular L‐glutamate/D‐serine levels and the expression of GluN2A/GluN2B. Therefore, these discrepancies suggest that the therapeutic targets for ADSHE seizures and preference impairment in S286L‐TG may rely on spatially dependent functions of NMDAR (synaptic‐ and extrasynaptic‐NMDARs) and L‐glutamate/D‐serine release from neurons and astrocytes. ADSHE seizures in S286L‐TG were resistant to carbamazepine (a voltage‐sensitive sodium channel inhibitor), and no abnormalities in neuronal exocytosis regulation mechanisms in S286L‐TG were detected [19, 23]. Considering the suppressive effects of astroglial pannexin1‐hemichannel inhibitor (probenecid) on ADSHE seizures, these already demonstrated functional abnormalities in S286L‐TG, emphasizing that selective inhibition of extrasynaptic‐NMDAR and/or astroglial transmitter release is a potential target for ADSHE seizures in S286L‐TG.

Neither memantine (low‐affinity extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibitor) nor FP802 (TwinF interface inhibitor) alone affected ADSHE seizure frequency, whereas the combined administration of memantine+FP802 decreased seizure frequency, accompanied by restored sucrose preference in S286L‐TG. In terms of seizure suppression, inhibition of both synaptic‐ and extrasynaptic‐NMDAR by MK‐801 was more rational than extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibition alone, since the inhibitory effect of MK‐801 was more potent than that of memantine+FP802. However, inhibition of extrasynaptic‐NMDAR containing dissociated from NMDAR/TRPM4 complex is probably more attractive than inhibition of both synaptic‐ and extrasynaptic‐NMDARs for comprehensive epilepsy treatment, including both epileptic seizure suppression and cognitive impairments.

Clinical Implication

Several small clinical trials have reported that memantine, which is approved as a procognitive agent, showed efficacy against seizure frequency and cognitive impairment; however, its effectiveness, if any, seemed to be limited [30, 68, 69]. This study also demonstrated that the chronic administration of memantine restored sucrose preference but did not decrease ADSHE seizure frequency, whereas MK‐801 potently decreased ADSHE seizures, but aggravated sucrose preference. The discrepancy between the effectiveness of MK‐801 and memantine requires a detailed discussion. Both MK‐801 and memantine are noncompetitive open‐channel NMDAR inhibitors [28, 70]. MK‐801 blocks both synaptic‐ and extrasynaptic‐NMDARs under pathological conditions, but synaptic‐NMDAR alone during healthy physiological conditions, due to its high affinity, slow off‐rate, and long dwell time; however, memantine predominantly blocks extrasynaptic‐NMDAR during both pathological and physiological conditions, due to its low affinity, fast off‐rate, and short dwell time [28, 70]. Considering the pharmacological properties of memantine, extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibition by memantine may be insufficient to suppress ADSHE seizures. This hypothesis is supported by the effects of the combined administration of memantine+FP802. FP802 alone disrupting NMDAR/TRPM4 complex (ameliorated CREB shunt‐off) but did not affect ADSHE seizure frequency or sucrose preference impairments; however, combined memantine+FP802 also ameliorated CREB shunt‐off but decreased ADSHE seizure frequency and restored sucrose preference impairments. These results suggest that memantine cannot block NMDAR within the NMDAR/TRPM4 complex. Indeed, the pharmacological properties of the NMDAR/TRPM4 complex and uncoupled NMDAR have been reported to differ [53]. The binding affinity and inhibitory effects of memantine on NMDAR within the NMDAR/TRPM4 complex should be clarified in further studies.

Limitation

This study has several limitations, and caution is required in interpreting the results. In this study, combined administration of FP802 and memantine synergistically decreased ADSHE seizures and improved sucrose preference impairments in S286L‐TG, which is a genetic rat model for carbamazepine‐resistant ADSHE seizures with cognitive impairments of ADSHE patients with S286L‐mutation; however, this must be clarified whether this combination is applicable to other types of epilepsy syndrome, such as temporal lobe epilepsy and epileptic encephalopathy with neurodevelopmental disorders, by the further studies. Additionally, this study was conducted with mainly single dose administration of each agent, based on previous studies, but the impacts of pharmacokinetic interactions especially between FP802 and memantine should also be clarified. This study, based on the previously elucidated functional abnormalities of thalamocortical glutamatergic transmission in S286L‐TG, partially identified over‐rapped pathophysiologies between ADSHE seizure and cognitive impairments in S286L‐TG adopting sucrose preference as an indicator of cognition. However, autism‐like cognitive impairment is speculated to be composed of complicated pathomechanisms, therefore cannot be adequately evaluated solely by sucrose preference. Therefore, in the future, thalamocortical dysfunction hypothesis must be necessary to strengthen using other behavioral studies [17, 18].

Conclusion

Neuropsychiatric/neurodevelopmental comorbidities are the most common and severe manifestation of comorbidities in epilepsy and has a greater impact on quality of life than epileptic seizures in some individuals [2, 3]. Currently, a shift from a seizure‐centered model to a patient‐centered approach in epilepsy medication is advocated, but there is still a lack of neuropsychiatric research exploring the pathophysiology of epilepsy and its neuropsychiatric/neurodevelopmental comorbidities, highlighting the need for increased research [3]. To respond to the current paradigm changes in epilepsy treatment, a major challenge has been overcoming the opposite glutamate‐related pathophysiology of epileptic seizures and cognitive impairment, which has been believed by the majority of epilepsy clinicians and researchers. This study demonstrated that synaptic‐NMDAR inhibition can potently suppress epileptic seizures but further aggravate the cognitive function. In contrast, selective extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibition may be effective in suppressing epileptic seizures and improving cognitive impairment. However, the inhibitory effect of memantine, a conventional preferential extrasynaptic‐NMDAR inhibitor, was not sufficient to suppress epileptic seizures, but its insufficient effect was enhanced by the concomitant administration of the NMDAR/TRPM4 complex disrupter FP802. Therefore, the development of compounds with the potent and selective inhibition of extrasynaptic‐NMDARs, including the NMDAR/TRPM4 complex, can contribute to providing a novel anti‐seizure medication class that can suppress epileptic seizures and improve cognitive impairment in patients with epilepsy with comorbid cognitive impairment.
