Skeletal editing via multi-step engineering of a modular polyketide synthase
Authors: Kei Kudo, Takuya Hashimoto, Takayoshi Awakawa, Lihan Zhang, Takehiro Nishimura, Junko Hashimoto, Ikuko Kozone, Noritaka Kagaya, Hikaru Suenaga, Adrian T Keatinge-Clay, Ikuro Abe, Kazuo Shin-ya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssembly line biosynthesis creates numerous structurally diverse natural products using a common modular synthetic strategy. The collinearity between the architectures of modular polyketide synthases (PKS) and the structures of their polyketide products would seem to render these biosynthetic machineries excellent platforms for designer biosynthesis, yet reliable strategies to reprogram these assembly lines without diminishing their activities have not been identified. Here, as a best practice for PKS engineering, we demonstrate the reprogramming of the mediomycin PKS without significant loss of productivity. Using in vitro CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing followed by heterologous expression, we reconstruct an inaccessible drug lead of the fibrinogen receptor, tetrafibricin, at 82 ± 3 mg/L yield, retaining 26% productivity after five-step module editing using an evolution-supported cut site, downstream of the acyltransferase domain. A macrocyclic aminopolyol is also accessed through thioesterase swapping. These results pave the way toward the rational reprogramming of PKSs to access desired complex organic molecules.Subject terms: Natural product synthesis, Biosynthesis, Protein design, Genetic engineering, Drug discovery and development

Introduction

Modular polyketide synthases (PKSs) are multidomain enzyme complexes that biosynthesize a wide variety of polyketide natural products, including the clinically important medicines erythromycin, tacrolimus (FK506), and ivermectin (a semi-synthetic derivative of avermectin)1. The reaction cycle of a PKS module is equivalent to that of the metazoan fatty acid synthase2–4 - a carbon building block that is passed from an acyltransferase (AT) domain to an acyl carrier protein (ACP) domain and a growing acyl chain that is bound to a ketosynthase (KS) domain are fused to generate an elongated chain that can be processed by accompanying ketoreductase (KR), dehydratase (DH), and enoylreductase (ER) domains. However, the multimodular nature of PKS assembly lines enables differential processing of each intermediate5–8. In theory, PKSs can be engineered to access designer polyketides; however, in practice9–14 the efficient (i.e., retaining production titre) and flexible reprogramming of PKSs is challenging due to our limited understanding of domain-domain interactions and substrate recognition10,15–17.

The PKS module is the set of domains responsible for one round of chain extension and processing. It was originally defined with an upstream KS and a downstream ACP18. However, studies have shown that KSs can act as gatekeepers that select between intermediates bound to the upstream ACP19–22, and that interactions between these two domains are important23,24. Additionally, our previous evolutionary analysis of aminopolyol PKSs revealed that KSs genetically co-migrate with upstream processing enzymes19 and we expanded this idea to other types of PKSs (Supplementary Fig. 1). This co-migration is apparent in modular PKSs both with and without embedded ATs, known as cis-AT and trans-AT PKSs19,25. How often AT domains co-migrate with neighboring enzymatic domains in cis-AT PKSs is likely related to the chemistry they perform together. Sets of processing enzymes+ACP+KS that incorporate an α-methyl substituent in a stereocontrolled manner collaborate with methylmalonyl-specific upstream ATs21. KSs that extend polyketides with ethylmalonyl extender units collaborate with ethylmalonyl-specific downstream ATs26. How well such sets of enzymatic domains tolerate AT swaps is the subject of active investigation27. While the genetic co-migration of processing enzymes and KSs within cis-AT PKSs was studied by comparing related aminopolyol PKSs, the co-migration of their predominantly malonyl-specific AT domains could not be studied due to gene conversion, which overwrites their evolutionary history19,28. Thus, to reprogram aminopolyol PKSs, we evaluated two alternative strategies to determine which was superior: AT+processing enzymes+ACP+KS units (exchange unit or “XU”) or processing enzymes+ACP+KS+AT units (“XUA”). “XU” was originally introduced in the context of nonribosomal peptide synthetase engineering29 and later adopted for PKS engineering14. Because KS and AT are structurally connected, engineering modular PKSs by XUA-based module editing might be a reasonable strategy (Fig. 1)30–32.

Here, we demonstrate the skeletal editing of aminopolyol polyketide structures through PKS reprogramming. Using a previously established heterologous expression system for mediomycin biosynthetic gene cluster19 as a platform, an artificial PKS was engineered through successive multidomain manipulations to produce tetrafibricin, a fibrinogen receptor inhibitor33,34, with a yield of 104 ± 4 µM (82 ± 3 mg/L) [mean ± s.e.m. (n = 3), the same applies below], demonstrating the most complex and efficient multistep PKS engineering achieved to date to the best of our knowledge. Furthermore, we produced an artificial macrolactone using the ACP-TE co-transplantation strategy developed in this study.

Results

Reconstructing the carbon scaffold of tetrafibricin

Tetrafibricin is an aminopolyol polyketide with potent fibrinogen receptor inhibition activity that was first isolated in 199333,34. Subsequent attempts to obtain tetrafibricin from the producing strain have been unsuccessful19, dropping this non-peptidyl antiplatelet drug candidate out of further development. Interestingly, the “producer” strain, Streptomyces neyagawaensis NR0557, instead produces a larger polyketide, neotetrafibricin. Based on the domain architecture of the neotetrafibricin PKS, misdocking between tfbORF5 and tfbORF9, premature hydrolysis by TE, or spontaneous degradation during the isolation process may have led to tetrafibricin formation. As an alternative approach, tetrafibricin has been targeted by chemical synthesis. While many fragment syntheses have been reported, none has yet yielded the final natural product35.

We previously reported that the structural diversification of aminopolyols is naturally driven by the recombination of the genes encoding PKSs19. Thus, we envisioned performing similar recombinations in the lab to transform the mediomycin PKS (medPKS) into a tetrafibricin-producing PKS (Supplementary Fig. 2a). To this end, the mediomycin biosynthetic gene cluster was cloned into a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) for in vitro gene editing using CRISPR/Cas9 (Supplementary Fig. 2b)13. The heterologous expression of this BAC in Streptomyces lividans TK23 ΔredD–X, an engineered host lacking the undecylprodigiosin biosynthetic gene cluster, was demonstrated in our previous work to enable the production of clethramycin and desulfoclethramycin (guanidinylated mediomycin and desulfomediomycin)19. When this work was initiated, we measured a combined yield of these polyketides of 533 ± 6 µM (635 ± 7 mg/L). Given that this level of productivity was sufficiently high, we selected this host for subsequent experiments. The guanidino group is not hydrolysed to an amine to provide mediomycin and desulfomediomycin since medX, the gene encoding the amidinohydrolase, was not originally included in the BAC because it is located outside of the mediomycin biosynthetic gene cluster36.

We first attempted to swap XUA7 and XUA9 within medPKS, as in a natural recombination event that occurred in the evolution of the aminopolyol PKSs19. In this campaign, XUA9 was first duplicated (PKSv1), but then the doubling was resolved (PKSv2), which had the benefit of minimizing opportunities for internal recombination. The cut site for the XUA boundary was set C-terminal to the first tyrosine of the conserved LPTY(A/P)FQ(H/R)xRYWL motif in the last half of the post-AT linker37, referred to as PAL227 (Supplementary Fig. 2b, d). This evolution-guided engineering19 preserves the interactions between KS, the upstream ACP, and the upstream processing enzymes. The genes encoding the engineered assembly line, PKSv1, were expressed in S. lividans TK23 ΔredD–X19. The anticipated products (Fig. 1b) 1a + 1b were generated at a combined yield of 436 ± 55 µM (524 ± 66 mg/L), 82% that of clethramycin and desulfoclethramycin (Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Fig. 3a). Next, the downstream XUA9 was replaced by XUA7 to create PKSv2 (Fig. 1a), which generated 2a + 2b at a combined yield of 298 ± 33 µM (347 ± 38 mg/L) (Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Fig. 3a). The structures of 1b and 2b were confirmed by HR-MS analyses (Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Figs. 10–24) and a series of NMR analyses (Supplementary Fig. 4, and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3) (see also Supplementary Figs. 25–55 for 1D and 2D NMR spectral data).

In the third step, we sought to trim the carboxy end of the polyketide product. It has been well-documented that such a truncation can be obtained through the appropriate positioning of a thioesterase (TE) domain38. Thus, we first positioned the mediomycin TE (medTE) downstream of the ACP domain of XUA19 to create PKSv3_medTE (Supplementary Fig. 2c, f). However, only a trace amount of the expected product 3 was detected by mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 5). Introduction of the pikromycin TE (pikTE) was also attempted (Supplementary Fig. 5); however, PKSv3_pikTE (Supplementary Fig. 2c, f) only generated 3 at a yield of 59 ± 7 µM (49 ± 6 mg/L), 20% that of 2a + 2b. Inspired by the recent ACP-centric view of PKS modules, we hypothesized that the natural ACP cognate of medTE might be necessary for efficient TE-catalysed hydrolysis. With the ACP + TE didomain from the medPKS positioned downstream of the KR domain of XUA19 (Supplementary Fig. 1e), PKSv3 (Fig. 1a) generated 3 at a yield of 249 ± 15 µM (205 ± 12 mg/L), 84% that of 2a + 2b (Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Fig. 3b). The sulfonated analogue of 3 was not observed.

In the fourth step, XUA11 was removed from PKSv3 (Fig. 1a). The resulting PKSv4 generated 4 at a yield of 265 ± 26 µM (211 ± 21 mg/L), 106% that of 3 (Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Fig. 3b).

Finally, a copy of XUA4 was inserted downstream of XUA2 (Fig. 1a). The resulting PKSv5 generated 5 at a yield of 140 ± 2 µM (115 ± 2 mg/L) (Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Fig. 3b). The yield of 5 was 53% that of 4.

XUA vs. XU strategy

At this stage, we had successfully created the polyketide skeleton of tetrafibricin through five steps of PKS engineering using the XUA strategy. We then compared the XUA strategy with the XU strategy, which employs a domain set with KS as the most downstream domain (AT+processing enzymes+ACP+KS) (Fig. 2a). The XU strategy has been effectively applied in recent years to modify several cis-AT PKSs and has been shown to be more effective than swapping using the traditional module boundary9,12,14,39.

For Steps 1, 2, 4, and 5, we generated PKSv1_XU, PKSv2_XU, PKSv4_XU and PKSv5_XU from medPKS, PKSv1, PKSv3 and PKSv4, respectively (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 2f), using the region 10 aa downstream of the GTNAH motif located at the C-terminal of the KS domain as the cut site for the XU strategy. This cut site was employed and shown to work in a previous study40. Step 3, which involves the migration of ACP-TE units, is not relevant to the comparison of the XUA and XU strategies.

As a result, PKSv1_XU, PKSv2_XU, PKSv4_XU, and PKSv5_XU showed 93%, 82%, 59%, and 51% yields of PKSv1, PKSv2, PKSv4, and PKSv5, respectively (Fig. 2c). The differences in yield between PKSv4 and PKSv4_XU (p < 0.001) and PKSv5 and PKSv5_XU (p = 0.002) were statistically significant, while those between PKSv1 and PKSv1_XU (p = 0.383) and PKSv2 and PKSv2_XU (p = 0.484) were not. These findings suggest that, at least in the context of aminopolyol PKS engineering, the XUA strategy appears more robust against productivity loss.

Production of tetrafibricin

The PKSv5 product 5 differs from tetrafibricin in its guanidino group. To convert this group to an amine, we aimed to co-express an amidinohydrolase gene from the aminopolyol-producing strains with PKSv5. To identify a promiscuous amidinohydrolase, tfbX1, tfbX2, ecoX, and medX were each co-expressed with PKSv4 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). As TfbX1 and EcoX efficiently converted 4 to 7, tfbX1 and ecoX were each expressed with PKSv5, and both of them successfully converted 5 to tetrafibricin. The more efficient combination, tfbX1 and PKSv5, produced tetrafibricin at a yield of 104 ± 4 µM (82 ± 3 mg/L) (Fig. 3b, and Supplementary Fig. 6b). The yield of tetrafibricin was 75% that of 5, demonstrating the efficiency of TfbX1. Notably, ecoX co-expression with PKSv3 or PKSv4 gave 6 (227 ± 15 µM; 178 ± 12 mg/L) or 7 (188 ± 39 µM; 142 ± 29 mg/L), respectively (Fig. 3b, and Supplementary Fig. 6b), showcasing the promiscuity of EcoX. The structures of 6, 7 and tetrafibricin were determined by HR-MS analysis (Supplementary Table 1) and a series of NMR analyses (Supplementary Fig. 4, and Supplementary Tables 4–6). Tetrafibricin produced through the described module editing showed potent fibrinogen receptor antagonistic activity, as previously reported (see Supplementary Discussion).

Notably, a transformant carrying PKSv5, cultured before monospore isolation (see Methods), generated 9 (Supplementary Fig. 7), a two-module-skipped version of 5. Co-expression of tfbX1 without isolating spores resulted in 10 (Supplementary Table 7) as the major product (tetrafibricin was observed as a minor product). Plausibly, in vivo recombination between two direct repeat regions encoding XUA4 (spanning 5322 bp) occurred. On the other hand, we observed that the productivity of 5 or tetrafibricin was not abolished even after a subculture, suggesting that the recombination event occurs during transformation. Interestingly, we did not observe an equivalent recombination for PKSv1, which possesses 2 copies of XUA9, for reasons that are unknown.

Production of an aminopolyol macrolide and its biological activity

Macrocyclic polyketides often exhibit potent bioactivities, as exemplified by clinical drugs such as erythromycin and amphotericin. By applying the ACP+TE didomain transplantation strategy that efficiently yielded 3, we further exploited TEs from other macrolactone PKS systems to engineer an aminopolyol macrolide. We transplanted the ACP+TE didomains from the PKSs that produce the macrocycles desertomycin and amphotericin for the ACP+TE didomain of medPKS (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 2f). Although the amphotericin ACP+TE transplant only provided trace quantities of cyclic product 8, the desertomycin ACP+TE transplant, PKSv6, yielded 8 at 9.6 ± 0.4 µM (11.5 ± 0.5 mg/L) (Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Fig. 8). The ester moiety of 8 was observed by NMR, confirming the production of an artificial 54-membered macrolide (Fig. 4c, and Supplementary Table 8). The desertomycin TE was also transplanted without its cognate ACP, generating PKSv6(TE) (Fig. 4a), but the yield was half that of PKSv6 (5.1 ± 0.3 µM; 6.1 ± 0.4 mg/L) (Fig. 4b).

The antibacterial and cytotoxic activities of 8 were evaluated. This cyclized clethramycin did not inhibit the growth of Micrococcus luteus and Staphylococcus aureus, while clethramycin showed weak activity against both strains (IC50 for M. luteus was 32.1 µM, agreeing with a previous report) (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Cytotoxicity was measured on T lymphoma Jurkat cells, human ovarian adenocarcinoma SKOV-3 cells, and malignant pleural mesothelioma MESO-1 cells. Whereas clethramycin showed cytotoxicity (IC50 0.32, 6.0, and 20 µM, respectively) equivalent to that of etoposide (0.28, 2.3, and 16 µM, respectively), 8 was only active against Jurkat cells and ~100-times less potent (23 µM) (Supplementary Fig. 9b).

Discussion

The efficient reprogramming of PKS assembly lines is a long-standing goal in biosynthetic engineering. While recent efforts have shown substantial improvements in titres of engineered PKSs, multi-step modular engineering is usually accompanied by large losses in activity at each step14,40. By employing an evolution-supported cut site, we performed five successive module editing steps to generate a medPKS variant that synthesizes the currently inaccessible drug lead, tetrafibricin, with only moderate loss of productivity. The 140 ± 2 µM yield of 5 and the 104 ± 4 µM yield of tetrafibricin are 26% and 20%, respectively, that of clethramycin + desulfoclethramycin from medPKS. Productivity changes from one engineered PKS to the next ranged from 53% to 106% (76% geometric mean, representing the step-by-step average of productivity loss) (Fig. 1c), demonstrating an unprecedented level of titre retention upon multistep PKS engineering. The amidinohydrolases TfbX1 and EcoX efficiently converted the guanidinylated products into their corresponding amino forms, making them promising biosynthetic tools for future engineering efforts. Preparative production of tetrafibricin was achieved at a yield of 82 ± 3 mg/L without metabolic engineering, culture condition screening, or mutagenesis. If these were performed, we envision the titre would reach the g/L scale41,42.

The sulfotransferase MedB partially sulfonates the products of medPKS, PKSv1, PKSv2, and PKSv6 such that the ratio of sulfonated to unsulfonated congener was 65/35 (clethramycin/desulfo-clethramycin), 54/46 (1a/1b), 34/66 (2a/2b), and 46/54 (8/desulfo-8), respectively. Thus, MedB was tolerant to the modified polyol moiety and even the cyclized skeleton. A previous report showed MedB sulfonates neotetrafibricin B in vitro19. Our observations support the substrate promiscuity of MedB, which may encourage further utilization of this enzyme as a tool in combinatorial biosynthesis. In contrast, considering that 3, 4, and 5 were not sulfonated, MedB may be sensitive to changes in the carboxy end of the polyketide.

Three requirements that need to be met for the efficient reprogramming of modular PKSs are that synthase architecture is not affected, that domain-domain interactions are not compromised, and that unnatural polyketide intermediates are not rejected by assembly line enzymes downstream of the introduced modifications. Using the cut site at the post-AT linker ensures that the KS + AT didomain architecture remains structurally intact30. That use of the post-AT cut site repeatedly enabled robust production of anticipated products, indicating that no mismatches between upstream and downstream domains were formed. The non-native interactions between KSs and downstream ACPs did not disrupt the function of engineered PKSs. Through the demonstration of aminopolyol engineering, both of the XUA and XU strategies preserved connections between KSs and their native upstream processing domains and ensured KSs were presented intermediates with the same functionalities at their α- and β-positions as in their natural substrates. A disadvantage of using the post-AT linker rather than the KS-AT linker12,14,39,43, is that the XUA can alter which α-substituents are introduced. Recent bioinformatic analysis has revealed different sequence fingerprints for KSs that accept intermediates with different chemistries at their α- and β-positions21, suggesting that α-substituent changes could impede transacylation from the upstream ACP. As our engineering campaign only involved malonyl-CoA-specific ATs, no unnatural α-substituents were presented to KSs.

We observed that editing using the post-AT cut site outperformed editing using the KS-AT linker cut site. The post-AT linker cut site has been employed to swap ATs27 and reductive loops44. We show that the cut site (i.e., after the LPTY motif) can be used for XUA-based editing of aminopolyol PKSs. Notably, a recent study from our lab illustrated another post-AT cut site at the end of PAL2 (i.e., after the RYWL motif) predominantly functioned in candicidin, rakicidin, and erythromycin PKSs45. This flexibility in acceptable cut sites within PAL2 does not conflict with the results of an AT-swapping study that compared the use of several cut sites46.

The activities of TE domains greatly impact the structures and bioactivities of polyketide natural products47. Our offloading engineering studies revealed that TE domains function better when transplanted with their cognate ACP domains. This is somewhat surprising because interactions unique to TEs and their upstream ACPs have not been reported48,49 and engineering with eryTE or pikTE has been successfully performed with diverse ACPs10,50–52. As medTE and dstTE show improved function when accompanied by their natural ACP, we hypothesize that maintaining the ACP-TE didomain is generally preferable when engineering PKSs. Our results suggest that interactions between a catalytic domain and its upstream ACP are not only important for KS but also for TE, in accord with the ACP-centric view of PKS modules. The failure of the amphotericin ACP-TE to catalyse aminopolyol cyclization may be due to poor recognition of the nonnative intermediate it is presented, since substrate structure has been observed to affect cyclization efficiency53. Except for one methyl group, the substrates for medTE and dstTE share the same 13-carbon substructure at their guanidino termini (Supplementary Fig. 8d). This structural similarity could explain why dstTE performs the desired cyclization.

Tetrafibricin has been targeted for total synthesis; however, to our knowledge, only protected analogues and fragments have been accessed35. Our results encourage using PKS module editing as a reasonable and alternative strategy for obtaining tetrafibricin and analogues thereof in yields appropriate for medicinal chemistry. The efficient programming of modular PKSs can be transformative in the synthesis of high yields of valuable natural product-based medicines.

In summary, we have reconstructed tetrafibricin through a 5-step modular reprogramming of a related PKS and have created unnatural linear and cyclized analogues of aminopolyol polyketides in preparative yields. Our strategy of using the evolution-supported post-AT cut site for XUA-based editing was highly efficient at each step. This precise editing in ~200 kb BACs was enabled by our in vitro CRISPR/Cas9 module-editing technology. The construction of a tetrafibricin PKS presented here will guide future engineering efforts to customize PKSs for the production of other desired polyketide-based compounds.

Methods

Plasmids and bacterial strains

Plasmids and bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 9 and 10, respectively.

The culturing procedures of bacterial strains followed our previous paper54. Specifically, the growth of Escherichia coli strains DH5α and NEB10β (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) used Luria broth (LB), which contained (per L of deionized water) 10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, and 5 g of NaCl, and was adjusted to pH 7.5 following formulation. The corresponding solid medium (LA) was prepared by supplementing LB with agar at 15 g/L. For the production of engineered products, each transformant of S. lividans TK23 ΔredD-X was inoculated into a 50-mL test tube containing 15 mL of seed medium, containing (per L of deionized water) 5 g of glucose, 15 g of soya flour, and 5 g of yeast extract; the cells were cultured with reciprocal shaking at 320 rpm and 27 °C for 2 days. A 375-μL aliquot of the resulting vegetative culture was used to inoculate a 125-mL flask containing 15 mL of the production media. The production media contained (per L of deionized water) 20 g of glycerol, 10 g of molasses, 5 g of casein, 1 g of Polypepton, and 4 g of CaCO3; the pH was adjusted to 7.2 following formulation. The fermentation cultures were grown on a rotary shaker at 180 rpm and 27 °C for 6 days. For quantification or large-scale preparation of the products, cells were grown under the same conditions in baffled 500-mL flasks, each containing 100 mL of the production media.

In vitro module editing

The principle of in vitro module editing is originally described in ref.6. In brief, a parent BAC was digested in vitro by Cas9, and then a donor DNA was ligated by Gibson assembly.

sgRNAs (see Supplementary Data 1 for target sequences) were prepared using the EnGen sgRNA Synthesis Kit, S. pyogenes (New England Biolabs), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The Cas9 (New England Biolabs) reaction mixture containing the specific combination of a parent BAC and sgRNAs (Supplementary Table 11) was incubated at 37 °C for 120 min. The digested BAC and the donor DNA (Supplementary Data 2) were subjected to Gibson assembly. After 50 min-incubation at 50 °C, the ligated DNA (edited BAC) was recovered by phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol treatment followed by 2-propanol precipitation, then transformed into E. coli NEB10β by electroporation. The transformants were selected on LA containing 100 µg mL−1 ampicillin and 50 µg mL−1 kanamycin. The obtained plasmids were further confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Supplementary Data 1) and restriction digestion.

Cloning of amidinohydrolases

medX was amplified by PCR using the genomic DNA of S. blastmyceticus NBRC12747 as a template. ecoX (AAX98207), tfbX1 (WP_190130807) and tfbX2 (WP_190128809) were synthesized and cloned into pUC-GW-Kan by GENEWIZ (South Plainfield, NJ), which were used as PCR templates. The primers used for PCR are listed in table S11. The PCR fragments were subcloned into the XbaI-HindIII site of pKU565T_ermE*p.

Transformation of Streptomyces lividans TK23 ΔredD-X

The BACs and expression vectors for amidinohydrolases were introduced into S. lividans TK23 ΔredD-X by PEG-assisted protoplast transformation. Subsequently, the transformants carrying pKU518med_PKSv5 were subjected to monospore isolation as follows. Mature spores were harvested from transformants grown on soya flour mannitol (SFM) agar plates containing (per L of tap water) 20 g of mannitol, 20 g of soya flour, and 20 g of agar. A dense spore suspension was serially diluted and plated onto fresh SFM agar. Well-separated single colonies were then picked and patched to new SFM agars for further analysis.

UHPLC/MS analysis of culture extract

One millilitre of culture broth was lyophilized and then extracted with 1 ml of methanol. Debris was removed by centrifugation, and the supernatant was filtrated through 0.22 µm PTFE membrane to give an analytical sample for UHPLC. Analytical UHPLC and HR-ESI-MS (positive mode) were performed using a ACQUITY UPLC System (Waters, Framingham, MA) in conjunction with a ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 i.d. × 100 mm) (Waters), a ACQUITY UPLC PDA eλ detector (Waters), and a XevoG2 ToF system (Waters). Mobile phase A was water + 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was 0.8 mL min−1, and the column temperature was kept at 55˚C by a column oven. The gradient elution program was 5−100% B over 5 min and 100% B for 1 min. The isocratic elution was performed by 37% B for clethramycin, desulfoclethramycin, 1a, 1b, 2a, 2b, and 8, 17% B for 3, 4, 6, and 7, and 15% for 5, tetrafibricin, 9, and 10 (n = 1 for each sample). Data acquisitions and analyses were performed by using MassLynx V4.1 software (Waters). The standard curve for clethramycin was obtained based on the peak area of UV chromatogram at 358 nm, and applied for quantifying clethraymcin and the compounds sharing the same conjugated double bond system with clethramycin (i.e., desulfoclethramycin, 1a, 1b, 2a, 2b, and 8). Similarly, the standard curve for tetrafibricin was obtained based on the peak area of UV chromatogram at 337 nm, and applied for quantifying tetrafibricin and the compounds sharing the same conjugated double bond system with tetrafibricin (i.e., 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, and 10).

Ltd, Kyoto, Japan) with 50 mM ammonium formate solution/acetonitrile as the mobile phase, with a gradient of separation: 0–25 min 30–45% B, 25–30 min 45–55% B, to afford 1.2 mg of 2b.

Isolation of 6

Two litres of fermentation broth of S. lividans TK23 ΔredD-X/pKU565T_ecoX/pKU518med_PKSv3 were centrifuged to separate a mycelial cake and supernatant. The mycelial cake was extracted twice with 500 mL of acetone, and the acetone was removed in vacuo. The residual aqueous extract and the supernatant of culture broth were separately loaded on a DIAION HP20 (Mitsubishi Chemical Co., Tokyo, Japan) column (100 mL) and eluted with a stepwise gradient of H2O–MeOH (0, 50, 100, 100% MeOH; 5 C.V. each). The MeOH solution was subjected to gel filtration chromatography using a Sephadex LH-20 (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) column developed with MeOH. The fraction containing 6 (706 mg) was further separated by Toyopearl HW-40F (Tosoh Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) developed with MeOH. The fraction containing 6 (65 mg) was purified by preparative reversed-phase HPLC using a CAPCELL PAK MG-II C18 column (5 µm, 20 i.d. × 250 mm) (Shiseido Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) with 50 mM ammonium formate solution/acetonitrile (84:16) as the mobile phase to afford 1.4 mg of 6.

Isolation of 7

NMR spectroscopy

NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker AVANCE III 900 (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) for 1b and 2b, and an NMR 600 NB CL spectrometer (Varian, Palo Alto, CA) for 6, 7, tetrafibricin, 8, and 10 at room temperature. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in ppm with the residual solvent signals as the internal standards (methanol-d4 at δH 3.31 and δC 49.0 ppm). Coupling constants (J) are reported in Hertz. The data are reported as (s = singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, m = multiplet, or unresolved; coupling constant(s)). 13C-NMR spectra were recorded with complete 1H decoupling.

The structures of 1b, 2b, 6, 7, tetrafibricin, and 10 were determined based on 1D and 2D NMR spectral analyses (See Supplementary Figs. 25–55 provided as a separate file). Key 2D NMR (COSY, HMBC, and HSQC-TOCSY) correlations established the substructures intended to be modified in each step (colored in red) (Supplementary Fig. 13). As the almost identical chemical shifts and the 2D NMR correlations indicate, the remaining substructures are the same as in the previous step.

Antibacterial assay

The procedure of the antibacterial assay followed our previous paper55. Specifically, S. aureus and M. luteus (laboratory stocks) were precultured in LB medium until the optical density at 620 nm became 0.5. The bacteria suspension was diluted 2000-fold with LB medium and inoculated into each well of a 384-well plate, followed by the addition of serially diluted test compounds. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, the optical density for each well was measured at 620 nm using an EnVision Multilabel Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, MA).

Cytotoxicity assay

The procedures of the cytotoxicity assay followed our previous paper13. Specifically, the cytotoxicities against T lymphoma Jurkat cells, human ovarian adenocarcinoma SKOV-3 cells, and malignant pleural mesothelioma MESO-1 cells were examined. Jurkat cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (50 U/mL), streptomycin (50 μg/mL), and Glutamax. SKOV-3 cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (50 μ/mL), and streptomycin (50 μg/mL). MESO-1 cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (50 μ/mL), and streptomycin (50 μg/mL). All cell lines were seeded in a 384-well plate at a density of 1000 cells/well in 20 μL of media and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. After 4 h, twofold dilution samples dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were added to the cell culture at the concentration of 0.5% (0.1 µL) and incubated for 72 h. Cell viabilities were measured using a CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay and EnVision multilabel plate reader.

Platelet assay

To prepare a fibrinogen-coated plate, 20 µL of 30 µg/mL fibrinogen (from human plasma) (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) in PBS was added to a high-binding 384-well microplate (781061, Greiner Bio-One) and kept at 4 °C overnight. After washing twice with 50 µL PBS, 20 µL of 5 times-diluted Blocking One (Nacalai tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) in PBS was added to each well, then kept at room temperature for 30 min. The blocking reagent was discarded, and wells were washed twice with 50 µL PBS.

To prepare pCMV3-C-OFPSpark-ITGA2B(G991C), site-directed mutagenesis on pCMV3-C-OFPSpark-ITGA2B was achieved by PCR using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs). The mutated linear dsDNA was amplified with primers ITGA2B(G991C)-F and ITGA2B(G991C)-R (Table S11) using pCMV3-C-OFPSpark-ITGA2B as a template. The PCR product was treated with KLD Enzyme Mix (New England Biolabs), and the ligated DNA was introduced into NEB5α (New England Biolabs).

HEK293 cells cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum were transfected by pCMV3-C-ITGA2B(G991C)-OFP and pCMV3-C-ITGA3(wt)-GFP with the assistance of Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The transfected cells were seeded in an aforementioned fibrinogen-coated plate at the density of 10,000 cells/well in 19 µL of media and immediately treated with 1 µL of the tetrafibricin (produced in this study) or RGDS peptide (positive control) (abcam, Cambridge, UK) solution. After incubating at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 24 h, 5 µL of Hoechst 33342 (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was added and incubated for 60 min. The supernatant was removed by decantation, and each well was washed twice with 50 µL of PBS. The number of cells bound to the plate was measured using Opera Phenix (PerkinElmer).

Bioinformatic analysis

Sequences used for phylogenetic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1) were obtained from NCBI GenBank database (Supplementary Table 12) and were processed on Geneious Prime 2025.1.3 (https://www.geneious.com) platform. Sequence alignments were created by MAFFT v7.49056 plugin using G-INS-i algorithm, BLOSUM45 scoring matrix, 1.7 gap open penalty, 0.103 offset value. Phylogenetic trees were constructed by FastTree 2.1.1157 plugin using WAG model, Gamma20 likelihood, and pseudocounts enabled. The amino acid sequences of five amidinohydrolases, TfbX1, TfbX2, EcoX, MedX, and DstH (BCB17038), were aligned by using Clustal Omega 1.2.258 with the default settings.

Statistics

A two-tailed Welch’s t-test was performed (n = 5 per group) with a significance level of 0.01.
