Intravascular Lithotripsy With Optical Coherence Tomography for Severely Calcified Coronary Artery Interventions
Authors: Sanya Chhikara, Pruthvi C Revaiah, Preetika Maurya, Rajesh Vijayvergiya, Balwinder Singh, Krishna Prasad Nevali, Nalin K Mahesh, Ajit Mehta, Seema Patrikar, Ankush Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKey words: calcified CAD, calcium fracture, calcified nodule, intravascular lithotripsy, OCT

Severe coronary artery calcification is associated with difficult device delivery, disruption of stent polymer, and stent underexpansion. Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has shown great promise in various Disrupt CAD trials1 for calcium modification.

What is the clinical question being addressed?

What are the safety, efficacy, and OCT findings of intravascular lithotripsy for severely calcified coronary lesions in a real-world population?

What is the main finding?

IVL achieved high procedural success and favorable outcomes, regardless of calcium fracture or calcified nodule presence.

This was a prospective, multicenter, single arm observational study designed to examine the safety, efficacy, and use of optical coherence tomography (OCT) and IVL for severely calcified coronary artery disease (CAD) in a real-world population. The study complied with the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the individual institutional ethics committees of all the participating institutions.

OCT was performed before IVL, after IVL, and after stent implantation. The Shockwave Medical Coronary Rx Intravascular Lithotripsy System (Shockwave Medical Inc) was used for calcium modification in the study. Each catheter emits a total of 80 shockwave pulses in 8 cycles. An average of 6.1 ± 0.6 cycles was used for each lesion. Rotational atherectomy was done per the operator’s discretion in 18 patients (17.5%). All angiographic and OCT images were reviewed and analyzed by 2 independent experienced cardiologists. If the measurements provided by both independent reviewers were within predefined agreement thresholds, the average of the 2 values was used as the final measurement. If the difference exceeded these thresholds, a third independent reviewer assessed the images. Final adjudication was based on the median of the 3 values for continuous variables or determined by consensus in case of categorical variables.

The primary safety endpoint included freedom from perforation, no-reflow/slow flow, flow-limiting dissection after IVL therapy, and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) during hospitalization and at 30 days. MACE was defined as a composite of cardiac death, myocardial infarction, and ischemia-driven target lesion revascularization (TLR). The primary efficacy endpoint was procedural success, defined as residual diameter stenosis of <30% on angiography and stent expansion of more than 80% as assessed by OCT. Summary data are presented as mean ± SD for continuous data and percentages for categorical data. For continuous variables, comparisons between groups were determined using Student’s t-tests. Random-effects linear regression model was used to examine the relationship between the degree of calcium arc and number of calcium fractures on OCT. Time-to-event (MACE) was assessed using survival analysis by Kaplan-Meier method. Statistical software used was SPSS 23.0 version.

In this study, 103 patients with 208 heavily calcified coronary lesions undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention were enrolled at five centers across India. Mean age of the study cohort was 70.2 ± 8.09 years with 81 (78.9%) males, 39 (37.9%) diabetics, and 56 (54.4%) hypertensives. Pre-IVL OCT showed a mean ± SD calcium arc of 269.7 ± 76.6 degrees, a calcium score as defined by Fujino et al2 of 3.9 ± 0.3, a minimum lumen area at maximum calcium of 2.8 ± 1.2 mm2, and a distal reference diameter of 3.1 ± 0.5 mm. Calcium nodules were seen in 26 (25.3%) patients. Sixty-five (63.1%) patients required balloon dilatation for IVL crossing. Following IVL, OCT revealed calcium fracture in 89 (86.4%) patients with ≥ 3 fractures seen in 42.7% patients and increase in minimum lumen area to 4.2 ± 1.3 mm2. After stent placement, minimum stent area (MSA) and eccentricity index3 achieved at calcium were 7.4 ± 1.8 mm2 and 0.88 ± 0.06, respectively.

The primary safety endpoint was achieved in 97.1% (95% CI: 93% to 100%) of patients. The primary efficacy endpoint was achieved in 96.1% (95% CI: 92%-99%) of patients. In comparison to the Disrupt CAD III study,4 which was designed for regulatory approval of coronary IVL and showed a MACE of 13.8% at 1-year follow-up, the 30-day and 1-year MACE rates in our study were 2.9% and 12.6%, respectively (Figure 1). We also observed acceptable rates of TLR (2.9%) and stent thrombosis (1.9%) compared to the CAD III study (4.3% TLR and 1.3% stent thrombosis).4 No patients developed flow limiting dissection, perforation, or slow flow after IVL therapy.

In terms of procedural outcomes at the site of maximum calcium, we saw stent expansion and MSAs of 101% ± 18.1% and 7.4 ± 1.8 mm2 compared to the 102% ± 29% and 6.5 ± 2.1 mm2 seen in the OCT subset of the CAD III study.4 Higher percentage of post-IVL NC balloon dilatation (74.8% vs 20.2% in CAD III) at higher pressure (20 ± 2.9 atm) was likely the reason behind the larger MSA. In our study, calcium fractures were seen in 86.4% of lesions compared with 67.4% in the OCT substudy of CAD III.

Using a random-effects linear regression model at the lesion level, we observed a linear trend (P = 0.034) between the degree of calcium arc and number of calcium fractures on OCT. Additionally, lesions with calcium arc ≥2700 had 3 times higher odds of having fractures compared to those with < 2700. However, there was no difference in final MSAs at the site of maximum calcium whether calcium fractures were present or not (MSA 7.6 ± 1.8 mm2 and 6.6 ± 1.7 mm2, respectively; P = 0.061). This finding would imply that even in the absence of visible fractures on OCT, one could expect adequate lesion modification and similar outcomes compared to when fractures are present. When we compared patients with calcified nodules to those without it, we noted no difference in MSA at calcium (7.6 ± 2.1 vs 7.4 ± 1.7; P = 0.49), stent expansion at calcium (99.5% ± 17.4% vs 101.5% ± 18.4%; P = 0.62), and eccentricity index (0.87 ± 0.08 vs 0.88 ± 0.06; P = 0.67). This was also seen in our previously published study.3

There were several limitations in this study. Firstly, this study was a single arm, prospective study and did not compare IVL to other calcium modification modalities. Additionally, the study population was limited to Indian centers and thus the findings may not be generalizable to other populations. The novelty of our study is the inclusion of heavily calcified complex cases (57.3% triple vessel disease, 41.7% bifurcation lesions, 34.9% left main interventions, and 21.4% patients having left ventricular ejection fraction <30%) representing a real-world scenario.

In conclusion, the major findings of our study are as follows: 1) primary safety and efficacy endpoints of IVL were very good and similar to previous studies with acceptable 30-day and 1-year outcomes despite the increased complexity of lesions; 2) absence of calcium fractures on OCT was not associated with lower MSAs or worse outcomes; and 3) there were no significant differences in the final MSA and stent expansion in the lesions with calcified nodules compared to those without calcified nodules.
