Amiodarone Induced Ventricular Fibrillation in Unveiled WPW Syndrome
Authors: Fadhlan Abdur Rahman, Natasya Febrilia Yulianti, Abdul Gofur, Rerdin Julario
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome predisposes patients to life-threatening tachyarrhythmias due to rapid ventricular activation via an accessory pathway. Malignant arrhythmias may occur unexpectedly, even in previously asymptomatic individuals.Case SummaryA 36-year-old man presented with sudden palpitations and a regular wide-complex tachycardia consistent with antidromic atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia. The rhythm rapidly deteriorated into preexcited atrial fibrillation and progressed to ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest requiring immediate defibrillation and cardiopulmonary resuscitation, with successful return of spontaneous circulation. Postresuscitation electrocardiography demonstrated persistent ventricular preexcitation. Electrophysiological study subsequently confirmed a left lateral accessory pathway, and successful radiofrequency catheter ablation resulted in complete resolution of ventricular preexcitation.DiscussionThis case highlights the malignant electrophysiological behavior of WPW and the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of wide-complex tachycardia, particularly in resource-limited settings.Take-Home MessagesWPW may rapidly progress from antidromic atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia to ventricular fibrillation. Early recognition, prompt electrical cardioversion/defibrillation, and timely referral for catheter ablation are critical.

History of Presentation

A 36-year-old previously healthy man presented to the emergency department with a 30-minute history of persistent palpitations that began shortly after coffee consumption. He denied chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, or prior similar episodes.

On arrival at the emergency department, the patient was alert and hemodynamically stable with a blood pressure of 106/60 mm Hg, a heart rate of 260 beats/min, a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air, and a temperature of 36.5 °C. Physical examination revealed no jugular venous distension, normal heart and lung sounds, and warm extremities with a capillary refill time of <2 seconds.

Past Medical History

There was no history of cardiovascular disease, medication use, or sudden cardiac death in the family.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis for a regular wide-complex tachycardia included ventricular tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardia with aberrant conduction, and preexcited tachycardia related to an accessory pathway, including antidromic atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia in Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome.

Management

Intravenous amiodarone was administered as a 150-mg bolus followed by infusion. During monitoring, the rhythm became irregular with a wide-complex tachyarrhythmia consistent with preexcited atrial fibrillation (AF) (Figure 3).Figure 3Atrial Fibrillation in Wolff-Parkinson-White SyndromeThis electrocardiogram demonstrates an irregularly irregular rhythm with wide and morphologically variable QRS complexes, characteristic of preexcited atrial fibrillation. Atrial Fibrillation in Wolff-Parkinson-White Syndrome

This electrocardiogram demonstrates an irregularly irregular rhythm with wide and morphologically variable QRS complexes, characteristic of preexcited atrial fibrillation. Notably, several R-R intervals between preexcitation signals are ≤250 milliseconds, indicating a high risk for degeneration into ventricular fibrillation. The findings highlight the urgency for prompt recognition and management in patients with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome presenting with atrial fibrillation.

While the infusion was ongoing, the patient developed ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest. Immediate defibrillation at 200 J and cardiopulmonary resuscitation were performed, achieving return of spontaneous circulation after 5 cycles.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postresuscitation electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with persistent ventricular preexcitation (Figures 4 and 5). The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for monitoring. His clinical course remained stable without recurrent tachyarrhythmias, and he was subsequently referred to a tertiary cardiac center for electrophysiological study and catheter ablation. Electrophysiological study confirmed a left lateral accessory pathway. Successful radiofrequency catheter ablation was subsequently performed. Discussion

WPW syndrome is characterized by an accessory atrioventricular pathway that bypasses the physiologic delay of the atrioventricular node and allows rapid ventricular activation. Although many individuals remain asymptomatic, the presence of a rapidly conducting pathway can permit extremely fast ventricular rates during AF, creating a substrate for ventricular fibrillation (VF) and sudden cardiac death.1, 2, 3 The lifetime risk of malignant arrhythmia is low but unpredictable, and life-threatening events may represent the first clinical manifestation of the disease.2,3 Epidemiologic studies further show that ventricular preexcitation is more frequently identified in younger male patients, consistent with the present case.4

This case illustrates the malignant electrophysiological behavior of WPW, in which a regular wide-complex tachycardia consistent with antidromic atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia (AVRT) rapidly deteriorated into preexcited AF and subsequently VF. Antidromic AVRT is uncommon compared with orthodromic AVRT but produces a fully preexcited wide-complex tachycardia that may closely mimic ventricular tachycardia, creating significant diagnostic uncertainty in emergency settings. In such circumstances, current algorithms appropriately recommend treating undifferentiated wide-complex tachycardia as ventricular tachycardia to avoid delays in life-saving therapy (Figure 7). Intravenous procainamide or amiodarone can be used in the hospital to terminate hemodynamically stable wide QRS complex tachycardia of unknown etiology. Adenosine should be avoided if preexcitation on resting electrocardiogram (ECG) indicates a preexcitation tachycardia. In cases with antidromic reentry, adenosine may precipitate cardiac arrest if there is induction of AF by adenosine, as may occasionally occur.4Figure 7Algorithm for the Acute Management of Patients Presenting With Wide-Complex Tachycardia in the Absence of an Established Diagnosis4i.v. = intravenous; other abbreviation as in Figure 6.

The pharmacologic management of preexcited AF remains challenging, particularly in resource-limited environments. Guideline-recommended therapy includes procainamide, flecainide, ibutilide, or electrical cardioversion, all of which slow accessory pathway conduction while avoiding preferential atrioventricular nodal blockade.1 Not only in our hospital, procainamide, ibutilide, and flecainide were not available in all medical facilities in Indonesia, limiting the use of recommended first-line agents and constraining therapeutic options. Similar to Mongolia, our country lacks all 3 agents. For Class 1C antiarrhythmic agents, Indonesia only has propafenone,6 which is not commonly stocked in all medical facilities, including our rural hospital setting. While not explicitly listed in public BPOM (Indonesian Food and Drug Administration) rejection data, the absence of these agents is likely due to a combination of market availability and therapeutic alternatives. These drugs generally fall under the “Special Access Scheme” category, where the drug can be imported only for specific patient needs, on the recommendation of cardiac electrophysiologists and not readily available in general hospitals, let alone in the emergency room. Clinicians in Indonesia have access to other antiarrhythmic drugs such as amiodarone, which is generally available.

Amiodarone remains controversial in this context. A systematic review involving 751 patients reported VF occurring exclusively in case reports, with several episodes directly attributed to amiodarone administration in preexcited AF.7,8 The proposed mechanism relates to the acute electrophysiological effects of intravenous amiodarone, which may suppress atrioventricular nodal conduction more than antegrade accessory pathway conduction, facilitating rapid ventricular activation and degeneration into VF.9 This mechanism provides a plausible explanation for the rapid clinical deterioration observed in this case. In the present case, intravenous amiodarone was selected in the setting of a regular wide-complex tachycardia with an initially uncertain mechanism, in a hemodynamically stable patient. At the time of presentation, the rhythm was interpreted as a stable wide-complex tachycardia, and pharmacologic therapy was initiated as an initial strategy. However, in retrospect, early electrical cardioversion would likely have represented a safer and more definitive approach, particularly given the possibility of an accessory pathway–mediated arrhythmia. This case therefore highlights the limitations of empiric pharmacologic therapy in undifferentiated wide-complex tachycardia and reinforces current recommendations favoring electrical cardioversion when diagnostic uncertainty persists.

When pharmacologic therapy is limited or diagnostic uncertainty persists, electrical cardioversion remains the most reliable and definitive acute therapy for preexcited AF.1,10 In the present case, prompt defibrillation restored sinus rhythm and prevented further deterioration, underscoring the importance of maintaining a low threshold for electrical cardioversion in unstable or deteriorating wide-complex tachycardia.

This case also highlights the therapeutic gap that exists in resource-limited settings, where recommended antiarrhythmic agents and electrophysiology services may be unavailable. Bridging this gap requires improved access to essential antiarrhythmic drugs, enhanced clinician awareness of WPW-specific management, and early referral to tertiary electrophysiology centers.

In this context, the present case offers several additional clinically relevant insights. Although VF following amiodarone administration in WPW syndrome has been previously reported, this case provides additional clinically relevant insights. It uniquely demonstrates a stepwise electrocardiographic progression from antidromic AVRT to preexcited AF and ultimately VF, offering direct visualization of dynamic arrhythmic degeneration that is rarely captured in prior reports. It also reflects real-world decision-making under diagnostic uncertainty in undifferentiated wide-complex tachycardia, highlighting the potential for empiric antiarrhythmic therapy to precipitate malignant arrhythmias. In addition, the unavailability of recommended agents such as procainamide, ibutilide, and flecainide underscores the impact of resource limitations on acute management. Collectively, these features extend prior observations beyond mechanistic confirmation and emphasize the interplay among electrophysiology, clinical decision-making, and health care system constraints.

Beyond these considerations, this case also emphasizes the challenges of risk stratification in WPW. Emerging evidence suggests that surface ECG features alone are insufficient to reliably predict malignant arrhythmic potential. Recent data, including a multicenter study by Robles et al,11 demonstrate that even patients with intermittent ventricular preexcitation historically considered a lower-risk subgroup, may exhibit electrophysiological properties associated with high-risk accessory pathways, including rapid antegrade conduction and inducible arrhythmias.11 These findings challenge the traditional reliance on noninvasive markers for risk stratification and highlight the limitations of surface ECG in identifying patients at risk of life-threatening events. This aligns with the present case, in which surface ECG findings alone could not predict the malignant clinical course. Collectively, these observations reinforce that noninvasive assessment is insufficient for definitive risk stratification and support early referral for electrophysiological study following stabilization.

Following referral to a tertiary electrophysiology center, the patient underwent electrophysiological study, which demonstrated a left lateral accessory pathway with antegrade conduction properties. Radiofrequency catheter ablation was successfully performed at the left lateral region via a transseptal approach, resulting in elimination of accessory pathway conduction within seconds of energy delivery. Postablation 12-lead ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm with complete resolution of ventricular preexcitation, including disappearance of the delta wave and normalization of QRS morphology, consistent with successful elimination of antegrade accessory pathway conduction (Figure 6). These findings generally support the initial surface ECG–based localization, although the precise electrophysiological mapping data were not fully available for review. Despite this limitation, the clinical course and postablation findings strongly support the presence of a rapidly conducting accessory pathway with malignant potential.

Radiofrequency catheter ablation remains the definitive treatment for symptomatic WPW and is recommended as first-line therapy because of its high success rate and favorable safety profile. Early referral for electrophysiological evaluation following stabilization is therefore essential to prevent recurrent malignant arrhythmias. In the present case, the occurrence of VF as an initial manifestation further underscores the importance of timely definitive intervention, even in patients without prior symptoms, to mitigate the risk of future life-threatening events. These considerations highlight the need for heightened clinical vigilance, early recognition of preexcited arrhythmias, and timely definitive management in patients with ventricular preexcitation.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the malignant arrhythmic potential of WPW syndrome in a previously asymptomatic patient who rapidly progressed from antidromic AVRT to preexcited AF and VF cardiac arrest. In resource-limited settings, management of undifferentiated wide-complex tachycardia may expose patients to suboptimal therapy when recommended agents are unavailable. Early recognition of preexcited arrhythmias, a low threshold for electrical cardioversion, and timely referral for catheter ablation are critical to prevent catastrophic outcomes.
