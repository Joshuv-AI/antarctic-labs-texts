Application of ultrasonography elastography in chronic transplanted kidney renal insufficiency: a narrative review
Authors: Anjie Chen, Yuting Wang, Yanrong Yang, Shu Luo, Xuelian Chen, Jiaojiao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveChronic renal allograft dysfunction, driven by fibrosis, remains a major challenge post-transplantation. Traditional biomarkers lack sensitivity for early detection, and biopsy involves certain risks. This review evaluates ultrasound elastography, particularly shear wave elastography (SWE), as a noninvasive tool for assessing graft fibrosis and guiding timely interventions.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted of the PubMed and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases (2010–2024), including studies in English and Chinese. Keywords included “ultrasound elastography”, “transplanted renal insufficiency”, “postoperative monitoring of transplanted kidneys” and “two-dimensional shear wave elastography”.Key Content and FindingsSWE demonstrates promise in detecting early fibrosis before functional decline, with transplanted kidneys’ superficial location enhancing feasibility. Studies indicate that SWE correlates with histopathological fibrosis (Banff scores) and graft dysfunction (e.g., estimated glomerular filtration rate decline and proteinuria). However, technical challenges include anatomical anisotropy, perfusion variability, and viscoelastic complexity, leading to inconsistent results. Innovations such as shear wave dispersion slope analysis improve rejection prediction, while high-frequency SWE enhances the assessment of chronic lesions. Among the notable clinical applications of SWE include its potential to reduce reliance on biopsy and the prediction of graft viability.ConclusionsSWE is potentially transformative for noninvasive graft monitoring, enabling early intervention and personalized care. Standardization of protocols and multicenter validation are critical to addressing the current limitations. Future research should focus on establishing normative stiffness values, refining viscoelastic models, and validating prognostic utility to influence clinical guidelines and policy frameworks for transplant management.Keywords: Ultrasound, shear wave imaging, elastography, transplanted renal insufficiency

Introduction

Renal transplantation remains the optimal treatment for end-stage renal disease and has significantly benefited short-term outcomes. Historically, improvements to survival were limited to the first year following transplantation, but mid- to long-term graft survival rates have been steadily increasing (1), with recent data indicating 1-year patient and graft survival rates exceeding 90% (2). However, expanding donor criteria, prolonging posttransplant survival, and sustaining graft function beyond 10 years remain key challenges. Late graft dysfunction manifests as elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (SCr) levels and decreases in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and proteinuria, progressing to transplant renal insufficiency (3). The primary pathological drivers of graft loss include interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and glomerulosclerosis (4). Transplant biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard but carries risks of hemorrhage, arteriovenous fistula, and graft loss (5), limiting its utility for routine monitoring. This underscores the urgent need for the development of noninvasive techniques that can comprehensively assess graft health. Ultrasound elastography has emerged as a promising tool for evaluating tissue stiffness through mechanical wave propagation. Unlike conventional imaging, it provides quantitative biomechanical data critical for detecting fibrosis—a hallmark of chronic graft injury. Previous studies demonstrated a significant association between the degree of renal fibrosis and glomerular filtration rate (GFR), SCr, and proteinuria. However, fibrosis may occur before significant changes in renal function are observed (6,7). A growing number of blood and urine biomarkers are being used in the detection of renal fibrosis. Cystatin-C, a cysteine protease inhibitor, is continuously produced by nucleated cells and released into the bloodstream. This substance is typically reabsorbed by the renal tubules and undergoes catabolic metabolism, rather than re-entering the bloodstream (8). In the process of identifying mild renal dysfunction, cystatin-C demonstrates high sensitivity to changes in renal function. Given the relatively short half-life of creatinine, cystatin-C has a significant advantage over creatinine in the assessment of acute kidney injury (AKI) (9). The detection of profibrotic growth factors such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) in urine may reflect ongoing renal inflammation and initial fibrotic changes, thereby serving as markers of fibrosis (10). For example, molecules including TGF-β-induced gene H3 (βig-H3) and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) have been studied: βig-H3 can be detected in the urine of patients with diabetes before the onset of proteinuria, indicating that βig-H3 is an early and sensitive biomarker for diabetic kidney fibrosis (11); meanwhile, PAI-1 has been shown to be associated with kidney damage and fibrosis in patients with diabetic nephropathy and progressive chronic glomerulonephritis (12). Given that these clinical biomarkers appear abnormally late relative to the onset of fibrosis, there is an urgent need for the early detection of fibrosis to enable early intervention. The noninvasive nature and repeatability of elastic imaging make it particularly suitable for serial assessment. In transplanted kidneys, superficial anatomical positioning and reduced respiratory motion interference enhance technical feasibility as compared to native organs. The related research highlights elastography’s diagnostic value in hepatic, thyroid, and breast pathologies, where tissue stiffening correlates with disease progression. For renal grafts, preliminary studies suggest that traditional biomarkers of shear wave elastography (SWE) are capable of detecting early fibrosis before functional decline becomes apparent. This positions SWE as a potentially transformative modality for long-term graft monitoring. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-646/rc).

Methods

We performed a literature search of the PubMed and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases using the following keywords: “ultrasound elastography”, “transplanted renal insufficiency”, “postoperative monitoring of transplanted kidneys” and “two-dimensional shear wave elastography”. The search was conducted over the time period from 2010 to 2024 and included articles published in English and Chinese. The eligible literature included reviews, systematic evaluations, meta-analyses, and human clinical trials, as indicated in Table 1. The time interval between patients’ first biopsies after transplantation and the technical method of measurement (imaging method, size of the sampling frame, and specific location of the measurement) were found to affect the results, with the sample size directly influencing the confidence of the results, and the choice of the technical method determining the accuracy and the applicability of the measurement. Moreover, the size of the sample directly affects the confidence of the results, while the technical method used determines the accuracy and the range of the measurement. The skill of the physician, including individual experience and technical proficiency, also affects the results. By comparing ultrasound parameters with various clinical parameters, including renal resistance index (RI), eGFR, and pathologic findings, we found that elastography was effective in evaluating and predicting transplant-related lesions. Therefore, we considered these subjective and objective factors when interpreting transplant renal function measurements to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results. Principle and classification

In recent years, ultrasound elastography, which is capable of detecting changes in the stiffness of tissues when subjected to mechanical effects, including compressive forces or shear waves, has attracted extensive research attention due to its excellent performance in noninvasively assessing the mechanical properties of tissues. The biophysical information provided by this technique is of considerable importance in the medical field, especially in the diagnosis and treatment of diseases that involve the phenomenon of tissue hardening. For example, in assessing the stiffness of organs such as the liver, thyroid, and mammary gland, SWE can provide critical quantitative data to help physicians arrive at more accurate diagnoses (the principle of transverse wave generation is shown in Figure 1; the process by which transverse waves quantify the degree of fibrosis is illustrated in Figure 2). In particular, for transplanted kidneys, it is relatively easy to implement elastography due to its relatively superficial location and lower susceptibility to being influenced by respiratory motility. Elastography methods can be categorized into static or quasistatic strain imaging involving internal or external pressure effects and transverse wave imaging involving dynamic excitation generated by ultrasound pulses. In static elastography techniques, the generation of stress relies on the manual compression of the tissue, whereas during quasistatic elastography, the characterization of stress over time is determined by physiological vibrations (26) (e.g., heartbeat). These imaging techniques are limited to qualitative analysis and do not allow for quantitative measurements due to the difficulty in measuring the applied forces: in static elastography, the manually applied compressive forces depend on the subjective judgment of the operator, whereas in quasistatic elastography, the stresses generated in organs with autonomous physiological movements, such as the heart, are uncontrollable and difficult to measure accurately (27). SWE is an advanced medical imaging technique that provides novel perspectives and tools for clinical diagnosis. It focuses on the measurement of tissue stiffness and stiffness-related parameters by measuring shear waves propagating through a medium, thus assessing the viscoelastic characteristics of these tissues. The shear wave technique perturbs tissue through use of the acoustic radiation force generated by the ultrasound beam, which can also be referred to as an “acoustic airflow”. This pressure exerts a thrust on the tissue in the direction of propagation, and an elastic medium, such as human tissue, responds to this thrust by exciting mechanical waves due to its inherent resilience. This process involves shear waves propagating laterally through the tissue. Transverse waves propagate at markedly lower velocities than do sound waves by several orders of magnitude, allowing for imaging at high frame rates via ultrasonic technology and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

ARFI. Assuming that a, b, and c are three elastic bodies, when ARFI acts vertically downward with force F on a, a compression wave P is generated, the body is excited to displace vertically downward, and the b body produces a shear effect. The resultant shear force also drives the b body to displace downward, and the c body remains unaltered, while the a body is restored to its original position. The shear force likewise drives the b and c bodies to recover, and therefore, the resulting shear wave S is the transverse wave, which is perpendicular to the direction of the propagation of P wave. Strain wave elastography

SWE was one of the early applications of elastography and among the first technological tools adopted in clinical practice, with the physician examining the tissue by applying probe pressure. This method has the ability to assess the elasticity in superficial organs such as the breast and thyroid, as confirmed by a previous study (29). However, for organs located deeper in the body, such as the liver and kidneys, some researchers have reservations regarding the elasticity detection and do not believe that this method can be applied equally effectively to these deep organs (30). An alternative to strain imaging, real-time tissue elastography (RTE), is a technique that quantifies the tissue displacement induced by the heartbeat, thus enabling imaging of the elastic properties of deep tissues. One study validated this approach via qualitative analysis and also demonstrated that renal elasticity values change significantly with the degree of renal fibrosis (31). Similarly, another study on autologous renal strain wave elastography found significant differences in values of the renal parenchymal strain index (SI) between healthy individuals and patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), which was effective in between these groups. However, in the CKD patient population, SI values are significantly different across disease stages (32). Therefore, studies addressing the relationship between elasticity and renal fibrosis using strain imaging techniques have primarily focused on kidney transplantation (33,34). Orlacchio et al. recruited 50 patients with clinically suspected transplanted renal fibrosis and used the Banff score to determine whether fibrosis was occurring in the cortex; moreover, the mean tissue stiffness measurement of fibrosis was assessed via area under the curve analysis of participants’ workup characteristics. The diagnostic accuracy was 0.95, suggesting that RTE is capable of assessing transplant renal fibrosis (35).

Acoustic radiation force impulse (ARFI) elastography

The term “ARFI” is commonly used to refer to an excitation technique that does not depend on the physical quantity to be measured. In contrast, the term “ARFI imaging” specifically refers to the excitation and measurement or display of tissue displacement or related physical processes utilizing an acoustic radiation force pulse. Therefore, the technique of using acoustic radiation force pulses to excite and display transverse wave velocities is also referred to as the “quantitative acoustic radiation force pulse technique” (36). In this technique, high-intensity, short-duration acoustic radiation forces are used to push tissue displacement perpendicular to the tissue surface, and the tissue is subsequently imaged through measurement of the tissue displacement within a specified region of interest (ROI). The elastography can be overlaid with B-mode images to assist the physician in accurately determining the location of the measurement (37). In contrast to traditional quasistatic strain elastography methods, ARFI imaging does not rely on the compression exerted by the transducer to acquire the image. This technique is unique in its ability to focus excitation on deep organs in the body, which makes it particularly useful in imaging deep organs that are difficult to deform through the application of pressure at the body surface. Nevertheless, similar to strain elasticity imaging techniques, the compressive effect of the transducer may nonetheless induce a nonlinear response in the tissue, and it should therefore be minimized during ARFI imaging. In addition, the “push” pulses used in ARFI imaging have longer durations than do conventional diagnostic pulses, and current imaging methods typically use relatively low frame rates to keep the acoustic output within diagnostic limits (36).

One-dimensional transient elastography (1D-TE)

1D-TE is currently the most widely used assessment technique in the field of liver fibrosis diagnosis (38), but it cannot directly superimpose B-mode images, which in turn cannot avoid interstitial areas such as blood vessels. Additionally, the transverse waves are generated by excitation from a superficial location, making the imaging results susceptible to effects such as ascites.

Point SWE

It is often necessary to enhance motivation efficiency through a focusing process. Focused ARFI beams have the greatest thrust effect in the focal region, where shear waves are generated with the highest intensity and propagate over relatively long distances; in the unfocused region, the “thrust” generated by the ARFI excitation is extremely weak, and accordingly, the resulting shear waves are extremely small. The method of evaluating the elasticity and stiffness of tissue by measuring the propagation velocity of shear waves generated at the focus of the ARFI excitation is called “point shear wave elastography” (p-SWE). Unlike strain imaging, it does not measure the displacement of the tissue itself. Conversely, ARFI excitation generates longitudinal waves first, after which a small portion of the longitudinal wave absorbs another portion of the acoustic energy, which is converted into transverse waves. Moreover, unlike 1D-TE, p-SWE visualizes the liver directly under the guidance of B-mode images such that the ROI is located in selective parenchyma; thus, interstitial matter such as blood vessels can be avoided, and the transverse waves originate deeper and are less affected by ascites (27,39).

Two-dimensional SWE (2D SWE) (40,41)

The aforementioned single-focus focusing methods are insufficient for realizing two- and even three-dimensional shear wave imaging. A large range of acoustic radiation forces must be used as driving forces to ensure that the generated shear waves cover as wide an area as possible. 2D SWE achieves faster imaging speeds than does conventional transverse wave propagation by exciting multiple focal regions in rapid succession. This technique generates a near-cylindrical transverse wave propagation cone, allowing real-time monitoring of 2D transverse wave propagation, which in turn measures the transverse wave velocity or Young’s modulus and generates the corresponding quantitative elasticity image (36). This technology enables real-time color quantitative elastography and superimposes it on the B-mode ultrasound image (42), thus enabling physicians to simultaneously capture rich assessment information such as tissue stiffness and anatomical structure (43). Different vendors have used a variety of strategies to accomplish this. For instance, Toshiba, Phillip, Mindray, and Siemens have used conventional ARFI excitation and implemented multipoint focusing for ARFI excitation. This results in a columnar “push” of focused ARFI, which produces a strong detectable shear wave over a wide area. Acoustic Super Sonic has adopted a different strategy: they use an ultrahigh-speed ARFI transmitter to rapidly fire point-focused ARFIs at different depths of focus in sequence, and because the pulses are fired at a much faster rate than the shear wave propagation speed, the rapid succession of rapidly fired point-focused pulses also creates an approximate columnar beam of thrust. This technique is known as “SuperSonic imaging” and is capable of generating sufficiently strong shear waves over a larger and wider range. For one, this approach allows for the coherent enhancement of the shear waves to increase the amplitude and propagation distance on the one hand; for another, it disperses the energy generated by focusing. The efficiency of this technique in generating shear waves is relatively high in a given area and at a fixed acoustic power. Objective factors, including the complexity, anatomy, functional status, and possible pathological changes of the transplanted kidney itself, can also exert a significant impact on the results. Alterations in tissue stiffness have been associated with a variety of diseases, such as malignant tissue, cirrhosis associated with fibrosis, and atherosclerosis and calcification. In elastically homogeneous materials, hardness can typically be expressed by the modulus of elasticity. However, for biological tissues, the determinants of stiffness are more complex, involving a variety of factors such as the fat content of the tissue, fibrous structure, etc. (28).

Renal stiffness is influenced by a variety of factors, including anatomy, anisotropy, blood perfusion, ureteral pressure, inflammation, and edema (44), and modulus of elasticity values are also affected by these factors. During visual and microscopic assessment, the renal anatomy exhibits extremely complex heterogeneous characteristics as compared to organs such as the liver and breast. Specifically, due to the globular nature of the glomerulus and the tortuous morphology of the renal tubules, the renal cortex is not arranged in a regular or neat linear structure. The renal medulla consists of Hele’s rings, straight small blood vessels, and collecting ducts, which are arranged parallel to one another from the renal peritoneum inward. When an acoustic pulse is emitted in the parallel direction of these structures and shear waves propagate perpendicularly along them, multiple interfaces are formed, resulting in a slowing down of the shear waves and consequently a reduction of the elastic modulus. In contrast, when acoustic radiation propagates perpendicular to these anisotropic structures, transverse waves penetrate them in parallel, but the lack of interfacial effects leads to faster shear wave propagation, resulting in a higher modulus of elasticity (45). These structures are oriented along different directions, creating a distinctive anisotropic feature (46). The related studies report a variety of results, indicating a curious paradox: Elasticity usually increases when kidney fibrosis increases, but kidney elasticity may occasionally be lower even when fibrosis is severe. For example, in patients with CKD, significant correlations between measured shear wave velocity (SWV) values and SCr, eGFR, and pathologic parameters have been reported (25). Moreover, renal SWV values decrease with decreased eGFR (47). Meanwhile, on SWE, the values of renal parenchymal hardness in patients with CKD, as well as the intrasubject variability of hardness, have been found to be significantly higher than those in healthy controls (48). In one study, ARFI imaging mean values showed a significant intermediate correlation with fibrosis grading (49); however, SWV was reported to be significantly different between transplantation samples with and without fibrosis in another study (15). In other research, the ARFI technique was found to not be effective in predicting the various stages of CKD, and there was no statistically significant correlation between SWV and any of the pathologic indicators associated with renal fibrosis (50); other work reported there being no correlation between ultrasound elasticity parameters and histopathologic alterations in allogeneic renal grafts (51).

The heterogeneity and anisotropy of the renal anatomy described above are important factors contributing to the inconsistent results in the related research. Ries et al. reported an anisotropy rate of 40% in the medulla and 22% in the cortex in an MRI diffusion experiment (46). Another study of an in vivo SWI technique found that the anisotropy rates in the renal medulla were significantly higher than those of the inner and outer cortex, with values of 31.8%, 29.7%, and 10.5%, respectively (52). In animal models, including porcine models, these features typically affect the elastic modulus and viscoelastic properties of the kidney, with studies indicating statistically significant differences in shear elasticity and shear viscosity within the kidney, suggesting that the overall structure of the kidney is not homogeneous (53). The kidney is surrounded by an outer fibrous membrane, which is similar in structure to the Glisson’s capsule of the liver and consists of dense connective tissue and elastic fibers. Any damage to the fibrous membrane may affect renal hemodynamics and urinary pressure, resulting in changes in renal stiffness measurements, which in turn affects overall renal function (54).

Gennisson et al. noted that the elasticity values in the kidneys vary in response to alterations in tissue blood flow and fluctuations in urine pressure levels (52), while other authors found renal perfusion to be reduced, leading to a decrease in SWV (55). Liu et al. (56) found that the longer the renal vein is ligated, the higher the Young’s modulus E of the renal cortex, renal medulla, and renal sinus is. In contrast, in the case of renal artery stenosis, Young’s modulus in all three of these regions demonstrates a tendency to decrease. In addition, they reported higher elasticity values in the cortical region of the kidney than in the medullary region, with the sinusoids exhibiting the lowest modulus of elasticity among the regions of the autologous kidney. Thus, arterial perfusion is a factor influencing the elasticity of isolated kidneys, and both in vivo and in vitro, cortical elasticity values change with greater sensitivity with changes in renal perfusion. Follow-up histopathologic findings confirmed structural and parenchymal changes consistent with changes in elasticity in all three of these regions. In the same study, a strong effect of urinary pressure on elasticity measurements of the kidney was also observed.

These studies illustrate that the complex structure of the kidney, anisotropy, and renal perfusion all significantly influence the measurement of renal elasticity. Given the lack of a uniform understanding of the variability of elastic characteristics in different renal diseases, cautious is advised in interpreting the results obtained with ultrasound elastography. Future studies should capture elastography measurements at specific angles or orientations, account for the perfusion status of the kidneys, and attempt to exclude samples with hydronephrosis and urinary tract obstruction to ensure the reproducibility of the results and thus improve the accuracy and reliability of the diagnosis.

Clinical applications of elastography

Acoustic palpation imaging for preoperative donor evaluation

Cadaveric donor kidneys have also shown a significant improvement in graft survival compared to the use of living donor kidneys. Although there may be a higher risk in the initial posttransplant phase, patients who have been on dialysis for a decade or more have shown a significant improvement in long-term survival after receiving a high-quality deceased donor kidney transplant. This phenomenon is a strong indication that techniques for obtaining and preserving transplanted kidneys have been optimized and that the delayed recovery of graft function and the effective management of graft-related complications have improved significantly. In addition, it reflects a significant advancement in the level of care for comorbidities in patients on long-term dialysis (57). Before transplantation, a biopsy of the donor’s kidney is first required to assess whether the stability of renal function can be maintained after transplantation, and the Remuzzi score for tissue biopsy before implantation is considered the primary criterion for kidney transplantation evaluation (58). However, complications from biopsy are consistently one of the destabilizing factors in achieving a good patient prognosis. Therefore, a novel approach—ultrasound sound touch elastography (STE)—was developed, and Pan et al. found that the use of isolated kidney STE can assess kidney quality and predict graft kidney function after transplantation (59). In this prospective study, the investigators applied a novel metric (STE value) and subsequently used a random forest algorithm to identify the key predictor variables associated with the Remuzzi score and allograft function. The results showed that the specificity of STE value significantly exceeded the Remuzzi score and that donor kidneys could be directly transplanted without biopsy when the STE value ≤11.741. The results of this study suggest that pretransplanted kidneys measured with isolated STE can be used to assess donor quality and avoid unnecessary biopsies, thereby preventing a variety of serious complications due to biopsies prior to transplantation.

Shear wave dispersion slope for the prediction of transplanted kidney rejection

The rejection reaction fundamentally entails the recipient’s immune system recognizing and responding to donor antigens, typically resulting in an immunological assault on the allogeneic tissue or organ. This can significantly impede the long-term viability and operational integrity of the transplanted organ or tissue. When rejection occurs in transplanted kidneys, it manifests itself as a change in the stiffness of the renal parenchyma, so its stiffness can be elastically quantified. This point is particularly important in distinguishing this condition from acute tubular injury, as it can also manifest as increased hardness due to surrounding edema. Previous studies on elastic imaging in AKI have found that SWE estimates of kidney stiffness are higher in patients with AKI than in those without AKI (60,61). However, human tissues are viscoelastic, and their viscosity is characterized by a variable lag in the return of the tissue to its original state after the application and removal of stress. Maralescu et al. measured tissue viscosity using viscous plane-wave ultrasound (ViPLUS) and correlated it with the patient’s clinical parameters, showing that eGFR correlated with the hardness of the renal cortex (r=0.5699; P<0.01) and tissue viscosity (r=0.3335; P=0.0180). This suggests that the ViPLUS technique could also be used to identify patients with chronic allograft injury (62). Currently, most ultrasound elastography models employ a simplified linear elasticity model, which primarily quantifies the elastic properties of tissues but tends to ignore their viscous components. In contrast, shear wave dispersion imaging—an elastography technique—can be used to assess tissue viscosity.

The principle of the transverse wave dispersion map is as follows (63): first, the displacement caused by the transverse wave is measured via color Doppler ultrasound imaging. Second, the shear wave signal is decomposed into corresponding frequency components via Fourier transform, and the phase change values of the shear wave at various frequencies are analyzed. Subsequently, the transverse wave velocity is roughly estimated by the phase difference method. Fourth, the SWV gradient is estimated based on the dependence of the SWV distribution on frequency. The SWE is affected by the slope of the viscoelastic medium, and its velocity gradient is related to the change in viscosity of the medium (64). Sugimoto et al. found that the dispersion slope showed higher predictive efficacy than did SWC values in predicting the severity of necroinflammation in tissues such as the liver (65), which was corroborated by Lee et al. (66). These studies indicate that inflammatory activity leads to increased values of the dispersion slope in the liver. Thus in the future, the viscosity of tissues such as transplanted kidneys may be assessable indirectly via the measurement of the shear wave dispersion slope to confirm that an inflammatory response is occurring. Previous reports on transplanted kidneys by dispersion slope are scarce. However, Kim et al. (63) measured the stiffness values of renal cortex, stiffness values of renal sinus fat, and shear wave dispersion slopes using SWE and further evaluated the clinical and pathological factors associated with the ratio of stiffness (RS) of the cortex to the renal sinus and the dispersion slope ratio (DSR). Of the 128 patients included in their study, 31 were placed into and acute rejection group and the remaining 97 into a non-acute rejection group. There was no statistically significant difference in the median RS values between the two groups, whereas the median DSR values of the acute rejection group were statistically significantly different from the mean RI values, which were higher than those of the non–acute rejection group. The mean of RI values and the median of DSR values were found to be predictors of acute rejection. The results support the potential application of shear wave dispersion slope in identifying rejection reactions. Furthermore, DSR values were correlated with interstitial fibrosis and renal tubular atrophy (IFTA) grade and total inflammation grade (P<0.05). This study also identified IFTA grade as a determinant of DSR value.

Yao et al. assessed the viscoelasticity of renal parenchyma using SWE combined with dispersive imaging and found that SWV and dispersion slope values were significantly higher in the patient group than in the control group (67). Chen et al. used shear wave dispersive ultrasonography vibrometry (SDUV) (68), and—similar to a previous study that also measured shear wave propagation velocity in tissues to quantify elasticity and tissue viscosity—found that the propagation process of transverse waves is independent of the magnitude of the ultrasound intensity at the focal point and the morphology of the beam; this was despite the fact that the different properties of the various materials limit the propagation of transverse waves. In SDUV, the transverse waves generated by the ultrasound beam are relatively small in amplitude and decay rapidly after propagation through the tissue. As a result, interference from transverse wave reverberation is reduced. A single SDUV acquisition takes about 0.1 seconds, so measurements can be selectively placed in a specific time window of the cardiac cycle (because this is the point in time when the measured site has the least amount of tissue movement due to cardiac activity). Because of the shortened working time, the method also allows for the simultaneous acquisition of multiple values in different parts of other organs, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of tissue status. Research on dispersion slope assessment of renal disease is ongoing, and subjective factors such as differences in methodology, choice of reference index, and frequency of measurements may all lead to results that differ substantially from those reported in previous studies. Ghonge et al. demonstrated that mean cortical stiffness is higher in patients with IFTA grade 2 than in those with grade 1, but the number of patients included in their study was too small (n=8) for these results to be generalizable (20), and so future large-sample studies are needed validate these findings.

High-frequency shear-wave line graphs: chronic changes in the transplanted kidney

According to Grenier et al.’s findings, alterations in renal cortical stiffness are not direct evidence of specific intrarenal pathologic changes, such as fibrosis, but rather are associated with a variety of minor renal lesions, especially chronic ones. The development of hardening in the glomeruli and renal arteries is also a key part of the chronic changes. Together, these lesions contribute to increased renal cortical stiffness. Therefore, a combination of these underlying pathologic changes needs to be considered in the assessment of renal cortical stiffness (16). To assess changes in chronic renal allografts, Yang et al. (69) created a novel nomogram using multivariate analysis. They found that the SWE technique demonstrated greater diagnostic efficacy in the follow-up evaluation of allogeneic kidney transplant recipients who developed severe chronic disease. Furthermore, post-transplantation, proteinuria level, GFR, ultrasound echo values, and elasticity values were found to be independent predictors of chronic lesion development. Based on the results of these multivariate analyses, Yang et al. constructed a nomogram model that can predict the probability of chronic kidney transplant disease occurrence. This involved several innovations: first, high-frequency SWE was applied for the first time to assess chronic lesions in transplanted kidneys. Compared with low-frequency probes, high-frequency probes enable more precise SWV measurements, thus providing a finer quantitative tool for the assessment of chronic lesions in transplanted kidneys (70). Second, the findings confirm that SWE values are mainly influenced by chronic changes, whereas acute changes have less impact on SWE values. Overall, the nomogram has a wide range of applications. Xu et al. (71) used contrast-enhanced ultrasound and clinical data to create a nomogram model that demonstrated excellent predictive efficacy for determining whether the rate of chronic glomerulosclerosis in transplanted kidneys exceeds 50%. Regarding acute renal lesions, Chen et al. (72) used ultrasound super-resolution imaging to noninvasively quantify the changes in the renal vascular system associated with the occurrence of AKI to the progression of CKD in mice. The subsequent histological results confirmed a significant correlation between the vascular density values and the imaging results, suggesting that this imaging technique can accurately assess the density of the microvessels during the acute changes in the kidneys. He et al. (73) used contrast-enhanced ultrasonography to detect blood perfusion in the kidneys of brain-dead donors and found that compared with donor kidneys without AKI, donor kidneys with AKI had significantly reduced microcirculatory blood perfusion. Reduced cortical perfusion may be a characteristic manifestation of AKI. Although this review’s focus is elastography, its combination with multimodal techniques such as ultrasound super-resolution imaging, it is expected to provide more accurate diagnostic efficacy in the future. Furthermore, high echogenicity can be used as a key indicator of moderate-to-severe chronic lesions in allogeneic renal transplant organs. One study (74) demonstrated that in autologous kidneys, high echogenicity and blurring of the boundaries between the cortex and the medulla are key signs of CKD, and thus in transplanted kidneys, transplanted kidney parenchyma may exhibit increased echogenicity with the progression of chronic changes such as fibrosis. In the future, this speculation can be tested via large-sample studies.

A new outlook: assessing transplant renal insufficiency

Chronic transplanted kidney nephropathy, which commonly develops more than 1 year after the completion of kidney transplantation, is the principal cause of progressive failure of the transplanted kidney and adversely affects the prognosis of a large number of patients with transplanted kidneys. In one study (75), within 1 year after transplantation, approximately 24.7% of recipients had moderate-to-severe chronic allograft nephropathy; 10 years after surgery, the percentage sharply increased to 89.8%. Early detection of allograft renal dysfunction is therefore urgent for improving patient survival. Ghonge et al. (20) conducted a predictive study of more than 60 patients who had undergone previous kidney transplants. They divided the patients into a functionally stable transplant group, an acute dysfunction group, and a chronic dysfunction group. Stable graft function was defined (76) as follows: (I) eGFR >50 mL/min without graft dysfunction in the previous 6 months; (II) SCr (normal range 6–12 mg/L for males and 5–11 mg/L for females); and (III) urine protein-negative. Meanwhile, acute graft dysfunction was defined as a rapid decrease in graft renal function (between a few hours and 3 months) according to the following criteria: (i) eGFR <50 mL/min; (ii) absolute SCr increase ≥0.3 mg/dL (≥26.4 mmol/L); (iii) an increase in SCr by ≥50%; and (iv) proteinuria, and prior to the episode, a baseline value similar to that of the normal allograft group. Chronic graft dysfunction was defined as a duration of progressive deterioration of graft renal function >3 months without recovery (eGFR <50 mL/min, increased SCr level, and proteinuria). In this study, the mean parenchymal stiffness values in the stable group were significantly different from those of the chronic dysfunction group, the mean parenchymal stiffness values in the stable group were significantly different from those in the chronic dysfunction group, the mean parenchymal stiffness values in the acute dysfunction group were significantly different from those in the chronic dysfunction group, and the parenchymal stiffness values of allografts from patients with mild IFTA (Banff class I) were significantly different from those from patients with moderate IFTA (Banff class II). This study also found the following: (I) parenchymal stiffness was negatively correlated with eGFR, which was in line with findings reported by Arndt et al. (13). (II) Parenchymal stiffness was positively correlated with the level of RI, with a mean RI of 0.71±0.11 for normal transplanted kidneys and 0.77±0.11 for kidneys with acute rejection, which was similar to the findings of Boas et al. (77). (III) Stable allografts and dysfunctional allografts were significantly different in terms mean RI. In contrast, the difference in RI values between the acute dysfunction and chronic dysfunction groups was nonsignificant; this suggests that although RI can reflect the functional status of the transplanted kidney, the index may be affected by a number of confounding factors during the actual examination process. As for other clinical parameters, such as eGFR and urinary protein, there were also significant differences between the three subgroups mentioned above. Overall, the review of the relevant literature suggests that shear wave acoustic elastography may reflect the functional status of homozygous kidneys at different times, but this should be further substantiated in studies with large samples and the inclusion of multiple parameters, such as pathology.

Discussion

The elastic properties of biological tissues exhibit significant anisotropy, viscoelasticity, and nonlinearity. When elasticity measurements are made, the results vary due to differences in strain direction, strain amplitude, and strain rate. If assumptions independent of these conditions are used in determining the elastic modulus, the results will significantly reflect the correlation with the disease state (36). These assumptions hold in certain clinical scenarios (e.g., fibrosis), but some studies have shown them to be invalid in other clinical applications (e.g., in patients with a high body mass index) (78). For example, the internal anisotropy of tissues leads to biased estimation of SWV at structural interfaces; the mechanical properties of tissues contain an elastic component, but the effect of their viscosity also has to be taken into account; linear elastic models are no longer applicable due to the nonlinear character of the stress-strain relationship, because the volume of tissues shrinks when subjected to pressure, and tissue stiffness rises with increasing compression, which in turn induces a reduction of the strain rate and an elevated transverse wave propagation velocity (28,36,79). If tumors or organs exhibit a high degree of heterogeneity are encountered, a more complex model may need to be employed to account for tissue viscoelasticity (80). Although the proposed hypotheses thus far have contributed to the progress of elastography, there remains uncertainty regarding the above-provided description of the mechanical properties of tissues. In this context, transmission-related boundary conditions, interferograms, and transverse wave reflections influence the accuracy of the transverse wave reconstruction technique, and artifacts appear as a result, thus increasing the complexity of the interpretation of the images or metrics (81). The effective application of elastography relies on a deeper understanding of the underlying physical principles and involves the use of a variety of technical tools. Conventional ultrasound may be limited in some cases, such as when various artifacts are present, or depend on the subjectivity of the physician (39). In addition, the lack of uniformity in system design and setup across manufacturers makes the comparison of measurements of different systems challenging (36,39,42,78).

Conclusions

Clinicians typically adjust treatment plans appropriately based on the severity of the condition. For example, patients with lower grades of T-cell-mediated rejection are treated with glucocorticoids, whereas those with higher grades receive anti-thymocyte globulin (82). It has been reported that discontinuing glucocorticoids does not increase rejection reactions when severe fibrosis does not occur, which greatly facilitates avoiding damage to the patient’s bones (83). A notable example of clinical intervention involves targeting early-stage fibrosis with novel therapeutic approaches, such as anti–TGF-β agents (84,85). Therefore, elastic imaging technology can quantify the degree of fibrosis in the kidneys caused by inflammation or rejection reactions, providing clinicians with indicators that aid in clinical diagnosis and the formulation of personalized follow-up plans, thereby avoiding excessive intervention or delayed treatment and improving patient prognosis. Despite the variety of complex challenges and dilemmas in the field of elastography of transplanted kidneys, there remain considerable enthusiasm and anticipation in the academic community for advancements in this technology. In order to render quantitative elastography more reliable and useful in clinic, first, large-scale studies are needed to determine the elasticity indices of healthy and diseased transplanted renal tissues and to improve the accuracy and reproducibility of elastography; moreover, the results of measurements from different vendors should be standardized with a uniform model to ensure the consistency of results across operators and devices. Second, multicenter clinical trials should be conducted to establish hardness metrics as a function of disease state and patient demographics and to verify the applicability and reliability of elastography in different populations and disease states. Third, artificial intelligence and big data analysis should be combined to further optimize the data-processing and interpretation capabilities of elastography and enhance its value in clinical decision-making. Finally, the joint application of elastography and other imaging technologies should be examined with the aim of obtaining more comprehensive diagnostic information and providing more precise guidance for the management and treatment of patients with transplanted kidneys. Through these efforts, ultrasound elastography can expected to play a critical role in the early diagnosis and monitoring in chronic transplanted kidney injury, thereby improving the long-term prognosis of patients.
