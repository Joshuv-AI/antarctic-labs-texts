Improving thermal shock resistance of segmented thermoelectric devices via metal foam integration
Authors: Kuai Yu, Jiayue Du, Haoyang Tong, Yuxin Sun, Xiaoyue Yang, Hao Wu, Xiao Zhang, Zihang Liu, Chun Li, Fengkai Guo, Jiehe Sui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThermal shock failure restricts the long-term reliable operation of high-performance segmented thermoelectric devices in extreme environments such as space exploration. Taking the skutterudite-Bi2Te3 segmented thermoelectric leg as an example, here we show that thermal shock failure originates from thermal stress concentration caused by the thermal expansion mismatch between the solder and thermoelectric materials. To address this, we design a Ni foam composite solder layer, the network of which constrains the solder matrix, reducing its effective thermal expansion, while absorbing strain energy through plastic deformation during thermal shock. The leg with a Ni foam composite solder layer withstands seven thermal shocks with intact interfaces and exhibits a predicted service life of about 133 cold-start cycles, approximately 6 times higher than the leg with a conventional solder layer. The metal foam composite soldering strategy provides a practical route to improving the stability of thermoelectric devices under extreme thermal conditions and supports their future application-driven development.Subject terms: Thermoelectrics, Electronic devices

Introduction

As human activities expand to explore the far side of the moon, distant galaxies, and the deep sea, the development of long-lifetime, maintenance-free power sources becomes increasingly critical1–3. Radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs), composed of radioisotope heat sources, thermoelectric (TE) devices, and heat dissipation systems, are regarded as a core technology for meeting the stable power supply demands of such missions4,5. Operating in the dynamic extreme environments poses a challenge to the reliability of TE devices, the core energy conversion components3,6. For example, there can be severe temperature changes during the day-night transitions in space, and sudden temperature spikes caused by a malfunction in the RTG’s internal thermal management system, or instantaneous Joule heating caused by electrical system short circuits. These termed thermal shocks, caused by sudden environmental changes or internal device failures, can generate transient thermal stresses that are much higher than those under steady-state conditions2,3,7, severely threatening the reliability of TE devices. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the dynamic thermo-mechanical failure mechanisms of TE devices under thermal shock loads and to propose corresponding optimization strategies.

TE devices are composed of multilayer structures incorporating an insulation layer, metal electrodes, solder layers, and TE materials with diffusion barrier layers3,8,9. To achieve high efficiency, the current main technical approach is for soldering high-performance TE materials from different temperature zones into a segmented structure10–14. It has been reported that a segmented structure can increase the conversion efficiency to over 15% at a temperature difference of 449 K13. Due to significant and irreconcilable differences in thermal expansion among the various TE materials constituting the segmented device, the heterogeneous material interfaces exhibit poor tolerance to thermal stress15,16. Furthermore, the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of the solder is usually much higher than that of TE materials, which further exacerbates this problem. For example, the CTE of traditional solder PbSnAg (28 ~ 30 × 10−6 K −1) is much higher than that of common TE materials, like skutterudite (SKD) (9 ~ 12 × 10−6 K −1) and Bi2Te3 (14 ~ 18 × 10−6 K −1)12,17,18. When TE devices are applied in cases of severe thermal shock or large temperature differences, the solder layer and its surrounding areas will experience stress concentrations, which can easily cause device failure, including delamination, cracking, and electrical connection faults16,19,20. However, current research on TE devices predominantly focuses on optimizing the performance and exploring the degradation mechanisms under steady-state or long-term cyclic conditions9,12,16,21–24, whereas research on performance degradation under thermal shock loading has been less investigated.

In this work, we take the most widely studied SKD-Bi2Te3 segmented TE leg joined with PbSnAg solder in the field as a typical example to preliminarily investigate the thermal shock resistance of TE devices and carry out optimization design12,14,25. After a single thermal shock test (from 523 K to room temperature), three segmented TE legs in five sets of parallel samples exhibited interfacial delamination, and all samples failed within three thermal shocks, as shown in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig 1. Interfacial delamination occurred at the Bi2Te3/Ti barrier layer interface, which has the weakest bonding strength (Supplementary Fig. 2). The main cause of the delamination is the excessive thermal stress caused by the high CTE mismatch between the solder layer and TE materials, indicating that the simple use of commercial solder to connect segmented TE legs cannot guarantee their reliability under severe service conditions26. To alleviate thermal stress and improve the thermal shock resistance of the segmented TE legs, a Ni foam composite soldering technique was introduced in the connection. After seven thermal shocks, all of the segmented SKD-Bi2Te3 legs maintained intact interfaces and exhibited good electrical connection, and the average number of thermal shock cycles the samples could withstand exceeded 10, as shown in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig 1. Through systematic research, it was found that Ni foam effectively reduces the CTE of the solder layer, alleviating interfacial residual stress during soldering and thermal stress during thermal shock, which is fundamental to preventing early interfacial cracking. Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. 1b, when thermal stress is transferred to the solder layer, the framework structure of the Ni foam actively absorbs and dissipates part of the strain energy through plastic deformation, rather than rigidly transferring all the stress to the other interfaces, which is the key dynamic mechanism for improving thermal shock resistance. Otherwise, the segmented TE legs with Ni foam composite soldering increased the predicted service life, with an expected capacity to withstand at least 79 cold starts, meeting the practical application requirements for high reliability. This exploration of metal foam composite soldering technology will provide support for the reliable operation of high-efficiency TE devices and facilitate their practical application in extreme environments.

Results and discussion

Design of Ni foam composite solder

The solder layer and the nearby interface are regions of stress concentration, which are high-risk zones for device failure20,27. Hence, this work focuses on the solder layer and the adjacent interface of the SKD-Bi2Te3 segmented TE leg, which comprises a Bi2Te3-Ti-Ni-solder layer-SKD structure (the preparation process is described in the experimental section). The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) surface mapping images of the SKD-Bi2Te3 segmented TE leg are provided in Supplementary Fig 3.

Since there is a severe CTE mismatch between traditional PbSnAg solder and TE materials, it is necessary to reduce the solder layer's CTE to alleviate residual and thermal shock stresses. It is well known that compositing powders with a low CTE to solder is an effective way to reduce the solder’s CTE28–30. Considering that Ni has a lower CTE (13 ~ 14 × 10−6 K −1) and good solder wettability31–33, Ni powder seems to be a good choice. To alleviate thermal stress, the solder's CTE needs to be reduced to an appropriate level. Since the excessive addition of Ni particles may lead to poor solder wettability, which degrades the mechanical properties of the joint34–36, we tried to add 20 wt.% Ni powder into commercial PbSnAg solder to decrease its CTE. As shown in Fig. 1c, the CTE of the Ni powder composite solder is only slightly lower than that of the PbSnAg solder, far from reaching the expected level (about 19 × 10−6 K −1 calculated by the Kerner model37,38).

Subsequently, the causes underlying this exception were investigated. Under an ideal dispersion and good combination condition, the expansion of the PbSnAg matrix should be restricted by the Ni particles. However, as shown in Fig. 2a, a complete and continuous brittle intermetallic compound (IMC, mainly Ni-Sn compounds, such as Ni3Sn4) layer forms on the surface of the Ni particles39, which serves as the preferred path for crack initiation and propagation. Since the CTE of Ni is much lower than that of the PbSnAg matrix, great thermal stress is generated at the reaction layer after soldering, and the reaction layer obviously becomes a zone of concentrated thermal stress. Therefore, the reaction layer cracked after thermal cycling, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4b. Due to the cracking, the restraining effect of Ni on the solder was weakened. Consequently, the overall reduction in the CTE of Ni powder composite solder is limited. In addition to the variation in CTE, with the addition of high-modulus Ni (~207 GPa), the Young’s modulus of the Ni powder composite solder shows a much higher value than that of the PbSnAg solder40,41, as presented in Supplementary Fig. 5. Materials with a higher Young’s modulus are less able to compensate for expansion or contraction differences caused by CTE mismatch, which exacerbates interfacial stress and compromises structural stability17. In general, regarding the strategy of optimizing the CTE and Young’s modulus of the solder layer to alleviate residual and thermal shock stresses, the Ni powder composite is clearly not a good choice.

Fortunately, we found that Ni foam with a continuous three-dimensional (3D) network structure may be suitable as a composite, because the solder and Ni foam could create a double interpenetrating structure, which can markedly hinder and deflect crack propagation to enhance the interfacial bonding42. Moreover, Ni foam is also considered capable of creating a global constraint to effectively reduce the CTE of the solder43–45. Thus, we added Ni foam at the same Ni powder-to-PbSnAg solder mass ratio. As presented in Supplementary Fig. 4c, d, the Ni foam is tightly bonded with the PbSnAg matrix, and the reaction layer interface remains intact before and after thermal cycling for the reasons mentioned above. More importantly, as shown in Fig. 1c, the CTE of the Ni foam composite solder is significantly reduced to below 19 × 10−6 K −1. In addition, although the Young’s modulus increases, it is still significantly lower than that of Ni powder composites, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Thermal expansion and stress redistribution

The reasons for the reduction in CTE can be summarized as follows. Firstly, a good interfacial bonding lays the foundation for interaction between PbSnAg and Ni foam. Secondly, this continuous 3D network leads to a significant reduction in the overall CTE by directly confining the matrix's expansion or contraction. To verify this, the strain distribution inside the PbSnAg during the cooling process was analyzed through finite element simulation. Initially, as shown in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 6a, a composite structure composed of the PbSnAg solder restrained with a Ni cubic framework (an equivalent model reflecting Ni foam’s open-pore structure and 3D connectivity46), and a same-sized pure PbSnAg cube without a framework as a control group were constructed. For the pure PbSnAg cube control group, it can freely contract in space without any constraints, and the internal strain is equal everywhere, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 6a. For the PbSnAg composed with a Ni framework, in Fig. 2b, it can be seen that the closer the PbSnAg is to the Ni framework, the more its strain value deviates from that of free contraction. To quantitatively analyze the restraining effect of the Ni framework on PbSnAg’s strain, the strain data along the line ab and cd, as shown in Fig. 2b, were extracted and plotted in Fig. 2c. Clearly, along the line ab, within the area closer to the Ni framework in the vertical direction (0 μm < x < 100 μm and 300 μm < x < 400 μm), the deformation of PbSnAg is severely restricted, while in the central areas relatively far from the Ni framework (100 μm < x < 300 μm), although the strain increases, it is still lower than that of the PbSnAg control group. On the line cd, the strain at the center is higher than that at the two ends, which is because the ends are severely constrained by the Ni framework. From the stress distribution map in Supplementary Fig. 6b, it can also be clearly observed that the stress inside the PbSnAg near the Ni framework region is significantly higher than that inside the pure PbSnAg. The above results indicate that the 3D network structure of the Ni foam can create an encapsulating and constraining effect on the matrix, greatly limiting the free expansion and contraction of the PbSnAg matrix and effectively reducing the CTE of the composite.

As for the Young’s modulus, the Ni foam composite solder expresses a lower value compared to that of the Ni powder composite, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. The most likely cause of this situation is that the effective bulk Young’s modulus of Ni foam is lower than that of solid Ni46. In foams, when they are compressed or stretched, the composed slender metal skeleton or cell walls primarily undergo bending deformation, rather than simple axial tension or compression46,47. Bending deformation requires much less stress to produce large strains, resulting in lower stiffness. Therefore, this low stiffness brought by the special foam structure partially offsets the increase in Young’s modulus caused by the intrinsically high modulus of the Ni bulk.

To analyze the effect of changes in the CTE and Young’s modulus of the solder layer on the interfacial stress, a model of a segmented SKD-Bi2Te3 TE leg was established to obtain the stress distribution of TE legs joined with three different solders after thermal shock through finite element simulation. In this work, the NiCr layer was designed as a diffusion barrier layer for the high-temperature end. Since it is located about 8.8 mm away from the solder layer (a distance much greater than the characteristic stress decay length), its influence on the thermal stress distribution near the solder layer and the Bi2Te3/Ti interface was negligible. Therefore, it was not discussed in the finite element simulation analysis below. Based on the tested results of the CTE and Young’s modulus, a purely elastic model was used to analyze the thermal stress caused by elastic deformation and thermal expansion, as shown in Fig. 2d. For the leg with the PbSnAg solder layer, the region near the solder layer experiences concentrated thermal stress due to the significant CTE mismatch. For the leg with a Ni powder composite solder layer, there is an inadequate reduction in CTE and a significant increase in Young’s modulus, leading to the highest thermal stress and thus making it the most prone to failure under thermal shock. For the leg with the Ni foam composite solder layer, the CTE is substantially reduced, and the Young’s modulus is moderately higher than that of PbSnAg. As a result, thermal stress within the solder layer and at its adjacent interfaces is alleviated to varying degrees. Notably, the thermal stress at the Bi2Te3/Ti barrier interface was not significantly relieved, which seemed to contradict the thermal shock test results mentioned above. To determine the cause of this situation, further analysis is needed. Looking back at the above content, only an elastic model was used, and the contradiction between the experiment and the simulation reminds us that there may still be plastic deformation at the interface. Therefore, the exploration of plasticity mechanisms was further conducted to determine the deep-seated reason why the leg with the Ni foam composite solder layer exhibits excellent thermal shock resistance.

Plastic deformation under thermal shock

To qualitatively evaluate the plastic deformation behavior of the Ni foam composite solder layer in segmented TE legs during thermal shock tests, the paired t-test was used to design an experiment to monitor the deformation behavior of the solder layer in segmented TE legs. The paired t-test (Supplementary Note 1) quantified the distance changes between corresponding points under different thermal conditions, yielding absolute test statistics |t| with the main purpose of determining whether thermal shock causes irreversible displacement of the markers (related to plastic deformation). A series of markers was placed on the joined segmented TE leg, and the corresponding SEM images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7. After soldering, the segmented TE legs achieved a firm connection, which was defined as the initial state. At this moment, the distance parallel to the interface stress direction (which best represents stress-induced deformation) between the two markers is defined as dN. After the subsequent thermal shock process, the marked points undergo relative displacement, and the distance between the two markers is defined as dN’. For distance changes, the statistic was denoted as |td | , where ‘d’ represents the range variation component parallel to the interface stress direction. When |td| exceeded the critical value of the t-distribution at a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05, corresponding to a maximum 5% probability of erroneous rejection), the displacement was considered significant, thereby confirming the occurrence of plastic deformation relieving the interfacial stress. Otherwise, only elastic deformation occurred. For clearer conceptual visualization, Fig. 3a presents a schematic of the process. After undergoing three thermal shock processes, the calculated |td| values for marker point displacement changes exceeded 0.05/2,5 (where ‘2’ denotes a two-tailed test and ‘5’ the degrees of freedom), indicating significant displacement and the box plot corresponding to the data distribution is shown in Fig. 3b. This result confirms that the Ni foam composite solder layer underwent significant plastic deformation, which obviously played a role in alleviating thermal stress during the thermal shock process.

Since the solder layer consisted of Ni foam and PbSnAg, it is unclear whether the Ni foam contributes to plastic deformation. Through comparing the SEM images in Supplementary Fig. 7, it was found that some markers are located on the PbSnAg, while others are on the Ni foam. It was found that the displacement changes of the markers on the Ni foam (marked points 2, 3, 5, and 6) have an average value of 4.26 μm, as shown in Fig. 4c. Of course, the markers on PbSnAg (marked points 1 and 4) also experienced significant displacement, with an average value of 1.53 μm. Normally, due to the low stiffness caused by the special foam structure, Ni foam can undergo greater bending deformation under external loads, whereas PbSnAg obviously does not exhibit this behavior. Although the displacements differ, it is clear that both Ni foam and PbSnAg contribute to plastic deformation. Given that 60% of the leg with the PbSnAg solder layer failed after a single thermal shock test, the plastic deformation contributed by PbSnAg alone is insufficient to withstand the high-strain-rate loads induced by thermal shock. For the leg with the Ni foam composite solder layer, the plastic deformation of the Ni foam and PbSnAg both contribute to alleviating interfacial stress during thermal shock, thereby improving thermal shock resistance.

Interface stability during thermal shocks

As mentioned above, the five legs joined with PbSnAg showed interfacial delamination after 1, 1, 1, 2, and 3 thermal shocks, respectively, corresponding to Supplementary Figs. 1 and 8. The legs of the Ni foam composite soldering remained intact after seven thermal shocks, and SEM images showed no macroscopic defects, as shown in Fig. 4a. Moreover, the five legs failed after 8, 9, 10, 12, and 13 thermal shocks, respectively, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. In addition to structural stability, the electrical contact performance of the interface needs to be evaluated. Then, the interface contact resistivity of the segmented TE legs after undergoing multiple thermal shocks was characterized (the detailed data are shown in Supplementary Fig. 9.), as shown in Fig. 4b. The contact resistivity of the Bi2Te3/Ti barrier interface increases with the number of thermal shocks, reaching about 2.6 times the initial value after seven thermal shocks. Meanwhile, the contact resistivity at the solder layer/SKD interface fluctuates around 2.0 μΩ·cm2 with the increasing number of thermal shocks. It is well known that contact resistivity is highly sensitive to interfacial micro-defects. The monotonic increase at the Bi2Te3/Ti interface is attributed to its intrinsically weak bonding (Supplementary Fig. 2) and the formation of brittle TiTe2, which leads to progressive microcracking under cyclic thermal stress. In contrast, the solder layer/SKD interface benefits from robust bonding and effective stress relaxation achieved by the plastic deformation of the Ni foam composite solder, as shown in Fig. 3. Additionally, its low contact resistivity (0–2 μΩ·cm2) is close to the measurement limit, making minor changes difficult to discern. Nevertheless, we can conclude that micro-defects continue to accumulate at the interface, even though plastic deformation mitigates the thermal stress induced by multiple thermal shocks; this is also why the joint fractures after the eighth thermal shock. The location of the interfacial delamination is consistent with that of the PbSnAg joined segmented TE leg, occurring at the weakest Bi2Te3/Ti barrier interface. It is worth mentioning that the overall effective thermal conductivity and thermal resistance of a SKD-PbSnAg + Ni foam-Ni joint before thermal shock and after experiencing three thermal shocks were measured, and the results are shown in the Supplementary Fig. 10. Thermal shock does not significantly change the joint’s thermal conductivity or resistance, showing that the plastic deformation of the Ni foam and PbSnAg does not block the main heat path. Thus, its effect on interfacial thermal conductivity and device energy efficiency is negligible.

Performance of segmented thermoelectric leg

Although the segmented TE leg fractured after eight thermal shocks, this occurred under an extreme condition when cooling rapidly from 523 K to room temperature (considered as 303 K), so how would it be in a practical service environment? Firstly, the above destructive life verification experiments were analyzed using three probabilistic statistical methods, and the detailed procedures are provided in Supplementary Note 1. Overall, the control group (solder layer~PbSnAg) exhibited rapid, premature and highly dispersed failure characteristics (coefficient of variation (CV) ≈ 56%, Weibull analysis’s shape parameter (β) ≈ 1.9), with 60% of the samples breaking in the first thermal shock test, reflecting the inherently poor thermal shock resistance of the TE leg with the PbSnAg solder layer. In contrast, the experimental group (solder layer~PbSnAg + Ni foam) showed significantly delayed and more concentrated failure behavior (CV ≈ 20%, β ≈ 5.8), with all sample lifetimes exceeding seven cycles and being evenly distributed, indicating that the Ni foam composite soldering technology significantly improved the samples’ thermal shock resistance and consistency, effectively suppressing premature failures. Based on the above analysis, the characteristic life (η) from the Weibull analysis was used to estimate the expected life of the sample in actual application environments. For the control group, η ≈ 1.9, and for the experimental group, η ≈ 11.2, which means that 63.2% of the samples in each group are expected to fail after 1.9 and 11.2 thermal shock cycles, respectively. Moreover, the Coffin-Manson model, widely applied in the field of integrated circuits (ICs), is adopted here. This model incorporates the log-normal distribution and the power-law relationship between thermal strain and service life (thermal strain is usually characterized by the temperature change range ΔT), and is commonly used to deduce the expected service life of devices under long-term service conditions48. At first, the model needs to determine the Coffin-Manson exponent m, which reflects the sensitivity to accumulated damage under thermal shock cycling, based on a comprehensive analysis of material properties, interface structure, and failure mechanisms. In this work, the interface where the segmented TE leg failed was the Bi2Te3/Ti barrier interface. In our previous work17, it has been determined that there is a certain thickness of the TiTe2 layer at the Bi2Te3/Ti interface, so the possible cause of the failure is the brittle fracture of the intermetallic compound TiTe2 under cyclic thermal stress. According to the study on brittle fracture of intermetallic compounds, the Coffin-Manson exponent m is taken as 448,49. For the Bi2Te3/Ti barrier interface, in an actual service environment, it undergoes a temperature change (ΔTfield) around 120 K during the cold start, which is from a completely powered-off state (with temperature around 303 K), to the rapid reconstruction of the temperature field (for the hot end Th = 823 K and cold end Tc = 303 K, the temperature of the Bi2Te3/Ti barrier interface is around 423 K). Under cyclic thermal stress generated at actual service, the segmented TE leg will eventually fail, and the number of cycles it can endure, that is, the number of cold starts, is defined as Nfield. For a thermal shock test environment, the temperature difference (ΔTtest) experienced by the segmented TE leg is around 220 K, and the maximum number of thermal shocks it can withstand is defined as Ntest. Based on the above, the calculated acceleration factor AF ≈ 11.3 (Fig. 4c), which means the thermal test conditions are approximately 11 times more severe than the actual service conditions. The expected lifetime Nfield under actual usage conditions can be obtained by multiplying the test life by the acceleration factor, as shown in Fig. 4c. The segmented TE legs joined with the PbSnAg can withstand no more than 23 cold starts, whereas those joined with Ni foam composite solder can withstand approximately 133 cold starts.

In addition to the expected lifespan improvement, the Ni foam composite solder layer can also maintain the TE performance of the segmented legs. As shown in Figs. 4d, e, the output power and conversion efficiency before thermal shock (0 cycles) and after seven thermal shocks (the leg remained intact after seven cycles) were measured. Under a situation of 823 K at the hot end and 303 K at the cold end, the maximum output power Pmax decreased from 0.39 to 0.37 W with a drop of about ~5%, and the maximum conversion efficiency ηmax decreased from ~8.4% to ~8.2% with only about a ~ 2% decline. The slight attenuation observed should be primarily attributed to measurement uncertainties. While a marginal increase in interfacial contact resistivity cannot be completely excluded, its contribution is difficult to quantify and likely negligible, given that contact resistance constitutes only a very small fraction of the device’s total internal resistance. Regardless of the exact cause, the very modest extent of these performance changes confirms that the Ni foam composite solder layer can not only withstand repeated thermal shocks but also maintain reliable energy conversion after the predicted ~133 cold‑start cycles.

In summary, this study investigated the thermal shock failure of SKD-Bi2Te3 segmented TE legs by integrating Ni foam into the PbSnAg solder. The 3D network structure of the Ni foam plays a dual key role. First, it imposes a global constraint on the PbSnAg matrix, significantly reducing the CTE of the solder layer and alleviating thermal mismatch stress. Second, through plastic deformation coordinated with the PbSnAg under thermal shock, it actively absorbs and dissipates strain energy, preventing stress concentrations from causing interfacial fractures. Experiments confirmed that the leg with a Ni foam composite solder layer could withstand seven severe thermal shocks without macroscopic failure and exhibited good electrical contact performance. Moreover, lifetime predictions based on an accelerated model indicate that the number of cold-start cycles that the leg can withstand under actual service conditions (~133 cycles) is much higher than that for the leg with the PbSnAg solder layer (~23 cycles). This work not only provides an effective metal foam composite soldering solution to address the thermal-shock reliability of segmented TE devices but also offers important insights into the design of high-efficiency TE devices in extreme thermal environments.

Methods

Preparation of the n-type segmented TE legs: The nominal composition of the n-type skutterudite(SKD) used is Yb0.3Co4Sb12/0.05CoSi9. High-purity Co pieces (99.95%, Alfa Aesar), Sb balls (99.999%, Alfa Aesar), Yb ingots (99.9%, Alfa Aesar) and Si pieces (99.9999%, Alfa Aesar) were weighed according to the nominal composition. The raw materials were loaded into a carbon crucible, sealed in an evacuated quartz tube, slowly heated to 1423 K, held for 3 hours and then cooled to room temperature. The obtained ingot was cleaned, placed in a quartz tube with a fine nozzle, induction-melted and injected onto a copper wheel rotating at 4000 r.p.m. under a high-purity Ar atmosphere. The resulting ribbons, droplets and pieces were collected and ground into fine powders in a glove box. The powders were then densified by hot pressing at 973 K for 1 h in a graphite die with a diameter of 12.7 mm under an axial pressure of 80 MPa. A Ni45Cr55 barrier layer was introduced on the n-type SKD to suppress interdiffusion between the TE leg and the electrode9. The NiCr powders were prepared by mixing Ni powder (15 µm, Alfa Aesar) and Cr powder (1 µm, Alfa Aesar). The mixed NiCr powders were placed on one side of the dense cylindrical SKD pellet and consolidated by hot pressing at 923 K for 20 min to form the SKD leg with a NiCr barrier layer.

The n-type extruded Bi2Te3 was purchased from Zhejiang Wangu Semiconductor Co., Ltd, which is composed of the elements Bi, Te, and Se (details can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 3), and the specific elemental stoichiometry is unclear. Ti powder (45 µm, Alfa Aesar) and Ni powder (5 µm, Alfa Aesar) were used to prepare the Ti barrier layer and Ni solderable layer for the mid-temperature ends and a Ni barrier layer for the low-temperature ends17. A Ni/Bi2Te3/Ti/Ni stacked structure was sintered in a spark plasma sintering furnace at 673 K for 5 min under a uniaxial pressure of 80 MPa.

The obtained TE material with the barrier layer was cut into blocks with a cross-sectional area of 4 × 4 mm and polished with sandpaper to ensure a flat surface. The blocks were then placed in anhydrous ethanol for ultrasonic cleaning. Solder paste was spread on the surfaces of the SKD (the end without a barrier layer) and the Bi2Te3 block, and the assembly was then placed in a specially made graphite mold to prevent tipping (with Bi2Te3 at the bottom). The mold was placed on a heating platform for soldering, with the heating platform temperature set at 643 K. After 30 seconds, the mold was moved to a workbench to cool naturally, producing segmented TE legs of n-type SKD-Bi2Te3. When soldering with PbSnAg solder, commercially available Pb92.5Sn5Ag2.5 solder paste (Zhenjiang Fanyada Electronics Technology Co., Ltd.) was evenly applied on the soldering surfaces. When using the Ni foam composite soldering, the Ni foam (thickness 300 µm, pore size 50–150 µm, porosity 83%, Kunshan Xingzhenghong Electronic Materials Co., Ltd.) was first cut into 4 × 4 mm pieces, which were then immersed in PbSnAg solder paste so that the solder fills the interior of the Ni foam. Next, the Ni foam filled with solder was placed between the SKD and Bi2Te3, and then put into the mold.

For the single-leg devices used to test conversion efficiency and output power, a transient liquid-phase (TLP) diffusion bonding technology was used to connect the single leg to the electrode. During the bonding process, a 15 μm thick Sn foil was sandwiched between the electrode and the barrier layer (hot end: NiCr, cold end: Ni), and then the whole assembly was placed in a vacuum brazing furnace and held at 523 K for 2 h.

Microstructure characterization: The microstructure was investigated by an SEM (SEM, ZEISS SIGMA 300) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS).

Thermal shock test: The thermal shock test with specified transition times was designed with reference to IEC 60068-2-14:200950. Before the test, the surfaces of the segmented TE legs of the joined SKD-Bi2Te3 were cleaned with anhydrous ethanol and then placed in the laboratory environment for stabilization for half an hour. One muffle furnace was set as the high-temperature chamber at 523 K, and a box with surrounding wind barriers was set as the low-temperature chamber (room temperature ~303 K). The TE legs were placed in the high-temperature chamber for 10 min, then quickly transferred with tweezers to the low-temperature chamber, with a transfer time of about 10 s, and allowed to cool in the low-temperature chamber for 5 min. One thermal shock cycle was completed, after which the interface bonding and electrical connectivity of the TE legs were checked. If no issues were found, the above steps were repeated until electrical or mechanical connection failure occurred in the TE legs.

Contact resistivity measurement: The contact resistivity was measured using the four-point probe method. The sample was clamped between two Cu electrodes to form a closed electrical loop, and a constant current (I) of 0.1 A was applied through the two outer electrodes. The voltage was measured using two probes, with one probe fixed on one side of the joint, while the other probe was moved stepwise across the joint interface. These two probes were positioned perpendicular to the joint's side and top surfaces, respectively. During the measurement, the voltage changed when the moving probe passed through the joint interface. The voltage jumps across the interface, ΔV, were obtained from the voltage-position profile. The interfacial contact resistivity was then calculated according to ρc = AΔV/I, where A is the cross-sectional area of the joint. Contact resistivity can describe the electrical conductivity between two contacting bodies and is related to the reliability and efficiency of current transmission.

The bonding strength test: a mechanical property test platform was used to measure shear strength, with a load speed of 0.005 mm/min. The strength error bar is obtained by measuring five sample sets. The segmented TE legs of the joined SKD-Bi2Te3 were clamped with the mold, and the indenter of the electronic universal testing machine was aligned with the interface to be tested. During the test, as the indenter descended, the interface was primarily subjected to shear stress, thereby reflecting the joint's overall strength.

Young’s modulus test: Temperature-dependent transverse and longitudinal sound velocities were measured using an ultrasonic testing instrument (OmniScan SX, Olympus). The Young’s modulus (E) value is then calculated by the transverse (vt) and longitudinal (vl) sound velocities, and the density (ρ).

Coefficient of thermal expansion test: the solder composite of Ni powder (diameter 65 µm, Alfa Aesar) and Ni foam (thickness 1 mm, cut into disks with a diameter of 12.7 mm, stack the two pieces together for use) is made by mixing an equal mass of Ni powder with Ni foam and the same mass of PbSnAg solder paste. The resulting mixture was placed into a graphite mold with an inner diameter of 12.7 mm, with a layer of graphite paper on both the top and bottom. Then, the mold was placed into a hot-press sintering furnace, heated to 583 K without applying axial pressure, held for 1 min, and then cooled to obtain a composite solder with a diameter of 12.7 mm. Next, the obtained disks were cut and polished into bars measuring 12 × 2 × 2 mm. The CTE of the bars was measured using a thermomechanical analyzer (TMA 402, Netzsch).

Finite element simulation analysis: The commercial finite element analysis software COMSOL Multiphysics was used to optimize the geometry of the TE module. A finite element analysis solver (ABAQUS) was used to simulate stress and strain.

For the finite element model of the Ni cubic framework composite, the cubic framework model was equivalently simplified based on the open-cell 3D network structure of Ni foam. The unit cell type was a simple cubic structure, with the framework cross-sectional side length set to 100 μm and the overall unit cell side length set to 400 μm, corresponding to a Ni foam porosity of 50% and a Ni volume fraction of 50%. No external mechanical constraints were applied to the model during cooling, and only free deformation due to thermal strain was considered. The temperature load was decreased from 473 K (corresponding to the set temperature of the CTE measurement) to 303 K (room temperature). The entire cooling process was treated as a steady-state, thermally coupled, mechanically coupled analysis, without considering transient effects. The interface between the Ni framework and the PbSnAg was assumed to be ideally bonded.

For the finite element model of the SKD-Bi2Te3 segmented TE leg, the SKD and Bi2Te3 heights were optimized using finite element simulation to 8.8 mm and 1.2 mm, respectively, with a cross-section of 4 × 4 mm (see Supplementary Fig. 12), and the other layer heights are 300 μm. An external mechanical constraint is applied to the hot end of the TE leg (at the outer surface of the NiCr layer). The temperature load was decreased from 573 K (corresponding to the set temperature of the soldering process) to 303 K (room temperature). The entire cooling process is treated as a steady‑state coupled thermal-mechanical analysis, ignoring transient effects. All interfaces were assumed to be ideally bonded.

Thermal diffusivity test: The laser flash method (LFA457, Netzsch) was used to measure the overall effective thermal diffusivity αeff of the SKD-PbSnAg+Ni foam-Ni joint in the temperature range of 300–500 K before thermal shock and after experiencing three thermal shocks. The effective volumetric heat capacity (ρCp)eff was obtained by thickness-weighted averaging according to the respective thickness fractions, density, and specific heat capacity of the three-layer materials, and then the effective thermal conductivity κeff was derived according to the Dulong-Petit law κeff = αeff(ρCp)eff. Combined with the total thickness h and cross-sectional area A (a disc with a diameter of 12.7 mm) of the joint, the overall thermal resistance, including bulk thermal resistance and interfacial contact thermal resistance, was obtained as Rth = h/(κeffA). All parameters used for the calculations are listed in Supplementary Table S5.
