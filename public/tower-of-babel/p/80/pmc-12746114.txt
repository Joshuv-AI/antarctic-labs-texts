To the Editor:

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) associated with left heart disease (PH-LHD) represents a major health issue worldwide, accounting for nearly 75% of cases of PH. PH-LHD eventually develops in more than half of patients with heart failure and confers poor prognosis. It is recognised that targeted therapies are lacking partially because the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood [1, 2].

The bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2 (BMPR2) gene, a key receptor in the signalling pathway that regulates cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis, plays a central role in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [3]. Several molecular mechanisms of pathogenicity for BMPR2 dysfunction have been proposed, such as disrupting the balance between vascular endothelial growth factor and bone morphogenetic protein signalling, increasing the expression of activin entailing cellular proliferation or the release of inflammatory mediators [4]. Human BMPR2 produces two primary transcripts, and serine and arginine rich splicing factor 2 (SRSF2), amongst others, regulates the balance between BMPR2 isoforms [5]. The A-isoform contains all 13 exons and is the functional protein [5]. Notably, even in the absence of a heritable mutation, levels of the BMPR2 A-isoform are reduced in blood and lung tissue from patients with PAH [5]. Although a role of BMPR2 isoforms in PH-LHD has been suggested [6–8], its implication has not been definitively established in the clinical setting. This study aims to address the role of BMPR2 and SRSF2 in patients with PH associated with valvular heart disease (VHD) and to explore their potential as prognostic biomarkers.

We conducted a post hoc analysis of the Sildenafil for Improving Outcomes after Valvular Correction (SIOVAC) phase 4 clinical trial (NCT00862043) [9], which included a cohort of 200 adult patients in a stable clinical condition with persistent PH (defined as mean pulmonary artery pressure ≥ 30 mmHg) after a complete and successful valvular correction procedure. Patients were randomised to receive either sildenafil or placebo. Blood sampling was collected at the clinical trial enrolment, before treatment arm allocation. After completing the 6-month treatment with sildenafil versus placebo, patients underwent an extended follow-up [10].

We have analysed BMPR2 gene variants in 87 patients from the SIOVAC cohort: patients with PH-VHD after corrected VHD (26 isolated mitral; 24 mitral + aortic; 22 mitral + tricuspid; 15 mitral + aortic + tricuspid) with available blood samples (figure 1a). In addition, we have analysed DNA samples from 22 patients with mild or no signs of PH after corrected VHD (five isolated mitral; eight isolated tricuspid; three mitral + aortic; four mitral + tricuspid; two mitral + aortic + tricuspid) with available blood samples (figure 1a). The 13 exons of the BMPR2 gene were amplified and sequenced by Sanger from DNA samples. The analysis of BMPR2 sequence data revealed a total of 12 different genetic variants: four synonymous, six missense, one intronic and one frameshift. Of these, nine have been previously described while three were novel. A BMPR2 genetic variant was present in 29 PH-VHD patients (33%) and in seven patients with mild PH (32%). Only five patients, all of whom had PH-VHD, carried a pathogenic frameshift variant (NM_001204.7(BMPR2): c.1748dup(p.Asn583fs)), which was validated by Sanger sequencing and next-generation sequencing.

Moreover, we have conducted a gene expression analysis in those patients with available blood samples stored in Tempus Blood RNA Tubes for whole-RNA isolation. This sample comprised 49 PH-LHD patients (out of 87 patients with BMPR2 gene variant analysis) and 19 patients with mild PH (out of 22 with BMPR2 gene variant analysis). We also included a set of eight healthy subjects (figure 1a). We determined the expression of whole BMPR2 gene by RNA-Seq and specific A-isoform and all the isoforms that include exon 2 and exon 3 following the description by Cogan et al. [5] (figure 1b), and SRSF2 gene by RT-qPCR using SYBR Green and a specific primer pair (Fw-GTTCCAAGCACAGCAGCAGA and Rv-ACATTTCACAGACAGTTCATTCCT for the BMPR2 A-isoform; Fw-CTTGGGATAGGTGAGAGTAGAAT and Rv-ACAACAGCAGAAACGGTATG for all the BMPR2 isoforms; and Fw-CGACGCTGAGGACGCTATGGATGC and Rv-CGGCTGCGAGACCTGGAACGACT for SRSF2). We calculated the relative expression using the 2–ΔΔCt method and GAPDH as housekeeping gene (Fw-TTTGGTATCGTGGAAGGACTCA and Rv-TCATCATATTTGGCAGGTTTTTCT). We have used the Kaplan–Meier method to characterise the global survival time conditioned on BMPR2 A-isoform expression using a median threshold. We did not identify differences in the overall expression of the BMPR2 gene or in the combined measurement of the described isoforms; however, we found differences in the specific expression of isoform A (figure 1b).

The expression of the BMPR2 A-isoform varied significantly across the two groups of patients and healthy subjects (figure 1c). Patients with PH-VHD exhibited 1.8-fold lower expression of A-isoform levels compared to patients with mild or no PH (p=3×10−4) and 2.8-fold lower expression compared to healthy subjects (p=6×10−7). Notably, patients with PH-LHD and low BMPR2 A-isoform expression (below median value) had reduced 4-year survival compared to those with high expression (above or equal median; p=0.029; figure 1d). Additionally, patients with low BMPR2 A-isoform levels also showed lower levels of SRSF2 splicing factor (r=0.670; p=1.2×10−5; figure 1e). Baseline clinical characteristics, including age, sex, and cardiovascular risk factors showed no association with the expression of BMPR2 or SRSF2. Intriguingly, the subgroup with high expression of BMPR2 A-isoform showed a median of mean pulmonary artery pressure of 40 (34–46) mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance of 3.79 (2.68–4.79) Wood units, compared to 36 (33–42) mmHg and 3.19 (2.14–4.58) Wood units in the low-expression subgroup, respectively. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p=0.170).

Previous studies have proposed BMPR2 gene expression as a marker for PAH severity [11], moreover a reduction in BMPR2 gene expression has been demonstrated in patients with VHD and PH compared to healthy subjects [12]. However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to establish a role for BMPR2 functional isoform expression, rather than the overall gene expression, in the prognosis of PH-LHD patients. Herein, we demonstrate that the expression of the BMPR2 A-isoform is significantly reduced in patients with PH-VHD compared to patients without PH and healthy subjects, particularly among those PH-VHD patients with poorer 4-year survival outcomes. Furthermore, pathogenic gene variants in exons and canonical splice sites were excluded as the cause of this differential expression; instead, BMPR2 A-isoform expression was related to expression of its SRSF2 splicing regulator.

The presumed homology between PAH and PH-LHD is currently under debate. Clinical trials focusing on the precapillary component have yielded negative results [1], and consistent evidence shows a significant remodelling in the postcapillary bed in the latter [13, 14]. These findings challenge the classic compartmentation of the pulmonary vasculature [15], and the current haemodynamic definition seems lacking with regard to explaining the entire pathobiological process. In fact, the observed trend of lower BMPR2 A-isoform expression in patients with isolated postcapillary PH compared to those with combined forms in our cohort further highlights that the diagnosis of vascular remodelling should not rely solely on right heart catheterisation. Furthermore, we found a significantly lower expression of the BMPR2 A-isoform in patients with previous VHD and mild PH, compared to healthy subjects, which may indicate a certain degree of pulmonary remodelling, although it does not meet the current criteria for PH diagnosis. Therefore, we anticipate a paradigm shift towards a new pulmonary vascular disease taxonomy centred on key pathological drivers through the recognition of overlap syndromes, omics analysis and deep phenotyping [7]. In this context, BMPR2 seems to have a relevant role, a finding consistent with the lack of efficacy of pulmonary vasodilators in PH-LHD, as these treatments fail to restore BMPR2 expression [16]. Notably, the imbalance between BMPR2 and activin-class ligands plays a crucial role not only in PAH but may also contribute to the pathogenesis of PH-LHD and heart failure [6]. In this context, drugs aimed at restoring the balance between BMPR2 and activin (NCT04945460), could be promising [6, 7]. Further research is needed to clarify the molecular and inflammatory pathways involved in the postcapillary remodelling changes that drive PH-LHD, possibly through BMPR2 dysregulation.

Nonetheless, certain limitations must be recognised. First, the absence of a control group (namely, patients with PAH or other forms of PH) limits the pathophysiological interpretation. Second, since the cohort included only PH-VHD patients, the generalisability of these findings to other PH-LHD subtypes remains uncertain. Furthermore, the study was conducted on patients whose blood samples were collected under specific conditions required for gene expression analysis, which limited the sample size and, consequently, the statistical power. Finally, the analysis was performed on blood, which is not the target tissue; therefore, validating these results is required to definitively demonstrate the key role of BMPR2 in PH-LHD and the prognostic value of the A-isoform.
