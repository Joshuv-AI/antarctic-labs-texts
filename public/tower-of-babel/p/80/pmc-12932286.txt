Extent of resection and perioperative neurological status as prognostic markers in glioblastoma: a population-based study
Authors: Eduardo Erasmo Mendoza Mireles, Hanne Blakstad, Andres Server, Henning Leske, Erlend Skaga, Mads Aarhus, Petter Brandal, Eirik Helseth, Lakshmi Nayak, Pål Andrè Rønning, Einar O. Vik-Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMaximal safe resection is the suggested standard for surgical treatment for patients with IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. Surgical safety is prioritized over radical extent of resection (EOR) because new neurological deficits are considered detrimental for survival. The aim of this study was to explore the combined impact of extent of resection and neurological status on survival in glioblastoma patients, using standardized tools for neurological function (neurological assessment in neuro-oncology (NANO scale) and extent of resection (RANO resect classification).MethodsPopulation-based study of all adult patients who underwent resection for newly diagnosed glioblastoma from 2019 to 2022 in a geographically defined region. EOR was evaluated according to the RANO resect classification. Neurological function was assessed using the NANO scale preoperatively, immediately after surgery, and before start of radiotherapy. Kaplan Meier survival analysis, chi-square and log-rank tests were used to compare groups.ResultsThe median overall survival (mOS) for 503 patients was 12.9 months. RANO resect class 1 was associated with significantly longer mOS compared to patients with RANO classes 2, 3, and 4 (21.0 vs. 14.8, 8.3, and 4.5 months, respectively; p < 0.01). Preoperatively neurologically intact patients (NANO-score 0) had a longer mOS, than patients with neurological deficits. Neither the postoperative NANO-score nor the change in the score impacted survival.ConclusionsBeing neurologically intact before surgery and supramaximal resection were independent, strong predictors of longer survival. Mild to moderate new postoperative neurological deficits did not impact mOS, suggesting that supramaximal resection may confer a survival benefit even when such deficits are induced.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11060-026-05481-y.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (CNS WHO grade 4 astrocytoma, IDH-wildtype) is the most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults. Despite multimodal therapy, including maximal safe resection followed by chemoradiation [1, 2], the median overall survival (mOS) is only 15 months [3, 4]. Key prognostic factors for survival include the extent of tumor resection (EOR), MGMT-promoter methylation status, patient functional status and age – with EOR being the only modifiable factor [5, 6]. The understanding of a beneficial EOR has evolved over time. In 2001, Lacroix et al. identified a 98% of contrast enhancing tumor as a threshold to provide a survival benefit [7]. A decade later, Sanai et al., demonstrated that survival advantage started at 78% EOR, with incremental benefits observed up to 95–100% [8]. Several studies associate gross total resection (GTR) or complete resection of contrast enhancing tumor (CRCET) with improved survival [3, 9]. More recently, supramaximal resection, defined as resection beyond the contrast enhancing (CE) tumor, has been associated with additional survival benefit [10, 11]. The Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) resect group recently proposed a standardized classification of EOR, emphasizing the survival benefit of resecting non-contrast enhancing (NCE) tumor [12, 13].

The patients’ overall functional status is typically assessed using the Karnofsky Performance Scale (KPS) or Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status [5, 14, 15]. While these scales evaluate global functional ability, they do not specifically assess neurological status. The Neurological Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (NANO) scale was developed as an objective, clinician-reported tool to assess neurological function across nine domains, complementing the radiological RANO criteria [16]. Compared to tools designed for ischemia, inflammation or degeneration, NANO scale offers a more tailored evaluation of neurological deficits in neuro-oncological patients [16]. As the NANO scale enables standardized reporting of neurological functions, it may prove useful for prognostic evaluation in the perioperative setting, particularly in grading the impact of new neurological deficits. However, a comprehensive understanding of how the pre- and postoperative neurological status influences survival in glioblastoma patients is lacking.

Here, we used both the NANO scale and the RANO resect class to evaluate clinical outcomes in a population-based cohort of glioblastoma patients undergoing surgical resection. We assessed the prognostic value of NANO-scores preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at initiation of radiotherapy, relative to EOR, using these standardized tools in a continuously recruited geographically defined cohort with minimal loss to follow-up.

Material and methods

Study design and participants

Oslo University Hospital (OUS) prospectively registers all patients undergoing brain tumor surgery in an institutional quality-control database. We used the quality-control tumor database for this analysis and retrieved all treatment-naive adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent their first surgery for a neuropathologically verified primary glioblastoma between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2022. The electronic medical records and tumor registry were regularly updated with data from the National Death Registry, minimizing the risk of patients being lost-to-follow-up. Data collection concluded on May 31st, 2025.

All Norwegian inhabitants are registered in The National Population Register and receive a unique identification number used in all communication between public authorities and individuals. This also facilitates communication between healthcare providers and individual patients. Norway is organized into four regional health authorities, Central-, Northern-, Western-, and South-Eastern Norway Regional Health Authority, each responsible for providing healthcare services to patients residing within its defined geographical area. Patients are routinely referred to treatment within their region of residence [17]. Patients with highly malignant tumors like glioblastoma rarely seek treatment outside their region of residence. OUS serves as the only referral center for oncological neurosurgery in the geographically defined South-Eastern Norway Regional Health Authority, with a population of 3.2 million (55% of Norway’s population) [18]. Neurosurgery is provided at two locations and radiotherapy treatment at three locations, ensuring comprehensive and standardized follow-up, and minimizing the risk of patients lost to follow-up.

All patients were operated by a neuro-oncology focused team trained in the use of intraoperative fluorescence visualization with 5-aminolevuleic acid, ultrasound, neurophysiological monitoring, awake craniotomy, and intraoperative MRI, as appropriate. Throughout the period a goal of maximal safe resection was aimed for. All cases were presented at plenary meetings, and the surgical target was decided by the treating surgeon together with the patient in shared decision-making.

The Neurological Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (NANO) scale

The NANO scale consists of the following nine domains: gait, strength, ataxia, sensation, visual fields, facial strength, language, level of consciousness, and behavior [16]. Most domains are scored from 0 to 3, except ataxia, sensation, facial strength, and behavior, which are scored from 0 to 2. A higher score indicates worse neurological status, while a score of zero indicates no deficits. The maximum composite score is 23 points. If a domain cannot be assessed, it is marked with an “X”. All NANO-scores were prospectively collected at the time of admission (1–2 days prior to surgery), and on the day of discharge. Patients were generally discharged two days after surgery; however, for surgeries performed on Fridays, discharge typically occurred 3 days after surgery. Given that radiotherapy can worsen neurological deficits by increasing edema, or exacerbate fatigue, neurological status was assessed prior to the initiation of radiotherapy to minimize potential bias [19]. NANO-scores prior to radiotherapy (PreRT NANO) were collected retrospectively. PreRT NANO was assessed by the neurooncologist before radiotherapy (RT) start, typically this was done 1–7 days before RT. At our institution, approximately half of all patients started RT within 4 weeks after surgery, followed by 37% who initiated RT between 4.1 to 6 weeks, and only 13% who started RT more than 6 weeks postoperatively [20]. Patients undergoing biopsy only were included in the pre- and postoperative analysis, as they may reflect surgical complications that could influence postoperative NANO-score. However, such patients were excluded for further analysis, since any neurological change observed at follow-up would not be a direct consequence from surgery. This approach was taken to avoid introducing bias into the assessment.

Mild neurological deficits were defined as a total NANO-score of 1–2 points, moderate deficits as 3–5 points, and severe deficits as 6 or more points. Thus, the aggregated NANO-score was divided into these 4 different groups. Changes in NANO-score groups were classified as improved (transition from a higher point group to a lower point group), worsened (transition from a lower point group to a higher point group) and unchanged.

Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) resect class

All patients included in this study underwent a postoperative MRI within the first 48 hours after surgery. The postoperative MRI protocol consisted of a three-dimensional T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE), axial T2-weighted fast spin-echo, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) in the transverse plan with corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), and contrast enhanced (gadopentate dimeglumine Magnevist, Bayer Schering Pharma, Berlin, Germany) three-dimensional T1-weighted MPRAGE. Total and residual CE tumor volumes were measured on contrast-enhanced T1-sequences, and NCE tumor volumes were measured on FLAIR and T2-weighted sequences. NCE tumor was defined as signal alternation beyond enhancing excluding edema and ischemic signals, and was identified based upon disruption of the anatomical architecture and its FLAIR/T2-signal intensity compared to cerebrospinal fluid or physiological white matter [13, 21]. Extent of resection was evaluated according to the RANO resect classification; class 1 (supramaximal CE resection): complete resection of CE tumor and less than or equal to 5 cm3 of NCE tumor residual; class 2 (maximal CE resection): complete resection of CE tumor with more than 5 cm3 of NCE tumor residual, or CE tumor residual less than or equal to 1 cm3; class 3 (submaximal CE resection): CE tumor residual more than 1 cm3; and class 4 (biopsy) with no reduction of tumor volume [13]. Volumetric tumor assessment was performed by the treating surgeon and validated by either author E.E.M. Mireles, M. Aarhus, or E.O. Vik-Mo using the Brainlab Elements software (Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany). In cases of uncertainty, neuroradiologist A.Server was consulted. Eloquence was defined according to the Sawaya grading system [22].

Molecular analysis

Glioblastoma was classified according to the WHO 2021 classification of CNS Tumors. IDH status was assessed using Sanger sequencing. MGMT-promoter methylation was assessed using pyrosequencing and classified as methylated when exceeding ≥9% [23]. TERT-promoter mutations were classified as either mutated or wildtype using Sanger sequencing.

Neurooncological treatment

Standard neurooncological treatment was defined as fractionated radiotherapy with a total dose of 54–60 Gy, combined with daily temozolomide for 30 days, followed by 6 cycles of temozolomide. Any deviation from the standard treatment was categorized as other treatment. Patients who did not receive either radiotherapy or chemotherapy were classified as no treatment group.

Statistical analysis

We used Kaplan Meier curves to calculate overall survival. Chi-square and log-rank tests were used to compare groups. We used pairwise comparisons and applied the Holm-Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing. Uni- and multivariable Cox regression models were applied to generate hazard ratios for explanatory variables. Schoenfeld residuals were calculated and examined graphically to assess the proportional hazards assumption in Cox regression. Neurological treatment and RANO resect classification violated this assumption, especially around 1 year (365 days), and this was addressed by stratifying the data into early and late periods. Variance inflation factor was used to assess collinearity. To avoid collinearity, we built a regression model using preoperative- and changes in NANO-scores. We also included laterality, the presence of preoperative contrast enhanced tumor, MGMT promotor methylation status, multifocality, and deep-seated tumor location as covariates. We defined tumors as deep seated when contrast-enhancing tumor was localized in the insula, corpus callosum, brain stem, thalamus, basal ganglia, and/or in the ventricles. Neurooncological treatment and 3-weeks follow-up NANO are time dependent variables. For the Cox regression analysis, we included only patients who underwent resection and had assessed a PreRT NANO-score. In this group, only one patient died before 30 days, therefore, it was unnecessary to adjust for time in the Cox regression. We used the concordance statistic (C-statistic) to assess the discriminative performance of the Cox regression models. All analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (v 4.5.0R Core Team 2021). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Population overview

Between 2019 and 2022, 503 patients were diagnosed with neuropathologically confirmed glioblastoma at OUS. Of these, 392 patients underwent resective surgery and 111 underwent biopsy alone. The median age at diagnosis was 65 years (range: 26–87 years). Detailed patient characteristics are outlined in Table 1. The crude incidence of neuropathologically verified glioblastoma was 5.2/100,000 person-years. The 30-days mortality following resection was 1.0% (4 patients), compared with 10.8% (12 patients) following biopsy. The overall median survival (mOS) was 12.9 months (95%CI: 11.4-14.1). The median follow-up time was 50.5 months, 45 (9%) patients were alive at analysis time, and only one patient (0.2%) lost to follow-up. The median time to discharge was 2 days for the entire population and remained consistent across all postoperative NANO-score groups. Patients who underwent surgical resection had a mOS of 14.9 months (95%CI: 13.7-15.8), compared to 4.5 months (95%CI: 3.7-5.8; p<0.0001) for biopsy-only patients. Survival correlates with preoperative, postoperative, and PreRT NANO-scores

We next analyzed the association between preoperative and postoperative NANO-scores in patients undergoing resection, the changes between them, and survival. Preoperatively, 153 patients (31%) had a NANO-score of 0 points, 163 patients (32%) had a score of 1–2 points, 141 patients (28%) had a score of 3–5, and 46 patients (9%) had 6 or more points. Resection was performed in 26 (57%) of the 46 patients with a preoperative NANO-score of 6 point or more. In this subgroup, 11 patients experienced neurological improvement with a NANO score of 5 or less after surgery. Postoperatively, 131 patients (26%) had a NANO-score of 0 points, 153 patients (30%) had 1–2 points, 151 patients (30%) had 3–5 points, and 67 patients (13%) had 6 or more points.

PreRT NANO-scores were assessable in 337 patients (67%). Of these, 128 patients were neurologically intact (NANO-score of 0 points), 109 had a score between 1 and 2, 76 had a NANO-score between 3 and 5, and only 24 patients had a score of 6 or more points. Although a specific NANO-score classification was not possible for 62 patients, we were able to determine ECOG performance status for 57 patients. Among these, 46 patients had an ECOG performance status between 0 and 2, and all except 2 received neurooncological treatment. In contrast, 11 patients had an ECOG performance status of 3 and 4, and only 3 of them received neurooncological treatment.

The mOS for patients with a preoperative NANO-score of 0, 1–2, 3–5, and 6 or more points was 17.9 months (95%CI: 14.9–21.0), 13.1 months (95%CI: 10.5–15.0; p< 0.001), 10.2 months (95%CI: 8.6–12.5; p<0.001), and 7.4 months (95%CI: 4.1–12.0; p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 1A). The association of supramaximal resection and neurological status on patient survival

Next, we analyzed the association of supramaximal resection (RANO resect class 1) and NANO-score groups with survival. We first evaluated the immediate postoperative NANO-score, as this timepoint is most likely to reflect neurological impact of EOR. We then assessed the overall NANO-score as this was less likely to encounter collinearity in a regression model.

Patients in RANO resect class 1 had a median overall survival of 23.6 months (95%CI: 18.1–31.7) for NANO-score 0 immediately postoperative, 21.1 months (95%CI: 14.6–26.8; p>0.99) for NANO 1–2, 21.0 months (95%CI: 14.2–29.9; p>0.99) for NANO 3–5, and 16.6 months (95% CI: 10.4-NA; p>0.99) for NANO-score 6 points or more (Fig. 3B).

When stratified by the overall NANO-score within RANO resect class 1, the mOS was 29.0 months (95%CI: 16.2–37.3) among patients who improved at initiation of radiotherapy compared to preoperative status, 22.2 months (95%CI: 20.3–28.6; p=0.72) for unchanged, and 14.1 (95%CI 12.0–26.3; p=0.53) for worsened (Fig. 3C). Finally, we performed a multivariable analysis adjusting for all RANO resection classes, immediate postoperative NANO-score and the overall NANO-score. This analysis suggests that the survival benefit associated with supramaximal resection may outweigh the impact of postoperative neurological status – provided those deficits are not severe (Fig. 3D).

Cox univariate and multivariable regression analysis

We conducted univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses to assess the association of patient-specific, disease-related and treatment-related variables with survival (Fig. 4). ECOG performance status was excluded from the main analysis due to potential interaction with the preoperative NANO-score. RANO resect class, MGMT promoter methylation status, preoperative NANO-score, and receipt of chemoradiation consistently demonstrated significant associations with survival across both univariate and multivariable analyses. In the multivariable model, maximal CE resection was associated with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.91 (95%CI 1.51–2.41; p<0.001) and submaximal CE resection with an HR of 4.20 (95%CI 3.11 to 5.67; p<0.001) compared with supramaximal resection.

Age was significantly associated with an increased hazard of death, corresponding to a 2% increase in hazard per year in the univariate analysis and 1% increase per year in the multivariable analysis. Sex, tumor laterality, multifocality, and absence of preoperative contrast-enhancing tumor were not significantly associated with hazard in either model.

To minimize collinearity among the different NANO-scores, we analyzed changes in NANO-score groups rather than scores at individual time points. A change in NANO-score groups between the preoperative and the immediate postoperative period was significantly associated with an increase of HR for patients who improved compared with those who worsened in the univariate analysis. However, this association was not significant in the multivariable analysis. In contrast, changes in NANO-score groups between the immediate postoperative period and prior to RT were associated with similar hazards in the univariate analysis (HR 1.34, 95%CI: 0.92–1.96; p=0.1); however, they were associated with an increased hazard in the multivariable analysis (HR 1.86, 95%CI: 1.31–2.65; p< 0.001). The Cox regression model demonstrated strong discriminative ability, with a concordance statistic (C-statistic) of 0.83 (standard error 0.01).

Discussion

In this prospectively collected cohort of glioblastoma patients from a geographically defined population, we found that lower RANO resection classification (i.e., more extensive resections) and lower preoperative NANO-scores (i.e., fewer neurological deficits) were independently associated with longer overall survival. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate both the extent of resection and neurological function using the standardized RANO resect classification and the NANO scale in combination. Of importance is that the suggested RANO resect classification is also able to predict survival in an unselected geographically defined population.

A key finding of this study is the strong association between lower RANO resect class and improved survival, highlighting the importance of minimizing both contrast-enhancing and non-contrast-enhancing tumor residuals, a conclusion supported by several recent studies [10, 24–26]. The RANO resect classification provides a structured framework to compare extent of resection. We present, to our knowledge, the first populationbased assessment that includes supramaximal resection [27, 28]. Our data support the clinical utility of this classification and reinforce the prognostic value of EOR on overall survival of glioblastoma patients.

Current guidelines for glioblastoma surgery emphasize maximal safe resection, as new neurological deficits traditionally have been considered negative prognostic factors for survival [11, 29, 30]. However, with advances in surgical techniques and intraoperative monitoring, major postoperative neurological deficits have become increasingly rare, as also observed in this study.

Most previous studies have assessed neurological outcomes by dichotomizing patients into those with or without new deficits or by using global functional scales (e.g., KPS). These approaches often focus solely on major deficits and may fail to capture more subtle but clinically relevant changes. There is not a standardized method for evaluating neurological status in glioblastoma studies. The national institute of health stroke scale (NIHSS) has been used, with the disadvantage that designed specific for stroke patients. The NANO scale is a neuro-oncological oriented grading system, which is easy for clinicians, both surgeons and oncologists, to use and can be assessed relatively quickly. Few studies have examined survival using the NANO scale in glioblastoma patients.

Lee et al. demonstrated that the NANO scale provides a more detailed and objective assessment of neurological function compared to ECOG and KPS [15]. However, their study used a relatively high cut-off (≥7 points), and although EOR was considered, neither supramaximal nor FLAIR-based resections were evaluated. Kasper et al. reported that both EOR and postoperative NANO-score were strong predictors of survival, particularly when assessed three months after surgery [31]. Nevertheless, their analysis did not stratify for specific EOR categories, and NANO-score was dichotomized at a threshold of 3.5 points. Ung et al. included both IDH-mutant astrocytomas (CNS WHO grade 4) and IDH-wildtype glioblastomas and found that lower preoperative NANO-scores (0–3 points) were associated with improved survival compared with scores ≥4 points [32].

We stratified patients into four groups based on neurological status: neurologically intact, and those with mild, moderate, or severe deficits. All groups showed differences in survival when compared to neurologically intact patients; however, patients with mild and moderate deficits had comparable survival. Notably, our cohort included few patients with severe neurological deficits, likely underestimating the impact of major surgical complications, including vascular injury.

With respect to specific neurological domains, McGirt et al. reported that motor or language deficits were independently associated with shorter survival, even after controlling for EOR and adjuvant therapy [33]. In our study, however, new postoperative deficits – regardless of domain – did not significantly affect survival. Overall, our findings suggest that EOR is a stronger predictor for survival than the presence of mild or moderate neurological deficits in the immediate postoperative period. However, patients who developed severe neurological deficits, had markedly poorer outcomes.

New or worsened neurological deficits in the immediate postoperative period were unexpectedly associated with improved median overall survival and a decreased hazard ratio in univariate analysis, compared with neurological improvement. However, this association was no longer significant following multivariable adjustment. While these findings are potentially interesting, the results must be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size in the worsened and improved subgroups. There is also an inherent dependency between preoperative NANO and its change after operation. Further analyses in larger cohorts, using Cox regression models that individually adjust for each change in NANO would be possible in a larger cohort. In contrast, neurological changes between the immediate postoperative period and prior to RT were not significantly associated with outcome in univariate analysis; however, patients with improved neurological function prior to RT showed a significantly decreased hazard ratio of death in the adjusted model. Importantly, supramaximal resection was consistently associated with favorable survival outcomes, regardless of postoperative or PreRT neurological status. These findings suggest that immediate postoperative neurological deficits may carry limited prognostic value when supramaximal resection is achieved, provided recovery occurs prior to initiation of adjuvant therapy. Changes in NANO-scores reflect combined effects across multiple neurological domains, making it difficult to detect consistent associations when analyzing each domain separately, and in subgrouping analysis as well. Moreover, transient factors such as edema, surgical manipulation, supplementary motor area syndrome, or hospital environment may influence neurological status in the early postoperative period [34, 35]. It is important to note that patients diagnosed with glioblastoma are not usually prioritized for specialized rehabilitation treatment after surgery, regardless of postoperative neurological status. This is mainly due to their relatively poor prognosis compared with other neurosurgical conditions, and because rehabilitation is typically interrupted by initiation of radiotherapy. However, patients admitted to neurosurgical or oncology wards were provided with physiotherapist and/or speech therapist regularly when clinically indicated. We would like to highlight the recovery potential observed in this study, as patients experiencing neurological improvement in the following weeks after surgery, were without intensive rehabilitation therapy. For younger patients, with MGMT-promotor unmethylated tumors, and with a supramaximal resection median survival is relatively long (mOS 50 months) – and the importance of a more comprehensive rehabilitation should be revisited [36]. The interaction between supramaximal resection and neurological deficits has not been explored before, especially with standardized tools as the NANO scale and the RANO resect classification. We found that supramaximal resection was independent from neurological status, regardless of severity and changes over time. However, measures should be taken to minimize risks of permanent deficits. Further studies in larger cohorts are needed to clarify the prognostic significance of severe neurological deficits and to explore domain -specific associations with survival. Since lost to follow-up is negligible in this population, nearly all censored patients are alive at the time of analysis. In addition, patients belonging to favorable subgroups can have a median survival exceeding 24 months [36]. To minimize right censoring as much as possible, we ensured a minimum interval of 30 months between last surgical procedure and survival analysis.

Limitations

First, this study was conducted at a single institution that provides neurosurgical care for the entire South-Eastern Norway Regional Health Authority. While this centralized structure reduces the risk of selection bias and ensures population-based recruitment, local clinical practices may influence data interpretation and limit external generalizability. Second, NANO-scores before RT were unavailable in 62 patients, potentially introducing bias in analyses at this timepoint. Additionally, more than 50% of patients had unchanged NANO-score after surgery, likely reflecting routine use of advanced surgical techniques such as neurophysiological monitoring, and awake craniotomy, in addition to maximal safe resection paradigm, reducing the risk of neurological deficits. Third, although NANO scoring has demonstrated high inter-observer reliability [16], the possibility of observer bias cannot be fully excluded as the NANO-scores were assigned by the treating neurosurgeon, introducing a risk of observer bias, particularly in the postoperative assessments.

Conclusion

For surgically treated glioblastoma patients, both intact neurologically preoperative function according to the NANO scale and supramaximal resection (RANO resect class 1) were strong predictors of favorable median overall survival. Our findings suggest that extent of resection is a stronger predictor of survival than immediate postoperative neurological deficits. These results support a strategy aimed at maximal tumor removal, even when associated with a risk of mild to moderate transient deficits.
