Health literacy of pregnant women and its association with methyl-group donor intake and folic acid supplementation: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ildikó Csölle, Attila Kovács, Szilvia Bokor, Simone Funke, Réka A Vass, Tibor Ertl, Dénes Molnár
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdequate health literacy (HL) can be particularly important among pregnant women, as it can have an impact on maternal and fetal health. Deliberate intake of folate through food and folic acid supplementation (FAs) is essential for women in reproductive age. Dietary intake of methyl-group donors (MGDs) via maternal nutrition may influence the infant’s health and may affect reproductive outcomes through epigenetic mechanisms.ObjectiveThe aim of our study was to assess HL in relation to (MGD) intake among pregnant women, and to assess health knowledge as part of health literacy.Methods124 pregnant women were involved in this cross-sectional study. Data were collected using two validated HL questionnaires, and a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) to estimate MGD intake. Statistical analyses included the calculation of means, correlation coefficients, and multiple linear regression analysis.ResultsThe HL of pregnant women was found problematic (50.8% Newest Vital Sign Test, NVS; 46.7% Brief Health Literacy Screening Tool, BRIEF). The estimated intake of dietary folate, methionine, choline and betaine were 264.7 ± 134.6 µg/d; 2303 ± 1209 mg/d; 248.1 ± 110.8 mg/d, and 127.1 ± 65.8 mg/d (mean ± standard deviation, SD), respectively. Before pregnancy 58.87% of respondents did not take FAs; during pregnancy, this decreased to 31.45%.ConclusionPregnant women did not reach adequate HL. Further evaluation of health literacy, MGD intake, is required to formulate suitable nutritional intake for pregnant women in Hungary. It is suggested that providing better information to pregnant women could improve their adherence to folic acid supplementation recommendations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25239-x.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines health literacy (HL) as “the cognitive and social skills that determine our motivation and ability to access, understand and use information to promote and maintain good health” [1, 2]. In the health care sector, in everyday practice, this may mean reading and understanding the patient’s informed consent form, the medication recommendation in the patient information leaflet, the ability to calculate the dose of medication, following recommendations and contraindications, numeracy in the context of nutrition (e.g. food portion, calories, carbohydrate and vitamin content) [2, 3]. However, health literacy requires the ability to navigate and be informed about the healthcare system [2, 3]. “Health literacy is a more important predictor of changes in an individual’s health and health status than age, income, employment status, education, and ethnicity” [3, 4]. Developing health literacy is a long-term program that can positively influence all aspects of life [1, 5]. A systematic review published in 2021 found a link between health literacy and health behaviour including lifestyle, knowledge, and how HL influences beliefs and attitudes, during pregnancy [6]. Increased folic acid intake during pregnancy is necessary for fetal growth and expansion of maternal reproductive organ blood volume [7, 8] for safe pregnancy outcomes, preventing the development of fetal neural tube defects (NTDs) [9–13]. Due to increased requirements, pregnant women may be at risk of folic acid deficiency, and the association between inadequate maternal folic acid status and the development of fetal NTDs is well established [9, 14–16]. Several suggestions for folic acid supplementation have been made in the past, including Czeizel’s (2011): 0.8 mg/day for folic acid supplementation [17]. The WHO recommends that women should take folic acid supplementation if they are planning a pregnancy (0.4 mg/day) and folic acid supplementation (0.4 mg/day) is also recommended during the first trimester (12 weeks) of pregnancy [10, 18]. Folic acid levels are influenced by a number of factors, such as diet, age, socioeconomic status, pregnancy as a natural state of increased folate requirement, and medical conditions associated with reduced folate absorption [8, 9, 19]. Optimal bioavailability of folic acid is important, thus the form used and the dose are also important factors. The aim of a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2012 was to summarise the dose-response relationship between folic acid intake and birth weight, placental weight and length of pregnancy. There was a significant association between the amount of folic acid intake and birth weight, but it was not considered conclusive evidence due to the small number of data from studies included in the analysis [14]. Folic acid plays a crucial role in the one-carbon metabolism along with other components such as methionine, betaine, and choline [7, 20]. Folate, methionine, and choline are essential nutrients and can be obtained through diet, whereas betaine is a dietary, non-essential nutrient that can be derived from choline, and the pathway of this conversion is irreversible [21]. There are animal studies investigating the epigenetic role of methyl-group donors (folate, methionine, choline, betaine) and the underlying mechanism of the one-carbon cycle [7, 22]. Methyl-group donors are thought to influence infant health and the reproductive outcomes through epigenetic mechanisms such as deoxyribonucleic acid methylation [7, 8, 22, 23]. This epigenetic change may influence the predisposition to develop several diseases by the offspring, including obesity and other noncommunicable diseases in later life [7, 8]. There are very few studies in Europe and Hungary that focus on the health literacy of pregnant women and its role in methyl-group donor intake [4].

The objectives of this cross-sectional study were to explore the health literacy levels of pregnant women, to provide data on methyl-group donor intake (including dietary folate, methionine, choline, betaine and folic acid supplementation), to map the source of health information, and to assess health knowledge as part of health literacy.

Methods

Study design

This is a descriptive, cross-sectional single-center study, conducted between April and October in 2023 as part of “ASTRAGEN – Transgenerational effects of assisted reproduction” ongoing study.

Setting

The survey took place at the ward of Prenatal Pathology, at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology of the University of Pécs, in Pécs, Hungary.

Study population

The study included pregnant women between the ages of 20 and 45 years, admitted to the hospital because of various reasons (bleeding, threatening miscarriage or other prenatal disorders). All the women conceived naturally. There was no selection for gestational age, or previous health status. Some of the pregnant women had no medical history, others were admitted with prenatal complaints, and the remaining had a history of certain diseases such as polycystic ovary syndrome, gestational diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disorders, obesity, etc. The exclusion criteria were the following: women who had been artificially inseminated, did not sign a written informed consent form, did not complete the questionnaire completely.

Data collection

The data was collected on randomly selected days after checking who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate in the survey. All eligible pregnant women had to complete four questionnaires, two validated questionnaires, a FFQ and one self-administered questionnaire: (1) a general questionnaire to collect information on lifestyle (except for medical history, and physical activity), health knowledge and health information seeking habits, folic acid taking habits etc. (2) a health literacy test (Newest Vital Sign, NVS) [24, 25] to assess functional health literacy (3) A brief health literacy questionnaire (BRIEF) [26–28], which is used to assess subjective health literacy (4) A semi-quantitative FFQ can also be used to estimate folate, choline, betaine and methionine intake [7, 29]. All data were collected through a personal interview conducted by a health professional. Our main outcomes were: (1) measuring HL among pregnant women, (2) to obtain the methyl-group donor intake (folate, methionine, betaine, choline) (3) investigation of the association between HL and MGD intake. Secondary outcomes were: (4) seeking a link between HL and taking folic acid supplement (5) to assess the source of health knowledge about dietary folate intake and FAs and its relationship with HL.

Description of the questionnaires

(1) The self-administered questionnaire designed by the authors. It was structured around four main groups of questions: general (e.g. age, anthropometric data, socioeconomic status, place of residence, education, number of previous children,) [15 questions]; health behaviour (e.g. smoking habits, alcohol consumption habits etc.) [4 questions] and, lifestyle-related information-seeking habits related to pregnancy [7 questions]; the health knowledge about dietary folate intake and FAs [11 questions]. (2) The Newest Vital Sign (NVS) [24, 25] is a validated measure of the risk of low health literacy. The patient sees a food label of a product and answers 6 questions. The examiner verbally asks the questions and scores the answers to assess reading and numeracy skills, as well as problem-solving skills used to determine an individual’s functional health literacy level, grouped into three scoring categories/0–1: high likelihood of limited literacy, 2–3: possibility of limited literacy, 4–6: adequate literacy/. A validated version of the NVS for the Hungarian adult population is available since 2021 [25, 28, 30]. (3) The Brief Health Literacy Screening Tool (BRIEF) can identify individuals with a marginal level of HL [26, 27, 31]. The BRIEF is a validated questionnaire for the Hungarian adult population is available since 2021 [28]. The BRIEF score is determined by the sum of points given for the answers {scoring categories: 4–12: inadequate literacy, 13–16: marginal literacy 17–20 = adequate literacy} [28, 31]. (4) Using an adapted version of the semi-quantitative FFQ (51 items), we assessed the estimated consumption of MGDs for the previous three months [29, 32].

Ethics

All volunteers received detailed information about the survey and could ask questions about the survey, which were answered by a health professional. After being clearly informed, and if the subjects agreed, they signed the consent form. The survey conformed to the national principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All coded data were handled and stored anonymously. All participants signed a written informed consent form.

Data analysis

Our statistical analysis included the assessment of the descriptive statistics (minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, median), correlation and regression analyses. We calculated the correlation coefficients to measure the relationship between the BRIEF and NVS scores and also these scores and the age of the pregnant women, their education, and the number of their children, and between the pregnant women’s education and the number of their children. Linear regression was used to determine the relationship between the education and NVS score of pregnant women. We used the Mann-Whitney U test to compare the BRIEF and NVS scores between the two groups of pregnant women without a child and with at least one child. The association between the pregnant women’s NVS scores and their health knowledge was assessed with the Chi-squared test. The association between NVS scores and methyl-group donor intake was assessed with T-test. For the seek of readability we named “high likelihood (50% or more) of limited literacy” as low, “possibly of limited literacy” as medium, and “adequate literacy” as high NVS category. Since the number of pregnant women belonging to the medium NVS category was very low (n = 5) therefore it was merged with the low category NVS group. The merged group was called “problematic” group. The significance level was set at 0.05 (5%). Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS statistical software (v28 × 64). Figures were generated using the R software environment (version 4.1.1).”

Results

In this survey, 148 pregnant women were interviewed [dropouts: participating in vitro fertilization (18), did not complete the survey (3), missing data in questionnaires (2), woman did not share personal data (1)]. A total of 124 women (week 28 to 40) who conceived naturally were included. The mean gestational age was 33.15 ± 6.65 (mean ± SD). The pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated from the height and weight reported by pregnant women. The average BMI was 26.12 ± 5.94 [kg/m2]. 41.93% of the respondents lived in rural areas, all participants were Caucasian, but we could not stratify the population by ethnicity. The most frequent educational level was graduation (Fig. 1). 35% of pregnant women had completed their education before the age of 18. Characteristics according to the level of HL are shown in Tables 1 and 2. The frequency of harmful habits was as follows: 31.5% of pregnant women smoked (between 1 and 10 cigarettes a day), 29.0% consumed coffee daily. Health literacy

Overall, health literacy scores measured by the NVS were nearly equally distributed between problematic and adequate HL levels. The mean (± SD) values of the NVS and BRIEF questionnaires were 2.51 (± 2.44), 14.87 (± 2.95), respectively. See more detailed information in Supplementary Tables 1–6, in Additional file 1.

Comparing results of NVS, BRIEF and certain main characteristics

The BRIEF and NVS scores were not different between pregnant women with no child or with at least one child {Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.14, p = 0.06 respectively}. See detailed information in Supplementary Table 6, in Additional file 1.

Methyl-group donor estimated intake based on FFQ

No significant association was found between HL levels (measured by NVS) and the estimated amount of investigated MGD intake (t-test critical value = 0.44; p-value = 0.66). Folic acid supplementation

Among pregnant women, the more than half did not take FAs before pregnancy, which decreased to one third during pregnancy (Fig. 2). 59.42% of women who consumed less than 250 micrograms of dietary folate per day through their diet (estimated by FFQ) did not take a folic acid supplement before pregnancy, while during pregnancy this number decreased to 31.88%.

Folic acid intake from supplementation among pregnant women (n = 124). Magenta colour: folic acid intake; grey colour: no folic acid intake

There was no statistically significant association between health literacy levels (measured by NVS) and folic acid supplementation before (Chi2 statistic, 0.12, p-value 0.72) and during pregnancy (Chi2 statistic, 2.46, p-value 0.11).

Source of health knowledge

We wanted to assess where pregnant women obtained their knowledge about healthy eating and intake of folic acid supplementation. Medical doctors (28.22%), the internet (social media, etc.) (26.61%), and family and friends (20.96%) were the most frequent sources of information on healthy nutrition for pregnant women. Dietitians, whose profession includes helping pregnant women with nutrition-related issues, were ranked only fourth in this assessment (9.6%). The mean value of the health knowledge assessment test was 6.12 ± 1.85 (mean ± SD). For additional data see the Supplementary Table 8, in Additional file 1.

Significant association was found between health literacy levels (measured by NVS) and results of health knowledge assessment test (Chi2 statistic = 17.569; critical value = 14.067; p-value = 0.0141).

Discussion

In the present cross-sectional study, we focused on health literacy of pregnant women, complemented by questions on methyl-group donor intake and folic acid supplementation. The main finding of the present study is that many of the pregnant women did not have adequate HL. We estimated the MGD intake, but found no association between HL and MGD intake, and between HL and FAs. Doctors and the internet were the main sources of health information. Health knowledge about folate intake and FAs was found to be lacking. Health literacy was measured in a general population in eight countries, including Hungary in an international comparative study planned by the European Consortium (European Health Literacy Survey, HLS-EU) [33]. The difference between the two NVS’s results was considerable (mean ± SD 4.2 ± 1.9 vs. 2.51 ± 2.4). Our data are closest to those of Spain (mean ± SD 2.6 ± 2.2) and Poland (2.9 ± 2.1) [33–35]. Although both surveys used the NVS, the former used a version that has not yet been validated in Hungary [28]. A systematic review published in 2021 found that the level of HL among pregnant women varies, our results may belong to the lower value group [6]. It should also be noted that the studies differed in terms of population (general population vs. pregnant women), age groups, educational level, and socioeconomic status. The BRIEF subjectively measures HL, while NVS is an objective method, therefore the simultaneous use of multiple tools is suggested to determine the level of HL, however the diversity of the used tools makes the comparison difficult [28, 36]. Subjective and objective HL tools measure different dimensions of HL, the method and variables are different, and thus the results can be inconsistent [28, 36]. A systematic review (2011) conducted whether low HL is associated with different health outcomes in any age group, no studies investigated pregnancy [5]. Concerning other outcomes, the results showed that low HL was associated with hospitalisation, inadequate medication intake, poorer ability to interpret food labels and poorer ability to interpret health messages [5]. Another study assessed the effectiveness of a communication poster campaign on folic acid intake and its consequences among women report as suggestions the need for a complex and more thoughtful focus on intervention to impact HL on reproductive women’s health knowledge and health behaviour [20]. According to the results of the Hungarian Nutrition and Dietary Status Survey (2014) the dietary folate intake of women (general population) was 133.1–148.7 µg/day (18–65 years) (estimated on the basis of a 3-day food diary) [37], while dietary folate intakes were around 160–200 µg/day in Hungary, in 2011 [17]. Current results confirm the data reported by Pauwels (mean ± SD 279.8 ± 95.66 µg/d [7] and are within the range reported by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) (196–271 µg/d) [38]. The study population and the methodology of dietary folate intake assessment was different in these studies. Estimated methionine intake in the present investigation was moderately higher than in the study by Pauwels (mean ± SD 1659.3 ± 443.6 mg/d) [7]. Data on dietary choline intake were very close to those reported in a German study (median choline intake was 263.5 ± 147.8 mg/day [39] and in the study by Pauwels (mean ± SD 280.3 ± 77.88 mg/d) [7], while other studies results showed higher values (mean 353 mg/d; 336 mg/d) [40, 41]. During pregnancy, the dietary choline consumption requirement is significantly higher, than in non-pregnant women. The adequate intake for choline in pregnant women is 480 mg/day according to the EFSA [42], which is not achieved by Hungarian pregnant women in this survey. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that adequate maternal choline intake has a beneficial effect on fetal brain development and child neurocognition [43]. Our result for betaine was slightly lower than in the study by Pauwels (173.2 ± 60,67 mg/d) [7] and considerably lower than in a Canadian cohort study (mean ± SD 215 ± 141 mg/d) [40]. Overall, there were only minor differences in the intakes of MGDs, which may be explained by the different populations, age, dietary habits. In the current study, we found no association between HL levels and the use of FAs. However Jin et al. found the nutritional knowledge of folic acid positively correlated with more effective FAs [44]. It is an important observation that 33.1% of pregnant women did not take folic acid, as confirmed by the results of other studies on folic acid intake [15, 45].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, that the Hungarian patients mainly came from one region, Baranya County, Pécs, and therefore the data are not nationally representative, and not generalizable. Secondly, the cross-sectional design limits the possibility of establishing causal relationships. Additionally, the included pregnant women represented a heterogeneous group from the point of view of underlying pathology (diabetes, hypothyroidism, hypertension, etc.), and duration of pregnancy. Further limitation is, that we did not collect data concerning medical history and physical activity of the pregnant women. Furthermore, the original FFQ was validated in reproductive age Flemish women, but the Hungarian version is not yet validated. When considering the generalizability of the results, the locality and clinical heterogeneity of our sample must be taken into account. The strength of the present study is that it is the first study in Hungary which analyses HL among pregnant women in relation to the intake of MGDs, including dietary folate, choline, methionine, betaine, and to search for a relationship between FAs and HL. Despite the mentioned limitations the present study may provide important information for health professionals on pregnant women’s HL and MGDs intake, and willingness to supplement folic acid.

Conclusion

In this study, health literacy was measured in pregnant women using two validated HL questionnaires. In the majority of pregnant women HL level was problematic. The main problem revealed by the present study is that a majority of women did not take folic acid supplements before, and 33.1% did not take it during the pregnancy, either. This underlines the importance of more effective counselling in the healthcare system. We found low choline dietary intake in pregnant women, however, choline has an important role in fetal brain development. Some new pregnancy vitamins already contain choline. This alerts general practice that choline intake should also be facilitated during pregnancy. Health literacy is an important factor in prenatal and postnatal periods, but further research is needed to determine which dimensions of HL influence health behaviour and changes in health status. We also suggest it is important to involve women undergoing artificial technology treatment in studies, too. We emphasise that research including lifestyle parameters is required on large number of populations.
