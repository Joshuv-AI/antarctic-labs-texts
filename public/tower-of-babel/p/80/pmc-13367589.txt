Prevalence and risk factors of cognitive impairment among geriatric patients at Prince Sultan Military Hospital, Taif, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Salwa Khalid Abu Asyah, Elham Saad Algethami, Bassam Abdulrahman Alzaidi, Rawan Naif Althomali, Nouf Jafar Alzahrani, Saud K. Alhothaly, Waleed Talal Alsulaimani, Abdullah Ahmed Almalki, Ramy Mohamed Ghazy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Cognitive impairment and dementia represent a growing public health challenge among the aging population in Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of cognitive impairment and identify associated risk factors.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted at the Prince Sultan Military Hospital Geriatric Clinic in Taif from November 2024 to May 2025. A sample of patients aged ≥65 years were recruited via simple random sampling. Data on sociodemographics, clinical history, and lifestyle were collected. Cognitive function was screened using the Arabic version of the Eight-item Alzheimer’s Dementia (AD8) questionnaire, administered to knowledgeable informants who had known the participant for at least 5 years with a score ≥ 2 indicating likely impairment.Results:The study sample consisted of 182 geriatric patients. Bivariate analysis revealed that cognitive impairment was significantly associated with below-average income (P = 0.01), requiring daily assistance (P = 0.001), depression (P = 0.034), hypothyroidism (P = 0.026), lack of regular exercise (P = 0.01), and subjective cognitive complaints (P < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment using stepwise logistic regression, independent predictors of cognitive impairment were: any cognitive complaint (aOR = 17.99, 95% CI: 7.8–46.3, P < 0.001), hypothyroidism (aOR = 4.27, 95% CI: 1.4–14.3, P = 0.014), below-average income (aOR = 3.40, 95% CI: 1.6–7.8, P = 0.003), and lack of regular exercise (aOR = 2.61, 95% CI: 1.1–6.2, P = 0.027).Conclusion:This study found a high prevalence of cognitive impairment among geriatric clinic patients in Taif. Key predictors include modifiable risks like physical inactivity, treatable conditions such as hypothyroidism, and socioeconomic and personal factors. The findings support routine cognitive screening and highlight priority areas for multidisciplinary interventions to reduce cognitive decline.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment refers to deficits in one or more areas of cognitive function, such as memory, language, executive function, attention, perceptual-motor skills, or social cognition.[1] Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) serves as an intermediate stage between normal cognitive functioning and dementia, where individuals generally maintain most of their functional abilities.[23] Dementia is diagnosed when cognitive decline becomes significant enough to disrupt daily activities, social interactions, or work performance.[4] This clinical syndrome has several underlying causes, including Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, and frontotemporal dementia. While all forms of dementia involve cognitive impairment, not all cognitive impairment progresses to dementia.[5] Worldwide, the prevalence of cognitive impairment varies between 5.1% and 41%. From 1991 to 2021, the global impact of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) in adults aged 65 and older saw a significant rise, with mortality increasing by 115% and prevalence by 160%. In 2021, around 49 million people were affected, with women being more impacted than men. ADRD led to 8.02 million deaths and resulted in 25.38 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) globally.[67]

Although several studies have examined cognitive impairment in Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), these have been predominantly conducted in Riyadh and among community-dwelling elders.[891011] A community-based study in Riyadh found that 45% of individuals aged 60 and older exhibited cognitive impairment, with 38.6% experiencing MCI and 6.4% diagnosed with dementia.[10] Additional studies conducted in primary care settings suggested a dementia prevalence of 11–16% among older adults,[8] while National Guard health records indicated that 3.4% of individuals aged 50 and above had dementia, primarily of the Alzheimer’s subtype.[11] Moreover, multimorbidity and chronic conditions, such as diabetes and stroke, have been linked to cognitive decline.[9] This underscores the pressing need for enhanced screening and management of risk factors in KSA.

Research in the Western region in KSA, particularly in Taif, and within hospital-based geriatric clinics remains limited. Few studies have systematically evaluated modifiable risk factors using validated screening tools in this clinical context. We hypothesize that cognitive impairment is common among older adults attending geriatric clinics in KSA and is closely associated with modifiable factors, including chronic conditions (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia), depression, low educational attainment, and lifestyle factors such as physical inactivity and smoking. Given the rapidly aging population and the paucity of data, this study aims to determine the prevalence of cognitive impairment and its contributing factors to inform targeted screening, early intervention, and prevention strategies in KSA.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was carried out over 6 months, from November 16, 2024 to May 25, 2025, at the Geriatric Clinic of Prince Sultan Military Hospital in Taif, KSA. The clinic offers regular follow-up and specialized care for elderly patients with chronic medical conditions.

Study population

The target population comprised geriatric patients aged 65 years and older attending the Geriatric Clinic at Prince Sultan Military Hospital, Taif, Saudi Arabia.

For participants with limited communication capacity, consent was obtained from a legally authorized representative, and the informant served as the sole respondent for the AD8 screening.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated to detect a significant difference between the hypothesized prevalence of cognitive impairment in older Saudi adults (31.2%)[9] and an expected prevalence of 45%[10] among those with multimorbidity using an exact binomial test to account for the discrete nature of proportion data. With α =0.05, 95% power, and an effect size of 0.138 (p1 – p0), the minimum sample was 162 participants. To account for an anticipated 10% nonresponse and ensure a ± 5% margin of error for prevalence estimates, the target was increased to 182 participants, which was the final recruitment number.

Sampling technique

We used simple random samplings to recruit participants. Initially, we compiled a list of all eligible patients scheduled for routine follow-up visits during the study period. Each patient on that list was assigned a unique identifier. We then utilized a random number generator to randomly select participants, ensuring that each patient had an equal opportunity to be included in the study. Once selected, we approached the patients during their clinic visits. Data collection was carried out through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained healthcare staff or researchers. These interviews occurred during patients’ regular clinic appointments in a private environment to ensure comfort and confidentiality.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire, which consisted of three main sections:

Section 1: We collected detailed sociodemographic and clinical information from all participants. The sociodemographic variables included age, gender, marital status, educational level, living arrangements, employment status, and income range, as well as whether participants required daily assistance with activities of daily living.

Section 2: The clinical variables included medical history and lifestyle factors, including the existence of chronic illnesses (such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, diabetes, depression, and hypothyroidism) as well as the participants’ engagement in regular physical activity. Additionally, we gathered data on family history of dementia and reported memory concerns.

Section 3: Participants were evaluated for cognitive impairment using the Arabic version of the eight-item Alzheimer’s Dementia (AD8) questionnaire, a validated tool for identifying early signs of changes in memory, judgment, orientation, and daily functioning.[12] The questionnaire was completed by the informants themselves or read aloud by the researcher. Participants were asked to concentrate on cognitive issues rather than physical changes, with about one second allowed between questions to promote accurate responses. Spontaneous self-corrections were encouraged and not considered errors. The AD8 includes eight items that evaluate changes in daily cognitive functioning, such as judgment and decision-making skills, decreased interest in hobbies or activities, repetitive questioning or storytelling, difficulty learning how to use familiar tools or appliances, disorientation to time (e.g., forgetting the month or year), challenges managing complex financial tasks, struggles to remember appointments or important dates, and other daily difficulties related to thinking or memory.[13] Each item on the AD8 was assigned a score of 1 point if a cognitive change was indicated (“Yes, a change”) and 0 points if no change was reported or if the response was “Don’t know.” The individual item scores were then totaled to produce a final AD8 score for each participant. According to established thresholds, scores ranging from 0 to 1 suggested normal cognitive function, while scores of 2 or higher indicated potential cognitive impairment that may require further assessment. Participants were made aware that the AD8 serves as a screening tool, not a diagnostic instrument, and that higher scores could suggest early cognitive changes linked to dementia. The AD8 was administered to all eligible participants during their routine follow-up appointments at the geriatric clinic.

Bias and confounders

Potential biases in the study included selection bias and information bias. Potential confounders included age, gender, marital status, education, living arrangements, employment status, income, chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, dyslipidemia, depression, hypothyroidism), smoking, regular exercise [Regular physical exercise was defined, per World Health Organization guidelines for older adults, as either at least 150 minutes of aerobic activity (e.g., walking, swimming, cycling) per week or resistance training at least twice weekly. Participants reported their usual weekly activities, and those meeting criteria were classified as regular exercisers,[14] yes, no], family history of dementia, and subjective memory complaints.

Outcome and studied variables

The primary outcome of the study was cognitive impairment. Secondary variables included sociodemographic factors, clinical characteristics, lifestyle factors, and subjective memory complaints.

Ethical considerations

The study was carried out in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Prince Sultan Military Hospital in Taif, Saudi Arabia (H-02-T-078; REC 2024-939). All participants were thoroughly informed about the study’s objectives, procedures, and their rights, including the ability to refuse participation or withdraw at any point without any impact on their medical care. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their caregivers. To ensure confidentiality and anonymity, coded identifiers were used, and all collected data were securely stored and utilized solely for research purposes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0, with a two-tailed P value of <0.05 deemed statistically significant. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed by means of Shapiro–Wilk tests and Q–Q plots, guiding the choice of appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests. Baseline characteristics were compared based on AD8 status using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. To identify independent predictors of a positive AD8 screen, we utilized multivariable logistic regression through four different approaches: a comprehensive model adjusted for age and gender, a combined model for cognitive complaints to address multicollinearity, a stepwise model based on the AIC, and a clinically informed model. Model performance was assessed using Hosmer–Lemeshow tests, ROC–AUC analysis, variance inflation factor (VIF), AIC/BIC, and McFadden’s pseudo-R². Results were presented as odds ratios accompanied by 95% confidence intervals. The amount of missing data was minimal (<5%) and was addressed through complete-case analysis, with sensitivity analyses confirming the robustness of the findings.

Discussion

Of 182 geriatric outpatients in Taif, Saudi Arabia, more than one third screened positive for cognitive impairment on the AD8. Independent predictors were subjective cognitive complaints, hypothyroidism, below-average income, and sedentary lifestyle. Traditional vascular risks (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) were not independent predictors.

Interpretation of the main study findings

The prevalence of memory impairment in this study was 37.9%, highlighting a major public health concern among older adults in KSA. This aligns with Abd-El Mohsen et al.,[15] who reported 31.6% mild and 17% severe cognitive impairment at Asir Central Hospital, though Alshammari et al.[16] found lower rates (17.1% mild, 3.8% severe) in Riyadh primary care. Internationally, the results are consistent with Park et al.,[17] who noted nearly universal age-related cognitive decline, and Tam and Wang[18] in Hong Kong, who reported 18.9% overall prevalence. Higher rates here may reflect sociodemographic, educational, and health differences, including depression (7.1%). Hale et al.[19] suggest that such impairments represent an early stage in normal cognitive aging rather than isolated clinical issues. These findings emphasize the need for routine cognitive screening in older adults, enabling early intervention to slow progression and improve quality of life.

In this study, subjective cognitive complaints were the strongest and most consistent predictor of objectively verified cognitive impairment: Older adults reporting such complaints were about 15–18 times more likely to show deficits on standardized tests. Jeste[20] noted that these complaints are heterogeneous and not always reliable—especially when based only on self-report—and that their prognostic value depends on population and context. The strong association in this hospital-based geriatric cohort may reflect higher rates of chronic illness, functional impairment, and intensive healthcare use, which can increase agreement between perceived and measured deficits. Although Jeste[20] found that informant reports often outperform self-reports in community samples, the continued significance of self-reported complaints across multiple fully adjusted models here suggests they have independent clinical value in outpatient geriatric care. Overall, these findings indicate that systematically assessing cognitive impairment in clinical settings is a practical, informative way to screen for older adults at high risk of cognitive impairment, especially when combined with formal cognitive testing.

In this study, lower income was a significant independent predictor of cognitive impairment, with older adults earning below-average income nearly three times more likely to experience decline. This aligns with the Women’s Health Study, which linked lower education and income to poorer cognitive performance,[21] and Iskandar et al.,[22] who reported a dose–response relationship between income and cognitive resilience. Longitudinal evidence from the Health and Retirement Study and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing further shows that low socioeconomic status increases dementia risk and accelerates cognitive decline, with lifestyle factors explaining only a small part of this effect.[23] These findings highlight the independent role of income in late-life cognitive health, supporting its consideration in risk assessments and preventive strategies.

In this study, hypothyroidism was a strong predictor of cognitive impairment, with affected elderly patients nearly four times more likely to screen positive across all models. This aligns with evidence linking thyroid dysfunction—particularly hypothyroidism—to cognitive decline. A systematic review by Alšauskė et al.[24] reported significant associations, varying by thyroid type and cognitive domain, while Muhammad et al.[25] observed 2-year declines in Mini-Mental State Examination MMSE and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores among hypothyroid elders. In contrast, van Vliet et al.[26] found no consistent link for subclinical thyroid dysfunction, suggesting effects are stronger in overt hypothyroidism or vulnerable populations. The strong association in our hospital-based sample may reflect higher comorbidity and more symptomatic thyroid disease. These findings highlight the importance of assessing thyroid function in elderly patients as hypothyroidism is a potentially reversible contributor to cognitive impairment.

In this study, lack of regular exercise was a significant risk factor for cognitive impairment in fully adjusted models, with physically inactive older adults more than twice as likely to be impaired. This aligns with extensive evidence that physical activity protects late-life cognition. A narrative review by Busse et al.[27] found that regular, especially aerobic, exercise supports cognitive function in both cognitively healthy and impaired older adults. Likewise, a large systematic review and meta-analysis by Iso-Markku et al.[28] showed that physical activity is linked to better late-life cognition and lower risk of decline, though with modest effect sizes, suggesting mainly population-level benefits. Longitudinal data from China further support this as Chen et al.[29] reported that moderate physical activity significantly improved memory and recall in middle-aged and older adults, whereas excessive activity could be harmful. The delayed emergence of statistical significance in our study may reflect interactions with comorbidities and other lifestyle factors, underscoring that regular, moderate physical activity contributes meaningfully to preserving cognitive health in older adults.

Strengths and limitations

This study addresses a key research gap by providing data from a specialized geriatric clinic in the understudied Western region of KSA. Simple random sampling from the clinic roster reduced selection bias within the clinic population, though this group may not represent all community-dwelling older adults in Taif. The Arabic AD8, while not yet formally validated in Saudi Arabia, has been used in regional studies, allowing cross-study comparisons. Rigorous statistical methods, including multiple modeling approaches and detailed fit statistics, strengthen confidence in the identified predictors. The study limitations include the cross-sectional design, limited generalizability due to a single clinical setting, the use of a screening rather than diagnostic tool, and potential recall and reporting biases from self- and informant-reported data.

Implication of this study

The study highlights key practical implications: Clinically, it advocates for routine cognitive and thyroid screenings and promoting physical activity in older adults; for public health, it calls for national dementia strategies and support for socioeconomically vulnerable elderly populations; and for research, it identifies treatable risk factors like hypothyroidism and inactivity, encouraging longitudinal and intervention studies to protect cognitive health in aging Saudis.

Conclusion and Recommendation

The study found a high prevalence of cognitive impairment among geriatric outpatients in Taif, KSA, with over one-third screening positive. Key independent risk factors included subjective cognitive complaints, hypothyroidism, low socioeconomic status, and physical inactivity, while traditional vascular comorbidities were not significant. The findings support integrating brief cognitive screening tools like the AD8 into routine geriatric assessments, alongside comprehensive management of hypothyroidism, promotion of physical activity, and attention to patient-reported cognitive concerns. Public health efforts should address socioeconomic barriers, and future research should focus on longitudinal and interventional studies to evaluate the impact of modifiable factors on cognitive health in the aging Saudi population.
