Synthesis and in vitro evaluation of dual-modality radio-fluorescent probes ([131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03) for cancer cell imaging
Authors: Eva Maria Widyasari, Alfian Mahardika Flourentin, Dwi Ramadhani, Hendris Wongso, Muhayatun Santoso, Benny Permana, Tita Puspitasari, Daryono Hadi Tjahjono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The combination of two modalities in a single probe for image-guided surgery is expected to be superior to single-modality approaches, as each modality addresses the other's limitations. As a surgical guide, a compound must have a strong affinity for the target. Adding spacers to a drug design is believed to increase a compound's binding affinity; therefore, this study aims to demonstrate the effect of spacers on a compound's affinity for cancer cells. Two hybrid probes [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03, were successfully synthesised and evaluated in vitro. [131I]I-BNF03 consists of benzothiazole, amino methyl benzoic acid spacer, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), and iodine-131, whereas [131I]I-WS03 contains the same components without spacers. The radiolabeling process yielded radiochemical purities of 97.04 ± 1.61% and 93.55 ± 0.50% for [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03, respectively. Confocal microscopy confirmed the cellular uptake of both fluorescent probes, BNF03 and WS03, in MCF-7 cells. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that the FITC fluorescence intensity in BNF03-treated cells was higher than in WS03-treated cells. Cellular uptake studies showed that the incorporation of a spacer significantly enhanced probe internalization in both MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells (p < 0.01), with the highest internalization reaching 81.81% per 5 × 105 cells in the MDA-MB-231 cell line. Consistent with these findings, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations also showed that [131I]I-BNF03 exhibited a more favorable binding interaction toward ERα than [131I]I-WS03 (−63.46 vs. −43.12 kcal mol−1).

Introduction

Surgical oncology is a rapidly evolving field of medicine. The global rise in cancer cases and their negative impacts have spurred greater interest in developing new treatments to improve and extend patients' lives. Surgery has become a cornerstone of this discipline, with significant advancements that have shaped its progress over the years. The obstacles to implementing tumor surgery are complex, involving medical technical factors, the patient's condition, and the tumor's location. Overall, the main challenge is balancing the complete removal of cancer cells with the preservation of the patient's organ function or quality of life.1

Fluorescence image-guided surgery (FIGS) has developed as a method to enhance surgical precision by providing real-time visual or radiological guidance during operations. This technique employs a guide agent that emits optical or radionuclide signals to highlight the target tissue during surgery, thereby enabling more accurate lesion detection than standard visualization methods. A dual-modality agent that combines fluorescent markers and radioisotopes improves intraoperative sensitivity and specificity, enabling initial navigation via radionuclide imaging before surgery, followed by fluorescent guidance throughout the procedure.2

Fluorescent agents such as fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) are commonly used for imaging, primarily because of their straightforward conjugation to target ligands, intense fluorescence, water solubility, and relatively high biocompatibility compared with many other fluorophores. FITC emits strong fluorescence when excited, so it is often used in various cellular and molecular imaging applications for labeling and tracking biological molecules.3 In addition to fluorescent signals, embedding radioisotopes into surgical guide agents enables radionuclide detection. Iodine-131 has long been used in nuclear medicine; its gamma emission facilitates imaging and tissue targeting, while its beta emission provides therapeutic benefit. Combining radionuclide and fluorescent signals within a single molecule enables dual-modality imaging, thereby enhancing the sensitivity and accuracy of tumor detection.4

Benzothiazole-derived heterocyclic compounds have attracted widespread attention as potential anticancer agents due to their cytotoxicity against various cancer cell lines. Previous research has shown that benzothiazole and its derivatives can inhibit cancer cell growth through several mechanisms, including DNA interactions, induction of cell death, and regulation of pathways that contribute to tumor expansion.5 In addition to its biological activity, the conjugated benzothiazole enables further chemical modifications to develop multifunctional agents.6 In dual-probe molecular design, the spacer between the benzothiazole anticancer nucleus and the labeling unit is crucial for shaping the molecule's biological and imaging properties. The BNF03 fluorescent probe was synthesised by adding a spacer between the benzothiazole compound and FITC. Meanwhile, the WS03 fluorescent probe is synthesised by directly reacting benzothiazole compounds with FITC. In drug compound design, a spacer typically separates the two active groups to allow optimal interaction with the receptor's active site, thereby preventing steric hindrance or ensuring safe delivery of high-dose radiation in radiopharmaceuticals.7 Spacers can also increase molecular flexibility, thereby enhancing their affinity for receptors. In addition to their other functions, spacers can also be used to control various physicochemical properties of a drug compound, including lipophilicity, solubility, cell penetration, biodistribution, and the signal-to-background ratio in imaging agents and radiotracers.7,8

An in vitro evaluation is a vital initial phase in the development of surgical guide agents, particularly for assessing cellular accumulation and the intensity of fluorescent and radionuclide signals in relevant cancer cell models.9 This stage serves as a proof of concept before in vivo testing and subsequent translational studies. Limited research exists on the systematic synthesis of benzothiazole-derived anticancer compounds labeled with FITC and 131I, and on the spacer effects through in vitro testing. Therefore, this study aims to synthesize and evaluate benzothiazole-based surgical guide compounds labeled with FITC and 131I, and to compare in vitro activity between molecules with and without spacers, as a basis for developing more precise anticancer surgical guide agents. As a surgical guide for of solid tumors, this study uses MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells, a breast cancer cell line. The novelty of this study lies in the development and evaluation of newly synthesized radio-fluorescent probes as potential dual-modality agents for cancer imaging. By integrating radioactive and fluorescent functionalities into a single molecular platform, these probes offer the potential to combine the high sensitivity of nuclear imaging with the high spatial resolution of fluorescence imaging. The present work investigated their synthesis, characterization, radiolabeling properties, cellular uptake in breast cancer cells, and imaging potential, providing a foundation for the development of next-generation cancer imaging agents.

Methods

Materials and instruments

All chemicals and solvents that were used in this study were purchased from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. 4-Aminomethyl benzoic acid (97%), di-tert-butyl decarbonate (≥99%), FITC (≥90%), 2-amino-6-chlorobenzothiazole (99%), and other analytical-grade reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. High-purity (HPLC-grade) solvents were utilised for reactions and purification. Monitoring of reactions was undertaken using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) on plastic-backed silica gel sheets (silica gel 60 F254, 0.20 mm thick, 20 × 20 cm), with a UV lamp utilised at wavelengths of 254 and 365 nm. Solvent residues were removed by concentrating under low pressure with a Heidolph Hei-VAP rotavapor. The purified synthesised compounds and intermediates were obtained through preparative chromatography on PLC plates (silica gel 60 F254 1 mm, 20 × 20 cm). The proton (1H) and carbon (13C) NMR spectra were obtained using a BRUKER ASCEND 700 MHz system. Mass spectra were recorded with TOF-ESI-MS Waters Q-Tof MS Xevo and LC-HRMS Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 120. Compound purity was assessed by HPLC on an Agilent 1260 Infinity G1314F, employing a 5 µm, 4.6 × 150 mm C18 column.

In vitro analysis of BNF03 and WS03

Cell culture preparation

MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 (ATCC) cell lines were used for in vitro studies, including internalization assays, confocal imaging, and flow cytometry analysis. These cell lines were cultured in a complete medium that contained RPMI 1640 (Gibco, USA), 10% of FBS (Gibco, USA), 1% of 10 000 U mL−1 penicillin-streptomycin (GIBCO, USA), and 0.5% of 250 µg mL−1 amphotericin B (Gibco, USA). The cell lines were incubated to confluence under appropriate conditions of 37 °C, 5% of CO2, and controlled humidity. The cell lines were cultivated and prepared in accordance with the test protocol.

Confocal imaging

The efficacy of fluorescent probes in internalizing into MCF-7 cell lines was assessed using confocal imaging and flow cytometry. 0.3 × 106 of MCF-7 cell lines were grown in a 6-well plate (TPP, Swiss). Upon achieving 80% confluency, the cell culture was treated with fluorescent probes (BNF03 and WS03) at 0.05 mM for incubation times of 5, 15, 60, and 120 minutes. Subsequently, all treatment groups, including the untreated group, were observed, and fluorescence intensity was captured using confocal microscopy (ZEISS LSM 900). The fluorescence intensity measurement was conducted using CellProfiler software that was downloaded (https://www.cellprofiler.org) and installed on a laptop with an Intel® Core i3-6100 CPU @ 2.30 GHz processor, 4.09 GB RAM, and Windows 10 Pro 64-bit operating system. The CellProfiler pipelines are available from the lead contact upon request.

Flow cytometry analysis

The in vitro experimental design was paralleled that employed in the confocal imaging. The flow cytometry experiments were conducted using an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The fluorescence intensity of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) was measured on the BL1 channel (excitation laser: 488 nm, emission filter: 530/30 nm). The histogram displayed a single parameter (fluorescence intensity pattern) for MCF-7 cells stained with the fluorescent probes (BNF03 and WS03), obtained for each experimental group.

Radiolabeling

The fluorescent probes BNF03 and WS03 were labeled with a β-/γ radionuclide (iodine-131) via direct radioiodination at low pH (pH = 4, using glacial acetic acid). In brief, 240/320 µg of WS03/BNF03 was dissolved in 10% DMSO and stirred for 1.0 minutes. Then, 50 µL of 10 mg mL−1 chloramine-T solution in water was added gently, followed by the slow addition of approximately 500 µCi of NaI-131. The mixture was shaken at 25 °C and 700 rpm for 30 minutes in an incubation mixer. Next, 50 µL of 20 mg mL−1 Na2S2O5 was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 1 minute to produce the radiolabeled compounds [131I]I-WS03 and [131I]I-BNF03. The radiochemical purity was assessed using TLC-SG F254 (0.5 × 10 cm) with 0.9% saline as the mobile phase. The first peak in the chromatogram indicates the product, while subsequent peaks represent residual iodine. The RCP of the labeled probes was calculated using a TLC scanner (TLC-240, Comecer) by dividing each product's radioactivity by the total radioactivity on the plate.

Cold labeling

Cold labeling here is used to determine iodination in the compound. Iodination is performed using a procedure similar to radiolabeling, with chloramine-T as the oxidant. In brief, 240/320 µg of WS03/BNF03 was dissolved in 10% DMSO and stirred for 1.0 minutes. Then, 50 µL of a 10 mg mL−1 chloramine-T solution in water was added gently, followed by approximately 1 eq. of KI.10 The mixture was shaken at 25 °C and 700 rpm for 30 minutes in an incubation mixer. A 50 µL of 20 mg mL−1 Na2S2O5 was then added, and the mixture was vortexed for 1 minute to produce the iodinated compounds of I-WS03 and I-BNF03.

Cellular uptake

The cellular uptake of radio-fluorescent probes was assessed in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines according to the procedure described in a previous study.3 The MCF-7 and MDA-MB 231 cell lines were preincubated overnight in 24-well plates at a density of 0.5 × 105 cells per well. After the cells were preincubated, the medium was discarded, and the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, US). Subsequently, the cells were treated with I-131 (control), [131I]I-BNF03, and [131I]I-WS03 at a radioactivity concentration of 0.037 MBq in 500 µL Hanks balanced salt solution (HBSS) (Gibco, USA) for each well. The untreated and treated groups were incubated at 37 °C for 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 h. Upon completion of the incubation period, cells were rinsed with HBSS (2 times) and then lysed in 0.25 M NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich, US). The cell lysates were then collected, and the radioactivity within the cells was measured with a Gamma Counter (Caprac-t, Capintec). Statistical analysis

In this study, all experiments were performed with three replications. Statistical differences in cellular uptake were assessed using two-way ANOVA (Tukey's method) with multiple comparisons. The confidence level for the statistical analysis was set at 99%. All graphs that display means and standard deviation (SD) were created using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0.

Computational analysis

The estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) was used as the target receptor in computational studies. The estrogen receptor crystal structure was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/), and the PDB ID applied was 3ERT. Compounds [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03 were built by GaussView. The Gaussian 09 package optimized the geometry using the B3LYP density functional with a 3-12G basis set. The lowest energy conformation of the ligand was carried out for further docking analysis. AutoDockTools 1.5.6 was used to prepare the receptor and ligand input files for the docking simulation. The grid covering the binding pocket of the estrogen receptors mapped 80 × 54 × 54 points with a spacing of 0.375 Å, centered at x, y, and z coordinates of 23, -1, and 22. All docking parameters were set to their default values, and each simulation was run for 100 iterations. Maximum energy evaluations of 2.5 × 106 were applied using Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm searches, and docking results were further visualized using Discovery Studio software. To determine the optimal ligand conformation on the flexible receptor, molecular dynamics simulations were performed using AMBER 20 (licensed and purchased by BRIN) for 200 ns at 310 K and 1 atm. Visualizations of the stable conformation and interactions were rendered using UCSF Chimera and BIOVIA Discovery Studio (freeware).

Results and discussion

Chemical synthesis

The fluorescent probes BNF03 were synthesised using a four-step procedure (Scheme 1A), and WS03 were synthesised using a one-step procedure (Scheme 1B). The structures of the compounds were confirmed by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and LCMS/HRMS. The fluorescent probes BNF03 and WS03 were synthesised based on a benzothiazole scaffold featuring a visible fluorophore FITC. Briefly, the protection of the amino group of 4-aminomethyl benzoic acid (AMBA) to give compound 1 in 87% yield is described in the previous report.11 The amide coupling reaction of spacer 1 with commercially available 2-amino-6-chlorobenzothiazole afforded spacer-protected amine 2 in 53% yield. Subsequently, Boc deprotection using TFA in dichloromethane gave the target ligand with spacer 3 in 82% yield, which then underwent a condensation reaction with fluorogenic compound FITC in the presence of DIPEA to afford the fluorescent probe BNF03 (4) in low yield (18%). Meanwhile, the fluorescent probe WS03 (5) was obtained by condensing 2-amino-6-chlorobenzothiazole with FITC in the presence of DIPEA, in 25% yield.

In vitro evaluation

Currently, benzothiazole has been extensively researched for the treatment of many diseases, particularly oncological disorders. Here, the assessment of WS03 and BNF03 as potential diagnostic probes has been conducted during the in vitro phase. Unlabeled and radioisotope-labeled probe candidates were evaluated against breast cancer cell lines. This study evaluated fluorescent probes in MCF-7 cell lines to assess the ligands as vehicles for transporting radioisotopes to the target site. MCF-7 is a cell line widely used as a model for human breast cancer and Erα+.12 Because FITC, a fluorophore, has been conjugated to the probe's candidate, accumulation in cell lines can be visualized using fluorescence microscopy. Fig. 1 shows that both probes exhibited a progressive increase in cellular accumulation over the incubation period. The confocal imaging results indicated that the BNF03 treatment groups exhibited higher intensity compared to the WS03 groups (Fig. 1A). The mean integrated intensity of BNF03 was more than twice that of WS03 (Fig. 1B). This suggests that the BNF03 molecule can be more extensively accumulated in cell lines. Thus, the stronger fluorescence signal observed for BNF03 is consistent with a greater amount of probe accumulated in the cells. Flow cytometry analysis also showed that the fluorescence intensity of BNF03 was higher than that of the WS03 groups (Fig. 2A and B).

Radiolabeling

In the present study, we successfully radioiodinated fluorescent probes BNF03 and WS03 to produce the hybrid probes [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03 using chloramine T as the oxidizing agent. Iodine-131, with a half-life of 8.02 days, emits gamma and beta radiation and has potential for both therapeutic and diagnostic applications.13 Therefore, this radionuclide was used as a radioactive label to create hybrid probes for this study. The radioiodination of BNF03 and WS03 was achieved by replacing the hydrogen atom of the phenol group of the FITC unit with an iodine atom via an electrophilic substitution mechanism. The chemical structure of [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03 was proposed (Scheme 2). However, to some extent, di-iodination might also occur at the FITC moiety, as indicated by a prior study. Radio TLC analysis showed radiochemical purities of 97.04 ± 1.61% for [131I]I-BNF03 and 93.55 ± 0.50% for [131I]I-WS03. Peaks for the hybrid probes appeared at Rf values of 0.0–0.2, clearly separate from unreacted [131I]Na, which was found at Rf values of 0.6–1.0.

Cellular uptake of radio-fluorescent probes

The privileged structure of benzothiazole can be easily modified to form various of benzothiazole derivatives that are linked to anticancer effects.14 The radio-labeled probes, [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03, were assessed in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines to evaluate their ability to penetrate human breast cancerous cells. The cellular uptake profiles of the unlabeled probes and iodine-131-labeled probes were similar (Fig. 1, 2 and 3). The studies demonstrated that the probes functioned as carriers for iodine-131 and markedly enhanced cellular accumulation (p < 0.0001). Moreover, the probe's cellular uptake is time-dependent, with progressive increases in radioactive accumulation and fluorescence intensity. The greatest cellular uptake was observed in the iodine-131-labeled BNF03 probe. Interestingly, the [131I]I-BNF03 compound exhibited a higher cellular accumulation than [131I]I-WS03 in both the MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines. This implies that AMBA's presence in BNF03 significantly influences cellular accumulation capacity. The highest accumulation was observed in MDA-MB-231 after 3 hours of incubation, at 81.81% per 5 × 105 cells (Fig. 3). In addition, the cellular accumulation of [131I]I-BNF03 increased up to 14 times compared to [131I]I-WS03 and exceeded 130 times that of unlabeled iodine-131. Overall, the addition of the spacer in [131I]I-BNF03 significantly improves accumulation in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 relative to iodine-131 and [131I]I-WS03.

Computational analysis

The computational study was conducted to determine why in vitro testing of the compound [131I]I-BNF03 showed higher uptake in MCF-7 cells than [131I]I-WS03. Molecular docking was performed to determine the ligand's binding pose in the binding pocket, and the best results were subsequently subjected to molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to assess the interaction's stability over time. The dominant receptor in MCF-7 cells is Estrogen Receptor alpha (ERα);15,16 therefore, ERα (PDB ID 3ERT) was used in this study. The results of the molecular dynamics simulation showed that both compounds [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03 remained stable in the binding pocket for 200 ns of the simulation (Fig. 4 and 5). In addition, interactions between ligands ([131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03) and residues were also observed. The ligand [131I]I-BNF03 appears to have more interactions compared to [131I]I-WS03 (Fig. 4 and 5). The existence of spacers here provides additional interactions, both hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. In the ligand [131I]I-BNF03, there is a π–π interaction that is not found in [131I]I-WS03. π–π interactions are known to have a great effect on complex stability, bond affinity, and even target selectivity.

In addition, MMPBSA (Molecular Mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann) calculations have been carried out to estimate binding free energy (ΔG binding) between ligands and proteins following Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations (Table 1). MMPBSA calculations indicate that the van der Waals interaction is an important element in this compound. This is consistent with Table 2, which indicates that hydrophobic interactions are the primary driving force for these compounds. As a compound to be used in guided surgery, it must have a strong affinity for receptors. From Table 2, it is seen that the compound [131I]I-BNF03 has a higher binding free energy than [131I]I-WS03 and OHT. This is one of the factors that may explain why the compound [131I]I-BNF03 shows greater uptake than [131I]I-WS03 in MCF-7 cells.

Furthermore, the RMSD profile shows that hybrid probes have RMSD values below 3 Å (Fig. 6A). The flow of hybrid compounds resembles that of OHT. [131I]I-BNF03 has stabilized within 30 ns of the simulation and has a larger RMSD than OHT. While [131I]I-WS03 has an RMSD similar to that of OHT, it takes longer to reorganize its structure to reach an optimal position. The simulated pattern of the hybrid compounds resembles that of the OHT native ligand (Fig. 6B). The molecular dynamics for the RMSF of hybrid compounds are generally lower than those for OHT. Furthermore, the similarities in the RMSF patterns between OHT and the test compounds suggest that both compounds may interact comparably with proteins. These interactions of test or native compounds with specific protein residues signify the possession of similar modes of action in the target protein.17

Our findings indicated that benzothiazole-derivative probes can serve as carriers for the accumulation of FITC and iodine-131 in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines. The insertion of AMBA markedly improved cellular accumulation. Other studies reported that incorporating AMBA into the peptide backbone enhanced cellular uptake via endocytosis.18 The heterocyclic scaffold of benzothiazole compounds, combined with their lipophilic properties, typically enables them to cross the lipid bilayer of cancer cell membranes via passive diffusion.19 However, this study has not identified the cellular uptake pathway that AMBA affects to facilitate cellular accumulation. Based on the in silico studies, the presence of spacers here provides additional interactions, both hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions against Erα (Fig. 4 and 5). In addition, the log P value prediction using pkCSM web server (https://biosig.unimelb.edu.au/pkcsm/) showed that [131I]I-BNF03 was more lipophilic than [131I]I-WS03, with a value of 8.27 and 7.34, respectively. It's indicated that the AMBA addition in BNF03 enhanced the lipophilicity of the compound. These results strengthened the in vitro findings that the cellular accumulation of the BNF03 probe exceeds that of the WS03 probe. The balance between hydrophobicity and residual positive charge affects how easily compounds penetrate cells.18,20 However, the cellular uptake mechanisms of unlabeled and iodine-131-labeled probes remain unidentified. Research has shown that benzothiazole compounds possess anticancer properties through multiple mechanisms, such as topoisomerase II suppression in myeloid leukemia cancer cells,21 DNA damage stimulation in MDA-MB-231 cells,22 cell cycle stalling at the pre-G1 and G2/M phases, and the induction of programmed cell death via increased expression of caspase-3, p53, and Bax, alongside decreased expression of Bcl-2 in MCF-7 cells,23 as well as modulation of signaling pathways and immune responses.24

Conclusion

In the present study, two hybrid fluorescence/radiotracer probes, [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03, were successfully synthesised and evaluated through computational and in vitro studies as potential agents for cancer imaging and surgical guidance. The results show that the hybrid radio-fluorescent probes [131I]I-BNF03 and [131I]I-WS03 can be taken up by MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. The probe [131I]I-BNF03, which has a spacer, demonstrated higher fluorescence intensity, increased cellular uptake, and a higher binding interaction for the ERα receptor than that of [131I]I-WS03, which has no spacer. Although further studies are required to provide more comprehensive evidence, the present results indicated that the incorporation of spacer in the probe structure may improve the targeting performance of hybrid probes for image-guided surgery. Despite the study afforded promising results, it has several limitations, including the lack of physiological stability evaluation, assessment in non-cancerous cell lines, and in vivo validation in tumor-bearing animal models. Thus, future studies should investigate probe stability, cellular selectivity, biodistribution, and tumor-targeting performance, as well as correlate fluorescence imaging findings with histopathological analyses to further evaluate their potential for tumor visualization and margin delineation.
