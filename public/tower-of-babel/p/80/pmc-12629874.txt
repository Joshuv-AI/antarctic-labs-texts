Systematic comparison of GPT models for the analysis of pathology reports in a low-resource language: A case study for Turkish
Authors: Omer Faruk Dilbaz, Muhammet Nusret Ozates, Beyza Bolat, Cigdem Gunduz-Demir, Ibrahim Kulac
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLarge language models (LLMs) can process text for various applications, including surgical pathology reports, but studies primarily focus on English. Their performance has not been systematically studied for a low-resource language. To analyze the performance of various LLMs, 759 Turkish pathology reports from 5 different procedures were selected.MethodsWe used 10 examples from every procedure to optimize prompts for OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o mini, and GPT-4o. The rest was used to test generalizability.ResultsThe GPT-4o model performed superior in processing Turkish reports (12%-25% over GPT-3.5 Turbo, 3%-16% over GPT-4o mini). English-translated versions of the reports have been demonstrated to enhance accuracy, especially for GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4o mini. GPT4-o showed comparable results for Turkish and English. A 12% to 22% performance gap was observed between GPT-4o and GPT-3.5 Turbo for English-translated reports. Domain-related tips in prompts increased accuracy. Results of larger test sets were parallel for all models with the validation set. The GPT-4o model yielded the most accurate results, while the GPT-4o mini model demonstrated intermediate performance. The GPT-3.5 Turbo model exhibited the least accuracy.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, for the first time in the literature, we have demonstrated the performance of GPT models in Turkish surgical pathology reports, and results indicate that data extracted by GPT-4o are almost ready for direct application.

INTRODUCTION

Despite significant efforts, pathology reports remain unstructured. These data need to be converted into a structured format to perform downstream analysis. This manual conversion is time-consuming and error-prone. Computational information extractors can systematically process these reports for rapid and objective conversions. There is a growing interest in using large language models (LLMs) to extract information from text for various applications,1 including medicine. Earlier studies used the encoder part of the transformer model,2 such as BERT.3 Researchers continued to pretrain a “vanilla” BERT model using PubMed abstracts.4 They outperformed the vanilla version, and this model was further trained5 to propose Clinical BERT. Thereafter, the tokenizer was extended6 and pretrained on renal pathology reports.

With significant improvements in generative LLMs and their ability to understand and generate text, particularly GPT-3,7 GPT-4,8 and later models, they are becoming the state-of-the-art practice for information extraction. The latest models have shown the ability of in-context learning, with even zero or a few examples on the prompt,9 making them ideal for any text-based downstream tasks without further training. The LLMs have been tested for various tasks in medicine. The GPT-4 model was used to extract structured information from English pathology reports, giving requested column names and possible answers without any domain knowledge in the prompt.10 These reports also showed that LLMs can come up with a conclusion using the information in the report, such as determining N1 stage for metastatic lymph nodes even though N1 has not been stated in the report. Similarly, an open-weight LLM model, LLama 2, was used to create a pipeline for information extraction, emphasizing the positive impact of the model size and the use of prompt engineering methods.11

Although these models led to promising results, they were tested in English with a limited number of parameters, and their performance has yet to be systematically studied for a low-resource language. To address this gap, we selected Turkish and systematically analyzed the performance of various OpenAI models, including GPT-4o,12 GPT-4o mini,13 and GPT-3.5 Turbo.14

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data Set

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Koç University (protocol number: 2024.352.IRB2.151), and the study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was conducted on pathology reports of prostatectomies, lung resections, colectomies, nephrectomies, and gastrectomies signed out between June 2021 and February 2024 at Koç University Hospital. Reports were exported from the laboratory information system and deidentified. A total of 782 reports were reviewed by a pathologist (I.K.) and/or a senior pathology resident (O.F.D.) for eligibility. Only resection procedures for primary solid tumors were included. Reports with nonneoplastic features and metastases were excluded, and 759 reports from 5 different procedures with variable complexities were selected. Among those, 10 reports from each procedure were randomly selected and manually annotated for the validation and optimization of the prompts. The remaining reports were used to test the generalizability of the chosen settings. For each procedure, spreadsheet templates with the desired structure were created according to the College of American Pathologists’ synoptic cancer reporting15 protocols. Subsequently, selected reports were reviewed to ensure compliance with the College of American Pathologists format. Figure 1 illustrates this data set process.

Data preparation. Suitable pathology reports are selected, and the deidentified versions are saved in a spreadsheet such that every row contains 1 deidentified report. Later, to evaluate the model and prompt performance, 10 reports are chosen randomly and annotated according to the College of American Pathologists’ synoptic cancer reporting protocols by a senior pathology resident. The remaining reports are used as test samples to measure the generalization capability of the model and the best-performing prompt for each specimen type.

Methodology

Following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines, this study systematically compares the use of 3 LLMs (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o mini, and GPT-4o) in pathology reports written in Turkish. Each LLM is used in 2 settings: (1) the original reports are fed directly into the prompt, and (2) they are first translated into English and then fed into the prompt. It is worth noting that we used the first setting to evaluate the language understanding capability in Turkish, while the second setting was chosen because all state-of-the-art LLMs have been trained and fine-tuned for instruction following mostly in English, which leads to differences in output quality.16 A common strategy is to translate an input into English to allow the model to use its English language understanding capabilities. We use the GPT-4o model for translation as it is the most powerful model for Turkish among the 3 models. Independent of the setting, all other parts of the prompts were written in English to maximize instruction following capabilities.

Prompt development

A prompt is an input to guide LLMs by conveying the user’s intent and specifying how the model’s output should be.16 The quality of the prompt affects the model performance,17,18 particularly in smaller models. When interacting with an LLM, 2 types of prompts are commonly used: a system prompt and a user prompt. Although there is no strict definition of which prompt should be used for which part of the input, the system prompt is often used to explain the role/persona of the model, the rules of an interaction, and what to expect from the interaction. Giving personas to LLMs can improve model accuracy and output quality.19 We use a system prompt to assign the model a pathologist persona by instructing it as follows: “You are an expert doctor in pathology. You are asked to extract necessary information from the pathology reports of cancer patients.” On the other hand, the user prompt provides the specific input context (eg, a pathology report for a patient with cancer who underwent a gastrectomy), details the expected task, and specifies the desired output format to facilitate parsing the model’s answer. In our work, to define the task clearly, we structure the user prompt into 3 sections: 1) a description of the problem and the desired outcome, (2) possible options for each variable to be extracted, and (3) “tips” that contain additional instructions and domain-specific knowledge about the task. Experiment settings and evaluation methodology

Prompt engineering is the iterative process of developing and refining a prompt by modifying the prompting technique.16 As shown in Figure 4, we use a prefilled/preextracted set of 10 cases as a validation data set during the prompt engineering process. We initially evaluated validation data sets automatically using exact matches and then reviewed misclassified columns manually to identify errors and improve the prompts. A different number of variables were extracted for each specimen type: 23 variables for prostatectomy, 31 for colectomy, 16 for nephrectomy, 21 for gastrectomy, and 40 for lung resection. For every model, we used the same system prompt and chose a temperature value of 0.5 for both the report translation and variable extraction process to reduce the randomness of the output. All experiments on the validation sets were repeated 5 times, and their averages were taken into consideration. Once acceptable performance was achieved on the validation data sets, we evaluated the model performance on larger test sets to measure the generalization of results. A senior pathology resident manually filled out variables for all resection types, and subsequently, variables were cross-checked.

Prompt engineering process. Model evaluation: The developed prompt for the selected specimen type and the patient record are fed into a large language model, and the model’s output is converted into a Python dictionary. This output is then compared with the expected output, and if they are different, a medical expert and an artificial intelligence researcher review them to determine if the difference is a real error or caused by a nonexact match.

Accuracy was used as the evaluation metric. We reported the results based on the number of correctly classified variables. In particular, we calculated accuracy as x/(y∙n), where x represents the total number of correctly classified variables, y is the number of extracted columns, and n is the number of patients. This metric allows comprehensive evaluation to detect common mistakes in the models.

To assess the significance of these accuracies, we calculated 95% confidence intervals using the Wald method for each sample type and model.

RESULTS

Findings on the development cohort

We first evaluated the models on Turkish pathology reports, testing each on 10 cases per specimen type. GPT-3.5 Turbo achieved the highest accuracy in lung resection but performed the worst in gastrectomy and colectomy. GPT-4o mini outperformed GPT-3.5 Turbo across all resection procedures, with the highest accuracy observed in lung resections, followed by colectomy and prostatectomy. The GPT-4o model demonstrated notable superior performance in processing Turkish reports, with an improvement of 15% to 25% over GPT-3.5 Turbo and 3% to 16% over GPT-4o mini. The most accurate result (>95%) was obtained for nephrectomy.

We then assessed the models on English translations of the same reports. For GPT-3.5 Turbo, using English-translated versions improved accuracy for all procedures except gastrectomy. Although the ranking of accuracy across the procedures remained largely unchanged, the accuracy of the gastrectomy became the worst. The greatest improvement was observed for colectomy, prostatectomy, and nephrectomy, with an increase of 4%, followed by lung resection, with an increase of 3%. For the GPT-4o mini model, a notable improvement was observed in the accuracy of colectomy, with an increase of 7%. This was the highest improvement observed for this specific model and across all models. Other procedures demonstrated consistency. The GPT-4o model had comparable accuracy on both the original reports and their translations. It also performed better for colectomy reports, with a 3% increase. In addition, for English-translated reports, GPT-4o was better than GPT-3.5 Turbo across all resection procedures, while it exhibited nearly identical performance to GPT-4o mini for lung resection. A 12% to 22% performance gap was observed between GPT-4o and GPT-3.5 Turbo models for English-translated reports of all resection procedures. However, it achieved an improvement of 1% to 17% over GPT-4o mini. Although all models performed reasonably well on the original Turkish reports, most showed improved accuracy when tested on English translations. However, due to the limited number of cases in the validation cohort and report-level aggregates, we did not conduct formal statistical comparisons. Effects of tips on accuracy

We provided a detailed explanation of the terms “N/A,” “absent,” and “cannot be determined.” During the prompt development process, we consistently observed that the models struggled to distinguish between these values. Furthermore, we specified in the prompt that making use of information from other columns was permissible to determine the value of another column, which may have facilitated self-education. For example, some pTNM parameters may begin with the letter “y.” Thus, we specified that if one of these parameters starts with “y,” all subsequent parameters must also begin with “y.” This led to a significant improvement in accuracy. Moreover, we explicitly stated that the model should classify a situation as unifocal if there are no separate tumor foci or if the multifocal parameter is absent. Lastly, we added a critical general tip related to formatting. We specified that if the model cannot extract information, it must leave the corresponding column as an empty string unless “N/A” is used and should output only the result without any additional commentary or indicators. The procedure-specific tips led to a substantial enhancement in the accuracy of the results. Details are provided in Supplementary File 3.

The number of reports in the test sets was 277, 66, 140, 71, and 155 for prostatectomy, nephrectomy, lung resection, gastrectomy, and colectomy, respectively. GPT-3.5 Turbo produced similar results for most procedures. On the other hand, it yielded better test set accuracy for gastrectomy. This enhancement can be ascribed to the simplified identification of less intricate parameters such as “margin(s) involved by invasive carcinoma,” “margin status for dysplasia,” “margin(s) involved by low-grade dysplasia,” and “margin(s) involved by carcinoma in situ.” The GPT-4o mini led to a decrease in accuracy in lung resection due to the model speculating on “tumor grade” and “the closest margins to invasive carcinoma,” despite these details not being explicitly stated in the reports. Additionally, subtle differences in the classification of “N/A,” “absent,” or “negative” for “direct invasion of adjacent structures” and “margin status in noninvasive tumor” parameters contributed to the decrease. The GPT-4o mini model achieved better test accuracy for gastrectomy and nephrectomy and similar results for others. The increase in nephrectomy may be due to the model performing better for tumor size format and slightly better for sarcomatoid-rhabdoid feature parameters. The GPT-4o model yielded comparable test set accuracy for most reports. Moreover, it also performed better for gastrectomy reports, with a 5% increase. The enhanced accuracy of gastrectomy reports may be attributable to the same underlying factors observed in other models. All these findings emphasize the need for better prompt engineering and the ability to better fine-tune the model to minimize such errors and improve overall performance.

DISCUSSION

Artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted information extraction from medical records has the potential to organize more structured data for future applications. Given the central role of pathology in interdisciplinary medical research, structured information from reports is crucial. Our study showed that LLMs provide a promising alternative for this task. We not only compared the performance of various LLM models but also assessed them in Turkish, a low-resource language.

Previously, Choi et al18 tested the ability of LLMs to extract structured data from medical reports and published promising results. Unlu et al20 utilized retrieval augmented generation, empowered by GPT-4, to extract inclusion/exclusion criteria from free-text clinical reports, thereby enhancing efficiency and lowering costs. Huang et al21 worked on scanned PDF versions of pathology reports and showed an average accuracy of 89%. GPT-4 Turbo showed a superior performance for most metrics. Pais et al22 demonstrated that an AI-assisted system combining LLMs, domain-specific expertise, and human-in-the-loop feedback improved medication prescription workflows. However, to effectively streamline AI-generated spreadsheets into workflows, efforts should focus on improving model accuracy. Obtaining comprehensive data sets is essential to monitor model prediction stability.23 This topic was explored in a pilot study and demonstrated to the pathology community that LLMs hold significant potential.24

In our study, GPT-4o effectively extracted data from various pathology reports with high accuracy. It outperformed all other models on both the original reports and their translations. The proprietary nature of these LLMs makes it difficult to fully explain performance differences. However, GPT-4o’s higher parameter size likely accounts for its superior accuracy compared to GPT-3.5 Turbo. In Turkish, the performance gap is largely due to the limited training data and the agglutinative nature of the language, which increases token count and complicates model understanding. Because the GPT-4o mini model shares the same tokenizer and supports the same range of languages as GPT-4o, we assume that it is based on GPT-4o, which is much more powerful than GPT-3.5 Turbo. The GPT-4o mini model also outperforms GPT-3.5 Turbo by a large margin in other benchmarks,13 which also explains performance differences for the translated reports. Proper tokenization enhances a model’s ability to learn medical terms.6 For example, the GPT-4o and the GPT-4o mini model use a more efficient tokenizer for Turkish, reducing the token count by 7.38% compared to older models.

In our experiments, all models fail for some specific tasks. We grouped them as major and minor errors. The following was considered a major error: incorrect determination of tumor involvement or distances related to the status of surgical margins, generation of hallucinatory data, or inappropriate response to parameters that could affect the patient’s treatment outcome, such as tumor type, pattern, grade, lymphovascular invasion, or noncompliance with the desired prefix rules for tumor stage. Errors that did not affect the patient’s treatment outcome and were related to formatting or confusion between terminologies like “absent” and “N/A” were considered minor. Generative models are known to create hallucinated responses.25,26 To alleviate this issue, we provided clear prompts, giving domain-specific instructions and options for each parameter to conform outputs to predefined templates. Despite our efforts to minimize fabricated responses, the models’ performances remained highly dependent on prompt engineering. Using the same prompt and the same data set, models sometimes extracted information correctly, while at other times, they made mistakes. These inconsistencies emphasize an important issue of using LLMs for medical applications: hallucinated outputs can undermine trust in AI. Pathologists should recognize that AI tools are prone to errors and biases and use these tools accordingly. Moreover, incorporating LLM-based tools into health care settings faces challenges related to cost, computing power, and data privacy.27 There is a need to further investigate all these issues.10

CONCLUSION

Designing our prompts following international pathology reporting standards, the models led to accurate results, even though the prompts were optimized on smaller data sets. This is the first example of using multiple models on pathology reports written in Turkish. Our experiments showed that the GPT-4o model was particularly good at extracting data for medical research and other applications, even in a low-resource language such as Turkish. Currently, all state-of-the-art models have proprietary licenses and are gated by application programming interfaces (APIs). While these models demonstrate impressive performance, as also seen in our results, open-source LLMs that can be run locally are preferred due to patient privacy concerns. On the other hand, using smaller and open-source models on low-resource languages presents challenges. Most of them are limited to English and widely spoken European languages. Additionally, they are more prone to hallucinations, and outputs are harder to control, especially in low-resource languages. Assessing and improving the open-source models’ quality should be further studied for improved performance and broader applicability in medical contexts, particularly for languages with limited data. Finally, the development of standardized evaluation metrics for pathology reports in low-resource languages would be beneficial for comparing and tracking the progress of different models. Nevertheless, the difficulties of employing smaller models emphasize the benefits of using GPT models. Our study serves as a noteworthy illustration of this utilization.
