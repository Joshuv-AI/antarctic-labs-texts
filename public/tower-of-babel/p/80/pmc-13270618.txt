Awareness, knowledge, perception and behavioral risk predictors of acceptability of long-acting injectable lenacapavir HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis in Ghana: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ebenezer Senu, Cornelia Quainoo Donkor, Success Acheampomaa Oppong, Daniel Dzemedo Nouwati, Emmanuel Ntori, George Kyei Agyen, Alfred Effah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-acting injectable HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), particularly lenacapavir, has demonstrated near-complete efficacy, yet its public health impact depends on community awareness, knowledge and acceptance. This cross-sectional study assessed awareness, knowledge, perceptions, acceptability, and predictors of lenacapavir use among adults in Ghana. We found awareness, knowledge, and perceptions were generally low, whilst acceptability was moderate (60.5%). Poor perception [(aOR = 0.12, 95% CI (0.05–0.29), p < 0.001)] and irregular exposure to online health information [(aOR = 0.24, 95% CI (0.07–0.77), p = 0.017)] were independently associated, 88.0% and 76.0% respectively, with lower acceptability. Targeted education, health worker engagement, and supportive policies are essential to facilitate lenacapavir uptake in Ghana.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-026-00881-6.Keywords: Acceptability, Awareness, HIV prevention, Lenacapavir, Pre-exposure prophylaxis

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection and its advanced form, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), remain major global public health challenges. As of 2024, approximately 40.8 million people were living with HIV worldwide, with sub-Saharan Africa accounting for nearly two-thirds of this burden WHO [17]. Ghana has a national HIV prevalence of approximately 1.6%, with disproportionately higher rates among key populations such as female sex workers and adolescent girls and young women [3]. Although global HIV incidence has declined over the past decade, progress has been uneven, and new infections remain concentrated in low- and middle-income countries UNAIDS [16]. This persistent burden indicates the need for innovative, scalable, and acceptable HIV prevention strategies that align with the lived realities and preferences of populations at risk.

Daily oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) has significantly reduced HIV transmission risk; however, its effectiveness is limited by adherence challenges, pill fatigue, stigma, and privacy concerns [1, 9, 12, 13]. These limitations highlight the central importance of PrEP acceptability, as even highly efficacious biomedical interventions cannot achieve meaningful public health impact without sustained user uptake and willingness to use them consistently. Consequently, interest has grown in long-acting HIV prevention technologies that reduce dosing frequency and dependence on daily user behavior. Lenacapavir is a first-in-class capsid inhibitor administered as a subcutaneous injection every six months and has demonstrated near-complete efficacy in recent clinical trials [2, 6]. Its long-acting profile offers potential advantages for individuals who experience difficulties with daily oral PrEP. However, the successful translation of Lenacapavir’s clinical efficacy into real-world HIV prevention will depend heavily on community acceptance and readiness, shaped by factors such as individual perceptions of safety and effectiveness, trust in health systems and providers, and exposure to accurate health information. Evidence from prior HIV prevention innovations indicates that biomedical efficacy alone does not ensure public health impact; awareness, knowledge, perception, and acceptability remain critical determinants of uptake [4].

To date, no study in Ghana has assessed public readiness for long-acting injectable PrEP, including Lenacapavir. Understanding the acceptability of Lenacapavir and the factors that influence it is therefore essential for anticipating potential implementation challenges and informing context-appropriate rollout strategies. This study therefore assessed awareness, knowledge, perceptions, acceptability, and associated factors related to Lenacapavir among adults in Ghana to inform future policy, education, and implementation strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and study site

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among adults aged 18 years and older residing in Ghana. Participants were recruited nationwide, with representation from Greater Accra, Ashanti, and other regions, to capture geographic diversity in access to HIV prevention information.

Study population and sampling

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 18 years residing in Ghana, able to read English, and willing to provide informed consent. Individuals outside Ghana, below the age threshold, or with incomplete or duplicate responses were excluded. A minimum sample size of 300 was estimated using a single population proportion formula assuming 50% acceptability in the absence of prior data. Due to feasibility constraints, 285 complete responses were analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 1). Convenience sampling was employed using online platforms. While this approach facilitated rapid recruitment across multiple regions, it may have preferentially included individuals with internet access and higher health-information exposure.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Committee on Human Research, Publication, and Ethics of Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST) (CHRPE/AP/462/23). Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Variables and measurement

Awareness was assessed based on a set of structured questions on Lenacapavir, and correctly identified as an HIV prevention option. Responses were scored dichotomously (yes = 1, no = 0). Knowledge was measured using multiple items assessing understanding of Lenacapavir’s purpose, dosing interval, and mode of administration, with correct responses summed to generate a composite knowledge score. Perception was assessed using Likert-scale items evaluating perceived effectiveness, safety, convenience, and community acceptability of Lenacapavir. Acceptability, the primary outcome, was measured using items assessing willingness to use Lenacapavir, preference relative to daily oral PrEP, and willingness to recommend it to others. For all domains, item scores were summed to generate composite scores, which were categorized into low and high levels using median cut-off values for analysis. Internal consistency was acceptable for all domains (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79 for awareness, 0.83 for knowledge, and 0.82 for acceptability). Due to the absence of validated thresholds for this study population, the median score was used to dichotomize all domains. Acceptability was dichotomized at the median, with scores ≥ median coded as high acceptability (1) and scores < median coded as low acceptability (0) for regression analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics. Associations between independent variables and acceptability were examined using Chi-square tests and logistic regression. Variables significant at p < 0.05 were included in a multivariable model to identify independent predictors of high acceptability. A participant inclusion flow diagram was developed to illustrate recruitment, exclusion of incomplete responses, and the final analytic sample used for regression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Results

Awareness, knowledge, perception, and acceptability of lenacapavir

Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of awareness, knowledge, perception, and acceptability of Lenacapavir among study participants. The majority demonstrated low awareness of Lenacapavir (68.9%), while only 31.1% had high awareness. Knowledge was largely inadequate, with 84.7% classified as having poor knowledge and only 15.3% demonstrating adequate knowledge. Perception of Lenacapavir as an HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis was predominantly poor (75.4%), whereas 24.6% exhibited a good perception. Acceptability was moderate; 39.5% of participants had high acceptability of Lenacapavir, while 60.5% had low acceptability (Fig. 1).

Variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analysis, along with clinically relevant variables, were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemes how goodness-of-fit test (p > 0.05 indicating adequate fit). Multicollinearity was evaluated using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), with all values < 2 indicating no significant multicollinearity. In the multivariable logistic regression model, three factors remained independently associated with acceptability. Participants residing in Greater Accra [(aOR = 0.35, 95% CI (0.13–0.95), p = 0.040)] had significantly lower odds of high acceptability compared to those in other regions. Individuals with irregular weekly exposure to online health information [(aOR = 0.24, 95% CI (0.07–0.77), p = 0.017)] had 76% lower odds of high acceptability compared with those exposed daily. Most notably, participants with poor perception of Lenacapavir [(aOR = 0.12, 95% CI (0.05–0.29), p < 0.001)] were 88% less likely to report high acceptability compared with those with good perception. Awareness and knowledge levels were not independently associated with acceptability after adjustment for confounders (Table 1).

Discussion

This study provides early evidence on community readiness for Lenacapavir as a long-acting injectable HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis in Ghana, with particular emphasis on factors associated with acceptability. The findings indicate that while awareness and knowledge of Lenacapavir were low, perception was strongly associated with differences in acceptability across participants. The dominance of perception as the strongest associated factor of acceptability is consistent with prior research on both oral PrEP and long-acting injectable HIV prevention. Studies have shown that perceived safety, trust in biomedical innovations, and confidence in effectiveness are more influential than factual knowledge alone in determining willingness to adopt new HIV prevention tools [8, 15]. Although causal relationships cannot be inferred, neutral or uncertain perceptions, as observed in this study, are common during early stages of biomedical innovation introduction and may represent a critical opportunity for targeted education before negative attitudes become entrenched [4].

The finding that awareness and knowledge were not independently associated with acceptability contrasts with assumptions that increased information alone guarantees uptake. Similar observations have been reported in studies of injectable cabotegravir and oral PrEP, where individuals expressed willingness to use PrEP despite limited technical understanding, provided they trusted the intervention and the health system delivering it [10, 11]. The finding that low awareness was associated with higher acceptability in univariable analysis is counterintuitive but may reflect several behavioral and methodological dynamics. First, individuals with limited prior knowledge may express hypothetical acceptance, indicating willingness in principle without fully understanding potential limitations or concerns. Second, a novelty effect may lead to favorable perceptions of new biomedical interventions before critical appraisal occurs. Third, social desirability bias may have influenced participants to report willingness to adopt a perceived beneficial health intervention. These dynamics have been observed in early-stage acceptability studies of novel HIV prevention technologies and underscore the importance of interpreting acceptability alongside depth of understanding. Together, these findings suggest that communication strategies may benefit from prioritizing positive perceptions and trust rather than focusing solely on biomedical details.

Lower acceptability among participants with irregular exposure to online health information was observed, aligning with evidence that consistent engagement with health information platforms is associated with greater confidencein novel HIV prevention technologies [7]. Digital and media-based health communication has been shown to shape attitudes toward PrEP, particularly among younger populations in sub-Saharan Africa, where online platforms increasingly serve as primary sources of health information.

The reduced acceptability observed among residents of Greater Accra may reflect contextual factors, including heightened exposure to competing health narratives, stigma, or skepticism toward new biomedical interventions in urban settings, as reported in prior HIV prevention research [14]. Urban populations have previously been shown to report greater concern regarding cost, side effects, and service delivery complexity, which have been identified as persistent barriers to PrEP uptake [5].

These findings reinforce existing evidence that successful implementation of long-acting HIV prevention technologies requires more than clinical efficacy. While causal conclusions cannot be drawn from this cross-sectional study, interventions that enhance positive perception, strengthen trust in health systems, and ensure consistent access to accurate health information are likely to be important considerations for maximizing acceptability and potential future uptake of Lenacapavir in Ghana and similar settings.

Limitations

This study is limited by the use of an online convenience sampling strategy, which may have introduced selection bias and underrepresented adults without internet access or lower digital literacy, thereby limiting generalizability. Although broad dissemination across multiple platforms (including social media, institutional networks, and professional groups), targeted outreach to diverse demographic groups, and efforts to ensure survey clarity and device accessibility were implemented to enhance inclusivity, representation bias cannot be fully excluded. Additionally, a smaller sample size in the study may have reduced statistical power, limiting the ability to detect small but meaningful associations and increasing the risk of Type II error. Therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies using larger, probability-based samples are recommended to enhance generalizability and statistical robustness.

Conclusion

Awareness and knowledge of Lenacapavir remain low among adults in Ghana, yet the acceptability of long-acting injectable HIV prevention is moderate. Perception is the strongest predictor of acceptability, and trust in health workers represents a major facilitator. Addressing informational gaps, affordability concerns, and perception-related barriers will be essential to translate Lenacapavir’s clinical promise into a meaningful public health impact in Ghana.
