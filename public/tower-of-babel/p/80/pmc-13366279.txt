Clinically Relevant Motion of Canine Thoracic Tumors Is Seen Between Inspiratory and Expiratory Breath Holds on Computed Tomography Performed for Radiation Treatment Planning
Authors: Carolynne R. Kruckman‐Gatesy, Susan M. LaRue, Lynn R. Griffin, Mary‐Keara Boss, Gregg Griffenhagen, Sangeeta Rao, Cory N. Wakamatsu, Del Leary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdvancements in radiation therapy allow for targeting smaller tumor volumes with exceedingly conformal precision. Target motion during the breathing cycle needs to be anticipated. To date, no information exists that characterizes the motion of canine thoracic tumors during the respiratory cycle. The primary purpose of this study is to assess the pattern of motion of thoracic tumors in three‐dimensional (3D) space between inspiratory and expiratory breath holds as a surrogate measurement of the breathing cycle. A secondary aim is to evaluate whether tumor position within the thorax relates to the degree of motion. Fourteen dogs were prospectively enrolled and received thoracic computed tomography (CT) scans with pre‐ and post‐contrast inspiratory and expiratory phases achieved via manual breath holds. Gross tumor volume (GTV) and normal structures were delineated on inspiratory and expiratory image sets, and shifts were measured and analyzed. Smaller tumors had more motion in the dorsoventral direction compared to larger tumors (p = 0.047; rs = −0.44). The same observation was appreciated in the craniocaudal direction (p = 0.008; rs = −0.56). Dogs with multiple tumors had more motion in the dorsoventral direction and the craniocaudal direction compared to dogs with solitary tumors (p = 0.035; p = 0.022). Caudal tumors had significantly more 3D motion (p < 0.001) and craniocaudal motion (p < 0.001) compared to cranial tumors, whereas there was significantly more lateral motion (p < 0.001) between peripheral and central tumors, but no significant difference in 3D motion (p = 0.24). The variability of tumor motion between canine patients supports individualized assessments of each case to ensure best practice.

Introduction

Access to targeted radiation oncology services in veterinary medicine has expanded in recent decades [1]. This translates into more veterinary patients receiving advanced imaging and radiation planning of the chest cavity. Knowledge of thoracic tumor motion during respiration is critical for treatment planning and safe delivery of radiation. As more radiation oncologists are using stereotactic techniques to deliver high‐dose, highly conformal, hypofractionated radiation plans, it is important to accurately target tumors and avoid unplanned dose delivery to adjacent organs at risk (OARs), especially the sensitive intrathoracic structures [2, 3]. Motion during acquisition of computed tomography (CT) scans can also induce artifacts that reduce the ability to interpret mass/organ margins or size and thereby decrease the ability of radiologists to safely acquire CT‐guided aspirates and biopsies of tissues or to deliver intratumoral injections [4].

Radiation oncologists need to determine when respiratory management under anesthesia is required for canine patients. Breath holds are employed in human and veterinary medicine to manage respiratory motion, and multiple studies have been performed in dogs to determine the safety of breath holds [3, 5, 6, 7]. Other motion management strategies include neuromuscular blockade, respiratory gating, dynamic couch tracking, and jet ventilation [8, 9]. Performing breath holds for patients lengthens treatment time and requires experienced anesthesia staff; however, this procedure may be warranted for select cases. Breath holds can also be performed with basic anesthetic equipment, unlike other respiratory management techniques, making them a widely accessible motion management strategy.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the motion of canine thoracic tumors and a selection of normal thoracic structures in the lateral, dorsoventral, and craniocaudal directions (x, y, and z) between inspiratory and expiratory breath holds as a surrogate measurement of the breathing cycle. A secondary aim is to determine if the position of the tumor within the thorax (i.e., cranial vs. caudal and central vs. peripheral) significantly affects the degree and/or direction of tumor motion. The authors hypothesized that caudal tumors would exhibit greater motion than cranial tumors, and peripheral tumors would exhibit more lateral motion compared to central tumors. On the basis of the findings in this study, we hope to make recommendations for when to employ respiratory management during CT imaging and radiation therapy (RT).

Methods

A prospective pilot case series was performed. Dogs were considered for inclusion if they were client‐owned dogs presenting for a thoracic CT scan; there was a known or high suspicion of an intra‐ or extrathoracic mass, including mediastinal, pulmonary, pleural, cardiac, intramuscular, subcutaneous, or dermal locations; and the dog was otherwise hemodynamically stable at presentation. Patients with known, or evidence of, intracranial disease (i.e., brain tumor, epilepsy, and inflammatory disease) were excluded from recruitment. The aggressive ventilation applied to patients with intracranial disease to maintain a low‐normal PaCO [2], plus the application of positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP) for short periods, was considered potentially detrimental to intracranial pressure. Additional exclusion criteria included rib fractures or aggressive bone lesions that precluded positioning in sternal recumbency for the CT scan and patients with diffuse and/or serious pulmonary or pleural disease, such as known bulla, pneumothorax, or end‐stage, extensive pulmonary neoplastic/inflammatory processes that might preclude high inspiratory pressure breath holds. A dog could be removed from inclusion if any of these exclusion criteria were identified or if the patient became unstable during acquisition of the CT scan. The study allowed for the inclusion of up to 12 dogs as a pilot, or exploratory, project. No power analysis was performed, and the recruitment number was based on available funding for the project. The cohort was expanded to 14 dogs when initial recruitment included 2 dogs without tumors as seen on CT imaging. These two dogs without thoracic tumors were included for assessment of normal structure motion and excluded from tumor motion analysis. All inclusion and exclusion criteria were evaluated and approved by our institution's clinical review board prior to patient enrollment (CRB protocol number 2017–130). Owners offered both verbal consent of participation in this prospective study to a radiation oncology, medical oncology, or clinical trials clinician and signed a client consent form prior to their dog's enrollment. General demographic and patient information was gathered at presentation, including age, breed, weight, gender, tumor diagnosis if available, and lung volume as determined by contouring of the organ within the radiation treatment planning software.

All dogs were under general anesthesia with mechanical ventilation and positioned in sternal recumbency for the CT scan. The anesthesia and anesthesia monitoring protocols were based on the dog's individual needs. Anesthesia monitoring included continuous electrocardiogram (ECG), pulse oximetry (SpO2), capnography, oscillometric indirect blood pressure, temperature, peak inspiratory pressure (PIP), and end‐tidal inhalant concentration or propofol infusion rate. Physiologic values were maintained in an appropriate range but were not standardized among patients, with the exception of the pressure during breath holds for imaging. If a thoracic metastasis evaluation CT was ordered, the dog was positioned within a high‐sided trough, and only pre‐contrast images were acquired. If a dog was enrolled with a radiation‐planning setup, the dog was immobilized in a Styrofoam bead‐style cushion (Vaculock bag, Civco model MT‐VL‐37, Civco Systems, Orange City, IA, USA). The CT was performed using a Philips Gemini TF Big Bore 16‐slice scanner (Philips Gemini TF Big Bore 16‐slice scanner, Philips Medical Systems, the Nederlands, B.V.). Two non‐contrast, volumetric (helical) data sets were obtained through the thorax: first at inspiratory pulmonary pressures of 10–12 cm H2O and second at end‐expiratory pulmonary pressures of 0 cm H2O. Omnipaque 350 (GE Healthcare, Princeton, NJ, USA) contrast media was then injected intravenously (2 mL/kg) prior to the post‐contrast series. The acquisition slice thickness was 2 mm, and the field‐of‐view on the patients varied between 32 and 35 cm with a rotation time of 1 s and a pitch of 1. The kVp was consistently set at 120 with a current varying between 250 and 275 mA. The time between inspiratory and expiratory scans was, on average, 3 min. Two post‐contrast data sets were also obtained, the first obtained with appropriate timing following injection of contrast at inspiratory pulmonary pressures of 10–12 cm H2O, and the second at delayed timing for contrast enhancement at end‐expiratory pulmonary pressures of 0 cm H2O. Each breath–hold was maintained on average for 20–30 s and induced via post‐hyperventilation apnea. Images were reconstructed at 2.0 mm contiguous intervals with a 512 matrix, using the smooth algorithm (Philips iDose4, Suzhou Industrial Park, Jiangsu, China). Each dog was directly supervised during the CT by an attending boarded anesthesiologist (GG). Dogs were discharged to their owners following full recovery from anesthesia on the same day as the CT scan.

The CT images were then imported into the Varian Eclipse treatment planning system (version 13.1; Varian Medical Systems, Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Normal structures were contoured on the post‐contrast, expiratory CT scan for analysis. Normal structures included the carina, the 5th thoracic vertebra, the 12th thoracic vertebra, and the left 7th rib. The grossly evident tumor was delineated on both the inspiratory post‐contrast and expiratory post‐contrast CT scans and defined as the gross tumor volume (GTV). If a dog did not receive contrast as part of the CT protocol, the GTV was contoured on the inspiratory pre‐contrast and expiratory pre‐contrast image sets. All contouring was performed independently by a single radiation oncology resident (CKG). The GTV and normal structure volume assessments and geometric center of mass were determined by the treatment planning software. Using the difference between mass center positions from the inspiratory to expiratory image sets, the absolute motion differences in centimeters in the lateral (x), dorsoventral (y), and craniocaudal (z) directions of each GTV and normal structure were recorded. The net magnitude of motion in three dimensions (3D) was also calculated for each normal structure group and the GTVs with the Pythagorean theorem in 3D.

The minimum, maximum, mean, and median motion changes were recorded for the GTV volumes and collectively for each normal structure, as well as interquartile ranges. The dogs were stratified into groups for analysis by the same investigator who performed the contouring of the GTV volumes (CKG), and then stratification was confirmed by a second investigator (DL). The thorax was visually subdivided into thirds in the coronal, expiratory, post‐contrast CT image set. If a tumor, or the mass center of the GTV, was cranial to the carina as determined on a single slice at the bifurcation of the mainstem bronchi, it was categorized as “cranial,” whereas if it was caudal to the carina, it was categorized as “caudal.” If a tumor, or the mass center of the GTV, fell within the central 33% of the coronal thoracic diameter as determined on a single slice at the level of the carina, it was categorized as “central”; if the mass center of the tumor fell outside (to either side) of the central 33% of the thorax volume, it was categorized as “peripheral.” Dogs were also dichotomized into those with a single or multiple tumors for analysis.

The means with interquartile ranges were reported for normal continuous data, and the medians with interquartile ranges were reported for the non‐normal continuous data. The continuous data were evaluated for normal distribution with the Shapiro–Wilk test. If normality was not met, the data were converted into a log scale. The data were analyzed using a linear analysis comparing cranial to caudal and central to peripheral planes. A correlation analysis was performed when both variables were continuous. If the data did not meet normality, a nonparametric Spearman's Rho analysis was performed. A pvalue of 0.05 was used to evaluate statistical significance. An independent statistician (SR) performed all statistical analyses using SAS v 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Fourteen dogs met the inclusion criteria and were prospectively enrolled in this study. Two dogs were enrolled and did not have a thoracic mass on CT evaluation. Three dogs were enrolled with multiple thoracic masses. Median age at enrollment was 9 years (range, 4–14 years). Nine dogs were castrated males, two were sexually intact males, and three were spayed females. There were four dogs of mixed breed, two Labrador retrievers, two dogs of Golden Retriever‐Poodle lineage, two Chihuahuas, and one each of the following: Flat‐coated Retriever, Bouvier des Flanders, French Briard, and Gordon Setter. One Chihuahua and the Bouvier des Flanders did not have tumors. Median weight at enrollment was 29.0 kg (range, 2.4–39.9 kg). The two Chihuahuas each weighed 2.4 and 8.2 kg. The weight of the next smallest dog when the two Chihuahuas were excluded was 25.2 kg. The median weight when the Chihuahuas were excluded was 29.9 kg (range, 25.2–39.9 kg).

The total number of thoracic masses evaluated was 21. All dogs with a mass, except one, had either a cytological or histological diagnosis. Nineteen of the 21 masses were neoplastic, including bronchogenic carcinoma, grade II pulmonary carcinoma, papillary pulmonary carcinoma, metastatic pulmonary nodules, histiocytic sarcoma, melanosarcoma, ectopic thyroid carcinoma, and thymoma. The two nonneoplastic masses included a lipoma in a Golden Retriever‐Poodle and an infectious mass diagnosed as fibrinopurulent, ulcerative, caseating bronchiectasis in a Chihuahua. The one dog without a definitive diagnosis of his pulmonary masses had a histologically confirmed chondroblastic osteosarcoma of the left distal radius. The multiple, small pulmonary nodules seen on CT for this dog were highly suspicious for metastatic osteosarcoma but were not sampled.

Information on GTV volume and lung volume was available in all patients with mass lesions. If a mass was originating within the lung parenchyma, the lung contour excluded the GTV contour. Lung and GTV volumes were based on the expiratory, post‐contrast CT image contours. The mean and median lung volumes were 1800 and 1776 cm3 (IQR, 1496 and 2125 cm3), respectively. The mean and median GTV volumes were 84.5 and 3.64 cm3 (IQR, 0.17 to 159.07 cm3), respectively. Eight tumors were defined as cranial, and 13 were defined as caudal, whereas 10 tumors were central and 11 tumors were peripheral. Nine of the tumors were solitary, whereas 12 of the tumors were present in dogs with multiple tumors.

The collective GTV motion data are displayed in Table 1. The median motion differences in the x, y, and z directions for the GTV were 0.15 cm (IQR, 0.05–0.26 cm), 0.25 cm (IQR, 0.12–0.40 cm), and 0.98 cm (IQR, 0.58–1.8 cm), respectively (Figure 1). Additionally, the mean and median of the 3D magnitude of tumor motion were 1.32 and 1.12 cm (IQR 0.80–2.0 cm), respectively. Normal thoracic structures were also evaluated for motion. This was done to act as an internal control for different positions within the thorax, with the carina and vertebrae accounting for central locations, the 5th vertebra accounting for a cranial position, the 12th vertebra accounting for a caudal position, and the left 7th rib accounting for a peripheral position. The median, mean, and magnitude of 3D motion differences are recorded in Table 2. The most notable findings included the >6 mm median motion difference of the carina in the craniocaudal direction (Figure 2). Second, there is notable dorsoventral motion of the 12th thoracic vertebra with a mean motion value of >3 mm (Figure 3).

The 3D magnitude of GTV motion was also inversely correlated with GTV volume (p = 0.014, rs = −0.53). Discussion

There was clinically relevant thoracic tumor motion for this cohort of canine patients, particularly in the craniocaudal (z) direction (Figure 1). This impacts planning target volume (PTV) margins for RT. PTVs are expansions around GTVs or around clinically suspected microscopic disease (clinical target volume; CTV) and account for intra‐ and inter‐fractional patient setup differences. This includes accounting for the motion of internal organs during respiration. With the advent of highly conformal irradiation in the form of intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), tumors in the thoracic cavity are now accessible to definitive radiation protocols that were prohibitively toxic to adjacent normal tissues with more traditional, manually calculated plans or 3D‐conformal radiation therapy (3DCRT). These protocols may be hypofractionated with stereotactic immobilization, known as stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT). This approach prescribes 1–5, once‐daily treatments instead of 15–20, once‐daily treatments, as traditional radiation protocols utilize. This can be beneficial for fragile anesthetic patients and is much more attractive to veterinary clients logistically. However, these high‐dose‐per‐fraction protocols also require a high degree of targeting confidence, as the wide PTV margins traditionally used would increase toxicity to surrounding OARs [10].

Although median motion differences of the GTVs were equal to or less than 3 mm in the lateral or dorsoventral directions, the median motion differences between inspiratory and expiratory breath holds in the craniocaudal direction were 0.98 cm. The PTVs employed at this institution for thoracic SBRT have historically been symmetrical and 2–5 mm, with most plans having PTV margins of 3 mm. Therefore, any tumor motion of >3 mm in any one direction should have respiratory management for radiation delivery, or one should consider expanded and/or anisotropic rather than isotropic PTV margins. Data from human radiation oncology support the use of respiratory management techniques if the 3D movement of the GTV is ≥5 mm between inspiratory and expiratory phases, as this reduces the risk of geographic miss as well as excessive lung irradiation [11]. Several important points should be addressed when extrapolating recommendations from human literature to veterinary patients. First, the magnitude of 3D motion of these canine thoracic tumors was on average >1 cm between breath holds, even using conservative inspiratory pressures (10–12 cm H2O). Second, canine patients are almost always smaller than humans, arguing for a smaller threshold of motion before employing respiratory management, or risk overexposure of OARs to excessive radiation dose. Third, the GTV motion for individual dogs was highly variable, with some patients having motion approaching 1 cm in the lateral or dorsoventral directions. The sizes of the patients varied widely, likely contributing to the differences in the magnitude of tumor motion. This is a consideration more relevant in veterinary medicine, with its wide diversity of canine body shapes and sizes, compared to human medicine, where body shape and size are much more uniform. All of these factors argue for more individualized evaluation of canine thoracic tumor motion on CT scan and do not lend themselves to making blanket recommendations for PTV expansions for radiation delivery.

Normal thoracic structures were also evaluated for motion as surrogates for different positions within the thorax (Table 2). These measurements help clarify the differences between how dogs breathe and how humans breathe. During normal respiration, the human thorax and abdomen rise and fall in a predictable movement in the supine position, and tracking devices for respiratory gating are designed with this in mind [11, 12]. The conformation of the dog thorax (with the exception of barrel‐chested breeds like bulldogs) is much narrower compared to humans. The measurements acquired in this study show more chest wall movement in the mediolateral direction for dogs, so human respiratory‐gating approaches are not so easily transferable.

Interestingly, the internal organ motion is quite similar between humans and dogs despite the observed differences in external chest wall movement, in that the relative greatest movement of tumor targets is in the craniocaudal, or superior–inferior (SI), direction in both species [4, 9, 13, 14, 15, 16]. In Yin et al., it was observed that dogs placed in a supine position under general anesthesia, with the liver marked by surgical clamps, saw the most movement in the SI direction; secondarily, they reported a reduction of movement in the SI direction by 83% by using jet ventilation compared to standard mechanical ventilation [9]. Oliveira et al. also noted that there was moderate variability with abdominal organ movement between dog subjects, but overall dorsal recumbency (supine) was superior for organ position reproducibility, with the exception of the liver and bladder, which were more reproducible in ventral (prone) recumbency [4]. Pediatric radiotherapy is likely more relatable to veterinary radiotherapy since a higher percentage of pediatric patients are anesthetized and have a smaller body size. Meijer et al., which evaluated respiratory motion in children, showed that the largest intrafractional difference in cranial abdominal organ motion was in the craniocaudal direction [13].

Caudal tumors move significantly more in the craniocaudal direction when compared to cranial tumors (Figure 4). Shen et al. reported that pulmonary tumors in the human thoracic cavity move more in the caudal lung lobes compared to the cranial lung lobes [14]. In both species, organ or tumor proximity to the diaphragm is correlated with more movement. Peripheral tumors did move significantly more in the lateral direction when compared to central tumors in our study (Figure 5); however, there was no significant difference in the magnitude of 3D motion between peripheral and central tumors (p = 0.24). In our study population, there were three tumors that were considered “borderline” (<0.6 cm from the cut‐off) between the central and peripheral categories, potentially diminishing a significant difference between the two locations. As previously stated, these findings should not preclude a more individualized approach to determining respiratory motion management. However, if a CT scan of a peripheral or caudal thoracic tumor is acquired without respiratory management, asymmetrical, increased PTV margins in the lateral and craniocaudal directions, respectively, should be considered. Asymmetrical PTV expansions have been described for head and neck lymph node targets in dogs after documenting predictable inter‐fractional target motion [17]. If sensitive OARs (i.e., heart, esophagus, and trachea) are adjacent to target structures, breath holds for respiratory management instead of increasing PTV margins are strongly recommended to minimize the risk of organ toxicity. More research into this question of thoracic tumor motion with a larger cohort is needed to validate the findings within this pilot study.

The tumors with smaller GTV volumes moved more between respiratory phases compared to larger tumors. When considering that the lung tissue expands and contracts uniformly, and these pulmonary tumors sit within this otherwise homogeneous tissue (save for some bronchi and blood vessels), the movement relative to the size of the smaller tumors would be greater than that of the larger ones. It was also found that dogs with multiple tumors had more tumor motion than those with solitary tumors. Whether this is due to the inherent nature that multiple tumors are often smaller overall, thus more movement relative to their small size, or due to a structural change within the lung tissue that causes more movement, is open for further investigation.

Although preparations were made to perform neuromuscular blockade to control respiration, each patient was sufficiently apneic for each CT scan by hyperventilating the dog prior to each breath‐hold. Therefore, neuroparalytic drugs were never employed to acquire the image sets. Neuromuscular blockade should be considered when delivering RT to patients with thoracic tumors if employing breath holds for respiratory management. The duration of radiation treatment, especially for intensity‐modulated plans, is longer than a typical CT acquisition, so patients are much more likely to start spontaneously breathing in the middle of treatment delivery. This increases the risk of excessive dose to OARs and diminished dose to the tumor volumes if PTV expansions were not generated when accounting for free breathing.

It was interesting to note that smaller dogs presented with smaller tumors, whereas older dogs presented with larger tumors on analysis. We postulated that smaller dogs would have smaller absolute tumor size at presentation because symptoms would likely be more obvious. A 3 cm chest mass would be much more troublesome to a Chihuahua compared to a Golden Retriever with the same size tumor. When considering older patients, owners may be more likely to write off changes in energy or appetite to “getting older” or “slowing down,” and thereby present for veterinary care only when symptoms were more severe, and tumors were larger.

There are limitations to this study. First, inspiratory breath holds were not performed at the reported maximum safe inspiratory pressure (20 cm H2O) [5]. Therefore, the motion differences measured in this study may be a conservative estimate of free‐breathing motion. That being said, most patients do not attempt inspiratory breaths at maximal tidal volume at rest, awake, or when anesthetized. In addition, the tissue around pulmonary tumors can be fragile, and application of high PIPs can predispose patients to bullae expansion and pneumothorax. It is important to note that many PEEP valves are only able to generate 10 cm H2O, and so this value increases the generalizability of the findings into more practices. Second, since each structure was manually contoured, there may be differences between inspiratory and expiratory tumor volumes that translate as erroneous changes to the geometric mass center of the GTV. Contouring of the GTV and the normal structures was performed by a single investigator (CKG) to minimize this bias; however, differences in contrast enhancement and pulmonary atelectasis that changed between image sets could also contribute to differences in structure margins. There was no intra‐observer reliability assessment for this investigator. The same investigator who performed the contouring of the GTV volumes stratified the patients into the central–peripheral and cranial–caudal categories, which was then verified by a second investigator (DL). Because neither author was blinded to the motion measurements, this introduces potential bias into the conclusion presented here. Third, there was no control group of patients enrolled to assess the motion of normal structures when a tumor was not present. Two patients enrolled in this study did not have a tumor; however, this number was too low to perform meaningful analysis between groups. Finally, the low number of study participants, the disparate body sizes, heterogeneity of tumor types, and differences in body conformations are significant limitations. Only 14 patients were recruited to this study over the course of several months. There was no power analysis performed. The question of quantifying thoracic tumor or organ motion during the breathing cycle of canine patients is novel, with no other studies available from which to draw assumptions necessary for a power analysis. Two veterinary publications have investigated respiratory motion in dogs: one case study evaluating abdominal organ motion during respiration [4] and a second case series evaluating real‐time, 3D motion tracking of the external thoracic wall in eight dogs [16]. Documenting respiratory motion of internal canine thoracic organs or tumors has yet to be described. There may be differences in motion between benign and malignant tumors, and this analysis was not performed due to the low number of benign masses (only two nonneoplastic masses). A larger cohort of dogs should be considered for any future investigations, and the significance of these study findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the exploratory nature.

Respiratory management for RT has been extensively evaluated and documented for humans [8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17, 18, 19], but this research is lacking for veterinary species [4, 15, 16]. Our study documents that the motion of thoracic tumors during the breathing cycle can vary widely during respiration and between dogs, anywhere from 1 to 2 mm up to almost 3 cm. Several normal structures move more during respiration than might have been previously thought (i.e., carina, 12th thoracic vertebra). Caudal tumors have a predisposition for increased motion compared to cranial tumors in the craniocaudal (z) direction, and peripheral tumors are predisposed to increased motion compared to central tumors in the lateral (x) direction. It should also be noted that the respiratory cycle is not continuous, but a more significant portion of it is spent in expiration than inspiration, further complicating the clinician's ability to predict the proximity of OARs to the GTV and dose delivery to the tumor volumes. The wide variability of motion between patients suggests that respiratory management with inspiratory and expiratory breath holds during acquisition of a CT scan is warranted, regardless of where a tumor is located within or on the thorax. These authors propose using expiratory breath holds for respiratory management during RT for any tumor motion >3 mm, as well as individualized PTV expansions for thoracic tumors, ultimately resulting in better tumor targeting and minimal radiation exposure to normal tissues.
