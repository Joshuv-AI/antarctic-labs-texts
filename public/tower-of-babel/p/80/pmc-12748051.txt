Prevalence and Duration of Potential Drug–Drug Interactions Among US Nursing Home Residents, 2018–2020
Authors: Laura A Reich, Lori A Daiello, Sarah D Berry, Adam M D'Amico, Douglas P Kiel, Daniel A Harris, Kaleen N Hayes, Charles E Leonard, Yu‐Chia (Sam) Hsu, Andrew R Zullo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNursing home (NH) residents are at increased risk of drug–drug interactions (DDIs) due to multimorbidity and polypharmacy. While prior research suggests that many DDIs lead to adverse drug events in older adults, the extent of exposure to potentially clinically relevant DDIs among United States (US) NH residents is largely unknown.MethodsIn this cohort study, we calculated the prevalence and duration of exposure to 98 potential DDIs among US NH residents from 2018 to 2020. DDIs were sourced from three expert consensus publications, some of which defined similar interactions and overlapping drug combinations, allowing comparisons within and across lists. Data were drawn from Medicare claims linked to Minimum Data Set 3.0 clinical assessments. Eligible residents included Medicare Fee‐for‐Service beneficiaries aged ≥ 66 years living in NHs with observable Part D prescription drug data. DDI exposure was defined as ≥ 1 day of concurrent use of orally administered medications. Prevalence was calculated as the proportion of residents exposed to each DDI; duration was measured as the median number of days residents concurrently used the medications of interest.ResultsAmong 485,251 NH residents, 61.6% experienced ≥ 1 potential DDI over 272,780 person‐years. The 12 most prevalent DDIs involved central nervous system (CNS)‐active drugs, anticholinergics, antihypertensives, opioids, and diuretics. Of these DDIs, concurrent use of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and heart rate‐reducing drugs had the longest median exposure duration (81 days; Q1–Q3, 24–235). The most prevalent DDI, concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs, was observed in 27.1% (95% CLs, 27.0%, 27.2%) of residents.ConclusionsNearly two‐thirds of NH residents were exposed to medication combinations linked to potential DDIs, although the prevalence and duration of exposure associated with individual DDIs varied. Future research should determine which DDIs are most clinically significant and investigate barriers to reducing exposure duration in this high‐risk population.Keywords: drug–drug interactions, nursing home, older adults, polypharmacy

Introduction

Polypharmacy is common among nursing home (NH) residents, who often take multiple medications to manage their health conditions [1]. Compared to community‐dwelling older adults, NH residents have greater functional and cognitive impairment, making them particularly vulnerable to drug–drug interactions (DDIs) and adverse drug events [2, 3, 4, 5]. A DDI occurs when the use of one drug alters the effects of another drug [6], which can subsequently cause clinically significant adverse drug events such as fall‐related injuries, worsening cognitive impairment, emergency hospitalizations, and mortality [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Several studies have evaluated the prevalence of potential DDIs among NH residents [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17], with estimates ranging from 4.4% to 88.4%. Variation in DDI prevalence likely stems, in part, from differences in geographic setting, methods of DDI identification, and study design. Most prior studies were conducted outside of the United States (US) in a select number of NHs, where patterns of potentially inappropriate medication use, care needs, and long‐term care settings differ [18, 19]. Additionally, studies have used various methods to identify DDIs, including drug information databases (e.g., Micromedex) [20]. However, major drug databases often disagree on which drugs interact and the severity of these interactions [21, 22, 23, 24]. As an alternative approach, expert consensus DDI lists have been developed to support clinical decision‐making and generate comparable prevalence estimates across studies [25, 26, 27]. These consensus lists prioritize potentially clinically relevant DDIs, given that the severity of DDIs varies and only some lead to adverse drug events [2, 28]. As a final consideration, the cross‐sectional design of many studies limits their ability to assess the length of time individuals are exposed to DDIs. Since some adverse drug reactions are related to cumulative dosage and duration of medication use, prolonged exposure to DDIs may further elevate the risk of adverse drug events [3].

This study aimed to estimate the prevalence and duration of exposure to potentially clinically relevant DDIs among over 400,000 US NH residents using lists from three expert consensus publications. Assessing the duration of exposure to DDIs provides a more clinically relevant view of drug usage patterns and can better inform prescribing decisions, such as dose adjustments or deprescribing, to reduce residents' extended exposure to harmful DDIs.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

This retrospective cohort study analyzed US NH residents using linked Minimum Data Set (MDS) 3.0 clinical assessments and Medicare data from 2017 to 2020; 2017 data captured prevalent medication use and resident characteristics. Medicare data included Fee‐for‐Service (FFS) enrollment and demographic information from the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF), Medicare Provider Analysis and Review (MedPAR) hospital and skilled nursing facility claims, and Part D prescription drug claims. Background on MDS and Medicare is publicly available [29, 30, 31]. The Brown University Institutional Review Board approved the study and waived informed consent due to the use of deidentified secondary data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines (Table S1) [32].

Study Population and NH Stays

Individuals admitted to a NH and enrolled in Medicare FFS Parts A, B, and D at any point from 2018 to 2020 were eligible for the study. Using a recently developed method, we identified continuous periods when individuals resided in a NH and had observable Part D prescription drug data [33]. These periods, linked to MDS admission assessments, are hereafter referred to as NH stays. Residents could contribute multiple NH stays to the study. Stays began on NH entry or re‐entry when individuals were not covered by the Part A post‐acute care or hospitalization benefit. Stays ended at the first occurrence of: disenrollment from Medicare FFS Parts A, B, or D; enrollment in Medicare Advantage; NH discharge; Part A post‐acute care or hospitalization; death; or study end (December 31, 2020).

The cohort was restricted to residents eligible for Medicare based on age at enrollment. NH stays were included if the resident was aged ≥ 66 years at the start of the stay and had ≥ 12 months of continuous enrollment in Medicare FFS Parts A, B, and D, without enrollment in Medicare Advantage prior to the stay.

Identification of Potential DDIs

We included 98 potentially clinically relevant DDIs from three expert consensus publications: Anrys et al. [25], the 2023 American Geriatrics Society (AGS) Beers Criteria [26], and Capiau et al. [27] These sources were selected to enable comparison across lists and provide a more comprehensive assessment of potential DDIs. Anrys et al. [25] and the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26] identified DDIs relevant to older adults, whereas Capiau et al. [27] focused on interactions involving direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), which are increasingly prescribed in US NHs [34]. Each list was developed using structured expert consensus methods to ensure clinical relevance.

Some DDIs across the lists were defined similarly and included overlapping drug combinations. For example, “concomitant use of ≥ 3 centrally acting drugs” (Anrys et al. [25]) and “any combination of ≥ 3 central nervous system (CNS)‐active drugs” (2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26]) came from distinct sources and are therefore listed separately. The former includes opioids, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, Z‐drugs, barbiturates, antiepileptics, and all antidepressants, while the latter includes a similar set but specifies tricyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and adds skeletal muscle relaxants. For consistency, we hereafter refer to “concomitant use of ≥ 3 centrally acting drugs” (Anrys et al. [25]) as “concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs.”

Resources from the consensus lists, including a DDI detection algorithm by Anrys et al. [25], were used to identify the generic drugs (i.e., active ingredients) for each DDI. Three clinicians reviewed these generic drugs in triplicate and added medications based on pharmacology references and US clinical guidelines. Because Capiau et al. [27] restricted drugs to those on the Belgian market, we added therapeutic equivalent medications available in the US. For DDIs involving anticholinergic medications, we included medications classified as receiving a score ≥ 2 (moderate to strong anticholinergic activity) on the anticholinergic burden calculator [35].

We restricted the analysis to orally administered drugs that were available in the US and observed in the Part D prescription claims for ≥ 1 cohort member. Our final list of DDIs, including the generic drug names, is available on GitHub [36].

Medication Use Episodes and DDI Exposure

We constructed continuous periods of medication use by adding the days' supply to dispensing dates in the Part D prescription claims. Because claims do not distinguish between scheduled and as needed (pro re nata) medications, all dispensings were treated the same. To capture prevalent medication dispensings that may have been supplied prior to and overlapped with January 1, 2018, we included a 3‐month lookback. Drugs listed under multiple names in the Part D data (e.g., verapamil and verapamil hydrochloride) were grouped together when forming use episodes. Overlapping days' supply for the same drug were treated as a single day of use, and episodes extending beyond December 31, 2020 were truncated at the study end date.

We subsequently identified days of medication use overlap (i.e., concurrent use) for drugs associated with each DDI during residents' NH stays. When identifying exposure to DDIs involving multiple classes (e.g., concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs), we ignored overlap between drugs within the same class (e.g., two antipsychotics) to account for potential drug switching and because therapeutic duplication was not our focus. A resident was considered exposed to a DDI if they had ≥ 1 day of concurrent use for the drugs of interest during their NH stays (Figure 1A).

Resident Characteristics

We identified demographic and clinical characteristics for NH residents. For individuals with multiple NH stays, we randomly selected one stay to capture characteristics. Demographic information, including age at NH entry, sex, and race/ethnicity (based on the Research Triangle Institute race code [37]), was obtained from the MBSF. We also calculated the duration, in days, of the NH stay. Clinical characteristics included multimorbidity, cognitive function, diagnoses of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), and medication use. Multimorbidity was assessed using the Gagne Combined Comorbidity Score (range: −2 to 26, where higher scores indicate greater multimorbidity), based on MedPAR hospital claims and MDS data from the 12 months preceding the NH stay [38, 39]. Cognitive function was assessed using the most recent Cognitive Function Scale score recorded in the MDS prior to the selected stay [40]. We identified diagnoses of ADRD and CKD prior to the NH stay using the first occurrence dates from the 27 Chronic Conditions Warehouse (CCW) algorithms [41], enabling us to ascertain diagnoses dating back to 1999. To quantify medication use, we measured the number of unique orally administered active ingredients dispensed during the NH stay, as determined by Part D prescription claims.

Statistical Analysis

We computed descriptive statistics for demographic and clinical characteristics in the total analytic sample and stratified by exposure to ≥ 1 DDI from any of the consensus lists. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) for symmetric distributions and median (first quartile [Q1], third quartile [Q3]) for skewed distributions. If a variable was missing, it was reported as missing in the analysis.

To assess the prevalence of the 98 DDIs, we calculated the proportion of residents exposed to each DDI during their NH stays, dividing the number of exposed residents by the total cohort size. We used the Clopper–Pearson exact method to compute 95% confidence limits (CLs). To evaluate the duration of DDI exposure, we determined the median number of days residents were exposed to a DDI, restricting it to those with ≥ 1 day of concurrent medication use for the given DDI. Days of DDI exposure were not required to be consecutive.

We conducted a stability analysis to assess whether DDI prevalence and duration differ when one drug is discontinued before its supply run‐out date. For drug pairs where one medication started before the other during a NH stay, we truncated the earlier drug's episode halfway through its last dispensing's days' supply (Figure 1B).

Additionally, although some consensus publications define DDIs at the medication class level, DDIs can be compared more concretely when reported at the individual drug level. Therefore, we calculated the prevalence and duration of exposure for the 50 most common individual drug combinations within potential DDIs involving ≥ 3 medications: “concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs” (Anrys et al. [25]) and “any combination of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs” (2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26]).

All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Study Population

The study included 485,251 NH residents who contributed 709,330 NH stays and 272,780 person‐years of follow‐up (Figure 2). These stays were associated with 15,678 unique NH facilities. On average, each resident had 1.5 NH stays (SD, 0.90), with 145,756 (30.0%) contributing multiple stays. Of these residents, 186,543 (38.4%) were not exposed to a DDI, while 298,708 (61.6%) had ≥ 1 DDI. The mean age of residents was 84.7 years (SD, 8.1); 325,845 (67.2%) were female, and 408,786 (84.2%) were White. Compared to residents not exposed to a DDI, individuals exposed to ≥ 1 DDI were more commonly female (70.0% vs. 62.6%), White (86.3% vs. 80.9%), and diagnosed with ADRD (73.4% vs. 69.1%) or CKD (70.2% vs. 66.6%). They also had a lower prevalence of severe cognitive impairment, as measured by the Cognitive Function Scale (5.4% vs. 7.2%), more dispensings of unique orally administered active ingredients (mean, 8.7 [SD, 5.4] vs. 3.3 [SD, 3.4]), and longer NH stays (median: 79 days [Q1, 22; Q3, 247] vs. 29 days [Q1, 9; Q3, 119]). Variation in Prevalence of DDI Exposure

From 2018 to 2020, 58.5% of residents experienced ≥ 1 DDI from Anrys et al. [25], 32.0% experienced ≥ 1 DDI from the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26], and 9.1% experienced ≥ 1 DDI from Capiau et al. [27] Among residents with ≥ 2 NH stays, 11.4% were exposed to the same potential DDIs across all stays.

As shown in Table 2, the five most prevalent DDIs were “concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs” (27.1% [95% CLs, 27.0%, 27.2%]), “any combination of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs” (19.8% [95% CLs, 19.7%, 19.9%]), “SSRI + another serotonergic drug” (15.0% [95% CLs, 14.9%, 15.1%]), “SSRI + loop or thiazide diuretic” (14.8% [95% CLs, 14.7%, 14.9%]), and “concomitant prescription of ≥ 2 anticholinergic drugs” (11.0% [95% CLs, 10.9%, 11.1%]).

Duration of DDI Exposure

The duration of DDI exposure varied across the 98 potential DDIs. For DDIs with ≥ 1 exposed resident, the median duration of concurrent use ranged from 4 to 112 days (Figure 3). Among the 12 most prevalent DDIs, the longest median exposure days were observed for “acetylcholinesterase inhibitor + a drug that reduces heart rate” (81 [Q1, 24; Q3, 235]), “SSRI + loop or thiazide diuretic” (63 [Q1, 18; Q3, 195]), “angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) or a potassium‐sparing diuretic + a potassium supplement” (48 [Q1, 14; Q3, 166]), “SSRI + another serotonergic drug” (45 [Q1, 15; Q3, 149]), and “concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs” (37 [Q1, 11; Q3, 140]) (Table 2).

Stability Analysis

When medication use was truncated at discontinuation, prevalence estimates were slightly attenuated for most DDIs. Among DDIs with any concurrent use, the median relative decrease in prevalence was 5.2% (Q1, 2.7%; Q3, 9.8%). The largest relative change was observed for “anticoagulants (i.e., warfarin) + DOACs,” which decreased in prevalence by 34.3% (from 0.7% to 0.5%). The median change in exposure duration was 2 days (Q1, 1; Q3, 5), corresponding to a median absolute relative change of 8.3% (Q1, 3.2%; Q3, 12.1%). Complete data for all DDIs are available in Tables S2–S4.

CNS‐Active Drugs at the Individual Medication Level

We calculated the prevalence and duration of exposure for the 50 most common individual drug combinations within DDIs involving ≥ 3 medications: “concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs” (Anrys et al. [25]) and “any combination of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs” (2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26]). Among the 131,580 residents exposed to “concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs,” the most common individual drug combinations were “lorazepam + mirtazapine + morphine” (2.4%; median exposure duration, 3 days [Q1, 2; Q3, 6]), “gabapentin + tramadol + trazodone” (1.8%; median exposure duration, 19 days [Q1, 8; Q3, 55]), and “gabapentin + sertraline + tramadol” (1.8%; median exposure duration, 20 days [Q1, 8; Q3, 61]). Of the 96,236 residents exposed to “any combination of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs,” the most common individual drug combinations were “gabapentin + sertraline + tramadol” (2.5%; median exposure duration, 20 days [Q1, 8; Q3, 61]), “lorazepam + morphine + sertraline” (2.4%; median exposure duration, 4 days [Q1, 2; Q3, 6]), and “lorazepam + quetiapine + sertraline” (2.4%; median exposure duration, 16 days [Q1, 7; Q3, 52]). Gabapentin and lorazepam appeared most frequently in both CNS‐active individual drug combination lists. See Tables S5 and S6 for more details.

Discussion

We found that nearly two‐thirds of US NH residents studied were exposed to one or more potential DDIs between 2018 and 2020. The 12 most prevalent DDIs included medication classes that are frequently prescribed in NHs, such as CNS‐active medications, anticholinergics, antihypertensives, opioids, and diuretics. These common DDIs are associated with several potential adverse clinical outcomes that increase the risk of hospitalizations and mortality, such as falls, cognitive impairment, cardiovascular events, sedation‐related adverse events, and electrolyte disturbances [3, 10, 11, 25, 26, 27]. Furthermore, the duration of exposure to individual DDIs varied, with the most prevalent DDIs not always corresponding to the longest exposure durations. Residents exposed to potential DDIs also had longer NH stays, which may have increased their opportunities for concurrent drug use. Our findings highlight the need to address barriers to reducing the duration of exposure to potential DDIs, especially among NH residents, who are particularly vulnerable to adverse drug events [3, 4, 5].

Prevalence estimates varied across the expert consensus lists. More than half of US NH residents were exposed to DDIs from Anrys et al. [25], whereas less than one‐third were exposed to DDIs from the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26], and fewer than 10% were exposed to DDIs from Capiau et al. [27] This finding aligns with prior literature, where prevalence estimates vary based on the DDI identification method [20, 42]. Indeed, the three expert consensus publications differed in their approaches to generating lists of potential DDIs. Anrys et al. [25] compiled DDI lists from existing literature, and DDIs considered at least moderately severe were included in the final publication. In contrast, the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26] provided an updated list of potentially inappropriate medications for older adults in the US. Although the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26] DDIs were supported by moderate to high‐quality evidence, they were not intended to be comprehensive; medications dispensed to fewer than 4000 Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 or older in 2020 were excluded. Capiau et al. [27] developed a list of DDIs with DOACs for community dwellers in Belgium. The differing approaches and lack of agreement between consensus lists may cause confusion and lead clinicians to overlook potentially clinically relevant DDIs during prescribing decisions [43, 44]. More comprehensive DDI resources for older adults, like those of Anrys et al. [25], may be useful in NH settings because they synthesize multiple DDI lists while maintaining clinical relevance, provided they are regularly updated to include new medications, align with evolving prescribing practices, and incorporate emerging drug safety evidence.

The prevalence of DDIs in our study from Anrys et al. [25] aligns with several baseline estimates from the COME‐ON trial, conducted in Belgian NHs [17]. The percentages of residents with at least one DDI were similar (58.5% vs. 52.7%), and “concomitant use of ≥ 3 CNS‐active drugs,” “SSRI + another serotonergic drug,” and “SSRI + loop or thiazide diuretic” were among the five most prevalent DDIs in both studies. The prevalence of Anrys et al. [25] DDIs involving psychotropic medications (i.e., antipsychotics, antidepressants, anxiolytics, and sedative‐hypnotics) was generally higher in the present study and COME‐ON trial than in studies among older hospitalized adults [45, 46]. This finding is consistent with the high prevalence of psychotropic polypharmacy among NH residents, particularly those with ADRD [47]. Psychotropic medications were also implicated in several of the most prevalent DDIs from the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [26] and Capiau et al. [27] Among the Capiau et al. [27] DDIs, “SSRIs or SNRIs + DOACs” had the highest prevalence and longest duration of exposure. Given the association of psychotropic DDIs with severe adverse drug events [48], careful monitoring and management of these medications in NHs is essential.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the duration of exposure to potential DDIs among NH residents. By examining exposure periods, we can assess drug usage patterns and evaluate the real‐world risk of DDIs in older, multimorbid adults. For example, among residents who concurrently used at least three CNS‐active drugs, combinations of lorazepam and morphine were common but had a short median duration of use (e.g., 3–4 days). Since lorazepam and morphine are often prescribed during end‐of‐life care [49], they may be intentionally used in combination for short periods to manage residents' symptoms. Future research is needed to understand the impact of DDI exposure duration on adverse drug events.

Our estimates were generally consistent after assuming the earlier‐starting drug was discontinued before its supply run‐out date. This suggests that residents are still experiencing prolonged exposure to potential DDIs, even if medication discontinuation is occurring. However, a few interactions showed more substantial changes. For instance, “anticoagulants (i.e., warfarin) + DOACs” had a 34.3% decrease in prevalence, which may reflect medication substitution in cases where these drugs are prescribed for the same indication. Nevertheless, most interactions showed little change, underscoring that many DDIs persist under conservative assumptions about medication discontinuation.

This study has several limitations. First, we identified prevalent exposure to potential DDIs, recognizing that there is limited evidence on which interactions result in clinically significant outcomes. Some potential DDIs may be intentional when the benefits of the medications outweigh the theoretical risks, and providers may manage these risks through dose adjustments or careful monitoring. We did not assess incident prescribing, dosing schedules, or dose changes, which could influence whether concurrent use results in adverse outcomes. Second, due to the nature of prescription claims data, we could not observe medication administrations and may have misclassified concurrent use in some cases, particularly for medications taken as needed. Furthermore, some drugs included in the expert consensus lists are available without a prescription in the US and are therefore not captured in Medicare Part D. This likely leads to an underestimation of the prevalence of certain DDIs, such as those involving aspirin and over‐the‐counter NSAIDs. Third, these results may not generalize to US NH residents not enrolled in Medicare FFS or lacking observable Part D data. Fourth, both prescribing practices and the patient population in US NHs may have changed since 2018–2020. Even so, our analysis includes more recent data than many prior DDI prevalence studies in NH populations [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Finally, several DDIs in the study include medications that are infrequently prescribed (e.g., digoxin), limiting the utility of some of our findings in modern routine practice [50].

Conclusions

Our study provides a comprehensive assessment of the prevalence and duration of exposure to potentially clinically relevant DDIs among a subset of US NH residents. Nearly two‐thirds of residents studied were exposed to at least one DDI between 2018 and 2020, although residents' duration of exposure to individual DDIs varied. Future work should aim to determine which DDIs are the most clinically significant and investigate barriers to reducing exposure duration, including patterns of incident prescribing and the effectiveness of drug monitoring in NHs.
