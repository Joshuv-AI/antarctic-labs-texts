Genome‐Wide Association Study of Cognitive Function in Population‐Based Cohorts in Japan: The Tohoku Medical Megabank Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging Study
Authors: Genki Shinoda, Taku Obara, Ippei Takahashi, Hisashi Ohseto, Chikana Kawaguchi, Akira Narita, Aoi Noda, Keiko Murakami, Masatsugu Orui, Mami Ishikuro, Makiko Taira, Hiroshi Sakamoto, Naoki Nakaya, Atsushi Hozawa, Nobuo Fuse, Gen Tamiya, Shunji Mugikura, Kyoko Suzuki, Shinichi Kuriyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHeritability of cognitive function is estimated to be 50%–80%. Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have identified multiple cognitive function‐associated loci, primarily in the European population. However, those in Asian populations, particularly in individuals of Japanese ancestry, remain limited. Hence, this GWAS aimed to identify cognitive function‐associated genetic loci in elderly individuals of Japanese ancestry. Herein, 2571 elderly participants from the Tohoku Medical Megabank Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging Study were included. Their cognitive function was assessed using the Japanese version of the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), and both binary and continuous MMSE scores were analysed. Genotyping was performed using the Affymetrix Axiom Japonica Array v2, and imputation was conducted with 3.5KJPNv2 and 1KGP3. Statistical analyses were performed using FastGWA‐GLMM and FastGWA for binary and continuous MMSE scores, respectively. Although no genome‐wide significant loci were identified using binary MMSE values, the following two were detected using continuous MMSE values: rs77877360 (20p12.1) near BANF2 and SNX5 and rs9460729 (6p22.3) near PRL and HDGFL1. Additionally, functional annotation suggested the involvement of these loci in pathways related to cognitive function, including chromatin structure regulation, neuroinflammation, and iron metabolism. Notably, SNX5, identified through chromatin‐interaction mapping, has been implicated in neurodegenerative processes, particularly in Parkinson's disease. The findings of this study provide preliminary genome‐wide evidence suggesting a genetic predisposition to impaired cognitive function in elderly Japanese individuals.Keywords: allele frequency, cognitive function, GWAS, Japanese ancestry, linkage disequilibrium

Introduction

Cognitive function refers to a series of cerebral activities, such as attention, memory, language, reasoning, and executive functioning, which are important determinants of quality of life [1, 2]. Reportedly, cognitive function can be affected by various factors. For instance, cognitive reserve, which refers to the brain's ability to compensate for age‐related or pathological decline, is crucial for maintaining cognitive function in the elderly [3, 4]. Additionally, cognitive function is affected by genetic factors, and a meta‐analysis of twin studies estimated that the heritability of cognitive function is approximately 50%–80% [5]. This substantial heritability underscores the importance of further studies, including identifying genetic contributors, to elucidate the genetic basis of cognitive function and mechanisms underlying cognitive reserve and resilience against brain ageing and diseases.

Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) are useful for identifying genomic loci associated with specific diseases or other complex traits [6]. Twenty‐two GWASs on cognitive function have been performed for individuals of European ancestry [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. For example, in 2011, the earliest GWAS on cognitive function was conducted in 3511 individuals [15]. No significant genome‐wide loci were detected [15]. Subsequently, the first results attaining genome‐wide significance, in three loci, were identified in a GWAS of cognitive function in 53,949 individuals reported by the Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology (CHARGE) consortium [7]. The largest GWAS to date was recently conducted on 300,486 individuals from the Cognitive Genomics Consortium (COGENT), CHARGE and UK Biobank, and 148 significant loci associated with cognitive function were identified [8].

However, only four GWASs on cognitive function in individuals of Asian ancestry have been reported [16, 17, 18, 19]. Additionally, to the best of our knowledge, no GWASs on cognitive function in individuals of Japanese ancestry have been conducted. Since the genetic backgrounds of Asian ancestry, including those of Japanese ancestry, such as related to allele frequency and linkage disequilibrium (LD), differ from those of European ancestry [20], analysing the genomes of Asian ancestry is important. For example, in the case of Alzheimer's disease (AD), in which cognitive impairment is the primary symptom, a novel AD‐associated locus specific to Japanese ancestry has been identified [21]. Therefore, it is possible that loci associated with cognitive functions specific to Japanese ancestry can be identified.

In the present study, we conducted GWASs of cognitive function in elderly participants of the Tohoku Medical Megabank Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (TMM Brain MRI) Study to explore genetic loci associated with cognitive function in Japanese ancestry.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Participants aged 65 years and over were recruited from the TMM Brain MRI Study, a community‐based cohort study aimed at promoting advances in personalised healthcare and neurology [22]. Participants in the TMM Brain MRI Study were recruited among those who had previously participated in the Tohoku Medical Megabank Project Birth and Three‐Generation (TMM BirThree) Cohort Study [23] and the Tohoku Medical Megabank Project Community‐Based (TMM CommCohort) Cohort Study [24]. The TMM CommCohort Study was further divided into surveys based on municipal health check‐ups and those based on assessment centres [24].

Sociodemographic data, lifestyle data, and medical histories were collected from the TMM BirThree Cohort Study or the TMM CommCohort Cohort Study. Participants were also asked to complete questionnaires such as those related to the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) [25] and Center for Epidemiologic Studies‐Depression (CES‐D) [26] Scale. Cognitive function data were collected from 3631 participants of the TMM brain MRI study, who undertook the Japanese version of the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) participants with missing genotype data (n = 960), (ii) those who exceeded four standard deviations (SD) in the 1st or 2nd genetic principal components (n = 8), (iii) those with missing MMSE data (n = 10), (iv) those who self‐reported a history of dementia or stroke (n = 53) and (v) those with missing education history data (n = 29) were excluded. After applying these criteria, a total of 2571 participants were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

All participants provided written informed consent. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Tohoku University Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization (2023‐4‐117).

Measures of Cognitive Function

Cognitive function was measured using the MMSE in a form adapted for Japanese administration [22]. The MMSE is considered the gold standard for screening dementia [27]. It includes a 30‐point cognitive assessment encompassing five subsections: orientation, memory, language, executive functioning, and visuospatial functioning. The MMSE was conducted by qualified professionals such as clinical psychologists, nurses, medical technologists and others holding relevant licenses. Our study used both binary and continuous values of the MMSE as a phenotype of cognitive function. The binary value classification was based on the criteria for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) used in previous studies [28, 29], defining participants with a score of 27 points or lower on the MMSE as cases (low cognitive performance: LCP) and those with a score of 28 points or higher as controls (normal cognitive performance: NCP).

Genotyping, Imputation, and Quality Control

Details pertaining to genotyping performed in the TMM have been previously described [30]. Briefly, participants were genotyped using the Affymetrix Axiom Japonica Array v2 (JPA v2) in 19 batches, with 50 plates per batch. Following batch genotyping, samples with a call rate < 0.95 or samples with unusually high identity‐by‐descent (IBD) values compared with other samples were excluded. In addition, loci with a Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) p value < 1.00 × 10−5, minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01, or missing fraction > 0.01 were excluded from each batch. A direct genotype dataset in the PLINK BED format was obtained by merging the genotype datasets for the 19 batches. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the—pca approx option in PLINK 2.0 [31], on the direct genotype dataset, and individuals with > 4 SD for principal components were excluded. To prepare an imputed genotype dataset, pre‐phasing was performed using SHAPEIT2 [32] along with the—duohmm option [33], which incorporates information on the relatedness between individuals to increase the accuracy of phasing. The phased genotypes were subsequently imputed with a cross‐imputed panel of 3552 Japanese individuals (3.5KJPN) v2 [34] and the 1000 Genomes Project (1KGP) 3 [35] using IMPUTE4 [36]. To create a cross‐imputation panel for 3.5KJPNv2 [34] and 1KGP3 [35], the merge_ref_panels option in IMPUTE2 was applied [37]. Consequently, we obtained an imputed genotype dataset in the Oxford BGEN format (https://www.well.ox.ac.uk/gav/qctool/). Among the genotype imputation data, those with minor allele frequencies < 0.01, missing fraction > 0.05, HWE p values < 1.00 × 10−5, and imputation information scores < 0.4 were excluded. Ultimately, 11,737,866 loci were included in the GWASs.

Statistics and GWAS

Basic characteristics between the LCP and NPC groups were compared using the chi‐square test for categorical variables and the t‐test for continuous variables. GWAS was performed for the MMSE score, which was utilised as both a continuous trait, ranging from 0 to 30 (n = 2571), and a binary trait, categorised with a cut‐off value of 27 points (NCP group, n = 1830; LCP group, n = 741). The Genome‐Wide Complex Trait Analysis software (version 1.94.0) was used to identify associations between autosomal loci and cognitive function [38]. The association between loci and binary values was evaluated using FastGWA‐GLMM, a genome‐wide association tool based on the generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) method [39]. In contrast, the association between loci and continuous values was assessed using FastGWA based on a mixed linear model (MLM) [40]. Both methods were robust against population stratification and familial relationships [20]. GWASs were performed, adjusting for the top 10 PCs calculated from the PCA of the direct genotyping dataset, with age as a continuous covariate and sex, education level (junior high school or less, high school, high school, and other), and cohort type (BirThree Cohort Study, CommCohort Study surveys based on municipal health check‐ups, and surveys based on assessment centres) as categorical covariates. Environmental factors such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and sleep duration were not included as covariates to avoid over‐adjustment. These variables may mediate genetic effects, and adjusting for them could obscure true genotype–phenotype associations. Genome‐wide statistical significance was set at the conventional p value threshold of < 5 × 10−8.

Visualisation and Post‐GWAS Analysis

Manhattan plots and quantile‐quantile plots (Q‐Q plots) for GWASs were generated using R (version 4.1.0). Clumping was applied using PRSice‐2 (version 2.3.5) [41] to determine independent loci, and only the most significant locus was reported. The detected loci were annotated using the ANNOVAR software [42].

Univariate linkage disequilibrium score (LDSC) regression was performed on the summary statistics from the GWAS on continuous values of MMSE [43]. Computation of the heritability Z‐score provided a measure of the polygenic signal in the dataset.

Positional mapping, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) mapping, and chromatin interaction mapping were performed using the Functional Mapping and Annotation of GWASs (FUMA) platform designed for prioritising, annotating, and interpreting GWAS results [44]. We first identified significant independent variants based on their p value (p < 5 × 10−8) and independence of each other (r2 < 0.6 in the 1000 Genome phase 3 East Asia reference) within a 1‐Mb window. Thereafter, lead variants independent of each other (r2 < 0.1) were identified from the significant independent variants. We then mapped variants to genes based on their physical location on the genome using positional mapping. We used ANNOVAR annotations, which are based on a maximum distance (default 10 kb) between variants and genes. Variants were then mapped to genes using eQTL mapping if they showed a significant eQTL association, indicating that the expression of the respective genes was associated with allelic variation at the variant. The eQTL sources were two different reference panels, that is, the Genotype‐Tissue Expression project (GTEx) and the Brain eQTL Almanac (BRAINEAC). Finally, variants were mapped to genes using Chromatin Interaction Mapping if there was a significant chromatin interaction between trait‐associated genomic regions containing the variants and the promoter regions of genes. This mapping was used to identify long‐range interactions without strict distance boundaries. We incorporated Hi‐C experiments of 21 tissues and cell types in FUMA. Promoter regions were defined as 250 bp upstream and 500 bp downstream of the transcription start site by default.

Discussion

We conducted GWASs on the genetic components of cognitive function in a cohort of 2571 elderly Japanese individuals. In this study, two significant loci were identified using continuous MMSE values: rs9460729 near PRL and HDGFL1, and rs77877360 near BANF2 and SNX5. In contrast, no significant loci were observed for binary MMSE values. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to identify novel loci associated with cognitive function in elderly Japanese individuals.

To evaluate the novelty of our findings, we compared the identified two loci with previously reported genetic associations for cognitive function and related traits using the NHGRI‐EBI GWAS catalogue, including studies based on European ancestry. No associations were found in previous GWASs at these loci, suggesting that our findings are novel. The novelty of these findings may be attributed to several factors. First, our study was a GWAS of Japanese ancestry, which may have led to specific results. That is, differences in allele frequencies and LD could potentially affect the strength of associations observed for each locus. Second, cognitive function is influenced by genetic factors, environmental exposure, and their complex interactions; therefore, differences in lifestyle and living environment among ancestries may contribute to the differences in GWAS results due to interactions [45, 46]. Third, we used a cognitive function measure in this study that differed from those used in previous studies. Caution must be exercised when interpreting GWAS results using different measures of cognitive function.

All genes identified through annotation could be reasonably described as being associated with cognitive function. BANF2 regulates the function of BANF1, which plays a role in chromatin structure, segregation, and post‐mitotic nuclear assembly [47]. It is significantly associated with total PHF‐tau [48] levels and hypothyroidism [49], which are associated with the pathology of cognitive decline. Targeting PRL is emerging as a promising treatment for cognitive impairment that acts through several mechanisms [50]. It enhances memory and cognitive function by regulating Drd2 gene expression and may contribute to neuroimmunomodulation or neuronal cell protection via microglial activation in the hippocampus. Additionally, PRL mediates both pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory properties of the central nervous system, suggesting that it can modulate neuroinflammation to prevent or delay cognitive decline. Moreover, prolactin, encoded by PRL, has been reported to have an antidepressant effect [51]. In our study, the LCP group tended to have higher CES‐D scores, which are an indicator of depression (Table 1). This suggests that PRL mutations may be involved in biological pathways leading to low cognitive function through depressive pathology. HDGFL1 has previously been reported to be significantly associated with total iron binding capacity [52]. Increased iron deposition in the brain with ageing has been shown to contribute to cognitive decline and AD onset [53]. These genes were previously shown to be associated with diseases that cause cognitive decline, contribute to cognitive protection, and are involved in mechanisms of brain damage. Therefore, the results of our GWAS are biologically plausible.

The results of chromatin‐interaction mapping revealed SNX5 as a key regulator in intracellular trafficking, particularly in endosomal sorting [54]. Reportedly, in Parkinson's disease (PD), SNX5 is upregulated and promotes ferroptosis, an iron‐dependent form of cell death, thereby inducing neurodegeneration. This suggests the involvement of SNX5 in oxidative stress pathways, which affect dopaminergic neurons, making it a potential therapeutic target for PD [55]. Although the direct role of SNX5 in cognitive function remains unclear, its involvement in neurodegeneration and cellular stress responses suggests its association with cognitive decline. Further research is needed to explore the roles of SNX5 as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

Our findings revealed two novel genome‐wide significant loci in Japanese ancestry, for which no GWAS on cognitive function has been conducted compared to the 22 GWASs conducted for European ancestry. Owing to different genetic backgrounds, the transferability of genetic findings between populations is generally limited [56]. Our GWAS findings provide information on loci specific to Japanese ancestry. In this study, genome‐wide significant loci were identified in the GWAS of continuous values, whereas no significant loci were identified in the GWAS of binary values using the cut‐off score. Although categorisation using a cut‐off score helps assess cognitive impairment [28, 29], it can result in massive loss of information [57]. Therefore, a GWAS with continuous values may prevent loss of information and be effective in detecting significant loci. Moreover, three of the four identified genes were reported to be associated with biological pathways of cognitive decline or improvement. These findings may aid in understanding the pathophysiology of cognitive impairment and associated diseases. These loci may serve as potential therapeutic targets for cognitive decline [58, 59].

This study had several limitations. First, our study population included nearly 2500 participants, which was relatively small for a GWAS because previous studies included approximately 50,000–300,000 participants of European descent [7, 8, 60]. This limits the detection power of our study and necessitates replication. Furthermore, the small sample size may have led to false negatives and could be a potential reason for why loci reported as significant in previous studies were not detected. On the other hand, we identified two variants that reached genome‐wide significance. However, given the low allele frequency and the limited sample size, the possibility of false positive associations cannot be ruled out and warrants caution in interpretation. Second, cognitive function was assessed using MMSE scores only. The MMSE is the most commonly used screening tool to provide an overall measure of cognitive function in clinical and research studies [61]. However, the MMSE emphasises the assessment of language and orientation; thus, it is less sensitive to assessments of memory, executive functioning, and visuospatial functioning [62, 63]. Furthermore, since the MMSE was conducted only cross‐sectionally, it cannot fully differentiate whether cognitive function was originally low or had declined due to aging or other factors. Third, the generalizability of our results is limited because this study only included participants from the TMM Brain MRI Study living in Miyagi Prefecture [22]. Therefore, the results were limited to a part of Japanese ancestry. In addition, this study targeted relatively healthy older adults and may thus not have captured genetic factors associated with more severe cognitive impairment.

Conclusion

Our study provides preliminary genome‐wide evidence suggesting a genetic predisposition to cognitive dysfunction in elderly Japanese individuals. Notably, we identified two novel loci, with potential as candidate genes and possible contributions to cognitive function, underscoring the importance of ancestry‐specific genetic studies. However, given the limited statistical power and rarity of the associated variants, further replication in larger cohorts and functional studies is essential to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying these associations.
