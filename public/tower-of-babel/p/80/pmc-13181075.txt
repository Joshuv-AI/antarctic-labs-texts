The effects of perceived labor rights protection on job performance in platform flexible employees: The role of career resilience and person–environment fit
Authors: Ruofan Xu, De’en Hou, Kunyu Sun, Ziwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWithin the context of labor market transformations driven by the digital economy, the protection of platform flexible employees’ rights has emerged as a critical issue requiring comprehensive analysis. While existing research predominantly focuses on the legal frameworks and institutional structures governing rights protection, there is a significant gap in understanding how perceived labor rights protection influences the job performance of platform flexible employees. Drawing on Self-Determination Theory, this study develops a moderated mediation model to examine the impact of perceived labor rights protection on the job performance of platform flexible employees. It also explores the mediating role of career resilience and the moderating effect of person–environment fit in this relationship. We conducted an empirical analysis using a sample of 521 platform flexible employees in China. The results indicate that career resilience mediates the relationship between perceived labor rights protection and job performance, while person–environment fit moderates this mechanism. This research enriches the theoretical literature on platform flexible employees’ perceptions and job performance, extends the application of SDT to platform-based flexible employment, and offers new insights for enhancing the job performance of platform flexible employees.Keywords: Platform flexible employees, Perceived labor rights protection, Career resilience, Job performance, Person–environment fitSubject terms: Business and management, Business and management, Psychology, Psychology, Sociology

Introduction

With the rapid rise of the digital platform economy, employment structures have undergone profound transformation, giving rise to a large group of platform flexible employees1,2. In China, more than 200 million individuals are currently engaged in flexible platform-based work1. While these workers contribute significantly to the development of the digital economy through greater autonomy in job selection and work arrangements3,4, the majority remain outside standard employment relationships and formal social security systems, resulting in the absence of legally recognized labor identities5,6. Many platform flexible employees experience insufficient protection of labor rights and external stigmatization4, which undermines their career resilience and reduces work efficiency.

Perceived labor rights protection refers to an individual’s subjective evaluation of the extent to which their labor rights are acknowledged and safeguarded by governments, platform enterprises, and social organizations7. Such perceptions play a critical role in enhancing platform flexible employees’ career resilience and job performance7,8. Therefore, systematically examining their perceptions of labor rights protection and its potential individual and organizational consequences is essential for understanding the underlying psychological mechanisms of platform-based flexible work. Moreover, such research can strengthen platform enterprises’ motivation to safeguard labor rights and contribute to the sustainable development of the digital economy.

Research on platform-based flexible employment can be traced back to early studies on informal employment relations, which primarily examined traditional forms of flexible work such as part-time and temporary employment9,10. With the growing integration of digital platforms and flexible labor in recent years, platform flexible employees have attracted increasing scholarly attention. Existing research in this field has mainly focused on three areas.

First, from an institutional perspective, scholars argue that current regulatory frameworks are insufficient to comprehensively safeguard the rights of platform flexible employees. As a result, their labor relations remain ambiguous11, they are excluded from full social security coverage12, and they lack effective channels for dispute resolution when conflicts occur4. Second, studies on job quality and worker well-being suggest that unstable income structures and limited social security reduce workers’ sense of security13, high work intensity increases health risks6, and uncertain career prospects lead to anxiety, stress, and emotional strain2,7,14, undermining employees’ quality of work life15. Third, from the perspective of algorithmic management and work processes, research indicates that digital platforms govern flexible labor through algorithmic rules, restructuring work autonomy and control mechanisms6,14. Although such systems may enhance efficiency10,16, their opacity, embedded biases, and excessive surveillance can heighten stress17, foster counterproductive work behaviors18, erode well-being and impair job performance14,19, revealing a distinct “algorithmic double-edged sword” effect20.

Despite these contributions, several gaps remain in the existing literature. First, prior research has largely emphasized institutional labor protection while paying insufficient attention to platform flexible employees’ perceived labor rights protection and its implications at the individual level. Second, although studies have examined psychological factors such as security, well-being, and autonomy, they offer limited explanation of how platform flexible employees develop sustained work motivation and translate it into work outcomes under highly unstable working conditions. Finally, although existing research has acknowledged the important implications of changing job characteristics, it has not sufficiently incorporated the specific work environment experienced by platform flexible employees.

To address these gaps, this study draws on Self-Determination Theory (SDT) to explain how perceived labor rights protection shapes platform flexible employees’ sustained engagement and job performance. SDT posits that individual motivation is driven by the fulfillment of three basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness21. When the external environment supports these needs, individuals develop autonomous motivation, which leads to sustained effort, emotional stability, and proactive adaptation; conversely, when these needs are unmet, controlled motivation and negative responses are more likely to emerge20,22,23. Within the context of platform-based flexible work, perceived labor rights protection is not only an institutional experience but also a key contextual cue signaling the extent to which basic psychological needs can be fulfilled7.

When platform flexible employees perceive that their labor rights are recognized and protected, their basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are more likely to be fulfilled, leading them to experience the work environment as supportive rather than controlling21,22. Such positive perceptions can activate stronger autonomous motivation, encouraging employees to engage in their work and confront difficulties and challenges with greater initiative24. Career resilience, defined as a dynamic process through which individuals cope with work stress, adversity, and challenges by continuously learning, adjusting behaviors, and mobilizing resources25,26, may thus represent a key psychological mechanism linking perceived labor rights protection to job performance.

Within this motivation-driven process, platform flexible employees with higher levels of career resilience are better able to remain engaged under algorithmic management and heightened environmental uncertainty, adapt constructively to contextual demands, and ultimately achieve superior work outcomes27–29. Self-Determination Theory further posits that the degree to which the external environment supports basic psychological needs shapes the quality of individual motivation30. In the context of the platform economy, the dynamic alignment between platform flexible employees and the algorithmically governed work environment provides a deeper explanation for variations in their motivational states and levels of engagement31,32.

Based on the above discussion, we develop a theoretical framework that links perceived labor rights protection, career resilience, and job performance, and introduce person–environment fit as a moderating factor to examine the mechanism through which perceived labor rights protection influences the job performance of platform flexible employees. This study offers three main contributions: First, the study identifies the mechanism through which perceived labor rights protection enhances job performance. This advances the understanding of psychological processes among flexible workers in nonstandard employment arrangements and compensates for the limited empirical research beyond policy- and law-oriented discussions33,34. Second, this study considers the changing work environment of platform flexible employees and demonstrates the positive moderating role of person–environment fit in the proposed model. By clarifying the contextual boundary conditions under which perceived labor rights protection operates, the study provides theoretical support for more targeted interventions in complex platform governance2,32. Third, the study applies Self-Determination Theory to the platform labor context and illuminates the motivational pathway linking perceived labor rights protection to job performance through career resilience. This extends the theoretical application of Self-Determination Theory to nonstandard employment groups and enriches the understanding of motivation in algorithmically governed work settings12,35.

Theoretical background and hypotheses

Perceived labor rights protection and career resilience of platform flexible employees

Perceived labor rights protection refers to platform flexible employees’ subjective evaluation of the extent to which the platform provides compensation and benefits, employment policies, operational rules, and labor protection measures 7. Within the “de-labor relationship” context shaped by digital labor platforms, perceived labor rights protection serves as a key indicator for assessing workers’ livelihood conditions1,6,7,15.

Career resilience refers to a work-centered dynamic process in which individuals continuously learn, reflect, and adjust to maintain career development and psychological balance when facing environmental changes, career shocks, or adversity27,36. For platform flexible employees, this involves updating platform-related skills, sustaining performance under the high-pressure interactions among platforms, merchants, and users, and rebuilding occupational confidence amid income fluctuations and limited organizational support. Unlike psychological resilience, which emphasizes general emotional regulation and coping capacities across life domains37, career resilience specifically highlights adaptive behaviors, continuous learning, and sustained engagement within work and career development contexts26,27.

According to Self-Determination Theory, an individual’s motivation is influenced by their perception of the external environment38. This theoretical framework provides a central explanatory pathway for understanding how perceived labor rights protection affects career resilience: perceived labor rights protection reflects a psychologically supportive work environment that stimulates autonomous motivation and fosters career resilience. When employees perceive the platform’s systems as fair, experience autonomy in work arrangements, feel secure in their employment, and believe they are respected, they are more likely to internalize these external conditions into self-determined motivation30. This process encourages them to remain engaged in their work, proactively acquire new knowledge, and effectively cope with challenges.

Platform flexible employees often operate under stringent algorithmic control, face high work intensity, experience low social recognition, and lack adequate protection4,16. Perceived labor rights protection, as a supportive contextual perception, is closely linked to the fulfillment of basic psychological needs35. Transparent and flexible algorithmic rules allow platform flexible employees to perceive autonomy in choosing when, where, and how to work, thereby satisfying their need for autonomy. Reasonable workflow standards, skill development programs, and clear advancement paths enable them to feel competent in addressing work-related challenges, fulfilling their need for competence. In addition, comprehensive policies and protection mechanisms enhance social connection and meet their need for relatedness. The satisfaction of these needs activates work motivation, prompting platform flexible employees to proactively pursue their work goals when facing difficulties21.

Existing research shows that individuals with stronger autonomous motivation are better able to regulate their emotions23, adjust their strategies when facing difficulties31,39, and mobilize internal resources to sustain high levels of effort30. Accordingly, platform flexible employees who possess strong motivation can effectively self-regulate and enhance their adaptive capacity23. They are more likely to view challenges as opportunities for growth rather than threats, and thus remain flexible, persistent, and resilient in dynamic and uncertain platform-based work environments. In sum, autonomous motivation not only strengthens sustained engagement but also enhances individuals’ capacity to cope with stress and change, thereby providing the psychological foundation for the development of career resilience27,28.

Specifically, when platform flexible employees perceive a high level of labor rights protection, they tend to develop a more optimistic view of their career prospects, experience less emotional exhaustion when dealing with customer complaints, and remain determined to overcome setbacks during career downturns. Driven by autonomous motivation, they are better able to cope with work-related difficulties. In contrast, when perceived labor rights protection is low, their basic psychological needs are less likely to be satisfied. As a result, external motivation fluctuates with unstable income and uncertain career prospects, internal motivation becomes depleted, and their career resilience is weakened. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1

Perceived labor rights protection positively influences the career resilience of platform flexible employees.

Career resilience and job performance of platform flexible employees

Within the framework of Self-Determination Theory, career resilience represents the concrete manifestation of motivation at the individual state level23,26,36. It reflects a sustained condition in which individuals internalize external incentives, enhance their motivation, confront career challenges, and strive to progress despite adversity40, thereby maintaining the continuity and effectiveness of their job performance.

Platform-based flexible work is characterized by strong algorithmic control, high work intensity, substantial pressure, and considerable uncertainty. Career resilience has a significant positive effect on the job performance of platform flexible employees by buffering the stress caused by occupational uncertainty and by promoting proactive adaptation. As a dynamic and continuous process of adjustment, career resilience enables individuals to cope with environmental changes, career shocks, and adversity through ongoing learning, reflection, and behavioral modification, thereby maintaining career development and personal balance. It also assists individuals in internalizing external demands, such as platform rules and customer evaluations, into self-endorsed goals that generate sustained autonomous motivation and ultimately lead to improved job outcomes. These effects are reflected in the following aspects:

First, individuals with high career resilience possess stronger adaptability and recovery capacity when encountering adversity26. They are better able to cope with the strict algorithmic management of platforms, transforming algorithmic control and customer evaluation pressures into challenges aligned with their own capabilities21,25,41. By autonomously selecting and actively completing complex tasks, they satisfy their needs for autonomy and competence23, thereby reducing the negative effects of occupational stress. Second, highly resilient workers are more inclined to proactively learn platform rules and optimize service strategies, thereby adapting to algorithms and performing effectively1,6. For example, ride-hailing drivers enhance order efficiency through route planning algorithms, while food-delivery couriers secure priority dispatch opportunities by maintaining relationships with merchants. Finally, the “gig-based” nature of the platform economy amplifies occupational uncertainty10,13. Individuals with high career resilience can leverage the motivational drive derived from the satisfaction of basic psychological needs to effectively cope with the uncertainties of flexible employment, proactively adapt to work demands, optimize their behaviors, and ultimately enhance service efficiency and customer satisfaction.

Empirical research shows that the career resilience of non-standard employees is positively correlated with their job satisfaction, career beliefs, and work outcomes25,27,36. Moreover, in situations lacking organizational support, individual resilience serves as an alternative protective factor, driving performance by reducing the sense of loss of control caused by algorithmic management1,27,29. Therefore, this study posits that career resilience is not only the “psychological armor” for platform flexible employees to cope with career uncertainty but also a key variable that directly drives job performance through enhanced motivation and optimized behavior. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2

Career resilience positively influences the job performance of platform flexible employees.

Perceived labor rights protection and job performance of platform flexible employees

Self-Determination Theory posits that satisfying basic psychological needs is a prerequisite for intrinsic motivation38. Compared with standard employees, platform flexible employees typically engage in work that is less innovative, requires lower levels of skill, and involves high task repetition, with a greater emphasis on physical rather than cognitive labor4,13,29. Therefore, flexible employees who possess stronger work motivation and greater willingness to devote time and effort to their tasks are more likely to achieve superior job outcomes. Perceived labor rights protection enhances job performance by fulfilling workers’ needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness and thereby fostering intrinsic motivation42,43. Prior studies indicate that positive perceptions have beneficial effects on employee behaviors and outcomes in traditional employment contexts44–46. When workers perceive strong labor rights protection, platform algorithms function as supportive structures rather than purely control mechanisms. Workers can choose order times and service areas to suit their personal preferences, which enhances work efficiency16,22. Conversely, insufficient perceived labor rights protection creates insecurity and leaves basic psychological needs unmet. Workers feel disadvantaged relative to platform firms and fear inadequate pay or job loss, which undermines their motivation1,23. When confronted with challenges such as order delays or customer complaints, they resort to negative coping strategies that hinder performance2,3.

Given the repetitive nature of tasks, lower skill requirements, and limited opportunities for innovation in platform-based flexible work4,12,41, employees with stronger motivation and a greater willingness to invest physical effort and energy in their work are more likely to achieve better job performance38. Therefore, perceived labor rights protection serves as a crucial motivational factor for enhancing job performance. On the basis of these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3

Perceived labor rights protection positively influences job performance among platform flexible employees.

Mediating role of career resilience

Drawing on Self-Determination Theory, this study posits that career resilience serves as the core mechanism through which perceived labor rights protection affects job performance26,38. Perceived labor rights protection represents the psychological feedback of platform flexible employees to a supportive work context. This positive perception stimulates autonomous motivation, enabling employees to exhibit greater learning and adaptive capacity when facing pressure and challenges, thereby achieving better work outcomes. This motivation-driven adaptive process reflects the transformation mechanism from perception to outcome. In the platform economy, platform flexible employees face weakened labor relations, strict algorithmic governance, and frequent disputes1,14, relying on career resilience to cope with environmental uncertainty.

Perceived labor rights protection fosters career resilience by satisfying autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs, which generates autonomous motivation and improves job performance. Firstly, when workers recognize transparent pay rules and fair algorithmic enforcement, they view platform regulations as tools for self-management rather than external control35. This reframing increases autonomy in service decisions and encourages workers to proactively optimize their strategies for the platform environment31. For example, when rights protection is strong, workers view dispatch algorithms and time limits as supportive cues, allowing greater decision-making flexibility in complex tasks6,12. Driven by autonomous motivation, they tackle challenging assignments, boosting job performance and earnings28.

Secondly, where platforms offer training programs and advancement paths, workers experience a positive cycle of skill growth and improved service quality. By satisfying competence needs, workers develop autonomous motivation, which increases their resilience and leads to better performance in challenging environments21,38. Improved task execution efficiency and enhanced core capabilities help workers respond to technological updates and evolving customer demands23,47.

Finally, perceived labor rights protection reinforces the sustained motivational basis of career resilience through the satisfaction of relatedness needs. Comprehensive policy and legal support, fair platform rules, and harmonious supervisor–subordinate relationships enable platform flexible employees to feel valued by the government, society, platform enterprises, and colleagues, thereby increasing their motivation to engage in their work10,48. Grievance and feedback channels provided by platforms and regulators rebuild social ties among digital workers2,7,29. When workers perceive organizational support, they form supportive relationships with managers and peers, assist one another, and engage in extra-role behaviors48,49. These social connections facilitate knowledge sharing and informal mentoring, satisfying relational needs, strengthening career resilience, and sustain stable human-capital accumulation even under adversity50,51. On this basis, we propose:

Hypothesis 4

Career resilience mediates the relationship between perceived labor rights protection and job performance of platform flexible employees.

Moderating mechanisms of person–environment fit

Person–environment fit refers to the degree of compatibility between individuals and their work environment in terms of abilities, values, and social connections52. Prior research suggests that external environments shape individual motivation47, and that the extent to which personal attributes align with work-environment characteristics influences how individuals interpret and internalize contextual cues, thereby shaping their motivational states and adaptive responses21,53,54.

In the platform economy, the motivational effect of perceived labor rights protection among platform flexible employees depends not only on whether supportive institutional arrangements are in place, but also on whether workers experience congruence with their work environment. The extent to which perceived labor rights protection fosters career resilience is contingent on employees’ fit with their work environment in terms of ability requirements, value alignment, and social connection needs. Such fit moderates the psychological and motivational processes through which perceived labor rights protection is translated into career resilience24,30,55. Specifically, demands–abilities fit, person–organization fit, and person–group fit jointly constitute the framework through which person–environment fit exerts its moderating role54.

First, demands–abilities fit influences the motivational pathway by supporting competence-need fulfillment. When platform flexible employees’ knowledge and skills align with job requirements, the motivation generated by perceived labor rights protection is more fully realized23,32. Under such conditions, employees are more likely to internalize platform rules as tools for self-management and flexibly translate algorithmic requirements into behavioral guidelines, enabling them to cope effectively with work-related difficulties. By contrast, when employees’ skills do not meet platform demands, perceived labor rights protection may be less effective in facilitating recovery from setbacks due to perceived inefficacy. Misfit may also introduce cognitive bias, whereby platform-provided training is interpreted as a formal requirement rather than developmental support, thereby weakening the positive effects of perceived labor rights protection.

Second, person–organization fit strengthens the motivational function of perceived labor rights protection through value congruence. When employees identify with a platform’s values and management systems, perceived labor rights protection becomes an important mechanism for transforming controlled motivation into autonomous motivation38,52. This alignment reframes algorithmic rules from instruments of control into meaning-providing frameworks, leading employees to interpret order allocation and service evaluation rules as professional standards rather than power constraints. In contrast, value incongruence may lead employees to interpret labor protection measures as superficial or as implicit forms of control, thereby undermining career resilience.

Third, person–group fit enhances the relatedness-need fulfillment effect of perceived labor rights protection through social connections. In fragmented platform work, coworker support networks complement formal protection mechanisms1,56. When employees develop functionally complementary relationships with coworkers, perceived labor rights protection can generate a multiplier effect through group identification. Conversely, social isolation may weaken its motivational impact and partially offset its positive effect on career resilience. On this basis, we propose:

Hypothesis 5

Person–environment fit moderates the relationship between perceived labor rights protection and career resilience such that the relationship is stronger when person–environment fit is high than when it is low.

Career resilience is conceptualized as a dynamic adaptive process, the formation and development of which are deeply rooted in the continuous satisfaction of the three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness23,26. Throughout their careers, individuals gradually establish and strengthen adaptive mechanisms for coping with occupational adversity through ongoing need satisfaction, learning, and adjustment25. In the context of platform-based flexible employment, perceived labor rights protection provides the psychological foundation for career resilience by safeguarding these basic needs22,23. When person–environment fit is high, workers perceive platform work as an autonomous choice and fully satisfy their basic psychological needs, thereby generating stronger intrinsic motivation32. This motivation drives them to manage work pressures, balance multiple relationships, and achieve better outcomes through continuous problem solving38. Consequently, career resilience is enhanced and mediates the effect of perceived labor rights protection on job performance.

Conversely, low person–environment fit causes workers to feel controlled by platform algorithms1,32. Under these conditions, autonomous motivation shifts to controlled motivation38, basic psychological needs are thwarted28,50, and career resilience weakens. This manifests as reduced agency, passive coping with challenges, and poor outcomes, thereby attenuating the mediating role of career resilience. On this basis, we propose:

Hypothesis 6

Person–environment fit positively moderates the indirect effect of perceived labor rights protection on job performance through career resilience, such that this indirect effect is stronger at higher levels of person–environment fit.

Methods

Sample and procedures

This study targeted the platform enterprises with the largest and most representative market shares in China: Meituan, Ele.me, DiDi Chuxing, and JD.com. In 2020, Meituan held 68.2% of the food-delivery market. With consent and assistance from regional managers, we randomly contacted the heads of 25 branch offices in major platform-economy hubs in China (Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, Tianjin, Jinan, and Qingdao) to explain the study’s objectives and procedures. From each branch, we randomly selected 5% of platform flexible employees (delivery riders, ride-hailing drivers, and logistics couriers) to participate in the survey. These groups represent the largest and most representative category of flexible employees in China, accounting for more than 90 percent of the total. Although their specific job tasks vary somewhat, they share common characteristics such as work flexibility, autonomy, and order-oriented employment1,16,29. They lack access to comprehensive social insurance, do not have formal employment relationships, and must carefully navigate the tripartite relationship between platforms, themselves, and consumers4,14,34. Respondents were informed that data would be anonymized and used solely for academic research, with no negative repercussions for individuals or enterprises; clarifying that there were no right or wrong answers reduced concerns and ensured authentic responses.

To control some sources of common method bias, we collected data in two stages54,57,58. In the first wave, we distributed 576 questionnaires to platform flexible employees to measure perceived labor rights protection, career resilience, and person–environment fit. One month later, we distributed 576 questionnaires to platform managers to assess job performance. Because performance data are regarded as commercial secrets by platform enterprises and therefore could not be provided directly to researchers, supervisors were instructed to complete the evaluations based on the platform data available to them. We emphasized that they should report objectively according to these records in order to minimize subjective interference. This two-wave design mitigated the influence of transient environmental factors and reduced some potential self-report bias. Each participant was assigned a unique identification code to enable matching across waves. After excluding invalid responses (e.g., patterned answers or incomplete questionnaires), the final sample comprised 521 valid questionnaires, yielding an effective response rate of 90.5%. Most respondents were male, and their ages were primarily concentrated between 20 and 50 years. Regarding educational background, most respondents held a high school or vocational school diploma, while a smaller proportion had completed an undergraduate degree. Overall, these demographic characteristics are consistent with findings from prior studies on platform flexible employees. Detailed demographic information is presented in Appendix Table.

Measures

Most scales were adapted from measures published in authoritative English-language journals using Brislin’s back-translation method to ensure semantic equivalence59. An HR management PhD candidate and a linguistics PhD candidate independently translated the scales into Chinese. The translations were cross-checked and reconciled to ensure accuracy and consistency. We performed iterative back-translation until equivalence was achieved in content, semantics, format, and applicability. Human resource management experts then reviewed and refined the translations to develop the preliminary questionnaire. Before the main survey, we conducted a pilot study with 50 respondents. Feedback informed revisions to item wording and semantics. All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). The specific measurement methods employed were as follows:

Perceived labor rights protection (LR)

We employed the scale devised by Xu7, which primarily incorporates 17 measurement items. An example item is “I can independently select my work tasks, schedule, and methods.” In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the five-point scale is 0.891.

Career resilience (CA)

We utilized the scale formulated by Mishra27, which primarily includes 9 measurement items. An example is “I am capable of effectively managing various pressures and the constraints of rules in the workplace.” In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the five-point scale is 0.832.

Job performance (JP)

We adopted the scale developed by Chen60, which mainly encompasses eight measurement items. For instance, “Employees can strive to produce higher-quality work.” In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the five-point scale is 0.781.

Person–environment fit (PEF)

We adopted the scale designed by Greguras54, which primarily consists of 9 measurement items. An example is “My personal values align with the company’s culture and values.” In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the five-point scale is 0.843.

Control variables

To enhance the accuracy of the estimates and remain consistent with prior research, we included gender, age, and education as control variables for platform flexible employees6,15,29,54,61. These variables may affect the outcomes under investigation. For example, younger workers often value schedule flexibility, seek autonomy over their time, and derive greater motivation. Among platform flexible employees, those who are younger tend to exhibit more positive psychological and behavioral outcomes. Platform flexible employees differ from standard employees in gender composition, with a higher proportion of men. Gender may influence environmental perceptions and capabilities. Finally, education reflects accumulated knowledge and human capital that may shape perceptions and its effects on job performance.

Data analysis

The data-analysis process strictly followed these steps. First, we assessed common method bias. Second, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis using Mplus 8.3 to verify the discriminant validity of the four core variables. Third, we examined inter-variable correlations and tested our hypotheses62,63.

Results

Common method variance (CMV)

Although data were collected in two waves, all focal predictors were self-reported, whereas job performance was supervisor-reported, warranting vigilance for potential common method bias64. We applied Harman’s single-factor test by performing an unrotated exploratory factor analysis on the four key variables. Multiple factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1 emerged, cumulatively explaining 58.7% of the variance; the first factor accounted for 30.8%, well below the 40% threshold. These results indicate no significant common method bias, preserving the validity of our conclusions.

We then tested the mediation and moderated mediation effects using a bootstrap procedure (5,000 resamples). As shown in Table 4, the indirect effect of perceived labor rights protection on job performance through career resilience was significant (B = 0.131, Boot SE = 0.039, 95% CI [0.056, 0.206]), providing support for Hypothesis 4. Furthermore, the conditional indirect effect was stronger when person–environment fit was high (B = 0.187, Boot SE = 0.035, 95% CI [0.118, 0.256]) than when it was low (B = 0.124, Boot SE = 0.041, 95% CI [0.088, 0.160]). The difference between the conditional indirect effects was significant (difference = 0.063, Boot SE = 0.022, 95% CI [0.038, 0.088]), indicating a significant first-stage moderated mediation effect. Therefore, Hypothesis 6 was supported.

Discussion

Theoretical implications

Our study presents three primary theoretical contributions:

First, this study reveals the mechanism through which perceived labor rights protection affects job performance among platform flexible employees, thereby deepening the understanding of psychological processes in nonstandard employment contexts. Existing literature on the protection of flexible employees’ rights has primarily focused on legal and policy perspectives, with limited empirical evidence from psychological and behavioral dimensions4,11,33. Moreover, prior research on perception and career resilience has mainly centered on standard employment relationships8,25,44, examining how individual perceptions and organizational factors influence resilience and its effects on job satisfaction, emotional exhaustion, and organizational commitment, while paying insufficient attention to how perceptions affect work outcomes. Platform flexible employees encounter unique challenges, including algorithmic governance, customer evaluation pressures, the absence of formal contractual agreements, and weakened organizational attachment, which fundamentally reshape the antecedents and functional mechanisms of career resilience in nonstandard employment contexts1,4,12,29. Through empirical analysis, this study identifies the perception–career resilience–performance mechanism among platform flexible employees and demonstrates that perceived labor rights protection enhances career resilience, which in turn improves job performance. These findings provide new evidence for understanding the subjective initiative and work outcomes of flexible employees within large standardized platforms, thereby enriching research on the psychological mechanisms of nonstandard employment.

Second, this study identifies the moderating role of person–environment fit in the influence pathway linking perceived labor rights protection, career resilience, and job performance, thereby clarifying the contextual boundary conditions under which perceived labor rights protection exerts its effects. Although prior research has discussed individual and environmental moderators of the perception–outcome relationship, limited attention has been paid to the distinctive characteristics of the platform economy32,52. Platform flexible employees typically face low entry barriers1, high task repetitiveness4, ambiguous labor relations12, and substantial pressure from both customers and platform management6,65. In such a complex work environment, it is particularly important to examine how situational factors shape the influence of perceptions on outcomes. The empirical findings indicate that person–environment fit positively moderates the effect of perceived labor rights protection on career resilience. When the level of person–environment fit is higher, the positive impact of perceived labor rights protection on career resilience becomes stronger, and the mediating role of career resilience is also reinforced. This finding clarifies the boundary conditions under which perceived labor rights protection operates and provides useful directions for future research on environmental factors affecting platform flexible employees in the platform economy52,66,67.

Third, this study deepens the application of Self-Determination Theory within the context of platform-based work and enriches research on the motivation of flexible workers. Previous research on SDT has primarily focused on standard employees with formal labor contracts, who typically have clearly defined working hours, locations, and methods, as well as comprehensive systems of rights protection23,30,38. In contrast, the fragmented and autonomous nature of platform flexible employment leads to substantial differences in psychological needs and work motivation compared with standard employees1,12. The findings of this study demonstrate that perceived labor rights protection can stimulate motivation, promote the development of career resilience, and enhance job performance. This suggests that within digital platform work, positive work states are not solely driven by external institutional safeguards but also by individuals’ internalization of supportive environments and their capacity for self-regulation. By applying Self-Determination Theory to the platform flexible employment context, this study extends its use in this domain and contributes to the literature on work motivation among flexible employees17,35,38.

Practical implications

Our study also delivers several practical recommendations:

First, the study’s findings indicate that perceived labor rights protection and career resilience are positively associated with job outcomes. Accordingly, large standardized platform enterprises should establish mechanisms to strengthen workers’ perceptions of labor rights protection and enhance job performance through the cultivation of career resilience. Such enterprises need to move beyond the current logic of pursuing algorithmic efficiency alone by instituting transparent rights-protection rules and feedback mechanisms to reinforce workers’ perceptions of protection. For example, reducing excessively strict order completion time limits and creating appeal channels for exceptional orders can help ensure workers’ trust in algorithmic decision-making.

Second, because person–environment fit positively moderates the effect of perceived labor rights protection on career resilience and job performance, platform managers should recruit and retain workers whose values and capabilities align with platform requirements. They should also design algorithmic rules and foster a platform culture that reflect the realities of platform-based flexible work. By enhancing person–environment fit, platforms enable workers to leverage perceived labor rights protection to develop adaptive strategies, strengthen career resilience, and improve job performance.

Third, ensuring that platform flexible employees perceive robust labor rights protection benefits not only the workers by improving their outcomes and earnings, but also the platforms by driving profitability and sustainable growth. Policymakers should therefore develop a tiered, differentiated protection framework rather than enforcing one-size-fits-all regulations. For instance, drawing on JD.com’s approach, governments could mandate that platforms contribute to workers’ social insurance while supporting vocational training and certification systems tailored to flexible work. Such measures would elevate perceived labor rights protection and facilitate mutual development of workers and platform enterprises.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations.

First, our sample included only workers from China’s largest, well-regulated platform enterprises. These companies have well-established management systems, place great importance on corporate reputation and social impact, and prioritize protecting workers’ rights. Emerging micro-platforms and other types of workers, such as contract-bound livestream hosts, differ in their characteristics and job features. Their needs, motivations, and perceptions may differ, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Subsequent research should stratify samples across different types of platform enterprises and worker groups to analyze the effects of heterogeneity, thereby producing more accurate conclusions and nuanced insights.

Second, all data were self-reported and supervisor-reported by platform flexible employees and managers. Although data were collected in two waves separated by one month, the relatively short interval means that external environmental factors, temporal effects, and individual differences may still introduce common method bias and affect accuracy. Future studies could employ longer-term longitudinal designs and adopt alternative measures, such as using labor-dispute litigation outcomes to capture perceived labor rights protection, establishing confidentiality agreements with platform enterprises to access selected non-sensitive performance data from their databases under supervision, or conducting case studies on rights-protection perceptions, thereby enhancing both the rigor and generalizability.

Finally, although this study examined the positive impact of perceived labor rights protection on job performance from a motivational perspective, we did not measure or investigate the three core psychological needs proposed by Self-Determination Theory, nor did we incorporate key work characteristics (e.g., job autonomy, nonstandard arrangements, task variety, and skill diversity) and competitive mechanisms into our framework. Future research could focus on the fundamental attributes of platform-based flexible work, examine the role of the three psychological needs, and introduce additional mechanisms such as perceived organizational support, psychological safety, and perceived decent work. Such efforts would enable a more comprehensive analysis of how these characteristics and perceptions shape individual behaviors and outcomes.

Conclusion

This study investigates the mechanisms and boundary conditions through which perceived labor rights protection affects job performance among platform flexible employees. The results indicate that career resilience mediates the relationship between perceived labor rights protection and job performance. Moreover, person–environment fit serves as a boundary condition by moderating the positive effect of perceived labor rights protection on career resilience, thereby strengthening the indirect effect on job performance. These findings provide practical insights for platform organizations seeking to enhance worker performance and strengthen competitiveness in the digital economy.
