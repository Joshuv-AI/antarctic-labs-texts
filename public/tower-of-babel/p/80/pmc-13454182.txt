SGLT2 inhibition with empagliflozin attenuates retinal oxidative stress and damage in diabetic mice
Authors: Yutong Xia, Jiru Zhu, Xin Yu, Zhitong Chen, Jiayun Ge, Liyue Zhang, Yanqing Li, Ye Shen, Xiuyi Li, Qianjie Yang, Jianping Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the effects of empagliflozin (EMPA), an SGLT2 inhibitor, on retinal damage in diabetic mice, exploring its potential as a therapeutic strategy for diabetic retinopathy (DR). DR was induced by a high-fat diet and streptozotocin injections, followed by EMPA treatment. The study employed multiple detection methods, including assessments of hyperglycemia levels, detection of retinal oxidative stress markers, mitochondrial-associated alterations (TOM20 expression), histological examination of retinal tissue, electroretinography for evaluating retinal function, and the expression of structural or junction-associated proteins, including ZO-1, occludin, and vimentin. EMPA treatment exerted significant beneficial effects on diabetic mice with retinopathy. It notably alleviated hyperglycemia, reduced retinal oxidative stress (as demonstrated by decreased ROS production and increased levels of antioxidant proteins HO-1 and NRF2), and partially normalized TOM20 expression. Histological analysis revealed that EMPA mitigated retinal thinning, reduced retinal cell apoptosis, and attenuated gliosis. Electroretinography results showed that EMPA improved retinal function by preserving the amplitudes of a-waves and b-waves. Additionally, EMPA increased the expression of retinal structural or junction-associated proteins. In conclusion, EMPA treatment was associated with attenuation of retinal injury in diabetic mice, along with improvements in systemic glucose levels, oxidative stress markers, and retinal structure and function. However, as EMPA also reduced blood glucose levels, it remains unclear whether these retinal effects are independent of glycemic control. These findings support further investigation into the potential role of EMPA in early diabetic retinal injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53536-x.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a common complication of diabetes, representing a leading cause of vision loss worldwide and the primary cause of blindness in individuals of working age1. DR manifests through a variety of retinal abnormalities, including microaneurysms, neovascularization, retinal detachment, and, in severe cases, complete blindness2,3. Based on the severity of the disease, DR is classified into two main stages: non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)4,5. NPDR, the most common form of DR, is typically characterized by microvascular changes, including retinal edema and hard exudates. Over time, as the disease progresses, these changes can become more severe, leading to ischemia and hypoxia in the retina, which can trigger the growth of abnormal new blood vessels. These abnormal vessels can further damage the retina and lead to the characteristic features of PDR, such as vitreous hemorrhage and retinal fibrosis6–8. As the global prevalence of diabetes continues to rise, the incidence of DR is also increasing, exacerbating the burden on public health systems.

Current treatment options for DR include systemic therapies, such as blood glucose and blood pressure control, in addition to localized interventions like retinal laser photocoagulation, vitrectomy, and intraocular injections of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors or corticosteroids9. However, these therapies are generally targeted at the advanced stages of DR and have limited capacity to restore vision once it is lost10,11. Therefore, it is critical to identify the early pathogenic mechanisms underlying DR. Understanding these mechanisms could lead to novel therapeutic strategies aimed at preventing the progression of the disease before irreversible retinal damage occurs. Early intervention could be key to mitigating the long-term consequences of DR and preserving vision in affected individuals. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors demonstrate significant therapeutic efficacy in ameliorating diabetic metabolic disorders through their unique multi-target mechanisms12,13. In recent years, extensive clinical studies have confirmed their definitive protective effects against diabetes-related cardiovascular and renal complications14,15. Given the pleiotropic metabolic and tissue-protective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors, we hypothesized that empagliflozin (EMPA) may exert beneficial effects on early diabetic retinal injury. However, systematic research on this potential mechanism remains scarce. This study analyzed the beneficial effects of EMPA in diabetic mice and explored potential mechanisms to prevent the onset of DR or slow its progression.

Results

The impact of empagliflozin on body weight and blood glucose levels in STZ-induced diabetic mice

To investigate the effects of EMPA on the retinas of diabetic mice, as shown in our experimental flowchart, we randomly divided the mice into three groups: Control, DM, and DM+EMPA. A stable diabetic model was established by feeding a high-fat diet and administering a low dose of STZ (Fig. 1A) to induce diabetic mouse model. The therapeutic effects of EMPA on diabetic mice were observed (Fig. 1B). There were no significant differences in baseline body weight and blood glucose levels across the groups (Fig. 1C, D). After STZ injection, blood glucose levels increased significantly, and after 8 weeks of EMPA injection, blood glucose levels were partially alleviated (Fig. 1C). The Control group showed stable weight, while both the DM and DM+EMPA groups had slight weight increases. However, following the 8-week treatment period, their body weight decreased (Fig. 1D). Additionally, representative FFA images suggested that DM group developed retinal vascular abnormalities, (Fig. 1E). These results indicate that a stable diabetic model was successfully established, and EMPA effectively alleviates hyperglycemia in diabetic mice.

EMPA effectively alleviated retinal damage in STZ-induced diabetic mice

We observed retinal morphology using HE staining and found that the retinal cells in the DM group mice were arranged more loosely, with a general thinning of the retinal thickness, particularly noticeable in the outer nuclear layer (Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, we found that EMPA could inhibit the disorganization of retinal cell arrangement caused by diabetes and reduce the thinning of the retinal thickness (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, TUNEL staining revealed localized cell apoptosis in the DM group (Fig. 2C). Additionally, the ERG results reflected the impact of the DM group and EMPA treatment on retinal function. Compared to the Control group, the DM group showed a downward trend in the amplitudes of both a-wave and b-wave, with more noticeable reductions at the 0.1 ERG and 1.0 ERG. However, we found that EMPA effectively alleviated the decrease in the amplitudes of both waves (Fig. 2D-I). This suggests that EMPA has a positive effect on protecting the ultrastructure and enhancing the function of retinal neurons in DR.

EMPA alleviates activated gliosis and lipid peroxidation processes in diabetic mice

To further investigate the protective effects of EMPA in DR, we examined Müller cell activation and retinal lipid peroxidation in the DM group using immunofluorescence staining for GFAP and 4-HNE. In the DM group, GFAP immunoreactivity was markedly elevated, reflecting significant Müller cell gliosis, whereas EMPA treatment substantially reduced GFAP expression, indicating alleviation of glial activation (Fig. 3A and B). Additionally, 4-HNE, a well-established marker of lipid peroxidation, was significantly increased in retinal ganglion cells of diabetic mice, highlighting oxidative lipid damage. Treatment with EMPA effectively decreased 4-HNE levels, suggesting a protective antioxidant effect (Fig. 3C and D). In addition, we evaluated the expression of ACSL4, a key enzyme involved in lipid peroxidation and observed a notable upregulation in the DM group. EMPA treatment mitigated this increase, further supporting its role in reducing lipid peroxidative stress in the diabetic retina (Fig. 3E and F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that EMPA attenuates both gliosis and lipid peroxidation, contributing to its retinal protective effects in diabetic mice.

EMPA alleviated retinal oxidative stress in STZ-induced diabetic mice

To further investigate the effect of EMPA on oxidative stress in STZ-induced diabetic mice, we performed DHE staining. The results demonstrated that EMPA significantly alleviated the STZ-induced the reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in the retina of STZ-induced diabetic mice as indicated in DHE staining and corresponding fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4A and B). Furthermore, the expression of key oxidative stress-related proteins, including HO-1 and NRF2, was markedly increased following EMPA treatment (Fig. 4C and D), immunofluorescence analysis of HO-1 further confirmed these findings (Fig. 4E and F). Taken together, these results indicate that EMPA mitigates retinal oxidative stress by upregulating antioxidant defense mechanisms through enhanced expression of HO-1 and NRF2.

EMPA alleviated mitochondrial damage in the retina of diabetic mice and altered Ki67 immunoreactivity in retinal tissue

In CR retinas, the expression of the mitochondrial marker TOM20 was robust and uniformly distributed throughout the retinal layers, as shown by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5A and C). The expression of TOM20 was significantly reduced, indicating mitochondrial damage. However, in diabetic retinas treated with EMPA, the expression of TOM20 was restored to levels comparable to those in control retinas. Western blot analysis confirmed the immunofluorescence findings (Fig. 5B and D). Furthermore, the proliferative ability of retinal cells was assessed by the expression of Ki67, a marker of cell proliferation, in diabetic retinas, the number of Ki67-positive cells was significantly reduced, indicating decreased retinal cell proliferation. However, in the EMPA group, the number of Ki67+ cells was partially restored (Fig. 5E and F), suggesting that EMPA treatment may be associated with altered retinal cellular proliferative activity.

EMPA mitigated retinal structural and junctional changes induced by STZ

Next, we examined the expression of retinal-associated structural and junction-associated proteins in diabetic mice. In normal retinal tissue, the expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Vimentin were within the normal range, indicating intact retinal structure and functional intercellular junctions16. However, in the DM group, the expression of ZO-1, Occludin, and Vimentin was significantly reduced, reflecting the disruption of both retinal structure and intercellular junction function (Fig. 6). Treatment with EMPA notably restored the expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Vimentin in the retina (Fig. 6), suggesting that the drug effectively mitigates retinal damage caused by DR and helps improve both intercellular junctions and retinal structural integrity.

Discussion

DR is one of the most common complications of diabetes, and its pathogenesis is multifaceted17. In this study, we explored the potential of EMPA as a therapeutic strategy for DR. Our research demonstrated that EMPA effectively alleviates pathological changes and visual dysfunction in the retinas of DM mice. EMPA not only significantly reduces blood glucose levels but also mitigates retinal lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress in DM mice, while improving retinal tight junctions. Overall, our findings suggest that EMPA may slow the progression of DR and provide insight into the underlying mechanisms of its protective effects.

In our experiment, STZ-induced diabetic mice exhibited significantly elevated blood glucose levels, a hallmark of diabetes. After 8 weeks of EMPA treatment, blood glucose levels were partially alleviated. This finding is consistent with previous research, confirming that EMPA, a SGLT2 inhibitor, plays a key role in lowering hyperglycemia. To explore the potential mechanisms by which EMPA protects the retinas of DM mice, we focused on the role of activated gliosis and increased oxidative stress, both of which are associated with retinal damage in DR. Our results showed that GFAP expression, a marker for Müller cell activation18, was elevated in the DM group, but EMPA treatment significantly reduced this expression. Similarly, lipid peroxidation, as indicated by the 4-HNE marker19, was markedly increased in DM mice, but EMPA treatment alleviated this oxidative damage.

DHE staining and subsequent analysis further confirmed that EMPA significantly lowered ROS levels20, highlighting its antioxidant properties. In EMPA-treated mice, the increased expression of oxidative stress-related proteins such as HO-1 and NRF2 suggests that EMPA alleviates oxidative damage by upregulating antioxidant defenses. However, further studies are required to definitively establish the causal link between these changes and retinal protection. Mitochondrial damage is another key feature of DR21. EMPA treatment reversed the reduced expression of the mitochondrial marker TOM20 in diabetic mice, suggesting that EMPA may improve mitochondrial homeostasis. However, further investigation into mitochondrial function, such as ATP production or membrane potential, is needed to confirm the functional recovery of mitochondria. This is particularly significant, as mitochondrial dysfunction is closely linked to the progression of diabetic retinopathy. Although EMPA improved retinal oxidative stress, structure, and function in diabetic mice, these effects were accompanied by reduced blood glucose levels. Therefore, we cannot determine whether the retinal benefits are independent of glycemic control. As no comparator glucose-lowering treatment was included, it remains unclear whether similar effects could be achieved with other antidiabetic therapies. Further studies with glycemia-matched controls are needed to distinguish glucose-dependent from drug-specific effects.

A key finding of our study is that EMPA helps restore retinal structural integrity and intercellular junctions. In diabetic mice, the expression of retinal structural proteins, such as ZO-1, Occludin, and Vimentin, was significantly reduced, reflecting disruption of retinal barrier and intercellular junctions. However, EMPA treatment significantly restored the expression levels of these proteins, which are essential for maintaining the integrity of retinal cells and preventing vascular leakage. This suggests that EMPA may improve retinal barrier function and contribute to the overall protection of retinal tissue in DR.

Conclusions

In summary, EMPA treatment was associated with reduced hyperglycemia and improvements in multiple markers of retinal injury in STZ-induced diabetic mice. These effects were accompanied by reduced oxidative stress and preservation of retinal structure and function. However, the contribution of glycemic control cannot be excluded, and further studies are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms and clinical relevance.

Materials and methods

Study ethics declarations

Animal care and interventions were undertaken in accordance with the National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Experimental Animals, and the animal protocol (ZJCLA- IACUC-20030153) was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of the Hangzhou Medical College Laboratory Animal Center. The study was carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Experimental animal and study design

Seven-week-old male mice were purchased from the Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free animal facility at 22 ± 2 °C with 50–60% humidity, on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. The animals were randomly assigned to three experimental groups: (1) control (CR) group which received a normal diet and intraperitoneal injections of vehicle (2% DMSO in 0.9% saline); (2) diabetes mellitus (DM) group, which was fed a high-fat diet (HFD) and induced with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) by five consecutive daily intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ; 40 mg/kg), followed by vehicle (2% DMSO in 0.9% saline) treatment; and (3) DM + EMPA group, which was treated in the same manner as the DM group but received empagliflozin (EMPA) treatment.

The HFD (60% fat, 20% protein and 20% carbohydrates) was purchased from Fanbo biology. To induce T2DM, STZ (40 mg/kg) was administered via intraperitoneal injection for five consecutive days to the mice in the DM and DM + EMPA groups. EMPA (MedChemExpress, USA) was dissolved in vehicle (2% DMSO in 0.9% saline) and administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day for 8 weeks. Diabetes was diagnosed by tail vein blood glucose measurements using a glucometer, with T2DM defined as blood glucose levels exceeding 20 mmol/L. Blood glucose levels and body weight were routinely monitored throughout the study.

Histology and immunofluorescence of Retina

Mice were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (1%, 70 mg/kg body weight, Sigma-Aldrich, P3761) prior to euthanasia, followed by cervical dislocation, then the eyeballs of the mice were surgically excised and immediately fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The retina tissue was then embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 6 μm slices. The sections were subsequently stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) and observed under a microscope. For immunofluorescence staining, the following steps were performed: deparaffinization and gradient alcohol treatment, antigen retrieval using EDTA, blocking with a blocking solution at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with a specific primary antibody. The next day, after washing, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody, stained with DAPI for nuclear labeling, and images were captured using an FV3000 microscope (Olympus, Japan).

The primary antibodies used were TOM20 (ABclonal, A19403), GFAP (Proteintech, 16825-1-AP), 4-HNE (Abcam, ab48506), HO-1 (ABclonal, A27713), Anti-RBPMS (Abcam, ab152101), ZO-1 (Proteintech, 21773-1-AP), Occludin (Proteintech, 27260-1-AP), Vimentin (Proteintech, 10366-1-AP).

Western blot

Extract retinal proteins from mice and determine the protein concentration using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Measure protein content with antibodies for ACSL4 (Servicebio, O60488), HO-1 (ABclonal, A27713), NRF2 (Proteintech, 16396-1-AP), TOM20 (ABclonal, A19403), and β-Actin (Beyotime, P0970M). Protein bands are visualized using the enhanced ECL reagent kit (Biosharp, China) on a Bio-Rad imaging system, and the data are analyzed with ImageJ software.

Retinal photography and function evaluation

In all in vivo experiments, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (Sigma-Aldrich, P3761, 70 mg/kg body weight). The pupils were dilated with 1% tropicamide, and the corneal surface was anesthetized and moistened with 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops.

For fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA), mice were intraperitoneally injected with 0.1 mL of 10% fluorescein sodium immediately before imaging (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA). FFA images were acquired at 5–10 min after fluorescein injection, corresponding to the late phase of angiography as defined in previous studies22,23. The retinal function was assessed using electroretinography (ERG), captured by an electrophysiological diagnostic system (ROLAND Consult, Germany). Mice were dark-adapted overnight before testing. Dim red light was used during the procedure. A reference electrode was inserted subcutaneously in the oral region, and a ground electrode was inserted subcutaneously in the tail. Two ring electrodes were placed on the cornea of the mice. Visual ERG responses were recorded at stimulation intensities of 0.01, 0.1, 1, 3, and 10 cd.s/m². The amplitudes of the a-wave, b-wave, and oscillatory potentials (OPs) were analyzed as indicators of retinal function. Statistical analysis was performed on the amplitudes of the a-wave, b-wave, and OPs.

Dihydroethidium (DHE) detection

Frozen retinal sections were incubated with DHE (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark and subsequently restained with DAPI for 10 min. The images were captured using a confocal microscope (FV3000).

TUNEL detection

Detection of cell apoptosis in the mouse retina using TUNEL staining. According to the instructions, add the TUNEL reaction mixture containing TdT enzyme and fluorescently labeled dUTP (C1089, Beyotime, China) to the sections, and incubate at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Stain with DAPI at 37 °C (C0002, Beyotime, China), and subsequently collect images using a fluorescence microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan) in the 594 nm channel.

Data analysis

Statistical data were expressed as mean ± SD. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the main effects of time and treatment group (Control, DM, DM+EMPA). Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test for significant effects or interactions. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons at individual time points. P < 0.05 was considered significant. All the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism.
