Systematic evaluation of porcine platelet lysate as a fetal bovine serum substitute: enhancing cell proliferation, maintaining differentiation potential, and ensuring immunophenotypic stability
Authors: Siqing Chen, Xiaotian Chen, Shijie Meng, Qiang Yuan, Huiling Wu, Weihang Hu, Li Zhou, Letian Shan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study systematically evaluated the potential of porcine platelet-lysate (pPL) as a fetal bovine serum (FBS) substitute in cell culture. By culturing tumor cells, adult cells, and stem cells, it analyzed the differences and advantages of pPL over FBS.MethodspPL was prepared using the freeze - thaw method. Its biological effects were assessed by cell counting, LDH cytotoxicity assays, flow cytometry, and adipogenic/osteogenic/chondrogenic differentiation experiments, combined with qPCR and histological staining.ResultspPL significantly promoted the proliferation of A375 and HDF cells, with much lower LDH release than the FBS group, indicating less cellular stress. Flow cytometry showed that MSCs in the pPL group stably expressed mesenchymal markers CD73 ⁺ /CD90 ⁺ (>95%) and had lowCD34 ⁻ (＜2％), meeting ISCT standards. Differentiation experiments revealed that the pPL group had better osteogenic differentiation efficiency, similar chondrogenic ability, and slightly lower but normal adipogenic differentiation compared to the FBS group.ConclusionspPL effectively supports the in vitro expansion of various cells, helps maintain the immunophenotypic stability of MSCs, and shows superiority in osteogenic differentiation. With its unique “proliferation-first, differentiation-controllable” advantage from a natural growth factor network, pPL offers a new approach for developing FBS substitutes.

Background

Over the past few years, the cell-based industry, with mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) technology at its core, has witnessed rapid growth and demonstrated significant application potential across biomedical research, drug development, and regenerative medicine. As a critical enabling technology, cell culture systems fundamentally determine bioprocessing outcomes through their impacts on cellular viability, phenotypic stability, and manufacturing scalability. For decades, fetal bovine serum (FBS) has served as the gold standard culture supplement due to its empirically demonstrated mitogenic properties and nutrient density. However, accumulating evidence reveals significant limitations compromising its suitability for modern bioproduction requirements. First, FBS has a complex and ill-defined composition, leading to batch-to-batch variability. Furthermore, unidentified small molecules, such as endogenous metabolites, may interfere with cell function and compromise the reproducibility of experimental results [1, 2]. Second, potential pathogen contamination, including mycoplasma, poses a risk [3, 4]. This contamination may disrupt cell phenotype stability, thereby restricting FBS’s application in clinical cell therapy [5–7]. Moreover, FBS’s supply and cost-related challenges also hinder its use in large-scale production [8]. Against this backdrop, developing a stable, safe, and economical serum substitute to replace FBS has become of paramount importance for the sustainable development of the cell-based industry.

Numerous FBS alternatives have been investigated in recent years [9]. These include chemically defined media, plant/yeast hydrolysates, recombinant human-derived protein complexes, and platelet-derived products. By accurately formulating nutrients, hormones, and growth factors, chemically defined media can standardize animal-component-free culture, markedly decreasing batch variation and pathogen risks [10]. It can replace FBS in stem cell expansion and sustain cell pluripotency [11]. Nevertheless, its high cost and cell type-specific selectivity hinder large-scale production [12]. Plant/yeast hydrolysates (like algal extracts and rapeseed protein) can provide low-cost plant-derived nutrients and support cell proliferation in low-serum conditions. But the absence of key growth factors (such as IGF) can undermine cell differentiation potential [13–15]. Recombinant protein complexes, which combine growth factors like PDGF and VEGF to replicate the physiological environment, show high proliferation efficiency in CAR-T cell culture. However, the high production cost of recombinant proteins restricts clinical application [16]. Most synthetic media fail to fully replicate serum’s complex nutritional network, leading to lower cell proliferation rates or impaired differentiation potential [17].

As a platelet-derived biological supplement, platelet lysate (PL), loaded with diverse growth factors, has drawn increasing attention. It effectively enhances cell proliferation and preserves stem cell characteristics by supplying these factors along with extracellular matrix components. Comparative studies demonstrate that human PL (hPL) not only enhances MSC proliferation rates compared to FBS but also better maintains immunomodulatory properties and exosome secretory functions critical for regenerative applications [18–20]. Porcine PL (pPL), with easily accessible raw materials and 85% homology with human growth factors, is an emerging alternative. Studies show it can be used for cell proliferation and product expression research [21, 22]. Nevertheless, critical challenges impede PL standardization for industrial adoption. Current limitations span three dimensions: (1) Insufficient characterization of species-specific efficacy variations, with over 80% of published data focused on PL systems [23]; (2) Unresolved mechanistic insights into PL’s bidirectional differentiation regulation, particularly regarding TGF-β/Smad pathway dynamics and epigenetic modifications during osteo/chondrogenic induction [24–26]; (3) Process-induced variability in growth factor profiles and residual fibrinogen-mediated medium gelation requiring anticoagulant supplementation. To address these limitations, this study compares pPL and FBS’s efficacy, evaluates their effects on cell proliferation and immunophenotype, and investigates their impacts on xenogeneic MSCs’ differentiation regulation. Also, based on existing PL product process defects, this study conducts key indicators’ quality control in pPL and monitors safety through continuous subculturing of different cells, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for PL’s standardized application.

Methods

Reagents and materials

Fetal bovine serum was purchased from Cell Max (London, USA). Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Basal Media (Shanghai, China). 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA, Penicillin-Streptomycin, and co-massive (Bradford) protein assay kit were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (MA, USA). DMEM/HIGH GLUCOSE culture medium was purchased from Cytiva (Shanghai, China). Human mesenchymal stem cell serum-free basal medium was purchased from TBD (Tianjin, China). The Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Cytotoxicity Assay Kit was purchased from DOJINDO (Kyushu, Japan). Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell differentiation kits for adipogenesis, osteogenesis, and chondrogenesis were purchased from OriCell (Guangzhou, China). Beyopure™ ultrapure water was purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). HiScript II 2-step RT-PCR reverse transcription kit was purchased from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). Real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (qPCR) kits and TRIzol reagent were purchased from TaKaRa Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Dalian, China). PCR primers and Water-DEPC treated water were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Mouse anti-human CD34-PE and mouse anti-human CD73-PE were purchased from BD Biosciences (Becton, USA). PE anti-human CD90 (Thy1) was purchased from Biolegend (California, USA). All male Bama pigs were purchased from Guangdong Mingzhu Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (No. SCXK (Yue) 2022-0061, China).

Preparation and quality control of pPL

Peripheral blood from 6-week-old female Bama pigs (n = 3) was collected and placed in a 50 ml centrifuge tube containing 4% sodium citrate anticoagulant (whole blood: 4% sodium citrate = 9:1). The sample was left at 4 °C for 2 h and then centrifuged at 185×g, 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant (PRP) was transferred into a clean 15 ml centrifuge tube and subjected to freeze-thaw cycles [27] (stored overnight at -80 °C, then thawed in a 37 °C water bath, repeated three times) to obtain pPL. For cell experiments, the pPL was inactivated with the following steps: incubating in a 56 °C water bath for 30 min (shaking gently every 5 min to ensure even heating). After inactivation, the sample was centrifuged at 2000×g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter for sterile processing and aliquoted according to experimental needs before being stored at -20 °C. The work has been reported in line with the Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0.

Platelet-derived growth factor-AA (PDGF-AA), platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB), and platelet-derived growth factor-AB (PDGF-AB) concentrations in the samples were measured using a porcine ELISA kit (Dogesce, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Tex Laboratories, CA, USA). The concentration of each growth factor in the samples was calculated by constructing a standard curve. The total protein concentration in the samples was determined using a Pierce detergent-compatible Bradford assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cells and cell cultures

Human malignant melanoma cells (A375) and Human dermal fibroblasts (HDF) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSCs) were provided by the Preparation Center of Hangzhou Sanjiang Shangyu Biotechnology Co. Ltd (The Cell Resource Bank and Integrated Cell Preparation Center of Xiaoshan District, Hangzhou Regional Cell Preparation Center). A375 and HDF cells were cultured in cell culture dishes (Thermo, MA, USA) with DMEM medium supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin. In this experiment, DMEM was supplemented with 5% and 10% pPL or 10% FBS. The cells were cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator, with the medium replaced twice a week. Stem cells were cultured in human mesenchymal stem cell serum-free basic medium containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin. For subsequent differentiation and immunophenotyping experiments, 5% and 10% pPL or 10% FBS were added to the medium.

Cell viability assay

A375 and HDF cells were seeded in 24-well plates containing 5% pPL, 10% pPL, and 10% FBS basal medium, with a cell density of 0.5 × 104 cells per well. On Day 3, Day 7, and Day 10, the culture supernatants were collected. The cell growth and morphological changes were observed under a light microscope at 10× magnification. Photos were taken for record-keeping. Afterward, cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and counted.

LDH release assay

The lactate dehydrogenase content released into the culture medium was measured to assess cell damage, which in turn reflects cell viability. Cells from each group were cultured continuously for 10 days, with 500 µl of supernatant collected on Day 3, Day 7, and Day 10 (stored at -80 °C), for a total of 3 time points. The LDH release was measured using an LDH detection kit, and the relative cell viability (100-cell damage rate) was calculated as a percentage.

Flow cytometry analysis

Immunophenotyping was analyzed using a fluorescence-activated cell sorter (FACS) Canto II (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) with the following antibodies: Mouse anti-human CD34-PE (1:50), CD73-PE (1:50), and CD90-PE (1:100). Cells were harvested by digestion with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, washed 2–3 times with PBS, and stained at 4 °C for 20 min, with gentle shaking every 5 min. Afterward, cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed on the flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using BD Accuri C6 software (Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Differentiation assay

The differentiation potential of hucMSCs into adipocytes, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes was tested using specific differentiation kits for adipogenesis, osteogenesis, and chondrogenesis. Each differentiation medium was supplemented with 5% pPL, 10% pPL, and 10% FBS. Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 in 6-well plates and underwent adipogenesis, osteogenesis, and chondrogenesis according to the instructions provided by the differentiation kits. After 3 weeks of differentiation, cells were stained and observed under a light microscope, with images taken for record-keeping.

Quantification of staining

After staining, a quantitative analysis of hucMSCs differentiation was performed to assess adipogenesis, osteogenesis, and chondrogenesis. Oil Red O Staining (Adipogenesis): Oil Red O was used to confirm the presence of lipid droplets. Cells were washed once with PBS, and 500 µl isopropanol was added to each well. The cells were incubated at room temperature for 10 min, followed by measurement of optical density at 520 nm to quantify the lipid content. Alizarin Red Staining (Osteogenesis): Alizarin Red was used to detect calcium deposition. After washing the cells once with PBS, 500 µl of 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (dissolved in deionized water) was added. Cells were incubated at room temperature for 15 min, and optical density was measured at 562 nm to quantify calcium deposits. Alcian Blue Staining (Chondrogenesis): Alcian Blue was used to assess the formation of cartilage matrix. Image J software was employed to quantitatively analyze the stained area.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis (qRT-PCR)

After the differentiation of hucMSCs, total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent. The RNA quality was assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Then, 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR. For the qPCR reaction, a final volume of 20 µl was used, containing: 10 µl SYBR Green (2×), 0.4 µl forward PCR primer, 0.4 µl reverse PCR primer, 1 µl cDNA template (0.25 µg), 8.2 µl ddH2O. The real-time PCR program consisted of the following steps:

Initial denaturation: 95 °C for 1 min (1 cycle).

Amplification: 95 °C for 15 s, followed by 60 °C for 1 min, for 40 cycles.

Dissociation curve: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, 95 °C for 15 s (1 cycle).

Each sample was run in triplicate. β-ACTIN was used as the internal reference gene. The mRNA expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All results were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8. For comparisons between two groups, a t-test was used; for comparisons involving two or more groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The beneficial effect of pPL on the proliferation of cells cultured in vitro

pPL contains a spectrum of bioactive factors, including platelet-derived growth factors (PDGF-AA, -AB, -BB), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), basic fibroblast growth factor (b-FGF), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [19, 28]. To evaluate the FBS-substitution potential and broad applicability of pPL in supporting diverse cell types [29], A375 melanoma cells and HDFs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 5% pPL, 10% pPL, or 10% FBS as a positive control.

Over a 10-day observation period, morphological observation revealed distinct cellular responses (Figs. 1a and 2a). FBS-treated cells exhibited significant cellular hypertrophy, whereas pPL-treated groups maintained stable cellular morphology and size. Quantitative proliferation assays demonstrated dose-dependent enhancement of growth kinetics in pPL-supplemented media. For A375 cells, proliferation rates in 5% pPL (10.50 ± 0.50 × 10⁴ cells) and 10% pPL (13.43 ± 0.60 × 10⁴ cells) markedly exceeded those in FBS (6.17 ± 1.04 × 10⁴ cells) (Fig. 1b). Similarly, HDFs achieved optimal growth in 10% pPL (4.30 ± 0.30 × 10⁴ cells), surpassing both 5% pPL (2.27 ± 0.25 × 10⁴ cells) and FBS (1.13 ± 0.32 × 10⁴ cells) (Fig. 2b).

LDH release assays corroborated these proliferative trends (Figs. 1c and 2c). A375 cells in FBS exhibited significantly elevated LDH levels by day 10 (p < 0.0001). HDFs in FBS showed progressive LDH accumulation, peaking at day 7 (p < 0.0001) before partial resolution by day 10, likely reflecting entry into growth plateau. Notably, pPL-treated groups maintained sustained low baseline LDH values across both cell types, indicative of superior biocompatibility.

Collectively, pPL not only supported robust in vitro expansion of multiple cell lineages but also demonstrated significantly enhanced proliferative efficacy compared to conventional FBS-based systems. The pronounced LDH elevation in FBS groups further suggests heightened cellular stress under FBS-supplemented conditions, whereas pPL’s minimal cytotoxicity underscores its potential as a versatile FBS alternative.

Impact of pPL on the surface immune phenotype of HucMSCs

HucMSCs are a kind of adult stem cells with self-renewal and multi-differentiation potential, widely used in developmental biology research, disease model construction, and regenerative medicine. However, the in-vitro culture of hucMSCs depends on a strictly optimized culture medium system to maintain their survival rate, proliferation activity, and differentiation characteristics [30]. The selection of serum supplements is crucial for cell phenotype stability. Given that surface markers of hucMSCs are easily affected by the culture environment, this study systematically assessed the impact of different concentrations of pPL (5%, 10%) and 10% FBS on the immune phenotype of hucMSCs through flow cytometry, aiming to verify the biosafety of pPL as a FBS substitute.

Based on the MSC identification standards set by the International Society for Cellular Therapy (ISCT) and the International Federation for Adipose Therapeutics and Science (IFATS) [31], the experiment focused on detecting the core phenotype markers of hucMSCs. After two weeks of continuous culture, flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 3) showed that in all experimental groups (including 5% pPL, 10% pPL and 10% FBS), hucMSCs stably expressed typical mesenchymal markers CD73 (ecto-5’-nucleotidase) and CD90 (Thy-1 antigen), with a positivity rate of over 95% (no significant difference between groups, p >0.05). Meanwhile, CD34, associated with hematopoietic progenitor cells and endothelial cells, was negatively expressed in all groups (positivity rate < 2%), and the levels were consistent across groups. These results indicate that replacing traditional FBS with pPL does not cause a shift in the expression pattern or abnormal expression levels of surface markers on hucMSCs.

Furthermore, this study confirms that pPL not only supports the in-vitro expansion of hucMSCs but also completely preserves their immune phenotype characteristics. This feature is crucial for maintaining the therapeutic potential of MSCs, such as their immune-modulatory function and homing ability.

Oil Red O staining revealed that hucMSCs in all experimental groups could form typical lipid droplets, but the FBS group had higher lipid accumulation than the pPL-treated groups (Fig. 4a). qPCR further indicated that the expression levels of adipogenic key regulatory genes (FABP4, PPARG) in the differentiated groups were upregulated compared to the undifferentiated group on day 21 (p < 0.0001). The FBS group had a higher gene expression peak than the 5% pPL group (p < 0.01), suggesting FBS may more strongly activate adipogenic differentiation pathways (Fig. 4c).

After 21 days of induction, Alizarin Red staining confirmed extracellular calcium deposition and matrix mineralization in all groups (Fig. 5a). Quantitative analysis showed that the pPL groups had significantly larger calcified nodule areas than the FBS group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5b). Molecular analysis revealed that osteogenic marker genes (OPN, ALP) were significantly upregulated in all groups after induction. The 5% pPL group showed the strongest activation: OPN expression was 1.7 times higher than in the FBS group (p < 0.01), and ALP expression was 2.7 times higher (p < 0.001). The 10% pPL group also maintained higher expression levels than the FBS group (Fig. 5c).

Alcian Blue staining showed that both pPL and FBS groups effectively promoted glycosaminoglycan deposition, with no significant differences in staining intensity (p > 0.05) (Fig. 6a and b). However, qPCR revealed differential expression patterns of chondrogenic regulatory genes (SOX9, COL2A1). The 5% pPL group had SOX9 expression 1.6 times higher than the 10% FBS group (p < 0.05), while the 10% pPL group showed no significant difference from the FBS group (p > 0.05). The 10% pPL group had the lowest COL2A1 expression (1.7 times lower than FBS, p < 0.05), while the 5% pPL group had a similar expression to the FBS group (p > 0.05).

This study confirms that pPL preserves the multilineage differentiation potential of hucMSCs. Its osteogenic and chondrogenic induction efficiency matches FBS, though adipogenic differentiation intensity is slightly lower than traditional serum systems. However, there is no directional shift or functional defect in differentiation.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of pPL as a functional alternative to FBS in supporting the culture and differentiation of MSCs. Key findings demonstrate that pPL maintains phenotypic stability in MSCs, preserving surface marker expression (CD73⁺ >98%, CD90⁺ >95%, CD34⁻ <2%) without altering marker expression patterns, thereby establishing its efficacy as a FBS substitute (Fig. 3). Multilineage differentiation assays further confirm that pPL retains the differentiation potential of MSCs, with adipogenic and chondrogenic induction efficiencies comparable to FBS, and no directional bias or functional defects observed (Figs. 4 and 6). Additionally, pPL exhibits superior characteristics over FBS, including enhanced in vitro expansion of multiple cell types and reduced cellular stress, as evidenced by consistently lower baseline LDH levels in pPL-treated groups, indicative of reduced membrane damage and superior biocompatibility (Figs. 1 and 2). In osteogenic differentiation, pPL-treated groups demonstrated significantly larger calcified nodule areas and upregulated expression of osteogenic marker genes, highlighting its enhanced osteogenic induction capacity (Fig. 5). Notably, pPL exhibited no limitations in supporting cell proliferation, maintaining phenotypic stability, or regulating differentiation, with performance meeting or exceeding traditional serum systems. The study’s innovations include: (1) systematic validation of pPL as a multifunctional FBS substitute, demonstrating its broad applicability and concentration-dependent effects across diverse cell types; (2) unique maintenance of MSC immunophenotypic stability, addressing limitations of traditional serum systems and mitigating immunogenic risks associated with FBS; and (3) clarification of pPL’s differential regulatory effects on multilineage differentiation, revealing an “osteogenic advantage effect” that provides novel strategies for targeted differentiation in regenerative medicine. This study provides robust experimental evidence for pPL’s application in cell culture and offers critical insights for selecting FBS substitutes in cellular systems.

Recent studies highlight that pPL and its bioactive microenvironment, enriched with diverse growth factors and signaling molecules, synergistically drive cell proliferation by activating critical signaling pathways (Table 2) [19]. PDGF, a key mitogen in pPL, activates the PDGFR-β receptor, stimulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis. This upregulates Cyclin D1/CDK4 complex expression to promote G1/S phase transition while inhibiting pro-apoptotic Bax activity, thereby enhancing cell proliferation [32–34]. FGF-2 maintains proliferative homeostasis by suppressing Twist2 and Spry4 expression, preventing excessive ERK pathway activation [35]. Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) enhances ribosomal biogenesis and protein translation via the IGF-1R/PI3K pathway and upregulates anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 through mTORC1 [36]. In hepatic repair, IGF-1 synergizes with HGF to drive hepatic stem cell differentiation and tissue regeneration [37]. EGF promotes proliferation via ERK1/2 signaling [38], while excessive p38 MAPK activation induces cellular senescence or differentiation [39]. TGF-β exhibits concentration-dependent regulation: low concentrations enhance cell survival via the TAK1-MAPK pathway, while high concentrations inhibit Wnt/β-catenin signaling through Smad2/3 activation, balancing proliferation and differentiation [40, 41]. Collectively, pPL’s multifactorial composition creates a synergistic pro-proliferative microenvironment, activating key pathways (e.g., PI3K/AKT, MAPK) and modulating gene expression to amplify its effects on cell proliferation.

Summary of the mechanisms of major bioactive factors in PL on stem cells

PL orchestrates differential regulation of stem cell differentiation through factor combinations, exhibiting concentration-dependent and cell type-specific mechanisms. Members of the TGF-β family regulate multilineage differentiation via Smad signaling: TGF-β1 promotes osteogenic differentiation by upregulating RUNX2 expression through the Smad2/3/4 complex [42–44], while TGF-β3 drives chondrogenesis via the Smad1/5 pathway [45, 46]. Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) are central drivers of osteogenesis. Heterodimers of BMP-2/6 activate the ALK3 receptor, triggering SMAD1/5/8 and p38 MAPK pathways, thereby upregulating osteogenic markers Runx2 and Osterix and promoting calcified nodule formation [47, 48]. The BMP-2/6-Wnt/β-catenin crosstalk enhances osteogenic efficiency by enabling SMAD1/5/8 to directly bind β-catenin, facilitating its transcriptional complex formation with TCF4 [49]. VEGF, beyond its angiogenic role, promotes adipogenic differentiation of MSCs via PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2 signaling [50, 51], while also regulating vascular cell differentiation from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) through the VEGFR2/STAT3 axis [52]. Hypoxia enhances osteogenic differentiation and bone defect healing via the VEGF/STAT3 pathway [53]. Epigenetic regulation further modulates differentiation: TGF-β1 suppresses promoter demethylation of differentiation-related genes through Smad3-DNMT3A interactions [54, 55], while PDGF-activated PI3K/AKT signaling enhances histone acetyltransferase (HAT) activity to promote chromatin opening of proliferation-related genes. TGF-β maintains silencing of differentiation-inhibiting genes by recruiting histone deacetylases (HDACs) [56]. These mechanisms collectively ensure precise regulation of stem cell differentiation through distinct signaling pathways, supporting tissue development and functional homeostasis. PL creates a unique microenvironment characterized by “proliferation prioritization with controllable differentiation,” leveraging multi-factor synergy, metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic regulation. This dynamic interplay highlights the complexity of differentiation processes and underscores the regulatory capacity of multi-factor networks in stem cell biology.

While this study evaluated the effects of pPL on cell proliferation, phenotypic maintenance, and differentiation regulation in MSCs, several critical limitations warrant consideration. First, pPL production involves complex processes, including platelet collection, lysis, and filtration, which require precise experimental operations and stringent quality control. These steps increase both cost and time investment. Second, its porcine-derived growth factors (e.g., PDGF, TGF-β) pose potential risks of host immune reactions. Additionally, the risk of porcine endogenous retrovirus (PERV) contamination remains a biosafety concern. While prior quality control assessments of pPL were conducted [60], detailed profiling of growth factors and comparisons with recombinant analogs were not performed. To advance pPL as a viable FBS substitute, these limitations must be resolved. Future research priorities include: (1) optimizing pPL production protocols to enhance stability and consistency; (2) conducting comprehensive growth factor analyses to elucidate their mechanisms of action; and (3) rigorously evaluating pPL’s biosafety, particularly regarding immunogenicity and PERV risks. These improvements will pave the way for broader applications of pPL in cell culture systems and regenerative medicine.
