Selection of specific and efficient siRNAs in new cellular model for Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome therapy
Authors: Volha Dzianisava, Katarzyna Piekarowicz, Magdalena Machowska, Ryszard Rzepecki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome is a fatal genetic disorder caused by a point mutation in the gene encoding the nuclear envelope protein lamin A/C. The most frequent mutation leads to the synthesis of a shorter version of lamin A named progerin, which accumulates under the nuclear membrane, leading to nuclear lamina disorganization and affecting gene expression and signaling. In our study, we designed a set of small interfering RNA (siRNA) sequences for the selective and efficient downregulation of progerin level and tested them in our new progeria HeLa cellular model and patients' fibroblasts. We showed efficient downregulation of progerin level without affecting endogenous lamin A or lamin C levels. Additionally, we observed an additive effect of the combination of our siRNAs with lonafarnib—the sole drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration for progeria syndrome therapy. The selected siRNAs also worked efficiently in all three tested patient fibroblast lines, even after extended post-transfection incubation with low siRNA doses. Therefore, we believe that the development of genetic drugs may be a promising therapeutic tool for Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome.

Introduction

Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS) is a rare genetic disorder caused by a mutation in the LMNA gene. The LMNA gene encodes structural proteins of the nuclear lamina—lamins A and C. Normally, newly synthesized prelamin A is farnesylated in the cytoplasm at the C terminus and then transported into the nucleus. The farnesyl group anchors lamin A at the inner nuclear membrane, disabling the formation of lamin A polymers,1 the main components of the nuclear lamina that exhibit structural functions and are responsible for the maintenance of nuclear shape.2 This group is subsequently removed by the endoprotease ZMPSTE24 (zinc metallopeptidase STE24). This leads to the release of mature lamin A to the nucleoplasm, where lamin A polymerizes and forms a nuclear lamina. Lamins, in addition to their structural function, participate in DNA organization, signaling, transcriptional regulation, and DNA repair.3,4

In the vast majority of cases, a de novo point mutation arises specifically at position c.1824C>T (p.G608G) in the LMNA gene, which activates a new cryptic splice site in exon 11 of the lamin A coding sequence. As a result, after splicing, mRNA lacks part of exon 11 and encodes a shorter version of lamin A named progerin, which lacks the 50 amino acids necessary for sequence endoprotease recognition. Therefore, ZMPSTE24 cannot cleave progerin, and the post-translational maturation of progerin is disrupted. Thus, progerin remains anchored to the inner nuclear envelope.5,6,7 Progerin accumulation at the nuclear envelope disrupts lamina organization,8 signaling pathway activity,9,10,11,12,13 and chromatin organization14,15 and may lead to increased levels of DNA damage15,16,17,18,19 as well as to abnormal nuclear morphology, most notably observed at late passages of patient fibroblast cultures.20,21

HGPS manifests in children at approximately 2 years of age, and the typical symptoms include impaired growth and weight, alopecia, kidney failure, cardiovascular diseases, and atherosclerosis. The average age of death is 13–14 years, mostly because of cardiovascular problems.22 Currently, clinically approved HGPS therapy focuses on symptomatic treatment. The only drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical use is lonafarnib, which is a nonspecific farnesylation inhibitor that reduces progerin accumulation in the nucleus. Lonafarnib treatment improves patients’ phenotype and increases their lifespan by 5 years.23 Improvements in cardiovascular function and reduced frequencies of clinical strokes, headaches, and seizures, as well as in vascular stiffness, bone structure, and audiological status, were observed.24,25 Nevertheless, lonafarnib does not reduce all symptoms and is not able to restore the normal phenotype. Furthermore, these effects are observed after long-term treatment (>2 years), and treatment is associated with frequent adverse reactions of the gastrointestinal tract and other constitutional effects (fatigue, nausea, and fever).25

Many different nonspecific drugs have been tested for use in the treatment of HGPS, including triple therapy involving lonafarnib, zoledronic acid, and pravastatin26,27; intermittent treatment with lonafarnib and sulforaphane28; and all-trans retinoic acid and rapamycin treatment.29,30 The abovementioned strategies were effective at improving selected markers of the HGPS phenotype. However, the activities of the discussed drugs are not specific to progerin-dependent malfunctioning processes. Rather, these drugs affect the activity and properties of a wide range of proteins and genes, altering cellular metabolism. Therefore, fully predicting the possible side effects of these drugs in patients is difficult. Gene therapy is considered to be the most promising and effective method for treating HGPS because it aims to directly correct the mutation, modify aberrant splicing, or decrease progerin transcript or protein levels.

Various strategies for HGPS gene therapy have already been designed. Some of them were based on gene editing approaches (CRISPR-Cas or adenine base editors)31,32,33 or on RNAi,34,35,36 whereas others demonstrate splice site blocking using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs).37,38,39,40 One of the very first strategies was tested in patients’ fibroblasts; it was based on lentiviral vectors and the overexpression of small hairpin RNA (shRNA) specifically targeting the unspliced progerin pre-mRNA or mature progerin. Ultimately, only one shRNA sequence, which recognizes the junction of progerin mRNA exons 11–12, was chosen. A decrease in the progerin level to 26% of that in nontransfected patients’ fibroblasts and a nonsignificant reduction in the lamin A level were observed.34 Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) have never been used to date in studies aimed at progerin silencing. However, they have several advantages over other tools used in gene therapy; e.g., they exhibit high delivery efficiency and do not require viral vectors that may be potentially oncogenic and trigger an immune response.

Several studies have focused on splice site blocking strategies. Morpholino oligonucleotides, which are resistant to enzymatic degradation, were designed to recognize a mutated sequence in exon 11 of progerin-coding pre-mRNA and act by blocking the access of proteins involved in splicing to the cryptic splice site.37 Morpholino oligonucleotides efficiently downregulate progerin in a mouse model with the mutation LMNAG609G, which corresponds to the human mutation.39 Similar results were obtained in a wild-type mouse model with an additional exogenous insertion of the mutated human LMNAG608G gene, making it more suitable for gene therapy studies.41 Treated mice were characterized by an extended lifespan, a lack of weight loss, and reduced vascular smooth muscle cell (SMC) loss, which are considered phenotypic improvements.38 However, the LMNA expression profile is more complicated in such a mouse model. Specifically, human lamin A, lamin C, and progerin were expressed from the LMNAG608G allele and endogenous mouse lamin A and lamin C were expressed from LMNAwt/wt alleles, affecting the ratio between lamin A and progerin. Similarly, in the homozygous LMNAG608G/G608G mice, the protein ratio was altered.

Other modified oligonucleotides, such as conjugates with a lipid group, were also effective at reducing progerin levels in a mouse LMNAG608G model.40 All the described strategies successively reduced the progerin level, but a simultaneous decrease in the lamin A levels was noted in most studies. Maintaining lamin A level seems to be beneficial because haploinsufficiency of the LMNA gene has been reported in humans.42 These findings suggest that gene therapy strategies in which lamin A downregulation is noted might be suboptimal to achieve full rescue of the HGPS phenotype in patients.43

In most studies, patient fibroblasts have been used for genetic drug selection.32,37,38 However, this model has several limitations, such as a limited amount of patient material, a limited number of passages, and various proliferation rates of donor cells. Additionally, the progerin level and cell morphology may vary depending on the number of passages and donor age.2,13,20,21,44,45,46 A partial solution to these problems is hTERT-immortalized patient fibroblasts.34,40 However, similar to nonmodified fibroblasts, such a model enables only the analysis of endogenous progerin levels, which is a limiting factor for fast, quantitative, and efficient genetic drug screening and selection. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived from HGPS patient cells, which can further differentiate into several cell types, may provide alternative solutions.47 However, these cells are very expensive to generate and differentiate, which may limit their use in drug screenings. Other HGPS cellular models include modified cell lines that overexpress N-terminal-tagged progerin, e.g., normal human dermal fibroblasts,20,48 hTERT-immortalized human dermal fibroblasts,34,48,49 and HeLa and HEK293 cells.18,33,48 Compared with patient-derived cells, such models have relatively high proliferation rates and overexpress progerin with a tag that enables fast and easy identification. However, the progerin level in these cells remains much higher than that observed in patient cells. For this reason, another cellular model with a progerin overexpression level similar to that of endogenous lamin A is needed for genetic drug selection.

In this work, we present the development of a potential RNA-based therapy for HGPS using siRNA. We established two criteria for designing siRNA sequences: the specific downregulation of progerin protein level and the maintenance of lamin A protein level.

This therapy was tested in our new cellular model—HeLa cells with stable, single-gene-copy-based GFP-progerin or GFP-lamin A protein expression at a physiological level similar to the endogenous lamin A level. This model enables fast and efficient screening and selection of siRNA sequences, which is based on quantitative measurements of the fluorescence intensity of treated cells using flow cytometry. The obtained results were validated by western blot analysis. We selected four of the most promising siRNA sequences for progerin downregulation that did not downregulate lamin A level in our HeLa cell model. Three of these siRNAs were also efficient and specific in HGPS patients' fibroblasts. Moreover, in the HeLa cell model, we examined the combination of siRNAs and lonafarnib, a clinically approved farnesylation inhibitor for HGPS therapy, and demonstrated the additive effect of the drugs.

Taken together, the results of the described selection of efficient and specific progerin-silencing siRNAs using a new cellular model demonstrate that such a model has general strong potential for the development of RNA/DNA drugs for genetic disorder therapy in humans. With this model, we were able to select several siRNAs that downregulate progerin levels without significantly decreasing lamin A levels. Selected siRNAs are very promising genetic drugs for the development of HGPS gene therapy.

Results

Development of the HGPS cellular model

In our study, we aimed to design, select, and validate siRNA sequences that can downregulate progerin protein levels without lamin A downregulation. Therefore, a suitable HGPS cellular model for fast, easy, quantitative, and reproducible screening of siRNA sequences is needed. For this purpose, HeLa cells were transduced with retroviral vectors to achieve stable overexpression of GFP (control), GFP-lamin A, or GFP-progerin. The conditions of transduction and selection were adjusted to obtain cells with stable expression of one copy of the transgene (see retrovirus production and cell line preparation in the materials and methods section). The number of transgene copies per cell was assessed using qPCR, confirming that HeLa sublines contain, on average, one copy of the transgene (Figure 1A). Consequently, the expression of the GFP-lamin A and GFP-progerin proteins was similar to that of endogenous lamin A (Figure 1B). We also confirmed the overexpression of GFP-progerin in the developed subline with progerin-specific antibodies (Figure 1B). The use of a GFP tag fused with lamin A and progerin enables the visualization of the presence and localization of overexpressed proteins in living cells (Figure 1C) and the measurement of the GFP fluorescence intensity with flow cytometry (Figure 1D), thereby facilitating the analysis of changes in protein levels using this approach. Design and selection of siRNAs

Developed sublines were used to test and select siRNA sequences designed by our group (see materials and methods and Table S1). To achieve the specific downregulation of progerin, we designed 14 siRNAs whose antisense strands recognize the unique junction of exons 11–12 of progerin mRNA, which is absent in wild-type lamin A/C (Figures 3A and 3B; Table S1). This region consists of an excess of GC pairs and cytosine 5-mers in exon 12, which markedly complicates siRNA design. Prog1 siRNA treatment did not result in any changes in any of the analyzed sublines (Figure 4A). Prog2, Prog3, Prog10, and Prog13 siRNAs decreased the fluorescence intensity of HeLa GFP-progerin cells by 52%–70%, which was not a satisfactory result. Prog4, Prog7, and Prog8 siRNA treatment reduced the fluorescence intensity of HeLa GFP-progerin to 29%–46%, but they also reduced GFP-lamin A levels (Figures 4A and S2). Moreover, we observed an increase in cell mortality (data not shown) in all sublines after Prog4 and Prog8 treatment. The most prominent effect of lowering the fluorescence intensity in HeLa GFP-progerin cells was observed for Prog5, Prog6, Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14, as they reduced the fluorescence intensities to 24%–43%, depending on the sequence, without simultaneously decreasing the GFP-lamin A level. After Prog11 treatment, the fluorescence intensity of the HeLa GFP-progerin cells decreased to 45% and the fluorescence intensity of the HeLa GFP and HeLa GFP-lamin A cells decreased to 73% and 79%, respectively (both statistically insignificant). Therefore, this sequence was not investigated further, as better candidates were prioritized.

Nontreated cells and those treated with the scrambled sequence ([siRNA Scr] designed on the basis of the Prog3 sequence) did not show any significant changes in fluorescence intensity in any of the sublines (Figure 4A). As expected, the maximum decrease in fluorescence intensity to 15% was observed for siRNA lamin A/C (positive control) for both HeLa GFP-lamin A and HeLa GFP-progerin cells. ProgH3 siRNA reduced the fluorescence intensity of HeLa GFP-progerin cells to 65% (statistically significant) and increased the fluorescence in HeLa GFP-lamin A cells to 133%; however, these changes were not statistically significant. Prog9 siRNA increased the fluorescence intensity in HeLa GFP-lamin A cells to 131%. In the HeLa GFP subline, only Prog14 siRNA led to a statistically significant increase in fluorescence intensity to 118%. Nevertheless, we chose to continue evaluation with western blotting.

Next, we used western blotting to analyze the most efficient siRNAs, namely, Prog5, Prog6, Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14, and the less efficient ProgH3, Prog3, and Prog10, to confirm whether the observed moderate reduction in the fluorescence signal correlated with protein-level changes (Figures 4B and 4C). Additionally, we analyzed the siRNAs Prog1, Prog2, and Prog4 using western blotting to provide a broader comparative framework across the designed candidates (Figure S2A). The western blot results were subsequently quantified using densitometry analysis (Figures 4C and S2B).

After siRNA treatment, endogenous lamin A levels remained stable (except for those of Prog4 and lamin A/C). Prog5 siRNA, which was previously selected as one of the most promising on the basis of flow cytometry, reduced the GFP-progerin levels to 63% and GFP-lamin A to 76%. Prog9 also reduced the level of GFP-progerin to 63%, but the level of GFP-lamin A decreased to only 83%. The siRNAs Prog6 and Prog14 reduced GFP-progerin to 33% and 29%, respectively, and GFP-lamin A level to 73% and 70%, respectively. Finally, Prog12 showed the most promising result, with a decrease in GFP-progerin to 44% and a decrease in GFP-lamin A level to 87%. Thus, Prog12 was chosen as the best based on our design criteria, achieving an efficient decrease in the GFP-progerin level with minimal impact on the GFP-lamin A level.

The decrease in GFP-progerin level after treatment with the chosen siRNAs was confirmed. Combination of siRNA and lonafarnib treatment

After promising results were obtained with the designed siRNAs, we analyzed the effects of their combination with lonafarnib, which is a farnesylation inhibitor and is currently the sole drug approved by the FDA for clinical use in the treatment of HGPS.55 We chose the most effective siRNAs, Prog6, Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14, to be tested together with lonafarnib. The treatment conditions and concentrations of lonafarnib and the siRNAs were adapted for combination effect analysis, which assumes no more than a 50% decrease in fluorescence intensity in the HeLa GFP-progerin subline when the drugs were used alone (see materials and methods section). After the cells were treated with siRNA and/or lonafarnib, the fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry (Figure 6). In the HeLa GFP-lamin A subline, treatment with 2 μM lonafarnib alone reduced the fluorescence intensity to 76% and, in combination with siRNAs, to 73%–79%. Thus, no significant differences were detected between the fluorescence intensity of HeLa GFP-lamin A cells treated with lonafarnib alone and those treated with the combination of lonafarnib and siRNAs. In the HeLa GFP-progerin subline, treatment with lonafarnib alone led to a significant decrease in the fluorescence intensity to 84%. Treatment with siRNAs alone under conditions adjusted for this experiment resulted in decreases in fluorescence intensity to 57% (Prog6), 76% (Prog9), 64% (Prog12), and 59% (Prog14). Moreover, the fluorescence intensity in cells treated with Prog6 siRNA followed by DMSO (lonafarnib solvent) was reduced to 54%. The combination of siRNAs and lonafarnib treatment reduced the fluorescence intensity in HeLa cells expressing GFP-progerin to 46% (Prog6 and lonafarnib), 64% (Prog9 and lonafarnib), 55% (Prog12 and lonafarnib), and 50% (Prog14 and lonafarnib), representing a statistically significant difference for all siRNAs except for Prog14, compared with the effect of siRNA treatment alone. We also observed significant changes in fluorescence intensity (to 87%–92%) in the HeLa GFP subline after treatment with a combination of Prog6 siRNA and DMSO as well as a negative control siRNA and DMSO or lonafarnib, indicating that the decrease may reflect general cellular stress or nonspecific cellular responses rather than specific silencing effects.

We calculated the combination index (CI) for siRNAs and lonafarnib to determine the characteristics of their interaction (synergy, the combined effect of the two drugs is greater than the sum of their individual effects; additivity, the combined effect of the two drugs is equal to the sum of their individual effects; or antagonism, the combined effect is less than the expected sum of their individual effects) using the Bliss independence model given that the drugs act independently.56 The results indicated that the combination of lonafarnib and siRNA Prog6 (CI = 1.025 [±0.030]), Prog9 (CI = 1.056 [±0.159]), Prog12 (CI = 1.066 [±0.144]), and Prog14 (CI = 1.034 [±0.120]) had an additive effect on the GFP-progerin cell line, as the effect of the drug combination was not significantly different from the sum of the effects caused by independent drug treatment (CI = 1 implies an additive effect, CI > 1 indicates synergy, and CI < 1 indicates antagonism).

The flow cytometry results were comparable for all the siRNAs; thus, we performed western blot analysis for only one representative siRNA, Prog6 (Figure S5). We noted that lonafarnib treatment in all sublines (untransduced, with GFP, GFP-lamin A, and GFP-progerin overexpression) resulted in an additional band above endogenous lamin A corresponding to prelamin A (Figure S5). A distinct decrease in the GFP-progerin level was detected after Prog6 treatment and after treatment with Prog6 and lonafarnib.

We did not observe any changes in NPC localization or nuclear morphology after lonafarnib treatment in any of the cell lines, as determined by immunofluorescence analysis (Figure S6). Simultaneously, we noted the relocalization of lamin B1 from the nuclear envelope to the nucleoplasm, as was expected after lonafarnib treatment (Figure S6). The localization of lamin C after lonafarnib treatment was not affected (Figure S7).

Progerin downregulation by siRNAs in patients' fibroblasts

To evaluate the usefulness of our siRNAs for HGPS therapy, we analyzed the efficiency of progerin downregulation in HGPS patients' fibroblasts. We chose the siRNAs Prog6, Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14 because they were the most efficient at downregulating GFP-progerin in the HeLa cellular model while simultaneously minimally lowering the level of GFP-lamin A.

To determine the optimal conditions for HGPS fibroblast transfection, cells were treated with various concentrations of Prog9 siRNA and progerin levels were analyzed by western blotting (Figure S8A). Even the lowest tested concentration (0.47 nM) led to a decrease in the progerin level to 48% of that of the nontreated control (the other concentrations led to a similar decrease); however, the efficiency of siRNA transfection differed across the concentrations (Figure S8B). Moreover, the lamin A level was reduced to only 71%–90%. The fibroblast proliferation rate was not affected by treatment with Prog9 or lamin A/C-Cy3 siRNAs (Figures S8C and S8D).

To obtain enhanced effects and clear measurements of progerin levels and to limit the possibility of toxicity, we performed a double transfection with the siRNA concentration reduced to 2 nM (in comparison to HeLa treatment) and extended the incubation time after treatment (Figure 7A), as the fibroblast doubling time is significantly lower than that in HeLa cells. Lamin A levels were unaffected in the cells from healthy donors, except for the CTRL368 cells treated with Prog6, in which the lamin A level was reduced to 71% (Figure 7B). Surprisingly, in HGPS patient cell lines, Prog6 decreased lamin A levels to 43%–58%, and the progerin levels was reduced to 17%–30%, depending on the cell line (Figure 7C). Compared with Scr, the other tested siRNAs, including Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14, did not affect lamin A levels in patient fibroblasts and efficiently decreased progerin levels to 39%–55% (Prog9), 27%–42% (Prog12), and 38%–43% (Prog14). ProgH3 appeared to efficiently decrease progerin levels in all three patient cell lines to 46%–56% despite its inefficiency in the HeLa cell line, as determined by western blot analysis. The results confirmed the efficiency and specificity of the Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14 siRNAs developed with a HeLa-based model in an HGPS patient-derived model system.

Off-target activity of siRNAs

We performed an in silico analysis of the potential off-targets for all the siRNAs tested in this study (Table S2) using BLAST-based alignments, as described in the materials and methods section. As expected, both progerin and lamin A transcripts were identified among the predicted targets, confirming the intended sequence complementarity. The number of predicted off-targets ranged from 13 transcripts originating from 3 genes for both strands of the negative control siRNA to 99 transcripts corresponding to 17 genes for both strands of the lamin A/C siRNA. Among the siRNAs designed to target progerin mRNA, the lowest number of predicted targets was observed for siRNA Prog6, with 30 transcripts targeting 11 genes. The greatest number was found for the Prog4 siRNA, with 74 potential target transcripts originating from 20 genes (Table S2; Figure S9).

On the basis of the literature, alignments with a query coverage less than 78% are unlikely to represent meaningful interactions and can be excluded.57 Applying this threshold and omitting alignments for LMNA, we identified 29 potential off-target sequences corresponding to 12 genes (Table S2), including the predicted targets for all the siRNAs used in our studies. Among the siRNAs designed to target progerin mRNA, the greatest number of potential off-targets was found for the Prog4 (8 transcripts from 5 genes), Prog1 (3 transcripts from 3 different genes), and Prog8 (4 transcripts from 1 gene) siRNAs as a target of the antisense strands (Table S2). These results were not further analyzed in detail, as these siRNAs were not demonstrated to be efficient. However, the off-targets detected for siRNA Prog4 and Prog8 may explain the cytotoxic effects observed after transfection.

An important finding from this analysis was the identification of the Ras homolog family member C (RhoC) transcript variant 3 (GeneBank: NM_001042679) as a potential off-target for the sense strands of several siRNAs, including ProgH3, Prog3, Prog5, Prog6, Prog9, Prog10, Prog12, Prog13, and Prog14 with 81%–96% query coverage (Table S2). The complementary sequence was located in the 5′ UTR of RhoC mRNA and contained a single mismatch at positions 11–13 of the complementary nucleotide sequence depending on the siRNA sequence. RhoC is a small GTPase involved in the regulation of actin cytoskeleton dynamics, cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis and has been implicated in tumor progression and metastasis.58 We did not observe any drastic changes in cell morphology or physiology after treatment with the siRNAs mentioned above. Notably, the predicted off-target interaction involves only 1 of 3 existing transcript variants encoding RhoC, and the complementary region is located within the 5′ UTR. This region is generally less tolerant of mismatches compared with the 3′ UTR, which further reduces the likelihood of effective target recognition.59,60 Nevertheless, this potential off-target interaction may require investigation in future studies.

Discussion

HGPS is a severe genetic disorder for which efficient treatment is lacking. As a consequence of the LMNA gene mutation leading to aberrant mRNA splicing, the toxic protein progerin is produced (Figure 3A). To ensure a long-term therapeutic effect, lowering progerin levels seems to be the most reasonable solution and gene therapy strategies are potentially the most promising.7 Therefore, we applied an RNAi strategy to achieve efficient progerin downregulation.

To develop easy, reproducible, quantitative methods for siRNA selection, we designed a new cellular model based on HeLa cells. Compared with patient fibroblasts, which have been used frequently for HGPS studies and for new drug screenings,29,32,34,37,38 our model has several advantages. The HeLa cell proliferation rate is high (Figures S1, S8C, and S8D) and the number of passages is not restricted; consequently, it is easier to obtain the required amount of material and perform quick screenings. iPSCs have also been utilized for drug screening in the context of HGPS treatment and offer a partial solution to the limited availability of biological material.61,62,63,64 However, compared with the development and culture of HeLa cells, the development and culture of iPSCs remain time consuming and costly. Moreover, the overexpression of proteins fused with GFP facilitates their localization in living cells and the measurement of fluorescence intensity, which correlates with the amount of fused protein and distinguishes endogenous and exogenous proteins (Figures 1B–1D). Compared with previously developed cellular models of HGPS based on HeLa cells,18,44,65,66 our model consists of a single-copy gene with stable expression (in ≈95% of cells in the subline; Figure 1D), reflecting a more natural state.

In HGPS fibroblasts and other models, e.g., HeLa cells with high levels of exogenous progerin expression, abnormal nuclear morphology, abnormal NPC protein aggregation in nuclear envelope invaginations, and the absence of lamin B1 in blebs with highly deformed nuclei at late passages were observed.21,67 However, in our model, we did not observe such differences (Figure 2). This was likely because of (1) a low level of exogenous progerin (lower than or similar to the level of endogenous lamin A) and (2) a fast proliferation rate that forces frequent nuclear lamina reassembly and does not allow for the accumulation of progerin between cell divisions. This results in the inability for rapidly proliferating HeLa cells to accumulate progerin because of its frequent depolymerization, exposure to degradation, and shorter GFP-progerin half-life compared with cells with a lower proliferation rate (e.g., the half-life of lamin A differs between cancer cells and human fibroblasts).68,69 Moreover, in newly isolated fibroblasts from HGPS patients, only 7.2% of the cells displayed misshapen nuclei.48 Among mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), the rate was 13% in heterozygous cells and 24% in homozygous cells.48 In HeLa and NHDF cells, after transient transfection with GFP-progerin, which typically led to substantial overexpression, 29% of the cells had misshapen nuclei.48 Another study revealed that in MEFs harboring two human LMNAG608G alleles, approximately 58% of cells displayed nuclear abnormalities.33 Among SMCs of mice carrying a single human LMNAG608G allele, approximately 18% of the nuclei were misshapen (Kim et al., preprint, 2024). Taken together, these data show that the occurrence of nuclear abnormalities depends on multiple factors, including the expression level of progerin and the specific cell type. However, the abovementioned studies used a binary visual assessment of nuclear shape, whereas our study employed a quantitative analysis.

The increased area and perimeter of the nuclei of the HeLa GFP-progerin cell line and the increased circularity and solidity of the HeLa GFP-lamin A nuclei (Figure 2B) may be explained by the specific effect of exogenous protein expression or simply as an effect of increased amounts of farnesylated protein at the nuclear envelope or both given that both may contribute to the increase in nuclear lamina volume and nuclear size.70,71,72,73,74 Simultaneously, overexpression of GFP-lamin A may increase nuclear stiffness and increase the resistance of nuclear lamina and cell nuclei to deformation.75,76

Our aim was to select siRNA sequences for efficient HGPS therapy that lower progerin levels as much as possible and do not significantly reduce lamin A expression. Previously, it was demonstrated that the ratio between lamin A and progerin must be greater than or equal to 2:1 to achieve a positive therapeutic effect.77 Moreover, our concerns regarding the preservation of lamin A levels were due to the risk of haploinsufficiency induction and minimization of the therapeutic side effects risk caused by lamin A downregulation.42

From another perspective, it was previously reported that lamin A expression is dispensable in mice.78 Compared with wild-type mice, mice that expressed only lamin C and not lamin A did not show any difference in lifespan or body weight. Nevertheless, the same work revealed that mouse fibroblasts that express only lamin C exhibit some differences in nuclear shape and deformability.78 Despite the obvious differences in lifespan and the metabolic rate between mice and humans, it should be noted that in laminopathies, the phenotype of a particular disease is more severe in humans than in mice. For example, in Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy, only homozygous mouse models develop disease symptoms.79,80,81 However, in humans, symptoms manifest as long as a heterozygous mutation in LMNA exists.82 Moreover, Zmpste24−/− mice with lamin C overexpression and an absence of lamin A presented a reduced number of misshaped nuclei, compared to Zmpste24−/− mice with lamin A expression; however, the number of defective nuclei was still significantly greater in these mice than in wild-type mice.83 These results suggest that the simultaneous downregulation of both progerin and lamin A for HGPS therapy is more beneficial than the preservation of progerin expression; nevertheless, this strategy is suboptimal. Given that lamin A deficiency in mouse fibroblasts and Zmpste24−/− mice causes very mild phenotypes, we consider that the preservation of lamin A is likely more critical in humans and should be considered in therapeutic designs.

In the screening of HeLa cells using flow cytometry (Figure 4A) and western blotting (Figures 4B, 4C, and S2), we selected five siRNA sequences—Prog5, Prog6, Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14—as the most effective candidates. These siRNAs reduced the GFP-progerin levels by more than 30%, whereas the decrease in the GFP-lamin A levels did not exceed 30%. We did not observe the cytotoxicity of the selected siRNAs, visible nuclear structure alterations, or differences in lamin B1 localization, as indicated by the immunofluorescence results (Figure 5).

Currently, lonafarnib is the only approved treatment for HGPS that significantly extends patients' lifespan when it is administered over a long-term period (more than 5 years).84 In clinical trials for new therapies for rare diseases, the tested treatment may be combined with an existing, partially effective treatment (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06775041).85 Maintaining lonafarnib treatment during clinical trials of a new therapeutic is medically and ethically justified because discontinuance of the only drug with a clinical benefit could pose a serious risk to patients. If a new treatment fails, patients treated with lonafarnib will continue receiving effective treatment, which is important given the progressive nature of HGPS. Moreover, combination therapy may provide some benefits over the use of single therapeutics.26,27,28,29 In our study, we evaluated the effect of combining lonafarnib with the most effective siRNAs (Prog6, Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14; Figure 6). We assessed whether the siRNA-mediated downregulation of progerin level can be achieved without compromising the effects of lonafarnib and whether the two approaches are compatible. Combined treatment resulted in an additive effect for all the tested siRNAs, as indicated by the CI values, supporting the notion of a combined therapy option. Lonafarnib, a drug targeting farnesylation, contributes to the overall reduction in the expression of both lamin A and progerin. From our point of view, this highlights the importance of selecting siRNA sequences with particular emphasis on those that reduce progerin expression without additionally compromising lamin A levels. Such selectivity should help to avoid additional downregulation of lamin A, which may limit the applicability of the combined approach.

To better understand the clinical potential of these siRNAs, we tested them in fibroblasts from 3 HGPS patients of different ages, sexes, and genetic backgrounds. Fibroblast transfection confirmed that the selected siRNAs, namely, Prog6, Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14, efficiently decreased progerin levels in the 3 cell lines (Figure 7C). Surprisingly, ProgH3 was also effective in this model but was not so effective in HeLa cells (Figures 4B and 4C). Lamin A levels were preserved in the siRNA-treated patient and control fibroblasts for all the tested siRNAs except Prog6 (Figures 7B and 7C). We hypothesize that this is a result of several factors. First, differences in the levels of lamin A and progerin mRNA between cell lines may explain this finding as a smaller amount of progerin mRNA in the cell increases the probability of lamin A mRNA binding. The second reason lies in variations in RNAi pathway activity induced by the different genetic backgrounds of the cell lines. Finally, the proliferation rate also affects the final amount of target mRNA and siRNA in the cell, as each cell division leads to a dilution of its content; thus, cells with a slower proliferation rate may retain more siRNA. Taken together, these observations emphasize the importance of the data obtained from patient-derived fibroblasts, as they illustrate how cell line differences can influence the final outcome.

In other studies using ASOs and patient cells, the downregulation of progerin reached 25% or even less than 20%38,40,86; however, the level of lamin A decreased to 50%–60%,38 36%, and 20%.40,86 Nevertheless, we believe that even the increase in the lamin C level after ASO treatment may not be beneficial enough to ameliorate HGPS symptoms. Although we did not analyze phenotypic changes in this study, we showed that siRNAs reduced progerin levels in cells of patients who differed in terms of age, sex, and genetic background, without any serious toxicity. These results are important for the selection and evaluation of siRNAs and future in vivo studies, as they confirm that siRNAs function in disease-relevant human cells.

Among the most effective siRNA sequences (e.g., Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14), a key shared feature was that their seed region aligned with the junction between exons 11 and 12 of progerin mRNA (Figure 3B). In contrast, this feature was absent in the ineffective siRNAs, such as Prog1 and Prog3. When analyzing the structure of effective siRNAs in the context of the rules for siRNA design,51,54,87,88 we were unable to identify strict correlations that explain why some siRNAs performed better than others did.

The structural characteristics of the siRNAs, including the stability of the 5′ end of the strands, self-complementarity, and nucleotide composition at key positions, along with siRNA specificity and efficiency, are summarized in Table S1. These widely used design rules are seemingly more applicable when the full mRNA sequence may be utilized as a potential target region, allowing optimal binding site selection across a broad region. In our case, the target region was limited to a short sequence specific for progerin mRNA, making rational design alone insufficient, and empirical tests were more appropriate and successful.

When summarizing the structural features of the siRNAs, we noted that the ineffective siRNAs Prog1 and Prog3 contain 5 consecutive guanines located centrally at positions 9–13 of the antisense strand within the cleavage site. Such sequences likely form G-quadruplex structures that prevent target binding or cleavage.54 Efficient siRNAs lacked these motifs (e.g., Prog14) or had them near the 5′ end (e.g., Prog12), which appears less disruptive.

Notably, compared with those of effective and simultaneously specific siRNAs (e.g., Prog12 and Prog14), the antisense strands of siRNAs Prog7 and Prog8 exhibited high thermodynamic stability when hybridized to lamin A mRNA, which may explain their nonspecificity (Table S1). In addition, the 5′ ends of the Prog4 and Prog7 antisense strands were highly unstable, similar to those of lamin A/C siRNA. This likely promoted more favorable incorporation of these antisense strands into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) than the antisense strands of effective siRNAs with highly stable 5′ ends (e.g., Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14). As a result, when equal amounts of siRNAs were used, siRNAs Prog4 and Prog7 formed more RISC-antisense strand complexes, increasing the probability of lamin A mRNA recognition.

An important consideration in antisense gene therapies concerns off-target activity. The selected siRNAs did not negatively affect HeLa cell proliferation or the proliferation of patients' fibroblasts. Nevertheless, the absence of obvious adverse effects in cellular models does not satisfactorily prove the absence of nonspecific siRNA interactions. As an off-target assessment, we conducted BLAST alignments for both the sense and antisense strands of all the tested siRNAs. This analysis revealed only RhoC transcript variant 3 (GeneBank: NM_001042679) as a potential off-target for all the siRNAs selected for validation in patient-derived fibroblasts (Table S2). This transcript encodes one of 3 isoforms of the small GTPase RhoC and shows a high degree of sequence complementarity to the siRNAs (81%–96%). To our knowledge, the tissue specificity of this transcript variant remains unclear. It is also unknown whether its silencing could be functionally compensated by the two remaining transcript variants.58 Therefore, its actual biological impact is uncertain.

It is important to emphasize that the obtained results represent only possible off-target interactions based on sequence complementarity. The presence of a complementary region in mRNA does not guarantee the interaction itself. For instance, lamin A mRNAs (GeneBank: NM_170707.4; NM_001406991.1; NM_001406983.1) were predicted to be targets for some siRNAs, but not all of them led to a reduction in lamin A protein levels (e.g., Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14; Figure 7C; Table S2). Effective interactions depend on multiple factors, including the amount of siRNA in the cell, the frequency of loading of siRNA strands into the RISC, the expression level of the target transcript, and the accessibility of the mRNA target region.60 Ideally, in vivo studies using selected siRNA candidates should be performed for a comprehensive assessment of the biological impact of gene silencing, if it occurs. Moreover, in vivo studies allow the evaluation of possible tissue-specific effects.

A mouse model with the human LMNAG608G allele added to the homozygous LMNAwt/wt mouse genotype is available and appropriate for our future studies.38,41 Moreover, a minipig with a mutation in one allele of the porcine LMNA gene may also be used as a larger animal model, as there are no differences compared with human lamin A mRNA at the siRNA-binding site.89 In addition, a mouse model with a mouse LMNAG609G gene mutation is rather inappropriate for therapies that directly target human LMNA gene expression because of sequence differences.39

siRNA-based therapies have already been approved for other diseases (e.g., patisiran and nusinersen),90,91 and sequential administration is possible without a significant risk of an immune response observed after the application of viral vectors or caused by pre-existing immunity. Although our study focused on the functional validation of siRNAs, we recognize that clinical translation may require further optimization. In its unmodified form, siRNA has a limited half-life in biological fluids and cells,92 which is problematic for therapeutic application. Chemical modifications, such as 2′-O-methyl or 2′-fluoro ribose substitutions, phosphorothioate linkages, or locked nucleic acids, prolong intracellular stability.93,94,95,96,97 These modifications have also been reported to reduce off-target interactions.98 Moreover, Benedicto et al.43 reported that the selective elimination of progerin in vascular SMCs but not endothelial cells prevents the exacerbation of atherosclerosis in a mouse model of HGPS, highlighting the possibility of tissue-specific targeting, which may be achieved by conjugation with delivery molecules or lipid encapsulation to increase uptake and reduce dosing frequency. In this context, the lead sequences (i.e., Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14) are promising candidates for further studies as modified oligos with lipid cross-linking, liposomes, or lipoplexes.

Our siRNA sequences may also be employed in recombinant adeno-associated virus and encoded in expression cassettes with general promoters, such as histone H1 or U6 snRNA promoters. Addressing all the tissues targeted by the vector and the low likelihood of expression silencing may be advantageous. It is also possible to use tissue-selective promoters to direct expression into selected tissues (e.g., heart and muscles).99 This can be achieved by the expression of siRNA sequences as shRNAs or microRNAs.

To summarize, we selected three siRNAs, Prog9, Prog12, and Prog14, which efficiently and specifically decrease progerin levels without affecting lamin A in both our novel HeLa cellular model and HGPS patient fibroblasts. We also demonstrated the potential of combined siRNA and lonafarnib treatment in HeLa cells. Thus, we assume that our sequences constitute promising potential drugs for RNA therapies targeting HGPS.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Human cervical adenocarcinoma cell line HeLa was purchased from the Cell Line Collection of the Polish Academy of Sciences, Institute of Immunology and Experimental Therapy in Wroclaw, Poland. HeLa cells and derived sublines were cultured in MEM α medium with GlutaMAX (#32571-028, Gibco, Carlsbad, California, USA) with the addition of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (#F7524, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (#15240-062, Gibco, Carlsbad, California, USA).

Modified human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T Phoenix-AMPHO (#CRL-3213, ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA) was purchased from LGC. HEK293T Phoenix-AMPHO cells were cultured in DMEM with high glucose (4.5 g/L glucose, #11960-044, Gibco, Carlsbad, California, USA), supplemented with 10% FBS (#10500-064, Gibco, Carlsbad, California, USA), GlutaMAX supplement (#35050-038, Gibco, Carlsbad, California, USA), and antibiotic (#30–2300, ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA).

Human fibroblast cell lines from patients with HGPS were obtained from Progeria Research Foundation: HGADFN271 (here HGPS271, male, age 1 year 2 months), HGADFN155 (here HGPS155, female, age 1 year 2 months), and HGADFN367 (here HGPS367, female, age 3 years). Control cell lines from healthy donors were obtained from Progeria Research Foundation as well: HGMDFN368 (here CTRL368, female, age 31 years 7 months) cell line derived from the healthy mother of HGADFN367 cell line donor and HGMDFN090 (here CTRL090, female, age 37 years 10 months). Fibroblasts were cultured in FGM-2 BulletKit medium (#CC-3132 and CC-4126, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland).

All cell lines were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Plasmids for retrovirus production

Plasmids pBABE-puro-GFP-wt-lamin A (#17662, Addgene, Watertown, Massachusetts, USA) and pBABE-puro-GFP-progerin (#17663, Addgene, Watertown, Massachusetts, USA) were a gift from Tom Misteli,49 and pBabe puro IRES-EGFP (#14430, Addgene, Watertown, Massachusetts, USA) was a gift from L. Miguel Martins. Plasmids were amplified using the E. coli DH5ɑ strain and purified using GeneJET Plasmid Midiprep Kit (#K0481, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

siRNA sequences and farnesylation inhibitor

siRNA sequences were designed by our group and synthesized by GeneCust, Boynes, France. siRNA lamin A/C was used as a positive control and was designed to downregulate lamins A and C as well as progerin. siRNA negative control is supposed to not recognize any human mRNA. Both sequences were commercially available in GeneCust, Boynes, France (#SI-POS A08001; SI-NC A06001, respectively). siRNAs ProgH3 and ProgH3_scr were designed based on Huang S et al.34 and refer to the shRNA3 sequence, to compare the efficiency of different siRNA sequences targeted to progerin mRNA. We also designed an additional scramble sequence based on progerin-targeted siRNA.

In our study, we used lonafarnib (#SML 1457, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) as a farnesylation inhibitor. It was dissolved in DMSO and used at a final concentration of 2 μM.

Retrovirus production and preparation of cell lines

For retrovirus production, HEK293T AMPHO-Phoenix cells were seeded in a T25 flask (BD Falcon, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) to obtain 70% confluency during transfection. Cells were separately transfected with pBabe plasmids encoding GFP, GFP-lamin A, or GFP-progerin using TurboFect reagent (#R0531, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with 8 μL/4 μg of DNA. The medium was replaced 5 h later.

HeLa cells were seeded at a density of 70,000 cells per well in 6-well plates 24 h before transduction. Medium containing retroviral particles was collected 48 h after transfection of HEK293T cells, purified via centrifugation (5,000 × g, 5 min), and used for HeLa cell transduction with addition of 10 μg/mL polybrene (#H9268, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA). Wells with transduction efficiency of 5%–10% were selected to obtain cells transduced with approximately 1 copy of the retrovirus. HeLa sublines were selected with 0.3 μg/mL puromycin (#ant-pr-1, InvivoGen, San Diego, California, USA) treatment followed by sorting of GFP-expressing cells with a MoFlo XDP cell sorter (Beckman Coulter).

siRNA transfection and lonafarnib treatment

For siRNA transfections followed by flow cytometry and western blot analyses, cells were seeded 24 h earlier to achieve 20%–30% confluence (60,000 HeLa cells or 40,000 of fibroblasts per well of a 6-well plate). The transfection was performed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (#13778030, Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) at a final concentration of 0.14% v/v (3 μL per well) and siRNAs at a final concentration of 7 nM (15 pmol per well) for HeLa cells and 2 nM (4 pmol per well) for patients’ fibroblasts, if not described elsewise. The transfection mixture was prepared according to the Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection protocol. HeLa cells were incubated for 72 h with transfection mixture (without medium change) and then collected and analyzed. Patients’ fibroblasts were transfected for 4 days for single transfection or, as described in Figure 7A, first, for 3 days, after which the medium was replaced and the cells were passaged 3 days later. The following day, cells were transfected again for 3 days, followed by another 3-day incubation in fresh medium before being collected for western blot analysis.

To test the combination of siRNA and lonafarnib treatment, HeLa cells were seeded on a 12-well plate 24 h before transfection to achieve 20%–30% confluence (25,000 cells per well). The transfection mixture contained 0.1% v/v (1.2 μL per well) Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent and siRNA at a final concentration of 7 nM (7.5 pmol per well). Six hours after transfection, the medium was replaced with a medium containing lonafarnib at a final concentration of 2 μM, and then the cells were incubated for 72 h and analyzed. siRNA and lonafarnib concentrations were adjusted to observe less than 50% of progerin decrease, which is necessary for calculation of the combination effect.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry analysis of HeLa cells with overexpression of GFP, GFP-lamin A, or GFP-progerin was performed 72 h after siRNA transfection if not described elsewise. Cells were detached by trypsinization (0.05% trypsin-EDTA, #25300-054, Gibco, Carlsbad, California, USA) and then centrifuged for 5 min at 400 × g. The pellet was resuspended in PBS (phosphate-buffered saline), centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS again. Cells were kept on ice until the analysis. The analysis was performed with a NovoCyte Flow Cytometer (Accela, San Ramon, California, USA). At the first step, nontreated HeLa cells were analyzed to set gates for the alive cell population (forward scatter, side scatter, and autofluorescence). Next, transfected cells were analyzed—the fluorescence intensity of cells from the gates determined for the control HeLa cell population or untreated fibroblast cells was measured (at least for 12,000 cells).

Quantification of transgene copy number per cell

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from HeLa sublines with a Universal DNA/RNA/Protein Purification Kit (#E3597, EurX, Gdansk, Poland). Plasmid pReceiver-M03 (#EX-EGFP-M03, GeneCopoeia, Rockville, Maryland, USA) with inserted maspin coding sequence and GFP served for standard curve of maspin and GFP copy number in the sample (5 × 10−17–5 × 10−12 g of plasmid DNA or 6–617,434 copies).

qPCR was conducted using the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System Software (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). 10 ng of gDNA from HeLa sublines was used. SYBR Green Master Mix (#A25776, Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and primers were used for analysis: 5′-CACCAAACCAGTGCAGATGATG-3′ and 5′-ATCCTCCACATCCTTGGGTAGT-3′ for maspin and 5′-GACGTAAACGGCCACAAGTT-3′ and 5′-AAGTCGTGCTGCTTCATGT-3′ for GFP. Initial denaturation was performed for 10 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (20 s at 95°C) and annealing/extension (30 s at 60°C). Then, a melting curve analysis was conducted. Each sample was analyzed in technical triplicates.

The quantity of GFP and maspin copies in gDNA probes was calculated with the relative standard curve method, using the plasmid DNA containing both amplicons. Assuming that 1 HeLa cell contains 3 copies of the SERPINB5 gene coding for maspin (as the SERPINB5 gene is located on chromosome 18, which is triplicated in HeLa cells),100 the number of cells in the DNA probe was determined. Finally, the average amount of GFP gene copies per cell was calculated.

Western blot analysis

The excess of HeLa cells analyzed with flow cytometry was used for western blot analysis. Fibroblasts were collected from the whole well after transfection. Cells were centrifuged and resuspended in 2x Laemmli buffer to a final concentration of 1 × 106 cells per 100 μL. Cells were lysed for 10 min at 96°C and stored at −20°C for later analysis. Protein lysates were separated by 10% denaturing SDS-PAGE, with 6% stacking gel, using the equivalent of 40,000 HeLa cells per well or 20,000 fibroblasts per well. Next, proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane by wet transfer. The membrane was stained with Ponceau S, washed with PBST (PBS with 0.075% Tween 20), and then blocked in 5% non-fat milk in PBST. Next, it was stained with primary antibodies diluted with 5% non-fat milk in PBST (MαProgerin, #ab66587, Abcam Limited, Cambridge, UK; MαLamin A/C, #SAB4200236, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA; RαGFP, #sc-390394, SCBT, Dallas, Texas, USA; RαGAPDH, #G9545, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA; MαAlfa-tubulin, #12G10, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, Iowa City, Iowa, USA; MαAlfa-tubulin, # ab7291, Abcam Limited, Cambridge, UK) at 4°C overnight. Primary antibodies were washed out 3 times in PBST and then the membrane was incubated for 1 h with secondary antibodies in 5% non-fat milk in PBST (DαMouse antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase [HRP], #715-035-151, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Ely, UK; GαRabbit conjugated with HRP, #111-035-144, Jackson ImmunoResearch Ely, UK). Proteins were visualized using ECL substrate (#170–5060, Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA), SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (#34577, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), or Immobilon Crescendo Western HRP substrate (#WBLUR0100, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) with the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imaging system.

The densitometry analysis was performed using ImageLab software from Bio-Rad. Data were normalized to GAPDH or alfa-tubulin intensity and presented on graphs in relation to cells transfected with negative control siRNA or Scr siRNA or after DMSO treatment, when the combination of siRNA and lonafarnib treatment was analyzed.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded on coverslips placed in a 12-well plate (25,000 cells per well), transfected, and/or treated with lonafarnib on the next day according to the procedures described above. After 3 days of incubation, cells on coverslips were fixed for 20 min in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and permeabilized at 4°C for 5 min with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS. Next, cells were washed 3 times in PBS and blocked in 1% FBS in PBS for 60 min. Fixed cells were stained with primary antibodies in PBS with 1% FBS (MαLamin A, #NBP1-97917-133A2, Novus, Centennial, Colorado, USA; RαLamin B1, #12987-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, Illinois, USA; MαNup107 [Mab414], #MMS-120P, Covance, Durham, North Carolina, USA) overnight at 4°C. The next day, antibodies were washed 3 times with PBS, and cells were stained with secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescent dyes for 60 min (DαMouse conjugated with TRITC560, #715-025-150; DαRabbit conjugated with TRITC560, #715-025-152; DαMouse conjugated with DL649, #715-495-150, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Ely, UK). The unbound antibodies were washed 3 times with PBS; coverslips were mounted on glass slides with mounting medium with DAPI and analyzed with an SP8 confocal fluorescent microscope (Leica), using the 63x objective and LasX software.

The analyses of immunofluorescence intensity and nuclear morphology were performed using ImageJ software. For nuclear morphology description, circularity was determined as Circularity = 4π × (area/(perimeter)ˆ2) and solidity was determined as Solidity = area/(convex area), where convex area is the area of a figure, whose perimeter (convex hull) connects the protruding points of the studied figure and lies outside it.101

Proliferation assay

To analyze the proliferation rates, cells were seeded on a 6-well plate (60,000 cells per well for HeLa cells or 40,000 cells for fibroblasts). The next day, the plate was placed in an IncuCyte automatic microscope (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany), where it was automatically scanned every 2 h. The confluence of cells at respective time points was analyzed with IncuCyte ZOOM software, n ≥ 3.

siRNA structure characterization and off-target activity analysis

To characterize the structural and sequence properties of the designed siRNAs, several computational analyses were performed. The thermodynamic stability of the 5′ ends of both the sense and antisense strands of each siRNA duplex was calculated using the DuplexFold web server available at the RNAstructure Web Server (https://rna.urmc.rochester.edu/RNAstructureWeb/Servers/DuplexFold/DuplexFold.html).102

Secondary structure formation tendencies of siRNA strands were evaluated by calculating the minimum free energy (ΔG) of each strand using RNAfold from the ViennaRNA Web Services (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at//cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi).103 Additionally, the minimum free energy of the duplex formed between the antisense siRNA strand and progerin mRNA (GeneBank: NM_001282626.2) or lamin A (GeneBank: NM_001406983.1) mRNA was also computed using RNAfold.

Key nucleotide positions within both siRNA strands, known to influence silencing efficiency, were identified and annotated according to established siRNA design rules, including the Ui-Tei rules, the Reynolds rules, and the Amarzguioui rule,52,53,54 as well as updated guidelines reviewed by Friedrich and Aigner.51

Potential off-target interactions were assessed for each siRNA strand independently, as both sense and antisense strands may be loaded into the RISC complex with varying efficiency. Sequence similarity searches were performed using BLASTN against the RefSeq_RNA database for Homo sapiens (taxid: 9606), with the algorithm optimized for somewhat similar sequences (blastn). The search parameters were adjusted as follows: maximum target sequences, 500; enabled short input sequence optimization; expected threshold, 0.05 (default); word size, 7; match/mismatch scores, 2, −3 (default); gap costs: existence, 5 and extension, 2 (changed to prioritize ungapped alignments).

From the BLAST output, we excluded alignments showing identical sequences between siRNA and mRNA (strand orientation plus/plus) and retained only those showing complementarity (strand orientation plus/minus), which reflects the potential for siRNA-mRNA duplex formation. Additionally, predicted transcripts and matches lacking complementarity at the second or third nucleotide of the siRNA from the 5′ end were excluded, since these positions are critical for RISC-mediated target recognition and gene silencing, as previously described.88

Statistical analysis of results

The results were analyzed with Real Statistics Resource Pack software (https://www.real-statistics.com). The equality of variances was tested with Levene’s test. If not stated otherwise, data with equal variances were analyzed with the one-way ANOVA test and Tukey’s range test. Data with unequal variances were analyzed next with the Kruskal-Wallis test and Games-Howell test.
