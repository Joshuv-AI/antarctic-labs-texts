Revised Ocular Trauma Score (rOTS): to develop and internally validate a predictive model for visual outcomes after open globe injury
Authors: Parinee Kemchoknatee, Jayanton Patumanond, Somporn Chantra, Pennung Thongtong, Nattaporn Vongsa, Rinrada Kreesang, Dolchanok Dolman, Thansit Srisombut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe aimed to develop and validate a prognostic scoring model for predicting poor visual outcomes in patients with open globe injury (OGI).DesignA retrospective cohort study of patients with OGI from two teaching hospitals in Thailand.Methods311 patients diagnosed with OGI between 2016 and 2023 were used to develop a multivariable logistic regression model predicting final visual acuity aimed at 6 months post-OGI. Visual outcomes were categorised into two groups using 20/200 as the cut-off for legal blindness. The model’s performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Internal validation was conducted with bootstrapping for 500 replications.Results133 patients (42.77%) had visual acuity worse than 20/200 at the 6-month follow-up. The median follow-up time was 4.14 months, with an IQR of 3.00–11.74 months. Initial visual acuity (VA), relative afferent pupillary defect, rupture and eyelid injury were among the strongest predictors of visual outcome. Discrimination and calibration of the scoring model were satisfactory, with a C-statistic of 0.8671, a slope of 1 and a calibration-in-the-large of 0. Risk groups were created, categorised as mild, moderate and severe, with a C-statistic of 0.8094. The ORs for poor final VA (≤20/200) at 6 months were 1.51 (95% CI, 0.93 to 2.48) and 45.06 (95% CI, 11.20 to 387.94) in the moderate and severe risk groups, respectively.ConclusionsOur prognostic model (revised Ocular Trauma Score) can be seamlessly used in emergency settings to predict visual outcomes in patients presenting with OGI. Presenting visual acuity (VA) is the strongest predictor. Interpretation should be made with caution due to several limitations, including the predominance of severe cases inherent to a referral-based setting, the relatively small sample size and the absence of paediatric patients. External validation of our model is needed.

Introduction

Open globe injury (OGI) is a condition of a full-thickness laceration of the eyeball which is divided into rupture, perforating, penetrating and intraocular foreign body (IOFB).1 The incidence of OGI, approximately 3.5 per 100 000 person-years, depended on the nature of the trauma.25 Despite advancements in microsurgical technique, several studies have reported poor visual outcome.57 Main predictive factors of poor visual outcome included poor baseline VA, relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD), type of OGI, higher zone of injury, eyelid injury, orbital bone fracture, vitreous haemorrhage (VH) and retinal detachment (RD).5 6 8 However, some factors have been inconclusive, such as duration before primary repair.9 10

Several prognostic models for predicting poor visual outcomes have been developed for early use after injury.1113 One of the most widely used scores is the Ocular Trauma Score (OTS) by Kuhn et al in 2002, developed from over 2500 injuries from the US and Hungarian Eye Injury Registries.11 These models facilitate prognostication for patients to better align their expectations and enable ophthalmologists to detect complications throughout the injury course. The models typically incorporate risk factors derived from the mechanism of injury and complicated ophthalmic examinations. Recently, a model including the factor of orbital fracture from CT has been reported to have increased predictability.13 However, delays in ophthalmic examinations can occur, particularly in remote areas, thereby limiting the use of these models in the early course of injury. As a result, in practice, ophthalmologists often rely solely on one prognostic factor—presenting visual acuity to predict poor outcomes. However, this approach may overlook other important factors such as the zone and RAPD. This may unintentionally delay care for patients with moderate or mild presenting VA who have other significant factors.

Moreover, previous models, including the most widely used, lack proper external validation due to a lack of model specifications in the original article.11 Therefore, we aim to develop and internally validate a clinical predictive model for poor visual outcomes following OGI in the Thai population. The model incorporated simple clinical predictors that can be evaluated by non-ophthalmologists in emergency settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective cohort study design was conducted to develop a predictive model for final visual acuity using electronic database from two teaching hospitals. All eligible cases of OGI diagnosed between January 2016 and December 2023 were collected from Rajavithi and Banphaeo Hospitals in Thailand. Initial screening for OGI cases was performed using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes S05.2, S05.3, S05.4, S05.5 and S05.6. The inclusion criteria comprised OGI cases aged 18–80, with a follow-up duration exceeding 3 months and who underwent primary surgery at our centres. Exclusion criteria were concurrent eye conditions such as intraocular disease, pseudophakia, other diseases that influence visual acuity and missing key data—defined as both initial and final VA, or more than 50% of predictive variables. The development and validation of this prediction model were in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis statement.14

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Data collection

Data were collected from the electronic medical record. Patient demographics were collected, including age and gender, presenting VA, nature of injury, time to presentation and time to primary repair. Other injury-related characteristics were collected in accordance with the United States Eye Injury Registry and the International Society of Ocular Trauma (Birmingham Eye Trauma Terminology.15 16 Wound lengths at the time of primary globe repair were documented. The VA was converted to logarithm of minimal angle of resolution (logMAR) for statistical analysis as follows: no light perception (NLP); light perception (LP); hand movement; counting finger to 3.4, 3.1, 2.9 and 2.6 logMAR, respectively.17

Missing presenting VA data were permitted for inclusion, as it is possible in cases who were unable to have their VA accurately measured on initial assessment. Complete-case analysis was used for model development. Missing factors and their frequency were reported.

Outcomes and definitions of variables

The outcome of our analysis was the final VA after OGI at a 6-month period post-OGI, according to the original OTS.11 For patients with a shorter or longer follow-up duration, we used their last VA data within 1 year post-OGI, which is considered a stable VA and a representative of the final VA affected by the OGI. A cut-off of 20/200 was used in our analysis to identify cases that met the criteria for legal blindness.

Based on the OTS and previous studies,1113 candidate predictors were identified. These included presenting VA, type of injury (penetration and rupture), presence of IOFB, RAPD, zone and eyelid injury.

Statistical analysis, model development and validation

Patients were categorised based on their final VA as better or worse than 20/200. A histogram and Shapiro-Wilk test were applied to test the normality. Demographics were compared using χ2 or exact probability test and t-tests or rank sum tests, as appropriate. All demographics and OGI-related factors were evaluated as predictive factors for final VA worse than 20/200 using univariable logistic regression and presented as an area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC). Potential predictors, defined as those with an AUC≥0.6 and a significant p value, were included in the multivariable logistic regression model.

A further model was generated using stepwise selection, with a p value≥0.05 for the removal of factors. The final model included five factors selected following a consensus opinion by ophthalmologists to generalise clinical utilisation to first contract providers with limited ophthalmic examination. The final model was reviewed and endorsed by ophthalmologists and emergency physicians at our centres.

The β-coefficients for each predictor were divided by the smallest coefficient in the model and rounded to the nearest half (0.5) to transform into points.18 Points for each predictor were added up to obtain a total score. The performance of the model derived from total score was assessed. Calibration of the model was analysed with a calibration plot to examine the agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcome for poor final VA. Bootstrap was employed for internal validation with 500 replications. Risk scores were categorised into three risk groups: mild, moderate and severe. The predictive ability of each risk group was calculated and presented as an ORs with 95% CI.

Results

After exclusions, the study cohort consisted of 311 consecutive OGI patients who underwent primary surgery at our centres (online supplemental figure 1). Complete-case analysis without imputation was conducted in 298 patients. There were a total of 165 patients (53.05%) with final VA better than 20/200, while 144 patients (46.95%) exhibited poor VA of worse than 20/200 in the cohort of consecutive OGI cases. Age was significantly older in the poor outcome group, with a mean (SD) of 38.59 years (15.77) compared with 43.35 years (17.38) in the good and poor outcome groups, respectively (p=0.013). The majority of patients in this study had initial visual acuity at finger count or worse (59.73%). At presentation, patients with poor final VA were more likely to present with a poorer initial VA, with a mean logMAR of 1.41 (1.11) compared with 2.73 (0.73) in those with good final VA (p<0.001). They were more frequently presented with penetrating and rupture globe injuries (both p values<0.001) and were more likely to have RAPD (3.03% vs 27.27%; p<0.001). Zone II injuries were most common in the cohort, while zone III injuries were significantly more prevalent in the worse outcome groups. VH, vitreous prolapse and RD were all more likely in the worse outcome group. Wound length data were missing in 50 cases and were significantly longer in the worse outcome group, with a mean of 5.82 mm (3.15) and 8.39 mm (3.36) in the good and worse outcome groups, respectively (p<0.001). Eyelid injuries were also a significant predictor variable, observed in 19% of the good outcome group compared with 27% in the poor outcome group (p=0.034). The median follow-up time was 4.13 months. Mean of final VA in logMAR was 1.34 with mean difference −0.66 (95% CI −0.53 to –0.78). (table 1) More than half of patients had VA better than 20/200 while 4.7% of patients had NLP. Endophthalmitis or panophthalmitis accounted for 27 patients or 8.74%. 9 patients (2.91%) underwent enucleation. Among all clinical predictors, initial VA had the strongest predictive strength, with an AUC of 0.8180 (table 2).

Various nature of injuries is detailed and shown in online supplemental table 1. Among the known causes, injuries from wood/branch/bamboo, hammer nail and bodily assault were the most common. The second most common cause of injury was metal fragments, accounting for 7.72%. Flying stone and glass/ceramic fragments were the third most common causes.

The final best clinical multivariable model for poor visual outcomes includes initial VA, RAPD, rupture, zone and eyelid injuries (table 3). Compared with the full model, the discrimination ability of the final reduced model with clinical predictors in categories remains satisfactory (AUC; 0.8686 vs 0.8700 in the full model, online supplemental table 2). In terms of parsimony, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) are comparable between the final reduced model and the full model (AIC, 283.098 vs 280.645 in the full model). An item score was assigned to each predictor using its logistic regression coefficient, as mentioned earlier in the methods section. The rOTS was obtained by adding up all scores from each predictor, resulting in score ranges from 0 to 19.5 points. The discriminative ability of the rOTS is comparable to the model from which it is derived (AUC, 0.8671 vs 0.8686 in the final reduced model, online supplemental table 2, figure 1B). Internal validation was conducted using the bootstrap procedure with 500 replications, resulting in a bootstrap shrinkage of 0.992 and a C-statistic of 0.867 (bootstrap 95% CI, 0.833 to 0.906), indicating optimistic performance in similar future patient. A calibration plot in figure 1C showed good agreement between the predicted probabilities and the actual outcomes observed in the cohort, with a slope of 1 and calibration-in-the-large of 0. The score predicted the risk of poor visual outcome increased as the risk score moved upward, also demonstrating good calibration with alignment between actual and observed risks (figure 1A).

The risk groups were created and stratified into three groups: mild (rOTS below 5), moderate (5–12) and severe (>12) to facilitate clinical visualisation of patient’s risk for poor final VA (figure 1A). The discriminatory ability of risk groups was acceptable, with AUC of 0.8094. The OR for the moderate risk group was 1.51 (95% CI, 0.93 to 2.48) and for the severe group, it was 45.06 (95% CI, 11.20 to 387.94). The predictive accuracy of risk groups for identifying patients with a poor visual outcome worse than 20/200 yielded a sensitivity of 81.82% and a specificity of 70.91% (online supplemental table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we develop and internally validate a prognostic multivariable regression model for predicting final visual outcomes, with a cut-off of final visual outcome into better or worse than 20/200, a threshold for legal blindness. The goal was to achieve a simple scoring model that can accurately predict patient’s visual outcome following OGI. Our model incorporates user-friendly clinical predictors that can be easily applied in emergency departments by non-ophthalmologists. Originally, the OTS has been developed to aid in this assessment.11

The original OTS categorised final visual outcomes into five groups based on predictors scores ranging from 0 to 100, including negative score predictors. Since then, numerous retrospectives and prospective trials have validated the correlation between OTS and final visual outcome following an OGI.1921 However, evaluating predictors within the OTS may be challenging for non-ophthalmologists. Additionally, its complex scoring system, including negative score predictors, can be difficult for users. Moreover, the OTS encompasses a wide range of visual outcomes, from better than 20/40 to 20/200 and 1/200 to 19/200. Although OTS provided an accepted predictive accuracy and is universally accepted. Some researchers reported a limitation or uncertain reliability in predicting, especially in a milder or very severe category VA group using OTS.22 Furthermore, the OTS incorporates predictors that may not manifest initially, such as endophthalmitis and RD. This limitation restricts its utility in emergency settings. In our setting, the cases were primarily referred from lower-level emergency departments or other hospitals for surgical intervention, often presenting with a poor prognosis on initial presentation. The mean follow-up duration in our study was longer than 6 months, but could be a good representative for patients’ stable final visual acuity following injury. Our models rely on simple clinical predictors of OGI, which can be examined by primary healthcare providers. Although several studies have identified IOFB as a predictor for poor visual outcomes.23 24 Combined with other predictors, IOFB significantly enhances the predictive ability for poor visual outcomes.24 However, the detection of IOFB is not feasible without an orbital CT. Even with CT performed, this predictor may be underused initially due to imaging delays and the non-detectability of non-metallic foreign bodies.25

The clinical characteristics ranked from strongest to weakest predictive value for poor visual outcomes were worse initial VA,24 26 27 RAPD,6 8 23 27 zone,2 24 28 RD,29 VH,1 29 lens injury21 29 30 and eyelid injuries.10 24 31 Other factors were wound length, IOFB and orbital fractures. After consulting with ophthalmologists and senior emergency staff at our centres to ensure face validity, our rOTS model consists of five predictors: initial VA, RAPD, rupture, zone and eyelid injuries. Compared with the original OTS, rOTS represents a reduction from six predictors: initial VA, RAPD, rupture, zone, RD and endophthalmitis. Recently developed scoring systems using classification and regression trees to predict vision (LP or better) versus no vision (NLP) included four predictors: initial VA, RAPD, zone and eyelid injuries. The model reported good accuracy with sensitivity and specificity values of 93.5% and 73.9%, respectively.12 Among the mentioned scoring systems, OTS has been the most used score in many studies, consistently demonstrating good correlation.12 20 21 With a sensitivity and specificity of over 97.4% and 100%, respectively.12 However, the original OTS lacks model’s specification, posing challenges for external validation and model updates.11

The rOTS model has been developed within the context of a tertiary care centre, thus reflecting the true prognosis of patients with similar severity. Furthermore, we categorised risk into mild, moderate and severe to enhance risk grouping and improve prognostic visualisation. Identifying the severe group may prompt ophthalmologists to evaluate and consider preventive vitrectomy earlier.32 Notably, a model specifically developed for predicting RD following OGI highlights the importance of further trial in this area.33

Since the detection of RAPD represents damage to the posterior segment, along with other signs such as RD or VH,1 as expected, a significant predictor in our model. This finding is consistent with the previous literature,1 2 6 8 26 31 34 and thus, RAPD was included in our final model. However, RD and VH were excluded to mitigate collinearity issues and due to the challenges for evaluation by non-ophthalmologists. One advantage of RAPD is its utility in assessing unconscious patients and cases where cervical spine immobilisation is necessary. Zone and eyelid injuries were also identified as significant predictors in numerous previous studies and were included in the rOTS model. Their inclusion was attributed to their ease of evaluation and their discrimination ability for poor visual outcomes. However, compared with other predictors, eyelid and rupture were found to have the least predictive strength in the final model. Eyelid injuries are known to be correlated with poor visual outcomes, particularly when lid laceration involves adnexal injury.24 Similarly, zone III injuries, especially complex corneoscleral and posterior wounds, are associated with poor visual outcomes.28 Therefore, the zone was included in our model. Conversely, wound length was omitted due to the delay in its evaluation, as it is measured intraoperatively and due to multicollinearity issues with zone. In our dataset, wound length was missing in 50 patients, limiting its utility. To address this, we used the zone of injury as a surrogate marker for wound extent, allowing us to incorporate an estimate of injury severity while maintaining model stability. Zone, though requiring detailed examination, can be performed via external ocular evaluation, with the original classification system being clear enough for non-ophthalmologists to use.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in our study. First, there was a selection bias towards more severe cases due to the nature of referral medical centres, as depicted by the high percentage (69.14%) of presenting VA worse than 20/200. Second, our study’s retrospective nature and the utilisation of clinical variables as predictors may be constrained by their accuracy and reliability within routine clinical practice. Moreover, the relatively small sample size and the absence of paediatric cases in our cohort could limit the robustness of our score. To address this, we employed a resampling method to validate the performance of our score and ensure its generalisability to other populations. However, further model validation with future cohorts is needed. Additionally, a gap in applicability for paediatric and elderly populations needs to be addressed. Visual outcomes in OGI are primarily determined by the nature of the injury, as demonstrated by our cohort’s results, which align with previous studies.6 8 Thus, our score seems appropriately generalisable to non-Thai populations.

In conclusion, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first revision of the OTS designed specifically for non-ophthalmologist and emergency settings. We believe that this approach provides practical value by enhancing routine accessibility and simplifying the application.
