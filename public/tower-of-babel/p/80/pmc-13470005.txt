Spatial transcriptomic atlas of aggressive osteosarcomas reveals shared immune landscape and targetable surface markers
Authors: Gaël Moquin-Beaudry, Maria Eugénia Marques Da Costa, Pierre Khneisser, Corentin Thuilliez, Baptiste Audinot, Hanane Zair, Nicolas Signolle, Michael McDermott, Marie-Dominique Tabone, Jean-Yves Scoazec, Cécile Badoual, Birgit Geoerger, Damien Drubay, Nathalie Gaspar, Antonin Marchais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteosarcoma is characterized by extensive inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity, contributing to treatment resistance and poor outcomes. Here, we present a comprehensive spatial transcriptomics analysis of osteosarcoma, encompassing primary tumors and local or metastatic relapses across diverse phenotypic subtypes. Despite this heterogeneity, we identify a nine-gene cell surface signature with theranostic potential, validated in independent datasets and shown by immunohistochemistry to be distributed across distinct tumor compartments, supporting multi-targeted therapeutic strategies. Analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment reveals systematic lymphoid exclusion, differential myeloid infiltration patterns, and a type I interferon response signature that may explain the failure of IFN-α supplementation in prior trials. Notably, we provide evidence of monocyte-derived osteoclastic differentiation within human osteosarcoma lung metastases, identifying precursor populations with complex secretory phenotypes representing potential immunomodulatory targets. This study offers biological insights and translational opportunities while providing a resource for the osteosarcoma research community.Subject terms: Bone cancer, Cancer microenvironment, Tumour immunology, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is a rare tumor, yet it remains the most common primary bone cancer in adolescents and young adults1,2. Despite decades of research, treatment options and patient survival rates have remained largely unchanged since the introduction of combined chemotherapy (CT) and surgery over 40 years ago. No Phase II trial for relapsed osteosarcoma has successfully led to a widely accepted first-line Phase III trial, and no targeted therapies against tumor cells or the tumor microenvironment (TME) are routinely available. Approximately one-third of patients with osteosarcoma experience treatment failure, leading to metastatic relapse and, ultimately, death. This highlights a critical unmet need for new therapeutic strategies3,4 aimed at improving survival rates by better controlling metastatic potential from the time of diagnosis.

Osteosarcoma is characterized by significant phenotypic heterogeneity, aneuploidy, and a high frequency of complex chromosomal aberrations and epigenetic dysregulation5. However, our current understanding of the disease’s etiology does not fully capture its complexity, highlighting a major knowledge gap in osteosarcoma biology. Tumor cell plasticity plays a crucial role in how osteosarcoma cells interact with and adapt to different TME, particularly the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME).

At the primary tumor site, osteosarcoma cells engage in continuous crosstalk with the bone microenvironment by producing extracellular matrix components and expressing bone-specific ligands such as RANKL, OPG, and CSF1. This plasticity, likely inherited from the cancer cell of origin, appears to be context-dependent. However, the regulatory mechanisms that drive or inhibit plasticity, the influence of the surrounding TME, and the impact of plasticity on prognosis and treatment response remain poorly understood.

Due to the rarity of osteosarcoma, large-scale molecular datasets have only recently provided insights into its complex biology, enabling researchers to propose new disease classifications6–8 These advances have been facilitated by research consortia, such as the Fight OsteoSarcoma Through European Research (FOSTER) consortium and the Children’s Oncology Group (COG), which have helped centralize and organize resources. Recent studies have put forth multiple yet increasingly convergent theories regarding the cellular origins of osteosarcoma and its prognostic and classification factors, marking significant progress in the field6,8,9.

The metastatic progression of osteosarcoma is a key prognostic factor and the leading cause of death among patients with osteosarcoma, with a 5-year overall survival rate below 30%10,11. Metastases most commonly develop in the lungs, while distant bone and soft tissue involvement are less frequent. Metastases are classified based on their timing: synchronous if present at diagnosis, and metachronous if they appear later, further categorized as early (within 2 years), late (after 2 years), or very late (after 10 years), with incidence rates decreasing over time12,13.

Osteosarcoma metastases are thought to follow the classical metastatic cascade, involving local invasion, intravasation into the bloodstream, survival in circulation, extravasation, dormancy, and eventual outgrowth at distant sites. However, the timing and characteristics of tumor cell dissemination in osteosarcoma remain poorly understood. Histological comparisons of primary tumors and metastases have shown differing histology in 33–40% of lung metastases14,15. Additionally, molecular analyses of matched primary and metastatic tumors have revealed a consistent increase in mutational burden and genomic instability in metastases16 as well as significant heterogeneity among metastases. These findings suggest both linear and parallel evolutionary trajectories, indicating that dissemination may occur at different time points, from early primary tumors to late-stage disease, and that metastases may follow distinct evolutionary paths.

While bulk omics methods have provided broad molecular characterization of osteosarcoma cohorts, next-generation omics technologies now allow for a deeper understanding of intra-tumoral heterogeneity. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) refers to a set of techniques that map RNA transcripts to specific spatial coordinates within a tissue sample, offering varying levels of resolution. Among these, next-generation sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics (NGS-ST) enables whole-transcriptome mapping onto histological sections, providing highly specific signals for analysis.

Unlike panel-based methods, which are limited to predefined gene sets, NGS-ST offers an unbiased approach to studying cellular and molecular dynamics within tumor samples. This allows for a more comprehensive characterization of A) cancer cell phenotypes, their distribution, and intra-tumoral heterogeneity, and B) interactions between cancer cells and the TME at both local and cohort-wide levels. While pioneer ST studies in ten osteosarcomas have offered first glimpses into heterogeneity and cellular architecture17,18, comprehensive characterization of the immune landscape and identification of actionable therapeutic targets remain limited.

In this study, we apply NGS-ST to dissect the cellular mechanisms found in 26 aggressive osteosarcomas to deepen our understanding of tumoral cellular dynamics, challenge theories about cellular plasticity in osteosarcoma, describe cancer-TIME interplay, and identify biomarkers and actionable targets for the clinic.

Results

Joint spatial transcriptomics analysis of 26 primary and metastatic relapse osteosarcomas

To study the crosstalk between tumor cells and the microenvironment of aggressive osteosarcoma, we processed 9 primary tumors and 17 metastatic or local relapses of osteosarcoma from 19 patients (median age = 16 years [range 10-23]) by next generation sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics (NGS-ST) using the Visium protocol from 10x genomics (Fig. 1A). The dataset is composed of 5 paired primary and relapse tumors (PT1-5, MR1-4, LR5), 4 single primary tumors (PT6-9), 2 lung relapses from the same patient 1 year apart (MR10A-MR10B), 2 sections from the same lung metastasis (MR11A-MR11B) and 8 single metastatic or local relapse (M/LR12-19) (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 1). Twelve of these tumors have been characterized genetically and transcriptionally by WES and bulk RNA-seq (Supplementary Table 119) and four metastatic samples were established as orthotopic and subcutaneous PDX models20,21. Clinically, 17 of the 19 patients died from relapsed disease (Supplementary Table 1). Whole slide images were independently annotated by a pathologist to identify tumor histological features (Fig. 1C). All samples were processed jointly using the CellsFromSpace (CFS) workflow (Fig. 1A)22, based on independent component analysis (ICA). This reference-free signal decomposition methodology identifies cell type-associated expression signatures, enabling the direct mapping of precise cancerous, stromal, and immune components that are shared or distinct across all samples. In this dataset, we retained 144 independent components (ICs) specific to cell types or cell activities after manual curation and annotation (Supplementary Dataset 1). To validate the robustness of the reference-free deconvolution, we performed orthogonal cell type deconvolution with two tools using a single-cell–derived reference (See Methods). Broad concordance was observed in the major compartment assignments, although finer subpopulation distinctions were less consistent, even between reference-based deconvolution methods, reflecting known limitations of reference-based approaches (Supplementary Fig. 1). ICs were used to calculate 2-dimensional Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) representations showing spots mostly clustering by sample (Fig. 1D). This is not surprising, and we consider UMAP to be an imperfect representation of spot-based ST as spots contain mixtures of different cell populations, i.e., ICs. Many ICs, especially cancer-associated ones due to inter-tumor heterogeneity, are found predominantly in single samples, which drives the clustering of spots primarily by sample.

Transcriptomic profiling of osteosarcoma heterogeneity

To study intra-tumoral heterogeneity and reduce cross-sample variability, ICs corresponding to identical broad phenotypes were combined. This allowed the cartography of different tumor phenotypes and the extraction of the associated gene markers (Fig. 2A). For each sample, cancer spots were isolated (See Method) and mean cancer phenotype compositions were calculated to assess intra- and inter-tumor heterogeneity. Tumors were clustered based on mean composition, ranging from undifferentiated to multilineage phenotypes, highlighting the variation in differentiation potential across osteosarcoma tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The generated dataset contained samples with notable features relevant to studying the TME's influence on osteosarcoma development. First, endochondral ossification at the growth plate is a highly organized process characterized by layered cellular expansion and differentiation with expression of canonical genes (Fig. 2B). In this study, beyond the high levels of differentiation found in some metastatic samples, we identified metastatic tumors phenocopying the layered structure of healthy developing bone based on the expression of ossification canonical markers (Fig. 2C). Despite the absence of the canonical master regulators of normal growth plate resting zone (RZ) activation Indian Hedgehog (Ihh) and Parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP), we detected the expression of Forkhead box A2 (FOXA2) associated with a RZ stem cell population exhibiting high self-replication and osteo-chondro progenitor potential23. We mapped expression of multiple genes associated with different chondrocytic differentiation states from the proliferation, hypertrophic and provisional calcification zones (PZ, HZ, and PCZ, respectively). These include critical regulators like the PZ-associated SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) and fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3), and HZ-associated FGFR1, Parathyroid hormone 1 receptor (PTH1R), and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R). We also mapped cartilage and calcification-associated ECM proteins, including collagen type 2 alpha chain 1 (COL2A1) and aggrecan (ACAN) of the RZ; type X collagen (COL10A1) as a critical marker of the HZ; and alkaline phosphatase (ALPL) and Hyaluronan and proteoglycan link protein 1 (HAPLN1) as key calcification factors in the PCZ. Ossification concludes in the metaphyseal ossification zone (OZ) through invasion of osteoclastic and osteoblastic cells from trabecular bone. While osteoclasts degrade newly formed ECM, osteoblasts substitute growth plate cartilage with bone ECM, including Collagens 1 and 3 alpha chains (COL1A1 & COL3A1). Mapping these phenotypes in the lung metastatic relapse sample MR15 revealed clear, semi-organized layering of subpopulations, with RZ-like stem cells towards the tumor core and an OZ-like osteoblastic phenotype at the outer margin, suggesting considerable intra-tumoral physical growth pressure (Fig. 2C).

Second, the sample pair MR11A-B, consisting of adjacent zones of a single tumor, displayed numerous tumoral vascular embolisms as annotated by the pathologist (Fig. 2D). We identified transcriptional signatures associated with the vascular-embolizing tumor subpopulation (Fig. 2E). Gremlin 1 (GREM1), a bone morphogenic protein (BMP) antagonist identified as a marker of bone osteochondroreticular stem cells in healthy bone24, was enriched in vascular-embolizing tumor populations. Additionally, multiple genes associated with cellular migration and proliferation in cancer, including POSTN25, ITGA526, TIMP127, and ACTA228, were specifically associated with these embolisms.

Third, sample MR10A offers a compelling example of the influence of the TME on osteosarcoma phenotype. While most of the tumor in this sample exhibits an osteoblastic phenotype, we identified a nodule with a distinctly chondroblastic expression signature (IC 56, Supplementary Dataset 1) developing adjacent to normal bronchial cartilage (Fig. 2F). This pattern was also observed in independent histological data from Eigenbrood et al.18. (Supplementary Fig. 4). The MR10B sample from the same patient at subsequent relapse displayed a predominantly fibroblastic phenotype, highlighting the high differentiation potential in this aggressive osteosarcoma.

These observations represent illustrative cases that underscore the remarkable phenotypic plasticity of osteosarcoma, but they remain hypothesis-generating, as their prevalence and clinical significance cannot be established in this study. Nevertheless, they raise the question of whether the microenvironment influences or selects for specific tumor phenotypes.

Osteosarcoma surfaceome analysis identifies targetable cancer-specific proteins

From a clinical perspective, the observed phenotypic diversity within and between samples raised questions about the feasibility of identifying shared therapeutic targets from the phenotyping of a few samples. To overcome this limit, we investigated the expression of surface markers by comparing the spatial expression of individual cell membrane-associated genes to the aggregated Cancer and Stroma weights distributions using Lee’s bivariate spatial association measure29. Of the 3545 assessed cell surface protein genes, 306 showed significant spatial enrichment across samples with Cancer versus Stromal regions. Functional enrichment of these genes using the Gene Ontology: Biological Process, KEGG, Reactome and Wikipathways databases highlighted diverse functional families of the enriched genes (Fig. 3A,B). While some categories were expected for bone cancer cells (biomineral tissue development, metabolic processes, transmembrane transport, signal sensing and transduction, and cell adhesion), others were more atypical (Glial ensheathment, Neuron projection morphogenesis, Synapse assembly, and muscle contraction). Eighteen genes with significant enrichment in Cancer regions across all samples were identified and categorized by broad primary function: Metabolic processes (ENO1, GPC1), membrane transport (SNTA1, FRMD6, ANKH, MELTF), microenvironment (ME) sensing or signaling (LRRC15, PTH1R, FGFR1, MAGED1, CDK16), cell adhesion (MXRA8, NCAM1, SDK2, CLMP), and ME modulation (MMP14, RHBDL2, IFITM5) (Fig. 3C). Experimental targeted therapies using CAR T cells, antibody-drug conjugates or other approaches have already been developed against six of these top targets, namely ENO130–33, GPC134–37, LRRC1538–40, FGFR141,42, NCAM143–45 (clinical trials NCT06224855 & NCT054373), and MMP1446. Background expression in normal tissues for these genes was highly variable between genes and tissues when analyzed with the GTEx multi-gene analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5A), which does not include a normal bone tissue reference. While about half of the genes displayed moderate to high expression levels in most tissues, RHBDL2, SDK2, IFITM5, LRRC15, NCMA1, and MELTF showed low expression in all tissues, notably in the lungs.

To validate the specificity of this molecular profile on an independent bulk RNAseq cohort and prune the signature further, we employed sample scoring on the Treehouse Childhood Cancer Initiative’s Tumor Compendium (TCCITC) v1147. This led to the definition of an osteosarcoma specific 9-gene diagnostic signature (FRMD6, ANKH, LRRC15, PTH1R, MXRA8, CLMP, MMP14, RHBDL2, IFITM5) by selecting individual genes with enriched expression in osteosarcoma tumors (Fig. 3D left, See methods). The 9-gene signature was further validated against bulk RNAseq cohorts of primary and relapse tumors (OS2006 and MAPPYACTS trials respectively) independent from the TCCITC and showed robust score consistency at both timepoints (Fig. 3D right). It also maintained osteosarcoma specificity at relapse against 20 other cancer types (Supplementary Fig. 5B). The 9-gene signature was used to calculate a combined expression score which confirmed the spatial tumor-specificity of the signature (Fig. 3E). This 9-gene signature was also validated against independent ST data of metastatic osteosarcoma and the single cell reference dataset and showed comparable performance with slightly improved specificity against normal cartilage tissue and other stromal populations when compared to the 23 gene osteosarcoma signature proposed by Eigenbrood et al.18. Validation at the protein level of the 9-gene surfaceome signature was conducted when validated IHC-compatible antibodies could be obtained (Fig. 3F) (MMP14, PTH1R, MXRA8, ANKH, RHBDL2, LRRC15). Protein expression closely matched mRNA expression for most targets, apart for ANKH for which lone diffuse cells and giant multinucleated cells were stained in most samples (Fig. 3F). Occasional non-tumor staining was observed, often on tissue-resident macrophages, correlating with scRNAseq expression (Supplementary Fig. 6). The surface expression of the nine proteins composing this signature represents a valuable advantage for the development of theranostic clinical applications.

Immune infiltration dynamics in metastatic osteosarcoma

To assess the modulation of the immune microenvironment related to the tumor heterogeneity, we conducted a systematic spatial correlation analysis by generating IC interaction networks for each sample to identify common cellular niches within the cohort (Supplementary Fig. 8). This methodology allows the detection of immune cell populations relative to cancer regions (See Methods Context analysis) to distinguish Cancer-excluded (CE) from Cancer-infiltrating (CI) immune populations on nine metastatic samples containing significant amounts of lung parenchyma.

This analysis first confirmed that all lymphoid populations, primarily composed of different flavors of antigen-producing plasma cells, were systematically and significantly excluded from cancerous regions (Fig. 4A).

For the myeloid compartment, of the 18 ICs annotated as myeloid, six were found to be significantly (FDR < 0.05) CI and six CE (Fig. 4B–D, Supplementary Fig. 9). Two of the CE populations corresponded to Lung-resident macrophages (IC 13: PPARG Macro and IC 31: CCL18 lung Macro) with similar expression signatures including high MARCO, C1Q, and TREM1 (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Dataset 1). Both were abundant in the lung parenchyma and excluded from tumor masses. They nevertheless tended to accumulate at the outer tumor margin (Supplementary Fig. 9A), as observed in other studies18. For CI myeloid cells, six cell types were identified with characteristic expression profiles, falling into three categories: anti-inflammatory, high interferon response, and osteoclastic (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Dataset 1). IC 66 corresponds to a M2-like C1Qhi CD14hi anti-inflammatory macrophage population present in relatively high abundance in our samples. While able to infiltrate tumors, they are also detected in the surrounding lung parenchyma (Supplementary Fig. 9B), suggesting a low tropism towards tumor tissue, but a clear infiltration capacity. Three CI cell populations showed high interferon-response signatures (ICs 74, 37 & 68, Fig. 4B–D, Supplementary Dataset 1). Among these, IC 68 specifically expresses IL7R, a receptor known to promote osteoclast differentiation48–50, and multiple heat shock proteins (HSPs), also shown to promote osteoclast formation51. This suggests that IC 68 represents an osteoclastic precursor cell population. ICs 38 and 10 also correspond to cells of osteoclastic lineage, based on their transcriptomic signatures, with IC 10 highly enriched in mature osteoclastic markers ACP5, MMP9, and CTSK (Fig. 4C). Histological analysis confirmed the mature multinucleated phenotype associated with IC 10 while IC 38-rich spots contained mononucleated myeloid cells (Supplementary Fig. 10). Together with IC 68, this describes three discrete subpopulations within the osteoclastic lineage, all of which exhibited significant tumor infiltration potential increasing with osteoclast maturity. These populations were also abundant in most of the other 17 samples analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 5C) and independently detected in other metastatic osteosarcoma ST datasets (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Osteoclastic tumor cell tropism

We confirmed the tumor tropism of osteoclastic cells in lung metastasis first by identifying regions of interest with well-defined cancerous structures, such as venous embolism, showing clear CI vs CE distribution patterns (Fig. 5A). The CD68 pan-macrophage marker was detected at the transcript and protein levels both in cancerous and non-cancerous regions. Tissue-resident CE myeloid populations were associated with the expression of Mannose Receptor C-type 1 (MRC1/CD206), displaying an accumulation of signal at the outer cancer lesion margins, particularly near venous embolisms (Fig. 5B, C, dotted lines). Secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) RNA expression was found to be associated with numerous cancer ICs and major CI myeloid ICs 66, 38, and 10 (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Dataset 1). However, protein expression was observed in three patterns: osteoid cancer ECM, mononucleated CD68+ cells and giant multinucleated CD68+ cells (Fig. 5D). This confirms the myeloid (osteoclastic) nature of the giant polynucleated cells found in osteosarcoma, even within lung metastases, along with mononucleated pre-osteoclastic SPP1 + CD68+ cells. These SPP1 + CD68+ cells were detected only within tumor tissues and localized at the core of venous embolisms, enriched along the inner margin and deep within the metastatic core (Fig. 5C, E, dashed lines). In contrast, peritumoral regions harbored clusters of CD206/MRC1 + CD68+ lung resident macrophages as predicted by ST (Fig. 5C, E). TREM1 expression paralleled that of the CE marker MRC1, whereas TREM2 was detected across CE and CI myeloid clusters with enrichment near tumor cores and vascular emboli, consistent with its proposed association with osteoclastic and immunosuppressive phenotypes (Fig. 5C-D, Supplementary fig. 12).

Taken together, these results demonstrate the ability of osteosarcoma tumors to recruit and promote the maturation of osteoclastic populations in both local and metastatic tumors, and the distinct ability of these cells to infiltrate osteosarcoma tumors as opposed to tissue-resident macrophages.

Specific markers of myeloid infiltrates in osteosarcoma tumors

To identify potential mechanisms of myeloid cell infiltration within osteosarcoma, we analyzed the transcriptomic signatures of CI cell populations and their TME. Gene set analysis of IC signatures revealed few common genes between infiltrating populations, suggesting multiple mechanisms enabling tumor infiltration by these populations (Fig. 6A). By considering all contributory genes of CI versus CE myeloid ICs, we identified 196 shared genes, 411 genes associated exclusively with infiltrating populations and 367 with excluded populations (Fig. 6B). Notably, while canonical proinflammatory markers TREM1 and IL1B were associated only with CE cells, immunosuppressive markers TREM2 and VSIG4 were expressed by both CE and CI cells. Nevertheless, multiple CI-specific genes were associated with tumor-associated macrophage migration, and inflammation promotion or response, SPP152, DPP453, NRP154, SDC455, and CCR156.

Using the MSigDB Hallmarks database, a gene set enrichment analysis of the genes lists associated with CI and CE showed similar enrichment profiles, except for significant increases in response to interferons (IFN), especially IFN Alpha Response in CI-specific signatures (Fig. 6C). As expected, most of the 60 genes associated with this signature were found between the three IFN-enriched ICs 74, 37 & 68. This suggests the presence of a tumor-intrinsic IFN-ɑ production influencing the recruitment and/or activation of certain innate immune populations.

To identify potential osteoclast recruitment mechanisms, we first confirmed the expression of canonical pro-osteoclastic receptors RANK/TNFRSF11A and CSF1R, which are both associated with mature osteoclasts (IC 10) (Fig. 6D, Supplementary Dataset 1). CCR1, a key chemotactic receptor for osteoclast recruitment, differentiation, and migration to sites of bone degradation56,57, was also associated with IC 10 (Fig. 6B, D). Both CSF1R and CCR1 were also associated with the CI C1Qhi CD14 Macro (IC 66). RANKL/TNFSF11, CSF1, and CCL5/RANTES, the respective ligands of these osteoclastic receptors displayed colocalized expression in the studied osteosarcoma samples suggesting their involvement in osteoclastic recruitment and differentiation in metastatic tumors (Fig. 6D). While no specific population was associated with RANKL/TNFSF11 expression, CSF1 expression was associated with cancer ICs including IC 80 (MMP13 hi ECM remodeling), which also expresses high levels of Vitronectin receptor ligands SPP1, TNC, FN1, and THY1.

Ligand-receptor analysis of soluble interactions among myeloid populations revealed reinforced communication between CI populations, primarily through the SPP1, CSF, CCR1, and Annexin pathways. The IL-7R-expressing monocytic population (IC 68) emerged as a central hub, ranking as a top sender and receiver of interactions, while C1Q-high CD14 macrophages (IC 66) and mature osteoclasts (IC 10) were prominent receivers (Fig. 6E). ICs 66 and 10 also responded to cancer-associated chemokines (CCL5, CCL8, CCL14) through CCR1 (Fig. 6F). ECM binding analysis identified interactions between cancer-derived collagens, laminins, thrombospondins, FN1, and TNC and myeloid receptors CD44, SDC4, and vitronectin receptor, with IC 68 expressing both SDC4 and CD44 (Fig. 6G).

Spatially aware ECM ligand-receptor analysis of receptors found in CI populations confirmed that most of the top interactions across samples implicated integrins (Fig. 6G). Integrin alpha 11 (ITGA11), associated with CI IC 74 (IFN resp Macro), binds type I collagens (COL1A1 & COL1A2), highly abundant in osteosarcoma tumors. However, expression of ITGA11’s major dimerization partner integrin beta 1 (ITGB1) was not specifically associated with this IC. However, both subunits of the Vitronectin receptor were exclusively associated with mature osteoclasts (IC 10). This receptor recognizes the R-G-D peptide pattern, present on numerous bone extracellular matrix ligands found abundantly in osteosarcoma tumors, including osteopontin (SPP158), tenascin C (TNC59,60), pleiotrophin (PTN61), integrin-binding sialoprotein (IBSP62), and periostin (POSTN63), providing evidence of its importance for the adhesion, infiltration, and maintenance of osteoclasts within tumors.

Discussion

In recent years, significant efforts have been made to enhance the molecular characterization of osteosarcoma to identify distinct disease subtypes and refine therapeutic strategies. In this study, we used sequencing-based ST to analyze 26 aggressive osteosarcoma samples, aiming to explore the diversity of cancer phenotypes and their cellular context within both primary tumors and relapse metastases. These samples were collected from 19 different patients, allowing for an investigation of tumor heterogeneity, the composition and activity of the TME, and their potential impact on disease progression, as well as the identification of therapeutic strategies in osteosarcoma.

While orthogonal validation using a reference-based deconvolution approach supported the overall cellular composition inferred by our reference-free analysis, several caveats warrant consideration. Reference-based methods inherently rely on the quality and completeness of single-cell reference datasets, which remain limited for osteosarcoma in part due to the challenges of processing mineralized tissue. At the time of analysis, as only seventeen single-cell tumor samples can be found in public repositories, it cannot capture the full phenotypic and transcriptional diversity of osteosarcoma, in addition to the processing bias associated with single-cell approaches. Moreover, cross-platform differences, annotation subjectivity, and the absence of a universally accepted single-cell atlas can propagate classification biases. In contrast, reference-free approaches, as applied here, enable unbiased discovery of context-specific gene modules, providing a more faithful representation of the underlying tumor biology.

Osteosarcoma tumors display extensive inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity, both of which were captured in our dataset. We observed diverse manifestations of intra-tumoral differentiation in metastases, including endochondral ossification-like organization, distinct gene signatures in vascular-embolizing subpopulations, and regional differentiation patterns shaped by the TME. These findings raise important questions about the cell of origin of osteosarcoma metastases and the extent to which the metastatic niche imprints phenotypic identity. This cellular plasticity may itself drive metastatic progression, as cancer cell plasticity is a recognized hallmark of metastatic dissemination64, potentially explaining why some metastases exhibited highly differentiated phenotypes.

Tumor plasticity also has direct implications for clinical management. Distinct tumor phenotypes activate different signaling pathways, resulting in variable chemosensitivity and heterogeneous availability of therapeutic targets within a single tumor. To identify targetable features common to osteosarcoma, we defined a 9-gene osteosarcoma-specific surface signature. Validation of the spatially derived signature using bulk RNA sequencing at diagnosis and relapse confirmed its specificity for osteosarcoma, while immunohistochemical analysis revealed a heterogeneous distribution of signature components across different cell populations, suggesting that no single target is uniformly expressed throughout the tumor.

Despite this heterogeneity, a validated pan-osteosarcoma surface signature offers several clinical advantages. Diagnostically, a focused immunohistochemistry panel incorporating the validated markers could be integrated into existing pathology workflows to improve accuracy in histologically ambiguous cases or to confirm osteosarcoma origin in metastatic lesions of unknown primary. For surgical applications, the surface localization of these markers renders them amenable to molecular imaging approaches, such as fluorescently-labeled antibody conjugates, that could improve real-time tumor margin delineation and guide the extent of resection. Therapeutically, the compartmentalized expression pattern we observed argues against single-target approaches and instead supports combination strategies or sequential targeting based on individual tumor profiles. Furthermore, because the signature is consistently detected across osteosarcoma tumors, it could facilitate the expansion of targeted therapies from metastatic relapse to primary disease, potentially accelerating clinical trial design. Notably, LRRC15-directed antibody-drug conjugates (ABBV-085) have completed Phase I evaluation in solid tumors, and MMP14 inhibitors are in preclinical development, providing near-term opportunities for biomarker-stratified trials in osteosarcoma, along with a strong rationale to initiate preclinical evaluation of the other targets. The minimal expression of several signature genes in normal adult tissues further supports their suitability for antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) or chimeric antigen receptor T-cell approaches, although expression in developing bone remains to be fully characterized. A practical implementation pathway would involve retrospective assessment of signature expression in existing clinical trial cohorts (e.g., EURAMOS, OS2006) to establish prognostic or predictive value, followed by prospective validation and development of standardized scoring criteria for patient selection. With initiatives such as the European OASIS program, which aims to accelerate targeted treatment development, a signature-driven precision medicine strategy for osteosarcoma is increasingly feasible.

Our ST approach enabled unbiased detection and annotation of immune cell populations within the TIME in both primary and metastatic relapse tumors, revealing distinct spatial relationships between immune and cancer cells. We confirmed the systematic exclusion of all lymphoid populations from tumor masses and further distinguished myeloid populations that were excluded from tumors versus those that actively infiltrated them.

Among excluded populations, lung-resident macrophages accumulated near the outer tumor margin in a seemingly reactive pattern rather than penetrating the tumor core. We identified a subpopulation of lung resident macrophages expressing high levels of CCL18, a chemokine implicated in lung inflammation65, associated with M2-like macrophages and involved in immune evasion across multiple cancer types66. Whether these CCL18-expressing macrophages contribute to immunosuppression at the tumor periphery or represent a reactive population warrants further investigation. In contrast, cancer-infiltrating myeloid cells exhibited a robust type I IFN response, suggesting intrinsic type I IFN production within osteosarcoma tumors despite low IFNA gene expression. This endogenous interferon signaling may help explain the lack of clinical benefit observed with pegylated IFN-α−2b maintenance therapy in good responders within the EURAMOS-1 clinical trial67, supplementation may be redundant in tumors already exhibiting type I IFN pathway activation.

C1Q-high macrophages, previously associated with a favorable prognosis in osteosarcoma68, were also found within these aggressive tumors, suggesting that their anti-tumoral effect, if present, is insufficient to prevent progression. More strikingly, we identified three populations along the osteoclastic differentiation lineage, from IL7R-expressing precursors to mature multinucleated osteoclasts, abundant in primary and metastatic samples. This observation suggests active osteoclastogenesis within the TME. Previous studies of myeloid infiltration in osteosarcoma have largely overlooked osteoclast-lineage cells; our spatial analysis reveals that they are major constituents of the myeloid infiltrate. To our knowledge, this represents the first direct evidence of osteoclastic populations in human osteosarcoma lung metastases. The IC 68 precursor cells were highly sensitive to cancer-derived chemotactic and ECM signaling cues and exhibited a complex secretory phenotype encompassing both immunostimulatory (CXCL10, CXCL11, TNFSF10) and immunosuppressive (GDF15, IL11, ANXA1) mediators, as well as factors promoting angiogenesis (VEGFC) and monocyte recruitment (CCL2, CSF1). This functional duality suggests that osteoclastic precursors may exert context-dependent effects on the TME. However, given their robust secretory phenotypes and capacity for tumor infiltration, they may represent a more tractable immunomodulatory target than mature osteoclasts, the targeting of which in previous clinical trials with the RANKL-blocking antibody denosumab at relapse69 or the biphosphonate zoledronic acid at diagnosis70,71 failed to demonstrate clinical benefit. Both CCR172 and IL-7R49,73 blockade have been shown to impair osteoclastogenesis, though further work is required to characterize the immunological and pro-tumoral properties of these precursor populations to identify optimal therapeutic strategies.

Finally, we examined the expression of TREM2, a well-established marker of immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) across multiple cancers74. In osteosarcoma metastases, TREM2 was expressed by both cancer-infiltrating and cancer-excluded myeloid populations. Cancer-excluded Lung-resident macrophages co-expressed TREM2 and TREM1, indicating a distinct immunological profile, while among infiltrating cells, TREM2 was detected in both C1Qhi macrophages and immature osteoclastic precursors. Because TREM2 signaling is required for osteoclastic differentiation75,76, its expression in osteosarcoma likely reflects lineage identity rather than immunosuppressive function alone. This widespread distribution across cell types with potentially opposing prognostic implications suggests that TREM2 may not serve as a reliable standalone prognostic marker in osteosarcoma.

Study limits

This study has several limitations. ST cohorts are inherently constrained by technical complexity and cost, and our sample size may appear modest by conventional genomics standards. However, the depth of spatial and transcriptomic information captured per sample generates a data density that fundamentally differs from bulk sequencing approaches: each tissue section yields thousands of spatially resolved transcriptomes, enabling robust identification of cellular populations and their spatial relationships. Our findings demonstrated consistent patterns across samples from multiple patients and timepoints, and key observations were validated through orthogonal methods, including immunohistochemistry and bulk RNA sequencing. Nevertheless, while comparatively large for an ST study of a rare cancer, we acknowledge that larger cohorts would strengthen statistical power and enable a more comprehensive assessment of inter-patient variability.

The 10x Visium platform is further constrained by its predefined capture area, meaning only a portion of each tissue section is profiled. This can result in incomplete spatial coverage, particularly in large or heterogeneous tissues where biologically relevant regions may be missed. Histological limitations on obtaining sufficiently large primary tumor samples reduced the robustness of conclusions at diagnosis and precluded longitudinal comparisons. Additionally, budgetary constraints prevented same-patient inter-metastatic sampling that could have clarified whether differentiation potential is conserved during metastatic dissemination and to what extent the metastatic niche shapes tumor phenotype.

These challenges are not unique to our study. Standardized international protocols for tissue harvest and preservation would help ensure sample quality for ST and other emerging technologies. Furthermore, biomarkers identified in ST cohorts require validation using cost-effective approaches, such as immunohistochemistry on whole tissue sections, to expand cohort sizes and more comprehensively assess intra- and inter-tumor heterogeneity. However, such validation is often hindered by the limited availability of robust, validated antibodies for novel or rare targets. Many spatially resolved markers lack commercially available antibodies suitable for formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues, and developing or validating new reagents requires substantial time and resources. This antibody gap represents a significant bottleneck in translating ST discoveries to clinical application.

While our ligand-receptor and pathway analyses provide spatially resolved evidence for tumor-immune interactions, these findings remain correlative. Functional validation using co-culture systems, organoids, or in vivo models will be essential for confirming the proposed mechanisms. In particular, the immunomodulatory potential of osteoclastic precursors warrants experimental investigation through CCR1 or IL-7R blockade.

Finally, ST data could be leveraged to train machine learning models that integrate with widely available modalities such as histopathology and radiological imaging. Such models could enhance diagnostic accuracy, predict disease progression, and identify biomarkers to guide personalized treatment, thereby extending the clinical impact of spatial technologies beyond specialized research settings.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive ST analysis of osteosarcoma, encompassing primary tumors and local or metastatic relapses. We provide a high-resolution molecular and cellular landscape of the disease across a phenotypically diverse cohort, ranging from globally undifferentiated tumors to highly organized osteo-chondroblastic subtypes.

Despite this heterogeneity, we identified a nine-gene cell surface signature with theranostic potential, validated in independent bulk RNA sequencing datasets and confirmed by immunohistochemistry to be distributed across distinct tumor compartments. This signature may enhance diagnostic accuracy, guide surgical margin assessment, and inform multi-targeted therapeutic strategies tailored to individual tumor profiles.

Our analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment revealed systematic exclusion of lymphoid populations and differential infiltration by myeloid subsets. We identified CCL18-expressing lung-resident macrophages accumulating at the tumor margin, and characterized infiltrating myeloid cells with a type I interferon response signature, a finding that may explain the lack of benefit from IFN-α supplementation in prior clinical trials. Notably, we provide the first direct evidence in humans of monocyte-derived osteoclastic differentiation within osteosarcoma lung metastases, identifying precursor populations with complex secretory phenotypes that may represent novel immunomodulatory targets.

Together, these findings offer new biological insights and translational opportunities for osteosarcoma. With targeted therapies already in development for signature genes such as LRRC15 and MMP14, and emerging strategies to modulate osteoclast precursors via CCR1 or IL-7R blockade, these findings may have near-term translational relevance. This dataset also constitutes a resource for the research community, enabling cross-cohort comparisons and multi-omics integration that may accelerate progress toward improved outcomes for patients with this disease.

Methods

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations and was approved by the CPP Ile-de-France III Ethics Board (MAPPYACTS, #3272), CPP Ile-de-France VII Ethics Board (OS2006, #06-017), and Gustave Roussy Institutional Review Board (OsteoMap, IRB2022-146).

Pediatric sample collection

Human patient samples used were accrued through the MAPPYACTS (NCT02613962, Molecular Profiling for Pediatric and Young Adult Cancer Treatment Stratification) and OS2006 (NCT00470223, Combined CT With or Without Zoledronic Acid for Patients With Osteosarcoma) clinical trials, and OsteoMap study for spatial transcriptomic analysis of osteosarcoma patients treated at Gustave Roussy. The study included 26 samples from 19 patients (11 male, 8 female; median age 16 years, range 10–23 years). Sex was determined from clinical records and was assigned at birth; gender identity was not collected. Written informed consent for the optional ancillary study of molecular profiling was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians (for minors) in accordance with the study’s coordinating entity’s scientific and ethics boards. Consent included permission to publish anonymized clinical and molecular data. To protect participant privacy, age is reported as ranges ([10–14], [15–19], [20–24] years) rather than exact values, and no direct identifiers are included in the published data. No compensation was provided for sample donation. Sex- and gender-based analyses were not performed, as the sample size was insufficient for adequately powered subgroup comparisons and osteosarcoma biology has not been shown to differ substantially between sexes; however, sex-disaggregated data are provided in Supplementary Table 1 to enable future meta-analyses.

Spatial transcriptomics library preparation

Sample preparation

ST was performed for 26 osteosarcoma samples (Supplementary Table 1) with Visium V1® technology (10X Genomics, California) according to Visium Spatial Gene expression for FFPE protocol (CG000407), using the human whole transcriptome probe set. Tissue areas up to 6.5 × 6.5 mm were pre-selected by a pathologist following Hematoxylin & Eosin staining. 5 µm sections of the selected areas were sectioned, dehydrated, and deposited on the Visium Gene Expression V1 chip as recommended.

Slide imaging

Mounted Visium slides were then incubated in a section dryer oven and then kept in a desiccator at room temperature overnight. According to Visium’s general protocol, deparaffinization was performed the next day by placing slides in a section dryer oven at 60 °C for 2 h followed by successive baths in xylene and ethanol with gradient concentration. H&E staining and coverslipping were then performed and Visium slides were digitized at 10x magnification using an Olympus VS120 slide scanner using the VS-ASW acquisition software (v2.9.2).

Library preparation

Upon coverslip removal, slides were placed into the Visium cassette to begin decrosslinking. To do so, Visium slides were incubated with TE buffer (Ready-to–use, Genemed) in a Thermocyler at 70 °C for 60 min. Pre-hybridization with Perm Enzyme B and Tween-20 was performed, followed by hybridization with the human whole transcriptome probe kit (PN1000364 or PN1000363). After overnight incubation, four washes with FFPE post-Hyb wash buffer were performed, followed by ligation enzyme addition and incubation in a Thermocyler at 37 °C for 60 min followed by two washes with post-ligation wash buffer. RNA digestion and probe release were performed by incubation with RNase enzyme at 37 °C for 30 min prior to permeabilized with Perm enzyme B for 40 min at 37 °C. Probe extension was then performed by incubating samples for 15 min at 45 °C with the extension enzyme, followed by elution with 0.08 M KOH, and 1 M Tris-HCL. Libraries were generated by adding 50 μl of Amp Mix (PN-2000047) and 5 μl of dual index Kit/plate Ts Set A well ID (PN-3000511) to each sample and amplified using the appropriate number of cycles, based on cDNA qPCR quantification. Amplicons were then cleaned up using SPRIselect reagent (Beckman Colter B23318), and stored at −20 °C in EB buffer until sequencing.

Library sequencing

cDNA libraries were quality controlled with DNF-915 Reagent Kit (35-5000 bp) on the 5200 Fragment Analyzer system (Agilent) prior to sequencing. All libraries were sequenced with a 10-base index read (dual index), a 28-base Read1 containing cell-identifying barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), and a 50-base Read2 containing transcript sequences using S4 FlowCells on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. Sequencing was performed to obtain an estimated read depth of at least 50k read pairs per tissue-covered spot. Image analysis and base calling were performed using the Real Time Analysis 3 software (RTA3, Illumina) with default parameters.

Sequence alignment

Full resolution images were extracted from whole slide imaging output and rotated using QuPath v0.5 (RRID: SCR_018257) to align fiducials in the expected conformation. Exported full-resolution images and sequencing reads were input into the 10X Genomics SpaceRanger v2.0.1 pipeline to be aligned against the standard GRCh38 reference genome with the count function for each sample. Samples contained between 461 and 4352 spots (total 54,588) with between 26,814 and 185,240 mean reads per spot under tissue (average 66,773, Supplementary Table 2).

Joint data processing

Preprocessing

Samples were then processed and analyzed using the CellsFromSpace (CFS) workflow22. Briefly, CFS is a R package based on the Seurat (v5.0.1) object format and uses independent component analysis (ICA) to decompose ST data without a reference dataset. To do a joint analysis of multiple samples, individual filtered matrix (h5 format) from SpaceRanger (10X Genomics) and their associated images are imported using Seurat’s Load10X_Spatial function. After normalization using the SCTransform function, all samples are merged into a single object using the merge function and joint ICA is computed with all features using the PrepNormData and ICASpatial functions from CFS to obtain 150 independent components (ICs) using the icafast method [ica package v1.0-3].

Component annotation and robustness

To ensure reproducibility of ICA-based decomposition, we implemented a semi-automatic curation pipeline combining quantitative filtering with expert biological annotation. Using the graphical interface provided by CFS, each IC was manually annotated by experts based on spatial distribution and contributory gene signature. Contributory genes were defined as genes with a z-score of 3 or higher in the ICA feature loadings matrix. In our analysis, ICs were defined on average by 138 [range 5–316] contributory genes (Supplementary Table 2). Analysis of gene lists and loadings weight, and functional enrichment of contributory gene lists using the EnrichR R package (v3.2, RRID: SCR_001575) through the Enrich_ICA function in CFS provided a detailed description of cellular functions and identities associated with each IC. This step also enables the manual elimination of non-descriptive or artifact-associated ICs to improve signal quality. After manual curation and annotation, we retained 144 independent components distributed across four major compartments: Cancer (64 ICs, 17 broad phenotypes), Stroma (50 ICs, 16 broad phenotypes), Myeloid (18 ICs, 7 broad phenotypes), and Lymphoid (12 ICs, 2 broad phenotypes). This multi-level annotation framework enables flexible aggregation that reduces noise from individual components while preserving biological resolution. Related components capturing complementary aspects of the same biological process were combined into composite signatures at the “broad” or “Type” level, further improving signal stability across samples.

Compositional analysis

For each sample, IC weights were converted to compositional data by dividing the positive weights by the total positive IC weights per spot. A 2.5% high-pass filter was applied, and the ratio recalculated to eliminate noise. For broad and Type annotation levels, compositional values of corresponding ICs were then added together for each spot.

Compositional profiling

To classify samples based on their cancer composition, spots with a Cancer value of 0.4 or above were first filtered. In every sample, the average composition of each broad cancer annotation in filtered spots was calculated and normalized using centered log-ratio (CLR), yielding a 17-feature profile per sample. Samples were clustered using Ward’s minimum variance method on Euclidian distances to obtain four clusters. Differential enrichment analysis on compositional values was done using a one-tailed non-parametric Wilcoxon test.

Single-cell reference and spatial deconvolution validation

For orthogonal validation, we performed reference-based deconvolution using a custom single-cell reference in Seurat by integrating publicly available scRNA-seq datasets from GEO (accession numbers GSE162454 and GSE152048), representing 17 individual tumors in total. After standard quality control, normalization, and clustering (Louvain algorithm with 0.5 resolution), cell types were annotated based on canonical marker expression, yielding 26 distinct broad cell types. We acknowledge that osteosarcoma lacks a comprehensive single-cell atlas and standardized cell-type definitions, which introduces classification biases and limits the generalizability of reference-based approaches, a key justification for our primary use of reference-free decomposition.

This reference was employed to run the seeded non-negative matrix factorization regression method SPOTlight v1.12.177 and robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) method from the spacexr package v2.2.178 with default parameters to deconvolute ST spots. Composition correlation using spearman’s ranked correlation was calculated after centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation of predicted spot compositions using the clr function from the R package compositions v2.079. Concordant cell type localization across methods provided orthogonal validation of the primary decomposition results.

External ST dataset validation

To further assess robustness and reproducibility, we analyzed a publicly available osteosarcoma ST dataset18 obtained from Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.14594702). Data were reprocessed using the same workflow applied to our primary dataset to generate 100 ICs. Signatures identified in our primary dataset were projected using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function. Consistent identification of key biological signatures (including tumor, myeloid, and osteoclast-lineage populations) and concordant spatial patterns across both cohorts confirmed the robustness of our decomposition framework.

Surfaceome profiling

To identify Cancer-specific genes encoding cell membrane proteins, Lee’s bivariate spatial correlation was calculated using the spdep R package (v1.380) for each sample between “Cancer” and “Stroma” Types signal densities and every cell membrane-associated gene signal density (UniProt keyword KW-1003). Lee’s L values were compared between “Cancer” and “Stroma” to identify genes specifically enriched in Cancer across all samples. 306 of the 3545 tested genes were found to be significantly enriched in all samples (one-sided Welch’s two-sample t-test, with p value adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (FDR), significance established as FDR < 0.05). Manual curation of the 306-gene list based on FDR, estimate values, and literature yielded 18 top genes of interest.

Functional enrichment of the 306 genes was done using the g:Profiler g:GOSt tool (RRID:SCR_006809)81 in Ordered Query mode against the Gene Ontology: Biological Process (GO:BP, BioMart 2024-01-17 release version), KEGG Pathway (FTP 2024-01-22 release version), Reactome (BioMart 2024-01-25 release version) and WikiPathways (20240101 version) databases with max term size = 1000. Statistical significance is assessed using a one-sided cumulative hypergeometric test with correction for multiple testing using the g:SCS (Set Counts and Sizes) threshold. Resulting enrichments were processed using EnrichmentMap (v3.5.082) and AutoAnnotate (v1.4.0) within Cytoscape (v3.10.0, RRID: SCR_016052).

Background gene expression analysis was conducted using the multi sample analysis tool from the GTEx portal (https://gtexportal.org/home/multiGeneQueryPage, accessed 01/07/25)83.

Bulk RNAseq scoring

Single and multi-gene signature specificity were evaluated against 12 656 bulk RNAseq samples from the 61 cancer subtypes containing 5 or more observations in the Treehouse Childhood Cancer Initiative’s Tumor Compendium v11 dataset47(Supplementary Table 3) using the rank-based single sample scoring method singscore (v.1.24.0)84,85. Genes from the 18-gene osteosarcoma signature were first scored individually to assess specificity in osteosarcoma against other cancers. This identified 9 genes for which osteosarcoma scored highest or second-highest on average. This 9-gene signature was then used to score all bulk RNAseq samples in the Tumor Compendium dataset and was validated using bulk RNAseq data of primary tumors from the OS2006 cohort6 and of metastatic relapse tumors from the MAPPYACTS cohort19.

An ST tumor score for each spot was also generated using the 9-gene osteosarcoma signature using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function.

Context analysis

Lee’s bivariate spatial correlation analysis was calculated using the Lee function from the R package spdep (v1.3-6)86 between all IC pairs using embedding weights in each sample to produce a spatial correlation matrix using the global L statistic. Network representations of each sample were generated using the computed spatial correlation matrices with the igraph R package (v2.0.387). IC spatial niche analysis for a specific IC was conducted by summing up all L values by IC Type annotation per sample, therein named sumweight values. Statistical cohort-wide analysis of sumweight distributions per IC between Cancer and Stroma regions was computed using paired two-tailed Student’s t-test (stats R package v4.4).

Functional enrichment of IC gene sets against MSigDB Hallmarks database was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.12.688). Statistical significance was assessed using a one-sided hypergeometric test for over-representation, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method.

Ligand-receptor interaction analysis was performed using the CellChat database (v2.1.2)89 to identify potential immune recruitment mechanisms. Log-transformed IC gene contribution scores (Sig values) were used to construct expression matrices, and filtered to include only “Secreted” or “ECM-receptor” signaling pathways from the CellChat database, excluding ACKR1 interactions involved in chemokine sequestration and degradation without signaling. Interactions required all receptor subunits to be expressed by receiver populations. Interaction scores between sender and receiver populations were calculated as the product of ligand expression in the sender and receptor expression in the receiver. For hybrid analyses, individual myeloid ICs were compared with aggregated cancer populations (e.g., Osteoblatic, Hypoxia) to assess population-specific signaling patterns. Interactions were visualized as directional network circle plots using igraph (v2.0.3), where node size represents connectivity (total degree), edge width indicates interaction strength, and arrows denote signal direction from ligand-expressing (sender) to receptor-expressing (receiver) populations. Pathway- specific analyses focused on chemokine signaling (CCL/CXCL families) to identify chemotactic mechanisms and ECM-receptor signaling for cellular binding, migration, and infiltration. Only populations with detected interactions are displayed.

Spatial pairwise ligand-receptor analysis was performed by computing Lee’s bivariate spatial correlation between ligand and receptor pairs of interest extracted from the CellTalkDB database90 for relevant samples. Interactions were filtered based on the presence of receptor genes in the contributory gene list for cell populations of interest and global L value distributions between samples plotted as boxplots to identify the most robust interactions.

Immunohistochemistry

BOND automated immunostainer instrument (Leica Biosystems) was used to perform immunohistochemistry of the following antibodies: ANKH (Abcam ab211460, polyclonal rabbit, 1:1000, Lot: 117502-2), LRRC15 (Cell Signaling 52305, clone E4X8J, rabbit, 1:75, Lot: 2), MMP14 (Abcam ab51074, clone EP1264Y, rabbit, 1:1000, Lot: 1011445-30), MXRA8 (ThermoFisher PA5-63178, polyclonal rabbit, 1:100, Lot: 000035132), PTH1R (Proteintech 29115-1-AP, polyclonal rabbit, 1:500, Lot: 00094091), and RHBDL2 (Sigma HPA043807, polyclonal rabbit, 1:50, Lot: 000015261).

Paraffin sections were processed for heat-induced antigen retrieval (ER2 corresponding EDTA buffer pH9) for 20 min at 100 °C. Slides were incubated with the antibody for 30 min at room temperature. The antibodies were detected by the Bond Polymer Refine Detection kit. The signal was revealed with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were acquired using an Olympus VS200 slide scanner using the VS200 ASW acquisition software (4.1.2).

Immunofluorescence

Simultaneous multiplex immunofluorescence staining was conducted using the OPAL protocol (AKOYA Biosciences) and BOND RX automated system (Leica Biosystems). Briefly, after dewaxing and rehydration, tissue sections were submitted to heat-induced antigen retrieval (ER2 buffer Ph9) for 20 min at 100 °C. Slides were then incubated sequentially with the following seven primary antibodies and their corresponding OPAL fluorophore (AKOYA Biosciences) in order: CD206 (Cell Signaling 24595, clone E6T5J, 1:3000, Lot: 1 & 3, 30 min at 37 °C, OPAL 650/690), TREM2 (Cell Signaling 91068, clone D8I4C, 1:100, Lot: 3 & 4, 30 min at 37 °C, OPAL 520), CD68 (DAKO M0876, clone PG-M1, 1:100, Lot: 41267758, and DAKO M0814, clone KP1, 1:1000, Lot: 41757442, both 30 min at 37 °C, OPAL 620), pan-cytokeratin (Diagnostic BioSystems Mob 190-05, clone AE1-AE3, 1:300, Lot: R396-QD & 5063-QD, 30 min at room temperature, OPAL 570), and SPP1 (Abcam ab214050, clone EPR21139-316, 1:800, Lot: GR3356288-1 & 1089668-13, 30 min at 37 °C, OPAL 780).

Sections were finally counterstained with DAPI (AKOYA Biosciences), mounted with glass coverslips (Labelians) and imaged using a Vectra Polaris histology scanner (AKOYA Biosciences) using the PhenoImager HT acquisition software (v2.1.0).

Image processing

Immunofluorescence images were visualized and processed using QuPath v0.5. Brightness and contrast adjustments were applied uniformly across entire images to optimize visualization of signal boundaries; no non-linear adjustments, selective region enhancements, or gamma corrections were applied. Adjustments were applied consistently across all samples. Raw, unprocessed images are available upon request.

Statistics and Reproducibility

ST was performed once per sample for all 26 samples included in the study. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size; samples were selected based on the availability of consented tissue and clinical annotation. No data were excluded from the analyses; however, specific analyses were restricted to sample subsets based on biological or technical criteria: immune infiltration analyses (Figs. 4–6) were performed on nine metastatic samples containing sufficient stromal tissue to enable robust signal comparison between cancer and stromal regions; immunohistochemistry and multiplex immunofluorescence validation were performed on 21 of the 26 samples, with five samples excluded due to tissue depletion or block unavailability. The experiments were not randomized, as this is an observational study of archival tissue. Investigators were not blinded to sample identity during data collection and analysis, as clinical and histopathological context were required for annotation. Representative images shown in figures reflect patterns consistently observed across analyzed samples.

Data availability

The Visium spatial transcriptomic data generated in this study have been deposited in the GEO database under accession code GSE293065. The submitted data includes the sequencing files, count matrices and H&E image obtained from SpaceRanger for each sample as well as the processed joint ICA matrices. The validation ST dataset used in this study is available through Zenodo [10.5281/zenodo.14594702], bulk RNAseq data from the UCSC Treehouse Childhood Cancer Initiative is available at [https://public.gi.ucsc.edu/~ekephart/public-data/previous-compendia.html#tumor_v11_polyA], and single cell data from GEO datasets GSE162454 and GSE152048.
