Ovarian cancer-derived ADAMTS2 promotes the omental pre-metastatic niche formation through remodeling collagen I
Authors: Jing Guo, Xinyi Wu, Qiulei Wu, Lin Huang, Wenhan Li, Yan Wei, Le Chen, Feiquan Ying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) frequently metastasizes to the omentum, yet the mechanisms driving this site-specific dissemination remain incompletely understood. Through transcriptomic profiling, we previously identified ADAMTS2 (A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motifs 2) as the most upregulated gene in highly metastatic EOC (EOC-HM) cells. Here, we investigated the functional role of ADAMTS2 in orchestrating the omental metastatic niche.ResultsIn orthotopic mouse models, silencing ADAMTS2 in EOC-HM cells significantly suppressed spontaneous intraperitoneal metastasis and extended survival. Mechanistically, we observed a negative correlation between tumoral ADAMTS2 expression and omental collagen deposition in both murine models and patient samples, as visualized by polarized picrosirius red staining. Furthermore, EOC-HM cells secreted elevated levels of ADAMTS2 compared to parental lines, and intraperitoneal injection of this ADAMTS2-rich supernatant was sufficient to reduce omental collagen density in vivo. In vitro assays confirmed that ADAMTS2 knockdown impaired the invasive capacity of EOC cells through collagen I matrices and diminished the collagenolytic activity of their conditioned media.ConclusionsThese findings identify tumor-derived ADAMTS2 as a key initiator of extracellular matrix remodeling in the omentum that facilitates subsequent metastatic colonization, providing mechanistic insight into premetastatic niche formation and suggesting ADAMTS2 as a potential therapeutic target in EOC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00903-3.Keywords: Ovarian tumor, Omental metastasis, Extracellular matrix, Metalloproteinase

Introduction

Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is the most lethal malignancy of the female reproductive system, with a 5-year overall survival of 46% [1]. The poor survival is largely due to the late stage at diagnosis in up to 80% patients with peritoneal metastases [2]. The omentum is the most frequently involved intraperitoneal organ, and patients with omental metastasis have a dismal 5-year OS of 26% [3]. However, the mechanisms underlying EOC omental metastasis remain incompletely understood [4].

The omentum is a preferred metastatic site for ovarian cancer cells [5]. The omentum is a large, adipose-rich peritoneal fold that drapes over the abdominal organs composed of adipocytes [6], immune cell-rich milky spots [7, 8], vascular networks [9], and abundant extracellular matrix components [10]. The preferred metastatic sites of solid tumors are often determined by the formation of premetastatic niches, which recruit detached tumor cells and facilitate their metastatic colonization. Recent studies suggest that, for hematogenous metastasizing tumors such as breast cancer, bone marrow-derived cells establish premetastatic niches by inducing E-selectin expression on vascular endothelium, which mediates the adhesion and extravasation of circulating tumor cells [11]. Unlike other solid tumors, ovarian cancer spreads primarily rather more often through direct intraperitoneal dissemination than through blood vessels. The existence of premetastatic niches in the omentum has been identified, characterized by typical alterations such as immune cell recruitment [12–14], altered mesothelial cells [15], activated cancer-associated fibroblasts [16] and extracellular matrix remodeling [17], collectively fostering an immunosuppressive and matrix-remodeling microenvironment conducive to tumor cell colonization [18]. However, the question of how the tumors engage the omentum is still open.

To address this question, we established a spontaneous metastatic mouse model via orthotopic transplantation coupled with in vivo selection, which successfully yielded highly metastatic ovarian cancer cell sublines (ES2-HM and ID8-HM) exhibiting pronounced omental tropism [19]. To identify the key factors driving omental metastasis, we performed transcriptomic profiling of these HM sublines versus their parental counterparts, and the results revealed significant enrichment of genes related to extracellular matrix regulation. Among these, A Disintegrin and Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin Type 1 Motifs 2 (ADAMTS2) exhibited the highest expression fold change, and subsequent histopathological validation demonstrated that ADAMTS2 was significantly associated with omental metastasis and poorer prognosis in EOC patients [20]. These findings identified ADAMTS2 as one of the most prominently upregulated genes in highly metastatic ovarian cancer cells, highlighting its potential relevance to omental dissemination. These findings trigger our interest in its potential role in omental metastasis of EOC.

ADAMTS2, a 1 211 amino acid-long enzyme encoded by the ADAMTS2 gene, belongs to the ADAMTS family of extracellular protease [21]. ADAMTS2 contains characteristic metalloprotease, disintegrin-like, and thrombospondin type I motifs, and functions as a procollagen N-proteinase responsible for cleaving the N-propeptide of type I procollagen, thereby facilitating collagen fibril maturation and extracellular matrix assembly [22]. Recent studies have revealed that ADAMTS2 possesses collagenase activity, capable of cleaving the C‑propeptide of collagen I, which suggests a potential role in collagen quality control [23, 24]. Emerging evidence indicates that ADAMTS2 is upregulated in several malignancies (e.g., lung and breast cancers) and promotes tumor invasion by remodeling the tumor-associated extracellular matrix [25].

Here, we demonstrate the supportive role of tumoral ADAMTS2 in ovarian cancer omental metastasis in a spontaneous metastatic mouse model, which is in consistence with the positive association between ADAMTS2 level and omental metastasis observed in EOC patients. Moreover, we found ADAMTS2 might remodel the omental microenvironment by cleaving type I collagen, which facilitated the invasion of tumor cells. These findings highlight ADAMTS2 as a driver of EOC omental dissemination, providing a novel perspective on premetastatic niche formation.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and tumor conditional medium (TCM)

The murine ovarian cancer (OC) cell line ID8 expressing firefly luciferase was a gift of Xiao Haihua’s laboratory, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China. The human OC cell line ES2 cells were purchased from China Center for Type Culture Collection, Wuhan, China. ES2-HM and ID8-HM cells were established via in vivo selection according to the previous report [8]. All cell lines were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (L310KJ, BasalMedia Technologies, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FSP500, Excell, China). For subcutaneous inoculation, cells were collected in a logarithmic growth phase. The cells have been authenticated using short tandem repeat profiling within the last two years and routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector (D101, Vazyme, China) prior to experimental use. When the cell density reached 85–90%, the original culture medium was removed and the cells were washed once with PBS. Then 10 ml of serum-free culture medium was added and continue culturing for 24 h. The cell culture supernatant, as known as TCM, was collected and filtered through a 0.22-micron filter. The serum-free medium was used as the control medium. The TCM was stored at 4 ℃.

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals of China and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Female BALB/c Nude mice aged 4 weeks (Charles River, China) and female C57BL/6 N aged 4 weeks (Charles River, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. The activities of mice were observed daily. Orthotopic inoculation was conducted on mice aged 5 weeks (4 mice per group) according to the protocol reported previously [7]. Briefly, tissue blocks (sized around 0.5 mm3) derived from subcutaneous tumors were orthotopically implanted into the ovarian bursa of the mice. The blocks were approximately 0.5 mm³ in volume and were macroscopically matched to ensure a consistent initial tumor burden across groups. The initial tumor burden was assessed 5 days post-implantation using bioluminescent imaging. The bioluminescent imaging was conducted to monitor the progress of tumor twice every 7days. For the pre-metastasis model, mice were euthanized and tissues were harvested approximately 12 days after model establishment. For the survival assay, mice were euthanized when obvious ascites was developed and their lifespan was recorded. The tumor-free models were conducted on mice aged 5 weeks (4 mice per group), and 200 µl TCM or the control medium were injected intraperitoneally every two days. The body weights were recorded before each injection. After 15 days, the mice were euthanized and the omentum and mesentery were collected.

Human subjects

The research protocol, including the use of human samples, was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided informed written consent. All research procedures were performed in accordance with the approved protocols. The associated information of samples used in this study is provided in the additional files (Table S1 and S2).

Quantitative reverse transcription- polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

titative reverse transcription- polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)Total RNAs of cells were isolated using VAMNE Magnetic Cell/Tissue Total RNA Kit (RMA101-C2-P1, Vazyme, China), followed by reverse transcription with ABScript III RT Master Mix (RK20429, Abclonal, China). qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RK21203, Abclonal, China) on a qTOWER3 Real-Time PCR System (analytikjena, Germany). The relative expression of each mRNA was normalized using 2−ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The following primers (Tsingke, China) used were used: 5’-CTGGCAAGCATTGTTTTAAAGGA-3’ (forward primer) and 5’-GGAGCCAAACGGACTCCAA-3’ (reverse Primer) for ADAMTS2. 5’-CCTTCCTGGGCATGGAGTC-3’ (forward primer) and 5’-TGATCTTCATTGTGCTGGGTG-3’ (reverse Primer) for ACTB.

Short hairpin RNA transfection

The ADAMTS2-knockdown cells were generated via lentiviral transduction of short hairpin RNA (shRNA) conducted by Genechem Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Transfection was performed in 96-well plates. The transfection was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 10,000 cells were seeded per well. And an appropriate volume of viral stock was added according to the viral titer to achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 15–30. An appropriate concentration of Blasticidin S (S180J0, BasalMedia Technologies, China) was added for continued selection after 16–24 h.

The knockdown efficiency was validated by PCR and Western blot at both mRNA and protein levels. The target sequences of the ADAMTS2 shRNAs were as follows: 5’- CGGACGATGACTACAACAT − 3’ (shRNA1) and 5’- CCACGATGAATACCACGAT − 3’ (shRNA2). The negative control (shNC) was provided by the supplier, the sequence of which was 5’-CTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3’.

Bioluminescent imaging (BLI)

For in vivo imaging, Disease progression was monitored by BLI on an in vivo imaging system (IVIS, Lumina II, Caliper Life Science, USA) twice every 7 days. The firefly luciferase substrate D-luciferin potassium salt (LS003, Guangzhou Biolight Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was intraperitoneally injected into the mice at a dose of 150 mg/kg, 15 min prior to imaging. During imaging, isoflurane was administered to keep the mice anesthetized. For ex vivo imaging, D-luciferin potassium salt solution (0.15 mg/mL) was applied to the tissue surface and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 2 min prior to imaging. The radiance value was measured by Living Image® Software (4.3.1, Caliper Life Science, USA) using the region of interest (ROI) tools.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) Staining

Tissues were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin, and the sections were used for IHC and HE staining. The HE staining was conducted by Wuhan Pinuofei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. In brief, after routine deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections stained with hematoxylin, differentiated in acid alcohol, blued in dilute ammonia water, and counterstained with eosin. Finally, the slides were dehydrated and mounted. For IHC, the sections were subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in pH 9.0 Tris-EDTA buffer after routine deparaffinization and rehydration. The following staining steps were conducted using a rabbit biotin-streptavidin horseradish peroxidase detection system (SP-9001, ZSGB-BIO, China). The ADAMTS2 antibody (PA5-78728, Invitrogen, USA) was diluted at 1:400, the ki-67 antibody (27309-1-AP, Proteintech, USA) was diluted at 1:4000, the cleaved-caspase3 antibody (#9664, CST, USA) was diluted at 1:400 and the Collagen Type I Alpha 1 (COL1A1) antibody (A24112PM, Abclonal, China) was diluted at 1:4000. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The staining images of tumor tissues were evaluated by two researchers, according to the staining intensity, the criteria of which was described in the previous published studies [20]. The mean optical density (OD) of COL1A1 staining were measured by Fiji [26]. The ki-67 positive ratios were calculated by QuPath 0.6.0 [27].

5-Ehtynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

A Cell-Light EdU kit (C10310-1, RiboBio, China) was used to detect the proliferative proportion of cancer cells. The assay was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescent images were captured at a magnification of 200×. At least 10 images were captured for each sample. Edu positive cell (red) numbers and total cell (blue) numbers were counted and Edu positive ratios of every image were calculated. The average Edu positive ratio was calculated for each sample. The assays were performed in triplicate.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

In migration assays, 50 000 cells suspended in 300 µl of serum-free medium were added to the upper compartment of permeable transwell insert in a 24-well plate (#3422, Corning, USA), and 400 µl of DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower compartment. Any air bubbles that may have existed under the chamber were eliminated. The plates were then incubated at 37℃ for 24 h. The non-invading cells on the apical side of the chamber were carefully removed with a cotton swab. Left cells were stained by calcium AM (C1429, Invitrogen, USA), and images were acquired of the upper, lower, left, right, and middle quadrants of the membrane under a fluorescence microscope at a magnification of 100×. The cell number was counted and averaged for each sample. For invasion assays, the transwell insert was coated with 200 µl of Matrigel (40183ES10, Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) diluted at 1:20–200 µl mix of Matrigel diluted at 1:20 and collagen I (40125ES50, Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) at a concentration of 50 µg/ml. The inserts were incubated at 37℃ for 2 h before the cells were seeded. The assays were performed in triplicate.

Western blot

For cell lysate, total cellular proteins were extracted by RIPA (P0013B, Beyotime, China) with protease inhibitor cooktail (P8340, Sigma, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For cell culture supernatant, proteins in supernatant were concentrated by Amicon® Ultra-4 Centrifugal Filter (UFC8030, Millipore, the USA) using a swing-bucket rotor at 2000 x g for 20 min in 4 ℃. Proteins were quantified by BCA assay (P10012, Beyotime, China) and 10 µg of each sample was loaded for blotting process. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-hADAMTS2 (1:1000, A10272, Abclonal, China), anti-mADAMTS2 (1:2000, 31032-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-GAPDH (1:10000, A19056, Abclonal, China) and anti-COL1A1 (1:2000, A24112PM, Abclonal, China). Ponceau S (P0022, Beyotime, China) was used to stain culture supernatant western blot for total protein concentrations. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Picrosirius red staining

Picrosirius red (G1472, Solarbio, China) was used to stain collagen in paraffin tissues. The assay was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, after deparaffinization and rehydration, tissue sections were stained with Weigert’s hematoxylin and picrosirius red. Then tissue sections were washed in two changes of acidified water. Finally, sections were dehydrated and cleared. Images were acquired at a magnification of 100× or 200×. Collagen fibers were stained red under bright-field microscopy. The distribution of type I collagen (orange-red fibers) was observed under a polarized light microscope (Olympus IX73). The positive ratios were analyzed by Fiji based on the bright-field micrographs.

Analysis of collagen I degradation

Collagen cleavage was analyzed according to a reported procedure with appropriate modifications [11]. In brief, 10 µl collagen I (40125ES50, Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was added into the sterile 1.5 ml tube at a concentration of 2 mg/ml. And 200 µl TCM with 2 µl 1 M HEPES (B110JV, BasalMedia Technologies, China) was added. The tubes were incubated at 37 ℃, 5% CO2. After 16 h, the mixture was centrifuged at 15,086 ×g and 4℃ for 10 min. The supernatants were mixed with loading buffer and cooktail. Digestion products were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Blue. Degraded collagen was detected by western blot following the procedure described above. Serum-free medium and 30U/ml collagenase I (40507ES76, Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were utilized as negative and positive control, respectively. The assays were performed in triplicate.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Graphpad Prism 10.1.2 was used to perform all statistical analysis. All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or in quartiles. The differences between two groups were analyzed by unpaired or paired t-test, Welch’s t-test or Mann-Whitney test. The differences among three groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance. KM analyses were performed in survival analyses and log-rank test was utilized. The results were considered to be statistically significant at P values < 0.05. More details were provided in figure legends.

Results

Tumoral ADAMTS2 promotes ovarian cancer omental metastasis

To investigate the functional role of ADAMTS2 in EOC omental metastasis, we established ES2-HM cells with stable ADAMTS2 knockdown through transfecting shRNA targeting ADAMTS2 (ES2-HMshADAMTS2). According to the PCR and Western blot analyses, shRNA #2 effectively suppressed ADAMTS2 protein expression in ES2-HM cells. Consequently, cells transfected with shRNA #2 were selected for further investigation (Fig. 1A and B and S1). Orthotopic xenograft models were generated in nude mice using ES2, ES2-HMshNC, and ES2-HMshADAMTS2 cells (Fig. 1C). All the cells were pre-labeled with luciferase, and tumor signal progression was monitored via BLI twice every 7 days. The ES2-HMshNC tumors exhibited earlier and wilder metastasis than the ES2-HMshADAMTS2 tumors, while the ES2 tumors barely metastasized until day 22 after tumor inoculation (Fig. 1D). Quantitatively, the overall intraperitoneal tumor burden was significantly decreased in the ES2-HMshADAMTS2, compared to the ES2-HMshNC group (Fig. 1E). At the experimental endpoint, the metastasis of omentum was confirmed by ex vivo BLI (Fig. S2a, S2b). No significant difference in tumor proliferation or tumor apoptosis was observed in vivo, as confirmed by Ki67 immunohistochemical staining (Fig. S2c) and cleaved-caspase3 immunohistochemical staining (Fig.S2d). Survival of the mice was significantly different between groups, with an extra 6.5-day survival in the ES2-HMshADAMTS2 group over the ES2-HMshNC group (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these findings suggested that high ADAMTS2 expression promoted ovarian cancer metastasis to the omentum in vivo.

Tumoral ADAMTS2 level is negatively associated with omental collagen deposit

Given the role of ADAMTS2 in type I collagen processing, we then analyzed the correlation between tumoral ADAMTS2 expression and collagen deposition in the omentum. The tumors derived from ES2-HMshNC had the highest expressions of ADAMTS2, and those from ES2 had the lowest expressions (Fig. 2A). We found in the omentum of mice bearing ovarian xenografts derived from ES2, ES2-HMshNC, and ES2-HMshADAMTS2 cells after metastasis was established, collagen fiber content and the expression of COL1A1 were significantly lower in the ES2-HMshNC group than in the other two groups (Fig. 2A). ADAMTS2 knockdown partially restored collagen Ⅰ deposition, although levels remained reduced compared with the ES2 group (Fig. 2B). Consistently, in ovarian cancer patients, higher ADAMTS2 expression was associated with lower collagen deposition in the omentum (Fig. 2C, S3a, Table S1). In the omentum of mice before metastasis was established, which was confirmed by BLI ex vivo (Fig S3b, S3c). ADAMTS2 knockdown also partially restored collagen Ⅰ deposition (Fig. 2D and E). Furthermore, comparing the omentum of ovarian cancer patients without omental metastasis and patients with ovarian non‑malignant tumors, we found that tumor formation reshaped the omental microenvironment, resulting in a higher level of collagen fiber deposition (Fig. 2F, Table S2). These results suggest that tumor-derived ADAMTS2 negatively regulates collagen accumulation in the omentum, which may contribute to enhanced omental metastatic capability of cancer cells.

Highly metastatic EOC cells form an omental pre-metastatic niche with decreased collagen deposit

To further investigate how ADAMTS2 influences the omental pre-metastatic niche, we obtained ES2, ES2‑HM, ID8, and ID8‑HM cell lines and found that highly metastatic cells and TCM exhibited higher ADAMTS2 expression (Fig. 3A and B, S4). Mice were treated every two days with TCM from ES2 and ES2‑HM cells or control medium, recorded weight (Figure S5a) and were euthanized on day 15 (Fig. 3C). We observed that the omentum of mice treated with tumor conditioned medium showed higher collagen Ⅰ content compared with the control group, indicating that tumor cells can remodel the omental microenvironment. Notably, the highly metastatic group reduced collagen Ⅰ fiber deposition in the omentum, suggesting that highly metastatic EOC cells may form an omental pre‑metastatic niche through decreased collagen deposition (Fig. 3D). A similar reduction in collagen was observed in the mesentery of the ES2‑HM group compared with ES2 group (Fig. 3E), suggesting pre‑metastatic niche remodeling in mesentery. In the ID8 and ID8‑HM models (Fig. 3F, S4b), analysis of omental and mesenteric tissues showed a consistent trend that ID8‑HM group displayed lower collagen Ⅰ deposition than ID8 group (Fig. 3G and H). These results suggested that the TCM from EOC cells could up-regulated the collagen levels in the omental and mesenteric niches. While, highly metastatic EOC cells may form an omental pre-metastatic niche with decreased collagen Ⅰ deposit.

ADAMTS2 processes collagen type I in vitro

To investigate the function of ADAMTS2 in epithelial ovarian cancer metastasis, we performed transwell migration and invasion assays (Fig. 4A). Highly metastatic cells exhibited significantly enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities (Fig. 4B). The invasive capacity of ES2-HMshADAMTS2 cells was markedly reduced when type I collagen was added to the matrix, indicating that low ADAMTS2 expression impairs the ability of highly metastatic cells to degrade collagen I, thereby attenuating their invasion in vitro (Fig. 4B). The TCM from ES2-HM cells could enhance the invasion of ES2 cells through Matrigel/collagen matrices. Moreover, compared to the ES2-HMshADAMTS2 cells, the TCM derived from ES2-HMshNC had a stronger ability to facilitate the invasion of ES2 cells in a concentration-dependent manner in the presence of matrigel supplemented with type I collagen (Fig. 4C and D). In contrast, EdU assays (Fig. 4E) showed no significant difference in proliferation between the ES2-HMshADAMTS2 and the ES2-HMshNC cells, thereby ruling out the effect of cell proliferation on the observed phenotype. The in vitro assay revealed that collagen I could be cleaved in the presence of the TCM from ES2-HMshNC, with the observation of small molecule proteins in the lane (Fig. 4F and G, S5, lane 3 and 7). While, the TCM from ES2-HMshADAMTS2 cells could not cleave collagen type I. Together, these findings demonstrate that ADAMTS2 promotes epithelial ovarian cancer cell invasion primarily through its collagen‑degrading activity.

Discussion

MMPs, ADAMs, and ADAMTSs belong to the metalloproteinase family and are involved in regulating the degradation and remodeling of the extracellular matrix [28]. Studies indicate that MMPs require proteolytic activity to influence VEGF secretion, thereby promoting ovarian cancer metastasis [29]. Under chronic stress conditions, the upregulation of MMP2 and MMP9 in ovarian cancer enhances angiogenesis and increases the invasive potential of ovarian cancer cells [30].

This study revealed that ADAMTS2, as a substrate-selective enzyme, promoted omental metastasis in ovarian cancer by degrading type I collagen, deepening our understanding of the functional specialization among matrix metalloproteinases in ovarian cancer metastasis, and providing a novel theoretical foundation for developing targeted therapies against the matrix components and their downstream signaling pathways.

The omentum is an apron-like organ of folded peritoneum that extends from the stomach and covers the intestines, serving as a common metastatic target for intra‑abdominal tumors such as ovarian cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer [31]. Studies have demonstrated that ADAMTS2 inhibits tumor growth by suppressing the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment in breast cancer, while simultaneously facilitating efficient lung metastasis [25]. These data suggest that ADAMTS2 exhibits anti‑tumor functions that constrain early‑stage progression yet promote high‑efficiency metastasis. In this study, we observed that ADAMTS2 was predominantly expressed in tumor cells, which was also detected in some fibroblasts and perivascular smooth muscle cells. Notably, studies have reported that ADAMTS2 can also be expressed by other cells in TME, including monocytes [32] and macrophages [33], suggesting that microenvironment-derived ADAMTS2 may contribute to metastatic dissemination. Further studies are required to elucidate the functional roles of ADAMTS2 from non-tumor cell sources in shaping the metastatic niche.

Collagen is the most abundant protein in mammals and within the extracellular matrix, accounting for 25%-35% of total body protein [34, 35]. Types I, III, and V collagen are the main components of omental collagen, with type I collagen being the most abundant [36]. Fibrillar collagen type I is a major component of tumor desmoplasia and has been causally linked to tumor cell survival and metastasis in many cancer types [37]. It has been demonstrated that type I collagen serves as the primary enzymatic substrate for ADAMTS2 [38]. In this study, the TCM from ES2-HM cells could degrade the type Ⅰ collagen in vivo. We observed that highly metastatic cells reduced the deposition of type I collagen in the pre-metastatic omentum. This suggests that collagen may play distinct roles at different stages of ovarian cancer progression. Previous studies have suggested that ADAMTS2 may have context-dependent roles in cancer, showing either tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive effects depending on tissue and microenvironmental conditions [25, 39]. As a procollagen-processing enzyme, ADAMTS2 can influence collagen organization and matrix properties [17, 22, 40]. In our study, ADAMTS2 appeared to facilitate omental metastasis by remodeling type I collagen in the pre-metastatic niche, suggesting a potential contribution to creating a microenvironment permissive for tumor colonization [10]. A previous study employed ADAMTS2 overexpression in HGSOC and demonstrated that it promoted epithelial–mesenchymal transition and enhances tumor cell invasion, thereby facilitating metastatic potential [39]. Recent studies have demonstrated that extracellular matrix remodeling, particularly alterations in collagen organization, represents an important feature of metastatic niche formation, influencing both immune cell recruitment and activation [41, 42], and may influence stromal cell behavior within the local microenvironment [43–46]. Although immune microenvironmental changes were not assessed in the present study, whether ADAMTS2-mediated collagen remodeling contributes to additional stromal or immune alterations during omental pre-metastatic niche formation warrants further investigation.

Current limitation includes the lack of direct rescue validation in vivo. Future studies will investigate whether ADAMTS2 overexpression promotes EOC cell metastasis by establishing stably ADAMTS2-overexpressing EOC cells and utilizing genetically engineered mouse models with microenvironmental cell-specific ADAMTS2 knockout. Given that ADAMTS2 facilitates metastasis by reducing collagen deposition, further investigations will also explore whether intraperitoneal supplementation of appropriate levels of collagen can inhibit omental tumor metastasis.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrated that tumor-derived ADAMTS2 promoted omental metastasis in ovarian cancer by reducing the deposition of type I collagen in the omental microenvironment, thereby facilitating the formation of a permissive pre-metastatic niche. This study revealed the mechanism for ADAMTS2 in driving EOC progression and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target against metastatic disease.
