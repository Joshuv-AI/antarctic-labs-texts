Identifying types of non‐pharmacological interventions administered by healthcare providers for older adults with neurocognitive disorders who have lower urinary tract symptoms: A scoping review
Authors: Kazue Ishikawa, Aki Kawada, Tomoko Kamei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsOlder adults with neurocognitive disorders (NCDs) often experience lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), which reduce quality of life (QOL) and increase care needs. Although non‐pharmacological interventions are recommended, their types and applications have not been comprehensively synthesized by severity levels or care settings. This scoping review aimed to identify the types of non‐pharmacological interventions administered by healthcare providers for older adults with NCDs who have LUTS, and to classify them by severity levels and care settings. This represents the first comprehensive attempt to organize this information, providing foundational data for developing clinical implementation models.MethodsThis review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, CINAHL Ultimate, and ICHUSHI Web was conducted for studies published through July 31, 2024. Eligible studies included participants aged 65 years or older with NCDs and LUTS. These interventions were categorized by type and mapped according to the severity of NCDs.ResultsEighteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Interventions were categorized into individualized, behavioral, cognitive, and educational interventions, based on the severity levels of NCD. Individualized voiding schedules, care plans, and caregiver education were employed across care settings.ConclusionNon‐pharmacological interventions were mapped into four types based on categories and severity levels of NCD. In milder conditions, interventions emphasized self‐management, such as pelvic floor muscle exercises and cognitive stimulation therapy. In more advanced conditions, caregiver‐led interventions and assistive technologies such as sensors and bladder scanners were employed.

INTRODUCTION

Older adults with neurocognitive disorders (NCDs) who also have lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) experience reduced quality of life (QOL) and diminished physical function (World Health Organization, 2021). The prevalence of urinary incontinence ranged from 11% at home to 93% in facilities (Averbeck et al., 2017). These coexisting conditions increase medical and long‐term care needs and pose a significant public health challenge due to their impact on healthcare costs (Japan Cabinet Secretariat, 2023). Additionally, family caregivers often have limited coping strategies, which increases their caregiving burden (Wagg & Lee, 2021).

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM‐5‐TR) classified NCDs into two main categories: mild neurocognitive disorder (mild NCD) and major neurocognitive disorder (M‐NCD). Mild NCD represents a prodromal stage of M‐NCD. M‐NCD is further divided into three severity levels: mild, moderate, and severe (American Psychiatric Association, 2023). Importantly, older adults with M‐NCDs are at least three times more likely to be diagnosed with urinary incontinence, a common lower urinary tract symptom, than peers with normal cognitive functioning (Grant et al., 2013).

The number of older adults with M‐NCDs is projected to reach 139 million worldwide by 2050 and 5.84 million in Japan by 2040 (Japan Cabinet Secretariat, 2023; World Health Organization, 2021). Therefore, integrated care strategies for older adults with mild NCD or M‐NCDs with LUTS are urgently needed (Liu, 2025).

The International Continence Society recommended non‐pharmacological interventions, including pelvic floor muscle exercises, physical exercises, prompted voiding, and individualized voiding schedules, as safe and effective (International Continence Society, n.d.; Booth & Bliss, 2020), with demonstrated effectiveness in older adults with mild M‐NCD (Stenzelius et al., 2015). Toileting behavior relies on multiple cognitive functions, including attention, memory, orientation, and executive functions (Nishimura, 2008). As cognitive function declines, urinary incontinence tends to increase (Bartolone et al., 2021), and contributing factors become multifactorial (Johnson II & Smith, 2019); yet there is no clear framework for non‐pharmacological interventions tailored to specific severity levels of NCDs (Wagg & Lee, 2021). Hence, clinicians face significant challenges in selecting and implementing appropriate interventions in practice (Ostaszkiewicz et al., 2023).

Previous systematic and scoping reviews have reported only on urinary management in cognitively normal individuals (Buck et al., 2024; Ostaszkiewicz et al., 2023) or have focused on pharmacological interventions for M‐NCDs (Averbeck et al., 2017). Therefore, a comprehensive review that organizes interventions for mild NCD or M‐NCD, considering the diversity of care settings and differences in interventions across healthcare providers, remains absent. Furthermore, evidence for non‐pharmacological interventions for LUTS other than urinary incontinence is also limited (Wagg & Lee, 2021). For these reasons, a scoping review was deemed the most appropriate methodology for comprehensively identifying, organizing, and synthesizing the available evidence.

OBJECTIVES

The objectives of this scoping review were to identify the types of non‐pharmacological interventions administered by healthcare providers to older adults with mild NCD or M‐NCDs who have lower urinary tract symptoms; to map these interventions by NCD category and severity across all care settings; and to identify existing gaps.

Research questions

This review aims to address the following research questions: What non‐pharmacological interventions have been reported for older adults with mild NCD or M‐NCDs who experience lower urinary tract symptoms, and how are these interventions characterized? What gaps are evident?

METHODS

Study design

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O'Malley (2005), refined by Levac et al. (2010) and the Joanna Briggs Institute (Aromataris et al., 2024; Peters et al., 2020). The review process was validated using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist (Tricco et al., 2018). The protocol was registered in the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/MKBDA).

Identifying relevant studies

Types of sources

This scoping review included studies employing qualitative, quantitative, mixed‐methods, descriptive, and case study designs. Quantitative designs included randomized and non‐randomized controlled trials, pre‐post intervention studies, quasi‐experimental studies, observational studies, and cohort studies. Qualitative designs were accepted without restrictions. Conference abstracts, protocols, reviews (e.g., systematic, scoping, and general reviews), and gray literature were excluded.

Search strategy

Combinations of keywords related to older adults, NCDs, lower urinary tract symptom management, and non‐pharmacological interventions were used (Appendix S1). The detailed search strategies for all databases, including database‐specific controlled vocabulary terms and search operators, are provided in Appendix S1. Although specific search terms were adapted to each database's indexing system to optimize retrieval, the conceptual structure of the search remained consistent, supporting transparency and reproducibility. Each database employs distinct controlled vocabularies; therefore, identical search terms across all databases could exclude relevant studies. The search was limited to studies published in English or Japanese. Articles published from the inception of each database through July 31, 2024, were included from the following databases: PubMed, Embase, CINAHL Ultimate, and ICHUSHI Web (Japanese Medical Journal Association database). Additionally, supplementary hand‐searching was conducted to identify further relevant studies. This included screening reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews, forward‐citation searching using Google Scholar, and reviewing recent issues of key journals in geriatric nursing and continence care. ICHUSHI Web uses a Japanese thesaurus to comprehensively index research conducted in Japanese clinical nursing and care settings. This enables the inclusion of Japanese‐language literature that is underrepresented in international databases, thereby minimizing language bias. Because factors such as toileting behavior, urinary habits, and care practices may be influenced by cultural contexts, it is important to include research findings from Japanese clinical settings. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the Japanese search terms and their English translations were documented in Appendix S1. The search strategy was developed with guidance from a university librarian.

Exclusion criteria

Under non‐pharmacological interventions, treatments administered solely by physicians (e.g., pharmacological therapy or surgery), interventions performed without prior planning, catheter management, and interventions not involving healthcare providers were excluded.

Evidence selection

Two authors (KI and AK) independently screened titles and abstracts of studies that met the search criteria, then assessed the full‐text articles. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. The search results were presented using the PRISMA flow diagram (McGowan et al., 2020), and the reasons for excluding full‐text articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were documented. The full‐text analysis was incorporated into the final scoping review.

Data extraction

Two authors (KI and AK) independently extracted data using a predefined structured format: author, year, country, study design, sample size, participant characteristics, intervention characteristics, nonpharmacological intervention type, and outcome measures. When outcome data were unclear, we contacted the authors by email and requested the data.

Intervention classification

Interventions were classified into four main types: (i) individualized interventions, (ii) behavioral interventions, (iii) cognitive interventions, and (iv) educational interventions, based on the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (Great Britain) (2018), Okano et al. (2020), and the Pharmaceutical and Medical Device Act (Government of Japan, 1960). When an intervention could not be clearly classified into one of the four predefined domains, or when multiple distinct components were implemented simultaneously, each component was classified individually to ensure comprehensive categorization.

Neurocognitive disorder classification

In the DSM‐5‐TR framework, NCDs were classified as mild NCD and M‐NCDs. M‐NCDs were further divided into mild, moderate, and severe categories based on severity. In this scoping review, NCDs were classified into four groups: (i) mild NCD: can maintain activities of daily living with some effort; (ii) mild M‐NCD: difficulties with instrumental activities of daily living; (iii) moderate M‐NCD: difficulties with basic activities of daily living; (iv) severe M‐NCD: completely dependent on others (American Psychiatric Association, 2023). For clarity, the categories are abbreviated as follows: mild NCD, mild M‐NCD, moderate M‐NCD, and severe M‐NCD. In each study, the average score on the cognitive assessment tool was mapped to the four severity classifications according to the scoring criteria specific to each instrument. Living environments were categorized as “facility” if participants did not reside in a home setting.

RESULTS

Search and study selection

The literature search identified 4442 articles, and after excluding duplicates, 3045 articles were subjected to primary screening. This resulted in 67 articles that underwent secondary screening. Applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria to these articles resulted in the final acceptance of 18 articles (Figure 1).

Individualized interventions

Individualized interventions were divided into two types: individualized care plans and voiding schedules. These interventions were tailored to older adults' needs and physical and cognitive status and were part of a multicomponent approach that combined behavioral, cognitive, and educational strategies. They served as key components of continence management. Care plans were developed based on evaluations of physical or bladder function, while voiding schedules were developed using diaries or postvoid residual measurements and customized to each individual's voiding abilities and habits. These interventions were delivered in various settings, including hospitals, facilities, and homes.

Hospital‐based interventions

For older adults with mild M‐NCD, individualized care plans were developed based on evaluations of bladder and physical function (Takahashi et al., 2016). Continence teams developed individualized voiding schedules using postvoid residual data (Takahashi et al., 2016). For those with moderate M‐NCD, interdisciplinary teams developed individualized care plans through goal setting (Poynter et al., 2013). For those with severe M‐NCD, ultrasound bladder scanner data were used to create toilet guidance and voiding schedules (Takimoto & Taniguchi, 2021).

Facility‐based interventions

For older adults with moderate M‐NCD, individualized care plans provided emotional support through meetings with advanced‐practice nurses (Ryden et al., 2000), and healthcare providers adjusted these plans during case conferences (Kohler et al., 2018). Advanced‐practice nurses helped caregivers implement individualized voiding schedules (Ryden et al., 2000). A diaper sensor system with alarms supported these schedules by notifying caregivers and prompting responses (Lancioni et al., 2011). In severe M‐NCD, individualized care plans and voiding schedules were designed using person‐centered care principles (Wijk et al., 2018).

Home‐based interventions

For those with mild NCD, nurses developed voiding schedules using voiding diaries (Colling et al., 2003). For moderate M‐NCD, caregivers adjusted individualized care plans to implement customized voiding schedules (Jirovec & Templin, 2001). In collaboration with caregivers and healthcare providers, nurse practitioners created individualized voiding schedules based on voiding diaries (Engberg et al., 2002; Jirovec & Templin, 2001). For those with severe M‐NCD, researchers developed individualized voiding schedules based on electronically transmitted voiding data (Rose et al., 2018).

Behavioral interventions

Behavioral interventions included timed voiding, prompted voiding, rehabilitation and exercise, pelvic floor muscle exercises, bladder training, medication, and device use. In facility settings, timed voiding was scheduled at fixed intervals, regardless of older adults' desire to urinate. For older adults with moderate M‐NCD, timed voiding was implemented every hour between 7 am and 10 pm (Jirovec, 1991) or every 2 h (Kunikata et al., 1993). Prompted voiding was performed at home for individuals with moderate M‐NCD. Caregivers provided verbal cues every 2 h to support intentional toileting (Engberg et al., 2002). Rehabilitation and exercise were implemented to improve physical mobility and continence. In hospital settings, physical and occupational therapists provided traditional rehabilitation for those with mild‐to‐moderate M‐NCD (Poynter et al., 2013; Takahashi et al., 2016). In facility settings, caregivers provided rehabilitation for older adults with moderate M‐NCD based on the 1984 AMA Council Scientific Affairs recommendations (Jirovec, 1991).

In hospitals, pelvic floor muscle exercises to improve urinary continence were implemented for those with mild M‐NCD, based on a functional assessment (Takahashi et al., 2016). In a home setting, pelvic floor muscle exercises with biofeedback support were implemented for those with mild M‐NCD (Lee et al., 2017). Nurse practitioners administered these exercises to those with moderate M‐NCD (Engberg et al., 2002).

Bladder training and medication were introduced selectively based on assessment and diagnosis. In the hospital, bladder training and medications were provided to older adults with mild M‐NCD according to a plan developed by the continence team (Takahashi et al., 2016). In the facility, medications, including anticholinergics, were provided to those with moderate M‐NCD (Kunikata et al., 1993).

Devices such as ultrasound bladder scanners and diaper‐mounted sensors supported data‐driven continence management. In hospitals, ultrasound bladder scanners were used to measure post‐void residual urine and assess bladder function in individuals with mild M‐NCD (Takahashi et al., 2016) and lower urinary tract function in those with severe M‐NCD (Takimoto & Taniguchi, 2021). In home settings, post‐void residuals were measured to assess bladder function in individuals with mild NCD (Colling et al., 2003). In facility settings, sensor systems were implemented for individuals with moderate M‐NCD and timed to guide toileting (Lancioni et al., 2011). In home settings, for individuals with severe M‐NCD, alarm data were sent to the researcher and used to establish voiding diary data (Rose et al., 2018).

Educational interventions

Educational interventions targeted older adults and caregivers, with content tailored to NCD categories and severity levels, as well as caregiving roles. Home‐based education focused on pelvic floor muscle exercise skills for those with mild M‐NCD (Lee et al., 2017). In facility settings, caregivers were taught continence management and individualized planning for those with moderate M‐NCD (Jirovec & Templin, 2001). For older adults with severe M‐NCD, caregivers were trained in the principles and use of patient‐centered care (Wijk et al., 2018). In facility settings, those with mild NCD were provided with education on implementing voiding schedules (Colling et al., 2003), and those with moderate M‐NCD received education on urinary incontinence, including knowledge and practice sessions (Kohler et al., 2018).

Cognitive interventions

Cognitive interventions were categorized into two main types: cognitive stimulation therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy. These interventions were implemented to support memory and improve voiding behavior in individuals with mild or moderate M‐NCDs.

Cognitive stimulation therapy was implemented in facility settings for older adults with mild M‐NCD. Interventions included working memory exercises and memory‐matching tasks delivered by speech therapists (Mansbach et al., 2020) and activities of daily living‐based memory exercises provided by occupational therapists (Jiménez Palomares et al., 2021).

Cognitive behavioral therapy was used to improve recognition and performance of toileting behaviors among older adults with moderate M‐NCD. In facility settings, cognitive‐behavioral therapy programs focused on supporting toilet use. In home settings, interventions included voice‐guided toileting via a portable audio device such as a Walkman (Lancioni et al., 2009) and memory‐based behavioral reminders (Jirovec & Templin, 2001).

Characteristics of non‐pharmacological interventions

The structure of non‐pharmacological interventions and support systems varied by NCD category and severity. This reflects the need for different strategies across cognitive stages:

Mild NCD and mild M‐NCD

Interventions were designed to support autonomy and self‐management, primarily among older adults. These interventions included bladder function assessments using an ultrasound bladder scanner (Colling et al., 2003; Takahashi et al., 2016), individualized care plans and voiding schedules (Colling et al., 2003; Dubuc et al., 2011), programs incorporating pelvic floor muscle exercises and cognitive stimulation training (Jiménez Palomares et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2017; Mansbach et al., 2020), and education for older adults or caregivers (Colling et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2017).

Moderate M‐NCD

The intervention involved multiple professionals, emphasizing structured support and interdisciplinary collaboration. It was designed to provide a multicomponent intervention combining behavioral interventions such as timed voiding (Jirovec, 1991; Kunikata et al., 1993), prompted voiding (Engberg et al., 2002), exercise (Jirovec, 1991; Poynter et al., 2013), cognitive behavioral therapy such as audio‐guided toileting (Lancioni et al., 2009; Lancioni et al., 2011), and education (Jirovec & Templin, 2001; Kohler et al., 2018), in addition to individualized care plans and voiding schedules (Engberg et al., 2002; Jirovec & Templin, 2001; Kohler et al., 2018; Lancioni et al., 2011; Poynter et al., 2013; Ryden et al., 2000).

Severe M‐NCD

Caregivers primarily led interventions for older adults with severe M‐NCD. These interventions were designed using a person‐centered approach; environmental modifications were incorporated, and technologies such as sensors attached to diapers and ultrasound bladder scanners were used to support continence management (Rose et al., 2018; Takimoto & Taniguchi, 2021; Wijk et al., 2018). Additionally, caregiver education focused on practical skills for continence management and on understanding M‐NCD‐related behaviors (Wijk et al., 2018).

As the categories and severity levels of NCDs worsened, intervention strategies shifted from self‐management to those led by caregivers. The settings were adjusted to include comprehensive interventions that combined cognitive and physical functions with continence management.

DISCUSSION

This scoping review classified non‐pharmacological interventions for older adults with NCDs into four domains—individualized, behavioral, cognitive, and educational—and mapped them across NCD severity levels and care settings. The synthesis revealed a clear, stepwise shift in the structure and focus of continence care as cognitive impairment progresses. These findings inform severity‐based intervention selection and provide a conceptual foundation for clinical decision‐making.

Progressive shifts in continence‐care strategies according to NCD severity

A key finding of this review is the progressive transformation of intervention strategies in line with NCDs. In mild NCD to mild M‐NCD, interventions primarily supported independence and self‐management, reflecting a stage in which cognitive abilities and executive function remain relatively preserved. Previous studies have similarly shown that behavioral interventions yield sustained benefits when cognitive capacity is sufficient for learning and self‐management (Chang et al., 2022). Clinical guidelines also indicate that continence‐support programs used for cognitively intact older adults can be applied to those with mild NCD (Japanese Society of Neurology Dementia Clinical Practice Guideline Development Committee, 2017).

In moderate M‐NCD, decreased initiative and impaired task sequencing necessitated more structured approaches, such as scheduled voiding and prompted voiding, delivered by caregivers. Evidence shows that prompted voiding can significantly reduce urinary incontinence in older adults with moderate M‐NCD (Newman, 2019), aligning with the patterns observed in this review.

In severe M‐NCD, where awareness of the urge to void and the link between sensation and action are substantially compromised (Ostaszkiewicz, 2018), care shifted toward caregiver‐directed and technology‐assisted interventions. This condition is termed functional incontinence by Johnson II and Smith (2019), consistent with this review.

Roles and limitations of the intervention domains

Individualized interventions: A foundational element across all severity levels

Bladder diaries, functional assessments, and individualized care based on multidisciplinary care plans played a central role regardless of severity. Previous studies have demonstrated that individualized care plans enhance urinary function improvement and intervention feasibility (Dubuc et al., 2011), emphasizing their importance. A distinctive feature of the study by Takahashi et al. (2016), conducted in Japan, was the active involvement of a multidisciplinary team in developing individualized urinary care plans, which enabled more precise personalization of interventions. This approach is consistent with previous reports indicating that complex, multicomponent interventions tend to be more effective than single‐component strategies, highlighting the importance of patient‐centered communication and dignity‐preserving care (Allen et al., 2023; Edwards et al., 2021).

Behavioral interventions: Effective in moderate M‐NCD

Behavioral interventions—scheduled voiding and prompted voiding—were especially beneficial for individuals who retain responsiveness to external cues but have diminished executive function or initiation. Previous research suggests that such interventions compensate for interruptions in toileting sequences and reduce incontinence episodes (Newman, 2019).

Cognitive interventions: Supporting elimination function in mild to moderate NCD

Cognitive stimulation and cognitive‐behavioral approaches assisted toileting behaviors by reinforcing memory, attention, and task sequencing required for continence (Spector et al., 2003). However, evidence for direct improvement in urinary incontinence remains limited, indicating the need for further evaluation.

Research gap

Overemphasis on urinary incontinence; lack of evidence for other lower urinary tract symptoms

Most existing studies focus on urinary incontinence, leaving limited evidence for non‐pharmacological interventions targeting nocturia, overactive bladder, and urinary retention. This imbalance has been previously noted (Roe et al., 2015), suggesting a need for broader investigations covering the full spectrum of lower urinary tract symptoms.

Insufficient standardization of caregiver education

Caregiver education significantly impacts incontinence outcomes (Colling et al., 2003; Wijk et al., 2018). However, many studies lacked detailed descriptions of educational content and delivery methods. The absence of standardized curricula poses challenges for reproducibility and clinical implementation. This gap also increases caregiver burden, highlighting the urgency for systematic education programs.

Need for an integrated, dynamic continence‐care model

Continence care is a dynamic process shaped by interactions among cognitive, physical, environmental, and caregiver‐related factors (Nishimura, 2008). Consistent with previous work emphasizing multicomponent and interdisciplinary approaches (Allen et al., 2023; Tannenbaum et al., 2013), this review underscores the importance of integrating multiple intervention domains according to the severity and characteristics of cognitive impairment.

The classification and mapping developed in this review offer foundational insights for developing an implementable clinical model that incorporates individualized, evidence‐based intervention pathways across the continuum of NCD severity.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, variations in validated neuropsychological tests and cutoff values may lead to inconsistencies in classifying cognitive severity. Second, most studies focused on urinary incontinence, limiting the evidence base for non‐pharmacological interventions for lower urinary tract symptoms. Third, only studies published in English or Japanese were included, introducing language bias. Fourth, potential biases arise from using the ICHUSHI Web. Because the database indexes Japanese‐language literature, sampling and information biases may occur due to variations in the completeness and consistency of abstracts, indexing terms, and metadata. Furthermore, access restrictions and the need for Japanese‐language searches may limit reproducibility for non‐Japanese‐speaking researchers. Finally, the lack of detailed documentation of educational protocols makes it difficult to assess the effectiveness and reproducibility of education.

Future directions

Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive outcome measures, such as QOL and caregiver burden. Furthermore, research needs to expand to reflect the diverse subtypes of lower urinary tract symptoms.

Advanced technologies, such as digital voiding monitors, may enhance the quality and precision of personalized care. Furthermore, interdisciplinary, community‐based support models that integrate caregiver education, behavioral therapy, and cognitive support should be developed. These models can provide flexible, sustainable, and person‐centered care that meets the needs of older adults and their caregivers.

CONCLUSIONS

Non‐pharmacological interventions for older adults with NCDs who have LUTS were categorized into four domains based on severity levels. Self‐management approaches were employed for milder conditions, while caregiver‐led interventions and assistive technologies were used for more advanced conditions. However, current evidence is predominantly focused on urinary incontinence, with limited research on other lower urinary tract symptoms, severity‐based evaluation, and caregiver education details. Future research should expand to diverse symptoms, evaluate interventions across severity levels, and develop standardized caregiver education and technology‐assisted care models.
