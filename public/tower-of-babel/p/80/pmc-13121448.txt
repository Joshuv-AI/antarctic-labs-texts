Cost-effective FRP solutions for enhancing strength and strain of sustainable concrete made with waste tyre rubber
Authors: Panumas Saingam, Burachat Chatveera, Qudeer Hussain, Gritsada Sua-iam, Preeda Chaimahawan, Chisanuphong Suthumma, Afaq Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the compressive behavior and analytical modeling of natural and rubberized concretes confined with cost-effective glass fiber-reinforced polymer (GFRP) jackets. Forty-two cylindrical specimens were tested under axial compression, including natural aggregate concrete (NAC) and rubberized concretes (RuC) prepared with 20% fine aggregate replacement using coarse (2.0 mm) and fine (0.425 mm) waste tire rubber. Both full and strip GFRP wrapping configurations with two, four, and six layers were examined. The results showed that GFRP confinement substantially enhanced both strength and ductility, transforming brittle failure into a gradual, energy-absorbing response. Full wrapping produced up to 63% and 90% strength increases for NAC and rubberized concretes, respectively, with ultimate strain gains exceeding 1300% in the fine-rubber mix. Strip wrapping achieved moderate yet significant improvements while offering material savings. Analytical models were developed for both concrete types to predict confined stress–strain behavior, achieving strong correlations (R2 = 0.84–0.99) between predicted and experimental data. The derived regression-based formulations successfully captured the influence of confinement pressure, rubber content, and wrapping configuration. These findings demonstrate that GFRP provides an economical and sustainable confinement solution for enhancing the performance of rubberized concrete in structural and retrofitting applications.Keywords: GFRP confinement, Rubberized concrete, Waste tyre rubber, Axial compression, Analytical model, Sustainable materialsSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The construction industry faces dual challenges of escalating demand for natural aggregates (NA) and mounting volumes of non-biodegradable waste. Among these waste streams, end-of-life tyres (ELTs) present particularly severe environmental concerns. Global tire disposal exceeds 1.5 billion units annually, generating approximately 30 million tons of rubber waste that threatens ecosystems through groundwater contamination, land consumption, and toxic emissions from tire fires1–4. The incorporation of recycled tyre rubber into concrete represents a promising circular economy strategy that simultaneously addresses waste management and resource conservation while reducing the carbon footprint of construction materials5–8. This approach aligns with the principles of the circular economy and contributes to reducing the carbon footprint of concrete production9–12. Rubberized concrete (RuC), produced by partially replacing natural aggregates with tire rubber particles, has demonstrated several advantageous properties. When crumb rubber replaces 10–20% of fine aggregates, the resulting concrete exhibits density reduction up to 25%, impact resistance improvements up to 80%, and increase in toughness up to 300% compared to conventional concrete13,14. These characteristics make RuC particularly suitable for applications requiring energy absorption and vibration damping, including highway barriers, railway buffers, and seismic-resistant elements15,16. However, the hydrophobic nature of rubber and its low elastic modulus (approximately 0.01 GPa compared to 30 GPa for natural aggregates) create weak interfacial transition zones (ITZs) that compromise mechanical properties17–22 leading to compressive strength reductions of 15–25% at 10% replacement and 35–50% at 20% replacement, with corresponding elastic modulus decreases of 20–30% and 40–55%, respectively23,24. These mechanical shortcomings remain the principal barrier to the broader structural application of RuC.

The influence of rubber particle size on RuC behavior has been a recurring theme in literature. Researchers have noted that finer crumb rubber (< 1 mm) increases surface area and weakens ITZ adhesion, while coarser crumb rubber (1–3 mm) behaves as stress concentrators that initiate cracks under loading24–26. Microstructural studies using SEM, and nano-indentation demonstrate that ITZ porosity, microcrack initiation, and dilation behavior are highly sensitive to rubber size27,28. Notably, Mo et al.29 reported that sand replacement with 2 mm rubber reduced compressive strength by more than 30%, whereas replacing with finer 0.5 mm rubber particles led to higher reductions approaching 45%. Similarly, Su et al.30 observed that concretes with 1–2 mm rubber demonstrated relatively less stiffness loss compared with mixes containing < 0.5 mm particles. Despite these findings, very few studies have systematically isolated the effect of particle size at a fixed replacement ratio. Most programs simultaneously vary replacement content and particle gradation, making it difficult to distinguish the role of size alone31–33. This gap is critical because particle size governs dilation patterns under axial loading, which in turn dictates the efficiency of external confinement systems. External confinement effectively mitigates RuC’s mechanical deficiencies through triaxial stress states that suppress crack propagation and mobilize the rubber’s lateral expansion capacity. Lateral restraint delays crack propagation, enhances compressive strength, and increases ductility by mobilizing higher ultimate strains34,35. For RuC, confinement can be particularly beneficial because the deformable rubberized matrix can sustain higher lateral dilation before rupture, allowing external jackets to develop significant confining pressures36,37. Constitutive models tailored for FRP-confined RuC indicate bilinear stress–strain behavior, extended post-peak ductility, and improved toughness compared to unconfined specimens38,39. Furthermore, confined RuC has exhibited superior cyclic and impact performance, highlighting its potential in seismic and blast-resistant structural applications40,41.

Among confinement technologies, fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) systems offer superior strength-to-weight ratios and corrosion resistance compared to steel jacketing. While, Carbon FRP (CFRP) delivers the highest confinement efficiency but remains costly, limiting large-scale applications in resource-constrained regions42,43. Glass FRP (GFRP), by contrast, offers a cost-effective alternative with wide availability and adequate mechanical performance44,45. Recent investigations demonstrate that low-cost GFRP (LC-GFRP) sheets fabricated from bidirectional E-glass fibers and polyester resin provide substantial enhancements in compressive behavior. Rodsin et al.46 reported that concrete confined with LC-GFRP experienced strength gains up to 72% and Saingam et al.47 reported that Hemp FRP confinement of brick aggregate concretes achieved 272% and 457% in the ultimate compressive strength and strain. These studies underscore the economic and mechanical viability of GFRP systems for sustainable concrete retrofitting.

The configuration of FRP application significantly influences both performance and economics. Full wrapping of FRP sheets ensures uniform confinement pressure and maximized enhancements, but it requires greater material consumption. Partial wrapping techniques such as strip wrapping, spirals, or banded configurations can achieve meaningful gains with reduced FRP quantities48,49. Experimental evidence indicates that strip-wrapped columns exhibit improved axial strength and ductility, although the level of improvement depends on strip spacing and fiber orientation50. From an economic perspective, partial wrapping reduces both material usage and labor costs, representing a pragmatic alternative where resources are limited51,52. However, systematic evaluations of full versus strip GFRP confinement for RuC are scarce, and most data comes from studies on normal concrete or CFRP systems. Despite substantial research on rubberized concrete and FRP confinement independently, critical knowledge gaps persist that hinder practical implementation. First, the specific influence of rubber particle size at constant replacement ratios on confinement efficiency remains unquantified, limiting optimization of rubber processing specifications. Second, Comparative assessments of full versus partial GFRP wrapping for RuC are lacking, preventing evidence-based selection of cost-effective strengthening schemes. Third, although GFRP is consistently described as economical, comprehensive analyses linking performance to material savings and cost remain scarce. Addressing these gaps is vital to extend the structural use of RuC and establish design guidelines for its confinement with affordable FRP systems.

Despite the growing body of research on FRP-confined concrete and rubberized concrete (see Table 1), several limitations persist in existing analytical and empirical models. Many previously proposed confinement models were developed for conventional concrete and are often applied to rubberized concrete without explicitly accounting for the altered interfacial transition zone, reduced stiffness, and enhanced deformability introduced by waste rubber particles. Furthermore, most available models are calibrated using limited experimental datasets and typically focus on fully wrapped configurations, thereby offering reduced predictive accuracy for partially confined or strip-wrapped systems. The influence of rubber particle size at a constant replacement ratio is also rarely isolated, leading to uncertainty in the applicability of existing formulations. These challenges highlight the need for refined predictive models that are calibrated specifically for GFRP-confined rubberized concrete and capable of reliably capturing the combined effects of confinement pressure, material heterogeneity, and wrapping configuration. This study addresses these gaps through a comprehensive experimental and analytical investigation of GFRP-confined rubberized concrete with controlled particle size variation. The research: (i) isolates the effect of rubber particle size (0.425 mm versus 2.0 mm) at 20% fine aggregate replacement on unconfined and confined behavior; (ii) quantifies the relative efficiency of full versus strip GFRP wrapping configurations through 42 × cylinder tests; (iii) develops cost-performance indices linking strength gain to material investment; and (iv) validates and refines existing confinement models for RuC applications. This represents the first systematic study combining particle size effects, wrapping configuration optimization, and economic analysis for GFRP-confined rubberized concrete, providing practical design guidelines for sustainable infrastructure applications.

Experimental framework

Test matrix

The experimental program comprised a total of 42 cylindrical specimens divided into three groups, as summarized in Table 2. Group 1 consisted of control specimens (C0) prepared without rubber content, Group 2 incorporated 20% crumb rubber by volume of fine aggregate retained on the 40-mesh sieve (R40), and Group 3 incorporated 20% crumb rubber retained on the 10-mesh sieve (R10). Within each group, specimens were tested in both unconfined and confined conditions. Confinement was provided using glass fibre-reinforced polymer (GFRP) wraps applied either as full jackets (F) or as discontinuous strip jackets (S), with the number of layers varied as 2, 4, or 6. For each configuration, two identical specimens were cast to ensure repeatability. The adopted specimen identification system follows the format Group–Configuration–Layers, where for example “R40–F2L” denotes a specimen with 20% rubber replacement of fine aggregates (40-mesh size), confined with a full GFRP wrap comprising two layers.

Materials

Ordinary Portland cement (OPC) conforming to ASTM C150 Type I59 specifications was used as the binder throughout this study. The cement exhibited a specific gravity of 3.15 and a Blaine fineness of approximately 340 m2/kg. Natural river sand was used as fine aggregate, with a fineness modulus of about 2.6, specific gravity of 2.65, and water absorption capacity of 1.0%. Crushed NA with a maximum size of 19 mm, specific gravity of 2.70, and water absorption of 1.8% were employed. To enhance sustainability, waste tyre rubber was incorporated as a partial replacement of fine aggregates at a fixed level of 20% by volume. Two particle sizes were considered, corresponding to retention on the 10-mesh (2.0 mm) and 40-mesh (0.425 mm) sieves, respectively. Figure 1 illustrates the appearance of the two crumb rubber fractions. The 40-mesh material consisted of fine rubber powder with particle sizes below 0.5 mm, while the 10-mesh material comprised coarser granules averaging around 2 mm. The choice of these two sizes was motivated by literature reports60,61 that fine crumb rubber increases ITZ porosity while coarse rubber tends to act as stress concentrators, both of which affect compressive strength and the efficiency of external confinement.

Waste tyre rubber particles used in the study: (a) fine rubber retained on the 40-mesh sieve (0.425 mm), and (b) coarse rubber retained on the 10-mesh sieve (2.0 mm).

Glass fiber-reinforced polymer (GFRP) sheets were used for external confinement of concrete cylinders as shown in Fig. 2. The strengthening system consisted of woven E-glass fibers impregnated with an unsaturated polyester resin. The average tensile strength of the laminate was approximately 380 MPa, while the tensile modulus was about 19 GPa. The composite exhibited an ultimate rupture strain close to 2.0%, and the cured thickness of a single ply was measured at roughly 0.35 mm. These properties ensured that the GFRP jackets were capable of mobilizing adequate hoop stresses under axial compression, providing the necessary confinement to compensate for the mechanical deficiencies of rubberized concrete.

Specimen preparation and strengthening schemes

Concrete cylinders with a diameter of 150 mm and a height of 300 mm were cast for all test groups as shown in Fig. 3. After demolding at 24 h, the specimens were cured in water at room temperature for 28 days before testing. Each group comprised unconfined control specimens as well as confined specimens strengthened with GFRP jackets. Prior to strengthening, the surface of the cylinders was prepared to ensure effective bonding. The specimen ends were ground to achieve parallel faces, and external surfaces were lightly polished to remove laitance. The specimens were then cleaned of dust and coated with a thin primer layer to promote adhesion of the resin. Two strengthening configurations were considered as full wrapping and strip wrapping. In the full wrap scheme, the GFRP sheets were applied continuously along the entire height of the cylinder, providing uniform confinement. In the strip wrap scheme, the GFRP was applied as two 50 mm wide bands at the specimen ends, separated by 50 mm clear spacing, with an additional 100 mm wide band placed at the mid-height of the cylinder. This arrangement was adopted to simulate material-efficient confinement while still mobilizing hoop stresses in critical zones. In both schemes, the number of GFRP layers varied as 2, 4, or 6. Each layer had a nominal thickness of approximately 0.35 mm, giving total jacket thicknesses of 0.70, 1.40, and 2.10 mm, respectively. The GFRP strengthening process followed a wet lay-up technique. Sheets of woven E-glass fiber fabric were cut to the required dimensions, ensuring a 150 mm overlap along the hoop direction to maintain continuity. An unsaturated polyester resin was applied uniformly to the prepared concrete surface, after which the fiber sheets were wrapped tightly around the specimen. Additional resin was applied on the outer surface to achieve full impregnation of the fibers. The lay-up was consolidated using hand rollers to eliminate entrapped air and ensure intimate contact between the fibers and concrete. After application, the strengthened specimens were cured under ambient laboratory conditions for seven days to allow the resin to reach full strength prior to testing. The details of the resulting specimen configurations are presented in Fig. 3. The control specimens (C0-Con, R40-Con, R10-Con) were tested without strengthening, while the confined specimens were labelled according to the group (C0, R40, or R10), type of confinement (F for full wrap, S for strip wrap), and the number of GFRP layers (2L, 4L, or 6L).

Instrumentation and test setup

All specimens were tested under monotonic axial compression using a servo-controlled universal testing machine (UTM) with a load capacity of 2000 kN as shown in Fig. 4. The tests were performed in accordance with ASTM C3962, with load applied under displacement control at a rate of 0.5 mm/min to ensure stable post-peak response. A pair of steel plates were placed at the specimen ends to achieve uniform load transfer and minimize platen friction. Axial deformations were recorded using two linear variable differential transducers (LVDTs) positioned diametrically opposite on a rigid stand attached to the specimen mid-height. This configuration provided an accurate measure of axial shortening while reducing the influence of platen compliance. Load data were continuously acquired from the UTM load cell and synchronized with displacement measurements through a computer-based data acquisition system.

Results and discussion

Failure modes

The failure patterns of all tested specimens are presented in Fig. 5. The unconfined control specimens (C0-Con, R40-Con, and R10-Con) failed in a brittle manner with vertical splitting cracks extending along the height of the cylinders. These cracks rapidly widened, leading to spalling of the cover concrete and disintegration into multiple fragments shortly after peak load. The extent of cracking was more pronounced in the rubberized mixes, particularly for R40-Con, where finer crumb rubber produced a weaker ITZ and resulted in larger crack openings compared to the natural aggregate control. In contrast, the confined specimens exhibited distinctly different behaviors. For the fully wrapped series (F2L, F4L, and F6L), visible longitudinal cracks developed in the concrete core but were restrained by the continuous GFRP jacket, delaying spalling and preventing sudden collapse. Failure was ultimately governed by rupture of the GFRP, which occurred suddenly and was often accompanied by explosive noise. As seen in Fig. 5, specimens with two layers (F2L) generally ruptured at mid-height where vertical cracks concentrated, while specimens with four and six layers (F4L and F6L) sustained greater loads and deformations before rupture, demonstrating the effectiveness of additional layers in enhancing confinement pressure. Strip-wrapped specimens (S2L, S4L, and S6L) displayed intermediate performance between unconfined and fully wrapped cylinders. In these specimens, cracks initiated in the unconfined regions between strips, as evident in Fig. 5, with bulging and localized crushing of concrete in these zones. For S2L, premature splitting of the concrete in the unwrapped regions controlled the failure, whereas S4L and S6L specimens resisted higher loads before eventual rupture of the strips at mid-height. Despite improvements with additional layers, strip wrapping was consistently less effective than full wrapping due to the presence of unconfined regions that remained vulnerable to localized damage. Overall, the observed failure modes confirm that GFRP confinement significantly improved the ductility of both natural and rubberized concretes. RuC (R10 and R40) exhibited larger lateral deformations prior to jacket rupture compared to the control group, highlighting their enhanced deformability under confinement. At the same time, the reduced confinement efficiency in strip-wrapped specimens illustrates the trade-off between material savings and structural performance.

Compressive stress–strain behaviour of concrete

The experimental stress–strain responses of all concrete mixes under axial compression are presented in Fig. 6, while the idealized schematic representation of the confined concrete response is shown in Fig. 7. The unconfined control specimens (C0-Con, R40-Con, and R10-Con) exhibited the characteristic brittle behavior of plain concrete, with a sharp increase in stress followed by an abrupt post-peak drop. This indicates a limited capacity for plastic deformation and low energy absorption. The typical shape of the unconfined and confined responses can be represented by the curve in Fig. 7, which includes the initial elastic portion with modulus , the nonlinear ascending region up to the unconfined peak stress , and the secondary branch governed by confinement pressure that extends the strain capacity to . A significant transformation in behavior was observed when the specimens were confined with GFRP wraps. As shown in Fig. 6, both natural aggregate concrete (C0 series) and rubberized concretes (R40 and R10 series) developed noticeably higher peak stresses and more gradual descending branches compared with their unconfined counterparts. The introduction of rubber particles reduced peak strength and stiffness compared to the C0 group, with R40-Con exhibiting the most pronounced reduction due to the finer particle size (0.425 mm), consistent with reports that finer rubber particles weaken the ITZ and reduce compressive strength more severely than coarser particles63,64. The fully wrapped specimens (Fig. 6a, c, e) displayed a clear strain-hardening region after reaching the unconfined strength, where lateral expansion of the concrete core activated the confining pressure provided by the GFRP jacket. Increasing the number of layers from two to six progressively enhanced confinement, producing higher ultimate stresses and larger ultimate strains. Among all configurations, the six-layer full wraps (F6L) achieved the most pronounced improvements, converting the abrupt failure of unconfined concrete into a ductile response characterized by extended deformation before rupture. These results are consistent with previous studies on FRP-confined concrete, where continuous jackets were shown to provide uniform lateral pressure and delay microcrack coalescence65,66.

In contrast, the strip-wrapped specimens (Fig. 6b, d, f) exhibited partial confinement behavior. The presence of unwrapped zones allowed localized bulging and cracking between the strips, resulting in lower strength and faster post-peak softening compared with the fully wrapped specimens. Similar observations on the reduced efficiency of strip confinement have been reported by Rodsin et al.67 and Joyklad et al.68, who emphasized the role of effective coverage ratio in governing confinement performance. Nevertheless, the confinement effect still increased with the number of layers, indicating that even discontinuous jackets contributed to delaying failure by restraining lateral dilation in the wrapped regions. When comparing the three groups, it is evident that the inclusion of crumb rubber reduced initial stiffness and peak stress in the unconfined state; however, it also enhanced deformability, enabling greater exploitation of confinement. This behavior indicates that RuC possesses higher deformability, which synergizes with the lateral confinement provided by GFRP jackets, allowing them to sustain larger strains before rupture. Such enhancement in ductility is in line with previous reports on FRP-confined lightweight or modified concretes69,70. The rubberized concretes, particularly the R40 and R10 series, showed larger ultimate strains under GFRP confinement than the natural aggregate mix. This indicates a synergistic interaction between the ductile rubberized matrix and the lateral restraint of the GFRP jacket, which together produced a more stable and gradual post-peak response. Overall, the stress–strain curves confirm that GFRP confinement effectively transforms the brittle compression behavior of concrete into a ductile and energy-absorbing form. The degree of improvement depends primarily on the confinement continuity (full > strip) and the number of GFRP layers, consistent with previously reported confinement mechanisms for FRP-wrapped concretes69,71. Each configuration was tested with two specimens. The observed scatter between twin specimens was small. However, due to the limited number of specimens, the results may not fully represent the variability in material and structural behavior.

Ultimate compressive strength and ultimate strain of confined specimens

The experimental results of ultimate compressive strength and strain are summarized in Table 3. The data confirms that external GFRP confinement markedly enhanced both strength and strain capacities compared with unconfined specimens. For the NAC (Group 1), the unconfined specimen (C0-Con) reached a strength of 34.8 MPa with an ultimate strain of 0.002. Full wrapping significantly improved performance, producing strength gains of 15.5%, 46.6%, and 62.6% for two, four, and six layers, respectively. The corresponding strain increases were 685%, 970%, and 1150%, showing a pronounced ductility enhancement with increasing confinement. Strip wrapping yielded smaller but consistent improvements, with maximum strength and strain gains of 22.7% and 850%, respectively. In the fine-rubber concrete (Group 2, R40), the unconfined strength was lower (≈ 19 MPa) because of the weaker interfacial bond caused by 0.425 mm rubber particles. Nevertheless, confinement proved highly effective: full wraps increased strength by up to 89.6% and strain by 1360%, while strip wraps produced maximum gains of 49% in strength and 1080% in strain. The large strain enhancement demonstrates the deformable nature of the rubberized matrix, which allowed greater activation of the GFRP confinement pressure. For the coarse-rubber concrete (Group 3, R10), the unconfined strength was higher (25 MPa) but exhibited similar confinement trends. Full wraps increased strength by 21–83% and strain by 650–1300%, whereas strip wrapping gave more moderate improvements of 12%–35% in strength and up to 1140% in strain. Although coarse rubber produced slightly lower relative strain gains than fine rubber, the absolute strength levels remained superior. Overall, the results indicate that (i) confinement efficiency increases with the number of GFRP layers, (ii) full wrapping provides substantially higher strength and strain enhancement than strip wrapping, and (iii) rubberized concretes, owing to their greater deformability, achieve higher relative strain gains under confinement. These findings are consistent with the confinement mechanisms illustrated in Fig. 7, where mobilized lateral pressure from the GFRP jacket extends the stress–strain response and delays brittle failure.

Effect of the confinement configuration

The influence of confinement configuration on strength and strain enhancement is illustrated in Figs. 8 and 9 and summarized in Table 3. The results clearly show that the full GFRP wraps provided a more uniform and effective confinement than the strip wraps for all three concrete types. As seen in Fig. 8, the increase in ultimate strength was consistently higher for fully wrapped specimens, reaching up to 62.6%, 89.6%, and 82.6% for Groups 1 (C0), 2 (R40), and 3 (R10), respectively. In contrast, the strip-wrapped counterparts achieved only about one-half of these gains. The continuous jackets restricted lateral expansion throughout the specimen height, allowing the GFRP layers to mobilize higher confining pressure, whereas the discontinuous strips left unconfined zones where cracking and local dilation initiated earlier.

A similar trend was observed for ultimate strain enhancement (Fig. 9). The fully wrapped specimens developed substantially greater ductility, with strain increases exceeding 1150% in the natural aggregate concrete and up to 1360% in the fine-rubber mix. Strip wrapping, though less efficient, still improved strain capacity by 720–1140%, indicating that even partial confinement can delay crushing and extend the deformation range. The higher strain gains observed in rubberized concretes confirm that their more deformable matrices enable better utilization of lateral confinement. Overall, the results confirm that confinement effectiveness depends strongly on jacket continuity and the number of GFRP layers. Full wraps mobilize higher confinement pressure, producing both greater strength and ductility, while strip wraps offer a cost-efficient alternative with moderate performance gains. The performance reported for strip wrapping is specific to the studied configuration (50 mm strips at the ends, 50 mm spacing, and 100 mm mid-height strips). Previous studies48,49 have shown that different coverage ratios and layouts can significantly affect FRP strengthening effectiveness. Therefore, the present results may not be representative of all possible strip arrangements.

The accuracy of the proposed analytical model for GFRP-confined NAC was verified by comparing both the predicted parameters and the complete stress–strain responses with experimental results. The comparison between the analytical and experimental stress–strain curves, presented in Fig. 12, further validates the robustness of the proposed model. For both full and strip wrapping configurations, the predicted curves closely follow the experimental trends, accurately reproducing the initial elastic portion, the nonlinear ascending branch, and the softening response beyond peak stress. Minor deviations are observed near the post-peak region, which can be attributed to localized cracking and variability in the bonding efficiency between the GFRP jacket and concrete surface. Nevertheless, the analytical curves consistently capture the stiffness transition and ductility improvement induced by confinement. Overall, the combined results confirm that the analytical model provides a realistic and reliable representation of the confined behavior of NAC. Limitations and comparison with previous works

The present study is limited to circular concrete cylinders subjected to monotonic axial compression and considers a single rubber replacement ratio with two particle sizes. Therefore, the direct applicability of the results to different cross-sectional shapes, reinforcement conditions, or cyclic loading scenarios requires further investigation. In addition, the proposed analytical models are regression-based and calibrated using the current experimental database; although high predictive accuracy was achieved (R2 = 0.84–0.99), extrapolation beyond the investigated range should be approached with caution. It should be noted that the current study is limited to 20% fine aggregate replacement with rubber, and for particle sizes of approximately 0.425 mm and 2.0 mm. Future work should explore other replacement levels (e.g., 10%, 15%, 25%) and investigate the interaction between rubber content and particle size under GFRP confinement. The strip wrapping outcomes are valid for the particular strip width and spacing used in this study. Future work should investigate varying strip widths and spacing while keeping the FRP volume constant to identify the most efficient layout for optimal performance. A limitation of this study is the relatively small number of specimens per configuration (n = 2). Future studies should consider larger sample sizes to better capture variability and improve the statistical robustness of the results. This study did not include microstructural characterization such as SEM or micro-CT imaging. Therefore, the mechanistic explanations for differences in performance between 0.425 mm and 2.0 mm rubber under confinement are inferred from prior work. Future studies should include microstructural observations and digital image correlation (DIC) to better understand strain localization and crack development.

Several experimental investigations have reported on the compressive response of FRP-confined rubberised concrete (RuC). Tufail et al.54 found that externally bonded CFRP significantly enhanced the compressive strength and deformability of RuC, recovering strength losses due to rubber inclusion, and their proposed predictive models offered improved estimates compared with conventional design guidelines not calibrated for RuC mixtures. Raffoul et al.39 similarly observed that FRP confinement substantially increases both strength and ultimate axial strain relative to unconfined rubberised concretes, with confinement effects activated earlier due to lateral expansion induced by rubber particles. More recent work by Cao et al.72 developed stress–strain models considering strain-rate effects, and reported that FRP confinement can compensate for strength reductions while retaining high ductility in rubber mixtures. Compared with these studies, the present results show consistent trends of improved strength and marked ductility gain, particularly under fine rubber particle conditions, while also extending understanding to strip versus full GFRP wrapping configurations.

Conclusions

This study experimentally and analytically investigated the compressive behavior of GFRP-confined natural and rubberized concretes, with particular emphasis on the influence of rubber particle size and confinement configuration. The results demonstrate that GFRP confinement effectively compensates for the strength reduction associated with rubber inclusion while significantly enhancing axial strain capacity. This behavior is primarily attributed to the increased lateral deformability of rubberized concrete, which activates confinement pressure at earlier loading stages and promotes a more stable post-peak response.

A clear dependence of confinement efficiency on rubber particle size was observed. Specimens incorporating fine rubber particles exhibited superior strength and ductility enhancement compared to those with coarse rubber, indicating improved stress transfer and reduced interfacial damage within the cementitious matrix. This finding highlights the importance of particle-scale material design when rubberized concrete is intended for use in confined structural applications.

Full wrapping provided the highest confinement efficiency, yielding strength gains up to 63% for natural aggregate concrete and 90% for rubberized concrete, whereas strip wrapping achieved moderate improvements with reduced material use. The comparison between full and strip GFRP confinement revealed that while full wrapping provides the highest strength and deformation capacity, strip confinement can achieve substantial ductility enhancement with reduced material usage. This trade-off between mechanical performance and material efficiency suggests that strip confinement may represent a viable and more sustainable strengthening strategy for applications where large deformation capacity is prioritized over peak strength.

Overall, the findings confirm that GFRP confinement can transform rubberized concrete from a low-strength, highly deformable material into a structurally viable and ductile composite. The study provides both mechanistic insight and practical predictive tools, supporting the use of confined rubberized concrete in sustainable structural applications and identifying clear pathways for future research, including cyclic loading, non-circular sections, and integration with data-driven modeling approaches.

This study focuses on the short-term compressive behavior of rubberized concrete under monotonic axial loading at 28 days. Durability under realistic environmental conditions, including freeze–thaw, wet–dry cycles, UV exposure, and elevated temperature, was not investigated. Therefore, while the proposed approach represents a potentially sustainable confinement solution in terms of waste utilization and material efficiency, its long-term performance and durability require further verification.
