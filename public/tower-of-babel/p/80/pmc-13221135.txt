Women in neurosurgical oncology: Mapping the milestones and shaping the future
Authors: Grace E Hey, Chloe L DeYoung, Kathleen S Botterbush, Erin N Walker, Maryam Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurosurgical oncology, which specifically focuses on the treatment of central nervous system tumors, emerged in 1879 after Dr. William Macewen successfully completed the first brain tumor resection. The field has since dramatically evolved due to advancements in neuroimaging, neuronavigation, surgical techniques, and improved knowledge of molecular biology, allowing for the administration of safer, more effective, patient-centered oncology care. Traditionally male-dominated, the entry of women in neurosurgery and neurosurgical oncology gained momentum in the 20th century thanks to the groundbreaking work of pioneers like Drs. Louise Eisenhardt, Diana Beck, Sofia Ionescu, and Alexa Canady. Their work has paved the way for future generations of women in neurosurgery. Today, several prominent female leaders and physician-scientists including Drs. Karin M. Muraszko, Roberta Glick, Isabelle Germano, Galareh Zadeh, Viviane Tabar, Linda Liau, Amy Heimberger, and Kate Drummond continue to advance the field through innovative research, surgical expertise, and commitment to patient care, inspiring and mentoring the next generation of neurosurgeons. This review explores the evolution of women’s roles in neurosurgery, historical milestones in neurosurgical oncology, and the contributions of prominent female neurosurgical oncologists whose dedication and achievements continue to shape the future of neurosurgical oncology.

Key Points

Although traditionally male-dominated, the field of neurosurgery and neurosurgical oncology has seen increasing contributions from women, starting in the 20th century with pioneers like Drs. Louise Eisenhardt, Diana Beck, Sofia Ionescu, and Alexa Canady. Their legacy continues through modern female leaders such as Drs. Karin M. Muraszko, Linda Liau, and others, who are advancing the field and mentoring the next generation of neurosurgeons.

Efforts should focus on expanding mentorship and sponsorship programs, providing career development opportunities, and addressing systemic barriers to academic promotion. These initiatives include establishing formal sponsorships, enhancing research collaboration, improving grant-writing resources, and promoting women’s accomplishments through inclusive events and social media. Brief History of Neurosurgical Oncology

Surgical neuro-oncology encompasses the treatment and management of primary and metastatic tumors within the central nervous system (CNS) through open surgical and minimally invasive procedures.1 The first successful brain tumor resection occurred in 1879, performed by William Macewen for the removal of a growth on the dura mater.2 This pioneering procedure marked the beginning of specialized brain tumor care, which continued to develop throughout the 19th century. Harvey Cushing, often regarded as the father of neurosurgery, played a significant role in shaping modern neurosurgical oncologic care. Dr. Cushing specifically worked to develop novel techniques for the removal of pituitary and skull-base tumors.3,4 His work in documenting and classifying brain tumors laid a foundation for understanding tumor biology and treatment strategies, influencing the evolution of modern neuro-oncologic care. Since then, brain tumor surgery has evolved to include advancements such as neruo-navigation, robotic-assistance, 5-ALA fluorescence guidance, and other innovative technologies and approaches, greatly enhancing precision and patient outcomes.5–7

History of Women in Neurosurgery

Though neurosurgery holds ancient origins, women were not known to partake in the field until the 15th century, in which Turkish author Serefeddin Sabuncuoglu illustrated the first female surgeon, Tabibes, performing a procedure on a fetus with hydrocephalus.8,9 However, it was not until the early 20th century that the number of female neurosurgeons began to rise, thanks in part to the father of neurosurgery himself (Figure 1). In 1919, Dr. Harvey Cushing addressed the American College of Surgeons (ACS) alongside his close colleague, Dr. Louise Eisenhardt, announcing the birth of a new specialty—Neurological Surgery.10 A tumor neuropathologist herself, Dr. Eisenhardt was clinically brilliant, diagnosing tumors in Dr. Cushing’s operating room as they were removed. Serving as the first editor of the Journal of Neurosurgery from 1944 to 1965, and the first female president of the Harvey Cushing Society (now the American Association of Neurological Surgeons, AANS) in 1938, Dr. Eisenhardt cemented her legacy as a leader in neurosurgery.10

Timeline of female involvement and accomplishments in neurosurgery.

These first decades of the century saw several other notable women enter the field, though the title of “first female neurosurgeon,” even within the US alone, remains a subject of debate. Following World War I, many European countries began allowing women to study medicine, and, subsequently, saw an increase in the number of female surgeons incorporating neurosurgery into their practice. However, the full-blooded title of “first female neurosurgeon” is often reserved for Dr. Diana Beck or Dr. Sofia Lonescu, with the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) bestowing the title to Dr. Lonescu.11,12 In 1939, Dr. Beck of England joined the field where she struggled to gain clinical respect due to her battle with myasthenia gravis.11 However, in 1943, she was appointed as a consultant neurosurgeon at London’s Royal Free Hospital, where she spent much of her career studying surgical interventions for intracerebral hemorrhage and idiopathic intracranial hypertension.11,12 Dr. Lonescu started her career 2 years later, where she is known for expanding neurosurgical care across her home country of Romania, while pioneering minimally invasive techniques for intracerebral hematomas.11,12

Across the Atlantic, only one woman was known to practice neurosurgery in the United States from 1928 until 1960—Dr. Dorothy Klenke Nash. Dr. Nash graduated from Columbia College of Physicians and Surgeons in 1927 and went on to build a nearly 40-year career with special interests in mental health and cerebral policy.13 In 1959, fellow Columbia graduate Dr. Ruth Kerr Jakoby followed in her footsteps and, in 1961, Dr. Jakoby was selected the first female diplomate of the American Board of Neurological Surgery (ABNS), followed soon after by her 1972 election as president of the Washington Academy of Neurosurgery.10 Dr. Joan Venes followed closely behind, becoming the first woman to be awarded the prestigious Van Wagenen Fellowship in 1974 before founding the American Society of Pediatric Neurosurgery and serving as chair of the Pediatric Section.10 In 1981, Dr. Alexa Irene Canady changed the face of neurosurgery when she became the first African American female neurosurgeon, training at Yale-New Haven Hospital to become board-certified in Pediatric Neurosurgery.8,14 She further cemented her legacy when she went on to serve as Chief of Neurosurgery at Children’s Hospital of Michigan from 1987 until 2001 and developed the anti-siphon shunt.8,14 In 1988, Dr. Frances Conley of Stanford University, who had a significant clinical and research interest in brain tumor immunotherapies, became the first tenured professor of neurosurgery in the United States before becoming the first female neurosurgery division chief at Palo Alto Veterans Affairs Hospital. Dr. Conley recently passed away in August 2024 following a long-fought illness.15

At the 1989 AANS Meeting in Atlanta, 3 women—Drs. Deborah Benzil, Gail Rosseau, and Karin Muraszko—met at a resident session where they were each surprised to see other women present. The rest of the day, they each invited any women neurosurgeons they happened to cross paths with to a meeting at the hotel restaurant, where nearly a dozen attendees ultimately gathered. Shortly thereafter, the AANS/CNS Section on Women in Neurosurgery (WINS) was born, with Dr. Benzil at the helm as the first chair.16 Dr. Benzil also went on to become the first female chair of the Council of State Neurological Societies, on top of her renowned clinical work in stereotactic spine surgery.16 Alongside her, Dr. Rosseau, with expertise in skull base, was the first woman elected to the AANS board of directors and the first female AANS officer.9 Dr. Muraszko also went on to serve as several firsts—the first female member of the ABNS, President of the Society of Neurological Surgeons (SNS), and department chair, herself a celebrated pediatric neurosurgeon.16

Women across the globe have made significant contributions to advancing neurosurgery outside of the United States and Europe. In 1959, Turkish neurosurgeon Dr. Ayisima Altinok co-founded the Turkish Neurosurgical Society and became instrumental in modernizing neurosurgery in her home country.17 Dr. Altinok was foundational in obtaining microscopes to perform microscopic neurosurgical procedures and was known for developing a novel technique to treat syringomyelia.17 Shortly after, Asia’s first female neurosurgeon, Dr. Thanjavur Santhanakrishna Kanaka emerged as a prominent figure in the realm of functional neurosurgery.18 Of note, Dr. Kanaka was the first Indian physician to pioneer functional neurosurgery and was part of one of the earliest teams performing stereotactic neurosurgery in India.18 Later across the world in 2013, Dr. Espérance Maman You Broalet became the first female neurosurgeon in West Africa, establishing a program to combat neural tube defects and hydrocephalus in the region known as “Espérance’s Hope.”8 Her work was largely focused on preventative healthcare and has been foundational to improving neurosurgical care in the region.

Despite the work of all of these trailblazers, women continue to be disproportionately underrepresented in organized neurosurgery and the field as a whole.8 Notably, while the AANS saw its first female neurosurgeon become president in 2018 with Dr. Shelly Timmons, the Congress of Neurological Surgeons (CNS) will honor its first female president, Dr. Martina Stippler, in 2025.8 Of the AANS/CNS Joint Sections and WFNS Ad-Hoc Committees, the AANS/CNS Joint Section on Tumors was the last to welcome a female chair, with Dr. Isabelle M. Germano becoming the first in 2022.8

Dr. Linda Liau is widely recognized for her work in brain tumor immunotherapy.19,20 She has led groundbreaking research on personalized dendritic cell vaccines, such as the DCVax®-L vaccine, which has shown promise in extending survival in patients with glioblastoma (GBM).21–23 She has additionally worked to help refine glioma classification guidelines to allow for more personalized treatment strategies.24 In addition to her role as a scientist, Dr. Liau has shaped the field of neuro-oncology through her leadership roles at UCLA and within national professional organizations. In recognition of her contributions, she was recently honored with the 2023 Charles Wilson Excellence Award from the AANS/CNS Tumor Section.25

Dr. Roberta Glick is a Professor of Neurosurgery at Rush University and Rosalind Franklin Medical School who has dedicated over 30 years to improving brain tumor care at Cook County and Mount Sinai Hospitals in Chicago, Illinois.26 She was the first woman to graduate from Northwestern University’s neurosurgical residency, and her early work in neuro-oncology focused on exploring the mechanism of tumor immunosuppression and the molecular basis of tumor growth.27–29 With the help of several other investigators and the AANS, Dr. Glick founded the Immunotherapy Task Force in 2000 to promote research and clinical applications of brain tumor immunotherapy. Her notable scientific accomplishments include the development of the Immuno-Gene Therapy tumor vaccine and the creation of a widely used stereotactic mouse model for intratumoral studies.28,30 She was the first woman to serve on the Editorial Board of Neurosurgery and the first woman on the executive committee of the AANS-CNS Tumor Section. Dr. Glick advocates for improved tumor treatment protocols and standard guidelines for the navigation of complex neuro-oncological cases.31,32 This advocacy spans beyond the United States as Dr. Glick pioneered the first international neurosurgical Bootcamp in Bolivia in 2015.31 She was recognized by the AANS/CNS Tumor Section in 2023, receiving the prestigious Mark Rosenblum Distinguished Service Award, for her contribution to the field and her dedication to advancing patient care.25

Dr. Isabelle Germano is the director of the Brain Tumor Center at Mount Sinai Hospital in New York.33,34 She holds the position of chairperson of the AANS/CNS Section on Tumors and was the past Chairperson of WINS33–35 Dr. Germano has expertise in the use and development of image-guided minimally invasive techniques to treat brain tumors.33,34 Her translational research focuses on the development of therapeutics for brain tumors including combined VEGF-A and PD-L1 inhibition for GBM,36 gene therapy for brain tumors,37,38 stem cell therapy for glioma,39 pre-irradiation for brain metastases,40,41 overcoming temozolomide resistance,42,43 and radiosurgery for CNS tumors.44–46 Dr. Germano has additionally contributed significantly to the enhancement of neurosurgical planning, allowing for improved safety and efficacy of tumor resection while sparing healthy tissues.47–49 More recently, Dr. Germano has begun to utilize artificial intelligence and machine learning to guide glioma diagnosis and determine survival prognosis.50,51

Dr. Gelareh Zadeh is the Head of the Division of Neurosurgery at Toronto Western Hospital, a senior scientist at Princess Margaret Cancer Centre, and chair of Neurosurgery at Mayo Clinic in Rochester.52–54 Dr. Zadeh’s research has helped to establish a new molecular classification system for meningiomas, which has been shown to predict patient outcomes more effectively than the World Health Organization classification system.55–57 Furthermore, she founded the International Consortium on Meningiomas to increase research and data-sharing to better classify meningiomas and develop more personalized treatments.58 Zadeh has additionally helped to develop minimally invasive tumor diagnostic methods that have shown promise in tumor identification, assessment of treatment response, and improving the safety of brain tumor diagnosis.59–63 Dr. Zadeh has further contributed to the field of neuro-oncology through her work on combination immunotherapy with pembrolizumab and oncolytic virotherapy to treat recurrent GBM.64,65

Dr. Viviane Tabar is a prominent physician-scientist whose research efforts largely focus on the utility of pluripotent stem cells in the treatment of CNS tumors.66–68 Her lab has used embryonic stem cells to identify key histone mutations as drivers of oncogenesis in pediatric brainstem gliomas, pediatric neuroendocrine tumors, adult glioma, and GBM.69–75 The Tabar Lab has found that stem cell therapy has the ability to remyelinate the brain and rescue behavioral deficits following radiation,76,77 developed improved neuronavigation strategies to aid in brain tumor resection,78 genetically modified neurons to express bevacizumab to treat GBM,79 and participate in multicenter trials that utilize combination therapy with multiple chemotherapeutics for high-grade gliomas.80,81 Dr. Tabar’s extensive background in neuro-oncology research and patient care has shaped her into a prominent leader in the field. Specifically, she served as the Chair of the Department of Neurosurgery at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York in 2017 and currently serves as the Theresa C. Feng Chair for Neurosurgical Oncology and the Vice Chair for Neurosurgical Research and Education.82

Dr. Karin M. Muraszko is a pediatric neurosurgeon known for her innovative research in complex childhood brain and spinal disorders.83 Though she has made scientific contributions in vascular neurosurgery, diversity and equity in the field, and pediatric spinal deformities, her oncology research focuses on pediatric brain malignancies. Specifically, she has helped to identify molecular subtypes of medulloblastoma84,85 and their effects on patient outcomes,86 biomarkers of high-grade gliomas,87–91 and the effects of thrombin on VEGF-induced tumor angiogenesis.92,93 Dr. Muraszko’s work has been integral in the development and successful execution of clinical trials designed to treat medulloblastoma with combined radio- and chemotherapy.94,95

Dr. Kate Drummond serves as the Director of Neurosurgery at the Royal Melbourne Hospital and Head of CNS Tumors for the Victorian Comprehensive Care Centre Parkville Precinct.96,97 Her neuro-oncology research largely revolves around the identification of blood biomarkers in brain tumor patients.98,99 Dr. Drummond’s work has led her to serve as a leader in the Brain POP (brain perioperative) clinical trial, which allows clinicians to precisely understand how a new drug affects brain tumors before and after treatment.100 This trial is currently active and is set to be completed in 2026.

Dr. Jennifer Molinterno serves as the Chief of Neurosurgical Oncology and Clinical Director of the Chênevert Family Brain Tumor Center at Yale Medicine.101 Dr. Moliterno has particular expertise in managing complex brain tumors and performs the highest volume of brain tumor surgery in the state of Connecticut.101 Her research group primarily focuses on the clinical implications of brain tumor genomics.102–104 Outside of the laboratory, Dr. Moliterno is actively involved in mentoring and advocating for women and other underrepresented groups in neurosurgery.

Dr. Alexandra Golby is a Professor of Neurosurgery and Radiology at Harvard Medical School who serves as the Director of Image-Guided Neurosurgery and Co-Director of the Advanced Multimodality Image Guided Operating Suite at Brigham and Women’s Hospital in Massachusetts.105 Dr. Golby’s clinical expertise and research interests center on image-guided neurosurgery for patients with brain tumors. Of note, Dr. Golby was the first to perform laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) in a clinical setting.105 LITT has since emerged as a promising minimally invasive treatment strategy for brain tumors due to its ability to precisely ablate tumor tissue while sparing surrounding healthy brain structures.106 Today, the Golby Lab continues to develop neuroimaging techniques that assist with neurosurgical planning and intraoperative guidance.107–113 Dr. Golby recently won the Innovator of the Year award at the 2024 CNS Annual Meeting.

Dr. Susan C. Pannullo has made significant contributions to the field of neurosurgical oncology and is one of the few physicians in the world board-certified in both neurology and neurosurgery. As the Director of Neurosurgical Radiosurgery and Neuro-oncology at Weill Cornell Medicine, her clinical expertise involves the use of advanced technologies such as Novalis, Gamma Knife, and CyberKnife for stereotactic radiosurgery in the treatment of brain and spinal tumors.114Her lab currently works to develop novel devices that assist with brain tumor diagnosis and stereotactic treatment.115–119 Dr. Pannullo’s contributions have allowed her to serve as a leader in several national neurosurgery organizations including the National Brain Tumor Society and AANS.114

Dr. Rupa Juthani has expertise in minimally invasive surgical approaches to treat brain tumors including endoscopic pituitary and skull base surgery, LITT, Gamma Knife radiosurgery, and fluorescence-guided surgery.120 Dr. Juthani’s research focuses on nanoparticle-based therapies for glioblastoma and brain metastases, with over 30 peer-reviewed publications and multiple book chapters.78,121,122 She has received recognition through awards such as the American Brain Tumor Association Young Investigator Award and inclusion in the New York SuperDoctors Rising Stars list in 2021 and 2022.120 Her contributions to neurosurgical oncology advance both surgical techniques and innovative therapeutic approaches for brain tumor patients.

Dr. Amy Heimberger is a leader in brain tumor immunotherapy who serves as the Vice Chair for Research in the Department of Neurological Surgery at Northwestern Feinberg School of Medicine.123 Dr. Heimberger and her lab have developed a novel peptide (PEP-3-KLH/CDX-110) vaccine for GBM that targets endothelial growth factor receptor,124 as well as a novel small molecule inhibitor, WP1066, that targets STAT3 for recurrent, malignant glioma.125 Additionally, she has worked to improve awake brain mapping for the resection of gliomas located near eloquent brain regions.126 This groundbreaking work has allowed her to gain significant recognition over the past decade, most notably being placed on the National Cancer Advisory Board by President Biden in 2021.127

Dr. Melanie Gephart practices at the Stanford Brain Tumor Center where she serves as the Director of the Stanford Brain Metastasis Consortium.128 Her oncological research focuses on disease progression of malignant brain tumors, including mechanisms that drive tumorigenesis and the supportive role of native brain cells.128,129 Dr. Gephart has worked to develop novel CSF-based biomarkers that can track brain cancer treatment response, relapse, and neurotoxicity.128 She additionally works to promote patient safety, understand the patient experience, and determine risk factors associated with brain cancer treatment.128–130 For example, Dr. Gephart has been working to develop healthier contrast alternatives to Gadolinium for cancer patients that require frequent neuroimaging.130

Dr. Beverly Walters has focused her clinical work on spinal disorders, spinal cord injury, brain injury, and glioma.131 As the first female neurosurgeon in Canada, Dr. Walters has paved the way for many Canadian women to pursue a career in neurosurgery.132 However, her work in advancing evidence-based medicine through standardizing the guidelines and methodology of randomized control trials (RCTs), case–control studies, meta-analyses, survey methodology, assessing the quality of medical literature, and developing a statistical technique to use as an algorithm when RCTs cannot be done has left a lasting and expansive impact on the field of neuro-oncology.133–136 She has taught and published extensively on these topics, earning an impressive list of recognition and prestigious roles. Her role as an educator has ultimately allowed for the dissemination of advanced methodologies and evidence-based practices, inspiring a generation of neurosurgeons to implement rigorous scientific approaches in their clinical and research endeavors.

Discussion

Over the past several decades, women have significantly shaped the field of neurosurgery. From pioneers such as Dr. Dorothy Klenke Nash, the first female neurosurgeon in the United States, to more recent trailblazers like Dr. Katrina Firlik, women have made substantial contributions to both academic neurosurgery and clinical practice. Despite these advances, the representation of women in neurosurgery remains disproportionate to that of their male colleagues. Although the number of female neurosurgeons and neurosurgery residents has increased significantly over the past decade in the United States, women constitute only approximately 8% of all practicing neurosurgeons and 30% of neurosurgery residents overall.137,138 Consequently, women constitute a significant minority of home program leaders,139 neurosurgical society leaders,140 and neurosurgical society awardees.141 Women are additionally less likely to receive research funding from the National Institute of Health,142 receive 20% less compensation as compared with men in the same positions,143 and tend to practice at lower academically ranked hospitals when compared to male neurosurgerons.139

The challenges faced by women in neurosurgery are multifaceted and exist in every phase from medical school, residency, and beyond.144,145 Women often experience gender bias,146 disparities in pay,147 and difficulties finding support and mentorship.148 The demanding nature of neurosurgical training and practice, including long hours and on-call responsibilities, disproportionately affects women, particularly those who wish to start or expand their families.149 The lack of structured parental leave policies in many institutions complicates this, making it exceedingly difficult to balance child-rearing with managing a surgical career.150 Additionally, the traditional promotion and tenure structures, which often emphasize research output and clinical hours, may disadvantage women who take time off for family planning or caregiving.151,152

Women in neurosurgical oncology may face additional unique challenges that magnify the complexity and intensity of neurosurgery. Neurosurgical oncology is a niche subspecialty, and the number of female surgeons, especially those who have risen to leadership roles, is even smaller. The lack of visible female mentors in this highly specialized field can make it harder for women to find guidance and support specific to the challenges of neurosurgical oncology, such as navigating academic research or managing complex cases. Furthermore, research in neuro-oncology is constantly evolving, a feat facilitated by the work of many oncologic neurosurgeons.153 The gender disparities experienced by women in terms of obtaining research funding and neurosurgical society awards that could help fund research, coupled with a lack of mentorship could create additional barriers to obtaining research grants, leadership positions, and opportunities to lead clinical trials in the oncology space.

Research has shown that female neurosurgeons are more likely to practice in states with more female physicians and paid parental leave policies.146 Implementing standardized parental leave policies and providing flexibility in call schedules for parents could help retain more women in neurosurgery. In addition, changes to promotion and tenure structures are needed to accommodate the career trajectories of women, which may be nontraditional in nature. Promotion and tenure in academic neurosurgery is highly influenced by research accomplishments.154,155 Women in neurosurgery are less likely to receive NIH funding143 and publish fewer first- and senior-author research papers as compared to men,156 mostly due to being more junior in rank. However, it is possible that this disparity could influence promotion and tenure status. Institutions should recognize the value of contributions outside of traditional academic benchmarks, such as leadership in professional societies, mentorship roles, and service to the community when determining promotion and tenure status. These changes could improve the promotion rates of female neurosurgeons, effectively supporting the diverse career paths of women in neurosurgery.

Neurosurgery needs women. Women bring diverse perspectives, leadership styles, and approaches to patient care that are essential to the holistic development of neurosurgery. Various programs and initiatives have been developed in recent years to empower women in neurosurgery by offering clinical practice, research, and leadership opportunities. The WINS section of the American Association of Neurological Surgeons and Congress of Neurologic Surgeons has emerged as the most prominent group in organized neurosurgery that empowers women in the field. WINS provides mentorship and networking opportunities for trainees at all levels, grants and scholarships to encourage women to engage in high-impact research, and leadership development programs that allow women in neurosurgery to hold positions at the local and national levels. The impact of WINS has been monumental in advancing the careers of women in the field and recruiting young women to pursue careers in neurosurgery.16 Other organizations including the SNS, ACS, ASBN, International Society for Pediatric Neurosurgery, and WFNS offer similar networking, mentorship, research, and leadership opportunities specifically for women in neurosurgery.

The efforts of these societies to promote gender equality in the field have paved the way for many women to pursue and maintain successful careers in neurosurgery. However, there is still work to be done (Figure 2). Mentorship programs offered by neurosurgical societies should continue to expand to reach more early-career female neurosurgeons, provide targeted support for underrepresented minorities, and foster long-term career development.157 It is important to distinguish the key differences between a mentor, defined as an individual who provides guidance, feedback, and advice from a sponsor, and who actively advocates for career advancement. While both are important in career advancement, sponsorship has been identified as being more critical in the professional advancement of women.158 To improve sponsorship for women in neurosurgery, neurosurgical societies, and academic institutions look to establish formal sponsorship programs that pair senior neurosurgeons with trainees. Scientific career development could be facilitated by the creation of working groups to write and publish manuscripts and collaborate on research projects. Workshops and other resources focused designed-to-teach and improve grant-writing skills could also provide critical support in securing research funding for female physician-scientists in neurosurgical oncology. Continued promotion of inclusive networking events and conferences additionally provides the opportunity for women to build relationships with potential sponsors. Neurosurgical organizations should continue to promote and publicly acknowledge the clinical, research, education, and leadership accomplishments of women in the field to promote visibility and increase opportunities for additional sponsorship. Social media use is becoming increasingly prevalent among neurosurgeons and could be leveraged for promoting female accomplishments.159,160 Advocacy efforts should address systemic barriers, particularly those affecting women and other underrepresented groups in academic promotion structures, to ensure equitable opportunities.

Conclusion

Women are an essential group in the field of neurosurgery and have made substantial contributions to the field in terms of research, education, leadership, and clinical excellence. However, challenges persist. Through sustained efforts in advocacy, mentorship, and institutional reform, the field of neurosurgery can continue to grow and benefit from the inclusion of more women. Addressing issues such as gender bias, lack of female representation in research and leadership, equitable parental leave, and changes in promotion and tenure structures will be critical in ensuring that more women can thrive in neurosurgery, leading to a more diverse, innovative, and inclusive environment that benefits both the profession and patient care.
