Effects of thermal exposure on bone surface characteristics and DNA recovery
Authors: Emma Thibodeau-Cadieux, Andrée M Beauchamp, Krista A Currie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn forensic cases involving burned human remains, DNA analysis and anthropological techniques are often required to aid identification. There are gaps in the literature regarding how cremation affects DNA preservation and skeletal integrity. This preliminary study aimed to examine how increasing temperature exposures impact DNA recovery and bone surface morphology. Using domestic pig (Sus scrofa) rib bones as a model and a muffle furnace, bones were exposed to temperatures ranging from 100 °C to 800 °C for 30 min. The resulting changes in colour and surface texture were observed both macroscopically and microscopically. DNA was extracted using the Applied Biosystems PrepFiler Express™ BTA Forensic DNA Extraction Kit on the AutoMate Express™ Forensic DNA Extraction System and quantified using a NanoDrop™ One UV–Vis Spectrophotometer. Bone colour changes followed an expected pattern, starting with the natural beige, transitioning to black at 350 °C, and then becoming lighter until reaching white at 800 °C. Microscopic observations showed the presence of surface striations at temperatures below 275 °C and the presence of porosity at temperatures above 275 °C. Bones with a black, grey, or white colour and visible porosity were observed in conjunction with a decrease in the amount of DNA. All the bone samples had DNA amounts exceeding 250 ng, suggesting potential suitability for short tandem repeat (STR) polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. This study furthers our understanding of the impacts of cremation on DNA recovery and bone surface integrity to aid assessment and management of remains.Keywords: DNA, Burned bone, Human identification, Cremation

Introduction

Accurate human identification is essential in cases involving suspicious deaths, accidents, natural disasters, mass fatalities, and situations where remains are found in an advanced state of decomposition or have been altered by environmental factors [1]. Identification is typically accomplished through the comparison of ante-mortem and post-mortem information [1]. For skeletal remains, such as bone and teeth, identification can be challenging and DNA has become the gold standard for identification but is typically used in tandem with forensic anthropology [2]. Cremated skeletal remains, those that have been dehydrated or oxidized due to heat exposure, pose an even greater challenge for identification due to the extensive transformations to remains [3,4]. Cremated remains can be encountered in a variety of different circumstances, including motor vehicle accidents, forest fires, house fires, homicides, and mass disasters, all of which can expose remains to varying temperature extremes [4,5]. An average house fire can reach 700 °C, a motor vehicle accident that involves ignition can reach temperatures around 900 °C–1100 °C, and firestorms have been shown to reach temperatures as high as 2000 °C [6].

Heat transformations in bone include macroscopic changes such as shrinkage, colour changes, and fragmentation which can hinder the work of forensic anthropologists by impacting their abilities to take accurate measurements and to accurately assess morphological features [7,8]. Colour changes have been widely studied under controlled environments and follow a fairly predictable pattern that can indicate the maximum temperature reached during the burn event [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Unheated bones have a white-beige colour and will become darker with increasing temperatures up to around 350 °C, after which, the bones start to lighten, taking on a greyish colouration until it reaches 700 °C–800 °C, at which point, bones will take a chalky white appearance [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Although this is the commonly documented pattern, many factors may influence the colour and appearance of burned bones including temperature, duration of exposure, oxygen availability, and the presence of contaminating objects during the heating process [8,12,14]. It is also important to note that bones do not burn uniformly due to their varying size, shape, and thickness. As a result, burned remains will display a range of colours despite all having been subjected to the same conditions [12]. The colour representing the most advanced stage of heat-related change can suggest the maximum temperatures reached during the burn event [12].

Heat causes microscopic bone composition changes, as demonstrated by Fernandez Castillo et al. (2013) [15] and Shipman et al. (1984) [13], which may be linked to DNA degradation with exposures above 100 °C [7,16]. Degradation causes fragmentation of DNA, resulting in reduced DNA quantity and quality, compromising subsequent DNA analysis [16]. Fernandez Castillo et al. (2013) observed longitudinal fractures in bones exposed to temperatures below 300 °C but no clear changes in the bone composition were noted. Osseous cells and organic tissue remained clearly visible at this level [15]. Crystalline started to form near 300 °C and, between temperatures of 400 °C and 600 °C, they noted an increased presence of crystalline formation, taking on a cubic form [15]. At increased temperatures, from 700 °C to 800 °C, the bone structure became increasingly crystalline and lost homogeneity [15]. Finally, at temperatures above 900 °C, there was a complete loss of osseous structure, and the formation of clusters. The observed structures become completely crystalline, but amorphous and granular in shape [15]. Shipman et al. (1984) found that below 185 °C, the bone surface was intact and continuous, but from 185 °C to 285 °C, the bone surface becomes more irregular and rough, with pores and fissures [13]. The roughness of the bone surface disappears after 285 °C, when the surface becomes glossy and smooth until 440 °C, when the bone surface appears highly particulate, turning frothy [13]. Finally, between 800 °C and 940 °C, the particles melt and assemble to form larger structures [13].

Previous studies have examined the impact of cremation, at various temperatures, on the amplification of both nuclear (nDNA) and mitochondrial (mtDNA) [7,10,17,18]. In some attempts, the successful amplification of DNA from cremated bones was limited to relatively low temperatures, under 200 °C [7,17]. Meanwhile, other studies have managed to amplify DNA from bones that had been cremated at higher temperatures and demonstrated a higher degree of physical damage [10,18]. Harbeck et al. (2011) reliably amplified mtDNA from bones that were cremated up to temperatures of 600 °C, and had limited success at temperatures of 700 °C [10]. Shwark et al. (2011) evaluated the efficacy of amplifying mtDNA and nDNA from cremated bones obtained from crime scenes, exhibiting varying preservation levels [18]. They found that nDNA could be amplified from bones that ranged from being well-preserved with limited change or damage, to those that had been burned to the point of exhibiting a solid black colour, whereas mtDNA was recovered from bones with blue-grey to blue-grey-white colouration, changes that reflect being heated to temperatures as high as 700 °C [18]. There has been limited success in recovering DNA from bones burned in temperatures over 700 °C. Most forensic scenarios in which cremated remains are encountered reach temperatures well above those in which DNA amplification has been successful.

Despite the existing body of research, gaps persist in the literature. There is a clear need for further research to improve our understanding of DNA recoverability from cremated bone to enhance the potential for successful identification. It has been suggested that transformations occurring to bones when exposed to heat may be used to assess the likelihood of recovering DNA from burned bones based on the macroscopic and microscopic visual changes [19]. It is important for researchers to specify which external structures may be indicative of DNA survivability. Therefore, this preliminary work aimed to assess the effect of heat on bone, with temperatures ranging from 100 °C up to 800 °C for an exposure duration of 30 min, on the quantity and quality of DNA using a commercially available DNA extraction kit designed for use in forensic DNA laboratories and optimized for bone extractions. This study also evaluated if there was an observable pattern between the macroscopic and microscopic changes in cremated bones and the potential for DNA recovery. Such patterns could aid in informing and guiding collection and analysis practices in forensic investigations maximizing the use of the often limited amounts of human remains available for identification.

Materials and methods

Bone samples

The study consisted of a full set of commercially prepared ribs from a domestic pig (Sus scrofa). Domestic pigs are widely accepted in the scientific literature as appropriate proxies for humans because of their similar anatomical and physiological characteristics [2]. A total of 24 rib bones were prepared and cleaned by removing all adhering soft tissue following standard forensic anthropology protocol for defleshing bones [20]. The process of physical tissue removal involved the use of dissection scissors and wooden sticks to cut and remove soft tissue adhering to the bone surface without damaging or scratching it. This was followed by chemical maceration using Tergazyme™ (Alconox, Inc.), a detergent which contains protease enzymes. Twenty-five grams of Tergazyme™ was mixed with 5 L of water in a large pot and heated to approximately 80 °C, ensuring not to reach a boil [20]. The bones remained in the heated Tergazyme™ solution for approximately three to 4 h until the soft tissue could easily be removed from the surface of the bone.

Thermal exposure

Groups of three rib bones were heated at 100, 200, 275, 350, 500, 650, and 800 °C for 30 min using a Lindberg™ muffle furnace. An additional set of three bones was left unheated, to be used as a control group. The bones were in individual UXcell™ 121x30 × 16mm corundum alumina crucible boats which were then placed into the furnace once it reached the desired temperature. After 30 min, the crucible and bones were removed from the furnace and allowed to cool to room temperature.

Macro and microscopic characteristics

Macroscopic bone characteristics, such as bone colouration and cracking on the outer surfaces, were visually assessed for each group of ribs. The microstructures were then observed using an Olympus SZ61 SZ2-ILST dissecting microscope. Macroscopic and microscopic images of the bone surface were taken for documentation and further analysis.

DNA extraction and quantification

Two of the largest bones within each temperature group were selected for DNA extraction to avoid large disparities in initial bone size. In preparation for DNA extraction, each bone was cleaned with soap and water using a clean toothbrush to remove any potential contaminants. The bones were then crushed into a powder using a mortar and pestle to increase the surface area available to the extraction reagents and therefore, maximizing the potential DNA recovery. To minimize contamination, the mortar and pestle were cleaned before each bone was crushed, following the protocol set out by Rancourt et al. (2023) [2].

DNA was extracted from 50 mg of powdered bone using the Applied Biosystems PrepFiler Express BTA™ Forensic DNA Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on the Applied Biosystems AutoMate Express™ Forensic DNA Extraction System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's protocol for bones. Each bone was extracted in triplicate and each extraction set included a negative control. The final elution volume was 40 μL.

Following extraction, 2 μL of each DNA sample was analyzed using the NanoDrop™ One UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to determine the quantity and quality (purity) of extracted DNA. The concentration of DNA was calculated by the NanoDrop™ One and reported in ng/μL.

Data analysis

The heat-related physical changes to the bones were assessed by visual comparison with a focus on the colour and the texture of the bone surface. Microscopic observations highlighted the absence or presence of fissures, cracks, delamination, and exposure of trabecular tissue.

The mean DNA yield (ng) and standard deviation of the bones exposed to the various temperatures were calculated based on the concentrations provided and the elution volume of 40 μL. The A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios of the DNA samples were compared to the standard DNA purity ratio of 1.8 and 2 respectively, to determine the quality of the samples.

To determine the differences between the DNA yields (ng) of the bones exposed to the different heat treatments, statistical analysis was completed using XLSTAT by Lumivero. Statistical significance was determined using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by the Dunn test with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Visual characteristics

Distinct differences were observed between bones heated at different temperatures as represented in Fig. 1. The unheated bones had a yellow-beige colour and the bones heated at 100 °C and 200 °C respectively, retained the beige colour, but were progressively paler than the unheated bones. These bones were observed to have exuded blood while being heated. The bones subjected to the 275 °C temperature treatment were dark brown and continued to exude blood. The bones took on a black, charred appearance at temperatures of 350 °C and 500 °C. At 500 °C, the bones were much drier than those heated at the lower temperatures. At 600 °C, the bones became lighter in colour, displaying a grey-blue colouration. Finally, at 800 °C, the bones exhibited a chalky white colour, showed clear signs of warping, and began to split in half exposing the trabecular bone within them.

Colour variations of unheated bone and bones exposed to temperatures ranging from 100 °C to 800 °C for 30 min.

Through the dissecting microscope, it was observed that the bones exposed to temperatures below 275 °C had a relatively smooth surface as shown in Fig. 2b–d and the unheated bones had a rugose surface texture (Fig. 2a). At 100 °C, the bone surface had shallow linear striations (Fig. 2b) which became sharper and more tightly clustered by 200 °C (Fig. 2c). At 275 °C, the two bones sampled for DNA showed different characteristics under the microscope with the larger bone displaying sharp, narrow striations (Fig. 2d), while the smaller bone displayed porosity at its surface which is a sign of delamination and occurs when the outer layer of bone is lost (Fig. 2e). Under the experimental conditions of this study that outer layer was burned off, exposing the internal trabecular bone. At 350 °C and subsequent higher temperatures, striations were lost and there was delamination characterized by the porous appearance, which increased with a rise in temperature (Fig. 2f–i). At 350 °C, there was a minimal amount of pores on the surface of the bone (Fig. 2f). There was a clear increase in the amount of porosity in the bones exposed to 500 °C (Fig. 2g) and these bones also had many microfractures on the surface (Fig. 3). By 650 °C, pores were evident on the bone surface even with the naked eye, but at microscopic level, the spaces and spicules that compose the cancellous bone started to become discernible through the uneven layer of cortical bone (Fig. 2h). The greatest amount of porosity was observed at 800 °C, where the inner bone structures are clearly exposed (Fig. 2i).

DNA quantity and quality

The average amount of DNA recovered (ng) from the bones exposed to the different heat treatments are shown in Fig. 4a and b. The results showed a gradual increase in the quantity of DNA up to 200 °C which is followed by a decrease for one of the two bones exposed to 275 °C. Following this dip in the amount of DNA recovered, there was a decrease for the bones exposed to 350 °C, 500 °C and 650 °C. At 800 °C, the bones had a slight increase in the amount of DNA recovered. The greatest amount of DNA was recovered from one of the bones heated to 100 °C with 46436 ng of DNA. The lowest amount of DNA was recovered from one of the bones exposed to 650 °C with an average of 284 ng of DNA. At 275 °C, there was a high amount of variability in the amount of DNA recovered from each of the bones with one yielding an average of 42436 ng of DNA and the smaller of the two bones yielding an average of 1340 ng of DNA, a difference found to be statistically significantly (p-value <0.05). Although some samples had lower DNA yields than others, all of the samples had DNA yields above 250 ng.

a–b. Mean DNA yield (ng) of bones exposed to increasing temperatures for 30 min. Data are displayed as the average of three replicates ± SD.

The results for the quality (purity) of the DNA samples showed that most heat treatments had average A260/A280 ratios near 1.8; however, some average A260/A280 ratios were below 1.6 for 350 °C and 500 °C exposures (Table 1). Unheated bones and bones heated to 100 °C and 200 °C, had A260/A280 ratios between 1.72 and 1.84. At 275 °C, the samples had more variability in average purity ratios ranging from 1.53 to 1.83, with all the samples having ratios below 1.6 coming from the smaller of the two bones (Bone 2). The bones exposed to 350 °C, had A260/A280 ratios below or equal to 1.6, with ratios between 1.49 and 1.6, whereas the bones exposed to 500 °C had only one sample with an average A260/280 ratio above 1.6 with ratios between 1.41 and 1.62. At 650 °C and 800 °C, the samples had ratios ranging from 1.54 to 1.91 and 1.48 to 1.91, respectively. On the other hand, the averages for the A260/A230 ratios were all found to be lower than the expected value of 2 or 2.2 (Table 1).

Discussion

The heat-related bone colour changes observed in this study followed an expected pattern identified in several previous studies [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]], starting from natural beige, transitioning to black, and then becoming lighter until it reached white. Although this pattern is commonly reported in the literature, the timing of transitions from colour to colour can occur at slightly different temperatures depending on the type of bone used, burning condition, and length of exposure [4,5]. With extended exposure time, colour changes typically occur at lower temperatures [9,10], whereas in this study, with 30 min exposure periods, the bones remained at earlier colour stages until higher temperatures were reached. The black colouration at 350 °C and 500 °C is associated with the charring/carbonization of the bones, whereas the white colour at the higher temperatures, around 800 °C, is associated with combustion or calcination, leaving only the inorganic portions of bones and requiring access to oxygen [9,21,22]. The heat-induced bone colour change is a reflection of the chemical and compositional changes occurring in bone, and therefore a result of a combination of factors.

At greater temperature exposures, there was an increase in bone porosity, which is associated with the decomposition and charring of the organic materials that compose the cortical bone. This exposes the inner trabeculae, also known as delamination [18,21,23]. In the thin compact bone of ribs, combustion may cause the loss of the superficial layers quicker than in bones with a thicker cortical layer [8]. The initial exposure of trabecular bones started in the smallest of the sampled bones heated at 275 °C and became progressively more pronounced as temperatures increased. By 800 °C, the detailed structures of the trabecular bone were clearly discernible through the microscope indicating significant compromise of the cortical layer due to heat exposure.

The trend in the amount of DNA recovered from the various thermal exposures in this study aligned with several previous studies that examined DNA recovery from burned bones [18,24] and teeth [25,26]. In the previous studies noted and this study, there was a decrease in the amount of DNA extracted from bones exposed to higher temperatures. Although high heat reduces DNA quantity, bones that were exposed to mild temperatures up to 200 °C had increased DNA yield compared to the unheated bones. It is hypothesized that moderate heating may have facilitated partial breakdown of cellular and bone matrix components, thereby improving DNA accessibility to the extraction reagents. In addition, the increased yield in DNA may be attributed to different physical compositions of the bones. In addition, a mild heat exposure also makes bone more brittle and easier to pulverize, improving cell lysis during DNA extraction [27]. At 275 °C, there was a significant difference in the average amount of DNA recovered from the two bones selected for DNA analysis, which may be due to a difference in the size and/or chemical composition of the bones [19,27]. The smaller of the two bones may have been more susceptible to thermal stress which likely contributed to the lower DNA yield that was recovered from it. This increased sensitivity to heat may be due to differences in bone density, mass, or microstructure, all of which can influence how heat penetrates and degrades bone tissue. Visual differences between the bones support this observation. The larger bone appeared lighter in colour and showed the presence of striations while the smaller bone had a darker brown colouration and showed signs of surface porosity, both of which are consistent with greater thermal damage.

All of the bone samples that went through DNA extraction yielded more than 250 ng of DNA which is generally considered a sufficient quantity for short tandem repeat (STR) typing. Most commercially available STR amplification kits suggest an optimal input range of 0.5–1.0 ng for amplification; however, forensic laboratories conduct internal validations and establish their own minimum threshold amounts, which are often lower than the recommended optimal range [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. Therefore, the DNAyields in this study would meet the lower threshold commonly accepted by many laboratories, supporting the potential for STR profiling even in samples exposed to varying heat conditions. However, it should be noted that DNA quantity alone does not guarantee successful STR analysis and factors such as DNA purity (absence or presence of inhibitors) and the extent of DNA degradation can affect STR results [32].

Most of the bones exposed to temperatures above 275 °C had A260/A280 purity ratios that were below 1.6, including the smaller of the two bones exposed to 275 °C, both bones exposed to 350 °C and 500 °C, and half of the samples exposed to 650 °C and 800 °C. These lower ratios may be associated with contaminants introduced into the DNA samples that are absorbed at or near 280 nm, such as proteins [33]. With the decomposition of organic materials, non-targeted proteins may be more susceptible to be extracted with the DNA, resulting in a slightly lower purity ratio. On the other hand, all of the samples reported low A260/A230 ratios (<2), which may be due to the use of a saline elution buffer during the automated extraction [33]. Salts are commonly absorbed around 230 nm, providing unstable values for the A260/A230 ratio, and may result in lower values than expected for a pure DNA sample [33].

There appeared to be a trend in the visual condition of the bones, both macroscopically and microscopically, and the amount of DNA recovered. The overall trend that was observed was that with an increase in temperature, there was a reduced amount of DNA recovered from each bone. With increasing porosity and a colour ranging between black, grey-blue, and chalky white, changes in bone associated with increased temperature exposure, there was a reduction in the amount of DNA extracted as well as a drop below 1.6 in the A260/A280 ratio representing a low ratio and possible protein contamination. The cremated bone visual cues of bone porosity and a black/grey/white colouration observed in this study reflect chemical and structural degradation caused by heat which impacted the bone DNA integrity. Given the results of this study, visual assessment may be used as a preliminary tool for predicting the potential success of DNA recovery when selecting samples from cremated remains for DNA analysis; however, it is recommended that further research employ a standardized or quantitative classification system for documenting morphological changes and include statistical analysis to assess potential correlations with DNA yield or quality.

Limitations and future studies

This preliminary study provided a valuable initial assessment of the impact and potential correlation of thermally altered bone and DNA integrity under controlled conditions; however, this study does have noted limitations. The sample size was limited with two bones examined for every temperature and triplicate DNA extractions performed from each bone. The results showed that there was variability in DNA yields between some bones exposed to the same temperature and within the same bone as demonstrated by the high standard deviation. Given these results, future research should expand on the data presented in this study by increasing the sample size to better understand the variability in DNA yields that were observed. An increased sample size would also allow for a stronger framework for a validated predictive model. A limitation of this study is the reliance on NanoDrop™ spectrophotometric measurements for DNA quantification, as this method cannot distinguish between DNA and other co-extracted contaminants such as proteins or salts, and therefore may overestimate DNA yield and purity. Additionally, this study used pig rib bones; however, other pig skeletal elements should be included in future research since different bone types differ in density and microstructure which can influence DNA preservation. The use of pig bones limits direct evaluation with human forensic quantification and STR profiling kits, which are the standard in forensic practice and are able to properly assess DNA degradation. Given these limitations, future studies should incorporate the use of human bones if feasible. Additional work should include the evaluation of different temperatures and burn durations, examine a range of environmental conditions including oxygen availability and humidity, and examine the influence of soft tissue on heat-related changes in bones.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the limited body of research specifically examining the impact of high heat on DNA recoverability from bone, supporting efforts for identifying cremated human remains. As a preliminary study under controlled conditions, the results demonstrated that the changes in bone colouration observed at increasing temperatures followed a predictable pattern, which may be used in conjunction with microscopic heat-related changes to aid in assessing the recoverability of DNA from cremated remains. This study also showed that although DNA yield and quality generally declined in bones displaying black, grey, or white colouration and visible surface porosity, DNA was still recoverable, even at temperatures as high as 800 °C after 30 min of exposure, indicating that cremated bones may still serve as potential viable sources of DNA. Given the challenging and often limited nature of cremated remains, and the importance of maximizing available resources, further research is needed to expand on these findings. Continued research will assist forensic practitioners in more accurately assessing the potential of successful DNA recovery and contribute to refining protocols that enhance identification efforts.
