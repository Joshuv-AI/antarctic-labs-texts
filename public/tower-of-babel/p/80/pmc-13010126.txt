Immunomodulatory-osteogenic dual-functional nanofibers augment screw anchorage in osteoporotic bone
Authors: Wei Jiang, Zeyu Han, Fushan Hou, Dong Li, Anquan Wang, Yi Shi, Pingping Bao, Xingyi Hua, Liang Wu, Tingjun Ye, Cailiang Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScrew fixation remains the primary clinical strategy for osteoporotic fractures (OPF). However, impaired osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and limited cellular migration often lead by osseointegration failure and screw loosening. Inspired by the architectural concept of "column–interface synergistic load-bearing", we developed a polydopamine-functionalized coaxial electrospun nanofiber scaffold (S-MS-PDA) to address both biological and mechanical fixation challenges at the screw–bone interface. The scaffold's coaxial design enabled the spatiotemporal sequential release of bioactive factors, including SDF-1 and P24, precisely modulating MAPK, JAK-STAT, Wnt, and bone remodeling pathways. This approach promoted BMSCs recruitment and osteogenic differentiation, rapidly establishing an early osteogenic microenvironment. Additionally, polydopamine-mediated interface engineering created a biomimetic transition zone, improving mechanical compatibility between the screw and bone and modulating local inflammation. In a rat osteoporosis model, the S-MS-PDA scaffold significantly reduced interfacial stress concentration and enhanced the bone-implant contact by 3.31 fold. This study presents a novel scaffold system that integrates biological activation with mechanical reinforcement, offering a promising tissue engineering strategy for the internal fixation of osteoporotic fractures.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Peri-implant, Electrospun, Osseointegration, M2 polarization

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a common orthopedic disease, with a prevalence of nearly 40% among individuals over 50 years of age. It is projected that by 2050, the global population affected by OP will reach 60 million [1]. Osteoporotic fractures (OPF) remain a major clinical challenge, particularly due to the high failure rate of internal fixation devices such as screws [2,3]. The compromised bone microarchitecture and impaired osteogenic capacity in osteoporosis significantly weaken the screw holding power, leading to inadequate mechanical anchorage and early loosening at the implant-bone interface [4]. In addition to reduced bone mass, the osteoporotic microenvironment is characterized by insufficient recruitment of bone mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), impaired osteogenic differentiation, heightened inflammatory responses, and poor interfacial integration—all of which contribute to fixation failure [5]. Current strategies aimed at improving screw fixation have primarily focused on optimizing implant geometry, altering material properties, or applying surface coatings to enhance mechanical interlocking [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Traditional fixation models, analogous to single load-bearing columns, struggle to achieve effective support and stable anchorage within the fragile bone structures resulting from OP [10]. Although these methods provide partial improvements, they fail to address the fundamental biological deficiencies in osteoporotic bone, especially the limited BMSCs homing and weakened osteoinductive signaling near the implant site [11].

Inspired by the architectural concept of "column–interface synergistic load-bearing," this study proposes enhancing biomechanical performance through microenvironmental regulation at the screw/bone interface. Specifically, a polydopamine (PDA) coating is introduced onto the implant surface. The catechol groups within PDA provide strong adhesion to metal surfaces while its inherent reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties can significantly modulate the immune-inflammatory microenvironment post-implantation, thus maintaining interfacial stability between screws and bone tissue. Moreover, while PDA improves "mechanical fixation", enhancing "biological fixation" at the screw/bone interface remains crucial for the treatment of OPF.

In osteoporotic patients, the bone healing process is often compromised due to local microenvironmental disturbances and endogenous cellular dysfunction, leading to markedly reduced osteogenic capacity [12]. Therefore, enhancing local BMSCs recruitment and osteogenic induction efficiency becomes another key challenge for interface integration. In this study, to address the limitations associated with traditional osteogenic factors such as BMP-2—including potential carcinogenicity and complex synthesis—a low-toxicity oligopeptide, P24, derived from the functional core sequence of BMP-2, was employed. P24 retains osteogenic inductive capabilities while minimizing adverse effects [13]. Additionally, the chemotactic cytokine stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) was introduced to stimulate the migration of endogenous BMSCs toward the implant/bone interface, ensuring rapid early-stage cellular recruitment. By precisely designing the spatial distribution of different bioactive components within the scaffold and controlling their release kinetics, bioactive molecules can be delivered to specific targets for sustained and targeted functional release.

A coaxial electrospinning technique was utilized to construct a core–shell nanofiber structure, effectively enhancing fiber stability and protecting active components [14,15]. P24 was encapsulated within the fiber core to achieve delayed release, while SDF-1 was conjugated to the fiber shell surface for rapid release, thus establishing a temporally and spatially coordinated release system. This sequential release strategy aims to rapidly recruit endogenous BMSCs during the early phase and subsequently induce their differentiation into the osteogenic lineage, collaboratively remodeling the osteogenic microenvironment at the defect site to achieve "biological fixation."

In this study, P24 was precisely encapsulated within a poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA)-based core-shell nanofiber core using a micro-sol technique(MS), while SDF-1 was covalently immobilized onto the fiber shell surface. A PDA coating was subsequently applied to the outer fiber surface to further enhance bioactivity and adhesion to titanium screws, ultimately constructing the multifunctional screw system S-MS-PDA-Ti, integrating both mechanical and biological fixation strategies. In vitro studies demonstrated that S-MS-PDA-Ti maintained excellent mechanical stability; the PDA coating enhanced screw holding power and alleviated interfacial inflammation, while the rapid release of SDF-1 promoted BMSCs recruitment and proliferation, and the sustained release of P24 facilitated the formation of an osteogenic or chondrogenic microenvironment. Animal experiments further confirmed that this material significantly enhanced screw anchorage strength and promoted bone interface osteogenesis in a rat OP model. In conclusion, by constructing a multifunctional interface system integrating "mechanical reinforcement" and "biological activation," this study proposes a novel material strategy for internal fixation treatment of osteoporotic fractures, providing both theoretical and practical foundations for achieving rapid implant/bone interface integration and bone tissue regeneration (Scheme 1).

Materials and methods

Material construction

First, 10 mg of sodium hyaluronate powder was dissolved in 990 mg of deionized water to prepare a 1 wt% hyaluronic acid (HA) aqueous solution. This solution was thoroughly mixed with pre-portioned peptide P24 to form a 1% HA-P24 aqueous solution. Then, 0.01 g of Span-80 was added to 4 g of dichloromethane (DCM) and stirred uniformly, followed by slow addition of 100 μl of the HA-P24 aqueous solution. High-speed stirring for 30 min yielded a stable water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion system containing HA microsol particles encapsulating P24. Subsequently, poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 94829; average ≈ 40,000) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were sequentially added to this emulsion with stirring to prepare the microsol (MS) spinning solution. The MS membrane was fabricated via electrospinning (parameters: solution feed rate 60 μl/min, applied voltage 15–20 kV, collection distance 15 cm). The resulting MS membrane was immersed in a surface modification solution (25 mL of 0.25 M NaOH mixed with 25 mL ethanol) for 2–3 min to cleave ester bonds and expose carboxyl groups, followed by three washes with deionized water. Next, the fiber surface was activated with 50 mM EDC/30 mM NHS in MES buffer (pH 6.0) for 30 min at 25 °C, then coupled with recombinant SDF-1α (10 μg/mL in PBS) at 4 °C for 12 h. After blocking with 1 M ethanolamine (pH 8.5) for 1 h, the membrane was washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20. Subsequently, the functionalized fibers were immersed in a 2 mg/mL dopamine hydrochloride solution in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5) and oscillated at 60 rpm for deposition. Upon reaching a coating thickness of 45 ± 5 nm (approximately 40 min at 25 °C), the fibers were removed, washed three times with deionized water, and dried. After immersion in dopamine solution, the electrospun fiber membrane was tightly wrapped 360° around the threads of the titanium screw.

Characterization analyses

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM): Electrospun samples were freeze-dried, sputter-coated with gold, and observed using a Hitachi SU8010 field-emission SEM at 5 kV acceleration voltage and 10 mm working distance.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): MS fibers were dispersed in ethanol, dropped onto copper grids, negatively stained with phosphotungstic acid, and observed using a JEM-2100 TEM at 200 kV.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR): Spectra were recorded using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer from 4000 to 400 cm−1 at 4 cm−1 resolution, averaging 32 scans. Samples were prepared with KBr pellets.

Tensile Strength Testing: Conducted using an Instron 5967 universal testing machine at a stretching rate of 10 mm/min with samples cut to 10 × 40 mm, repeated five times.

Adhesion Strength Test: The nanofiber membranes (PLLA, MS, S-MS, S-MS-PDA) were adhered to the titanium surface (bonding area: 10 mm × 10 mm). A tensile load was applied parallel to the interface using a universal testing machine at a speed of 1 mm/min until failure. The maximum load was recorded, and the shear adhesion strength was calculated.

Water Contact Angle (WCA): Measured using a Dataphysics OCA20 system with 2 μL DI water droplets, averaged over five measurements.

Isolation and culture of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs)

Primary BMSCs were isolated from 8-week-old rat. Briefly, uthanasia was performed in rats by overdose of isoflurane inhalation. Under sterile conditions, tibiae and femora were harvested, and bone marrow cells were flushed out using syringes. The collected cells were cultured in α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 0.01% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Medium was refreshed every three days. Cells were passaged upon reaching 80% confluency. Primary BMSCs were expanded in vitro, and only cells at passages 3–5 were used for all experiments to ensure consistent stemness and differentiation capacity. Cells were subcultured at 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA and reseeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL. Passage number was strictly controlled and matched across all experimental groups.

Osteogenic differentiation assay

Scaffold extracts were prepared by incubating electrospun membranes in serum-free α-MEM at 37 °C for 24 h. BMSCs were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured with osteogenic induction medium prepared using scaffold extracts supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.01% penicillin/streptomycin, 50 mg/L ascorbic acid(Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 10 nM dexamethasone(Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate(Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The medium was replaced every three days during the three-week osteogenic induction period.

CCK-8 and live/dead cell assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China) and a Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. BMSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 103 cells/well and cultured with scaffold extract medium. At days 1, 3, and 5, CCK-8 assays and live/dead staining were performed. For CCK-8, cells were incubated with 100 μL of culture medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent at 37 °C for 1.5 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a FlexStation 3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Japan). For live/dead staining, cells were incubated with 250 μL of Calcein-AM/PI working solution for 30 min and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining

ALP staining was performed after 7 days of osteogenic induction to assess ALP activity. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, washed with PBS, and then incubated with 500 μL of ALP staining solution (Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 12 h. Images were captured using a digital camera (Canon, Japan) or a microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Alizarin red staining

After 21 days of osteogenic induction, Alizarin Red staining was performed to assess mineralized nodule formation. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and washed with PBS for 30 min. Subsequently, 500 μL of 2% Alizarin Red solution (Beyotime, China) was added and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Images of calcium nodules were captured using a digital camera (Canon, Japan) or microscope (Olympus, Japan). For quantification, calcium nodules were dissolved in 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (Sangon Biotech, China) and absorbance was measured at 420 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo, USA).

Transwell migration assay

In the migration assay, the lower chamber of the Transwell insert was filled with 600 μL scaffold extract medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Third-passage BMSCs were serum-starved for 6 h and then seeded into the upper chamber at 5 × 104 cells/mL. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, non-migrated cells on the upper side of the membrane were removed with a cotton swab and washed with PBS. Migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, washed with PBS, and then counted under a light microscope.

Scratch assay

BMSCs were seeded into 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/well and cultured in α-MEM with 10% FBS until 90% confluence. After making a straight scratch with a sterile pipette tip (200uL) and washing with PBS, the medium was replaced with extract-containing medium. Images of the scratch area were captured at 0, 12, and 24 h under an inverted microscope at a fixed location (100 × magnification).

Immunofluorescence staining

On day 7, BMSCs seeded on different materials were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-Runx2 (1:200) and mouse anti-OPN (1:300) primary antibodies. After washing with PBS, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:500) were applied for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Runx2: Ex/Em = 488/519 nm; OPN: Ex/Em = 594/617 nm), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Animal experiment methods

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui Medical University. Animals were purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of Anhui Medical University and housed under standard pathogen-free conditions. Rat were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water and sufficient rest. Eighty-six adult female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (8 weeks old, ∼250 g) were used in this study. Six rats were randomly selected for the sham operation group (SHAM), in which only the adipose tissue around the ovaries was removed. The remaining OVX rats (n = 75) were then randomly allocated to five groups corresponding to the different screw coatings: Control, PLLA, MS, S-MS, and S-MS-PDA (n = 15 per group). From each group, 9 rats were used for Micro-CT evaluation and histological analysis, and 6 were used for biomechanical pull-out testing. The appropriate coatings were applied to the screws prior to implantation according to standard protocols. All rats underwent screw implantation surgery 8 weeks after OVX modeling. After aseptic preparation, a lateral incision was made to expose the distal femoral condyles. The soft tissues were carefully separated, and a Kirschner wire was used to drill a channel into the femoral condyle. The pre-coated titanium screws (length 6 mm, diameter 1.5 mm) were then inserted into the channel according to group assignment, ensuring accurate placement and tight fixation. All surgical procedures were performed under sterile conditions. Postoperatively, rats received daily intraperitoneal injections of penicillin (80,000 U) for 3 days, and general health was monitored throughout the study.

Micro-computed tomography (μCT) analysis

After treatment, rat femora were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Samples were scanned using a Skyscan 1176 (Skyscan) μCT system (voltage: 70 kV, current: 200 μA, resolution: 9 μm/pixel). Regions of interest (ROI) starting 0.45 mm below the growth plate and extending 1.35 mm were selected for 3D reconstruction and analysis using CTAn software, evaluating parameters including bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and connectivity density (Conn.D).

Pull-out and removal torque tests

Six samples from each group were randomly assigned for biomechanical testing. Half of the samples underwent the pull-out test as previously described. To provide a more comprehensive assessment of the interfacial bonding quality, the remaining samples were subjected to a removal torque test. The femur condyles containing the screw were securely fixed in a custom-made holder. A calibrated digital torque gauge (Tohnichi, Japan) was attached to the screw head. A counter-clockwise rotational force was applied at a constant rate until the screw loosened. The peak torque value recorded during the initial rotation was defined as the removal torque (N·cm), representing the shear strength of the bone-implant interface.

RNA sequencing and analysis

BMSCs were cultured to 90% confluence in α-MEM with 10% FBS. Treatment groups were switched to extract-containing α-MEM, while control groups remained unchanged. After 24 h, cells were harvested and frozen with liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol and submitted to Company for RNA sequencing. Bioinformatics analysis was conducted using R (v4.3.2). Principal component analysis (PCA) and heatmaps were used to assess sample clustering. Differential expression analysis was performed using the "edgeR" package. Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and GSEA analyses were performed using "clusterProfiler," and figures were generated using "ggplot2."

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with at least three biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for multiple group comparisons. Statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using Prism software. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.005).

Results

Characterization of the composite scaffold

Using (micro-sol)electrospinning technology, we successfully fabricated the S-MS-PDA scaffold and conducted detailed observations. After freeze-drying the electrospun fibers, their surface morphology was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 1A). The electrospun fibers of different compositions exhibited similar diameters, approximately 400 nm, and showed a random, non-aligned orientation. Moreover, a dense PDA coating was observed on the fiber surfaces. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed clear differences between conventional PLLA fibers and MS fibers. In the MS fibers, a distinct core–shell structure was observed, with a core encapsulating P24 surrounded by an outer PLLA shell, indicating the successful synthesis of core–shell structured MS fibers (Fig. 1B). Simultaneously, the presence of N and S elements in EDS mapping confirmed the successful encapsulation of P24 and SDF-1 within PLLA(Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, FITC-BSA was incorporated into the core structure of S-MS-PDA, and fluorescence microscopy revealed a distinct inner fluorescent signal, further confirming the presence of a core–shell architecture in the fabricated S-MS-PDA fibers(Supplementary Fig. 2).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was then performed to assess the chemical structure of different scaffold components. Comparing the FTIR spectra of PLLA and S-MS revealed an alteration in the absorption peak around 1740 cm−1 (characteristic of carboxyl groups) in S-MS (Supplementary Fig. 3). The consumption of carboxyl groups demonstrated the successful grafting of SDF-1 onto the activated MS. Additionally, the FTIR spectra of PLLA and MS were similar, likely because the HA and P24 components were completely encapsulated within the PLLA matrix, thereby masking characteristic HA peaks. The water contact angles (WCA) of the PLLA and MS fibers were 105.30° ± 3.39° and 112.48° ± 2.44°, respectively, showing no significant difference. However, after PDA coating, the surface contact angle of S-MS-PDA significantly decreased, reaching 52.17° ± 2.77°, suggesting that the PDA layer endowed the scaffold with markedly improved hydrophilicity (Fig. 1C and D). The stability of the coating is a prerequisite for its biological function. The lap-shear test results showed that the adhesion strength of the S-MS-PDA group was 2.71 ± 0.21 MPa, which was significantly higher than that of the non-adhesive PLLA group (0.42 ± 0.03 MPa) and the MS group (0.43 ± 0.03 MPa), as well as the S-MS group (0.50 ± 0.02 MPa). This robust adhesion is attributed to the abundant catechol groups in the PDA coating, which form strong coordination bonds with the titanium surface, ensuring that the coating remains intact during surgical implantation (Fig. 1E). The anchorage force of the screws was measured by pull-out testing (Fig. 1F). To further verify the macroscopic uniformity of the coating on the complex screw geometry, the S-MS-PDA coated screws were observed under SEM. The nanofiber membrane tightly and uniformly wrapped around the screw threads without obvious delamination or rupture, maintaining the thread profile (Supplementary Fig. 4). HPLC analysis showed that SDF released rapidly, reaching nearly 70% within the first seven days and plateauing around day 14, with cumulative release approaching 80%. In contrast, P24 released more slowly and steadily, gradually increasing to around 70% by day 21, demonstrating sustained, extended release (Fig. 1G). To evaluate the mechanical properties, stress–strain measurements were conducted. The tensile strengths of PLLA, MS, S-MS, and S-MS-PDA membranes were 4.52 ± 0.57, 3.95 ± 0.29, 3.87 ± 0.22, and 4.74 ± 0.34 MPa, respectively(Fig. 1H). Compared to PLLA membranes, the MS membranes showed a slight decrease in maximum tensile strength, likely due to the presence of the core-shell structure. Nevertheless, the mechanical properties of S-MS-PDA were significantly improved, which can be attributed to the deposition and reinforcement effects of the nanofiber architecture.

Bioactivity of the composite scaffold

The electrospun fibers serve as the direct contact interface between the titanium screw and the bone, and thus must exhibit excellent biocompatibility. Otherwise, they could cause cell death at the implant/bone interface, trigger immune responses, and ultimately lead to impaired bone healing. To assess biocompatibility, S-MS-PDA fibers were soaked in culture medium for 24 h to obtain the material extract, which was subsequently co-cultured with BMSCs. Cell viability was evaluated using Live/Dead staining and CCK-8 assays. After 24 h of co-culture, the majority of cells remained viable, with no significant differences observed among groups, and the survival rate exceeded 90% in all cases (Fig. 2A and B).

CCK-8 results showed that BMSC proliferation was significantly enhanced in the S-MS-PDA group. On day 3, absorbance increased by 44%, and by day 5, it was 47% higher than the control group, indicating a clear time-dependent proliferative advantage (Fig. 2C). These results demonstrate that the synthesized fiber coatings did not inhibit BMSCs proliferation, confirming good biocompatibility. The recruitment effect of the materials on BMSCs was evaluated by Transwell migration and scratch assays. Compared with the control and unmodified scaffolds, the S-MS-PDA group significantly enhanced BMSCs migration and scratch healing, indicating superior chemotactic and regenerative potential. In contrast, PLLA and MS alone showed only limited effects(Fig. 2D–G). Polydopamine (PDA) is known to reprogram macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. During material implantation, foreign body stimuli and the osteoporotic (OP) microenvironment often induce macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, resulting in the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and disruption of bone healing. Therefore, promoting macrophage reprogramming from M1 to M2 is considered beneficial for improving the bone regeneration microenvironment. To investigate this effect, we analyzed macrophage polarization using flow cytometry. After seven days of culture, the expression of CD206, a marker of M2 macrophages, was significantly higher in the S-MS-PDA group compared to the Con group, indicating the material's ability to promote M2 polarization (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, even after LPS-induced M1 polarization, S-MS-PDA treatment could still effectively reprogram M1 macrophages into the M2 phenotype (Fig. 2I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that S-MS-PDA not only promotes the polarization of naïve (M0) macrophages toward the M2 phenotype but also facilitates the reprogramming of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages into M2 cells, which may contribute to the amelioration of the inflammatory microenvironment and enhancement of bone tissue regeneration.

Osteogenic effects of the composite scaffold

The osteogenic effects of the nanofibers on BMSCs were assessed by evaluating alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and Alizarin Red staining. After 7 days, ALP activity in the MS and S-MS-PDA groups increased by 2.1-fold and 2.4-fold, respectively, compared with PLLA, demonstrating a strong early osteogenic effect of P24 (Fig. 3A and B). On day 21, Alizarin Red staining was performed to further evaluate the effects of different components on the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Denser calcium nodules were observed in the BMSCs cultured with MS and S-MS-PDA scaffolds, indicating higher osteogenic activity (Fig. 3C and D). Calcium nodules were dissolved in perchloric acid, and absorbance was measured at 420 nm. Statistical results showed that the absorbance values of the MS and S-MS-PDA groups were significantly higher than those of the groups without P24, further confirming the osteogenic effect of P24.

On day 7, immunofluorescence staining for Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and osteopontin (OPN) was performed. Semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescence images revealed that the fluorescence intensities in the MS and S-MS-PDA groups were approximately twice as high as those in the groups without P24. Runx2 protein is primarily expressed in osteoblasts and serves as a key regulator of bone development and maintenance, and the expression of OPN also indicates bone formation. Therefore, the fluorescence results suggest that nanofibers containing P24 significantly promote bone formation (Fig. 3E–H). Western blotting further confirmed that the expression levels of OPN and RUNX2 proteins in the MS and S-MS-PDA groups were significantly higher than those in the other two groups (Fig. 3I and J).

Establishment of an in vivo osteoporosis model

Following the previous protocols established by our research group, an osteoporosis model was created by performing ovariectomy (OVX) on SD rats. Micro-CT scanning was conducted 8 weeks after surgery to confirm the development of osteoporosis in both the sham-operated and OVX groups (Fig. 4A). Micro-CT analysis revealed a significant loss of bone volume, deterioration of the trabecular architecture, and an expansion of the bone marrow cavity within the femoral condyle of OVX rats compared to the sham-operated group. (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, histological assessment by H&E and Masson's trichrome staining corroborated the distinct morphological differences between the groups(Fig. 4C and D). Specifically, the sham group showed normal lamellar trabecular bone structure, whereas the OVX rats exhibited rod-shaped degeneration of the trabeculae. Quantitative analysis of Micro-CT data further revealed significant differences between the two groups in bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume (BV), connectivity density (Conn.D), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Sp), confirming the successful establishment of the osteoporosis model with significant differences compared to the sham group (Supplementary Fig. 5).

In vivo osteogenic effects of the composite scaffold

Titanium screws were surgically inserted into the femoral condyles of all osteoporotic rats eight weeks following the ovariectomy procedure. In this study, the group with direct screw implantation was referred to as the Control group. Prior to screw implantation, the materials—PLLA, MS, S-MS, and S-MS-PDA—were coated onto the surfaces of the screws, which were then implanted together into the screw channels in the femoral condyles, corresponding to the PLLA group, MS group, S-MS group, and S-MS-PDA group, respectively. The screws were accurately fixed onto the femoral condyles, and the size of the titanium screws matched the rat femoral condyle precisely. Eight weeks after implantation, all rats were sacrificed, and the femoral condyles were harvested for Micro-CT analysis to evaluate new bone formation around the screws (Fig. 4E). After 8 weeks, the BV/TV percentage in the S-MS-PDA group was significantly higher than that in the other groups. This result may be attributed to the stable adhesion of S-MS-PDA in the screw channel, its regulation of local macrophage polarization, and its effective recruitment and differentiation of BMSCs, thereby enhancing osseointegration. Micro-CT results were equally satisfactory, with the results showing that the BV/TV value (9.063 ± 0.531 %), Tb.N value (3.247 ± 0.249 1/mm), and Conn.D value (25.440 ± 1.634 1/mm3) of the S-MS-PDA group were the highest in all groups and were 3.16, 1.74, and 2.306 times for the control group (Fig. 4F–H). In addition, we evaluated the anchoring strength of screws based on pull-out tests, and the results showed that the pull-out force of screws in S-MS-PDA group (89.074 ± 4.441 N) was the highest after eight weeks of implantation, which was 1.238, 1.787, 2.064, and 2.171 times that of S-MS group (71.928 ± 6.118 N), MS group (49.844 ± 4.367 N), PLLA group (43.14 ± 3.167 N), and control group (41.03 ± 1.927 N), respectively (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, removal torque tests were conducted to evaluate the rotational stability and shear strength of the osseointegration, which reflects the quality of bone remodeling within the screw threads. The S-MS-PDA group exhibited a significantly higher removal torque of 16.300 ± 1.507 N cm compared to the S-MS group (11.020 ± 1.052 N cm), MS group (6.780 ± 0.823 N cm), and PLLA group (5.140 ± 0.336 N cm). The substantially increased removal torque in the S-MS-PDA group indicates that the biomimetic coating not only enhanced axial holding power but also improved the circumferential shear resistance, confirming the formation of high-quality, mature bone tissue at the interface (Fig. 4J).

Subsequently, the osteogenic situation was further evaluated through histopathological examinations (H&E, Masson staining, toluidine blue, and immunohistochemistry). By the eighth week, compared to the control group, the S-MS-PDA group exhibited a significant enhancement in new bone formation, with a more abundant trabecular structure around the screw (Fig. 5A–C). Quantitative analysis revealed that the area stained by H&E in the S-MS-PDA group (55.358 ± 3.422 %) was markedly increased, being 3.259 times that of the control group (16.986 ± 0.448) (Fig. 5D). A similar trend was observed in Masson staining as well (Fig. 5E). Moreover, the bone-implant contact (BIC) in the S-MS-PDA group was 3.311 times that of the control group(Fig. 5F). Finally, we assessed the protein expression levels of type I collagen and osteopontin (OPN) in each group through immunohistochemistry. The results indicated that the expression levels of both osteogenic marker proteins in the S-MS-PDA group were significantly elevated compared to other groups (Fig. 5G–I). In summary, through pathological detection of animal tissue sections, we successfully demonstrated that the osteogenic performance of S-MS-PDA is optimal. This indicates that the strategy of macrophage reprogramming to recruit and differentiate BMSCs can effectively function within the body.

Discussion

This study, based on the "structure-function" synergistic design concept, successfully constructed a multifunctional composite material [16], S-MS-PDA-Ti, capable of promoting implant/bone interface integration. Specifically, osteogenic active peptide P24 was encapsulated within the core of poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA) to form a core–shell structured micro-sol electrospun nanofiber (MS), stromal cell-derived factor-1α (SDF-1α) was grafted onto the fiber surface through chemical coupling, and a polydopamine (PDA) coating was applied to achieve stable integration with titanium screws [17]. This design employed a three-tier functionalization strategy—rapid surface release of SDF-1α to recruit BMSCs, sustained core release of P24 to induce osteogenic differentiation, and PDA-mediated interface modulation of the immune microenvironment—precisely targeting the core mechanisms underlying bone integration failure in OPF.

Dual impairment of BMSCs migration and osteogenesis within the OPF microenvironment is a key factor leading to bone integration failure. As core effector cells in bone regeneration, the directional migration and functional activation of BMSCs toward bone defects are critical to initiating the repair process [18]. In physiological bone healing, chemotactic factors such as SDF-1α and BMP-2 released at the injury site effectively recruit BMSCs to the defect area and induce their differentiation into osteoblasts within the osteogenic microenvironment, completing new bone deposition [19,20]. However, under pathological OP conditions, this process is disrupted in multiple ways: local inflammation factors (such as TNF-α and IL-6) are abnormally elevated, suppressing CXCR4 receptor expression on BMSCs and weakening their chemotactic responsiveness [21]; simultaneously, the aging microenvironment, characterized by ROS accumulation and Wnt/β-catenin signaling suppression, significantly impairs the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs [22]. This "quantity-function" dual deficiency results in insufficient BMSCs density within the OPF bone defect area, ultimately depleting the "repair cell pool" [23].

Traditional strategies attempting to supplement this deficiency through local injection of exogenous BMSCs or single factors (e.g., BMP-2) still face challenges such as low cell survival rates, burst release of factors, and short-lived therapeutic effects [24,25]. In this study, the spatiotemporal controlled release design of S-MS-PDA-Ti innovatively realized the precise recruitment and directed differentiation of endogenous BMSCs: early rapid release of SDF-1α mimicked physiological injury signals, activating the CXCR4/SDF-1α axis to efficiently recruit BMSCs from bone marrow and peripheral circulation to the bone-screw interface; sustained release of P24 continuously activated the BMP/Smad pathway, effectively promoting MSC osteogenic differentiation. Importantly, the introduction of the PDA coating, by scavenging ROS and modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway, significantly inhibited pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage activation while promoting M2 macrophage polarization, thus reversing the local microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory to a repair-dominated state and creating an "immune-osteogenic dual regulation" stem cell niche. Compared to single-factor delivery systems, this strategy greatly accelerated bone regeneration at the screw fixation site in OPF, further confirming the synergistic necessity of MSC functional recruitment and immune microenvironment remodeling for OPF bone regeneration.

In osteoporosis, multiple signaling cascades cooperate to disturb the balance between bone formation and resorption. Dysregulation of the MAPK pathway in osteoblast-lineage cells has been shown to mediate oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and impaired osteogenic differentiation, whereas appropriate activation of ERK and p38 MAPK promotes osteoblast maturation and bone formation [26,27]. Likewise, the JAK-STAT axis is a key downstream effector of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α; its chronic activation in the osteoporotic microenvironment enhances osteoclastogenesis and suppresses osteoblast function, thereby shifting bone remodeling toward resorption [28]. In contrast, canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling is essential for maintaining osteoblast commitment and bone mass, and its suppression is a well-recognized hallmark of osteoporotic bone, while restoration of Wnt activity improves bone microarchitecture and fracture resistance [29]. Consistent with these reports, our transcriptomic data revealed significant enrichment of MAPK, JAK-STAT, Wnt, and bone remodeling pathways in BMSCs treated with S-MS-PDA extracts, suggesting that the composite scaffold may promote osteogenic differentiation and partially rebalance osteoporotic bone remodeling through the coordinated modulation of these signaling cascades.

Despite the significant bone integration advantages exhibited by S-MS-PDA-Ti in OPF internal fixation therapy, this study still has the following limitations: first, the current experiments were conducted in a rat osteoporosis model, whose bone metabolism rate and mechanical loading patterns differ from those of humans, requiring further validation of long-term efficacy and biomechanical stability in large animal models (such as sheep or pig OP models); second, the degradation kinetics of the PDA coating and the long-term effects of its metabolic products on the local microenvironment remain unclear, necessitating systematic evaluation of its in vivo biosafety (such as liver and kidney function and immunotoxicity). Future research could explore the following directions: (1) developing personalized intelligent scaffolds integrating biosensing and feedback-controlled release systems to dynamically regulate factor release according to local inflammatory levels and stem cell density; (2) expanding multimodal therapeutic strategies, such as combining anti-osteoporotic drugs (e.g., bisphosphonates) or gene editing technologies (e.g., CRISPR-Cas9 targeting MSC osteogenic genes) to form a "mechanical fixation-bone metabolism regulation-regenerative repair" synergistic treatment system; (3) promoting clinical translation research, establishing standardized manufacturing processes, evaluating large-scale production cost-effectiveness, and exploring the applicability of the material across different orthopedic implantation scenarios (such as joint replacement and spinal fusion). Through interdisciplinary innovation, S-MS-PDA-Ti has the potential to move from the laboratory to clinical application, providing safer and more effective treatment options for OPF patients.

Conclusion

In this study, based on electrospinning technology, a multifunctional nanofiber capable of adhering to the surface of titanium screws was successfully fabricated. This nanofiber can reprogram macrophages toward M2 polarization and in situ recruit and differentiate BMSCs within the bone marrow cavity, providing strong support for bone repair in OPF. Although the designed S-MS-PDA-Ti exhibited certain osteogenic promotion capabilities in both in vitro and in vivo experiments, further investigations are still required. In vivo, the wear and degradation behavior of S-MS-PDA nanofibers under complex limb movement and immune environments need to be clarified. The interaction between the nanofibers and macrophages requires further exploration. Additionally, the strength and osteogenic capacity of S-MS-PDA-Ti need to be validated in larger animal models. In summary, the fabricated S-MS-PDA-Ti shows potential for the treatment of OPF.
