Male-specific amelioration of lipid metabolism by edible insect Locusta migratoria in rats fed a cholesterol diet: Ingredients, mechanisms, and sex-differences
Authors: Masaru Ochiai, Yume Takahashi, Bungo Shirouchi, Yoshihiro Suzuki, Kanan Mine, Tatsuyuki Takahashi, Jun Nagasao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEdible insects are expected to become alternative and sustainable food resources that will help ensure food security. Among them, the migratory locust (ML), which contains n-3 fatty acids (FA) that bind multiple lipid species, phytosterols, chitin, and resistant proteins, could ameliorate dyslipidemia by improving lipoprotein metabolism. However, since the positive effects of female hormones on lipid metabolism are well-known, the effects of ML supplementation on lipid metabolism may differ between sexes. This study investigated the ability of the ML supplementation to improve lipid metabolism in male and premenopausal female rats fed a 0.5% cholesterol diet for 4 weeks. Notably only in male rats, ML supplementation exhibited reduced plasma lipid levels in all major lipoprotein fractions, particularly the very small low-density lipoprotein fractions, which are more closely related to dyslipidemia. In the liver of male rats, ML supplementation suppressed enzymatic activities and mRNA expressions related to FA synthesis, FA desaturation, very low-density lipoprotein formation, and cholesterol synthesis, as well as dietary lipid absorption. Additionally in the cecum of male rats, ML supplementation increased certain alpha diversity indices and the abundance of the Akkermansia genus, which can improve lipid metabolism. Furthermore, ML supplementation increased anti-inflammatory n-3 FA levels, whereas suppressed inflammation-induced n-6 FA levels in the plasma and liver, and increased fecal lipid content in rats regardless of sex. Overall, ML supplementation was found to have an improvement effect on lipid metabolism, particularly in male rats, by suppressing lipid absorption and hepatic lipogenesis, as well as altering the cecal microbiota.Keywords: Edible insects, Migratory locust, Small-dense low-density lipoprotein, Cholesterol, Triacylglycerol, Functional lipids, Male rat

Introduction

The expected growth of the global population will require available resources to meet the nutritional needs of humans. New sustainable food resources, particularly those containing edible insects, are being explored due to recent concerns about ensuring food security. In 2013, the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations reported the importance of edible insects as sustainable food and feed resources. Certain species of edible insects had been approved by the European Union as novel food materials in 2018. Accordingly, the number of academic studies about edible insects have recently been increasing.

More than 2100 different species of edible insects have been identified worldwide. Furthermore, the macro-nutritional composition of several edible insects has been comprehensively reported. We also reported that edible migratory locusts (ML) contained proteins, lipids, chitin, a species of dietary fibers, and other nutrients (Ochiai, Inada, & Horiguchi, 2020; Ochiai & Komiya, 2021; Ochiai, Suzuki, Suzuki, Iwata, & Murayama, 2024). Regarding the nutritional quality of lipids, edible ML and silkworms have been found to be rich in n-3 α-linolenic acid (ALA), which is well known to improve lipid metabolism, not only in the triacylglycerol (TAG) forms but also in the various species of phospholipid (PL) forms (Ochiai & Komiya, 2021). The other studies also suggested that some edible locusts contained an abundance of various phytosterol species that can improve lipid metabolism (Mudalungu, Mokaya, & Tanga, 2023; Tzompa-Sosa et al., 2021). We previously revealed that ML supplementation ameliorated plasma lipid levels by improving lipoprotein and fatty acid (FA) metabolism, such as decreasing plasma low-density lipoprotein (LDL) lipid levels and FA-related indices of lipogenesis and atherosclerosis, in male rats (Ochiai et al., 2022).

The onset and development of dyslipidemia can induce cardiovascular disease (CVD), which is one of the most prevalent diseases. Atherosclerosis has been considered the primary cause of CVD, with elevated plasma LDL cholesterol (LDL-CHO) levels playing a critical role in the development and progression of CVD. Therefore, reducing plasma LDL-CHO levels can help prevent atherosclerosis and lower the risk of CVD. A review (D'Antonio, Battista, Sacchetti, Di Mattia, & Serafini, 2023) emphasized the need for more investigations on the nutritional and pharmaceutical potential, such as improving lipid metabolism, of edible insects for an obvious claim on their value.

By the way, our previous studies (Ochiai et al., 2020; Ochiai et al., 2022) on edible insects used male rats to circumvent metabolic oscillations curing during the estrus cycle in premenopausal female rats. No studies have yet investigated the effects of ML supplementation according to sex. According to a review (Palmisano, Zhu, & Stafford, 2017), the age of onset for the first myocardial infarction is later for women, indicating a lower risk of CVD than with men. Considering the etiologies for sex-differences in lipoprotein metabolism and the lower risk for CVD onset in premenopausal women from the molecular aspect, more than 1000 human hepatic genes display a sex-bias in their expressions, with the top biological pathways being listed for lipid metabolism, dyslipidemia, and CVD (Zhang et al., 2011). Additionally, 43% of lipid metabolism-related genes are transcriptionally regulated by estrogen receptor α (ERα) (Gao, Fält, Sandelin, Gustafsson, & Dahlman-Wright, 2008). Palmisano et al. (2017) also suggested that female-specific estrogens can affect lipid metabolism by modifying signaling to estrogen receptors. High secretion of estrogens can promote LDL-CHO clearance and prevent the onset and development of dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis. Thus, when Western-style foods, such as a high CHO diet, are provided, male rats with low blood estrogen levels are expected to be at a greater risk for the onset and development of dyslipidemia and liver diseases than are female rats. Additionally, many studies have shown that postmenopausal women with decreasing estrogen levels have impaired hepatic lipid metabolism and increased risks for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatosis liver disease (MASLD).

Although ML supplementation is expected to decrease plasma LDL levels and increased the liver uptake of plasma lipids, the previous results were limited to male rats fed a standard diet (Ochiai et al., 2022). These findings led us to hypothesize that the improvement effect of ML supplementation on lipid metabolism might differ according to sex. Therefore, the current study investigated the improvement effect of ML supplementation on lipid metabolism and its regulatory mechanisms in male and premenopausal female rats fed a CHO-containing diet.

Methods and materials

Preparation of ML and reagents

Powdered ML, which was manufactured by previously heat-treating for food hygiene by Thailand Unique (Udon-Thani, Thailand), was kindly provided by TAKEO (Tokyo, Japan). The powder did not contain any excipient. The proximate nutrient composition of ML was previously evaluated as follows: the contents of crude protein, crude fat, and chitin contents were 73.4 g, 4.4 g, and 10.7 g per 100 g of dry mass, respectively (Ochiai et al., 2024; Ochiai & Komiya, 2021). Research-grade reagents were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals (Osaka, Japan), Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA), Kanto Chemical (Tokyo), Muto Pure Chemicals (Tokyo), and Nakalai Tesque (Kyoto, Japan).

Lipids and lipid metabolite components in ML based on lipid-omics analysis

For the evaluation of lipids and lipid metabolomics components, a comprehensive analysis was conducted according to the Lipidome lab Non-targeted Lipidome Scan package (Lipidome lab, Akita, Japan), using liquid chromatography orbitrap mass spectrometry (LC-OrbitrapMS) based on previously described methods (Nishiumi et al., 2022; Takumi et al., 2022) (Supplementary method file). Raw data were treated using Lipid Search 5.1.6 (Mitsui Knowledge Industries, Tokyo), a lipid molecular identification software that identifies individual intact lipid molecules based on their molecular weight and fragmentation patterns from headgroup and FA composition. The relative values were calculated using the ratio of the chromatographic peak area of each analytical compound to that of the total analytical compounds. The sum of the molecular species of glycoglycerolipids, sphingolipids, fatty acyls, sterol lipids, glycerophospholipids (GPL), and prenol lipids was shown as 100%. The quantification and annotation methods used in this study corresponded to “absolute quantification Level 4” and “Fatty Acyl/Alkyl Level or Hydroxyl Group Level”, respectively as defined by the Lipidomics Standard initiative (Lipidomics Standards Initiative Consortium, 2019).

Steroid components in the total lipids from ML

Some steroid components in the total lipids from ML were measured as previously described (Shirouchi et al., 2023). Total lipids (1.39 g) were extracted from ML (10.0 g) for two days according to the Folch method (Folch, Lees, & Sloane Stanley, 1957). The solvent was evaporated to obtain the extracted lipids using a rotary evaporator (EYELA SB-1300, Tokyo Rikakikai, Tokyo) at 40 °C and then stored at −30 °C until analysis. The steroid contents in the total lipids were measured in triplicate using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) on the Shimadzu GC–MS-QP2010 Ultra (Shimadzu, Kyoto) equipped with an InertCap 5MS/NP capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm thickness, GL Sciences, Tokyo) by adding 5α-cholestane (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Harbor, MI, USA) as an internal standard. Details regarding the preparation and analytical conditions of GC–MS were described previously (Shirouchi et al., 2023). Identification of each steroid peak was performed by comparing the retention time and mass spectra of the authentic standards. Peaks with an S/N ratio of <3 were treated as the detection limit.

Animal care

Animal experiments were approved by the President of Kitasato University through the judgment of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kitasato University (Approval No. 21–058).

Twenty-five male and premenopausal female Sprague–Dawley rats (four-week-old) were purchased from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan). Each experiment was conducted during a separate period. The rats were housed in each cage and placed in an air-conditioned, specific-pathogen-free room maintained at 50% humidity and 22 °C ± 2 °C with lights on between 08:00 and 20:00. Free access to water and a commercial diet for rodents (CE-2; Japan Clea, Tokyo) were provided for a week for acclimation. Thereafter, the male and female rats were divided into the three dietary groups based on body weight to prevent any significant differences: normal diet group (N, n = 6), casein diet group (HC, n = 7), and ML diet group (ML, n = 6). Rats in the normal diet group were fed an AIN-93G diet, those in the casein diet group were fed an AIN-93G diet supplemented with 0.5% CHO and 0.05% cholic acid, and those in the ML diet group were fed an AIN-93G diet supplemented with 0.5% CHO and 0.05% cholic acid, with half of protein material (10%) being replaced with ML. The diet composition is described in Supplementary Table 1.

All rats were fed each respective diet ad libitum for four weeks. Body weight and food intake were monitored twice a week. To determine non-fasting plasma TAG, total CHO, and glucose levels, blood (0.1 mL) was collected in a heparin-coated capillary syringe from the tail vein and centrifuged (6200 ×g, 4 °C, 10 min) using a centrifugal separator (Model 3520, Kubota, Tokyo) to obtain plasma between 10:00 a.m. and 11:30 a.m. once every two weeks. In female rats, the non-fasting plasma samples were collected in the estrous pause phase. After the feeding period, male and female rats at the estrous pause phase were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia between 9:30 a.m. and 11:30 a.m. after an overnight fast. Blood (10 mL) was collected in a heparin-coated syringe from the abdominal aorta and subsequently centrifuged (6200 ×g, 4 °C, 15 min) using a centrifugal separator (Model 3520, Kubota) to obtain plasma for analyses, and the plasma was stored at −80 °C until analysis. The major organs and tissues were removed, weighed, and stored at −80 °C until biochemical analyses. The cecum was removed aseptically, after which its contents were frozen with liquid nitrogen, removed without thawing, weighed, and then stored in a sterilized microtube at −80 °C until microbiota analyses. A portion of the liver was stored in 10% neutral buffered formalin (pH 7.4) for fixation prior to histopathological evaluation. The feces excreted during the final three days of the feeding period were collected and freeze-dried using a freeze-dryer (EYELA type FD-1, Tokyo Rikakikai). Afterward, the dried feces were weighed, powdered using a food mill, and then stored at −30 °C until analysis.

Plasma biochemical parameters

Plasma biochemical analyses including the levels or activities of glucose, TAG, total-CHO, high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-CHO, PL, non-esterified FA (NEFA), total protein, albumin, urea nitrogen, creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and autotaxin (ATX) were measured enzymatically using their respective kits (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals) or using a DRI-CHEM NX 500 device (Fujifilm medical, Tokyo). Plasma levels of insulin, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and 17β-estradiol were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Fujifilm Shibayagi, Gunma, Japan; MBL, Tokyo; Cusabio Biochem, TX, USA; Enzo Life Sciences, NY, USA, respectively).

Plasma lipoprotein fractions

In addition to the enzymatic evaluations, plasma lipoprotein lipid levels were determined by the Immuno-Biological Laboratories (Gunma, Japan) using an online dual enzymatic method for TAG and CHO quantification using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) devise with a gel permeation column (Usui, Hara, Hosaki, & Okazaki, 2002). Each lipoprotein was further divided into several independent fractions based on lipoprotein particle size as previously reported (Okazaki & Yamashita, 2016): two fractions for chylomicron (CM), five fractions for very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), six fractions for LDL, and seven fractions for HDL. Among the LDL fractions (16.7 nm–31.3 nm), very small fractions (16.7 nm–22.9 nm) were defined as small-dense LDL (sd-LDL) fractions.

Liver lipid content

Total lipids in the liver were extracted for two days according to the Folch method (Folch, Lees, & Sloane Stanley, 1957). The TAG, CHO, and choline-containing PL contents in the liver were measured using their respective kits.

FA composition in the plasma and liver

Lipids extracted from the plasma and liver were methyl-esterified using a commercial kit (FA methyl-esterified kit for total lipids, Nacalai Tesque), after which FA methyl ester profiles were determined using a gas chromatography–flame ionization detector (GC-FID) system (GC2014, Shimadzu) equipped with a 60-m capillary column (TC70, GL Science) and pure helium carrier gas as described in our previous study (Ochiai & Komiya, 2021). The GC equipment's injector and detector temperatures were 250 °C and 260 °C, respectively, while the column oven temperature was constant at 180 °C for 40 min before increasing by 20 °C/min from 180 °C to 260 °C. The peak components were identified by comparing each retention time with that of FA methyl esters (GLC-211 and GLC-462, Funakoshi, Tokyo). The sum of the 13 FA percentages [C14:0, C16:0, C16:1cis (n-7), C18:0, C18:1cis (n-9), C18:1isomer, C18:2cis (n-6), C18:3cis (n-3), C18:3isomer, C20:4cis (n-6), C20:5cis (n-3), C22:5cis (n-3), and C22:6cis (n-3)] was estimated to be 100%. Indices of stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD) activity [ratios of C16:1cis (n-7)/C16:0 and C18:1cis (n-9)/C18:0] were calculated in the plasma and liver. The plasma C20:5/C20:4 ratio was also calculated as the indirect index of dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis.

Liver lipogenic enzymatic activity

The liver enzymatic activities of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH) and FA synthase (FASN) were measured as described (Ikeda et al., 2014) with slight modifications. The detailed methodology is presented in Supplementary method file. Each enzyme activity was expressed in units per mg of protein content after protein quantification using a protein assay Bradford reagent (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals).

Hepatic mRNA expression

For the reverse transcriptase quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), total RNA (≤500 ng/μL) was extracted from the frozen liver using an ISOSPIN Cell & Tissue RNA Kit (Nippon Gene, Tokyo). To quantify gene expression levels, mRNA levels were determined for sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs) (Srebf1 and Srebf2), LDL receptor (Ldlr), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (Hmgcr), acyl-CoA CHO acetyltransferase 2 (sterol O-acyltransferase 2) (Soat2), FA synthase (Fasn), microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (Mttp), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (Acaca), stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (Scd1), interleukin 6 receptor (Il6r), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma type 2 (Pparg2), proprotein convertase subtilisin kexin 9 (Pcsk9), farnesoid X receptor (Fxr) (nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H member 4, Nr1h4), small heterodimer partner (Shp) (nuclear receptor subfamily 0 group B member 2, Nr0b2), scavenger receptor class B type 1 (Scarb1), cytochrome P450 7A1 (Cyp7a1), ATP-binding cassette protein A1 (Abca1), estrogen receptor α (Era), and β-actin (Actb) in the liver using a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA). Briefly, cDNA of each sample was extracted using a PrimeScript RT Master Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) from total RNA in the liver (37 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 s, 4 °C for ∞). cDNA was subjected to PCR amplification in a final volume of 20 μL using a Fast SYBR green master mix (Applied biosystems, TX, USA) and each gene-specific primer. These primers were produced by Fasmac Co., Ltd. (Kanagawa, Japan), and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Real-time PCR was performed using a PCR device (StepOnePlus, Applied biosystems, CA, USA) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The ratio between the β-actin content in the standard and samples was defined as the normalization factor, and values for each diet group were quantified, with the N group being set as 1.00 in each male and female study.

Liver histopathological analysis

For the liver histopathological analysis, a portion of the liver after fixation in neutral buffered formalin was embedded in paraffin, sectioned serially at a thickness of 4 μm (3 slices per rat), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Briefly, the liver sections (5 mm cube) were deparaffinized with xylene and ethanol (80%–100%) and then rinsed with water for 5 min. Nuclear staining was conducted in a hematoxylin (Muto Pure Chemical) for 5 min, followed by color removal in water for 15 min. The samples were then stained with an eosin (Muto Pure Chemical) for 4 min, rinsed with 100% ethanol three times each for 1 s to remove the excess staining solution, and treated with xylene for three times before being sealed with a soft mount solution (Kanto Chemical) and a cover glass. Liver section evaluation was performed using a microscope (DFC7000T, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) at × 200. Histopathological findings from the liver tissues were scored using the metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) Clinical Research Network Scoring System (CRNSS), which was formerly known as nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) CRNSS (Kleiner et al., 2005; Omagari et al., 2023). All histopathological examinations were performed by a pathologist (J.N.) who was blinded to the group information.

Cecal analysis

The short-chain FA (SCFA) content in the cecum was determined according to the HPLC technical application datasheet for SCFA analysis using a post-column (Showa Denko K.K., Tokyo) as described previously (Ochiai et al., 2020). The content of acetate, propionate, and a mixture of n- and isobutylate in the cecum was determined using an HPLC system (LC10, Shimadzu) equipped with a column (Shodex RSpak KC-811, 8.0 mm × 300 mm, Showa Denko K.K.). Briefly, 100 mg of cecal content was homogenized in 1 mL of distilled water and then centrifuged at 10,000×g, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was then ultrafiltered (ø 0.45 μm), after which the filtrate was subjected to the measurement of pH and SCFA content. The column temperature was set at 50 °C, the mobile phase was 6 mM perchloric acid solution at a flow of 1.0 mL/min, the detector at the wavelength was set at 430 nm, and 10-fold diluted ST3-R (Showa Denko K.K.) was used as the post-column reagent.

Microbiota analysis was conducted as below. Intestinal bacterial DNA was extracted from the deep-frozen cecal contents from each male and female rat in the HC and ML groups using an ISOSPIN Fecal DNA kit (Nippon Gene). The gene encoding 16S rRNA was amplified via PCR to determine the composition of intestinal bacteria in cecal contents using meta-16S analysis. PCR-based 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing was performed using a 16S (V3 − V4) Metagenomic Library Construction Kit for NGS (Takara Bio). The first PCR products were tagged using a Nextera XT Index Kit with 24 indexes and 96 samples (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The final PCR products were mixed to achieve 10 ng of DNA and sent to Takara Bio for metagenomic analysis.

The profile (phylum and genus levels) of the microbiota in the cecum of each rat was analyzed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2, version 2024.10; https://docs.qiime2.org). Data were then denoised using DADA2 to merge the paired reads generating amplicon sequence variants to ensure sufficient depth to capture most features (Callahan, McMurdie, Rosen, Han, Johnson, & Holmes, 2016). For the taxonomic assignment, the SILVA 16S reference database (version_138) and the classifiers naïve Bayes algorithm were used (Bokulich et al., 2018). An even sampling depth selected after performing the alpha rarefaction module was used to assess the measurements of alpha and beta diversities. Alpha diversity was assessed using Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Observed species indexes. Beta diversity was assessed using Unweighted unifrac distance measures and presented using principal coordinates analysis plots.

Fecal nutrient analysis

Crude fat in the powdered feces was extracted using petroleum ether at 70 °C for 20 h according to the Soxhlet method. In addition, to measure each lipid content, the total lipids in the powdered feces were extracted for two days according to the Folch method (Folch et al., 1957). The TAG, CHO, and choline-containing PL contents in the fecal extract were measured using their respective kits. Total bile acids (TBA) in the feces were extracted using ethyl alcohol (65 °C, 1 h) and then measured using a kit (TBA, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Industries).

Statistical analysis

All evaluations, except for the cecal microbiota analysis and plasma lipoprotein analyses, in the animal experiment were performed at least twice for each sample, with the average then being calculated. Outsourced analyses were performed in accordance with the rules of each outsourcing company. Data were expressed as mean ± standard error (n = 6–7 per group). Statistical differences (p < 0.05) among the three diet groups in each experiment were determined using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Dunnett test (vs. HC group). The statistical analyses above were performed using a commercially available statistical package (BellCurve for Excel Ver. 409; SSRI, Tokyo). Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) in the cecal bacterial composition were separately evaluated for male and female rats using the Kruskal-Wallis test for alpha diversity indices and the permutational multivariate ANOVA for beta diversity indices. These statistical analyses regarding alpha and beta diversity were performed using QIIME2 software as described in Section 2–12.

Results and discussion

The ML supplementation improved the lipid metabolism in male rats, not in female rats, through the following expected mechanisms: favorable alterations of lipoprotein and FA metabolism and intestinal microbiota, suppression of lipogenesis in the liver, and acceleration of excess fat excretion, as discussed in Sections 3–1 and 3–3 and the expected mechanisms are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The improvement effect of ML on lipid metabolism in male rats

ML-induced reduction in plasma lipid levels in the VLDL and LDL fractions

This study was conducted based on the hypothesis that ML supplementation ameliorated dyslipidemia by regulating lipoprotein metabolism. Our previous study (Ochiai et al., 2022) employing standard diet feeding found that 10% ML supplementation fully reduced plasma LDL and sd-LDL lipid levels, which have generally been recognized as the main biomarkers of dyslipidemia and the onset and development of atherosclerosis and CVD, through mechanisms involving the upregulation of LDLR and LDL lipid uptake into the liver and suppression of hepatic lipogenesis. The hypothesis was that ML supplementation improved the uptake of LDL lipids into the liver without lipotoxicity and suppressed the hepatic lipid synthesis.

This study confirmed that dietary CHO could induce excess CHO supply to the plasma and liver and represented an increase in plasma CHO levels in non-HDL fractions and non-HDL ratio by several fold (Table 1). In the HC group, high levels of VLDL lipids and low levels of LDL lipids in plasma and high contents of TAG and CHO in the liver may be associated with the suppression of plasma PCSK9 levels (84%) and hepatic Pcsk9 mRNA expression (80%) (Fig. 1A; Table 1, Table 2, Table 3). Since PCSK9 inactivates the LDLR and transfer of plasma LDL lipids to the liver, enhancing LDLR by inhibiting PCSK9 activity could increase the circulating LDL lipid levels and improving lipid metabolism (Shen, Peng, Nees, Zhao, & Xu, 2013). Previously, the ML supplementation reduced plasma PCSK9 levels (45%) and increased hepatic Ldlr mRNA expression (×1.22) in male rats fed a standard diet (Ochiai et al., 2022). Similarly, our results revealed that ML supplementation further reduced plasma PCSK9 levels, which were already largely reduced by the CHO diet (Table 1). Studies have shown that SREBP2 is mainly involved in the regulation of PCSK9 function (Guo, Xia, Gu, & Zhang, 2020), but ML supplementation did not alter hepatic Srebf2 mRNA expression (Table 3) probably due to the excess liver uptake of dietary CHO and their related suppression of de novo lipogenesis through a feedback system. Therefore, the suppression of Pcsk9 mRNA expression might not be directly caused by regulating SREBP2 alone but might also involve other factors. Indeed, several compounds in ML could affect the binding to PCSK9 and inactivate the interaction between PCSK9 and LDLR, but the details are now being researched. Recently, PCSK9 inhibitors, such as polyphenols, carotenoids, n-3 PUFA, phytosterols, and protein hydrolysates, which are also present in ML and can exist in the blood without further decomposition, are attracting attention as a treatment for dyslipidemia (Lin, Xiao, Tang, Jiang, & Liu, 2018). n-3 PUFA could induce faster clearance of TAG-rich VLDL and LDL lipids and slower production of VLDL particles through the PPARα and SREBP1- and SREBP2-mediated pathways (Adorni, Zimetti, Lupo, Ruscica, & Ferri, 2020).

sd-LDL, which is generated from LDL by hepatic TAG lipase (HTGL), has been particularly recognized as a risk factor for atherosclerosis. Given that sd-LDL particles have a lower affinity to the binding site of LDLR and remain in the blood, these particles are easily susceptible to oxidation. The ML supplementation-induced reduction of plasma CHO levels in the smaller sd-LDL fractions could indicate the rapid clearance of LDL-CHO through the suppression of Pcsk9 mRNA expression (Fig. 1A; Table 3). TAG levels in VLDL fractions, whose TAG levels were dominant (87%), and almost all LDL fractions were also lowered in the ML group. Therefore, our study revealed that ML supplementation strongly regulated lipoprotein metabolism by reducing VLDL and LDL lipid levels.

The remaining plasma LDL lipids, which could not be transported into the liver through LDLR, can be transported by non-LDLR scavenger receptor family type B1 (SR-B1) present in macrophages. Stimulation of HDL-CHO uptake into the liver by upregulating SR-B1 can reverse CHO transport and reduce the risks of dyslipidemia (Palmisano et al., 2017). However, since hepatic Scarb1 mRNA expression was not enhanced in the ML group, ML supplementation could not effectively transport HDL-CHO into the liver, which might explain the low plasma HDL lipid levels in the ML group (Fig. 1A; Table 3). Their regulatory mechanisms are currently being investigated.

ML-induced reduction of plasma lipid levels in the CM fraction

Our previous study showed that ML supplementation reduced the plasma lipid levels in the CM fractions in the fasted state, wherein CM lipid levels are expected to be low (Ochiai et al., 2022). Dietary CHO is involved in the formation of bile acid (BA) micelles, leading to solubilization. Thereafter, soluble CHO is absorbed from the microvilli on the luminal side of the small intestinal epithelial cells, with estimates showing that humans are able to absorb around 50% of the total CHO in the diet. Unabsorbed CHO would ultimately be excreted as feces. The absorbed CHO is transported to the CM and secreted to the lymph, but a part of the absorbed CHO is again excreted into the small intestinal epithelial cells. Considering the dietary CHO absorption mechanisms, the lower CM lipid levels in the ML group could be caused by the following reasons: inhibition of CHO solubility in BA micelles, enhancement of the affinity of CHO to BA micelles, suppression of CM secretion from the small intestine, and upregulation of the CM remnant receptor (CMRR) (Fig. 1A). Given that ML-induced activation of LDLR can continuously transport lipids in LDL particles into the liver, lipid accumulation in the liver with low lipotoxicity could possibly occur. Therefore, ML may also inhibit lipid solubility and delay the transport of CM lipids into the liver through the CMRR. If ML supplementation suppressed the CMRR function, it is possible that the inhibition of lipid solubility in the BA micelles, which are probably induced by n-3 PUFA, PL, and phytosterols in ML, suppressed CM lipid levels. Thus, ML supplementation could reduce plasma CM lipid levels as well as VLDL, total LDL, and sd-LDL lipids (Fig. 1A). Moreover, in the fed state, wherein CM lipid levels are considered higher, plasma levels of TAG and total CHO were suppressed in the ML group at the 2nd and 4th week of the treatment period (Supplementary Table 3). ML supplementation did not significantly alter the plasma indices related to liver function and inflammation, such as AST, ALT, and IL-6 (Table 1). Additionally, ML supplementation also suppressed hepatic Il6r mRNA expression by 53% (Table 3). Thus, the lipids transported into the liver are contemporary and do not induce inflammation. A review (D'Antonio et al., 2023) also found that some edible insects secreted anti-inflammatory cytokines and antioxidant properties. Possible compounds in ML that affect the intestinal lipid absorption, CM secretion, and CM uptake into the liver include n-3 PUFA, phytosterols, PL, and chitin. A discussion regarding these compounds has been provided in Section 3–1-4.

Humans with hypertriglyceridemia have increased amounts of CM and VLDL remnants that remain in the blood and are not effectively metabolized. The remaining remnants are taken up by macrophages, and CHO are accumulated in the arteriosclerotic plaque, leading to an increase in the plasma levels of sd-LDL lipids, which have a low affinity for LDLR in the hepatocyte. sd-LDL is even more susceptible to denaturation and is not effectively metabolized by the normal metabolic system. We hypothesized that ML supplementation induced the amelioration of plasma biomarkers related to atherosclerosis, but unexpectedly it did not ameliorate the plasma ratio of C20:5 (n-3) to C20:4 (n-6) as a FA-related atherosclerosis index (Fig. 2Z; Supplementary Table 4). FA C20:4 (n-6), a pro-inflammatory FA, is enzymatically converted from C18:2 (n-6) was slightly decreased in the ML group; however, no difference in the index was observed between the HC and ML groups, which was consistent with the limited change in plasma IL-6 levels (Table 1).

ML-induced alteration of lipid accumulation in the liver

Our results have clarified that ML supplementation reduced plasma lipid levels and increased TAG and CHO accumulation in the liver but decreased de novo hepatic lipogenesis and observed little histopathological alterations in male rats (Table 1, Table 2; Figs. 1A, 2X, 2Y). These findings could be linked to the improvement in onset and development of fatty liver and MASLD.

Regarding lipogenesis-related hepatic mRNA expressions, the CHO diet upregulated the mRNA expression of Pparg2 and Scd1 but downregulated the mRNA expression of Srebf2, Ldlr, Pcsk9, and Hmgcr (Table 3). Cellular CHO levels in the liver are strictly controlled by a feedback system. The dietary CHO supply satisfied the hepatic CHO demand and downregulated mRNA expression involved in de novo hepatic CHO synthesis, such as Srebf2 and Hmgcr, and the hepatic CHO uptake, such as Ldlr and Pcsk9 (Table 3). In such a condition with an excess CHO supply, ML supplementation significantly or slightly reduced the lipogenesis-related mRNA expression of Hmgcr (×0.65), Acaca (×0.68), Scd1 (×0.33), Mttp (×0.78), Soat2 (×0.72), and Pcsk9 (×0.76) (Table 3). Suppression of these mRNA expressions by ML supplementation has been closely linked to a decrease in the lipogenic enzymatic activities of G6PDH and FASN, which are downstream transcription factors of SREBP1. The ML-induced downregulation of hepatic lipogenesis indicated that ML did not directly suppress Srebf1 mRNA expression but suppressed the activity of downstream enzymes (Table 3; Fig. 2Y). The current results are somewhat different from those of our previous study (Ochiai et al., 2022) because the exogenous CHO supply had different effects on the mRNA expression related to lipid synthesis. Previously, ML supplementation suppressed the protein expression of SREBP1 and the mRNA expression of Srebf1, Srebf2, Mttp, and Scd1 in rats fed with a CHO-free standard diet (Ochiai et al., 2022). In particular, the current study found that ML supplementation promoted a 35% downregulation in the mRNA expression of Hmgcr, which is a key factor for de novo hepatic CHO synthesis that is regulated by SREBP2 (Table 3). Statins, which are CHO-lowering agents, strongly inhibits HMG-CoA reductase activity and enhances LDLR function, leading to a reduction in hepatic CHO content and plasma LDL lipid levels. These findings are also expected when fish oil containing eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) is supplemented in rats (Choi, Goto, Ikeda, & Sugano, 1989). Given that n-3 PUFA are present in ML mainly in the form of TAG, DAG, MAG, several species of GPL, and phytosterol esters (Table 4 and Supplementary Table 5), the determination of active compounds in ML that inhibit HMG-CoA reductase activity can contribute toward reducing the use of statins with side effects.

Possible compounds for improving lipoprotein metabolism

Representatively, TAG and PL with ALA, several species of phytosterols, and chitin in ML can be possible compounds for ameliorating dyslipidemia and liver diseases according to a review (Lin et al., 2018). Comprehensive lipid analysis (Table 4; Supplementary Table 5) detected 454 molecular species in total and showed that PL, including sphingomyelin (15.5%), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE, 0.760%), lysophosphatidylcholine (0.563%), phosphatidylcholine (PC, 0.510%), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE, 0.320%), and others (less than 0.05%), were present at approximately 20% of the total lipids in ML, following glycoglycerolipid and fatty acyl groups, such as TAG (59.7%), diacylglycerol (DAG) (9.26%), monoacylglycerol (MAG) (1.62%), and FA (5.42%). Additionally, more than approximately half of TAG, DAG, MAG, and the major PL, such as PC (49.1%), PE (60.5%), LPE (51.4%), phosphatidylinositol (75.9%), were bound with ALA in each molecule (Table 4). As described in Supplementary Table 5, ALA was present in ML in the form of TAG (C16:0_C18:0_C18:3 and C16:0_C16:0_C18:3) at 6% in total lipids and DAG (C16:0_C18:3; C18:3_C18:3) at 1–2%, CHO ester at 1.5%, and several PL species at <0.4%. In particular, n-3 PUFA bound with PL forms have some functional advantages over TAG or FA ester forms. When explaining the characteristics of EPA/DHA, which are biosynthesized from ALA, the DHA-PL form was more stable to lipid oxidation than the DHA-TAG and DHA ethyl ester forms in rats (Song & Miyazawa, 2001). Nutritionally, the PL mixtures containing DHA/EPA increased EPA and DHA levels in the livers of rats while suppressing lipogenesis and TAG accumulation due to their excellent absorption and bioavailability (Chen et al., 2024; Rossmeisl et al., 2012). Moreover, a human study has shown that PL preferentially assimilates within HDL fraction (Sung et al., 2019). Our previous results showed that ML supplementation presented a high accumulation of ALA and EPA in the plasma and liver of rats (Ochiai et al., 2022). These results indicated that ALA in ML was absorbed and accumulated and that EPA and DPA were synthesized, which induced the suppression of ARA production. Thus, ML supplementation may suppress inflammation-related indices in rats fed with a CHO diet as similarly observed in rats fed with a standard diet (Ochiai et al., 2022).

The quantitative observation of several species of phytosterols in ML is characteristic as phytosterols were thought to be mainly found in plants. The abundance of several species of phytosterols in ML (Table 4, Table 5; Supplementary Table 5) are thought to be not only the bioaccumulation of phytosterols contained in the feed, mainly grass family plants, but also the de novo synthesis of phytosterols in ML. No research has been conducted on the phytosterol contents in the feed given to ML during the rearing stage. Several species of phytosterols are abundant and biosynthesized in some insects; major phytosterols, such as campesterol, β-sitosterol, and stigmasterol, are biosynthesized from squalene through a different pathway (Mudalungu et al., 2023). Squalene, which was enzymatically converted from acetyl-CoA by HMG-CoA reductase, was catalyzed to 2,3-oxidosqualene, which was then cyclized to generate the steroid backbone structure as represented in lanosterol. Therefore, our GC/MS data (Table 5) showed that high amounts of β-sitosterol, campesterol, and stigmasterol were considered internal synthetic products in insects, albeit not quite reaching the level of CHO. These three phytosterols are quickly biosynthesized from acetyl-CoA and accumulated in insects. The abundance of these phytosterols in ML can improve lipid metabolism. A review (Ikeda, 2015) found that phytosterols are generally less absorptive, but their supplementation reduces plasma CHO levels by inhibiting intestinal CHO absorption. A 24-h lymph collection evaluation in rats revealed decreased lymph collection rates of three phytosterols, namely campesterol (15%), β-sitosterol (≤3%), and stigmasterol (≤3%), compared to CHO (approximately 40%). These results can be mainly attributed to the low solubility of phytosterols in BA micelles and their resistance to binding with the intestinal CHO absorption receptor (Ikeda, 2015). Our results have shown that supplementation of phytosterols in ML increased the fecal lipid excretion in rats regardless of sex (Table 2) through an expected mechanism as described in Section 3–3. The total amount of phytosterols in lipids from ML was calculated to be 3.22 mg/g total lipids (Table 5). Considering a crude fat content of 4.0% in the ML powder, only approximately 1.3 mg of phytosterols were calculated to be contained in 100 g of ML. An improving effect of β-sitosterol (20 mg/kg/day p.o.) on lipid metabolism in rats, a dose equivalent to approximately 20 mg in 100 g of ML (Abo-Zaid, Moawed, Ismail, & Farrag, 2023). Therefore, considering that the total amount of phytosterols in the ML diet was quite insufficient for improving lipid metabolism, several compounds in ML could complementarily improve lipid metabolism in male rats.

ML-induced alteration of the cecal microbiota

Although a limited number of studies have explored the potential prebiotic effect of whole insects, the improving effect of ML supplementation on lipid metabolism could be related to the intestinal microbiota. As shown in Fig. 3, the major four phylum, indicating Firmicutes, Actinobacteruota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Bacteroidota, accounted for more than 90% of the total in the cecum of rats. ML supplementation increased the relative abundance of genus Akkermansia in male rats without cecal hypertrophy (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 6). Also, ML supplementation induced the increase in the values of observed species and Chao1 and little alterations of the values of Simpson and Shannon, which were alpha diversity indices, represented high abundance of bacterial species, but on the other hand, high proportion of certain phylum, e.g. Firmicutes, meant a low evenness of bacterial species (Fig. 3, male). Additionally, beta diversity, indicating the relationship of the microbial species between individuals of the same diet group were relatively close (Fig. 3). Akkermansia muciniphila, which belongs to phylum Verrucomicrobiota, is a human intestinal commensal bacterium that accounts for 3%–5% of the microbiota in humans (Derrien, Vaughan, Plugge, & de Vos, 2004). A. muciniphila has been widely regarded as one of the functional probiotics and an indicator of the effectiveness for the treatment of metabolic dysfunctions. Dietary antioxidants can modify the intestinal microbiota by scavenging oxygen radicals (Roopchand et al., 2015). Given that the ML contained antioxidant polyphenols (D'Antonio et al., 2023), ML may contribute to the increase in the presence of genus Akkermansia. The increase in the presence ratio of genus Akkermansia in the male ML group can be complementarily induced by several functional compounds in ML.

Chitin and resistant proteins in edible insects could alter the intestinal microbiota and suppress the digestion and absorption of nutrients. The amount of chitin ingested through the ML diet was equivalent to 1.1%. Altering intestinal microbiota induced by these compounds could help us understand these interactions. Decreases in several fecal bacteria, including genus Lactobacillus, and fecal SCFA contents were observed after the consumption of whole edible cricket powder for 14 days in healthy subjects (Stull et al., 2018; Stull & Weir, 2023). Our results also found that the ML diet slightly decreased the SCFA content (in μmol/g; 19% on average) without alterations of the content and pH value of cecum in male rats (Fig. 3).

As n-3 PUFA have been linked to intestinal health and prebiotic activity, oil types in diet can alter the relative expression of genes related to lipid metabolism and alter the cecal microbiota, such as an increase in the proportion of Akkermansia, in rats (Hosomi et al., 2019). Supplementation of 0.24% n-3 PUFA in the ML diet could provide high amounts of ALA, EPA, and DPA in plasma and liver and increase the production of anti-inflammatory compounds including SCFA by the intestinal microbiota, leading to reduced inflammation and improved overall intestinal environment. Notably, hepatic Il6r mRNA expression was suppressed at 53% by ML supplementation without alterations in the plasma IL-6 levels and reduction of ARA levels in the plasma and liver were observed (Table 1; Supplementary Tables 4 and 7).

Other functional compounds in ML can positively or negatively impact the microbiota profile, but ML whole supplementation may be beneficial for improving not only liver lipid metabolism but also the intestinal microbiota profile.

Little improvement effect of ML on lipid metabolism in female rats and their sex-differences

We hypothesized that the effects of ML supplementation on improving lipid metabolism can be influenced by the female hormones and sex-differences. Our current results indicated that ML supplementation increased fat accumulation in the adipose tissues around the female-specific reproductive tissues, while did not fully reduce hepatic lipid accumulation and plasma lipid levels of the VLDL and LDL fractions in female rats whose estrogen levels were not altered (Figs. 1B and 2X; Table 1, Table 2; Supplementary Table 6). Interestingly, in female rats, ML supplementation did not further reduce plasma TAG levels, which were remarkably lowered by the CHO diet. These results were not consistent with our results for male rats (Table 1). Similarly, ML supplementation slightly or significantly increased the liver lipid contents in female rats (Table 2). This may be related to the findings that liver disorder indices (AST, ALT, and ATX) in plasma, histopathological observations, such as steatosis and lobular inflammation, and n-3 PUFA compositions in plasma and liver were somewhat worsened by ML supplementation (Table 1; Fig. 2X; Supplementary Tables 4 and 7). Additionally, plasma total CHO levels in female rats were specifically increased by ML supplementation without alterations in HDL fraction levels (Table 1). Our results of CHO levels in each lipoprotein fraction indicated that the ML-induced increase in the VLDL fraction directly influenced the increase in the total CHO levels in female rats (Fig. 1B). The CHO diet also notably increased CHO levels in the VLDL fraction by approximately 17 times regardless of ML supplementation, whereas male rats showed only a 5-fold increase in such ratios (Fig. 1). The dietary CHO-induced increase in plasma VLDL lipid levels in female rats could be attributed to enhancement of VLDL secretion from the liver and insufficient capacity of VLDL clearance, such as insufficient efficiency in the conversion from VLDL to LDL and/or decline of the regulatory ability of LDLR. Similarly, the further increase in the plasma VLDL fraction levels caused by ML supplementation might be explained by the exacerbation of insufficient clearance of VLDL-CHO and/or the enhanced VLDL secretion into the plasma although the involved ML-derived components and mechanisms are investigated in progress.

These findings might be supplemented with explanations based on alterations in gene expression related to lipid metabolism in female rats. It was reported that the scores of liver genes involved in lipid metabolism varied with the 4-day estrous cycle in an ERα-dependent manner in a mouse study (Villa et al., 2012), demonstrating a close relationship between liver lipid metabolism and reproductive needs. Estrogens are important for improving lipid metabolism and decreasing the risk of CVD. Enhancing estrogen signaling, induced by binding to ERα, can favorably alter lipid metabolism; however, the effects of endogenous estrogens are complicated and influenced by the interaction with numerous hormones and other receptors to influence lipid metabolism.

Given that our results were obtained from premenopausal female rats, estrogen function should be taken into consideration. It should be noted that under conditions where estrogen functions normally, the estrogen function might fail to induce excess lipid accumulation and a severe MAS score in the livers of premenopausal female rats in HC group. TAG and PL contents in the liver of female rats were notably lower than those in male rats (Table 2). Estrogen signaling may contribute to sex-differences in the onset and development of dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis by promoting reverse CHO transport in the liver. Reverse CHO transport is a process by which CHO is removed from peripheral tissues culminating in the delivery of CHO to the liver for conversion to BA, which is ultimately delivered to the feces. Although the estrogen function during the reverse CHO transport pathway has not yet been comprehensively clarified, estrogen would enhance the excretion of CHO in macrophages (Palmisano et al., 2017). The reverse CHO transport system is activated to a much greater extent through the ERα particularly in female mice. We hypothesized that the improvement of lipid metabolism following ML supplementation may be influenced by estrogen, but our results have shown that the plasma 17β-estradiol levels at an estrous pause phase and fecal lipid excretion of female rats were not altered by ML supplementation (Table 1, Table 2). These findings were consistent with the fact that the CHO excretion capacity was not associated with plasma estrogen levels in premenopausal females (Badeau et al., 2013). On the other hand, at the estrous phase, when plasma estrogen levels are high, the hepatic estrogen signaling pathway has been shown to regulate hepatic CHO uptake and the ability of macrophages to release HDL, which are mediated through the ERα (Della Torre et al., 2016). It was also suggested that enhancing hepatic estrogen signaling through the ERα can suppress de novo lipogenesis, which enhances VLDL secretion from the liver. However, similar to plasma 17β-estradiol levels, ML supplementation showed little alterations in mRNA expression related to lipogenesis, such as Fasn, Mttp, Acaca, and Scd1 (Table 3). Although the CHO diet suppressed hepatic Era mRNA expression by approximately 50% in female rats, ML supplementation slightly (×1.23) recovered the Era mRNA expression and enhanced the mRNA expression of Shp (×1.44), which was a nuclear receptor and target gene of ERα that is regulated by FXR (Table 3). This slight increase in Fxr mRNA expression (×1.21) indicated the increase HDL uptake into the liver through SR-B1, leading to the possibility of plasma LDL lipid clearance in female rats. However, these alterations in the mRNA expression induced by ML supplementation were not significant.

Therefore, our results from premenopausal female rats have indicated that ML supplementation did not further enhance estrogen-like function or disrupt hormone balance, supporting the European Food Safety Authority's approval of ML powder as almost completely safe (EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA), et al., 2021). However, the degree to which estrogen signaling through ERα, ERβ, and other estrogen receptors contribute to lipid metabolism have not been well defined (Palmisano et al., 2017). Furthermore, to clarify the effects of ML supplementation on estrogen signaling, additional studies using rats in the postmenopausal stage or ovariectomy models are necessary.

The ML diet enhances lipid extraction in the feces in rats

It has been hypothesized that some components in ML could inhibit the digestion and absorption of fat and could further enhance the fat excretion in rats regardless of the sex. As expected, our results indicated that ML supplementation enhanced fecal fat excretion along with an increase in the amount of feces. The total excretion of TAG and PL was increased by ML supplementation, but the fecal nutrient composition was not different in either male or female rat study (Table 2). On the other hand, the amounts and rate of fecal TBA excretion were increased in male and female rats fed the CHO diet. BA in the gut-small intestines is closely associated with the digestion and absorption of dietary fat. BA is synthesized from CHO in the liver, secreted to the intestinal tract, and reabsorbed in the small intestine. The reabsorbed BA is further transferred to the liver and reutilized. Inhibition of intestinal BA reabsorption promotes the conversion of CHO to BA in the liver and lowers blood CHO levels. A part of the BA is deconjugated by the microbiota and moved to the large intestine and excreted as feces.

These enterohepatic circulations of BA are regulated by the nuclear receptor FXR. Activation of FXR in accordance with the BA content in the liver can lead to fecal BA excretion and liver BA uptake. FXR activation can induce SHP expression, which suppresses the CYP7A1 function. The BA-induced FXR activation led to SHP expression, which suppressed lipogenesis through the reduction of SREBP1 and lipogenic enzymatic activity (Watanabe et al., 2004). CYP7A1, a limiting enzyme producing BA from CHO and expressed in hepatocyte, is generally upregulated when the CHO content in the liver is increased. Upregulation of CYP7A1 is meant to be an increment in the excretion of CHO from hepatocytes. In male and female rats, the hepatic Cyp7a1 mRNA expression was upregulated 8.4 and 3.1 times, respectively, by ML supplementation (Table 3). Increases in the fecal TBA excretion and the low plasma TBA levels indicated that the intestinal BA reabsorption was suppressed by ML supplementation (Table 1, Table 2). The hepatic mRNA expression of Fxr and Shp, which are regulators of Cyp7a1, was downregulated by the CHO diet but regulated by ML supplementation in a different way between male and female rats (Table 3). Detailed mechanisms are not known, but the slight downregulation of hepatic mRNA expression of Fxr and Shp in male rats might be due to the marked upregulation of Cyp7a1 mRNA through a feedback system.

The increase in TAG excretion along with the increased fecal amount by ML supplementation can be explained by the inhibition of pancreatic lipase activity and lipid absorption induced by several components in ML (Table 2). A small increase in the fecal CHO excretion by ML supplementation in the male rats may be associated with the BA metabolism in the small intestine and liver. In male rats, CHO in the diet was effectively utilized for micelle formation and transported to the liver. CHO in the liver can be more effectively converted to BA by the CYP7A1 function as Cyp7a1 mRNA was more activated, and excess BA is returned to the small intestine and eventually excreted as feces. In fact, the BA ratio in the feces of male rats was approximately three times higher than that in the feces of female rats (Table 2). Therefore, although ML supplementation-induced these alterations, particularly in male rats, can be associated with the increment of fecal Akkermansia genus occupancy, as discussed in Section 3–1-5, and improvement effect of lipid metabolism, their regulatory mechanisms and intestinal functional factors remain an important topic for future research.

Conclusion

ML supplementation improved lipoprotein metabolism by lowering the plasma lipid levels of CM, VLDL, and LDL fractions in male rats fed a CHO diet. In parallel, hepatic lipogenesis was found to be suppressed despite the increased lipid content in the liver by enhancing LDL lipid uptake to the liver through the LDLR (Supplementary Fig. 1). Some functional compounds, such as n-3 ALA containing PL, phytosterols, and chitin, and favorable alteration of the intestinal microbiota can be expected to improve lipid metabolism. These benefits on the lipid metabolism exhibited by ML supplementation are male rats-specific, which indicates that it may be associated with estrogen signaling. However, estrogen-related mechanisms remain elucidated. An increase in the fecal fat excretion following ML supplementation was also observed in rats regardless of sex, but the related gene expression changes were more pronounced in male rats, which contributes to improved lipid metabolism. ML supplementation or consumption of its functional compounds can be a promising therapeutic strategy for dyslipidemia.
