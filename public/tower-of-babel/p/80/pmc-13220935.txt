Refining the Parkinson’s Disease Risk Estimator for Decline In Cognition Tool (PREDICT)
Authors: Tara C. Carlisle, Lauren B. Gunn-Sandell, William L. Lippitt, Shuai Zhu, Luis D. Medina, Samantha K. Holden, Amy W. Amara, Brianne M. Bettcher, Nichole E. Carlson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Although cognitive decline in Parkinson disease (PD) is common, there are currently no easily accessible clinical tools to predict the risk of cognitive decline in PD.Objective:To refine a tool to predict cognitive decline in PD and identify potentially modifiable factors.Methods:We used neuropsychological scores from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative de novo PD cohort to calculate a composite cognitive score (CCS) for each participant. Participants with a decrease of ≥0.5 SD in CCS between baseline and Year 4 were considered to have cognitive decline (PD-Decline), as predicted by conditional linear mixed modeling to account for nonignorable dropouts. We identified risk factors for PD-Decline using logistic regression and developed the PD Risk Estimator for Decline In Cognition Tool (PREDICT) scoring system using the regression β coefficients. The selected risk factors and the discriminative ability of the PREDICT scoring system were evaluated using bootstrapped samples.Results:Participants with excessive daytime sleepiness (odds ratio [OR]=2.8; 95% CI [1.3, 6.0]), moderate-severe motor symptoms (OR=2.6; 95% CI [1.2, 5.4]), and fewer years of formal education (OR=2.2; 95% CI [1.1, 4.1]) had significantly increased odds of being categorized as PD-decline. Possible PREDICT scores ranged from 0 to 3.5. The average bootstrap sensitivity and specificity were 59.9% and 43.5%, respectively.Conclusion:We identified excessive daytime sleepiness and moderate-severe motor symptoms as potentially modifiable factors. In the future, PREDICT may offer wider opportunities for personalized risk assessment and potential risk reduction.

Parkinson’s disease Risk Estimator for Decline In Cognition Tool

Cognitive decline in Parkinson disease (PD) is common (Batzu et al, 2024). Approximately 10–20% of individuals newly diagnosed with PD already have measurable cognitive impairment. Nearly half of individuals diagnosed with PD who have normal cognition develop cognitive impairment within 6 years, and new-onset mild cognitive impairment progresses to dementia within 5 years. Strikingly, over 80% of individuals with PD develop dementia over time. However, cognitive trajectories in PD are heterogeneous (Fereshtehnejad and Postuma, 2017). There is significant interest in reliably identifying subclasses of individuals with PD with differential cognitive trajectories to aid in personalized conversations about the prognosis and risk of cognitive decline.

Multiple predictor tools have been developed with the goal of identifying individuals with PD at greatest risk of more rapid cognitive decline. Many of these tools rely on tests that are often limited to tertiary/quaternary care centers or research settings, such as specialized examination procedures (Schrag et al, 2017), biomarkers from cerebrospinal fluid (Gramotnev et al, 2019; Schrag et al, 2017; Yousaf et al, 2019), advanced imaging modalities (Schrag et al, 2017; Yousaf et al, 2019), genetic testing (Gramotnev et al, 2019; Liu et al, 2017), or neuropsychological testing beyond typical screening tools (Hogue et al, 2018; Wilson et al, 2020). However, most individuals with PD are not under the care of movement disorder specialists (Pearson et al, 2023) and have more limited access to the complex testing required for these risk assessment tools. An office-based tool using demographic and clinical history has wider potential applicability (Dawson et al, 2018). In addition, many of the risk factors identified in prior tools are not currently modifiable and/or treatable and may not directly guide clinical practice in reducing the risk of cognitive decline in PD.

Previously, we developed the preliminary Parkinson’s disease Risk Estimator for Decline In Cognition Tool (pPREDICT) based on easily obtained demographic and clinical data (Carlisle et al, 2023). This initial pragmatic tool includes 14 dichotomous risk factors identified from the literature with a focus on potentially modifiable and/or treatable risk factors (Carlisle et al, 2023). Male sex, older age, excessive daytime sleepiness, and moderate-severe motor symptoms all significantly contributed to the model (Carlisle et al, 2023). However, pPREDICT has a modest area under the curve (AUC) and the inclusion of so many risk factors makes the tool cumbersome.

The goal of the current study was to develop a predictor tool that estimates the risk of cognitive decline in PD using data that is easily clinically available with a focus on identifying modifiable and/or treatable risk factors for personalized risk assessment and reduction. Although there are multiple potential contributors to cognitive decline in PD, we hypothesized that the initial pPREDICT could be refined to include only the risk factors that contribute the most. This would improve clinical applicability with fewer inputs required from administering clinicians. In addition, longitudinal clinical trials have significant retention challenges and cognitive decline is a known contributor to early trial exit (Chatfield et al, 2005), so a secondary goal was to account for the nonignorable bias present with standard analytic approaches. Here, we present the refined Parkinson’s disease Risk Estimator for Decline In Cognition Tool (PREDICT).

METHODS

Participants and Data

We obtained the data used in the preparation of this article from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) database (www.ppmi-info.org/access-data-specimens/download-data; RRID: SCR_006431) on September 9, 2019. We obtained updated neuropsychological data on June 7, 2021, from the same source. Up-to-date information regarding the PPMI study and database can be found at www.ppmi-info.org. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for participation and methods for the PPMI study protocols have been previously published in detail (Marek et al, 2011). The current study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (exempt protocol #20-3173).

We evaluated individuals from the PPMI de novo PD cohort (ie, newly diagnosed with PD) for inclusion in the current study (N = 450). We excluded individuals without available dopamine transporter imaging or scans without evidence of dopaminergic deficits. We also excluded three individuals with PPMI enrollment statuses of “excluded” (n =2) or “declined” (n = 1) and 29 individuals who did not have baseline neuropsychological testing scores (6.4%). Therefore, we included 418 participants in the final analysis for the current study, constituting 92.9% of the initial PPMI de novo PD cohort.

Composite Cognitive Scores

For PPMI, neuropsychological testing data were recorded at baseline and at each follow-up study visit (Years 1–4). We included five neuropsychological measures spanning different cognitive domains in our analysis, including: (a) the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale—IV Letter-Number Sequencing task (Wechsler, 1987) to evaluate attention and working memory, (b) the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (Smith, 1973) for executive function, (c) a category/semantic (animal) fluency task (Straus et al, 2006) for language, (d) the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test Delayed Recall task (Brandt, 1991) for memory, and (e) Benton’s Judgment of Line Orientation (Benton et al, 1978) for visuospatial function. For Benton’s Judgment of Line Orientation, the Symbol Digit Modalities test, and the category/semantic (animal) fluency task, we used normative data from testing manuals (Benton et al, 1978; Smith, 1973) or from previous studies (Tombaugh et al, 1999) to convert raw scores to z scores. For the Delayed Recall task and the Letter-Number Sequencing task, we converted the PPMI-derived t-scores and scaled scores, respectively, to z scores. We calculated composite cognitive scores (CCSs)—which are used in PD cognitive research (Wood et al, 2021)—as a mean of the five z scores for the baseline and subsequent yearly study visits.

Risk Factors

We obtained data relevant to potential risk factors from the PPMI database, including age, sex, race and ethnicity (here considered as proxies for the social constructs that may influence cognitive scores and possible genetic contributions) (Section 6, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144), years of education, body mass index, and history of vascular risk factors. Vascular risk factors included coronary artery disease, ischemic stroke and/or transient ischemic attack, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes. Additional clinical measures drawn from the PPMI included the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, REM Sleep Behavior Disorder Questionnaire, Movement Disorder Society—Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS)—Part III (motor section), Geriatric Depression Scale—Short Form, State-Trait Anxiety Inventory—State, Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), and Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE).

We calculated disease duration as the difference between the enrollment date for each participant and their PD diagnosis date. Other clinical factors considered as potential confounders in the analysis included levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) (Section 4, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144) and a self-reported diagnosis of sleep apnea (Section 7, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). We established the final list of risk factors and their associated dichotomous cutoff values based on a previous literature search (Figure 1) (Carlisle et al, 2023).

Study Completion

We classified participants as having completed the study if neuropsychological data were available for their Year 4 visit, or if Year 4 data were missing but later study visit data were available. We classified participants as not having completed the study if no neuropsychological data were available at or past the Year 4 study visit, even if they were otherwise eligible according to the study enrollment timeline.

Cognitive Trajectories

To distinguish between participants who maintained cognitive function and those with cognitive decline, we computed the change in CCS between baseline and Year 4 using predicted CCS values from a conditional linear mixed model (CLMM) (Moore et al, 2017). We defined participants with a decline in cognition of ≥0.5 SD as having maintained cognitive function (PD-Maintain). We defined those with a decline in cognition of ≥0.5 SD as having cognitive decline (PD-Decline). We used predicted values rather than measured scores to allow participants in the PD-Incomplete group to be included. The Movement Disorder Society Task Force Guidelines for diagnosing PD with mild cognitive impairment stipulates 1–2 SDs below norms on formal neuropsychological testing, but indicates there can also be a significant decline on serial neuropsychological testing (Litvan et al, 2012). Given that PPMI is a highly educated cohort, decline over a 4-year period was defined as ≥0.5 SD change in CCS from baseline to capture even subtle cognitive decline.

Statistical Analysis

We fitted a CLMM with random intercept and slope to account for the potential influence of nonignorable dropout by including a last study visit (or dropout time) varying slope through an interaction term with last study visit time and study visit year (Moore et al, 2017). The CLMM was fitted with CCS as the dependent variable from baseline to Year 4 study visit (Section 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). We determined the estimated slope for the average dropout time, and the slope is the population average of CCS change accounting for dropout time. We performed a bootstrap of the standard errors using simple random sampling with replacement for the marginal slope with 1000 replicates to obtain CIs. We then generated individual level predicted CCS values using empirical best linear unbiased prediction, which were carried into subsequent analysis steps as defined above. We did not consider risk factors in this first step of analysis, but they were included in the second stage using logistic regression.

We calculated descriptive statistics by cognitive trajectory (ie, PD-Maintain vs PD-Decline) and study completion (ie, participants who completed the study vs those who did not). Risk factors were characterized using proportions. We tested the differences between groups using Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate, or a two-sample t-test with unequal variances for all CCS values with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

We used logistic regression to identify the associations between potential risk factors and cognitive decline (Table 1). Two different models were fitted with and without the low physical activity risk factor (ie, PASE ≤90); however, the PASE-containing model was ultimately not included due to substantial nonignorable missingness (Section 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). We used backwards elimination with a cutoff of p < 0.125 to select covariates for the final model. Participants with missing outcomes and/or covariates were excluded from modeling.

Parameter estimates for both crude models and the final adjusted model were exponentiated and presented as crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI. To understand the impact of each modifiable risk factor on the absolute probability of being classified as PD-Decline, we computed the probability of decline in the presence or absence of zero, one, or two modifiable risk factors. We then adjusted for educational attainment (nonmodifiable) and subtracted the two probabilities. We used the mean adjusted predicted probability of excessive daytime sleepiness and moderate-severe motor symptoms when only one modifiable risk factor was present. To investigate the potential confounding effect of PD-specific medication use on our findings, we performed an extensive simulation sensitivity analysis for LEDD (Section 4, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144).

PREDICT Scoring Procedure

We adapted the scoring approach for PREDICT from previously reported methods (Kivipelto et al, 2006). We assigned risk scores to each of the three risk factors significantly associated with PD-Decline using β coefficients from the final logistic regression modeling. The smallest β coefficient in absolute value was 0.778. Therefore, all coefficients were standardized by multiplying by (0.778)−1 = 1.285 to convert the smallest β coefficient to one. We then rounded the standardized points to the nearest 0.5 to produce an easily calculated risk score. We plotted the receiver operating characteristic curve and calculated the naïve AUC as a measure of goodness of fit, also using the final logistic regression model.

Bootstrap Resampling

We subsequently performed two bootstrapping analyses. The first was used to estimate a bootstrap-corrected AUC for the PREDICT scoring built from the final logistic regression model. We drew 1000 sample replicates from the original sample with replacement, resulting in subsamples of equal size (n = 418). For this analysis, the selected features for the logistic regression model were fixed to be the same as those in the final logistic regression model.

For the second analysis, we drew 50,000 sample replicates from the original sample, resulting in subsamples of equal size (n = 418), to confirm the selected risk factors and the PREDICT scoring procedure (ie, procedural bootstrap) (Section 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). Within each replicate, we considered the individuals who were not selected to be a corresponding “out-of-bag” sample. Similar to our analyses of the PREDICT score, we fitted a logistic regression for each of the bootstrap sample replicates modeling the outcome of PD-Decline. We applied stepwise selection with backward elimination of the 13 potential risk factors (excluding PASE) to exclude risk factors under a cutoff of p ≥ 0.125. No risk factors were fixed in these models and intercept-only models were possible. We determined a 70% threshold as significant for the selection frequency for a risk factor across the modeling replicates a priori.

As for the PREDICT scores, for each replicate’s respective model, we converted the β coefficients of selected risk factors to risk score points. The risk factor points within each model and the respective out-of-bag sample were applied at the individual level by summing the applicable points for each risk factor present. To test the sensitivity and specificity, a threshold of >0 points was set for each of 50,000 unique scoring tools. Averages of these performance measures were determined and summarized numerically (Table S5, Section 5, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144) and plotted (Figure S5, Section 5, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). We performed data analyses using SAS software version 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Participant Statistics

Participants included 418 individuals from the de novo PPMI cohort with baseline neuropsychological scores (see Table 1 above). We classified participants according to those who completed the study (n = 355) and those who did not (n = 63). We classified participants by cognitive trajectory in the PD-Maintain (n = 353) and PD-Decline (n = 65) groups, as predicted by the CLMM. All participants who were completely missing a PASE score (n = 33) were also in the group of participants who did not complete the study. They were also significantly more likely to have fewer years of formal education (33.3% vs 15.8%). As a result, we did not use the PASE-containing model in selection for the final model (Section 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). Other than the PASE values, missingness was sparse (see Table 1 above).

Participants were 65.3% male (n = 273), 55.7% were <65 years old at enrollment (n = 233), and 91.3% identified as White and/or non-Hispanic/Latino (n = 380). Most participants (82.8%) had >12 years of education (n = 346), 65.2% (272/417) had a disease duration of <5 years (n = 272), and 89.5% had mild motor symptoms (n = 374; MDS-UPDRS Part III: <32). On the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, 78.2% (327/418) scored within normal range (n = 327; ≥26 points). In all, 64.3% (268/417) of participants had a high body mass index defined as ≥25 kg/m2 (n = 268), 76.8% had more than two reported vascular risk factors (n = 321), and 7.9% (33/418) had a diagnosis of sleep apnea (n = 33) (Section 7, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). On self-reported questionnaires, 79.2% reported moderate to high physical activity (n = 305; PASE >90), 84.4% had negative depression screening scores (n = 353; Geriatric Depression Scale–Short Form: <8), 92.6% had negative anxiety screening scores (n = 386; State-Trait Anxiety Inventory—State: <54), 80.1% screened negative for REM sleep behavior disorder (n = 335; REM Sleep Behavior Disorder Questionnaire: <5), and 90.9% screened negative for excessive daytime sleepiness (n = 380; ESS <11).

Of the 418 participants with complete baseline neuropsychological testing, there were 63 (15.1%) participants who did not complete the study and did not have neuropsychological test scores for the Year 4 study visit, despite being eligible. Among participants who did not complete the study, the final year visit spanned from baseline (n = 17, 27.0%) to the Year 3 visit (Year 1: n = 15, 23.8%; Year 2: n = 13, 20.6%; Year 3: n = 18, 28.6%). The mean CCS at last observation tended to be lower among participants who did not complete the study compared to those who completed the study at the same visit (Supplementary Material: Section 1, Figure S1a, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144), which is one indicator of nonignorable dropout. The only significant difference in risk factors between the groups of participants who did and did not complete the study was years of formal education (27.0% vs 15.5%; p = 0.03). However, the group of participants who did not complete the study also tended to be older (≥65 years; 55.6% vs 42.3%; p = 0.05) and were less likely to be male (55.6% vs 67.0%; p = 0.08) (see Table 1 above). The group of participants who did not complete the study had a lower measured mean CCS at baseline (−0.02; SD: 0.54) compared to the group of participants who completed the study (0.12; SD: 0.58; p = 0.08). The group of participants who did not complete the study also had a significantly lower measured mean CCS at the final visit (−0.17; SD: 0.77) than the group of participants who completed the study (0.13; SD: 0.76; p <0.01) at the Year 4 visit (n = 334).

Most participants were classified as PD-Maintain (n = 353; 84.4%) rather than PD-Decline (n = 65; 15.6%) (see Table 1 above). At baseline, the PD-Decline group was significantly more likely to have moderate-severe motor symptoms (p = 0.02), excessive daytime sleepiness (p = 0.02), and fewer years of formal education (p = 0.04) compared to the PD-Maintain group. At baseline, the PD-Decline group also had a significantly lower measured mean CCS (−0.07; SD: 0.60) compared to the PD-Maintain group (0.13; SD: 0.57; p = 0.02). At the Year 4 visit, the PD-Decline group had a significantly lower predicted mean CCS (−1.08; SD: 0.63) compared to the PD-Maintain group (0.20; SD: 0.57; p < 0.01). When comparing across the two participant classification systems (ie, cognitive trajectory and study completion) (see Table 1 above), the group of participants who did not complete the study included significantly more participants from the PD-Decline group (n = 46; 73.0%) than the group of participants who completed the study (n = 19; 5.4%; p <0.01).

Nonignorable Dropouts Identified by CLMM

The CLMM estimated a significant interaction between the time of last study visit and study visit year (0.080; 95% CI [0.039, 0.121]; p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). When comparing within the same study visit year, each additional year a participant remained in the study corresponded with an estimated average increase in the annual change in CCS of 0.080 (ie, there was a shallower decline for those who were measured longer compared to those who dropped out) (Figure 2B). We calculated the estimated slope for each dropout year (Section 1, Figure S1B, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). The marginal slope (ie, the estimated slope for the mean dropout time) was -0.025 (95% CI [−0.046, −0.004]; p = 0.02). The estimated average decline of those who dropped out after the baseline visit was 0.315 (95% CI [−0.476, −0.154]; p < 0.001) (Figure 2A).

Statistical Analysis

Logistic Regression Modeling

After adjusting for other covariates, we calculated adjusted ORs for each of the risk factors, with three identified as having increased odds of being categorized as PD-Decline (Figure 3A). The three risk factors were excessive daytime sleepiness (ESS: ≥11; aOR: 2.79; 95% CI [1.28, 6.06]), moderate-severe motor symptoms (MDS-UPDRS Part III: ≥32; aOR: 2.55; 95% CI [1.22, 5.35]), and fewer years of formal education (≤12 years; aOR: 2.18; 95% CI [1.15, 4.13]). The direction of associations in the crude regressions was similar. We also performed logistic regression analysis including self-reported physical activity (eg, PASE scores) (Section 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144).

Most participants did not demonstrate any of the three risk factors, which had a 10.9% baseline predicted probability of classification as PD-Decline. When stratifying by educational attainment status—which is modifiable only in early life—each additional potentially modifiable and/or treatable risk factor (ie, excessive daytime sleepiness, moderate-severe motor symptoms) increased the predicted probability of PD-Decline between 20% and 44% when fewer years of formal education was present and between 14% and 36% when educational attainment was higher (Figure 3B). We also considered predicted probabilities for other possible combinations of the identified risk factors (Section 3, Figure S3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). The sensitivity analyses did not show that LEDD significantly moderated effects between each of the PREDICT risk factors and designation as PD-Maintain versus PD-Decline. When evaluating LEDD as a potential confounder, LEDD did not change the findings of the PREDICT model (Section 4, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144).

PREDICT Score Calculation

Using the final model with the three risk factors, the standardized and rounded β coefficients resulted in a final PREDICT scoring system with a possible score range of 0 to 3.5 points (Figure 3C). The optimistic naïve AUC was 0.615 (SE: 0.037; 95% CI [0.54, 0.69]), and the bootstrap-corrected AUC was 0.603. Applying the PREDICT scoring to the population sample showed that participants with a PREDICT score above zero were more likely to be designated as PD-Decline (23.2%; p = 0.002) than those with a score of zero (11.6%).

Risk Factor Selection by Bootstrapping Procedure

Excessive daytime sleepiness, fewer years of formal education, and moderate-severe motor symptoms appeared in the bootstrap sample models for >70% of the logistic regressions (Table S3, Section 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). The next most frequently selected factors were non-White race and/or Hispanic/Latino ethnicity and REM sleep behavior disorder, selected in 61% of models. The remaining risk factors were selected in less than 60% of models (Table S3, Section 3 Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). When using a PREDICT score threshold of greater than zero, the mean procedural bootstrap sensitivity and specificity were 59.9% and 43.5%, respectively, across the 50,000 out-of-bag replicates (Table S5, Section 5 Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144).

DISCUSSION

The current study aimed to further refine pPREDICT, our initial pragmatic tool for estimating cognitive decline in PD using easily obtained demographic and clinical data, with a focus on potentially modifiable and/or treatable risk factors (Carlisle et al, 2023). The initial pPREDICT incorporated 14 risk factors that could be collected in a variety of clinical settings. These risk factors also do not require complex testing protocols often available only at tertiary/quaternary care centers or in research studies, given that many individuals with PD receive care elsewhere (Pearson et al, 2023). We corrected the refined PREDICT for nonignorable dropout, which could introduce bias from early study exit by individuals with cognitive decline (Moore et al, 2017). We also simplified the tool by including only the risk factors that significantly contributed to the model.

Although PREDICT only requires assessment of three risk factors, it showed a similar naïve AUC (0.615; 95% CI [0.54, 0.69]) as pPREDICT (0.68; 95% CI [0.61, 0.76]), which assesses fourteen risk factors. Although the AUC is lower for PREDICT than other tools in their respective populations (Dawson et al, 2018; Gramotnev et al, 2019; Hogue et al, 2018; Liu et al, 2017; Schrag et al, 2017), it does not rely on complex testing only available at tertiary/quaternary care centers or research settings. As a result, PREDICT may provide wider applicability, which is particularly important given that most patients with PD are not managed by Movement Disorder Specialists (Pearson et al, 2023). In addition, using dichotomous cut-offs allows for wider applicability as measurements are not standard across all research and clinical settings.

The risk factors that contributed to PREDICT were excessive daytime sleepiness, moderate-severe motor symptoms, and fewer years of formal education. Non-White and/or Hispanic/Latino race/ethnicity also had an increased likelihood of association with PD-Decline (Section 6, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144). Educational attainment has long been recognized as a modifiable risk factor for all-cause dementia in early life, but the benefits of ongoing educational endeavors cannot be completely separated from early life education (Livingston et al, 2020). Therefore, education is not clearly a modifiable risk factor for cognitive decline in later life. Despite these limitations, knowing educational attainment and the ethnic and/or racial identity of an individual may be informative for assessing personalized risk for cognitive decline. The potentially modifiable and/or treatable risk factors contributing to PREDICT were excessive daytime sleepiness and moderate-severe motor symptoms. Other modifiable and/or treatable risk factors did not contribute (Section 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CBN/A144).

It is a substantial challenge that the dopaminergic medications used to treat motor symptoms are also associated with excessive daytime sleepiness (Amara et al, 2017), thereby placing these two risk factors for cognitive decline at odds. However, in our analysis, we did not find evidence of LEDD as a potential confounder to the PREDICT model. It is not clear how these common symptoms of PD may affect other downstream risk factors for cognitive decline, including regular exercise, cognitively engaging activity, and social engagement (Sun and Armstrong, 2021). In addition, both excessive daytime sleepiness and motor severity can be reflections of disease severity and therefore may instead be proxies of disease progression. The de novo PPMI PD cohort is unique in that all participants are newly diagnosed with PD, but this does not control for the known heterogeneous trajectories of PD. Regardless, excessive daytime sleepiness and moderate-severe motor symptoms are still areas of interest as potential targets of intervention for modifying cognitive trajectories.

Potential Biases in Observational Trial Design

An important contributor to possible bias in the original pPREDICT was the inclusion of PASE, which was introduced after PPMI initiation. Given the known importance of physical activity in cognitive outcomes factors (Sun and Armstrong, 2021), we included PASE score in the initial fourteen risk factors. However, we removed it from the final PREDICT model because missing PASE data were isolated to the group of participants who did not complete the study and were more prevalent in the PD-Decline group. The introduction of assessments after the initiation of a study can create bias and false interpretation of data analysis, which highlights an avoidable cause of bias in longitudinal trials.

Because cognitive decline may be associated with early trial exit (Chatfield et al, 2005), retention bias is inherent in longitudinal studies. Individuals without cognitive decline or with less severe cognitive decline are more likely to participate longer. Retention of older adults can be especially challenging, which can lead to better retention of relatively more robust older adults compared to the general population (Pitkala and Strandberg, 2022). Overall, this retention bias is likely to concentrate individuals with less severe cognitive and functional decline in the longer follow-up visits. This retention bias falsely lowers measures of cognitive decline in a longitudinal study population compared to the real-world population, highlighting the importance of correcting for early trial exits especially when cognition and functional status are outcomes of interest. This is further compounded by somewhat rare outcomes over the relatively short course of observational trial participation, such as measurable cognitive decline, limiting the ability to measure clinically meaningful differences.

Multiple barriers have been identified and solutions proposed to improve both the recruitment and retention of participants and study partners for PD-specific longitudinal trials (Kehagia et al, 2022). The strongest recommendation continues to be limiting missing data via clinical trial conduct approaches, but there are also analysis methods for addressing data missingness (Little et al, 2012). PPMI has a relatively high retention rate; however, retention biases that have the potential to impact cognitive outcomes are still likely to be present. For example, in this study, there were more participants who completed the study (n = 355; 84.9%) than participants who did not (n = 63; 15.1%). Notably, the SD for CCS at baseline and final study visit across these groups were very similar.

In addition, there are known limitations to using standard linear mixed modeling to predict the contributions of risk factors to cognitive decline, given the variable psychometric properties of the tests used to assess cognition (Proust-Lima et al, 2011). Because dropout was likely nonignorable missing-not-at-random rather than missing-at-random, standard linear mixed modeling was not a comprehensive approach to handling data missingness. As a result, we used CLMM to account for missing cognitive data in participants who exited early. This is a well-established modeling approach to handle data missingness in longitudinal measurements (Wu and Bailey, 1988; Wu and Bailey, 1989) and allowed for these participants to be included in our analysis. Of the prior tools for measuring cognitive decline in PD (Dawson et al, 2018; Gramotnev et al, 2019; Hogue et al, 2018; Liu et al, 2017; Schrag et al, 2017; Yousaf et al, 2019; Wilson et al, 2020), PREDICT is the only one that corrects for early trial exit with this approach, rather than excluding these participants from analysis.

Other Limitations and Future Directions

There are limitations to the current study. First, there are no clear definitions of meaningful cognitive decline in PD. The Movement Disorder Society Task Force allows for the diagnosis of PD with mild cognitive impairment while within normal range for formal neuropsychological testing if there is a decline of at least 1 SD on serial testing (Litvan et al, 2012). However, the timescale of this decline is not detailed in the formal recommendations. In addition, there are currently no guidelines on specific cutoffs for CCSs. Given that PPMI includes a highly educated population, we determined that a decline in CCS of >0.5 SD over the four-year follow-up period would signify meaningful cognitive decline (PD-Decline) in the current study.

Second, there are known heterogeneities in PD trajectories. The PPMI de novo cohort includes only individuals diagnosed with PD within 2 years of the baseline assessment, naïve to dopaminergic medication, and at lower Hoehn and Yahr stages I–II (Marek et al, 2011). However, the overall study population still likely includes participants experiencing a wide variety of possible disease phases. This heterogeneity has prompted multiple prior attempts to subtype PD for motor and/or nonmotor symptoms, including cognition (Fereshtehnejad and Postuma, 2017). A more diffuse malignant clinical phenotype of PD is accompanied by more severe baseline PD-associated symptoms, including motor and nonmotor features (Fereshtehnejad et al, 2017). Similarly, in the current study, the PD-Decline group had a significantly lower baseline CCS compared to the PD-Maintain group. Given that individuals have different baselines at the time of presentation in both research and clinical settings, understanding how this impacts their overall trajectories over time is important to guide discussions about prognosis.

Lastly, PREDICT has yet to be externally validated. This includes validation not only in other populations, but also in conjunction with alternative measurements for the risk factors. Per the design goals for both pPREDICT and PREDICT, we used dichotomous cut-offs as measurements, but these are not standard across research and clinical settings (Carlisle et al, 2023). This approach obviates the power of using continuous variables but improves applicability. For example, although ESS is the most commonly used assessment for excessive daytime sleepiness, there are other tools available (McWhirter et al, 2007). Regardless, the validation of cognitive decline predictor tools is widely accepted as being an iterative process. More specifically, participants in cognitively-based trials are historically not representative of the greater population (Arce Renteria et al, 2023), which requires validation of tools developed in trial settings in a wide variety of populations.

CONCLUSION

The results of the current study represent an early step in developing a cognitive decline predictor tool for PD with wider potential applicability and a focus on potentially treatable/reversible risk factors. There are currently no disease-modifying pharmacotherapeutics for cognitive decline in PD, but there is some support and ongoing research for nonpharmacologic approaches, including physical exercise to improve cognition (Sun and Armstrong, 2021). Cognitive decline in PD does not have to be inevitable, even while waiting for the availability of disease-modifying therapies that improve cognitive outcomes in PD. PREDICT may offer the opportunity for personalized risk assessment and risk reduction.
