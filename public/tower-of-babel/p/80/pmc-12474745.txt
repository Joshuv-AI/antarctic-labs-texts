Hypoglycemia-Related Hospitalization in Adults with Type 2 Diabetes Receiving Insulin in Japan: Real-World Analysis Using the Medical Data Vision Database
Authors: Takeshi Miyatsuka, Maiko Kino, Elisabeth de Laguiche, Onkar Kakade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough many people with diabetes are treated with insulin in Japan, data on the incidence of hypoglycemia in this population are limited. This real-world, retrospective, cohort analysis assessed the incidence of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, and the time to first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, after insulin initiation in adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D) in Japan.MethodsAdults with T2D who initiated basal, basal-bolus, or premix insulin between January 2016 and September 2022 were identified from the Medical Data Vision (MDV) database. Index date was defined as the date on which the first insulin prescription was issued. The 12 months preceding and the 24 months after this date comprised the baseline and follow-up periods, respectively. Inverse probability of treatment weighting was used to address potential confounding effects.ResultsOf 65,668 individuals in the weighted cohort (median [interquartile range] age, 66.00 [55.00, 73.00] years), 21,846 (33.27%) initiated basal insulin, 21,852 (33.28%) initiated basal-bolus insulin, and 21,969 (33.45%) initiated premix insulin. During the follow-up period, 150 individuals (0.23%) had a hypoglycemia-related hospitalization; incidence rate (95% confidence interval [CI]): 0.35 (0.29, 0.40) per 100 person-years. Of those 150 individuals, 89 (0.41%) in the basal-bolus group experienced hypoglycemia-related hospitalization compared with 29 (0.13%; p < 0.001) and 33 (0.15%; p < 0.001) in the basal and premix groups, respectively. There was a trend towards a higher risk of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization in the basal-bolus group than in either the basal (hazard ratio [HR] 0.32; 95% CI 0.15, 0.66; p = 0.002) or premix group (HR 0.36; 95% CI 0.13, 1.03; p = 0.056). The basal-bolus group had the shortest time to first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization compared with the basal and premix groups.ConclusionsIn adults with T2D in Japan, treatment with basal-bolus insulin was associated with a higher incidence of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization than with basal or premix insulin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-025-01776-x.Keywords: Hypoglycemia, Hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, Insulin treatment, Japanese claims-based database, Type 2 diabetes

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Despite widespread insulin use among people with diabetes in Japan, information on the incidence of hypoglycemia remains limited.In Japan, insulin therapy may be recommended as a first-line treatment when absolute or relative indications are present, although it carries a known risk of hypoglycemia, particularly in elderly individuals.The aim of this analysis was to determine the incidence of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, as well as the time to first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, after insulin initiation in adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D) in Japan. What was learned from the study? Overall, a higher proportion of individuals who were receiving a basal-bolus insulin regimen experienced hypoglycemia-related hospitalization compared with those receiving basal or premix insulin. Across treatment groups, a higher proportion of those aged ≥ 65 years (versus those aged < 65 years) experienced hypoglycemia-related hospitalization.These data provide valuable insights into the incidence of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization in a Japanese population, highlighting the importance of careful administration and monitoring of insulin therapy to optimize its benefits for individuals with T2D in Japan.

Introduction

The global increase in the prevalence of diabetes is also reflected in Japan, where the prevalence increased from 7.9% in 2010 to 12.0% in 2024 (comprising 10.8 million people), with further increases predicted going forward [1, 2]. Research suggests that the increase in prevalence of diabetes in Japan is primarily attributable to an aging population [1]. Given the rarity of type 1 diabetes in Japan [3], these statistics are largely reflective of the population with type 2 diabetes (T2D).

The pathophysiology of T2D is known to differ between Western and Japanese populations. For example, T2D is primarily characterized by β-cell dysfunction owing to a lower level of β-cell function in Japanese individuals than is seen for Caucasians, in whom insulin resistance and obesity are the predominant characteristics [4]. These differences have led the Japan Diabetes Society to develop treatment guidelines tailored to the population with T2D in Japan, which recommend that insulin therapy is implemented in individuals with T2D with inadequate glycemic control despite also undergoing medical nutrition therapy, increased physical activity/exercise, and therapy with non-insulin glucose-lowering agents [5].

In these guidelines, the first step when considering pharmacological treatments is to determine the relative or absolute indications for insulin therapy, with safety as a primary concern [6]; however, use of exogenous insulin is associated with a known risk of hypoglycemia, which is elevated in elderly individuals [7, 8]. Consequently, the Japanese Clinical Practice Guideline for Diabetes 2019 recommends less strict blood glucose management for older individuals to mitigate this increased risk of hypoglycemia [5]. While several studies have described metabolic profiles of the Japanese population with T2D [9–11], data on the incidence of hypoglycemia in this population remain limited [12], and further investigation is needed to clarify the burden of hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations.

The Medical Data Vision (MDV) database contains electronic health insurance claims primarily from Diagnosis Procedure Combination hospitals, which comprise approximately 28% of advanced treatment hospitals in Japan, representing a patient population of approximately 45 million individuals [13, 14]. The MDV database can provide valuable insights on the population of people in Japan living with T2D, and has been used in previous studies to assess treatment-related outcomes with an insulin co-formulation and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors in this population [15, 16].

Since hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, which typically reflects a severe condition that cannot be managed in outpatient settings, is one of the most serious adverse outcomes in patients receiving insulin therapy, we aimed to investigate the incidence and risk of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, as well as the time to first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, in adults with T2D in Japan who were initiating insulin therapy, using data from the MDV database.

Methods

Study Objectives

The primary objectives of the study were to determine the incidence and risk of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, as well as the time to first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization in each treatment group. The secondary objectives comprised the same outcomes analyzed in subgroups of the basal insulin cohort, namely the basal-only or fixed-ratio combination (FRC)-only subgroups.

Study Design and Overview

This was an observational, retrospective cohort study utilizing data from the MDV database, spanning from January 2015 to September 2024. The index date was defined as the date of the first insulin prescription being issued to an individual, and prescriptions that were issued between January 2016 and September 2022 were considered for inclusion. The study baseline period comprised the 12 months preceding the index date and was used to collect pre-existing condition data for confounding factor adjustment. The follow-up period comprised the 24 months after the index date, during which hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations were assessed.

Participant Selection

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older with insulin-naive T2D who were initiating basal, basal-bolus, or premix insulin treatment within the index period and who had continuous data availability throughout the 1-year baseline and 2-year follow-up periods. Participants were included in the analysis if they were continuously enrolled with the same hospital during the 1-year before index and 2-years after the index date (instances of hospital transfer were censored from the analysis). All index prescriptions used to define groups were provided in outpatient settings.

The basal insulin group included individuals who had been prescribed basal insulin, including fixed-ratio combinations (FRCs; basal insulin and a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist [GLP-1 RA]), but not bolus or premix insulin. The basal insulin group also comprised two subgroups: participants receiving only basal insulin and those receiving only FRCs. Individuals who were receiving both basal insulin and FRCs or who had switched between basal insulin and FRC within 1 year after the index date (n = 691; 3.20% of the basal insulin group) were excluded. The basal-bolus insulin group included individuals who had been prescribed basal and bolus insulin but not premix insulin or FRCs. The premix insulin group included individuals who had been prescribed premix insulin but not basal insulin, FRCs, or bolus insulin. For each individual, at least two outpatient prescriptions were checked between the index date and 6 months post-index date, and between 7 months post-index date and 12 months post-index date. Ethics Statement

Ethics approval was not required for this retrospective study because only anonymized data from the MDV database were used. Permission was obtained to access the data from this database for this study from the database owners.

Study Definitions

Hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations were identified using relevant International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, as described previously [16]. Individuals who did not receive a glucose injection on the day of admission were excluded [16].

Statistical Analysis

All study variables were analyzed descriptively. For continuous variables, the mean and standard deviation (SD) were presented. The t-test was used for normally distributed data, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed data. For categorical or binary variables, frequency and proportion were reported and assessed using the Chi-squared test. For categories with low cell counts (< 5), the Fisher’s exact test was used. Statistical significance was predetermined at p < 0.05 (two-sided) and all p values were unadjusted.

Inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to balance covariates across the basal, basal-bolus, and premix treatment groups to address potential confounding factors and to reduce bias in treatment effect estimation. The baseline variables used for this analysis were age (18–64, 65–74, ≥ 75 years), sex (female, male), number of oral non-insulin glucose-lowering medications (0, 1–2, ≥ 3), cardiovascular disease (CVD; yes/no), hypertension, renal impairment (diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, or chronic glomerulonephritis; yes/no), obesity (yes/no), and dyslipidemia (yes/no). Details of how baseline variables were defined are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.0).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Individuals from the MDV database were grouped according to prescribed regimen; attrition of individuals from this original cohort is reported in Supplementary Table S2. Data from the original cohort were then subjected to IPTW (Supplementary Fig. S1). After weighting, the standard mean differences for baseline variables between the original cohort and the weighted cohort were below 0.05 across all variables, indicating that the cohort was well balanced (Fig. 1). This cohort was subsequently used for all analyses and is referred to as the weighted cohort. Demographics and baseline characteristics for the original cohort before IPTW, and for individuals in the weighted cohort after IPTW, are presented in Table 2. Of the 65,668 individuals (median [interquartile range (IQR)] age, 66.0 [55.0, 73.0] years) in the weighted cohort, 21,846 (33.3%) were receiving basal insulin, 21,852 (33.3%) were receiving basal-bolus insulin, and 21,969 (33.5%) were receiving premix insulin. Baseline glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) values were only available for 7975 individuals (12.1%) in the weighted cohort. The median (IQR) baseline HbA1c values were 9.1% (7.8%, 11.0%) for the basal group, 9.4% (7.9%, 11.6%) for the basal-bolus group, and 8.7% (7.5%, 10.2%) for the premix group. The median (IQR) values at 1 year after insulin initiation were 7.5% (6.9%, 8.4%) for the basal group, 7.4% (6.7%, 8.3%) for the basal-bolus group, and 7.5% (6.9%, 8.3%) for the premix group.

Hypertension was reported for 57.9%, 57.9%, and 56.7% of individuals in the basal, basal-bolus, and premix groups, respectively. Established CVD was reported for 36.4%, 36.4%, and 35.7% of individuals in the basal, basal-bolus, and premix groups, respectively, while chronic kidney disease (CKD) was reported for 22.6%, 23.7%, and 23.4% of individuals in the basal, basal-bolus, and premix groups, respectively.

The highest number of index insulin prescriptions was received via an internal medicine department (36,716 prescriptions; 47.6%), followed by 12,660 prescriptions (16.4%) at endocrinology medicine or endocrinology and metabolism departments, 7666 prescriptions (9.9%) at diabetes medicine departments, and 4313 prescriptions (5.6%) at diabetes clinics; 15,827 prescriptions (20.5%) were received at departments labelled as “other” (Table 2).

Of the 36,716 prescriptions that were received via an internal medicine department (10,864 in the basal group, 14,417 in the basal-bolus group, and 11,435 in the premix group), 1271 (11.7%) of the index prescriptions in the basal group were associated with an HbA1c record, as were 1427 (9.9%) in the basal-bolus group and 1732 (15.1%) in the premix group.

In the overall cohort, the median (IQR) index HbA1c values were largely similar across departments: 9.2% (7.7%, 11.2%) for internal medicine departments; 9.3% (8.0%, 11.1%) for endocrinology medicine or endocrinology and metabolism departments; 9.2% (8.0%, 11.2%) for diabetes medicine departments; 8.9% (8.0%, 11.3%) for diabetes clinics; and 8.3% (7.2%, 9.9%) for departments labelled as “other.” The highest median (IQR) HbA1c values were reported for the basal group by diabetes clinics (10.1% [7.9%, 11.9%]), for the basal-bolus group by internal medicine departments (10.0% [8.0%, 11.9%]), and for the premix group, which had the lowest median index HbA1c value, by internal medicine departments (8.9% [7.6%, 10.3%]). Reported median HbA1c values differed by department for the different insulin regimen groups and are presented in Table 2.

Hypoglycemia-Related Hospitalizations in the Weighted Cohort

Overall, 150 individuals (0.23%) had hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations during the 2-year follow-up period, equating to an incidence rate (95% CI) of 0.35 (0.29, 0.40) per 100 person-years (Table 3).

A statistically significantly higher proportion of individuals in the basal-bolus group (89 individuals [0.41%]) experienced hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations than did those in the basal (29 individuals [0.13%]; p < 0.001) or premix (33 individuals [0.15%]; p < 0.001) groups (note that the numbers of individuals in each group have been subject to rounding and consequently do not sum to N = 150).

Individuals in the basal and premix groups had a lower risk of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization than individuals in the basal-bolus group (Table 3).Compared with the basal-bolus group, the basal group had a hazard ratio (HR) (95% CI) of 0.32 (0.15, 0.66, p = 0.002) and the premix group had a HR (95% CI) of 0.36 (0.13, 1.03, p = 0.056), indicating a trend towards a reduced risk of hypoglycemia in these subsets. Furthermore, individuals in the basal-bolus group had the shortest median time to the first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization (269.02 d), which was statistically significantly shorter than that of the median time to the first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization reported for the basal (480.48 d, p = 0.001) and premix (604.71 d, p < 0.001) groups (Table 3 and Fig. 2).

When stratified by age, the incidence rate of hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations across insulin treatment subgroups was statistically significantly higher in individuals who were aged 65–74 years and aged 75 years or older than in those aged 18–64 years (p < 0.001). Notably, the median time to the first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization was numerically shorter in those aged 18–64 years (274.37 d) than in those aged 65–74 years (427.42 d) and those aged 75 years or older (456.57 d) (Table 3).

Hypoglycemia-Related Hospitalizations in the Basal Insulin Subgroups of the Weighted Cohort

The characteristics and baseline demographics of individuals in the basal insulin subgroups are presented in Table 4. Of those individuals receiving basal insulin, 19,661 (90.00%) were in the basal-only subgroup and 1494 (6.84%) were in the FRC subgroup. A slightly higher proportion of individuals aged 75 years or older was observed in the basal-only subgroup than in the FRC subgroup, while a slightly higher proportion of individuals aged 18–64 years was observed in the FRC subgroup than in the basal-only subgroup. The prevalence of comorbidities was similar across both subgroups.

Discussion

Overall, 0.23% of individuals in the weighted cohort experienced hypoglycemia-related hospitalization, translating to an incidence rate of 0.35 per 100 person-years. A higher proportion of individuals who were 65–74 years (0.32%) or 75 years or older (0.36%) had hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations than those who were 64 years or younger (0.10%), aligning with the known elevated risk of hypoglycemia in older individuals.

The basal-bolus group had the highest incidence rate of hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations, as well as the shortest time to the first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization compared with the basal and premix groups, which is not unexpected given the increased amount of insulin that individuals in the basal-bolus group would be receiving, as well as the challenge of managing a more complex treatment regimen. As expected, given the lower incidence rates of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization in the basal and premix groups, individuals in these groups also had a numerically lower risk of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization than individuals in the basal-bolus group. No events of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization were reported for the FRC basal insulin subgroup during the 2 years of follow-up, aligning with patterns of reduced incidence of hypoglycemia that has been reported previously for daily FRCs of basal insulin and GLP-1 RAs [17, 18]. However, the finding of no events of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization in this analysis is based on a small sample size and so caution should be taken with interpretation.

These findings have implications regarding the selection of an appropriate treatment regimen, given that hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations in individuals with T2D in Japan can incur considerable increases in healthcare resource utilization and costs. In a study conducted in 2018, the median total medical cost for hospitalized patients with hypoglycemia was ¥402,081, with ¥302,341 being directly attributable to the treatment of severe hypoglycemia and hospitalization [19].

The median HbA1c at index date for the overall cohort was 9.0%, with the highest value reported for the group of individuals who went on to initiate basal-bolus insulin therapy, 9.4%, suggesting inadequate glycemic control. These values are substantially higher than those reported previously for individuals in Japan with T2D, which ranged from 6.8% to 7.5% [9–12, 20]; however, there are pertinent differences between this study and previous reports that should be considered. First, it should be noted that only 12.1% of individuals in the current study had HbA1c values available at the index date; consequently, these values may not be truly representative of the entire weighted cohort or the wider Japanese population. Furthermore, HbA1c values were not necessarily recorded at equivalent time points in the current study versus other studies. For example, the lowest HbA1c value in the range of previously reported values was recorded at the onset of severe hypoglycemia [12], whereas in the current study, the recorded HbA1c values correspond to the point of insulin initiation in insulin-naive individuals. Given that the majority (63.9%) of individuals in this study were also not receiving oral non-insulin glucose-lowering medications, it is perhaps not surprising that their HbA1c values were elevated, indicating inadequate glycemic control. Conversely, where treatments were reported alongside HbA1c values in previous studies, 27.6–30.7% of individuals were using insulin (with or without concomitant glucose-lowering medications) [10, 12], 20.5–62.0% were using oral non-insulin glucose-lowering medications [10, 12], and 10.5% were receiving dietary intervention alone [10]. In one study, a prescription for an anti-hyperglycemic treatment was an inclusion criterion [9]. Consequently, these individuals may have been experiencing better glycemic control at the point at which the HbA1c values were recorded than those in this study. Lastly, the majority of individuals (68.1%) received their insulin prescriptions at medical departments that do not specialize in diabetes, possibly accounting for the higher HbA1c values present at the time of insulin initiation, as non-specialists might be less knowledgeable about timely treatment intensification or might be more cautious about the consequences of insulin treatment. Notably, a Japanese web-based survey showed that although the majority of clinicians considered HbA1c below 9.0% the best for insulin initiation, only approximately one-third of individuals with diabetes actually initiated insulin at this point [21]. One year after insulin initiation, glycemic control (as assessed by HbA1c levels) improved across all three treatment groups to comparable levels (median 7.4–7.5% [IQR 6.7%, 8.4%]).

The prevalence of diabetes-related comorbidities among the weighted cohort in the current study is largely in line with previous reports for individuals with T2D in Japan. The prevalence of CVD was 36.2%, which is comparable to the prevalence reported for the Japanese subgroup of the CAPTURE study (37.3%) and the prevalence of macrovascular disease reported by Yokomichi et al. for women (28.6%) and men (37.2%) [11, 22]. The prevalence of hypertension appears to be more variable between reports, with 57.5% reported in the current study, and 46.8% and 63.1% reported elsewhere [10, 20]. Similarly, the prevalence of CKD (23.2% in the current study) varies widely, ranging from 5.0% to 10.2% in one study, and 32.6% for women and 31.5% for men in another [9, 11]. However, this variance most likely reflects differences in the study populations and methodologies used.

Although hypoglycemia-related data in Japanese individuals are limited, one previous report did assess hospitalizations related to severe hypoglycemia among 193 responding facilities, at which approximately 346,939 individuals with diabetes (87.9% with T2D) received regular medical services [12]. Annually, 2237 of these individuals (0.64%) required medical services for severe hypoglycemia, with 1171 (i.e., 0.34% overall) subsequently admitted for treatment [12]. Another previous report which also used the MDV database to assess hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations in Japanese individuals with T2D, albeit receiving treatment with sodium-glucose transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, reported that out of 171,622 individuals, 0.13% experienced hypoglycemia-related hospitalization with an incidence rate of 0.60 per 100 person-years [16]. These previously reported incidences are higher than that seen in the present study (0.23%), which may be the result of differing study populations (e.g., there were no adults with type 1 diabetes in the current study) or the stringent inclusion criterion in the current study that required administration of a glucose injection on the day of admission. However, further studies assessing diabetes-related hypoglycemia in the Japanese population are required to provide more detailed insights.

Japan is an aging society, and the number of elderly people with T2D is increasing [2]. It is well known that elderly individuals are likely to have more comorbidities compared with younger individuals and are more vulnerable to hypoglycemia [7], a reality that is particularly relevant in Japan, where more than half of individuals living with diabetes are 65 years or older [6]. This is confirmed by the current study because a greater proportion of individuals who were 65 years or older had hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations than those who were younger than 65 years. Japan’s clinical practice guidelines state that strict HbA1c treatment targets should not be set for elderly people, instead prioritizing the avoidance of hypoglycemia [5]. However, because subjective symptoms of hypoglycemia can be subtle in elderly people, hypoglycemia unawareness and progression to severe hypoglycemia are more likely, which can result in hospitalization [5]. These factors suggest that additional care may be required when selecting or managing glucose-lowering treatments for older individuals, highlighting the importance of individualized treatment plans for elderly people living with T2D in Japan.

The basal-bolus group had both the highest incidence rate of hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations and the shortest median time to the first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization. These findings suggest that particular attention should be paid to the risk of hypoglycemia associated with initiating a complex treatment regimen such as basal-bolus insulin therapy. Indeed, if insulin is required for an individual with T2D, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommends using it in combination with a GLP-1 RA, given the beneficial effect on hypoglycemia risk, among other factors [8].

A strength of this analysis is that the MDV database enabled a large cohort size and a long study follow-up period to be captured; however, this analysis does have some limitations which should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results. Given that these data are derived from a single database and an inpatient setting, they may not reflect the broader population of individuals living with T2D in Japan. Moreover, the database itself has limitations. For instance, only information required for reimbursement is included in the insurance claim and captured in the database; therefore, the database may not include a record of all medical procedures performed. In addition, some instances of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization may not have been identified owing to disrupted/incomplete information for some individuals in the database (e.g., individuals who were transferred between different hospitals/clinics). The database also does not account for duplicates, meaning that one individual who experienced multiple hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations may have been counted multiple times in the database. HbA1c data were only available for a small proportion of individuals, and data regarding daily insulin doses are not available in the structured data fields of the MDV database [14], further limiting the contextualization of these hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations.

The strict definition of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization used for this analysis, which required both a relevant ICD-10 code and a same-day glucose injection upon admission to hospital, could have resulted in an underestimation of the true burden of these events, although this definition is likely to improve specificity by reducing misclassifications.

The finding of no hypoglycemia-related hospitalization events in the FRC subgroup should be interpreted with caution, as the small sample size of this subgroup limits statistical power and therefore introduces the risk of a chance finding or selection bias.

Individuals included in this analysis may have had medical histories that could potentially act as confounding factors on the association between insulin therapy regimen and the occurrence of hypoglycemia, e.g., some individuals may have comorbidities such as autoimmune disease or cancer, and may have undergone surgical procedures or be receiving steroids as part of their treatment.

Despite statistical significance, the absolute incidence of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization was low across all treatment groups, which may be owing to a combination of the strict definition of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization used for this analysis, characteristics of the included cohort, limitations of the MDV database, and the high standard of clinical care received in Japan.

Finally, although IPTW was used to account for potential confounders and to reduce the effect of selection bias, individuals who initiated basal-bolus insulin likely differed from those who initiated basal insulin with regard to disease progression and dysglycemia, which may in turn have affected vulnerability to hypoglycemia.

Conclusions

In adults with T2D in Japan, a higher incidence and risk of experiencing hypoglycemia-related hospitalization was seen in individuals receiving basal-bolus insulin than in those receiving basal or premix insulin, although the number of instances was low across all cohorts. The basal-bolus group also had the shortest time to the first hypoglycemia-related hospitalization compared with the basal and premix groups. When stratified by age, those who were 65–74 years of age or 75 years or older (versus those aged < 65 years) experienced a higher incidence of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization. Overall, these data provide valuable insights regarding the incidence of hypoglycemia-related hospitalization in Japan, especially as it relates to treatment regimen and age. These findings suggest that, especially in elderly individuals and those initiating basal-bolus therapy, the appropriate insulin treatment regimen should be carefully selected, administered, and monitored to help minimize the increased risk of severe hypoglycemia.
