Physical education and sports teachers’ health and well-being: workload, psychological resilience and social support perspective
Authors: Emrah Seçer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductıon and aimThe purpose of this study is to examine the relationship between physical education and sports teachers' perceptions of workload and their psychological well-being, and to evaluate the roles of psychological resilience and perceived social support in this relationship.MethodThe study employed a correlational survey design, and 276 physical education and sports teachers from public schools in Turkey participated. Data collection tools included the Teacher Workload Scale (TWP), the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), the Psychological Well-being Scale (PWB), and the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSS). The validity of the measurement models was tested using confirmatory factor analysis, and the hypotheses were evaluated using Hayes' Process Macro (Models 1, 4, and 7) and SPSS 25.ResultsUnder the first hypothesis, the indirect effect of workload on psychological well-being via psychological resilience was significant (b = −0.234, 95% CI [−0.327, −0.142]), explaining approximately 57% of the total variance. In the second hypothesis, perceived social support was found to moderate the relationship between workload and psychological resilience. At high levels of social support, the negative effect of workload on psychological resilience weakened (b = −0.079, 95% CI [−0.184, 0.025]). Within the scope of the third hypothesis, moderates mediation analysis showed that social support significantly mediated the indirect effect of workload on psychological well-being through psychological resilience (b = 0.004, 95% CI [0.001, 0.008]). While negative effects were evident at low levels of social support, they decreased significantly at high levels.ConclusionThe study reveals that the negative effect of teacher workload on psychological well-being operates through psychological resilience, and that perceived social support can mitigate these effects by moderating this process. The findings show that teachers' psychological resilience and social support resources play a critical role in mitigating the negative effects of workload. Educational policies and practices can be designed within the framework of workload management, strengthening social support mechanisms, and resilience-building programs.Keywords: Physical education and sports teachers, Workload, Psychological resilience, Perceived social support, Psychological well-being, Moderated mediation model

Introduction

Teachers form a fundamental “foundation of citizenship” in raising polite, innovative, and conscientious generations. Therefore, teacher well-being must be addressed as a shared global responsibility [1]. However, teaching is a structurally and contextually challenging profession, where job-related factors such as workload may lead to different individual and professional outcomes [2]. eachers’ psychological well-being is closely associated with school functioning, student outcomes, classroom climate, and overall educational quality [3–5], . Teachers face numerous professional stresses that can affect their mental health and lead to teacher burnout [6]. It is stated that the professional development process will contribute to the development of the psychological, social, and physical characteristics needed here [7]. Teacher workload is a multidimensional construct encompassing teaching duties, administrative responsibilities, reporting obligations, and school-based additional tasks [8, 9]. Teachers’ perceived increase in job expectations is positively associated with burnout and psychological distress and negatively associated with well-being [10, 11].

The Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model suggests that high job demands can deplete individuals’ energy and psychological resources, potentially leading to negative psychological outcomes when adequate resources are lacking [12, 13]. Similarly, Conservation of Resources (COR) theory proposes that stress occurs when individuals’ resources are threatened or lost, and sustained high workload may deplete psychological resources and reduce well-being [14].

Physical education and sports teachers take on responsibilities such as planning physical activities, ensuring student safety, and conducting extracurricular sports organizations, in addition to the general demands of the teaching profession. This role diversity differentiates the nature of job demands. Studies conducted with teacher samples show that high job demands are negatively associated with psychological well-being [10]. However, in the context of physical education teachers, the psychological processes shaping this relationship have been examined to a limited extent.

Psychological resilience is defined as an individual’s capacity to adapt to stressful conditions [4]. In teacher samples, resilience has been reported to be negatively associated with burnout and positively associated with well-being [15]. Within the framework of COR theory, resilience can be considered an individual psychological resource, and the weakening of this resource by workload may have indirect effects on well-being.

On the other hand, social support is considered a protective factor in stress processes. The Stress Buffering Hypothesis suggests that social support can mitigate the negative effects of stressors [16]. Furthermore, the Social Ecological Model emphasizes that individual health should be evaluated in the context of social relationships [17]. In this regard, perceived social support can be considered a contextual resource that can condition the effect of workload on psychological resilience.

This study aims to examine the effect of workload on psychological well-being among physical education and sports teachers, with psychological resilience as a mediating factor and perceived social support as a moderating factor.

Teacher workload and psychological well-being

Workload is defined as a job demand related to the quantity, complexity, and time pressure of the tasks expected to be performed within an individual’s job role [18]. In the context of education, teacher workload is a multidimensional construct that includes lesson preparation, assessment processes, administrative reporting, parent communication, and extracurricular duties. OECD data show that teachers can experience significant time pressure due to non-teaching duties [19]. This situation reveals that workload has not only a quantitative dimension but also a perceptual and psychosocial dimension.

Psychological well-being is considered a multidimensional construct encompassing positive assessments of an individual’s life and functioning, emotional balance, and psychological resources [20]. Ryff conceptualized psychological well-being through the dimensions of self-acceptance, autonomy, environmental mastery, life purpose, personal growth, and positive relationships [21]. The education literature has shown relationships among teachers’ levels of psychological well-being, burnout, and job satisfaction [22].

The relationship between job demands and psychological well-being is explained within the Job Demands-Resources Model. According to this model, high job demands can lead to energy depletion and deterioration in health when adequate job and personal resources are unavailable [23]. Recent studies of teacher samples have found that perceived workload is positively associated with psychological distress and burnout [22]. Similarly, the Conservation of Resources Theory suggests that the depletion of individuals’ resources triggers stress responses [24]. A continuous high workload can lead to the depletion of basic resources, such as time and psychological energy. Additionally, increased job demands are associated with burnout and psychological strain [10].

Studies conducted in the Turkish context demonstrate the relationship between workload and burnout. One study found a positive relationship between teachers’ perceptions of workload and their professional burnout [9], which supports the significance of workload in terms of its psychological consequences for educators. Furthermore, recent studies examining the relationships among teachers’ job satisfaction, stress, and burnout levels in Turkey reveal that stress-related psychological difficulties in the teaching profession can affect both teachers and the education system [25].

Specifically for physical education and sports teachers, tasks such as extracurricular sports organizations, tournaments, and physical security responsibilities can broaden the scope of job demands. The fact that the workload involves both physical and organizational dimensions may have more pronounced effects on psychological well-being. When JD-R and COR theories are considered together, it is theoretically consistent that high workload negatively affects psychological well-being.

The mediating role of psychological resilience

Psychological resilience refers to a person’s capacity to adapt to difficult and stressful situations, cope with negative emotions, and respond appropriately to environmental stressors [26–28]. The concept is theoretically grounded in Resilience Theory, which emphasizes that individuals’ ability to adapt under stress can be strengthened through problem-solving skills and cognitive flexibility processes [4]. From an etymological perspective, resilience originates from the Latin word “resilire,” meaning “to bounce back” or “to return to the original state” [29].

Within this framework, resilient individuals can evaluate problems from different perspectives and develop adaptive solutions by effectively leveraging cognitive flexibility [30]. In sports psychology, resilience has been conceptualized as both a personality trait and a protective mechanism against stressors, with positive associations reported with athletic performance, psychological well-being, and emotional balance [31–34]. This structure shows positive relationships with athletic performance, psychological well-being, and emotional balance.

In the educational context, psychological resilience is considered a key personal resource that helps teachers maintain well-being under high job demands and reduces vulnerability to burnout and psychological distress [35].

The Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model suggests that high job demands, such as workload, may lead to stress and burnout when individual resources are insufficient [23]. Within this framework, psychological resilience functions as a mediating personal resource that buffers the negative effects of job demands on well-being. Similarly, Conservation of Resources (COR) theory posits that individuals with stronger psychological resources are better able to protect their well-being under stress, whereas resource depletion increases vulnerability to strain [24].

Empirical studies support these theoretical assumptions, showing that teachers with higher resilience levels report lower burnout and higher job satisfaction [36], and that resilience may play a role in the relationship between professional demands such as workload and psychological well-being [37]. Evidence from the Turkish context further indicates a negative relationship between psychological resilience and burnout, highlighting resilience as a critical factor in coping with occupational risks [38]. Overall, these findings suggest that psychological resilience may mediate the relationship between workload and teachers’ psychological well-being by enabling individuals to maintain psychological functioning despite high job demands.

The moderating role of perceived social support

Perceived social support is defined as the emotional, informational, and instrumental support an individual receives from family, friends, colleagues, or the institutional environment [16]. In organizational contexts, social support has been shown to positively influence workplace outcomes for both individuals and institutions [39]. In educational settings, social support is considered a critical contextual resource that shapes how teachers experience and respond to job demands such as workload. It functions as a protective factor in the relationship between stress, burnout, and psychological well-being. Empirical evidence indicates that social support moderates the relationship between workload and psychological well-being, such that the negative effects of workload are stronger when social support is low, and weaker when social support is high [16, 40]. This finding supports the stress-buffering mechanism of social support. In addition, social support has been shown to enhance teachers’ psychological resilience, which in turn contributes to improved psychological well-being. For instance, Salvo-Garrido et al. reported that social support strengthens teacher resilience and is associated with higher levels of psychological well-being [35]. These findings suggest that social support operates as a protective resource in both resilience and well-being processes.

From a theoretical perspective, the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model posits that social support serves as a key job resource that mitigates the negative effects of job demands such as workload [23]. Similarly, Conservation of Resources (COR) theory suggests that individuals with greater access to social resources are better able to protect their psychological well-being under conditions of stress [24]. Wu and Gao’s recent research points to the protective/moderating effect of social support on psychological outcomes in the teacher context [41].

Teachers’ psychological well-being is a critical determinant not only for their individual welfare but also for the quality of the educational environment and student development. Physical education and sports teachers face a multi-layered workload, including lesson planning, extracurricular activities, and intensive student interaction. This situation can strain both their physical and psychological resources. Imbalances between workload and psychological well-being can result in teacher burnout, a tendency to leave the profession, and consequently, a decline in the quality of education.

In this context, empowering teachers with psychological and environmental resources such as resilience and social support emerges as a critical strategy that supports both individual well-being and the sustainability of the education system. Therefore, this study aims to examine the effects of workload on the psychological well-being of physical education and sports teachers and to investigate the mediating role of psychological resilience and the moderating role of perceived social support in this effect. The hypotheses of the study are formulated as follows:

H1: Teacher workload indirectly negatively affects psychological well-being through psychological resilience.

H1: Teacher workload indirectly negatively affects psychological well-being through psychological resilience.

H2: Perceived social support has a moderating role in the effect of teacher workload on psychological resilience.

H2: Perceived social support has a moderating role in the effect of teacher workload on psychological resilience.

H3: Perceived social support moderates the indirect effect of teacher workload on psychological well-being via psychological resilience (i.e., a moderated mediation effect). Specifically, the indirect effect of workload on psychological well-being through psychological resilience is attenuated among teachers with higher levels of perceived social support.

H3: Perceived social support moderates the indirect effect of teacher workload on psychological well-being via psychological resilience (i.e., a moderated mediation effect). Specifically, the indirect effect of workload on psychological well-being through psychological resilience is attenuated among teachers with higher levels of perceived social support.

Method

Research model

This study is structured according to the correlational survey model, a cross-sectional quantitative design used to determine relationships between two or more variables [42]. The correlational survey model allows for revealing patterns of co-variation between variables and statistically testing possible structural connections.

In recent years, there has been a strong trend in the social sciences to examine only direct, unidirectional effects between variables, which is limited in explaining the multi-layered and dynamic nature of social phenomena. Accordingly, more holistic models are preferred in research. It has become particularly important to consider moderator effects, which reveal the conditions under which a relationship is strengthened or weakened, and mediator effects, which explain the processes through which this relationship emerges.

Testing moderator and mediator variables within the same model allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of the theoretical framework; thus, the contextual and process-based patterns of relationships between variables can be statistically evaluated. It is stated that such integrated models contribute to the more robust empirical testing of theories and create a unique explanatory ground in the literature [43]. Research group

The research group was formed using the criterion sampling method, one of the purposeful sampling strategies. Criterion sampling involves selecting participants who possess predefined characteristics directly related to the research problem [44]. This approach strengthens methodological consistency by ensuring that the sample is structured in line with the theoretical framework and the research’s purpose. Indeed, the literature states that aligning the defined criteria with the research’s conceptual foundations is a decisive factor in the internal validity of the sampling process [45].

In this context, the following criteria were used for participation in the study: (a) having at least three years of teaching experience, (b) working in state schools affiliated with the Ministry of National Education, and (c) agreeing to participate in the study voluntarily. A total of 301 physical education and sports teachers who met these criteria were included in the study. During data cleaning, forms containing erroneous, incomplete, or outlier values were excluded, and analyses were conducted on 293 participants.

The adequacy of the sample size was evaluated within the framework of both theoretical assumptions and statistical criteria. Considering the recommended minimum sample size of 215 based on an approximate sampling error of ± 0.05 and a 95% confidence level, the current number of participants is above this threshold. Furthermore, the results of the power analysis conducted using the G*Power program (F tests; linear multiple regression: fixed model, R² increase; f² = 0.08; α = 0.05; power = 0.95; number of predictors = 3) determined that at least 219 participants were sufficient. These findings indicate that the sample of 293 individuals is statistically sufficient and has a reliable structure for analysis. Data collectıon tools

Teachers perception of workload scale

The scale developed by Keleş-Ay has a unidimensional structure and comprises 11 items [46]. The scale, organized on a five-point Likert scale, is rated between “Strongly disagree (1)” and “Strongly agree (5)”. There are no reverse-scored items on the scale—the scores on the scale range from 11 to 55. A high total score indicates that teachers’ workload perception is high, while a low score indicates that workload perception is relatively low. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient calculated during the scale development process was reported as 0.86.

Psychological well-being scale

Developed by Diener and colleagues [47, 48]; the Turkish adaptation, validity, and reliability studies of the scale were conducted by Telef [49]. The scale has a single-factor structure with eight items and is scored on a seven-point Likert-type scale. The exploratory factor analysis indicates that the items are grouped into a single factor, which explains 42% of the total variance. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient calculated as part of the reliability examination was reported as 0.80. Furthermore, analysis using the test-retest method determined that the correlation between the two applications was high, positively directed, and statistically significant (r= .86, p< .01).

Connor–davidson resilience scale- short form

The Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), developed by Connor and Davidson to assess stress coping and psychological resilience [26], consists of 25 items in its original form and has been adapted into a 10-item short form by [50]. The adaptation study for Turkish culture was conducted by Kaya and Odacı [51]. The validity and reliability analyses revealed that the short form showed a high level of correlation with the original long form. The confirmatory factor analysis results indicate that the single-factor structure with 10 items is valid. Reliability examinations reported a Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient of 0.81 and a split-half reliability of 0.78. The Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale Short Form consists of 10 items grouped into a single dimension and uses a 5-point Likert-type rating scale. Participants rate the items on a scale ranging from “0 = Not at all true” to “4 = Almost always true.” Total scores on the scale range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater psychological resilience and the use of more effective coping strategies; lower scores indicate relatively low psychological resilience and possible difficulties coping with stressful situations.

Multıdımensıonal perceıved socıal support scale

The scale developed by Zimet and colleagues was used to assess perceived social support levels [52]; the Turkish adaptation and validity–reliability studies were first conducted by Eker and colleagues. Validity and reliability analyses of the revised version of the scale were conducted by Eker and colleagues in [53]. The scale, consisting of 12 items across 3 subscales, measures individuals’ perceived social support. The subscales are defined as “family,” “friends,” and “special person.” The internal consistency coefficients for the scale were 0.89 for the total scale, 0.85 for the family subscale, 0.88 for the friends subscale, and 0.92 for the special person subscale. The scale, which uses a seven-point Likert-type rating, asks participants to rate each statement from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). As the scores given to the items increase, the level of perceived social support also increases. Each subscale consists of four items, and the sum of the relevant items constitutes the subscale score; the sum of the subscale scores constitutes the overall scale score. The total score on the scale ranges from 12 to 84, while the subscales range from 4 to 28 points. High scores on the scale indicate that the individual perceives a high level of social support [54].

To examine the construct validity of the scales used in the study, first- and second-order confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) were conducted (See Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). Second-order CFA was applied to scales consisting of multiple subdimensions, assuming that these subdimensions represent a higher-order latent structure. This approach allows testing whether the subdimensions cluster under a common higher-order structure. For unidimensional scales containing only one latent variable, first-order DFA was considered sufficient. This analysis strategy is consistent with the theoretical foundations of the scales and the measurement model applications suggested in the literature.

Given the multivariate normality assumption for the data, the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation method was used in the DFA processes. In the analyses, limited modifications appropriate to the theoretical framework were applied when deemed necessary to achieve the fit indices recommended in the literature. The model fit, validity, and reliability levels of the scales used in the study were evaluated in detail. The threshold values suggested by Byrne and Gürbüz were used as a basis for interpreting the fit indices [43, 55]. The Average Explained Variance (AVE) and Composite Reliability (CR) values were considered in the evaluation of convergent validity. In this context, it was observed that the AVE value of the Teacher Workload Scale (0.384) was below the recommended threshold value of 0.50. Although the AVE value of the Teacher Workload Scale (0.384) was below the recommended threshold, this is not uncommon in social science research when constructs are broad and multidimensional.

However, Fornell and Larcker and Hair et al. state that even if the AVE value is relatively low, the construct’s convergent validity can be accepted if the CR value is sufficient [56–58]. Given the high CR value for the structure in question, the measurement model was deemed acceptable. Furthermore, Maruf et al. state that when the CR value is appropriate, an AVE above 0.40 can also be considered sufficient [59].

The internal consistency reliability of the scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. George and Mallery classify an alpha coefficient above 0.80 as “good” reliability and above 0.90 as “excellent” reliability [60]. In this context, the reliability coefficients obtained are within the limits suggested in the literature. Ethical approval and data collection procedures

The research was approved by the Erzincan Binali Yıldırım University Human Research Ethics Committee for Health and Sports Sciences on October 24, 2025, with file number E-88012460-050.04-497589 and protocol number 10/03. Data were collected after participants provided written informed consent; they were clearly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time. All research procedures were conducted in full compliance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and international ethical standards. The data collection process was conducted via an online Google Form prepared for physical education and sports teachers.

Data analysis

Before proceeding with data analysis, missing values and outliers in the dataset were evaluated using SPSS 25.0. At this stage, the suitability of the data for the multivariate normality assumption was examined, and the relationships between independent, dependent, mediating, and moderating variables were determined using Pearson correlation analysis. The assumption of normality was tested using Mahalanobis distances, Z scores, and skewness and kurtosis values for the scale scores. The skewness and kurtosis coefficients fall within the ± 1.5 range [61], and the Z scores fall within the − 3 to + 3 range [62], indicating that the data conform to the assumption of normality.

Furthermore, linear relationships between variables were examined using scatter plots, and no significant deviations were detected. Analyses conducted to test for multicollinearity revealed tolerance values > 0.20 and VIF values < 10, indicating no multicollinearity among the independent variables. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed using AMOS 21 software to validate the factor structures of the measurement tools used in the study. Following validation of the factor structures, bootstrap-based regression analyses were used to examine the indirect and conditional relationships among the variables [63]. The PROCESS Macro (Hayes, 2022) was used in hypothesis testing; Model 4 was preferred for mediating effects, Model 1 for moderating effects, and Model 7 for moderated mediating effects. In the regression analyses, reliability was ensured via 5000 bootstrap resamples [64]. All PROCESS analyses were conducted using unstandardized coefficients (b), consistent with Hayes’ recommendations for mediation and moderation models. For the findings related to the model to be considered meaningful, the 95% confidence interval must not include 0 [64, 65].

Common Method Variance (CMV) assessment

Common Method Variance (CMV) was assessed using multiple diagnostic approaches to examine potential common method bias (CMB) in the data. First, Harman’s single-factor test was performed using the Principal Axis Factoring method on all measurement items. This method was applied according to the framework proposed by Podsakoff et al. (2024) to examine whether common method variance was present in the data set [66]. According to the analysis results, the unrotated single factor explained only 35.77% of the total variance. Compared to the commonly accepted 50% threshold in the literature (Eichhorn, 2014) [67], this ratio is well below it. This suggests that no single factor accounts for the majority of covariance among the variables. Second, as an additional assessment, the correlation matrix of latent variables was examined. Although the analysis findings show that the correlations between the variables are significant, the highest correlation was r = .717. This value is well below the r > .90 threshold in the literature, which indicates that common method variance is a serious problem [66]. Consequently, both Harman’s single-factor test and correlation matrix analyses reveal that common method bias is minimal in the dataset. However, it should be noted that Harman’s single-factor test alone is not sufficient to fully rule out common method variance; therefore, procedural remedies such as anonymity assurance and use of validated scales were also applied during data collection.

Process Model 4 (TWP→CD-RISC→PWB) was analyzed to test the study’s first hypothesis. Within Process Model 4, the mediating role of CD-RISC (M) in the effect of TWP (X) on PWB (Y) was examined. Analyses were conducted using Hayes’ (PROCESS Model 4) method and a 95% bootstrap confidence interval (5,000 resamples). The relationship between TWP and CD-RISC is negative and significant (b = − 0.250, p < .01). Similarly, the direct relationship between TWP and PWB is also negative and significant (b = − 0.258, p < .01). The relationship between CD-RISC and PWB is positive and quite strong (b = 0.933, p < .01). When examining the explanatory level of the model, the proportion of variance explained by TWP’s effect on CD-RISC was found to be R² = 0.128. Within the mediation model, the total variance explained for PWB is R² = 0.568, indicating a high level of explanatory power. However, this value should be interpreted with caution in cross-sectional self-report designs, as shared method variance may partly inflate explained variance. Bootstrap analyses revealed that TWP’s indirect effect on PWB via CD-RISC was significant (b = − 0.234, 95% CI [− 0.327, − 0.142]). The fact that the confidence interval does not include zero indicates that the mediating effect is statistically significant. The findings reveal that CD-RISC is a significant mediating variable in the relationship between TWP and PWB. These results support the H1 hypothesis.

Within Model 1, the moderating role of perceived social support (PSS) in the effect of teacher workload (TWP) on psychological resilience (CD-RISC) was examined. Analyses were conducted using Hayes PROCESS Model 1. Findings indicate that the main effect of TWP on psychological resilience is negative and significant (b = − 0.165, p < .01). This indicates that as TWP increases, psychological resilience decreases. In contrast, the main effect of PSS on psychological resilience is positive and significant (b = 0.165, p < .01); that is, as perceived social support increases, individuals’ psychological resilience levels rise. The most critical finding is that the TWP × PSS interaction term is significant (b = 0.005, p < .01). This indicates that perceived social support moderates the relationship between TWP and psychological resilience. The model’s explanatory level is R² = 0.282, indicating that the model explains 28.2% of the variance in psychological resilience. When examining simple slope analyses: At low PSS levels, the effect of TWP on psychological resilience is strong and negative (b = − 0.241, p < .01); at moderate PSS levels, the negative effect persists but weakens (b = − 0.150, p < .01), and finally, at high PSS levels, the effect of TWP on psychological resilience becomes statistically insignificant (b = − 0.079, 95% CI [− 0.184, 0.025]). These findings indicate that as perceived social support increases, the negative effect of TWP on psychological resilience weakens. In other words, a high level of social support can be considered a contextual resource associated with the weakening of the aforementioned negative relationship between TWP and psychological resilience. Therefore, the H2 hypothesis is supported. Details of the findings are presented in Table 4 and Fig. 6.

The significant moderated mediation index (b = 0.004, 95% CI [0.001, 0.008]) indicates that perceived social support moderates this indirect effect (see Table 4). According to the regression analysis, the indirect effect is negative and significant at the Low PSS level (b = − 0.225, 95% CI [− 0.306, − 0.149]). At medium PSS levels, the indirect effect remains significant but weakens (b = − 0.140, 95% CI [− 0.221, − 0.064]). In contrast, at high PSS levels, the indirect effect becomes statistically insignificant (b = − 0.074, 95% CI [− 0.179, 0.026]) (see Table 4). The fact that the confidence interval does not include zero indicates that the indirect effect varies systematically with the perceived level of social support. This finding shows that the mediating mechanism is not fixed but is conditioned by social support, which is a contextual resource. The results show that as perceived social support increases, the negative indirect effect of TWP on psychological well-being gradually decreases and disappears at high levels of social support. In other words, while TWP weakens psychological well-being more strongly in conditions of low social support, high social support acts as a protective resource that buffers this negative effect. Consequently, it was determined that the effect of TWP on psychological well-being varies with the level of social support and that this contextual factor mediates the process. Thus, the H3 hypothesis is supported. The results of the slope analysis are presented in Fig. 7.

Discussion

This study examined the effects of physical education and sports teachers’ perceived workload, psychological resilience, and perceived social support levels on psychological well-being. The findings revealed a significant and negative relationship between teacher workload and psychological well-being. As workload increases, psychological well-being levels decrease. Furthermore, psychological resilience serves as an important mediating factor in the relationship between workload and psychological well-being. In contrast, perceived social support plays a moderator role both on psychological resilience and in the indirect effect process. These findings indicate that teachers need not only individual resilience mechanisms but also social support resources to cope with stressful and demanding work conditions. High workload and occupational stress are consistently reported in the literature as negatively affecting teachers’ psychological well-being and increasing their intention to leave the profession [23, 24, 68]. This study demonstrates that these relationships are shaped by psychological resilience and social support. Excessive lesson load, administrative pressure, and lack of communication among staff are key contributors to work-related stress, while feelings of low achievement are associated with perceived personal inadequacy, high expectations, and unfair competition. Emotional exhaustion, in particular, is primarily driven by stress. These findings highlight the need to improve teachers’ coping strategies and working conditions. In this regard, providing professional support, balancing workload, and strengthening psychological support mechanisms may reduce burnout and enable teachers to guide students more effectively [69]. Moreover, psychological resilience has been identified as a critical factor that protects teachers from burnout and supports more effective coping with stress under increasing emotional demands, role ambiguity, and heavy workloads [70].

The first hypothesis of this study proposes that the negative effect of teacher workload on psychological well-being occurs indirectly through psychological resilience. The findings indicate that this relationship is significant. It was observed that high workloads weakened teachers’ psychological well-being, and this effect varied depending on their level of psychological resilience. These findings reveal that the relationship between teachers’ intense job demands and psychological health indicators cannot be limited to direct effects alone; personal resources also shape this relationship.

Theoretically, the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) Model states that an imbalance between job demands and individual resources leads to negative outcomes in terms of stress and well-being. It is emphasized that high demands, such as workload, rapidly deplete psychological energy and lead to a decline in well-being when sufficient resources are not provided [23, 71]. Therefore, when teachers experience high workloads, job demands strain their psychological resources, and this process can exert negative pressure on well-being through psychological resilience.

International empirical studies supporting the JD-R model have shown that when teachers face heavy workloads and complex tasks, their burnout increases and their psychological well-being decreases [72–74]. Research conducted among Australian teachers also found that workload is significantly associated with psychological distress [2]. Such findings provide evidence of a direct relationship between workload and psychological well-being.

Furthermore, other studies on the relationship between teacher resilience and psychological well-being have shown that psychological resilience acts as a personal resource. The literature indicates that teachers with high psychological resilience are more adaptable in the face of stress and report higher levels of psychological well-being [75, 76]. In this context, psychological resilience is considered a critical factor in maintaining psychological well-being for teachers in challenging working conditions. On the other hand, the literature also indicates that workload has complex influences on teachers’ psychological well-being. A high workload not only directly leads to psychological deterioration among teachers but can also deplete social and emotional resources. This situation not only reduces teachers’ subjective well-being but also negatively affects their professional retention [77]. In this context, personal resources, such as psychological resilience, serve as important buffers that can mitigate these negative effects, as predicted by the JD-R model.

In conclusion, the findings of H1 show not only the direct relationship between workload and psychological well-being, but also how this relationship is shaped by a mediating process involving psychological resilience. This is consistent with Resilience Theory, the JD-R model, and the protective factors framework, and provides a strong theoretical and empirical contribution to the literature on teacher psychology. However, these findings should be interpreted in light of the cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference and leaves open the possibility of reverse causality or omitted variable influences such as organizational climate or leadership style.

The findings indicate that higher psychological resilience among teachers is significantly associated with lower perceived job stress and lower levels of emotional exhaustion. In addition, perceived job stress fully mediates the relationship between psychological resilience and emotional exhaustion, highlighting the critical role of workload-related stress processes in the development of burnout. These results emphasize that psychological resilience is an important protective factor against teachers’ vulnerability to burnout under increasing workload and occupational pressures, and they underline the need for interventions aimed at strengthening resilience in educational settings [78]. Similarly, perceived administrative support significantly predicts both teachers’ psychological resilience and job performance, and psychological resilience mediates the relationship between administrative support and teacher performance. This finding suggests that support from school administration strengthens teachers’ capacity to cope with workload and job demands, thereby indirectly enhancing their performance [79]. Furthermore, significant positive relationships were found among teachers between perceived social support, coping strategies, and psychological well-being. In particular, evaluation support from colleagues and administrators was positively associated with both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping strategies, which in turn contributed to higher levels of psychological well-being [80].

Regarding Hypothesis 2, the results revealed that perceived social support moderates the relationship between teacher workload and psychological resilience. Specifically, the negative effect of workload on psychological resilience is weakened among teachers with higher levels of perceived social support. This indicates that, in addition to individual psychological resources, support from the school environment plays a crucial role in helping teachers cope with high workload, administrative pressure, and work-related stress. These findings are consistent with social support and stress-buffering theories, which propose that social support reduces the psychological impact of stress arising from demanding job conditions [16].

Studies in education show that the relationship between high workload and psychological well-being varies depending on teachers’ perceptions of organizational or social support [74]. When teachers face negative psychological consequences related to workload, having a strong social support network helps them maintain psychological resilience. Similarly, a comprehensive study found positive relationships between organizational support and teachers’ psychological well-being, while demands, such as workload, weakened these relationships [74]. This study revealed that social support has a direct positive effect on psychological well-being and conditions the interaction between workload and resilience. Furthermore, other studies have reported that perceived social support can reduce the negative effects of workload by increasing teachers’ professional resilience [81].

These findings support the notion that social support not only enhances well-being but also serves as a protective contextual resource for psychological resilience processes during periods of high workload [82].

Therefore, the findings of H2 indicate that perceived social support in educational settings can weaken the negative relationship between workload and psychological resilience, thereby exerting a critical moderating effect on teachers’ resilience. This result highlights the need for educational policies to include not only programs that develop individual resilience but also mechanisms to strengthen social and organizational support. Nevertheless, alternative explanations such as school leadership quality or institutional support climate may also contribute to the observed moderating effect of social support.

Research findings indicate that the indirect effect of teacher workload on psychological well-being occurs through psychological resilience and that this process is significantly moderated by perceived social support. Specifically, while the negative effect of workload on psychological well-being is pronounced among teachers with low levels of social support, this effect is significantly reduced or eliminated among those with high levels of social support. This result reinforces the mechanisms of interaction between social support and psychological resilience described in the literature [71, 75]. Theoretically, these findings can be interpreted within the framework of the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory and the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model [23, 24]. The COR theory proposes that individuals’ efforts to conserve and increase their resources in the face of stress are the fundamental mechanisms that determine psychological well-being. In this context, perceived social support serves as a critical psychological resource for teachers, and the negative effects of workload are buffered at high levels of social support. Similarly, the JD-R model indicates that demand factors such as workload can negatively affect psychological well-being. Still, these effects can be mitigated through resources (e.g., social support and resilience). These models strengthen the theoretical foundations of the findings and provide a robust framework for explaining the interactive role of social support and psychological resilience.

Empirically, recent studies in the educational context also present similar results. Wang (2024) confirmed the direct and indirect effects of teachers’ workload and organizational support on psychological well-being, and demonstrated that the social support factor moderates these effects [74]. Similarly, studies by Portelada et al. and El Alaiki et al. reveal that social support is a critical contextual resource in mitigating the negative psychological effects of workload [71, 73]. Furthermore, the study by Schoeps et al. hows that teachers’ psychological resilience is more effective in maintaining their psychological well-being under conditions of high social support [75]. These findings suggest that strengthening social support mechanisms in the educational environment can both increase teachers’ psychological resilience and reduce the negative effects of workload. Regarding educational quality, it is reported that weakening relationships between teachers and students with behavioral problems may negatively affect students’ academic adjustment by increasing their internalizing and externalizing behaviors [83]. At the same time, it is stated that the trust and support that teachers perceive from school management strengthen their ties to the organization and positively affect their performance [84]. For this reason, it is considered that teachers need a strong psychological structure.

Corbett and colleagues (2024b) state that high workloads increase teachers’ likelihood of experiencing health problems and that this situation needs to be addressed at the policy level [85]. It is stated that interventions to be developed should include social interaction, be integrable into teachers’ routines, and be designed in collaboration with teachers and school leaders [86], which will increase the effectiveness of the programs and the overall well-being of teachers [87, 88]. Therefore, school administrators and policymakers should develop strategies to strengthen teachers’ social support networks and prioritize resilience-building interventions such as cognitive-behavioral stress management workshops, peer mentoring systems, and structured school-based supervision programs. Furthermore, these findings suggest that interventions may be more effective at the individual and contextual levels when psychological resilience and social support are assessed together.

In conclusion, the relationship between physical education and sports teachers’ workload and psychological well-being is not only a direct effect but is also indirectly shaped through psychological resilience. Perceived social support moderates this process, contributing to a reduction in negative effects among teachers who receive high levels of support. This finding highlights the critical role that teachers’ psychological resilience and social support resources play in mitigating the negative effects of workload. Therefore, future studies should consider longitudinal designs to better establish temporal ordering and reduce potential bias from unobserved confounding variables.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

Strengths

The main strength of this study is that it addresses the complex relationship between teacher workload, psychological resilience, and perceived social support within an integrated model. The research examines the effects of workload on psychological well-being not only directly but also indirectly through psychological resilience and with moderating effects depending on the level of social support, offering a process- and context-oriented perspective. This approach explains the interaction between job demands and individual resources within the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) Model framework. It highlights the importance of individuals’ efforts to conserve and enhance their resources for psychological well-being, as outlined by the Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory. The rigorously validated validity and reliability values of the measurement tools used enhance the scientific credibility of the findings and ensure that the results are based on a solid foundation. Furthermore, bootstrap-based regression and moderator-mediation analyses enable the reliable examination of complex effect mechanisms and strengthen the explanatory power of the findings. Adequate sample size and statistical power analyses support the model’s consistency and the significance of the results, indicating that the study both theoretically examines the interaction between psychological resilience, social support, and workload, and at an applied level comprehensively evaluates the factors affecting teachers’ psychological well-being.

Limitations

The study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design constitutes one of the limitations of this study. Specifically, this design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships among variables and does not allow for the examination of temporal changes in the relationships between workload, psychological resilience, and psychological well-being over time. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Since the sample consisted only of physical education and sports teachers working in public schools in Turkey, generalizability beyond the cultural and institutional context is limited. Furthermore, the use of self-report scales raises the possibility that social desirability bias or response bias may have affected the data. In addition, the study examined potential interactions between variables, such as psychological resilience and perceived social support, solely using quantitative data, thereby limiting deeper insights that could be obtained through qualitative approaches. Moreover, perceived social support was treated as a global construct rather than being differentiated into its sub-dimensions (family, friends, and significant others), which may restrict a more nuanced understanding of specific sources of support. Finally, the potential influence of unmeasured contextual variables such as school leadership, organizational climate, and teacher autonomy was not included in the model, which may limit the explanatory scope of the findings.

Future directions

Future studies offer several suggestions for examining the relationships between workload and psychological well-being more comprehensively and with stronger causal evidence; however, these directions should be considered within a more integrated framework that combines individual, organizational, and contextual factors.

Longitudinal and experimental designs: Studies tracking changes in workload and psychological well-being over time can reveal causal relationships. For example, measuring teachers’ workload and psychological resilience levels during specific periods can help observe the effects of seasonal stress.

Cultural and institutional diversity: Studies involving teachers from different education systems and geographical contexts will increase the generalizability of the results. Different participant groups, such as private schools, high schools, or teachers from different disciplines, can also be included.

Qualitative data and mixed-methods: In-depth understanding of teachers’ experiences through interviews and focus groups can enable triangulation with quantitative findings and add contextual richness. Additionally, future research may benefit from examining perceived social support across its distinct dimensions (family, friends, and significant others), thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of how these support sources relate to teachers’ psychological well-being.

Intervention studies: It is important to develop research directly applicable to school administrators and policymakers. For example, social support programs, mentoring systems, and stress management training can be tested as interventions that will increase teachers’ psychological resilience and well-being.

Policy recommendations: The Ministry of Education and local governments can develop standard course-load policies, flexible task distribution, and additional support-staff practices to reduce teachers’ workload. Similarly, school administrators can offer regular professional development seminars to strengthen teachers’ social support networks and promote psychological resilience training.

Technology and digital support: Teachers’ access to stress management and social support can be increased through digital platforms. For example, online support groups or psychological support applications can offer innovative solutions that will lighten teachers’ load and strengthen their resilience.

These approaches offer steps that will directly contribute to both the academic literature and practice, ensuring teachers’ psychological well-being.

Conclusion

This study has revealed the critical roles of psychological resilience and perceived social support in the relationship between physical education and sports teachers’ workload and psychological well-being. The findings indicate that high workload can negatively affect teachers’ psychological well-being, but strong psychological resilience and social support resources can buffer this effect. The results guide the development of strategies to support teachers’ psychological well-being at both theoretical and practical levels. Education administrators and policymakers can plan practical interventions such as workload management, support systems, and resilience training based on these findings.
