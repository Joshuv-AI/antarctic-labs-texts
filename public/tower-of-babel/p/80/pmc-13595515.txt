Association of low-dose intra-arterial plus intravenous tirofiban with improved functional outcome in branch atheromatous disease-related stroke patients with early neurological deterioration
Authors: Xinxiu Liu, Miao Wu, Zhiyong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManagement of early neurological deterioration (END) in branch atheromatous disease (BAD)-related stroke remains challenging, and intravenous (IV) tirofiban’s efficacy is limited. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of low-dose intra-arterial (IA) combined with IV tirofiban in BAD-related stroke patients experiencing END.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients with BAD-related stroke who experienced END and were treated with tirofiban between May 2020 and December 2024. Based on the administration route of tirofiban, patients were divided into IA + IV (n = 45) or IV (n = 119) groups. The primary efficacy outcome was the proportion with an excellent functional outcome (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score 0–1) at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included favorable functional outcome (mRS 0–2), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at multiple timepoints, and early neurological improvement (ENI). Safety outcomes comprised symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), any ICH, mortality, and other serious adverse events.ResultsAfter adjustment for four prespecified prognostic factors, the IA + IV group had significantly higher rate of excellent functional outcome compared to the IV group (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 2.99; 95% CI, 1.25–7.16; P = 0.014). This finding was consistent after IPTW adjustment using 10 covariates (OR, 3.68; 95% CI, 1.48–9.14; P = 0.005). The IA + IV group also exhibited significantly lower NIHSS scores at 24 and 72 h and a higher rate of ENI (75.6% vs. 42.9%; adjusted OR, 4.12; 95% CI, 1.85–9.17; P < 0.001). No sICH occurred in either group. The incidence of any ICH (2.2% vs. 0.8%, P = 0.537) and 90-day all-cause mortality (0% vs. 1.7%, P = 0.997) did not differ significantly between IA + IV and IV groups.ConclusionsIn patients with BAD-related stroke experiencing END, adjunctive low-dose IA + IV tirofiban seems associated with higher odds of excellent functional outcome and faster early neurological recovery compared to IV tirofiban alone. The incidence of safety-related adverse events in this study was low; however, due to limited statistical power, the true safety profile requires further validation. Given the limitations of non-randomized design, procedural confounding, and selection bias, further high-level clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Introduction

Accounting for 20.4% of all ischemic strokes in Asian populations, branch atheromatous disease (BAD)-related stroke is characterized by a single subcortical infarct resulting from atherosclerotic stenosis or occlusion at the origin of a penetrating artery, without severe stenosis of the large parent artery [1, 2]. A notable clinical feature of BAD-related stroke is the potential for early neurological deterioration (END) and consequently leading to poor clinical outcomes [3, 4]. Compared to lacunar infarct caused by small vessel occlusion, the rate of END was significantly greater in BAD-related stroke (26.8%–37.5% vs. 6.3%–18.6%) [3, 4].

Currently the management of END in patients with BAD-related stroke remains a clinical challenge. Although intravenous thrombolysis is the cornerstone treatment for acute ischemic stroke within 4.5 h after symptom onset, including those due to BAD. However, some studies found that intravenous thrombolysis appeared to be ineffective in preventing END and improving functional outcomes among patients with BAD-related stroke [5, 6]. The estimated rate of END after intravenous thrombolysis was 13.8% [7]. Dual antiplatelet therapy with clopidogrel and aspirin has been shown to reduce END to some extent; however, its benefits are limited by issues such as pharmacological resistance and practical barriers like dysphagia, which can delay or prevent effective drug administration [8, 9]. Thus, there is a persistent need for effective treatment strategies for END caused by BAD-related stroke.

Tirofiban, a potent antiplatelet agent, inhibits the final common pathway of platelet aggregation by blocking the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor, thereby suppressing microvascular thrombosis and ameliorating cerebral perfusion [10, 11]. Owing to its rapid and potent antiplatelet effects, tirofiban is being used more frequently in acute ischemic stroke management. Several clinical trials have indicated that intravenous (IV) tirofiban can reduce the risk of END or improve neurological outcomes at 3 months in patients with END [12, 13]. Nevertheless, a proportion of END patients with BAD-related stroke still suffer further neurological deterioration even after receiving IV tirofiban [14, 15]. A potential explanation for this is the limited efficacy of the intravenous tirofiban, as some studies suggest its effects are influenced by the route of administration [16]. Compared to intravenous infusion alone, an intra-arterial (IA) bolus injection followed by intravenous maintenance enables tirofiban to reach an effective local concentration more quickly, consequently suppressing microthrombus formation more effectively and elevating the level of cerebral perfusion [17]. This combined approach has demonstrated promising results in stroke patients receiving endovascular treatment (EVT), without significantly increasing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) [16, 18, 19].

Nevertheless, the value of intra-arterial plus intravenous tirofiban specifically in patients with BAD-related stroke experiencing END has not been sufficiently studied. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of IA + IV tirofiban administration versus IV tirofiban alone in patients with BAD-related stroke who experience END.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) receipt of intravenous thrombolysis; (2) END caused by hemorrhagic transformation, metabolic disturbances, or malignant edema; (3) presence of ≥ 50% tandem stenosis in the ipsilateral extracranial artery; (4) receipt of other cerebrovascular interventions (e.g., angioplasty, thrombectomy, coil embolization) or additional treatments beyond standard medical management (e.g., remote ischemic conditioning) other than rehabilitation during hospitalization; (5) a pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS, ranging from 0 [no symptoms] to 6 [death]) score ≥ 2; (6) diagnosis of malignancy with an anticipated survival of less than 3 months; (7) absence of essential clinical, laboratory, or neuroimaging data required for analysis.

Patients were divided into two groups based on the tirofiban administration route: the IA + IV group (n = 45) with intra-arterial infusion via microcatheter during DSA followed by continuous IV infusion, and the IV group (n = 119) with intravenous infusion alone.

This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Geriatric Hospital (IRB No. BJLNYY-2021-011) and was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for data collection was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Furthermore, explicit consent for tirofiban use was documented before treatment initiation.

Tirofiban administration

All patients received standard medical management in accordance with the Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute ischemic stroke. Unless contraindicated, they underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), MRA, and CTA, and BAD-related stroke was diagnosed based on the aforementioned criteria. Upon the occurrence of END, an emergent non-contrast head computed tomography (CT) scan was immediately performed for all patients to exclude intracranial hemorrhage or malignant cerebral edema as the cause of neurological worsening.

At our institution, tirofiban is considered a rescue therapy for patients with BAD-related stroke who experience END, and was administered as early as possible within 24 h of END onset. The decision regarding the administration route (IA + IV versus IV alone) was made by the attending neurologist and a neurointerventionist, based on a comprehensive clinical assessment. Generally, IA + IV tirofiban was selected for patients who presented with rapid clinical progression, exhibited relatively severe symptoms following END, had no contraindications to endovascular intervention, and who (along with their families) consented to the procedure.

The specific tirofiban (Lunan Beite Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shandong, China; Specification: 50 mL/12.5 mg) administration protocols were as follows: Patients in the IA + IV group first underwent diagnostic DSA to confirm the absence of significant parent artery stenosis or occlusion, with non-visualization or poor visualization of the culprit penetrating artery. Subsequently, after establishing satisfactory interventional access, a microcatheter was advanced under microguidewire guidance. To ensure effective intra-arterial infusion, the microcatheter tip was generally positioned within 2 mm proximal to the parent artery of the culprit perforating artery. For example, in patients with anterior circulation stroke predominantly involving the basal ganglia, the presumed culprit vessel is the lenticulostriate artery arising from the middle cerebral artery. In such cases, the microcatheter tip was placed at the proximal M1 segment, within 2 mm distal to the bifurcation of the terminal internal carotid artery. Following microguidewire removal, an intra-arterial bolus of tirofiban was administered through the microcatheter at a dose of 0.25–0.5 mg (median dose 0.5 mg [IQR, 0.4–0.5 mg]), infused at a rate of approximately 50 µg/min. The IA dose was selected based on dose-related safety and efficacy evidence from prior studies [18, 23], with a target of approximately 6.7 µg/kg. Patients weighing > 75 kg received a fixed dose of 0.5 mg, while those weighing ≤ 75 kg received a weight-adjusted dose calculated at 6.7 µg/kg. Upon completion of the IA bolus and the angiographic procedure, a continuous intravenous infusion of tirofiban was maintained at a rate of 0.1 µg/(kg·min) for a duration of 48 h. Patients in the IV group received tirofiban via the intravenous route alone, with a loading dose of 0.4 µg/(kg·min) for 30 min, followed by a continuous maintenance infusion at a rate of 0.1 µg/(kg·min) for 47.5 h. For all patients, dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin (100 mg daily) and clopidogrel (75 mg daily) was initiated or resumed 4 h prior to the discontinuation of the tirofiban. This oral regimen was maintained until day 21 after symptom onset and then switched to single antiplatelet therapy. All patients also received concomitant statin therapy and other supportive treatments as per guideline recommendations.

Data collection and assessment

We retrospectively abstracted baseline characteristic data from the electronic medical records of all enrolled patients, including age, sex, medical history, lifestyle factors, and clinical and laboratory parameters at admission. Pre-stroke mRS and NIHSS scores at admission, at END, and at multiple timepoints after tirofiban were also documented. Additionally, we collected information on mRS scores at 90 days, sICH, any ICH, mortality, ischemic stroke recurrence, and other significant adverse events that occurred within 90 days.

The primary efficacy outcome was the proportion of patients achieving an excellent functional outcome (mRS score of 0–1) at 90 days. The secondary efficacy outcomes included (1) the ratio of patients with a favorable functional outcome (mRS score of 0–2) at 90 days; (2) the distribution of mRS score at 90 days; (3) the NIHSS score at 24 h, 72 h, 7 days, and 14 days following the initiation of tirofiban administration; and (4) early neurological improvement (ENI, defined as a reduction of ≥ 2 points in NIHSS from the score at END to the score at 7 days after tirofiban initiation) [15, 22].

Safety outcomes were rigorously evaluated and comprised the occurrence of symptomatic ICH ( assessed using European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study III classification [24]) and any ICH within 72 h after tirofiban, 90-day all-cause mortality, severe systemic bleeding events, ischemic stroke recurrence within 90 days (defined as acute onset of a new focal neurologic deficit accompanied by clinical or radiographic confirmation of a fresh cerebral infarct, which lasts for at least 24 h and cannot be explained by non-ischemic etiologies, such as intracranial infection, trauma, neoplasm, epileptic seizure, or progressive neurodegenerative disease [25]), and other serious adverse events including pneumonia, myocardial infarction, and deep vein thrombosis within 90 days.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or as median with interquartile range (IQR) for skewed distributions. Between-group comparisons for these variables were performed using Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and group differences were assessed with the chi-square test or, where appropriate, Fisher’s exact test.

For the primary and secondary binary outcomes (including the proportion of patients with mRS scores of 0–1 and 0–2 at 90 days, and early neurological improvement), as well as safety outcomes, binary logistic regression was used. Treatment effects are expressed as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). For the adjusted analyses, the models incorporated four prespecified prognostic factors: age, sex, history of prior cerebral infarction, and the NIHSS score at END. As the NIHSS scores at 24 h, 72 h, 7 days, and 14 days were non-normally distributed, unadjusted and adjusted analyses were performed using a generalized linear model with the Gamma distribution and log-link function. Results are presented as geometric mean ratios (GMR) with 95% CIs. An ordinal logistic regression model was applied to analyze the distribution of the 90-day mRS scores after verifying the proportional odds assumption via the Brant test, findings are reported as a common odds ratio.

To address potential selection bias, we performed inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) as a sensitivity analysis. Stabilized weights were calculated using a propensity score model that included 10 prespecified covariates: age, sex, NIHSS at END, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, prior stroke, infarct location, time from onset to END, and time from END to tirofiban initiation. Weights were truncated at the 99th percentile to reduce the influence of extreme values. Survey-weighted generalized linear models were used for IPTW-adjusted outcome analyses, with ORs for binary outcomes and GMRs for NIHSS scores.

A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for descriptive analyses and multivariable regression. R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for the IPTW sensitivity analysis.

Results

Between May 2020 and December 2024, we initially identified 653 consecutive patients with acute ischemic stroke who received tirofiban at our hospital. Among these, 196 patients met the diagnostic criteria for BAD–related stroke and experienced END. Of these 196 patients, 52 received combined intra-arterial plus intravenous tirofiban (IA + IV group), and 144 received intravenous tirofiban alone (IV group). After applying the predefined exclusion criteria, 7 patients in the IA + IV group and 25 patients in the IV group were excluded. Consequently, a final cohort of 164 patients (45 in the IA + IV group and 119 in the IV group) was included in the analysis. Efficacy outcomes

The efficacy outcomes for the two groups are summarized in Table 2. For the primary outcome of achieving an mRS score of 0–1 at 90 days, the IA + IV group showed a higher proportion (57.8%) compared with the IV group (46.2%). While the unadjusted analysis did not show a statistically significant difference (OR, 1.59; 95% CI, 0.80–3.18; P = 0.188), after adjustment for prespecified prognostic factors, the IA + IV group demonstrated a significantly higher likelihood of achieving an excellent functional outcome (adjusted OR, 2.99; 95% CI, 1.25–7.16; P = 0.014).

In terms of favorable functional outcome (mRS 0–2 at 90 days), the proportions were similar between the IA + IV and IV groups (77.8% vs. 76.5%). Since the proportional odds assumption was violated in the multivariable ordinal logistic regression model (Brant test, P < 0.001), the common odds ratio was not reported (Table 2).

Serial assessments of neurological severity measured by NIHSS score at 24 h, 72 h, 7 days, and 14 days after tirofiban administration showed consistent trends. After adjustment, the IA + IV group exhibited significantly lower NIHSS scores at 24 h (adjusted GMR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.77–0.98; P = 0.021) and 72 h (adjusted GMR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.70–0.98; P = 0.029) compared with the IV group, suggesting a more rapid neurological recovery in the combined treatment group. At 14 days, a borderline significant reduction was observed in the IA + IV group (adjusted GMR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.69–1.00; P = 0.050), while no significant difference was noted at 7 days (P = 0.177). Early neurological improvement was significantly more frequent in the IA + IV group than in the IV group (75.6% vs. 42.9%). This difference remained statistically significant in both unadjusted (OR, 4.12; 95% CI, 1.91–8.91; P < 0.001) and adjusted analyses (adjusted OR, 4.12; 95% CI, 1.85–9.17; P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

IPTW sensitivity analysis yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Table 2). After weighting, the IA + IV group remained associated with significantly higher odds of excellent functional outcome (OR, 3.68; 95% CI, 1.48–9.14; P = 0.005) and early neurological improvement (OR, 4.48; 95% CI, 1.57–12.80; P = 0.005). The IPTW-adjusted GMRs for NIHSS scores at 72 h, 7 days, and 14 days also favored the IA + IV group (P < 0.05).

No severe systemic bleeding events were reported in either group (Table 3).

Discussion

Our study revealed that, for patients with BAD-related stroke experiencing END, low-dose intra-arterial plus intravenous tirofiban was associated with higher odds of excellent functional outcome at 3 months and early neurological recovery compared with intravenous tirofiban. Additionally, the incidence of safety-related adverse events was low. Despite limited statistical power for safety endpoints, this study provides preliminary exploratory experience for future research.

Previous studies have indicated that the route of tirofiban administration significantly influences its clinical efficacy. In patients with acute ischemic stroke following EVT, several investigations have provided evidence supporting the IA + IV tirofiban regimen. For instance, studies by Zhao et al. and Guo et al. demonstrated that only the IA + IV tirofiban—not IV tirofiban alone or no tirofiban—was associated with improved 3-month functional outcomes and lower 3-month mortality without an observed increase in the risk of ICH [18, 26]. Favorable findings regarding this approach are not confined to anterior circulation stroke. Findings from the BASILAR Registry further indicated that, in patients with basilar artery occlusion after successful recanalization, IA + IV tirofiban was associated with better clinical outcomes and no higher frequency of sICH compared to IV tirofiban alone [19]. A recent meta-analysis published in 2024 also concluded that the administration of intra-arterial combined with postoperative maintenance doses of intravenous tirofiban is an effective and safe treatment strategy for AIS after EVT [27].

However, all previous studies focused on patients with large vessel occlusion stroke receiving EVT, and to date, no research has specifically investigated IA tirofiban administration in patients with BAD-related stroke experiencing END. To our knowledge, this study represents one of the first attempts to evaluate the efficacy of IA + IV tirofiban in this specific patient population. Our findings therefore provide preliminary exploratory experience and new insights into the management of this clinically challenging stroke subtype.

Mechanically, cerebral infarction caused by BAD, particularly when accompanied by END, primarily involves unstable atherosclerotic plaque at the orifice of the penetrating artery, microthrombus formation or propagation, and subsequent downstream microcirculatory hypoperfusion [28–30]. With intravenous tirofiban alone, systemic dilution may limit the rapid attainment of an effective drug concentration to inhibit platelet aggregation at the lesion site, potentially resulting in delayed therapeutic onset. In contrast, an intra-arterial bolus injection may boost local drug concentration more quickly and give tirofiban direct contact with the thrombus to prevent subsequent platelet aggregation, which may enhance microcirculation perfusion. Coupled with subsequent intravenous tirofiban maintenance infusion, this IA + IV regimen could theoretically better stabilize vulnerable plaques at the arterial orifice and prevent microthrombus proliferation and extension, potentially inhibiting the progression of END. Our findings, which indicate a trend toward greater NIHSS score improvement at 24 h and a higher rate of ENI in the IA + IV group, appear consistent with this proposed mechanistic hypothesis.

Regarding safety outcomes, the incidence of sICH, any ICH, and mortality was low (zero or near-zero) in both groups in our study, with no statistically significant differences. However, some prior studies suggested an association between IA tirofiban and higher rates of ICH and death in stroke patients following EVT [23, 31]. For instance, Wu et al. [23] found a dose-dependent effect of IA tirofiban on ICH. His study demonstrated that a full dose of IA tirofiban (10 µg/kg) increases the risk of major ICH after EVT for acute ischemic stroke. Similarly, Yang et al. [31], who also employed a 10 µg/kg dose, reported that IA tirofiban as an adjunct to EVT was associated with elevated rates of ICH and mortality. In contrast, many other studies [18, 19, 32] indicated lower-dose IA tirofiban was safe and did not increase the risk of ICH. The IA bolus dose used in our study was also low (0.25–0.5 mg, median dose 0.5 mg [IQR, 0.4–0.5 mg]), which may be related to the low observed incidence of bleeding events. Additionally, the increased risk of ICH associated with IA tirofiban post-EVT reported in some studies may be attributed to endothelial injury induced by the procedural manipulation or to hyperperfusion injury after recanalization. Finally, our study population consisted of patients with BAD-related stroke, who typically present with smaller infarct volumes and may have a relatively lower baseline risk of hemorrhage compared to those with large-territory infarctions or cardioembolic stroke. Nevertheless, due to the low event rates and small sample size, this study was underpowered for safety outcomes. Larger, adequately powered studies are needed to further evaluate the safety of low-dose IA + IV tirofiban in this patient population.

Notably, after multivariable adjustment, the IA + IV group was associated with a significantly higher proportion of excellent functional outcome (mRS 0–1) compared with the IV group, while no significant difference was observed for functional independence (mRS 0–2). This discrepancy is clinically noteworthy. One potential explanation is that IA + IV tirofiban may selectively enhance recovery in a specific patient subgroup, enabling them to move from mRS 2 (slight disability) to mRS 1 (no clinically significant disability), without substantially shifting the broader proportion achieving functional independence (mRS ≤ 2). This would suggest a concentrated effect at the upper end of the recovery spectrum rather than a general shift in the outcome distribution. Alternatively, this discordance may be explained by statistical instability due to the relatively small sample size, particularly in the IA + IV group. Furthermore, as noted in the statistical analysis section, the proportional odds assumption for the ordinal logistic regression model was violated (Brant test, P < 0.001), which may have affected the reliability of the mRS 0–2 comparison. Therefore, these findings require validation in larger prospective studies.

In our study, the time from END to tirofiban initiation was substantially longer in the IA + IV group than in the IV group. Although IPTW was performed, this time remained imbalanced after weighting, reflecting the inherent treatment delay associated with DSA preparation in the IA + IV group. This delay would bias the results against the IA + IV group, potentially underestimating the true treatment benefit. Nevertheless, the IA + IV group remained associated with better functional outcomes, suggesting that even more favorable results might be observed if the intra-arterial approach could be initiated as promptly as intravenous therapy. Furthermore, despite IPTW adjustment, some imbalance persisted for atrial fibrillation, and therefore the results should be interpreted with this in mind.

Additionally, it is important to acknowledge that the observed difference in outcomes may reflect not only intra-arterial tirofiban delivery but also the composite effect of the interventional strategy, as the two groups may have experienced two fundamentally different clinical pathways. Patients in the IV group received intravenous tirofiban and standard medical management, whereas those in the IA + IV group underwent diagnostic DSA, microcatheterization, operator-dependent procedural decisions, and more intensive periprocedural monitoring—all of which are integral components of the sequential intra-arterial plus intravenous tirofiban administration. Thus, the IA + IV strategy should be viewed as a comprehensive therapeutic approach rather than merely a different route of drug administration. This structural confounding cannot be fully addressed by statistical adjustment. Therefore, more rigorous randomized controlled trials are needed in the future.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, non-randomized observational study in which treatment allocation was determined by clinician discretion, introducing potential selection bias. The direction of this bias is uncertain: patients selected for IA + IV may have had more severe neurological deficits (biasing against the intervention) but also tended to be younger with fewer comorbidities (biasing in favor). Additionally, the two groups followed fundamentally different clinical pathways (interventional versus medical management), representing structural confounding that cannot be fully resolved by statistical adjustment. Another potential source of confounding is the variability in the IA tirofiban dose, which may introduce heterogeneity in the treatment effect. Furthermore, outcome assessment was not blinded, which may have introduced detection bias.

Second, the sample size of this study is relatively small, particularly in the IA + IV group, resulting in limited statistical power. The adjusted logistic regression model was potentially unstable due to the limited number of outcome events. Moreover, the incidence of safety outcomes, including sICH, any ICH, and mortality, was zero or near-zero in both groups, resulting in extremely wide confidence intervals; thus, the study was underpowered for safety outcomes.

Third, as patients who received intravenous thrombolysis were excluded from this study, our findings may not apply to those receiving concomitant intravenous thrombolysis. The safety and efficacy of IA + IV tirofiban in thrombolysed patients remain unknown, as the concomitant use of thrombolytics may alter bleeding risk. Further studies are needed to address this population. Additionally, all patients included in our study were Chinese, with a relatively high incidence of BAD compared to Western cohorts [33], limiting generalizability to other ethnic groups. This was also a single-center study, and institutional variations in procedural expertise, patient selection, and monitoring protocols may further limit the applicability of our findings to other centers. Therefore, large-scale, multicenter, randomized, controlled, blinded trials are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In patients with BAD-related stroke experiencing END, adjunctive low-dose IA + IV tirofiban seems associated with higher odds of excellent functional outcome and more rapid early neurological improvement compared to IV administration alone. The incidence of safety-related adverse events in this study was low; however, due to limited statistical power, the true safety profile requires further validation. Given the limitations of this study, further high-level clinical studies are warranted in the future.
