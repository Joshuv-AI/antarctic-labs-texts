Differential Expression of Galectin‐3, Caspase‐1, and Phosphorylated P53: Diagnostic Biomarkers for Basal Cell Carcinoma: A Case‐Control Study
Authors: Mahsa Niamoradi, Fatemeh Babajani, Seyed Askar Roghani, Mohammad Taghi Goodarzi, Soheila Asadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsBasal cell carcinoma (BCC) is a common form of skin cancer. By considering the role of Galectin‐3 (Gal‐3), Caspase‐1, and Phosphorylated p53 (pP53), they might have an essential role in BCC development. This study aimed to examine the genes and their corresponding proteins in the tumor tissue of patients with BCC and compare them to those in the tumor margins. Furthermore, their potential roles as biomarkers were evaluated.Materials and MethodsThe gene expression of Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 in 25 tumor tissues and 25 tumor margin tissues of BCC subjects was analyzed by Real‐Time PCR. The western blot was applied to assess Gal‐3, Caspase‐1, and pP53; moreover, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was utilized for the determination of these genes' RNA levels as potential biomarkers.ResultsOur findings indicated the increased Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 gene expression in tumor tissues, compared to the tumor margin tissue in BCC. Gal‐3, Caspase‐1, and pP53 protein expression in tumor tissues had a significant elevation, compared to tumor margin tissue samples. The result of ROC analysis showed that the AUC for Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 were 0.803 (sensitivity: 95%, specificity: 65%, p = 0.001) and 0.812 (sensitivity: 95%, specificity: 70%, p < 0.001), respectively.ConclusionThe upregulation of Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 and downregulation of P53 suggest these genes and proteins play a crucial role in the tumor microenvironment, and based on our findings, they can be considered potential biomarkers. Additionally, these genes and proteins may be regarded as therapeutic targets based on future functional studies.

Introduction

Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC) is a widespread type of skin malignancy, accounting for a significant share of all skin disorders, and its incidence is steadily increasing worldwide [1]. One of the most important proteins involved in BCC is Gal‐3, which was first recognized as a protein linked to cancer and metastasis [2]. Gal‐3, which functions as a carbohydrate‐binding protein, is expressed in normal and tumor cells. Moreover, it was shown that Gal‐3 is involved in cell adhesion, cell differentiation, cellular proliferation, tumor advancement, and metastatic dissemination [3]. Gal‐3 is expressed by various cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), including cancer cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells. Upregulation of Gal‐3 can contribute to an immunosuppressive environment, thereby promoting tumor growth and survival [4]. Another molecule that plays an important role is Caspase 1, which participates in the inflammatory process in BCC.

Caspase‐1 is a critical enzyme that contributes to initiating the inflammatory pathway in BCC. In BCC, various stimuli, including ultraviolet (UV) radiation, can trigger this pathway. UV exposure has been shown to activate Caspase‐1, subsequently leading to the cleavage of pro‐inflammatory cytokines into their functional states [5]. However, the function of Caspase‐1 in BCC has not been clearly identified yet.

Another essential factor in this path is pP53, which acts as a critical tumor suppressor, overseeing cell cycle progression, promoting apoptosis, and maintaining genomic stability. In BCC, alterations in P53 expression and phosphorylation status can significantly impact tumor progression [6].

Dysregulation of Gal‐3, Caspase‐1, and pP53in BCC is an area of interest due to its implications for tumor behavior and treatment outcome. Given that the presence of Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 may serve as biomarkers for BCC, monitoring their levels can provide insight into the disease trajectory and response to treatment.

On the other hand, the role of molecular biomarkers in BCC has garnered considerable attention, and studies evaluating various proteins and gene expression profiles involved in tumor initiation and progression have been conducted. For example, alterations in apoptosis‐related proteins and components of the TME are increasingly recognized as critical factors influencing BCC behavior [7]. A study by Khalil et al. investigated the clinicopathological characteristics of BCC in the maxillofacial region, evaluating various sophisticated cytological methods for diagnostic purposes [8]. These techniques included scraping, as well as scraping and printing methods utilizing Papanicolaou and Diff‐Quick stains. The findings indicated that cytological examination is a rapid, cost‐effective, and minimally invasive diagnostic modality. Furthermore, the study reported positive therapeutic and esthetic outcomes associated with the use of a diode laser for treatment [8].

Although BCC is a slow‐growing malignancy with an exceedingly rare metastatic proclivity, it remains one of the most prevalent malignant skin neoplasms, with a notable surge in global incidence. This highlights the critical need for timely diagnosis and treatment. In addition to conventional diagnostic methods (e.g., biopsy), advanced cytological techniques (e.g., scraping cytology) have emerged as rapid, minimally invasive, and cost‐effective alternatives, particularly beneficial for initial diagnosis in outpatient settings. Beyond traditional surgical excision, modern therapeutic approaches have also been explored, including the application of diode lasers (with wavelengths of 940 and 980 nm) for the treatment of BCC. Clinical studies have demonstrated that laser ablation can yield excellent functional and esthetic outcomes while being associated with relatively infrequent complications, making it a highly effective and acceptable alternative to conventional surgical techniques [8, 9].

In addition, further investigation into the differential expression of specific molecules, such as Galectins and Caspases, along with key tumor suppressors (e.g., pP53), is crucial for developing a comprehensive molecular signature for BCC. Such studies contribute to a deeper understanding of the disease's biology and can help fill gaps in diagnostic capabilities. This study aimed to investigate gene and protein expression of Gal‐3, Caspase‐1, and pP53 in tumor tissue of patients with BCC, compared to tumor margin tissue. Moreover, their potential roles were surveyed as biomarkers.

Methods

Patients and Tissue Samples

In this case‐control study, the tissue samples from BCC patients were acquired from the Iran National Tumor Bank, Cancer Institute (Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran). The subjects were selected based on inclusion (patients with BCC) and exclusion (subjects with a history of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and taking anti‐inflammatory drugs) criteria. Just as a pathologist has confirmed all cancerous tissue, all tumor margin tissues have also been confirmed by a pathologist to be noncancerous. Tumor margin tissues were taken from tissues adjacent to the tumor, and a pathologist also confirmed the nontumor nature of these tissues. The 25 tumor tissue samples, as the case group, and 25 tumor margin tissue samples, as the control group, were collected from BCC patients. After that, the tissue samples were immediately stored in liquid nitrogen for gene and protein expression analysis. The demographic characteristics of patients, including gender, history of alcohol consumption and smoking, as well as the clinicopathological characteristics of subjects, including tumor diameter and depth, tumor histology, histology grade, TNM staging, bone and perineural invasion, were recorded.

Ethical Considerations

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Islamic Azad University, Shahrood Branch, Shahrood, Iran (Code: IR.IAU.SHAHROOD.REC.1402.065) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardian(s).

The RNA Extraction, Galectin‐3, and Caspase‐1 Gene Expression

To analyze gene expression, the first step involved extracting RNA from tissue samples using the Kiazol (Kiazist, Iran) solution, following the manufacturer's instructions. The Nano‐Drop spectrophotometer was used to assess the quantity of extracted RNAs, and the optimal range is between 1.8 and 2 ng; our extracted RNA was also in this range. The quality of the extracted RNAs was examined by 1% TAE‐agarose electrophoresis, and our extracted RNAs were suitable in terms of integrity and quality. After that, the complementary DNA was synthesized using 2 µg of total RNA by the Parstous kit (Parstous, Iran). The expression of Gal‐3, Caspase‐1, and β‐actin (as reference) genes was evaluated using SYBR Green Real‐Time PCR Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark) by the Roche Light Cycler 96 System (Roche Life Science Deutschland GmbH, Germany). The list of primers used in the present study is presented in Table 1. The relative RNA level of Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 genes was normalized to the β‐actin gene expression, and fold changes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT formula.

The Protein Extraction and Galectin‐3, Caspase‐1, and pP53 Protein Expression

To extract protein, up to 40 mg of fresh frozen tissues were crushed with liquid nitrogen. Then, the total proteins were extracted by RIPA buffer (containing protease inhibitor cocktail), and the tubes containing extracted proteins were incubated on ice for 15 min. In the next step, the tubes were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 40 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected and stored at −70°C. The total protein level was determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method (BCA protein assay kit, Kiazisht, Iran). In the Western blot assay, the 40 µg of total protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred from the gel to the polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. After transfer, the PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat skimmed milk powder (Sigma Aldrich, USA) in Tris‐buffered saline with Tween‐20 (TBST) buffer for 2 h at room temperature. After blocking, the membranes were washed with TBST buffer (twice) and TBS buffer (once), each time for 10 min. After the washing step, the PVDF membranes were incubated with rabbit anti‐Gal‐3 antibody (ab76245, Abcam, USA), rabbit anti‐Caspase antibody (83383S, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti‐p‐p53 (phospho S392) antibody (ab33889, Abcam, USA), and rabbit anti‐β actin antibody (ab8227, Abcam, USA) overnight at 4°C. Then, the membranes were washed twice with TBST and once with TBS, and after washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (ab6721, Abcam, USA) for 2 h. Ultimately, after three times washing by TBST and TBS, the bands were visualized by BioRad ECL Select Western blot analysis Detection Reagent (BioRad, USA). The β‐actin was used for normalizing protein expression as an internal control, and the Image J software was used for densitometry analysis.

Data Analysis

The data analysis was performed by Prism5.00 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Statistical analyses followed the Guidelines for Reporting of Statistics for Clinical Research in Urology and the SAMPL guidelines to ensure accuracy and transparency in statistical reporting [13]. After assessing the normal distribution of continuous data using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, statistical differences between the studied groups were examined using Student's t‐test (paired t‐test). In contrast, data with non‐normal distribution were assessed using the Whitney U‐test. The data are presented as mean ± SEM or mean ± SD, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, the Prism 5.00 software was used to consider Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 gene expression as potential tumor markers for diagnosing BCC.

The Expression of Galectin‐3 and Caspase‐1 Genes in Subjects With BCC

As presented in Figure 1, the findings of the Real‐Time PCR method showed that the expression of the Gal‐3 gene in tumor tissues of subjects with BCC markedly increased, compared to tumor margin tissues (p < 0.001). Our data showed that the Gal‐3 expression in tumor tissues of subjects had a 4.4‐fold increase, compared to tumor margin tissues. Our observation revealed that the Caspase‐1 gene expression in tumor tissues increased up to 5.33‐fold (p < 0.001), compared to tumor margin tissues of subjects with BCC (Figure 1).

The Protein Expression of Gal‐3, Caspase‐1, and pP53 in Subjects With BCC

The results of Western blot analysis are depicted in Figure 2. Our findings (Figure 2B,C) indicated that protein expression of Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 in tumor tissues of subjects with BCC had a remarkable increase, compared to the tumor margin tissues (p = 0.021, p < 0.001, respectively). On the other hand, our assessment showed that the expression of the phosphorylated form of P53 (pP53) in tumor tissues was markedly (p = 0.047) lower than that of tumor margin tissues in subjects with BCC (Figure 2D).

The Results of ROC Analysis

To assess the potential properties of Gal‐3 (Figure 3A) and Caspase‐1 (Figure 3B) gene expression, the ROC analysis was applied. Our study demonstrated that Gal‐3 had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.803 (95% CI: 0.664–0.943), with a Youden index of 0.6, a sensitivity of 95%, and a specificity of 65% (p = 0.001). Additionally, our data showed that the AUC of Caspase‐1 gene expression was 0.812 (95% CI: 0.672–0.952), with a Youden index of 0.65, sensitivity of 95%, and specificity of 70% (p < 0.001).

Discussion

In the present study, Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 expression in tumor tissue was significantly increased in comparison with tumor margin tissues. This finding aligns with emerging evidence that highlights the crucial involvement of specific molecular players, such as Gal‐3, in orchestrating various aspects of this pathological process, particularly in BCC.

Therefore, understanding the mechanisms behind increased Gal‐3 expression in BCC involves examining its interplay with cellular components, signaling cascades, and the TME. In this regard, in the study conducted by Brustmann et al. [14], it was found that the expression of the Gal‐3 gene was increased in squamous cells of the vulva, compared to the control tissues. Gal‐3 expression is upregulated by various stressors existing in the TME, such as nutrient deficiency and hypoxia. Transcription regulators, such as HIF‐1α and NF‐κB, are inducers of Gal‐3 in these conditions, which consequently allows Gal‐3 to be secreted into the extracellular environment. Gal‐3 may, in turn, interact with glycoproteins or ingredients of the extracellular matrix (ECM) at the cell, which accounts for tumor cell motility and survival [15]. The findings from these investigations aligned with the results of our study; moreover, the TME is rich in inflammatory cytokines and growth factors that can upregulate Gal‐3 expression. For instance, interactions between tumor cells and stromal cells are capable of contributing to heightened Gal‐3 expression. In particular, studies have shown that when cancer cells interact with mesenchymal stem cells or other stromal components, there is a significant upregulation of Gal‐3 expression, suggesting a paracrine signaling mechanism [4, 16]. The pro‐tumorigenic role of Gal‐3 has been attributed to its ability to modulate various cellular processes. For instance, Gal‐3 can promote cell survival by inhibiting pro‐apoptotic pathways, enhance cell migration and invasion through its interactions with ECM components, and facilitate angiogenesis, all of which are critical for tumor progression [15]. In addition, some studies have shown variable expression of Gal‐3 in different subtypes of BCC or at various stages, suggesting its dynamic role in tumor evolution [17, 18]. Our findings of Gal‐3 overexpression in BCC samples strengthen its potential as a general diagnostic indicator for this type of skin cancer.

In parallel, this current investigation found that Gal‐3 can be recognized as a biomarker due to its sensitivity and specificity. The studies also show that elevated levels of Gal‐3 can be correlated with increased tumor growth and metastatic potential. It facilitates cancer cell detachment from the primary tumor site and promotes migration through the regulation of matrix metalloproteinases and other adhesion molecules. Specifically, Gal‐3 has been shown to enhance the expression of integrins and cadherins on tumor cells, which are crucial for their invasive capabilities [4, 19, 20].

In addition, in BCC, Gal‐3 promotes a protumor environment by polarizing macrophages to an M2 phenotype, suppressing T cell immunity, and activating fibroblasts to remodel the ECM. These changes support tumor growth, adhesion, and invasion, facilitating cancer progression [15]. Although the expression data of Gal‐3 in cancer are conflicting, Aureli et al. [21] showed the cytoplasmic reactivity of Gal‐3, as well as the loss of its nuclear expression, in colon carcinoma. Kao et al. [22] reported no significant difference between Gal‐3‐negative and positive tumors regarding age, tumor location, size, and stage. Based on this, it can be concluded that Gal‐3 can have different expression patterns in various tumor types.

Caspase‐1 is crucial for processes within the TME and the advancement of cancer, particularly in basal‐like cancers, although specific studies on caspase‐1 in BCC are limited. Caspase‐1 is crucial for the maturation of IL‐1β, an inflammatory cytokine that promotes tumor‐associated macrophage (TAM) recruitment. In basal‐like cancer, which shares some characteristics with BCC, the absence of estrogen receptors increases Caspase‐1 expression. This elevation facilitates IL‐1β secretion, leading to enhanced macrophage infiltration and tumor progression [23]. After activation by danger signals, Caspase‐1 initiates inflammatory responses that exhibit dual effects within the TME, promoting either anti‐tumor immunity or contributing to immunosuppression and tumor progression. Chronic activation of Caspase‐1 facilitates immune evasion by recruiting immunosuppressive cell populations, such as myeloid‐derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs), which inhibit T cell function and diminish anti‐tumor immune responses [24]. Furthermore, Caspase‐1 drives the polarization of macrophages toward an M2 phenotype, characterized by the secretion of immunosuppressive factors, and impairs the antigen‐presenting capabilities of dendritic cells, thereby weakening T cell activation [25]. This persistent inflammatory milieu can trigger T cell exhaustion, which is identifiable by the elevated expression of inhibitory receptors, such as PD‐1 and CTLA‐4, thereby reducing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, Caspase‐1‐mediated inflammation promotes tissue damage, oxidative stress, and ECM remodeling, all of which are associated with tumor proliferation and early carcinogenesis [26]. Moreover, Caspase‐1 activation by danger signals activates fibroblasts, leading to ECM changes and desmoplasia, and induces pyroptosis, reshaping the TME [27, 28]. In experimental tumor models, Caspase‐1 inhibition through either molecular or pharmacological approaches significantly reduced tumor growth and TAM infiltration. Caspase‐1 thereby not only contributes to tumor development but also to resistance against various immunological therapies, such as anti‐PD1 therapies. In the present study, it was found that Caspase‐1 can be known as a biomarker due to its sensitivity and specificity. Recent articles focus on the role of Caspase‐1 in BCC and tumor progression by means of inflammatory processes and interactions with the TME. In addition to the contents mentioned above in BCC, the interplay between Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 can create a complex environment that influences tumor progression: Gal‐3 may promote an immunosuppressive environment, while Caspase‐1 enhances inflammation. This duality can lead to an environment where the tumor can evade immune detection while still driving some inflammatory processes that may facilitate tumor progression. Both proteins influence ECM remodeling. Gal‐3's adhesion properties and Caspase‐1's role in inflammatory cytokine processing can collectively alter ECM dynamics, promoting tumor cell invasion and survival within the ECM [15, 29, 30]. Our findings, indicating a marked upregulation of Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 genes and protein expression in tumor tissues, compared to tumor margin tissues, are consistent with recent studies. These results confirm the potential role of these molecules in the pathogenesis of BCC. The increased expression of Gal‐3 has been reported in various tumors. It is linked to its involvement in processes, such as cell adhesion, migration, and apoptosis, all of which are key mechanisms in cancer progression. For example, Braeuer et al. performed various analyses on human metastatic melanoma cell lines and mouse models to investigate the role of the protein Gal‐3 in cancer progression. They found that Gal‐3 regulates the expression of a gene called autotaxin by modulating the transcription factor NFAT1. Silencing Gal‐3 led to a reduction in NFAT1 and autotaxin expression, which in turn decreased tumor growth and metastasis in vivo. This study identifies a previously unknown mechanism through which Gal‐3 contributes to melanoma progression. In addition, Aggarwal et al. conducted a study on 60 patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and 30 healthy individuals. They measured the levels of Gal‐1 and Gal‐3 proteins in serum and tumor tissue samples and also examined their effects on OSCC cell lines. The results showed that OSCC patients have significantly higher levels of these two proteins in their bloodstream and at the tumor sites. The researchers concluded that the levels of these proteins could serve as plausible markers for OSCC and may help screen high‐risk populations [31, 32].

Moreover, UV radiation, particularly UVA, is a potent inducer of ROS in skin cells through multiple interconnected pathways. This primarily involves the absorption of UV photons by endogenous chromophores, such as flavins, porphyrins, and NADH, leading to their excitation and subsequent energy transfer to molecular oxygen to form highly reactive species like singlet oxygen [33]. Additionally, UV can activate various enzymatic sources of ROS, including NADPH oxidases and xanthine oxidase, further augmenting ROS levels [34]. UV also damages the DNA of skin cells by producing ROS and causing oxidative stress. This damage leads to mutations in critical genes, including genes that regulate apoptosis (e.g., P53). At the same time, UV causes immunosuppression and chronic inflammation, which provides an environment in which mutated cells can escape the body's defense mechanisms, proliferate, and develop into skin cancer [35]. This oxidative stress causes widespread damage to cellular macromolecules, including DNA (e.g., 8‐oxoguanine), proteins, and lipids, thereby creating pro‐mutagenic lesions and directly initiating and promoting the development of skin cancer [36].

On the other hand, this sustained oxidative burden subsequently triggers chronic inflammation, as ROS activate pro‐inflammatory pathways, such as NF‐κB, resulting in the upregulation of cytokines and chemokines that foster a pro‐tumorigenic microenvironment [37]. Crucially, the accumulation of DNA damage from both direct UV absorption and ROS‐induced oxidative stress leads to mutations in critical genes, particularly tumor suppressor genes, such as P53, which regulates cell cycle arrest and DNA repair. When genes involved in apoptosis (e.g., P53 and Caspase‐1, as explored in our study) are mutated or their functions dysregulated, damaged, and potentially cancerous cells escape programmed cell death, proliferate uncontrollably, and contribute to the development and progression of skin malignancies, thereby framing the complex pathogenesis of skin cancer. The activation of P53 upon UV radiation leads to the transcriptional control of the genes participating in DNA repair mechanisms, particularly nucleotide excision repair (NER). P53 upregulates several genes essential for NER, such as XPC, XPA, and XPD [38]. NER is responsible for recognizing and repairing bulky DNA lesions, including cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers formed by UV light. P53 also induces the expression of p21(WAF1/CIP1), a cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor that enforces G1 arrest, providing cells time for DNA repair before proceeding to the S phase [39, 40, 41]. In addition, phosphorylation of the P53 protein is essential for its function as a tumor suppressor, particularly in the context of BCC. This modification influences P53's stability, activity, and interaction with other proteins, ultimately affecting cellular responses to stress and tumorigenesis. The results obtained from the present study show that the expression of pP53 in tumor tissue is significantly reduced, compared to tumor margin tissue.

The lower levels of phospho‐Ser392 may indicate a reduced ability of P53 to respond to DNA damage or stress signals in BCC cells [42]. Phosphorylation stabilizes P53 in response to diverse stressors, including DNA damage from UV radiation, which is particularly relevant for skin cancers. The stabilization allows P53 to accumulate and exert its tumor‐suppressive functions effectively [43]. Nevertheless, in cases where mutations occur within the P53 gene, the resulting mutant P53 can exhibit gain‐of‐function properties that promote tumor progression rather than suppression. These mutations can interfere with normal P53 functions and alter its phosphorylation dynamics, leading to enhanced survival and proliferation of cancer cells [44]. While alterations within the P53 gene occur frequently in many cancers, including BCC, reduced phosphorylation could represent an alternative mechanism of P53 inactivation even in the absence of direct gene mutation or as a complementary pathway [6, 45]. This finding is critical as it suggests that assessing pP53 levels could provide additional insights into the functional status of the P53 pathway in BCC, beyond just gene sequencing. According to a study by Matsumoto et al. [46], the phosphorylation states of P53 at Ser392 and Ser15 in various skin tumors, including BCC, were evaluated. It was found that phospho‐Ser392‐P53 levels were significantly lower in BCC, compared to other skin lesions, such as Bowen's disease and squamous cell carcinoma. In contrast, Ser15 phosphorylation showed a significant correlation with proliferation markers (Ki‐67) in BCC, suggesting that while overall phosphorylation may be reduced, specific modifications could still influence tumor development. The distinct patterns of pP53 could serve as potential biomarkers for assessing tumor aggressiveness and guiding treatment strategies in BCC. Research suggests that analyzing the expression levels of Gal‐3, Caspase‐1, and phosphorylation status might help to differentiate between various skin lesions and their likelihood of progression to malignancy [47]. In addition to the above, other established or emerging biomarkers of BCC include: PTCH1 Mutations, Bcl‐2, and Ki‐67. The PTCH1 gene participates in the Hedgehog signaling pathway, which is essential for cellular proliferation and development. Mutations in PTCH1 are the most prevalent genetic alteration in BCC, driving its development [48]. PTCH1 mutations are more established as a key driver of BCC. They are used for diagnosis and can provide some prognostic information. Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 may offer additional insights into tumor behavior beyond PTCH1 status. Furthermore, Bcl‐2 is a protein that inhibits apoptosis (programmed cell death). Overexpression of Bcl‐2 can promote tumor survival and growth in BCC [49]. Our findings can open avenues for developing noninvasive diagnostic assays which could revolutionize BCC detection by offering a less burdensome alternative to surgical procedures. However, our study is limited by its relatively small sample size and retrospective design. Future investigations should involve larger, prospective cohorts to validate these findings and assess the prognostic significance of Gal‐3, Caspase‐1, and pP53 expression in predicting BCC recurrence or aggressiveness. Furthermore, mechanistic studies are needed to fully elucidate the downstream signaling pathways and cellular interactions mediated by these molecules in BCC pathogenesis, which could potentially lead to the identification of novel therapeutic targets. Therefore, additional studies are required to confirm the clinical utility of emerging biomarkers.

Conclusion

In the present study, the findings indicated an elevation in the expression levels of Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 in the tumor tissue, compared to the tumor margin tissue; furthermore, the levels of pP53 in the tumor tissue showed a significant decrease. The interplay between these molecules has the potential to be considered a biomarker and therapeutic target in BCC. Additionally, further research is required to validate the clinical utility of emerging biomarkers, such as Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1, and to determine the optimal integration of these markers with established ones.

Limitations

Limitations of this study include a relatively small sample size, the lack of measurement of the expression levels of the studied genes in plasma samples, and the inability to assess cooperative interactions between them. Consequently, we could not determine the association of Gal‐3 and Caspase‐1 gene expression with the survival rate of the patients.
