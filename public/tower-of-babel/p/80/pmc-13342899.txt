Intrathyroidal thymic tissue: a pediatric case series from a secondary care center
Authors: Rita Silva Pereira, Joana Azevedo Silva, Marta Isabel Dias, Ana Miguel Simões, Sandra Mota Pereira, Vânia Martins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntrathyroidal ectopic thymus (IET) is a benign condition caused by aberrant thymic migration during embryogenesis. Often detected incidentally on ultrasound (US), it can mimic malignant thyroid nodules, leading to unnecessary interventions. We aimed to define the imaging characteristics of IET and emphasize an accurate diagnosis to avoid unwarranted procedures. We describe 5 pediatric cases of IET diagnosed by cervical US and monitored through long-term serial imaging. All lesions appeared as intrathyroidal nodules with echogenicity and internal foci typical of thymic tissue. Follow-up imaging showed no significant changes. None of the patients had thyroid dysfunction or clinical symptoms. The distinctive sonographic features of IET can differentiate it from malignant nodules, preventing unnecessary biopsies or surgeries. US follow-up confirmed a benign course and supports conservative management strategies.Keywords: Thyroid lesions, Ectopic thymus, Sonographic features

Highlights

· Intrathyroidal ectopic thymus is a benign developmental anomaly resulting from abnormal thymic migration during embryogenesis and is increasingly detected with the widespread use of pediatric ultrasound.

· Its characteristic sonographic appearance can closely mimic suspicious thyroid nodules, making accurate recognition essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary invasive procedures.

· Long-term imaging follow-up supports a benign and stable course, reinforcing conservative management as the preferred approach in most cases.

Introduction

Intrathyroidal ectopic thymus (IET) is a benign lesion resulting from aberrant thymic migration during embryogenesis and is often discovered incidentally [1]. Due to the presence of hyperechoic dots resembling calcifications, it can be mistaken for a malignant thyroid nodule [2,3]. This misidentification may lead to unnecessary surgeries or invasive diagnostic procedures in children [4]. In this case series, we present 5 pediatric cases of ectopic thymic tissue within the thyroid gland, followed at a Portuguese secondary care center.

Case reports

1. Case 1

A 4-year-old boy undergoing pediatric evaluation for sleep disturbances, hyperactivity, and lymphadenopathy underwent a cervical ultrasound (US), which revealed a nodular area in the left thyroid lobe (Fig. 1A). The lesion exhibited echogenicity similar to that of the thymus, suggesting intrathyroidal thymic tissue. A follow-up US performed 2 years later showed the nodule remained unchanged, with no signs of progression (Fig. 1B).

(A) Cervical ultrasound demonstrates a fusiform solid nodule measuring 11 mm in the mid-third of the left thyroid lobe (arrow), containing several echogenic foci (bright dots) with a “starry sky appearance,” suggestive of intrathyroidal thymic tissue. (B) A follow-up ultrasound confirms morphologic and dimensional stability of the nodule.

2. Case 2

A 13-month-old girl with an unremarkable medical history was referred for pediatric evaluation after a nodular lesion resembling thymic tissue was detected in the left thyroid lobe during a workup for suspected congenital torticollis (Fig. 2A). She underwent annual US follow-ups, which showed no changes over 5 years (Fig. 2B).

On ultrasonographic evaluation of the lower pole of the left thyroid lobe, (A) a solid ovoid nodule was identified (arrow), measuring 10 mm, and containing multiple linear and punctate hyperechoic foci with a “dot-and-dash pattern,” suggestive of thymic tissue echotexture. (B) Follow-up ultrasound demonstrated stability of the nodule.

3. Case 3

A 2-year-old girl with no significant medical history was initially hospitalized for suspected lymphadenitis. A subsequent US revealed a solid, isoechoic thyroid nodule in the left lobe with multiple millimetric hyperechoic foci. She was referred to a pediatric oncology center, where a fine-needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy was inconclusive. A follow-up US 1 year later suggested that the nodule resembled thymic tissue, consistent with an ectopic thymus (Fig. 3).

Cervical ultrasound demonstrated a fusiform, mildly hypoechoic solid nodule with 12 mm in the lower portion of the left thyroid lobe (arrow), containing multiple internal echogenic foci, features suggestive of intrathyroidal ectopic thymic tissue.

4. Case 4

A 4-year-old girl with a history of recurrent wheezing and otitis presented to the Emergency Department with a painful cervical mass that had developed that morning. US performed in an Emergency Department revealed lymphadenopathy and incidentally identified a hypoechoic intrathyroidal nodule in the left lobe with multiple millimetric hyperechoic foci (Fig. 4). A follow-up US 2 months later showed an echotexture consistent with thymic tissue.

Ultrasound shows a solid fusiform, hypoechoic nodule measuring 8 mm in the mid-third of the left thyroid lobe (arrow), containing discrete, nonshadowing echogenic foci. There is an extrathyroidal extension (double arrow) at the thyroid margin, which may represent communication with the thymus (not shown).

5. Case 5

A 4-year-old girl with acute cervical lymphadenopathy underwent a cervical US in the Emergency Department, which incidentally revealed a nodular formation in the lower third of the right thyroid lobe, warranting follow-up (Fig. 5). A few months later, 2 additional thyroid US scans were performed, both reporting bilateral solid nodules with some punctate echogenic foci, each measuring 4 mm, with an echotexture consistent with thymic tissue. In all 5 cases, the thyroid was neither visible nor palpable, and all children remained asymptomatic with normal thyroid function. US surveillance showed no significant changes in the lesions over time.

Ultrasound (US) demonstrates a sharply marginated, fusiform, solid, hypoechoic nodule with 4 mm in the middle portion of the right thyroid lobe (arrow), containing multiple punctate echogenic foci.

6. Ethics statement

According to institutional policies, formal Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee approval was not required for this retrospective case series because all data were fully anonymized and no patient-identifiable information was included. Written informed consent for publication of clinical information and anonymized imaging findings was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all patients.

Discussion

Intrathyroidal ectopic thymic tissue is usually discovered incidentally and is typically nonpalpable and asymptomatic [1,5]. The reported prevalence of IET among thyroid lesions detected by US varies among studies, ranging from 0.4% to 17.3%, with most series reporting a prevalence of 2%–4%. [6].

The thymus and thyroid glands both originate from the embryonic pharyngeal region [7]. By the eighth week of gestation, the thymus migrates caudally and medially to its final position in the superior anterior mediastinum [8]. Disruptions in this migration can lead to the development of ectopic thymic tissue along its usual pathway. Due to the close embryological relationship between the thymus and thyroid, thymic tissue may become embedded within the thyroid, making it the most common site for ectopic cervical thymus [1].

US is the preferred imaging modality for evaluating IET due to the distinctive sonographic features of the thymus. IET typically appears as a well-circumscribed hypoechoic lesion with echogenic linear and punctate foci corresponding to Hassall’s corpuscles, showing a fusiform shape on longitudinal views, well-defined margins, and a location within or near the middle or lower thirds of the thyroid gland. Small internal speckles should be carefully differentiated from microcalcifications by comparison with similar echogenic foci in the mediastinal thymus [8-10].

The differential diagnosis includes malignant thyroid nodules, nodular Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and intrathyroidal esophageal diverticulum [9]. Although rare in pediatric patients, solitary thyroid nodules carry a higher malignancy risk (up to 26.4%) compared to adults, underscoring the need for accurate differentiation among these entities [11-13].

Follow-up US typically shows stability in size, shape, and echogenicity of IET lesions over time [5,8,9]. IET often presents with punctate echogenic foci, yielding a Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TI-RADS) score ≥7 (TR5), indicating high suspicion for malignancy. This classification may prompt biopsy or follow-up imaging, potentially leading to unnecessary procedures or anxiety over a benign finding. Extrathyroidal extension (Fig. 4), commonly observed in IET, can further increase the TI-RADS score and heighten diagnostic concern [14]. Recognizing these sonographic characteristics is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary invasive interventions [8].

A systematic review of 619 pediatric patients with IET [9] reported that the most common echographic findings include a fusiform shape, hypoechoic echotexture, maximum diameter under 10 mm, and localization in the lower part of one or both thyroid lobes. The presence of these features should prompt clinicians to reconsider surgical intervention and instead favor conservative management.

In our case series, the lesions had an average diameter of 9 (range, 4–12) mm, were mostly fusiform, and demonstrated variable localization between the middle and lower thirds of the thyroid (Table 1), consistent with previous reports. In Fig. 4, the lower portion of the lesion extends toward and appears continuous with the thymus, exhibiting sonographic features consistent with thymic tissue. This continuity may serve as an important radiologic clue to the diagnosis of intrathyroidal thymus [15].

Among these 5 reported cases, 2 children underwent invasive procedures (2 hemithyroidectomies) and 1 underwent FNA. In our case series, only 1 child underwent FNA, and thymic lesions showed no growth or any evidence of malignant transformation in any of the patients. The literature indicates that nearly all ectopic thymic tissues remain quiescent over time, and current experience supports the safety of close clinical and imaging follow-up without surgical intervention [11].

This case series highlights the importance of recognizing intrathyroidal thymic tissue to ensure accurate diagnosis and prevent unnecessary biopsies or surgical interventions.

In conclusion, the growing use of US examinations in children has led to a higher detection rate of thyroid lesions [1]. Accurately recognizing the unique characteristics of IET, along with long-term follow-up, may help reduce medical iatrogenesis.
