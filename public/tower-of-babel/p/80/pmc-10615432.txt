SARS-CoV-2 viral liver aggregates and scarce parenchymal infection implicate systemic disease as a driver of abnormal liver function
Authors: Brian J Pepe-Mooney, Colton J Smith, Marc S Sherman, Trista E North, Robert F Padera, Wolfram Goessling
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Liver function tests (LFTs) are elevated in >50% of hospitalized individuals infected with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2), with increased enzyme levels correlating with a more severe COVID-19 course. Despite these observations, evaluations of viral presence within liver parenchyma and viral impact on liver function remain controversial.Methods and Results:Our work is a comprehensive immunopathological evaluation of liver tissue from 33 patients with severe, and ultimately fatal, cases of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Coupled with clinical data, we reveal the absence of SARS-CoV-2 infection in cholangiocytes and hepatocytes despite dramatic systemic viral presence. Critically, we identify significant focal viral sinusoidal aggregates in 2/33 patients and single viral RNA molecules circulating in the hepatic sinusoids of 15/33 patients. Utilizing co-immunofluorescence, focal viral liver aggregates in patients with COVID-19 were colocalized to platelet and fibrin clots, indicating the presence of virus-containing sinusoidal microthrombi. Furthermore, this patient cohort, from the initial months of the COVID-19 pandemic, demonstrates a general downtrend of LFTs over the course of the study timeline and serves as a remarkable historical time point of unattenuated viral replication within patients.Conclusions:Together, our findings indicate that elevated LFTs found in our patient cohort are not due to direct viral parenchymal infection with SARS-CoV-2 but rather likely a consequence of systemic complications of COVID-19. This work aids in the clinical treatment considerations of patients with SARS-CoV-2 as therapies for these patients may be considered in terms of their direct drug hepatotoxity rather than worsening hepatic function due to direct infection.

INTRODUCTION

COVID-19, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2), emerged as one of the greatest threats to public health in this century. One complication of COVID-19 that emerged in several large cohort studies is the high frequency (>50%) of abnormal liver function tests (LFTs), including elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, in affected patients.1,2 Indeed, abnormal LFTs have been detected more frequently in patients with severe disease.3 Despite these observations, the hepatic tropism of SARS-CoV-2, as assessed in vivo, remains controversial.

It has been well established that the SARS-CoV-2 virus uses the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor and the transmembrane serine protease (TMPRSS2) to gain cellular entry4, and several studies have demonstrated the expression of ACE2 in lung cells and liver cells. Particularly, ACE2 has been found to be expressed within the biliary tract of the liver, suggesting that these cells may be at risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection in vivo.5 Furthermore, SARS-CoV-2 has been shown to robustly infect biliary cells in vitro in an organoid culture system where cells maintain their expression of ACE2 and TMPRSS2.6 Infection of these cells has, therefore, been suggested as a potential mechanism for elevated LFTs observed in patients with COVID-19.7 Despite these arguments and cursory evidence of in vivo biliary cell infection suggested by protein immunohistological stains of the SARS-CoV-2 N protein, the characterization of the SARS-CoV-2 virus in vivo remains controversial.8

Direct viral infection of hepatocytes has also been proposed as a mechanism for the increase in liver injury observed on host infection, yet this has generated significant controversy. A single-cell–based risk stratification analysis for viral infection based on ACE-2 expression demonstrated that while cholangiocytes expressed high levels of ACE2, hepatocyte expression was minimal and therefore unlikely to be infected by SARS-CoV-2.5 Despite these findings, several studies have argued for the possible role of viral infection of hepatocytes in liver injury. Replication of SARS-CoV-2 has been observed in liver cancer cell lines, including Huh7 and HepG2 cells.9 Furthermore, Wang et al10 used transmission electron micrograph images to claim that SARS-CoV-2 directly infects the hepatic parenchyma. It has been debated, however, that cholesterol crystals could account for the “corona-like” particles observed in this particular study11, and these observations may be due to autolysis mechanisms in the liver.12 Furthermore, although work by Wanner and colleagues argued that SARS-CoV-2 could be found within hepatocytes, localization and cell type specificity were atypical for a normal hepatocyte pattern. Additional work has argued for direct SARS-CoV-2 viral hepatocyte invasion due to observations of hepatic necrosis foci located near periportal areas13 or for possible biliary system infection based on SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid immunohistochemistry (IHC).14 Additionally, work by Barreto et al15 has argued that direct hepatocyte infection can lead to increased glucose production in patients. Other work, however, has argued against direct parenchymal infection, detailing the lack of hepatic parenchymal infection in immunohistochemical analysis16 and that the central damage caused by SARS-CoV-2 on the liver may be due to systemic inflammation.17 Furthermore, single-cell and single-nuclei sequencing has found that while the SARS-CoV-2 virus was identified in the lung cells of patients, very few viral RNA reads were identified in the cells of the liver.18

With controversy regarding hepatic parenchymal infection demonstrated in the literature, but clear alteration of liver histology in patients who test positive for the virus, including increased steatosis and potential sinusoidal microthrombi,19 several mechanisms for elevated LFTs observed in patients with COVID-19 must be considered, including drug-induced hepatotoxicity, systemic immune dysregulation, or coagulopathy. Indeed, numerous reports of widespread immune dysregulation and microvascular thrombosis and their association with multiorgan failure have been documented.20 Despite accumulating evidence of liver injury in patients with severe COVID-19, viral hepatotropism remains controversial, and therefore, the mechanism of liver injury in viral infection remains undetermined.

Here, we evaluate postmortem liver histology samples and LFTs from a large cohort of 33 patients who died from SARS-CoV-2 infection from the initial emergence of the virus. This unique patient cohort represents a point in the historical timeline when current therapeutics did not exist and when the virus was unfortunately able to propagate unattenuated within the host. To assess viral presence in the liver, we applied single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH), immunofluorescence (IF), and immunohistochemical analyses. Remarkably, despite robust systemic infection, our work demonstrates the scarcity of any significant viral infection in hepatocytes, only identifying a single infected hepatocyte across histological sections from 33 different patients. Furthermore, our work demonstrates robust viral presence in intrasinusoidal viral aggregates in a small subset of patients. With the matched analysis of patient clinical data with our histological data, we are also able to determine that while virally induced microthrombi may contribute to liver injury in severe SARS-CoV-2 infection, LFT elevation does not correlate with incidences of viral aggregate or even viral presence within the liver. Rather, LFT elevation correlates with the natural historical timeline of the presentation of the disease (ie, during the pandemic timeline, patients presented with the virus) and higher viral presence within patient lungs. Our work, therefore, serves as an important landmark in the controversy surrounding potential SARS-CoV-2 hepatic parenchymal infection and clarifies that since there is scarce parenchymal viral presence in the liver, clinical therapies can be selected for patients based more directly on drug hepatoxicity and clinical context.

METHODS

Patient information

The Human Research Committee Institutional Review Board of Mass General Brigham on behalf of Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Massachusetts General Hospital approved the collection of the patient data and tissue samples included in this study under IRB 2020P001165 entitled “Pathologic Evaluation of COVID-19 and localization of SARS-CoV-2.” All relevant ethical regulations for work with human participants have been followed and necessary consent obtained, and all research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. The sample population consisted of 36 deceased patients who were hospitalized at Brigham and Women’s Hospital with autopsies performed between April 12, 2020, and June 13, 2020.

Sample preparation

Postmortem lung and liver tissue from 35 patients at Brigham and Women’s Hospital was isolated and examined. Thirty-three patients were SARS-CoV-2 positive (confirmed by reverse transcriptase PCR), while 3 patient samples were used for SARS-CoV-2 negative controls. Tissue was preserved by standard autopsy procedures as described in the literature and was performed within 24 hours of death.21 Tissue samples used in this study were formalin-fixed and paraffin embedded prior to our analysis.

Multiplex RNA smFISH

smFISH assays were performed utilizing the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) according to the user manual with slight modifications to incubation steps. Briefly, we incubated in Protease Plus solution for 30 minutes and performed a manual target retrieval for 25 minutes. For RNA hybridization, we used probes V-nCoV2019-S (ACD, 848561), V-nCoV2019-S-sense (ACD, 845701), Hs-ACE2 (ACD, 848151), Hs-TMPRSS2 (ACD, 470341), Hs-ALB (ACD, 600941), Hs-SPP1 (ACD, 420101), and Hs-CD68 (ACD, 560591). Slides were counterstained, mounted, and imaged using confocal microscopy.

smFISH with IF

For designated tissue samples, we combined the smFISH assay with protein IF. We first completed the smFISH assay described above up until the counterstaining step. After signal development, a blocking step was performed with 0.1% phosphate-buffered saline–Tween (PBST) solution containing 10% normal goat serum-PBST for 30 minutes at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with 10% normal goat serum-PBST with desired primary antibody dilutions. The primary antibodies include SARS-nucleocapsid protein (Novus Biologicals, NB100-56576), CD61 (Sigma, 161M-15), C4d (Alpco, 04-BI-RC4D), IgG (Abcam, ab200699), alpha-smooth muscle actin (R&D Systems, MAB1420-SP), COLEC11 (Abbexa, abx003772), and fibrin (EMD Millipore, MABS2155). After incubation in primary antibody, samples were washed in PBST and PBS for 5 minutes. Samples were then incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in a 10% normal goat serum-PBST solution containing secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 568 diluted at a ratio of 1:1000). The counterstain was then performed as described in the smFISH protocol.

Immunohistochemistry

For designated IHC analysis, ImmPACT AMEC Red Substrate Kit, Peroxidase (HRP)—(SK-4285) was used with anti-Rabbit HRP (ab6721). After rehydration and antigen retrieval with tris EDTA buffer, samples were blocked in 1X PBS/5% normal serum/0.3% Triton X-100. Primary staining was performed overnight (in 1X PBS/1% Bovine Serum Albumin/0.3% Triton X-100) followed by 3X wash in 1×PBS + 0.5% tween (PBST), then 10 minutes of 0.3% H2O2 in methanol. Samples were washed again in PBST 3X and placed in HRP secondary for 1 hour. AMEC IMPACT Red was then used to expose the antibody for 15 minutes. Samples were washed again in PBST 3X and counterstained with Gill’s hematoxylin #3 for 3 minutes. They were then washed in tap water for 1 minute and mounted. The primary antibodies for IHC include ACE2 (Abcam, ab108252) and TMPRSS2 (Abcam, ab109131).

Image acquisition

Images were acquired using scanning laser confocal microscopy (Yokogawa CSU-W1 and Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope). Confocal settings were unchanged for each image series unless otherwise indicated. Images were quantified from 5 regions of 10× images for each sample as well as a qualitative evaluation of the entire 1.5×2 cm, 10 µm liver sections for each patient sample.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) with regression analyses as indicated.

RESULTS

Absence of SARS-CoV-2 in cholangiocytes and in hepatic parenchyma of infected patients

To evaluate the presence of SARS-CoV-2 within the livers of deceased patients with COVID-19, we used a smFISH approach. RNA probes were validated for the sense (+) strand of the SARS-CoV-2 virus as well as for ACE2 using patient-matched lung samples to serve as positive controls. Viral RNA was clearly identified in pneumocytes, along the alveolar wall, and within the alveoli in patients infected with SARS-CoV-2, compared to uninfected control patients (Figure 1A, Supplemental Figure S1A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611). These regions have been previously reported as areas of infection by IHC for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein.21 In patient-matched liver samples, ACE2 expression was detected within cholangiocytes (Supplemental Figure S1B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611), as described.5 Importantly, while many larger biliary ducts showed high levels of ACE2 RNA staining, the expression was much more variable and even absent among smaller ductal structures, consistent with the reported variability in single-cell RNA sequencing data sets (Supplemental Figure S1B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611).22 This expression pattern was also confirmed by protein IHC for ACE-2 (Supplemental Figure S1D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611). We next evaluated liver tissue and did not detect SARS-CoV-2 viral RNA in cholangiocytes in any of the specimens examined (Figure 1B). We also evaluated RNA expression for TMPRSS2, which has been previously found to be expressed both in hepatocytes and cholangiocytes by single-cell analyses.22 We confirmed TMPRSS2 expression by RNA and protein IHC in both hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, but again saw no evidence of cholangiocyte infection (Supplemental Figure S1C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611). While no viral SARS-CoV-2 RNA was observed within the overwhelming majority of hepatocytes observed across the biopsies from 33 patient samples, a single example of viral infection within a hepatocyte was identified in 2 different sections (Figure 1C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, Supplemental Figure S1D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611). Hepatocyte lineage was confirmed by colocalization of the hepatocyte marker ALBUMIN with IF for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (Figure 1C). Positive hepatocytes were not observed in majority of hepatocytes analyzed nor in the control patient samples quantified (Supplemental Figure S1D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, S2A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, S2B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, S3C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, S2D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611). This lack of robust hepatocyte infection was particularly remarkable given the severe systemic nature of the SARS-CoV-2 infection identified in these patients. Even in a patient who demonstrated significantly high levels of virus within the lung, heart, kidney, and testis (Figure 1D), no evidence of hepatocyte infection was observed. Furthermore, viral infection was found to be present within cells of these respective tissues as made evident by the presence of negative sense (−) strand of the virus, which serves to identify cells that are actively performing RNA replication for repackaging (Supplemental Figure S3A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611).

SARS-CoV-2 virus is present in focal aggregates in hepatic sinusoids of postmortem liver tissue from patients with COVID-19

On further evaluation of our sample cohort, we identified 2 patients with significant areas of definitive SARS-CoV-2 RNA positivity interspersed throughout the liver, which were not present in uninfected patients (Figure 2A, B, Supplemental Figure S1D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611). These focal regions of positive SARS-CoV-2 RNA were clearly localized in the hepatic sinusoids between hepatocytes. Interestingly, none of the SARS-CoV-2 RNA-positive regions identified were ACE2 positive, and they did not overlap with cells identified by nuclear DAPI stain, suggesting that these positive regions were intact virions or free RNA occupying extracellular space. High-resolution imaging of these regions of focal aggregation clearly reveals the sinusoidal localization of single viral RNA particles that do not exhibit ACE2 positivity (Figure 2C, D). The presence of virus in these sinusoidal regions was also confirmed using a co-IF stain for SARS-CoV-2 RNA-in situ hybridization and IF for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (Supplemental Figure S4A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611). To further characterize and confirm localization of these viral aggregates within the lumen of hepatic sinusoids, IHC for COLEC11 (marker for quiescent hepatic stellate cells23), alpha-smooth muscle actin (marker for activated stellate cells), and smFISH for SARS-CoV-2 RNA was performed. These stains clearly revealed that viral particles in the lumen of sinusoids do not localize to hepatocytes, endothelial cells, or stellate cells (Figure 2E). In addition to the discrete regions of viral positivity observed in these 2 patient samples, areas of low copy number RNA viral positivity were also observed within the sinusoids of 15/33 additional patients (Figure 2F). These patients were identified as having liver sections which contained 6 or less identified viral particles by RNA-in situ hybridization.

To confirm that the robust regions of viral positivity in the sinusoids of the 2 high-viral RNA patients were not infected cells with actively replicating virus, smFISH was performed for the negative sense (−) strand of the virus, which serves to identify cells which have been infected by the virus and are actively performing RNA replication for repackaging.24 While clear regions of negative sense (−) SARS-CoV-2 RNA staining were present in lung tissue (Supplemental Figure S4B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611), we did not observe (−) antisense RNA in cholangiocytes within the liver, further confirming lack of viral infection in cholangiocytes (Supplemental Figure S4C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611). Furthermore, when we examined and evaluated serial histological sections which had substantial sense (+) SARS-CoV-2 RNA, we saw minimal negative (−) sense SARS-CoV-2 RNA in nonhepatic cells, with no signal identified in the hepatic parenchyma (Supplemental Figure S4D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611).

Since macrophages can become SARS-CoV-2 RNA positive, whether on direct infection or through phagocytosis of other SARS-CoV-2 infected cells,25 we evaluated whether the regions of positive sense (+) SARS-CoV-2 RNA in the liver colocalized to macrophages, utilizing an RNA- in situ hybridization probe for a pan-macrophage marker, CD68, which has been shown to be expressed in alveolar macrophages as well as liver resident macrophages (KCs).26 While alveolar macrophages were identified to have a clear correlation with sense (+) SARS-CoV-2 RNA positivity, viral aggregates in the liver did not express CD68 (Figure 2G). This observation further illustrates that these positive regions in the liver represent extracellular viral aggregates not associated with macrophages or other cell types.

After evaluating the postmortem liver tissue from our cohort of 33 individuals, we identified 2/33 (6%) with robust (>6 particles detected) viral presence throughout the parenchyma (high levels of virus designated as “+++”) (Figure 4A). LFT results were available for 27/33 patients and were evaluated at 2 different time points: (1) at the time closest to autopsy and (2) at the time closest to PCR confirmation of SARS-CoV-2 infection. The average number of days between the date of liver enzyme data collected and date of autopsy was within 1.85 ±2.9 days. For this data set, there were no differences in levels of AST, ALT, or alkaline phosphatase (ALP) observed between subcategories (Figure 4B and Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A612). For the second data set evaluating liver enzymes obtained at PCR confirmation of SARS-CoV-2 infection, the average number of days between the date of liver enzyme data collected and the date of PCR confirmation was within 1.68 ± 3.03 days. These data were again stratified based on the detection of viral RNA in the liver sinusoids (+++, +, − ) and did not show any significant differences in AST, ALT, or ALP between subcategories (Supplemental Figure S5A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611 and Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A613). Importantly, the LFTs obtained closest to the time of initial PCR confirmation of SARS-CoV-2 infection were acquired when all patients (except for patient 3) in this cohort were hemodynamically stable. This is an important observation because increased requirement for vasopressors could also restrict blood flow, leading to decreased perfusion of the liver parenchyma and therefore lead to increased LFTs. Given these 2 data points, the ability to detect viral RNA in the liver at the time of autopsy did not correlate with LFT elevations as evaluated close to autopsy or at initial presentation. This observation indicates that abnormal LFTs do not correlate with viral infection of the hepatic parenchyma or with viral presence in the sinusoids.

Interestingly, however, when patient LFTs were stratified based on date of autopsy, ALT, AST values as well as coagulation parameters (prothrombin time and international normalized ratio) values trended toward being more abnormal if the patient required hospitalization earlier in the historical timeline of the pandemic. Patient values were stratified based on the calendar time from the first autopsy (April 12, 2020) to the last (June 13, 2020) (Figure 4C). While AST, ALT, ALP, international normalized ratio, and prothrombin time values were not found to be significantly different when strictly stratified by the first 30 days and the last 30 days of the study (Supplemental Figure S5B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611, C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A611), ALT values were in fact found to have a negative slope (−0.02182; 95% CI of −0.07083 to −0.0008143) over time. The observation that ALT and AST levels trend downward over the course of the initial time frame of the pandemic but not ALP indicates a decrease in hepatocellular disease over time with no change in any potential cholestatic disease.27 These observations suggest that the ALT and AST levels may be a better marker for systemic disease caused by SARS-CoV-2 as opposed to being directly related to viral presence in the liver or due to direct viral infection of the hepatic parenchyma. In addition to liver enzymes, SARS-CoV-2 viral load, as determined by quantitative PCR, was also found to correlate with the date of patient hospitalization (Figure 4D).

Finally, the ability to detect viral RNA in hepatic sinusoids of patient samples correlated strongly (12/33 patients) with the ability to detect the presence of the virus in the matched lung samples using IHC for the SAR-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (Figure 4E). Additionally, a qRT-PCR analysis of SARS-CoV-2 from the lung tissue of the patient cohort demonstrated how patients with the highest relative viral load in the lungs correlated with those that had significant areas of definitive SARS-CoV-2 RNA positivity in the liver (Figure 4F). Together, these data indicate that if the virus is present in focal aggregates in the liver, the lungs are expected to be infected at high levels. Interestingly, when patient samples were also stratified based on increased viral load as determined by qRT-PCR of lung samples and then AST and ALT values were evaluated, it was determined that patients with higher viral load in the lung (lower CT values) had higher AST and ALT levels (Figure 4G). This observation is consistent with higher disease load and possible systemic infection being a central driver for elevated LFTs, as opposed to direct parenchymal infection.

The SARS-CoV-2 virus is localized to few regions of platelet aggregation in the liver

Systemic dysregulation of coagulation has been reported in many patients presenting with COVID-19.20,28 We therefore evaluated whether the regions of positive sense (+) SARS-CoV-2 RNA observed in the 2 high-viral patients also showed evidence of platelet aggregation. Co-immunofluorescent staining for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein and CD61, a marker for platelets29 in the liver, revealed distinct regions showing neighboring localization (Figure 5A), while both CD61 and SARS-CoV-2 staining were absent in patients without COVID-19. To determine whether megakaryocytes may be infected by the virus, we evaluated the bone marrow of the 2 highly SARS-CoV-2-infected patients in our cohort and compared to control samples. While we could clearly identify CD61-positive megakaryocytes and platelets within the bone marrow of patients, neither were infected by the virus in the samples examined (Figure 5B). Additionally, IF staining for fibrin protein and smFISH for (+) sense SARS-CoV-2 RNA revealed the presence of fibrin clot formation associated with the regions of focal virus localization (Figure 5C). The close association between these large viral aggregates, platelets, and fibrin in distinct areas of the hepatic lobule reveals a mechanism by which microthrombi may lead to viral aggregation within the liver.

Microvascular thrombosis and clot formation can occur through local activation of the complement cascade (through the classical, lectin, or alternative pathways as regulated by more than 30 proteins) on endothelial injury and exposure of basement membrane.30 Activation of the complement system in liver sinusoids may, therefore, play a role in the appearance of the observed viral aggregates. Indeed, patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 have been found to have an increased circulating component of the complement system, C5a.31 Therefore, to probe whether the observed microvascular injury in the liver was mediated through complement cascade activation, IHC was performed for C4d. C4d is one of the protein split products of complement activation and has been interpreted as a tracer for activation of the classical and lectin pathway as C4d remains attached to tissue after activation.32 It also has been identified throughout the lung parenchyma of patients with COVID-19.33 While extensive C4d deposition in the lung parenchyma was confirmed in patients with active SARS-CoV-2 infection, it was not identified in any of the liver patient samples (Figure 5D). These data suggest that there may be an alternative mechanism than the classical or lectin pathway, which may be responsible for the emergence of local viral microclots within sinusoids. Finally, to evaluate whether regions of microthrombi within the liver induce localized inflammation, I examined human liver sections for expression of the proinflammatory transcription factor NF-κB, but no signal was identified surrounding aggregate regions (Figure 5E).

DISCUSSION

Patients with COVID-19 frequently present with abnormal liver enzyme levels.34 A clear etiology, however, remains unknown, and explanations range from direct liver injury through viral infection of hepatocytes to drug-induced hepatotoxicity, systemic immune dysregulation, or microthrombi causing congestion and cell damage.35 Our work uses smFISH and co-IHC on liver samples from a large cohort of 33 patients with severe and ultimately fatal SARS-CoV-2 infection during the earliest phase of the pandemic to evaluate the role of the virus in liver enzyme abnormalities. Although expression of the ACE2 and TMPRSS2 receptors confirms the potential for cholangiocytes to be infected by SARS-CoV-2, no active viral infection within cholangiocytes was observed in any patient samples. Additionally, our data reveal the presence of the virus in 2/33 evaluated patients as focal aggregates within hepatic sinusoids but not within cells of the general hepatic parenchyma, with low levels of virus identified in the sinusoids of an additional 15/33 patients. The detection of small numbers of viral particles within the hepatic sinusoids is consistent with previous work showing the ability to detect virus in serum of patients with high-viral titer.36 Despite the detection of virus within the livers of these patients, direct parenchymal infection was only identified in a single hepatocyte within the entire cohort of 33 samples evaluated. This finding is particularly remarkable given the examples of the significant amount of virus identified in other organs within the same patient, suggesting that despite very high-viral titers, hepatocytes are unlikely to be robustly infected with SARS-CoV-2. While viral clearance and replication across different organ systems may be variable, these data suggest that either (1) the virus in the liver is rapidly cleared, without residual evidence of immune infiltration visualized on histological analysis, or (2) that more likely, hepatocyte infection is a highly unlikely event, even with increased viral presence. This variability in viral clearance and the possible resolution of microthombi in one organ system and not another may also explain how some patients in our cohort showed positive RNA in the liver but no RNA identified in the lungs.

In general, these findings suggest that although liver cells, including biliary cells, may be infected with SARS-CoV-2 in an in vitro setting, as highlighted by biliary organoid infection by the virus,6 they appear unlikely to be significantly vulnerable for infection in vivo, and therefore parenchymal infection is unlikely to be the major driver for elevated LFTs. There have been previous suggestions in the literature, such as those by Wanner and colleagues, that hepatocytes are possibly infected by SARS-CoV-2 in vivo. However, the cellular morphology presented by this work appears different from what would typically be seen with normal human hepatocytes.37 Furthermore, although Wanner and colleagues were able to isolate replication-competent virus from autopsy liver specimens, it has been well established that viral particles can circulate within plasma and may serve as the source of infectivity in this case.38 This is also consistent with our results showing the presence of viral particles within the sinusoids of the liver.

While we clearly identify a single infected hepatocyte by albumin and SARS-CoV-2 RNA colocalization, the overwhelming majority of hepatocytes in the entire cohort of 33 patients examined remained uninfected. Interestingly, our data also confirm that while there is minimal ACE2 expression in hepatocytes, TMPRSS2 is expressed by hepatocytes in vivo. While we identify scarce hepatocyte infection of SARS-CoV-2 within our patient cohort, situations where patients express higher levels of both ACE2 and TMPRSS2 within hepatocytes may lead to higher levels of hepatocyte infection and therefore cannot be summarily excluded within the broader population.

Evaluation of the LFTs for patients where virus could be identified in the liver revealed no significant difference between the presence and absence of virus and liver enzyme levels. Furthermore, the 2 patients who had the highest levels of viral aggregation did not correlate with the highest levels of abnormal LFTs. Interestingly, however, the trend of ALT, AST, and coagulation tests suggests that earlier cases in the historical timeline of our study tended to have higher abnormal values. This observation implies that these abnormal tests may be more likely reflective of severe systemic disease burden rather than hepatic damage due to viral infection. As clinical understanding of SARS-CoV-2 and management of COVID-19 progressed rapidly over the initial months of the pandemic, captured in our cohort,39 patient outcomes have improved. Indeed, a study evaluating patients with rheumatic and musculoskeletal disease with COVID-19 over a 6-month period has shown the risk of hospitalization to be lower in later time points in the study.40 This highlights the possibility that our observations may additionally represent a historical account of disease severity. Furthermore, our observation that higher viral load, as determined by qRT-PCR of lung samples, correlates with increased AST and ALT levels also supports the notion that a higher systemic disease burden, rather than direct parenchymal infection, may lead to increased LFTs.

Finally, our data show clearly highlight that direct parenchymal infection is not the central driver for elevated LFTs in our cohort of patients with COVID-19. Rather, we identify systemic coagulopathy and hepatic microthrombi as possible contributors in a likely multifactorial contribution to elevated liver enzymes. Several studies have identified potential endothelial damage and a thrombotic syndrome associated with COVID-19.20,28,41 Indeed, some patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection have been reported to progress to a hyperinflammatory state with systemic coagulative and fibrinolytic pathway dysregulation leading to thrombosis in the lung, kidney, heart, and liver.30,42 Work by McConnell and colleagues has detailed how in their cohort of patients with COVID-19, several livers show dilated sinusoids, liver congestion, and platelet deposition. Furthermore, they suggest that IL-6 signaling may cause this procoagulant state.43 Interestingly, SARS-CoV-2 has also been suggested to lead to platelet activation through the direct association of the virus in the serum with the ACE2 receptor expressed on platelets.44 Furthermore, COVID-19 has even been linked to both systemic arterial and venous thromboemboli, and has been shown to lead to skin vasculitis. Microthrombi have been observed in the heart and even have been suggested to appear within hepatic sinusoids based on pathological analysis.29,41,45 Specifically, these highlighted studies have shown that SARS-CoV-2 can be found associated with fibrin microthombi within small arterial vessels, particularly within the heart.46 Studies have even successfully identified high-viral titers of SARS-CoV-2 from isolated cardiac thromb.47 Interestingly, work from De Michele et al has also isolated SARS-CoV-2 spike protein from fibrin clots, suggesting the presence of the virus within microthrombi.48 Studies by Kondo and colleagues have suggested that SARS-CoV-2 may impact hepatic endothelial cells through direct infection; however, endothelial cells have shown resistance to and absence of SARS-CoV-2 infection both in vivo and in vitro.49,50 Despite several studies highlighting how SARS-CoV-2 viral infection can lead to microthrombi, additional studies for how SARS-CoV-2 infection may cause microthrombi within the liver, are still required.

With the identification of viral aggregates within sinusoids within the liver, our work offers a clear explanation for why PCR-based approaches have identified the presence of the virus within liver samples but have not visualized significant direct hepatocyte infection.16,29 Additionally, our work highlights how the hepatic microthrombi identified are not caused by canonical complement cascade dysregulation, as the viral aggregates in the liver are not associated with the lectin/C4d pathway. Excitingly, we demonstrate that viral aggregates are clearly present within hepatic sinusoids and are directly associated with platelet clots and fibrin microthrombi (Figure 6). These findings suggest an alternative pathway toward coagulation. Although our study evaluated histology from ultimately fatal infections of SARS-CoV-2, it will be necessary to consider how local inflammatory effects in the liver due to sinusoidal microthrombi may impact patients who have recovered from severe infection. Indeed, while our work aids in uncovering the effects of COVID-19 in the livers of severely ill patients, further investigation to understand the potential long-term effects on the liver and other organs will continue to be required.
