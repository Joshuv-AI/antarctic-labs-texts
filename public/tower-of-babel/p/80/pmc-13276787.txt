Dysregulation of Non‐Muscle Myosin IIA Assembly and Phosphorylation in S100A4 Null Mouse Lens
Authors: Rupalatha Maddala, Levi K Lankford, Nikolai P Skiba, Ponugoti V Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNon‐muscle myosin IIA (NMIIA), a motor protein plays a critical role in regulating cell morphology, adhesion, migration, contractility, and mechanotransduction across various tissues, including the ocular lens. S100A4, a known NMIIA‐interacting protein, is abundantly expressed and exhibits a discrete spatial distribution in lens fibers. Loss of S100A4 has previously been shown to associate with late‐onset lens opacification in mice. However, its role in regulating NMIIA activity, assembly, and actin cytoskeletal organization in the lens remains unclear. Using S100A4‐null mice, this study reveals that S100A4 co‐immunoprecipitates with NMIIA and that its absence leads to decreased NMIIA (Ser1943) phosphorylation, impaired NMIIA filament assembly, and disruption in actin cytoskeletal organization and polymerization in the lens. Quantitative proteomic analysis further identified decreased levels of CLIC5 and RNA binding protein‐SERBP1 in cytoskeletal‐ and membrane‐enriched fractions from S100A4‐null versus wild‐type lenses. Moreover, treatment of wild‐type mouse lenses in ex‐vivo with trifluoperazine, a known S100A4 inhibitor, induced lens opacification in association with increased insolubilization of S100A4, NMIIA and actin. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that S100A4 plays a critical role in regulating NMIIA activity and assembly, and maintaining actin cytoskeletal organization in the ocular lens, thereby contributing to lens transparency and homeostasis.Keywords: actin cytoskeleton, calcium, cataract, lens, non‐muscle myosin II, Phenothiazine, S100A4

Introduction

Among the different myosin II motor proteins, the non‐muscle myosin II (NMII) subfamily, comprising NMIIA, NMIIB, and NMIIC encoded by MYH9, MYH10, and MYH14 genes, respectively, is widely expressed in various tissues (Pecci et al. 2018; Sellers and Heissler 2019; Vicente‐Manzanares et al. 2009). NMIIs play crucial roles in cell contraction, adhesion, actin stress fiber organization, migration and mechanotransduction, and tissue architecture (Murrell et al. 2015; Vicente‐Manzanares et al. 2009). Dysregulation of NMII activity has been implicated in diverse pathological conditions including cataract formation (Hu et al. 2002; Pecci et al. 2018). Despite extensive investigation however, the mechanisms regulating NMII activity in different tissues remain only partially understood (Pecci et al. 2018; W. Zhang and Gunst 2017, 2020).

Like other myosin II subtypes of skeletal, smooth, and cardiac muscle, NMIIs exist as hexamers consisting of two myosin heavy chains, two essential light chains, and two regulatory light chains (Pecci et al. 2018; Vicente‐Manzanares et al. 2009). Each heavy chain consists of head, neck, coiled‐coil helical, and C‐terminal non‐helical tail regions. Phosphorylation of the regulatory light chain by kinases such as myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) and Rho‐associated kinase (ROCK) activates the actin dependent Mg2+‐ATPase activity of myosin, inducing the formation of anti‐parallel cross‐bridges with actin filaments that drive cellular contraction and force generation (Heissler and Sellers 2016; Pecci et al. 2018; Vicente‐Manzanares et al. 2009). In addition, direct phosphorylation of the NMII heavy chain at both helical and non‐helical regions by various kinases regulates NMII filament assembly and disassembly (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; Pecci et al. 2018). Beyond kinase‐mediated regulation, several non‐kinase proteins also bind to NMII and influence its activity and assembly (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; M. Kriajevska et al. 2000; Pecci et al. 2018).

One such protein is S100A4, a small molecular weight calcium‐binding protein that modulates NMIIA assembly dynamics (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; M. Kriajevska et al. 2000; Li and Bresnick 2006; Li et al. 2003). In in vitro studies, S100A4 has been demonstrated to bind to the C‐terminal coiled‐coil helical region of NMIIA and promote its disassembly (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; Dulyaninova et al. 2011; Kiss et al. 2012; Pecci et al. 2018). Interestingly, in smooth muscle tissue, S100A4 has been reported to promote NMII assembly and contractile activity, suggesting that its function may vary depending on the cellular context and tissue type (W. Zhang and Gunst 2020). The in vivo role of S100A4 in regulating NMII activity in non‐muscle tissues (e.g., the ocular lens), however, remains largely unexplored.

S100A4 belongs to the S100 family of vertebrate‐specific S100 proteins that typically exist as homodimers characterized by two EF‐hand calcium‐binding motifs. Despite structural similarity, S100 proteins exhibit distinct, tissue‐specific expression patterns and non‐redundant functions (Boye and Mælandsmo 2010; Donato et al. 2013; Garrett et al. 2006; Grigorian et al. 1993). Upon binding calcium, S100A4 undergoes a conformational change that enables interaction with target proteins (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; Garrett et al. 2006), with NMIIA being among its best‐characterized binding partners (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013). Through this interaction, S100A4 regulates actin cytoskeletal organization and cell adhesion, processes central to its well‐recognized role in cell migration, and the pathobiology of cancer metastasis and tissue fibrosis (Boye and Mælandsmo 2010; Garrett et al. 2006; Helfman et al. 2005; Mishra et al. 2012). Consequently, therapeutic efforts have targeted S100A4 expression or activity (Ismail et al. 2023; Mishra et al. 2012). Despite these pathophysiological associations, the physiological role of S100A4 in normal tissues remains poorly defined.

Recently, we discovered that S100A4 is robustly expressed in the ocular lens, exhibiting a discrete induction profile during lens epithelial differentiation and abundant localization to fiber cells (Maddala et al. 2021). The lens is a transparent, avascular tissue composed of a single anterior epithelial layer and elongated and differentiated fiber cells that form the bulk of the lens. Proper fiber cell elongation, differentiation, polarization, compaction, and terminal differentiation depend critically on actin cytoskeletal reorganization and cell–cell adhesive interactions (Cheng et al. 2017; Rao and Maddala 2006). Notably, loss of S100A4 leads to late‐onset lens opacification in mice (Maddala et al. 2021). Furthermore, mutations in MYH9 (encoding NMIIA) are associated with cataract formation in humans and animal models (Asensio‐Juárez et al. 2020; Heath et al. 2001; Islam et al. 2023; Y. Zhang et al. 2012). Although the lens exhibits many non‐muscle characteristics, the regulatory role of S100A4 in actin cytoskeletal organization and NMII activity in the lens has yet to be defined (Maddala et al. 2021).

In this study, we used S100A4‐null mice and pharmacological inhibition to determine the role of S100A4 in NMIIA phosphorylation, filament assembly, and actin cytoskeletal organization in the lens. Our findings reveal that loss of S100A4 disrupts NMIIA phosphorylation and assembly, leading to actin cytoskeletal disorganization in lens. These results demonstrate a functional coupling between S100A4 and NMIIA that is required for the maintenance of actin cytoskeletal architecture and normal lens function.

Materials and Methods

Mice

All experiments involving mice (S100A4 null mice, their littermate controls, and wild‐type mice) were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health and the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Duke University School of Medicine (protocol A213‐19‐10). Mice were housed in a pathogen‐free vivarium under a 12‐h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Euthanasia was performed using Euthasol, followed by decapitation to ensure death.

Well characterized breeding pairs of S100A4 null mice (C57BL/6 J background) were generously provided by the Anne Bresnick laboratory, Department of Biochemistry, Albert Einstein College of Medicine, Bronx, NY USA (Li et al. 2010; Maddala et al. 2021). Breeding and genotyping were performed as previously described (Maddala et al. 2021). Clear lenses from 6 to 7‐month‐old male and female S100A4 null (‐/‐) mice and their wildtype littermates were used for this study.

For experiments involving organ‐cultured lenses and S100A4 interactome analyses, lenses from 1‐month‐old C57BL/6 J wild‐type mice (both males and females) were used.

Immunoprecipitation

To identify S100A4 interacting proteins in mouse lens tissue, co‐immunoprecipitation (IP) assays were conducted using a polyclonal anti‐S100A4 antibody (Table S1) and Dynabeads Protein‐G (Invitrogen), following our previously described protocol (Maddala et al. 2024). Briefly, lenses from 1‐month‐old mice (pooled) were homogenized (2% w/v) in 1× IP buffer (Sigma‐Aldrich) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (1 tablet each per 10 mL; protease inhibitor, Cat. No. 11836170001; PhosStop, Cat. No. 04906837001, Millipore Sigma). Homogenization was carried out on ice using a Dounce glass homogenizer with 10 upward and 10 downward strokes. The homogenates were centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants collected. Protein concentration was determined using the Bio‐Rad Protein Assay reagent. IP assays were then performed using equal amounts of protein (100 µg; technical replicates) for both anti‐S100A4 and IgG control samples under identical conditions.

Extraction of Cytoskeletal‐Enriched Fraction

Cytoskeletal‐enriched fractions were prepared from lenses of 6‐month‐old S100A4‐/‐ mice and littermate controls, following the procedure described by Cho et. al (Choi et al. 2014). For each preparation, six pooled lenses were used (n = 3 biological replicates per group). Lenses were gently homogenized on ice in freshly prepared, ice‐cold cell lysis buffer (100 mg tissue/ml) using a Dounce glass homogenizer. The lysis buffer contained 50 mM PIPES, 50 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.1% NP‐40, 0.1% Triton X‐100, 0.1% Tween‐20, 5 mM sodium fluoride, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate, and protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Homogenates were transferred to Eppendorf tubes, placed on a rotating rocker, and incubated for 10 min at 4°C, followed by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant (cytosolic fraction) was collected and kept on ice.

Pellets were gently disrupted with a plastic pestle, rinsed twice with ice‐cold 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 7.5), and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in nuclease buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 7.5; 10 mM MgCl2; 2 mM CaCl2; 10 U/ml Benzonase nuclease; Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO). After centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), the nuclease buffer was removed, and the previously collected cytosolic lysate was added back to the pellets. Samples were disrupted again with a pestle and incubated on ice for 5 min to release DNA/RNA‐binding proteins. Following centrifugation, the resulting supernatants were combined with the cytosolic fraction.

Pellets were then washed three additional times with ice‐cold Tris‐HCl buffer, with centrifugation after each wash (8,000 × g, 5 min, 4°C). All pellet‐disruption steps were performed using a plastic pestle as needed. The final pellets, representing the cytoskeletal‐enriched fraction, were resuspended in 500 µl of 2% SDS in low‐binding Eppendorf tubes. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay, and samples were processed for proteomic analysis as described above. Equal amounts (25 µg) of cytoskeletal‐enriched protein from control and S100A4‐/‐ mice were subjected to tryptic digestion mass spectrometry.

Extraction of Membrane‐Enriched Fraction

Six‐month‐old lenses from S100A4‐/‐ mice and their respective littermate controls (four lenses pooled per sample) were homogenized in ice‐cold hypotonic buffer using a Dounce glass homogenizer. The buffer contained 10 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 0.2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM N‐ethylmaleimide, 2.0 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM NaF, 60 µM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 0.4 mM iodoacetamide, protease inhibitors, and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitors. Homogenates were first centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were further centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C. The supernatant obtained was designated as the cytosolic fraction.

The insoluble pellets were resuspended in hypotonic buffer and washed twice with the same buffer. The final membrane‐enriched protein pellets were solubilized in urea sample buffer containing 8 M urea, 20 mM Tris, 23 mM glycine, 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), saturated sucrose, and protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific). Equal amounts of membrane‐enriched fractions (25 µg protein) from control and S100A4‐/‐ lenses were subjected to tryptic digestion and mass spectrometry as described below.

Mass Spectrometry

The immunocomplexes, cytoskeletal‐enriched, and membrane‐enriched fractions described above were solubilized in phosphate‐buffered saline (pH 7.4) containing 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and incubated for 15 min at 55°C. Proteins were then alkylated with 25 mM iodoacetamide and subjected to tryptic digestion using the HILIC bead SP3 protocol, as previously described (Maddala et al. 2024).

Peptide digests were separated on an EasySpray PepMap Neo C18 column (75 µm × 150 mm, 2 µm, Thermo Scientific) over 90 min at a flow rate of 0.3 µL/min at 35°C using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific). A 5–30% gradient of mobile phase B was used, where mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile. Eluted peptides were introduced into a Q Exactive HF Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) via positive electrospray ionization at 1900 V, with a capillary temperature of 275°C. Data were collected in data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) mode with a resolution of 120,000 at m/z 200 for MS1 precursor scans. MS1 analysis was performed from 375 to 1500 m/z with a target AGC value of 1.0 × 106 ions, RF lens set to 30%, and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. Advanced peak detection and internal calibration (EIC) were enabled. Peptides were selected for MS/MS based on charge state filtering, monoisotopic peak detection, and dynamic exclusion of 20 s with a mass tolerance of 10 ppm. MS/MS fragmentation was performed using high‐energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 30 ± 5%, and fragment ions were detected in the ion trap using rapid scanning.

For protein identification and quantification, raw mass spectral data files were imported into Scaffold 5 (Proteome Software)Progenesis QI for Proteomics (Waters Inc.). Peptides were identified using Mascot version 2.5.2 (Matrix Science) by searching the UniProt 201923 reviewed mouse database (20,237 entries). Mascot search parameters were: 10 ppm precursor mass tolerance; 0.025 Da fragment‐ion tolerance; one missed cleavage allowed; fixed modification: carbamidomethylation of cysteine; variable modifications: oxidation of methionine and deamidation of asparagine and glutamine. Only proteins identified with ≥ 2 peptides at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 1% were included in quantification analysis.

Triton Solubility Assay

Triton‐soluble and ‐insoluble fractions from S100A4‐/‐ and littermate wild‐type mouse lenses were prepared following the protocol of Zhang and Gunst (W. Zhang and Gunst 2020) with minor modifications. Briefly, lenses from 6 to 7‐month‐old mice (four lenses per sample, pooled) were homogenized in 400 µl of Triton X‐100 lysis buffer containing 150 mM KCl, 20 mM PIPES, 10 mM imidazole (pH 7.0), 0.05% Triton X‐100, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM PMSF, and supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (PhosSTOP) at 4°C. After 5 min incubation at 4°C, lysates were centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected as the Triton‐soluble fraction. The pellet was resuspended in an equal volume (400 µl) of Triton X‐100 lysis buffer containing 0.8% SDS and 2 mM EDTA, gently disrupted with a plastic pestle, boiled for 5 min, and rotated for 1 h at room temperature. Following centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected as the Triton‐insoluble fraction. Protein concentrations of both fractions were determined using micro‐BCA assay, and equal amounts of protein (50 μg) were used for NMIIA immunoblot analysis.

Analysis of F‐Actin and G‐Actin

The relative proportions of F‐actin and G‐actin in S100A4‐/‐ and control lenses was analyzed as described by Zhang and Gunst (W. Zhang and Gunst 2020). Briefly, lenses from 6 to 7‐month‐old mice (four lenses per sample, pooled) were homogenized in 600 µL of F‐actin stabilization buffer [50 mM Pipes, pH 6.9; 50 mM NaCl; 5 mM MgCl2; 5 mM EGTA; 5% glycerol; 0.1% Triton X‐100; 0.1% Nonidet P‐40; 0.1% Tween‐20; 0.1% β‐mercaptoethanol; 0.001% anti‐foam; 1 mM ATP] supplemented with protease inhibitors and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor. Lysates were centrifuged at 150,000 × g for 60 min at 37°C (Optima™ TL Ultra centrifuge, Beckman Coulter, CA, USA). The supernatants were collected as G‐actin fractions, while the pellets, representing F‐actin fractions, were resuspended in 150 µL of ice‐cold water containing 10 µM cytochalasin D and incubated on ice for 1 h, mixing every 15 min to depolymerize F‐actin. Four microliters of each fraction (G‐actin and F‐actin) were subjected to immunoblotting using an anti‐actin antibody (clone AC‐40; Sigma). F‐actin to G‐actin ratios were quantified by densitometry.

Lens Fixation, Sectioning and Immunofluorescence

For paraffin sectioning, lenses from 6‐month‐old S100A4‐/‐ and littermate control mice were fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 48 h. To ensure proper fixation, a small incision was made at the optic stalk to facilitate optimal penetration of the fixative. Following fixation, lenses were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of 5 µm thickness were cut using a Leica Leitz 1512 microtome (Leica Biosystems) in either the sagittal or equatorial plane and stored at room temperature until further use.

For immunofluorescence, paraffin sections (three sections per slide from three independent mice) were deparaffinized in three changes of xylene, rehydrated through graded alcohols, and subjected to antigen retrieval by immersion in preheated 0.1 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 100°C for 20 min, as described previously (Maddala et al. 2011). After rinsing, sections were blocked for 10 min in a humidified chamber using Background Sniper solution (Biocare Medical, Concord, CA, USA), then incubated overnight at 4°C with β‐actin monoclonal antibodies (Table S1). Sections were washed with Tris‐buffered saline (TBS) and incubated with Alexa Fluor 594‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) in a dark humidified chamber for 2 h at room temperature. After a final TBS wash, sections were coverslipped with Vectamount mounting medium, sealed with nail polish, and imaged using a Nikon Eclipse 90i confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY, USA).

Lens Organ Culture and Drug Treatment

Freshly dissected lenses from 1‐month‐old mice were incubated at 37°C under 5% CO2 in low‐glucose DMEM (Invitrogen) supplemented with penicillin (100 U/ml) and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). After acclimatization in culture medium for 16–18 h, lenses were screened for any dissection‐associated damage, and damaged lenses were excluded, as previously described (Maddala et al. 2007). Contralateral clear lenses were then transferred to either control medium or medium containing 100 µM Trifluoperazine dihydrochloride (TFP; Millipore‐Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and incubated for 5 or 24 h. Lens transparency was monitored using a dissecting light microscope. At the end of the treatment period, images were captured under dark illumination with a Zeiss Axio Cam ERc 5 s camera (Zeiss, White Plains, NY, USA). Lenses were weighed, homogenized in hypotonic buffer, and fractionated to isolate total (800 × g), cytosolic (100,000 × g supernatant), and membrane‐enriched (100,000 × g pellet) fractions, as described above. Protein concentrations were determined using the Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit.

SDS‐PAGE and Immunoblot Analysis

Equal amounts of protein from total, cytosolic, and membrane‐enriched fractions of organ‐cultured lenses were separated by SDS‐PAGE using either 4–20% Criterion XT precast gels or 10% Bis‐acrylamide gels, with 1× MOPS SDS running buffer (NP0001, Invitrogen, Life Technologies). Gels were subsequently stained with GelCode Blue stain reagent and imaged using the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio‐Rad).

For immunoblotting, G‐actin, F‐actin, Triton‐soluble or ‐insoluble fractions, total (800 × g supernatant), cytosolic, and membrane‐enriched fractions were resolved on 6%, 10% and 15% SDS‐PAGE gels, and 4‐15% gradient SDS‐PAGE gels (Bio‐Rad) depending on the molecular weight of the protein of interest. Proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes by electrophoresis. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween‐20, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies (Table S1) at a 1:1000 dilution. After washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Immunoblots were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and imaged with the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System. Loading controls were assessed using multiple approaches. Nitrocellulose membranes were stained with 0.1% Ponceau S (5% acetic acid) prior to immunoblotting, and images were recorded. In addition, SDS–PAGE gels were stained with GelCode Blue after protein transfer to verify uniform loading. For selected experiments, identical sample volumes were loaded onto parallel SDS–PAGE gels, electrophoresed and transferred simultaneously under identical conditions. One membrane was probed for GAPDH as a loading control, whereas the corresponding membrane was probed for the indicated target proteins.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software). One‐tailed Student's t‐tests were used to assess differences between S100A4‐/‐ and littermate controls, and between untreated and drug‐treated lens specimens. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with a minimum sample size of four per group. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

S100A4 Interaction with NMIIA and Other Cytoskeletal Proteins in the Lens

To gain broader insight into the role of S100A4 in lens architecture and function, we defined the S100A4 interactome by performing immunoprecipitation (IP) followed by proteomic analysis using a S100A4 polyclonal antibody, an IgG control, and lens lysates obtained from 1‐month‐old wild‐type mice. For this experiment, the 800 × g supernatant from four pooled lenses and a technical replicate were analyzed.

Proteomic comparison of S100A4 IPs with IgG controls identified a set of proteins enriched by at least 1.5‐fold (Table 1). As expected, S100A4 was the most highly enriched protein, confirming the specificity of the antibody. The second most enriched protein was hornerin, a known fused‐type protein from the S100 family (Makino et al. 2001).

Several previously reported S100A4‐interacting proteins were also enriched, including non‐muscle myosin IIA (NMIIA; encoded by Myh9), multiple tropomyosins, actin, and annexin A2.

In addition to these recognized S100A4‐interacting proteins, the S100A4 IPs contained a broad array of cytoskeletal and cytoskeleton‐associated proteins, including spectrin, vimentin, filamin A and B, α‐actinin 1 and 4, annexins A6 and A2, and EH domain–containing protein 2. Several membrane‐associated or channel proteins such as VDAC2 and VDAC3, aquaporin‐0, and caveolae‐associated protein 1 were also enriched, along with dynamin‐like 120 kDa protein, nucleophosmin, junction plakoglobin, fatty acid‐binding protein 5, thioredoxin, proteasome subunit α5, F‐box only protein 50, and multiple crystallins.

To independently validate selected candidates identified by mass spectrometry, the presence of representative proteins including NMIIA was evaluated by immunoblotting using S100A4 IPs and IgG controls (Fig. S1). NMIIA was readily detected only in the S100A4 IP, confirming its interaction with S100A4 in the lens.

Impaired NMIIA Phosphorylation and Assembly in S100A4 Deficient Mouse Lenses

Although S100A4 expression is relatively low in most tissues under physiological conditions, it is notably abundant in the ocular lens, with strong enrichment in fiber cells relative to epithelial cells in mice (Maddala et al. 2021). Loss of S100A4 has been associated with late‐onset lens opacification (Maddala et al. 2021), yet its role in normal lens biology and the molecular basis of cataract formation in S100A4‐null mice remain unclear (Maddala et al. 2021). Because S100A4 coimmunoprecipitates with NMIIA and is well recognized as a regulator of NMIIA assembly and activity (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; Pecci et al. 2018), we investigated whether S100A4 deficiency alters NMIIA phosphorylation and assembly in the lens.

NMIIA phosphorylation at Ser1943, located near the C‐terminal end of the coiled‐coil domain, is mediated by casein kinase II (CK2) and is known to regulate NMIIA filament assembly and disassembly (Beach et al. 2011; Breckenridge et al. 2009; Dulyaninova et al. 2011; Dulyaninova et al. 2005; M. Kriajevska et al. 2000; Pecci et al. 2018; W. Zhang and Gunst 2020). To assess whether S100A4 loss affects NMIIA phosphorylation in the lens, we analyzed 800 × g supernatants from transparent lenses of 6 to 7 month‐month‐old S100A4–/– mice and littermate controls by immunoblotting with phospho‐Ser1943‐specific and total NMIIA antibodies. S100A4 deficiency led to a pronounced and significant reduction in phospho‐NMIIA (Ser1943) levels ( ~ 80%; n = 12) relative to controls, whereas total NMIIA levels were only minimally affected ( ~ 12% decrease; n = 12) (Figure 1A and B). Representative protein bands shown from the Gelcode blue stained SDS‐PAGE gel and Ponceau S‐stained nitrocellulose membrane confirm the equal protein loading for P‐NMIIA and NMIIA, respectively. Additionally, in these samples, the levels of GAPDH were found to be not different between S100A4‐/‐ and control lenses (Supplemental material; see original blots). These findings indicate that S100A4 deficiency impairs NMIIA phosphorylation in the lens.

Given the marked decrease in phospho‐NMIIA levels, we next evaluated whether NMIIA assembly was disrupted in S100A4‐deficient lenses. Using a Triton solubility assay as described by Zhang and Gunst (W. Zhang and Gunst 2020), we quantified NMIIA levels in Triton‐soluble and ‐insoluble fractions from S100A4‐/‐ and control lenses using equal amounts of protein. NMIIA levels in the insoluble (assembled) fraction were significantly reduced ( ~ 46%) in S100A4‐/‐ lenses (n = 7) relative to controls (Figure 1C, D). Conversely, the soluble fraction exhibited a robust and statistically significant increase in NMIIA levels in S100A4‐/‐ lenses compared with controls (Figure 1E,F), although greater variability was observed among the S100A4‐/‐ samples. Representative Ponceau S‐stained protein bands shown in Figure 1C and E confirmed equal protein loading in the soluble and insoluble fractions, respectively. For the soluble fraction samples, the levels of GAPDH were confirmed to be not different between the S100A4‐/‐ and control lenses (Supplemental material; see original blots). Consistently, the ratio of insoluble to soluble NMIIA was decreased ( ~ 87%) in S100A4‐/‐ lenses (Figure 1D).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that S100A4 deficiency leads to reduced NMIIA phosphorylation and impaired NMIIA assembly, resulting in increased disassembly of NMIIA in the lens.

Disruption of Actin Cytoskeletal Organization and Polymerization in S100A4 Deficient Lenses

Given that the S100A4 interactome in the lens contains several cytoskeletal proteins including NMIIA, actin, tropomyosin, spectrin, filamin, and α‐actinin and that loss of S100A4 leads to NMIIA disassembly, we next examined actin cytoskeletal organization in lenses from 6‐month‐old S100A4‐/‐ mice and their littermate controls. Paraffin‐embedded sagittal and equatorial sections from transparent S100A4‐/‐ and control lenses were analyzed for β‐actin distribution by immunofluorescence. In these lenses, β‐actin staining (low magnification images) was detected in the epithelium and cortical fiber cells (Fig. S2). The absence of staining in the nuclear fiber region likely reflects reduced antigen accessibility due to tissue hardness despite antigen retrieval (Fig. S2). Therefore, we focused our analyses on the outer and inner cortical regions of equatorial lens sections. The loss of S100A4 in S100A4‐/‐ lenses was verified by immunofluorescence (Fig. S2).

In control lenses, β‐actin was prominently localized in the cuboidal epithelial cells and the symmetrically arranged meridional long fibers, with strong enrichment at the short arms of the hexagonal fiber cells (Figure 2A). The dark oval zones correspond to fiber cell nuclei in the outer cortex. In contrast, S100A4‐/‐ lenses showed a mild disruption of meridional fiber alignment accompanied by reduced β‐actin staining. These differences are more evident in the magnified views of the outer cortical fibers (OCF) and inner cortical fibers (ICF), where β‐actin labeling is noticeably diminished in S100A4‐deficient lenses compared with controls (Figure 2B; magnified regions correspond to boxed areas in Figure 2A).

To complement the immunofluorescence studies, we also assessed actin polymerization biochemically by quantifying actin levels in detergent‐soluble (G‐actin) and detergent‐insoluble (F‐actin) fractions from S100A4‐/‐ and control lenses treated with cytochalasin D, following the method of Zhang and Gunst (2020). Using equal volumes of soluble and insoluble fractions, immunoblot analysis showed a significant decrease ( ~ 35%) in F‐actin in S100A4‐/‐ lenses relative to controls (Figure 2C,D). Conversely, G‐actin levels were moderately but significantly increased ( ~ 28%) in S100A4‐deficient lenses (Figure 2C,D). Total actin levels in whole lens lysates were unchanged, confirming equal loading and indicating that the observed differences reflect altered actin polymerization rather than changes in total actin abundance (Figure 2C,D). Notably, the F‐actin/G‐actin ratio was significantly reduced (by ~59%) in S100A4‐/‐ lenses (Figure 2D).

Together, these data demonstrate that the absence of S100A4 disrupts actin cytoskeletal organization and impairs actin polymerization in the lens.

Disruption of CLIC5 and SERBP1 Protein Profile in the Cytoskeletal‐and Membrane ‐Enriched Fractions of S100A4 Null Lenses

Because the S100A4 interactome included not only NMIIA and actin but also several other cytoskeletal and membrane‐associated proteins, we sought to further investigate the role of S100A4 in cytoskeletal networks and plasma membrane organization in the lens. To this end, we performed quantitative proteomic analyses of cytoskeletal‐enriched and membrane‐enriched fractions isolated from S100A4‐/‐ and control lenses (three to four pooled samples per group, processed separately as described in the Methods section).

Proteomic analysis of the membrane‐enriched fractions revealed multiple proteins that were differentially distributed between S100A4‐/‐ and control lenses. Table S2A &B lists proteins that were significantly decreased (45) or increased (15) in the S100A4‐/‐ samples. Cytoskeletal and cell‐adhesion–related proteins that were reduced in the membrane fraction of S100A4‐/‐ lenses included: integrin β1, neural cell adhesion molecule 1, chloride intracellular channel protein 5 (CLIC5), brain acid–soluble protein 1, tubulin β6 chain, plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 RNA‐binding protein (SERBP1), cytoplasmic dynein 1 intermediate chain 1, microtubule‐associated protein 4, limbic system–associated membrane protein, radixin, paralemmin‐1, and drebrin‐like protein. In contrast, Annexin A3, WD repeat–containing protein 1, neuronal cell adhesion molecule (NrCAM), Annexin A4, echinoderm microtubule‐associated protein‐like 2, aquaporin‐5, α‐crystallin A, and α‐crystallin B were significantly increased in S100A4‐/‐ lenses.

In comparison, the cytoskeletal‐enriched fractions from S100A4‐/‐ lenses displayed fewer proteins with altered abundance relative to controls (Table S3A &B). Proteins significantly decreased in the S100A4‐/‐ cytoskeletal fraction included tight junction protein ZO‐1, CLIC5, SERBP1, and α‐adducin. Proteins increased in this fraction included fatty acid binding protein 5, tubulin β2A chain, and T‐complex protein 1 subunit gamma.

We next examined proteins that were consistently altered in both membrane‐ and cytoskeletal‐enriched fractions. Only two proteins‐CLIC5 and SERBP1 showed significant decreases in both fractions of S100A4‐/‐ lenses compared with controls (Tables S2A and S3A). Immunoblot analyses of both membrane‐ and cytoskeletal‐enriched fractions demonstrated a pronounced and statistically significant reduction in CLIC5 and SERBP1 levels in S100A4‐/‐ lenses relative to control lenses (Figure 3A‐D).

Together, these findings demonstrate that loss of S100A4 disrupts the protein composition of both membrane‐ and cytoskeletal‐associated compartments in the lens. Given the tightly integrated relationship between the cytoskeleton and membrane architecture in lens fiber cells (Maddala et al. 2024), these results highlight a role for S100A4 in maintaining this interdependent structural organization.

Trifluoperazine‐Induced Lens Opacification

To further investigate the role of S100A4 in lens function, we utilized a pharmacological approach using trifluoperazine (TFP). Based on a biosensor‐guided high‐throughput screen, TFP was identified as an inhibitor that disrupts the interaction between S100A4 and NMIIA by promoting S100A4 oligomerization (Malashkevich et al. 2010). TFP, an antipsychotic drug has been associated with cataract formation in humans, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear (Isaac 1991; Margolis 1965).

To test the effects of TFP on the lens ex vivo, we cultured lenses from 1‐month‐old wild‐type mice in organ culture media and exposed them to 100 µM TFP, the minimum concentration required to inhibit S100A4 activity (Malashkevich et al. 2010). TFP treatment induced a time‐dependent loss of lens transparency, with lenses developing progressively severe cortical opacification after 5 and 24 h exposure, accompanied by significant increases in lens weight (Fig. S3A–D).

Analysis of soluble fractions (100,000 × g supernatant) revealed progressive reductions in S100A4 and NMIIA levels after 5 and 24 h of TFP exposure (Fig. S3E–H). In 24 h‐treated lenses, soluble β‐actin levels were also significantly reduced. In contrast, in the insoluble fractions from both 5 and 24 h TFP‐treated lenses, NMIIA levels were significantly increased compared to controls, and β‐actin levels were similarly elevated (Fig. S3I–L).

After 5 h of TFP treatment, total lysates (800 × g supernatant) showed a significant increase in NMIIA levels compared to controls. Despite this increase, phospho‐NMIIA (Ser1943) levels were significantly reduced. Total β‐actin levels remained unchanged (Fig. S4 A, B). In contrast, 24 h TFP treatment resulted in more pronounced opacity and greater lens swelling, and both NMIIA and phospho‐NMIIA levels were markedly decreased relative to controls, while β‐actin levels remained unchanged (Fig. S4 C, D).

To assess overall protein integrity, we examined total, soluble, and insoluble protein fractions by SDS–PAGE followed by Coomassie staining. TFP treatment produced distinct alterations in the protein profiles of the total and insoluble fractions compared to controls (Fig. S5, indicated with arrows).

Together, these findings indicate that TFP induces a progressive cortical lens opacity associated with increased lens weight, early upregulation NMIIA but subsequent loss of NMIIA and decreased p‐NMIIA, and increased insolubilization of S100A4, NMIIA, and actin, along with evidence of protein degradation.

Discussion

Our main objective in this study was to define the role of S100A4, a calcium‐binding protein that is robustly expressed in the ocular lens, where its absence leads to late‐onset opacification (Maddala et al. 2021). Using S100A4‐null mice, we demonstrate that S100A4 interacts with non‐muscle myosin IIA (NMIIA) and is required for proper NMIIA assembly, phosphorylation, and actin cytoskeletal organization in lens fibers. Specifically, S100A4 deficiency impairs NMIIA phosphorylation at Ser1943, disrupts NMIIA assembly, and reduces actin polymerization (F‐actin/G‐actin ratio), resulting in disorganization of the actin cytoskeleton in lens fibers. These findings are consistent with previous report showing that S100A4 regulates NMIIA assembly and actin polymerization in airway smooth muscle (W. Zhang and Gunst 2020), but they contrast with in vitro studies suggesting that S100A4 inhibits NMIIA phosphorylation and promotes NMIIA disassembly (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; M. Kriajevska et al. 2000). This difference highlights an important context‐dependent regulation of NMIIA by S100A4 under in vivo versus in vitro conditions.

Our interest in S100A4 stemmed from observing the prominent expression and distinctive localization of this protein in lens fibers, together with the finding that S100A4‐null mice develop late‐onset opalescent cataracts (Maddala et al. 2021). In our earlier study using 2–3‐month‐old S100A4‐null lenses, actin organization appeared largely preserved (Maddala et al. 2021). However, in this study, using 6 to 7‐month‐old S100A4‐/‐ mice, we observed disruptions in the cortical hexagonal fiber cell actin cytoskeleton organization compared to controls. These age‐dependent changes suggest that S100A4 plays a sustained role in maintaining cytoskeletal integrity and lens transparency during post adult life.

S100A4 is known to participate in diverse cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and migration and has been extensively studied in pathological contexts such as metastasis and fibrosis (Boye and Mælandsmo 2010; Garrett et al. 2006; Southern et al. 2024). Biochemical analyses across multiple cell types have identified NMIIA as a major S100A4‐interacting protein (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; Kiss et al. 2012; M. V. Kriajevska et al. 1994; Li and Bresnick 2006). NMIIA, like other myosin II isoforms, binds actin and regulates contraction, adhesion, and migration in numerous tissues (Pecci et al. 2018). Mechanistic studies using recombinant NMIIA fragments showed that S100A4 binds to the C‐terminal coiled‐coil region of NMIIA, modulating NMIIA filament disassembly (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013). Moreover, phosphorylation of NMIIA at Ser1943 mediated by CK2 has been shown to prevent S100A4 binding and protect NMIIA from S100A4‐induced disassembly in vitro (Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013). Intriguingly, NMIIA Ser1943 phosphorylation has also been reported to inhibit NMIIA assembly in both biochemical and cell‐based studies (Beach et al. 2011; Dulyaninova and Bresnick 2013; Dulyaninova et al. 2007; Egelhoff et al. 1993; Norwood Toro et al. 2018; Pecci et al. 2018). Our in vivo findings in S100A4‐null lenses show that loss of S100A4 leads to reduced Ser1943 phosphorylation and decreased NMIIA assembly, as evidenced by the shift of NMIIA from the Triton‐insoluble to soluble fraction. This decrease in NMIIA assembly was accompanied by reduced F‐actin levels and a lower F‐actin/G‐actin ratio, indicating defective actin polymerization and cytoskeletal organization. Together, these data reveal that S100A4 positively regulates NMIIA phosphorylation and assembly in the lens, and that its absence disrupts the actomyosin network essential for lens fiber structure and function.

Remarkably, the regulation of NMIIA assembly and actin polymerization by S100A4 in the lens mirrors that observed in airway smooth muscle (W. Zhang and Gunst 2020), despite the lack of smooth muscle–like features in the lens. The consistency of these in vivo findings across distinct tissues, and their divergence from in vitro results, suggests that additional regulatory or binding partners may modulate S100A4–NMIIA interactions in tissues and organs. Identifying these potential regulatory proteins will be an important direction for future studies.

Beyond NMIIA, our S100A4 interactome analysis revealed that S100A4 associates with multiple cytoskeletal and membrane‐associated proteins, including tropomyosins, actin, spectrin, and annexin II. The absence of S100A4 altered the levels of some of these proteins in both cytoskeletal‐ and membrane‐enriched fractions, suggesting a role for S100A4 in coordinating cytoskeletal and membrane organization. The primary effects of S100A4 deficiency are likely mediated through disrupted direct and indirect interactions with cytoskeletal components, with secondary consequences arising from actin disorganization.

Interestingly, although CLIC5 and the RNA‐binding protein SERBP1 were not identified in the S100A4 interactome, their levels were reduced in both cytoskeletal‐ and membrane‐enriched fractions of S100A4‐/‐ lenses. Given that CLIC5 interacts with actin and is essential for lens suture formation (Fan et al. 2018), and that loss of RNA‐binding proteins has been linked to cataractogenesis (Lachke 2022), these changes may further contribute to the lens abnormalities observed in S100A4‐deficient mice (Maddala et al. 2021). Supporting this notion, decreased CLIC5 levels and disrupted F‐actin organization have also been reported in lenses deficient in TDRD7, another RNA‐binding protein whose loss leads to cataract formation (Barnum et al. 2020). Additionally, several S100A4‐interacting proteins, as well as those dysregulated in the membrane and cytoskeletal fractions of S100A4‐/‐ lenses, are highly expressed in rodent and human lenses based on RNA‐seq and iSyTE datasets (Kakrana et al. 2018; Maddala et al. 2021). This observation further supports a broader role for S100A4 in maintaining lens architecture and function.

We also examined the effects of trifluoperazine (TFP), a known inhibitor of S100A4 (Malashkevich et al. 2010). TFP treatment altered lens transparency and increased lens weight, suggesting disruption of S100A4 function. However, since TFP also inhibits calmodulin and troponin C and affects gap junction coupling (Blödow et al. 2003; Massom et al. 1990; Vandonselaar et al. 1994), its specificity for S100A4 in this context remains uncertain. Although TFP exposure reduced NMIIA Ser1943 phosphorylation and induced S100A4 and NMIIA aggregation, caution is warranted in interpreting these effects due to the compound's broad target profile and the secondary consequences of lens opacification.

In conclusion, our findings establish S100A4 as a key regulator of NMIIA assembly, phosphorylation, and actin cytoskeletal organization in the ocular lens. The absence of S100A4 disrupts these processes, leading to disordered lens fiber architecture and compromised transparency. These results reveal a novel physiological role for S100A4 in maintaining lens homeostasis and provide new insight into cytoskeletal regulation mechanisms that preserve lens structure and function.
