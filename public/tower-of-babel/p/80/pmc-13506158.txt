The Small Ubiquitin-Like Modifier SUMO-3 Acts as a Neutrophil Chemoattractant via the Chemokine Receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2
Authors: Sean P. Giblin, Vlad Razvan Moiseanu, Christina Josephine Carrington, Audrey Naing, Killian Watkins, Tomoko Tsuchiya, Shiro Kanegasaki, James Edward Pease
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSmall ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMOs) are small peptides conjugated to proteins during post-translational modification, which have been reported to modulate several aspects of the immune system, notably in autoimmune disorders.MethodsWe used a SUMO-based bacterial expression system to create a recombinant protein putatively expressed by Toxocara canis, which we hypothesised might antagonise responses via the chemokine receptor CXCR1.ResultsAlthough our recombinant T. canis protein was devoid of antagonist activity, we serendipitously observed that recombinant SUMO-3 protein had chemotactic activity for CXCR1 transfectants. Further study found that SUMO-3 acted as a full agonist of CXCR1 and the closely related receptor CXCR2, the latter responses ablated by a CXCR2 antagonist. SUMO-3 showed similar efficacy at both receptors but reduced potency when compared to CXCL8, with chemotaxis observed at high nanomolar to micromolar concentrations. In receptor endocytosis assays, SUMO-3 induced internalisation of CXCR1 and CXCR2, with inferior potency and efficacy to CXCL8. Translating our findings to primary cells, a broad range of SUMO-3 concentration gradients were shown to induce the chemotaxis of human neutrophils. Finally, SUMO-3 was found to be released by necrotic cells into the extracellular milieu.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings suggest that SUMO-3 can induce the chemotaxis of neutrophils via CXCR1 and CXCR2. We postulate that in vivo, release of SUMO-3 from necrotic cells may serve to recruit neutrophils, contributing to tissue homeostasis and the resolution of inflammation.

Introduction

Neutrophils have been described as the foot soldiers of the immune system [1], the first of the innate immune cells to arrive at the scene of tissue damage and infection, with the job of immobilizing and destroying pathogens and beginning the tissue repair process. Key to this process are chemoattractants, released in response to infection or cell death and setting in motion the recruitment of neutrophils from the circulation to the tissues. This process is effected, in large part, by a variety of G protein-coupled receptors expressed on the neutrophil surface, which bind chemoattractants and guide the directed migration (chemotaxis) of neutrophils along attractant gradients. Prominent amongst neutrophil chemoattractants are a group of 6 chemokines dubbed ELR+ chemokines (CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL5, CXCL7, and CXCL8), which possess an N-terminal glutamate-leucine-arginine motif upstream of their first cysteine residue [2]. ELR+ chemokines are released by a variety of inflammatory and structural cells in response to infection and activate the receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2 [3]. The chemokine network has not gone unnoticed by microbes, with many reported strategies for exploitation and subversion [4]. ELR+ chemokines and the receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2 are front-line targets for evasion of neutrophil responses. For example, Streptococcus pyogenes (group A Streptococcus) can produce a cell-envelope protease known as SpyCEP [5], which specifically cleaves all ELR+ chemokines, rendering them inactive in neutrophil chemotaxis [6].

Neutrophils also play a role in the initial responses against some helminth infections. Adult Toxocara canis hookworms reside in the small intestines of dogs [7] and, in mouse studies, have been shown to induce an initial neutrophilia during early infection [8]. In vitro co-culture of canine neutrophils with infective stage T. canis larvae results in neutrophil activation, as evidenced by the generation of neutrophil extracellular traps or NETs [9], which are envisaged to impede the migration of infective larvae within the host. We hypothesized that helminths may produce novel molecules with the express purpose of disabling neutrophil:chemokine signalling networks. By probing a pubic database, we identified a potential gene product within the genome of T. canis named Tcan 09600.1 with significant homology to the first transmembrane domain of CXCR1. We named this protein the T. canis protein (TCP), which we envisaged might act as an inhibitor of ELR+ chemokine signalling by association with CXCR1.

To test our hypothesis, we expressed TCP as a fusion protein, in frame with the protein SUMO-3, a member of the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) family of proteins. SUMO proteins are small soluble proteins that are covalently conjugated to other proteins in a process known as SUMOylation, a predominantly nuclear process [10]. Four SUMO paralogues exist in humans, named SUMO-1-4 with varying degrees of sequence conservation (Fig. 1a). Initially produced as precursors, SUMO proteins undergo specific C-terminal truncation by enzymes known as sentrin-specific proteases to expose a di-glycine motif required for the conjugation process [11]. Overall, SUMOylation has many parallels to ubiquitination [12], and there is a growing appreciation that SUMOylation plays modulatory roles in innate and adaptive immunity by directly and indirectly targeting key transcription factors [13]. For example, SUMOylation has been shown to silence Ifnb1 gene expression and dampen the NF-κB-dependent production of cytokines following TLR signalling [14]. Similarly, SUMOylation of NFATc1 in T cells has been shown to reduce IL-2 expression [15], with enhanced tolerance reported in transgenic mice in which SUMO modification of NFATc1 is prohibited, presumably mediated by the proliferation of T regulatory cells [16].

Conjugated SUMO proteins can be removed from proteins by recombinant sentrin-specific proteases, making the system a convenient tool for the introduction and removal of tags at specific sites within proteins. Indeed, we have previously used the system to generate recombinant chemokines in bacteria with native amino-termini devoid of signal peptides [17]. In this study, we exploited the SUMO system to generate a recombinant SUMO-3-TCP fusion protein and examine its potential to antagonise CXCR1. Contrary to our original hypothesis, we were unable to show any antagonist activity for the SUMO-3-TCP fusion protein at CXCR1. However, we serendipitously discovered that SUMO-3 has chemotactic activity for neutrophils via CXCR1 and CXCR2 and is released by necrotic cells. We postulate that this may have implications for the host response to cell death and tissue damage.

Methods

Materials

All materials were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Paisley, UK) unless stated otherwise. CXCL8 was obtained from Bio-Techne (Abingdon, UK). Recombinant SUMO-1 and SUMO-2 proteins were purchased from the MRC PPU Reagents and Services, Faculty of Life Sciences, University of Dundee, UK. Their primary sequences are highlighted in Figure 1b.

Recombinant SUMO-3 Fusion and SUMO Proteins

SUMO-3 and SUMO-3-TCP were expressed and purified as fusion proteins containing an N-terminal 6-His tag, using previously described methods [17]. Briefly, a cDNA encoded by the Tcan 09600.1 transcript (online suppl. Fig. 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553655) identified in the WormBase ParaSite database (https://parasite.wormbase.org/index.html) was synthesized by (GenScript, Leiden, The Netherlands) in which the open reading frame of TCP was tagged at the 5′ end with a sequence encoding an in-frame 6-histidine tag directly after the initial ATG start codon. This was amplified by oligonucleotide primers (Eurofins, Ebersberg, Germany) using the Phusion 10x DNA PCR high fidelity kit from New England Biolabs (Hertfordshire, UK). The primers contained sites for the enzymes AgeI (5′) and BamH1(3′), allowing in-frame insertion into a modified version of the plasmid pE-SUMOpro3 AMP (LifeSensors Inc., PA, USA), as we have previously described [17]. Addition of the SUMO-3 Tag was predicted to aid solubility of the TCP protein and allowed purification of the protein by Immobilized Metal Affinity Chromatography via an N-terminal 6-His tag followed by clean-up via size exclusion chromatography as outlined in Supplementary Figure 2. Purified proteins were estimated to be 95% pure and were dialyzed into storage buffer (50 mm Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mm NaCl, and 10% glycerol) and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen prior to storage at −20°C.

Cell Culture

The murine pro-B cell lines Ba/F3 and L1.2 were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 in RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS, 25 mm HEPES, 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Media for Ba/F3 cells was supplemented with 1 ng/mL mouse IL-3 (PeproTech, London, UK). Stable Ba/F3 transfectants expressing human CXCR1 or human CXCR2 were generated using previously described techniques [18]. Cells were passaged every 2–3 days and maintained at around 1 × 106/mL. Prior to use in assays, cells were counted using a haemocytometer and resuspended in fresh media supplemented with 10 mm sodium butyrate for overnight culture. MOLT-4 cells [19] were purchased from the Culture Collections UK Health Security Agency (Porton Down, UK) and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS, 25 mm HEPES, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Tissue-culture grade Polymyxin-B was purchased as a sterile 17 mg/mL solution in water from Merck, UK.

Neutrophil Extraction and Preparation

Human neutrophils were extracted from whole blood samples and isolated using the MACSxpress® Whole Blood Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Miltenyi), according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Red cell lysis was carried out using hypotonic sodium chloride. Before conducting chemotaxis assays, neutrophils were resuspended in chemotaxis buffer (RPMI 1640, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 0.1% BSA), at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/ml and rested for 1 h at 37°C.

Chemotaxis Assays

All chemotaxis assays were carried out in a chemotaxis buffer comprised of RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 25 mm HEPES, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 0.1% BSA. Modified Boyden chamber assays were conducted using Corning® HTS Transwell®-96 plates with membranes containing 5 µm pores (Merck, Poole, UK) following a previously described method [20]. Briefly, cells were resuspended at 1 × 107 cells/mL in chemotaxis buffer. 120 μL of chemotaxis buffer or chemotaxis buffer supplemented with increasing concentrations of chemoattractant was placed in the lower chambers, the filter was replaced, and 50 μL of cell suspension (200,000 cells) was placed in the upper chambers. Plates were placed in a humidified box and incubated for 5 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. The filters were removed, and the lower chamber solution containing migrated cells was mixed with an equal volume of CellTiter-Glo reagent (Promega, Southampton, UK) and luminescence measured using a SpectraMax plate reader. The percentage of migrating cells was calculated as a percentage of the luminescence observed from cell inputs. Chemotactic indices were calculated by dividing the luminescence values from cells migrating to chemoattractant stimulus by the basal level of migration observed in the absence of stimulation.

Real-time chemotaxis analysis was performed using a 12-channel TAXIScan (Effector Cell Institute, Tokyo, Japan) operated in accordance with previous protocols [6, 20]. Each chamber was loaded with 1 µL of 5 × 105 neutrophils/mL suspended in chemotaxis buffer. After neutrophil alignment, 1 µL of chemoattractant at the stated concentration was added to the opposite end of the terrace to allow gradient formation. Images were captured every 60 s, and individual migration paths were manually tracked using the manual tracking function of ImageJ. Chemotaxis was quantified using the IBIDI chemotaxis tool and was expressed as velocity (μm/min) and y-axis directional forward migration index (yFMI). yFMI describes the forward migration of cells parallel to the chemokine gradient and is calculated by dividing the Δy value of a cell track end-point by the total accumulated distance travelled [21].

Analysis of Receptor Expression

Cells were stained with PE-labelled anti-CXCR1, anti-CXCR2, or anti-HA mAbs (Clones 8F1, 5E8, and 16B12, BioLegend, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, using TO-PRO3 to exclude dead cells. Analysis was carried out on a FACSCalibur or FACSDiva (BD Biosciences, Wokingham, UK) belonging to the Imperial College Core Flow Cytometry facilities (South Kensington and Hammersmith Campuses).

Competitive CXCL8 Binding Assays

These were carried out with a minor modification to the protocol of Schoofs and colleagues [22]. Cells were collected by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min, room temperature) and resuspended in chemotaxis buffer at 5 × 106/mL. Tubes containing 50 μL of 4 nm AF647-CXCL8 were placed on ice and 100 μL binding buffer (PBS, 0.1% BSA, 0.05% NaN3) with or without unlabelled chemoattractants at various concentrations (in duplicate). 50 μL of cell suspension was added with gentle mixing, and the cells were allowed to incubate for 30 min on ice. Cells were then washed with 200 μL of binding buffer, pelleted by centrifugation, resuspended in 200 μL of PBS with 1% paraformaldehyde, and transferred to FACS tubes. Cells were analysed on a FACSCalibur, gating on FFC/SSC characteristics and acquiring the signal in the FL-4 channel. Mean fluorescence indices were derived and expressed as a percentage of the control (cells incubated in buffer with AF647-CXCL8).

Receptor Endocytosis Assays

Receptor endocytosis assays were conducted to measure surface CXCR1 and CXCR2 expression levels on Ba/F3 cells after incubation with ligand as previously described [23]. Cells were incubated for 30 min at 37°C in chemotaxis buffer containing various concentrations of SUMO-3 or CXCL8, after which they were washed and stained for CXCR1 or CXCR2 using the flow cytometry methods described above. The reduction in fluorescent signal due to receptor endocytosis was calculated as a percentage of the staining observed in cells that were incubated in buffer alone.

MMP-9 Release following Neutrophil Degranulation

This methodology was based on that previously reported by Chakrabarti and colleagues [24]. Isolated neutrophils were resuspended in RPMI media at a density of 1 × 107 cells/mL and 50 µL volumes were aliquoted into 4 tubes. These were allowed to rest at 37°C for 30 min, after which an equal volume of supplemented RPMI media was added to the cells to generate final concentrations of 10 nm CXCL8, 1 μm SUMO-3 or 10 μm PMA (positive control). Cells were gently shaken on a Thermomixer at 37°C, 300 rpm, and degranulation was allowed to proceed for 30 min. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min, and the supernatants were removed and frozen at −20°C until required. As a negative control, cells were also shaken in the presence of RPMI alone. Supernatants were assayed for MMP-9 concentration using a Duo-set sandwich ELISA specific for human MMP-9 (Bio-Techne).

Release of HMGB-1 and SUMO-3 following Cell Necrosis

5 × 106 MOLT-4 cells were collected by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min) and washed in 1 mL of PBS, pH 7.4. The cells were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in 100 μL of PBS containing Roche cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, UK). Cell suspensions were then subjected to 3 rounds of freezing on dry ice (5 min) followed by incubation at 37°C (5 min). Cells were finally pelleted by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min), and the supernatant was removed and frozen at −20°C until required. Whole-cell MOLT-4 lysates were also generated by lysis of 1 × 107 MOLT-4 cells in RIPA buffer supplemented with Roche cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail. The protein content of lysates and supernatants was determined using a BCA assay (Pierce, UK) prior to analysis. 13 μg of MOLT-4 supernatant and 7 μg of whole MOLT-4 cell lysate were resolved alongside known amounts of recombinant SUMO-3 by reducing SDS-PAGE on 10% NuPAGE Bis-Tris gels. Multiplexed Western blots were performed using iBlot™ transfer with nitrocellulose membranes blocked in PBS-T with 5% BSA. Membranes were then probed with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4°C: high-mobility group nucleosome-binding protein (HMGB-1) detection with 1:2000 Mouse monoclonal (Clone 1F3, Bio-Techne) and SUMO-3 detection with 1:5000 rabbit anti-SUMO2/3 antisera (Proteintech). Following washing in PBS/0.05% Tween-20, secondary antibodies (anti-mouse; A11357/rabbit; A21109 Alexa Fluor™ conjugates) were used at a concentration of 0.01 µg/mL and subsequently detected with an Odyssey XF imager.

Molecular Modelling

CXCR1:SUMO-3 docking was predicted by AlphaFold3 using the Protenix Server [25]. The amino acid sequences for CXCR1(1–350) and SUMO-3 (1–92) were uploaded, and multiple sequence alignment was enabled to improve the folding accuracy. Models were analysed using the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (version 3.1.6.1, Schrödinger, LLC, USA). Hydrogen bonds predicted by PyMOL are shown as dashed lines and are <3.4 Å in length.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

In an effort to find parasite-encoded proteins with the potential to modulate chemokine receptor signaling, we searched a publicly available database containing several parasite genomes (https://parasite.wormbase.org/index.html). This resulted in the identification of 2 transcripts with homology to human CXCR1: Tcan 09600.1 and ALUE_0002211601, which are encoded by the genomes of T. canis and Ascaris lumbricoides, respectively. The predicted proteins from both species have homology with the N-terminus of CXCR1 (online suppl. Fig. S1), leading us to hypothesise that the proteins may interfere with ligand binding at this receptor, thus helping the worms to evade the host neutrophil response. We focussed our attention on the Tcan 09600.1 transcript and produced a recombinant protein in Escherichia coli using a SUMO-3 fusion system we and others have used previously to express and purify recombinant chemokines [17]. SUMO-3 is a member of the family of SUMOs [26] and, following activation, is ligated to a variety of proteins via its C-terminal di-glycine motif (Fig. 1a). The resulting fusion protein was named 6-His-SUMO-3-TCP, and as a relevant control for subsequent assays, we also generated 6-His-SUMO-3 protein (Fig. 1b). Given that the bulk of the TCP appears to mimic the first helix of CXCR1, which is hydrophobic, we retained the SUMO-3 fusion partner to aid with solubility. Ba/F3 transfectants stably expressing CXCR1 (Fig. 1c) were assessed for their chemotactic response by a modified Boyden chamber assay, using a fixed concentration of 1 nm CXCL8 in the presence or absence of increasing concentrations of SUMO-3 or SUMO-3-TCP (Fig. 1d). Robust responses to CXCL8 were observed, which, disappointingly, were unaffected by the addition of increasing concentrations of SUMO-3-TCP. Interestingly, the presence of increasing concentrations of the control SUMO-3 protein appeared to moderately increase the chemotactic response in a dose-dependent manner. We subsequently assessed whether either protein alone was able to act as an agonist and to recruit CXCR1 transfectants in chemotaxis. Whilst the SUMO-3-TCP protein was devoid of chemotactic activity, to our surprise, increasing concentrations of the SUMO-3 protein were able to recruit transfectants, with a 1 μm SUMO-3 concentration inducing migratory responses significantly above those of buffer alone (Fig. 1e).

The related protein ubiquitin has previously been reported to act as a ligand at the related chemokine receptor CXCR4 [27], which is expressed by many leukocyte subtypes. To test if SUMO-3 was acting via the CXCR1 introduced into the Ba/F3 transfectant line or via endogenous CXCR4, we compared chemotactic responses of the parental Ba/F3 line and the Ba/F3-CXCR1 line to fixed concentrations of CXCL12, CXCL8, and SUMO-3 (Fig. 2a). Both the parental line and CXCR1 transfectants appeared to respond to CXCL12, suggesting functional expression of CXCR4 on both cell lines. As expected, CXCL8 induced significant responses from Ba/F3-CXCR1 but not from Ba/F3 cells. Likewise, SUMO-3 was only able to elicit chemotactic responses from the Ba/F3-CXCR1 transfectants, supporting the notion that SUMO-3 is acting as a ligand at CXCR1. We subsequently compared CXCL8 and SUMO-3 in chemotaxis assays using a broad range of ligand concentrations (Fig. 2b). SUMO-3 appeared to have considerably lower potency but similar efficacy to CXCL8, with 1 μm SUMO-3 appearing to be the optimal concentration in inducing chemotactic responses. As a precaution against the potential influence of endotoxin on the properties of our recombinant proteins [28], chemotaxis assays were also performed with the inclusion of polymyxin-B at a concentration of 10 μg/mL in the chemotaxis buffer (online suppl. Fig. 3). Robust responses of CXCR1 transfectants to both CXCL8 and SUMO-3 in the presence of polymyxin-B reassured us that endotoxin contamination of our SUMO-3 preparation was not responsible for the chemotactic response observed.

CXCR1 shares considerable homology with CXCR2 and neutrophils utilise both receptors to navigate CXCL8 gradients [29]. We therefore assessed if SUMO-3 was also able to activate CXCR2. Ba/F3 transfectants stably expressing CXCR2 (Fig. 3a) were used in chemotaxis assays with an identical range of ligand concentrations (Fig. 3a). As we observed with CXCR1 transfectants (Fig. 2b), SUMO-3 appeared to have considerably lower potency but similar efficacy to CXCL8 in inducing chemotactic responses from CXCR2 transfectants (Fig. 3b), with 1 μm SUMO-3 appearing to be the optimal concentration in driving migration. Direct comparisons of SUMO-3 responses from both CXCR1 and CXCR2 transfectants revealed them to have similar dose-dependent profiles (Fig. 4a), with chemotactic activity only observed in the high nanomolar, low micromolar range. Chemotactic activity appears to be restricted to SUMO-3, as recombinant SUMO-1 and SUMO-2 proteins were unable to drive migration of either CXCR1 (Fig. 4b) or CXCR2 (Fig. 4c) transfectants when employed at fixed concentrations of 1 μm and 3 μm.

SUMO-3 but not SUMO-1 or SUMO-2 is chemotactic for Ba/F3 CXCR1 and CXCR2 transfectants. a Chemotactic responses of Ba/F3 CXCR1 and CXCR2 transfectants to identical concentration ranges of SUMO-3 (n = 4). Chemotactic responses of Ba/F3 CXCR1 (b) and CXCR2 transfectants (c) to fixed concentrations of SUMO-1, SUMO-2, and SUMO-3 (n = 3).

We subsequently expanded our range of assays with the Ba/F3 transfectant cell lines, to assess ligand binding and receptor endocytosis. In competitive ligand binding assays, whilst unlabelled CXCL8 was readily able to compete with 1 nm AF-647-labelled CXCL8 at both CXCR1 and CXCR2 transfectants in a concentration-dependent fashion, SUMO-3 was unable to compete for binding to either receptor, even when used at a 300-fold excess (Fig. 5a, b). The same transfectant lines were also used in endocytosis assays, with increasing concentrations of ligand incubated with cells at 37°C, prior to staining for either receptor and analysis by flow cytometry. Whilst CXCL8 induced significant endocytosis of both CXCR1 at all concentrations tested, SUMO-3 was much less potent and efficacious, with significant levels of receptor endocytosis only observed at 10 μm SUMO-3 (Fig. 5c). For CXCR2, SUMO-3 was a little more potent, inducing significant endocytosis at several concentrations approaching the efficacy of CXCL8 (Fig. 5d). Collectively, these data suggest that SUMO-3 is a partial agonist of CXCR1 and CXCR2 with relatively weak affinity for either receptor when compared to CXCL8.

Having established SUMO-3 as an agonist of CXCR1 and CXCR2 in transfectant cell lines, we set out to assess the functional relevance of this observation in terms of immune cell activation. Human neutrophils were purified by negative selection from healthy volunteers and assessed using a real-time chemotaxis assay, TAXIScan, as previously employed by our group [6]. 1 μL volumes of 100 nm CXCL8 or 20 μm SUMO-3 were used to generate gradients of chemoattractant, and the ability of neutrophils to traverse a 260 μm terrace was captured by time-lapse microscopy (Fig. 6). While little migration was seen in the absence of a stimulus (Fig. 6a), both CXCL8 and SUMO-3 were effective at recruiting neutrophils (Fig. 6b, c). Subsequent analysis of the tracks showed that both ligands significantly increased the velocity (Fig. 6d) and directional migration along the y-axis (yFMI), i.e., towards the source of the chemoattractant (Fig. 6e). We broadened these assays to assess navigation of a variety of SUMO-3 gradients produced by different concentrations, namely 100 nm, 1 μm, and 10 μm SUMO-3 (Fig. 7). All 3 concentrations of SUMO-3 were seen to recruit neutrophils above the levels recorded in the absence of a stimulus (Fig. 7a–d). In terms of velocity, only 10 μm SUMO-3 significantly increased levels above those seen in basal migration (Fig. 7e), whereas all 3 SUMO-3 concentrations induced significant levels of directional migration when compared to that observed in the absence of a stimulus (Fig. 7f). Thus, we conclude that SUMO-3 is a functional chemoattractant for human neutrophils at nanomolar concentrations.

We subsequently enquired whether SUMO-3 was able to elicit additional signaling responses in neutrophils. Since CXCL8 has been reported to induce neutrophil degranulation via CXCR2 activation [24], we turned to this assay, using MMP-9 released as a marker of the process (Fig. 8a). Neutrophils from 4 different donors were isolated and MMP-9 release detected by ELISA following stimulation with 10 nm CXCL8, 1 μm SUMO-3, or 10 μm PMA. PMA was observed to induce a significant release of MMP-9 above basal levels, with CXCL8 inducing a more modest release which failed to reach significance. In contrast, SUMO-3 was unable to induce release beyond that observed in the absence of stimulation. Thus, we conclude that the conformation of CXCR2 stabilized by SUMO-3 is unable to activate the signaling pathways leading to degranulation and that functional activity appears to be limited to inducing neutrophil chemotaxis.

Recombinant SUMO-3 of differing amounts was used as a positive control for the SUMO-2/3 antisera. Data are representative of three separate experiments.

Since SUMO-3 is predominantly located in the nucleus of cells, we envisaged any chemotactic activity for neutrophils to be restricted to SUMO-3 released by necrotic cells. To test this hypothesis, we subjected MOLT-4 cells to a modified version of a previously reported freeze-thaw cycle [30] to render them necrotic. We subsequently analyzed the proteins released into the supernatant by Western blot (Fig. 8b). As a control, we also probed the blot for HMGB-1, a well-characterized alarmin [31], predominantly located in the nucleus and previously reported to be released from necrotic cells [30]. Free SUMO-3 was detected in both the supernatants and lysates of MOLT-4 cells, running at an apparent molecular weight slightly in excess of the theoretical 11.6 kDa, as previously reported [32]. Numerous additional higher molecular weight bands presumably reflecting SUMOylated proteins were also detected in both fractions, as was HMGB-1, running at the anticipated 30 kDa. Thus, SUMO-3 is released by necrotic cells with the potential to act as an alarmin and facilitate neutrophil recruitment.

To further assess the molecular requirements for receptor activation, we utilized a panel of chimeric CXCR1:hCXCR3 receptors we had previously used to assess hCXCR3 activation [33]. The panel comprised the wild-type CXCR1 and CXCR3 constructs together with 2 constructs (chi-1 and chi-5) in which the N-termini of CXCR1 and hCXCR3 are exchanged (Fig. 9a), thus allowing the role of the N-terminus in receptor activation to be probed. All 4 constructs were transiently expressed on the surface of the mouse pre-B cell line L1.2, at detectable levels (Fig. 9b). We subsequently assessed the chemotactic responses of transfectants expressing either of the 4 constructs, using fixed concentrations of CXCL8, CXCL11, and SUMO-3 (Fig. 9c). As anticipated from previous experiments, CXCR1 transfectants responded to CXCL8 and SUMO-3 but not to CXCL11. Conversely, hCXCR3 transfectants responded to their cognate ligand CXCL11 but not to CXCL8 or SUMO-3. SUMO-3 was unable to induce chemotaxis from cells expressing either of the chimeric constructs, despite the chimeras being expressed at levels not significantly different from the parental constructs (Fig. 9b). Turning to CXCR2, we also assessed whether a small molecule inhibitor of CXCR2 named SB 225002 [34] was able to inhibit SUMO-3 signaling at this receptor. L1.2 cells transiently expressing CXCR2 were preincubated with buffer containing either vehicle (DMSO) or 10 μm SB 225002 and then subjected to Boyden chamber chemotaxis assays using 10 nm CXCL8 and 1 μm SUMO-3 as stimuli (Fig. 9d). As expected, the inhibitor was able to significantly inhibit CXCL8-induced migration to basal migratory levels. Similarly, when SUMO-3 responses were examined, SB 225002 was able to significantly reduce migration to basal levels. Collectively, these data suggest that activation of both CXCR1 and CXCR2 by SUMO-3 shares some elements with the cognate ligand CXCL8.

Discussion

We have described here, the serendipitous discovery that SUMO-3 has chemotactic activity for neutrophils, acting as a ligand for the chemokine receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2. Assays with both chemokine receptor transfectants and human neutrophils revealed SUMO-3 to be active in classical assays of chemokine receptor activation, namely chemotaxis and endocytosis. This is reminiscent of previous reports showing ubiquitin to bind to CXCR4 in monocyte-like cell lines and activate Gαi coupling leading to inhibition of cAMP accumulation and an influx of Ca2+ [27]. As we observed with the response of our CXCR1 and CXCR2 transfectants to SUMO-3, micromolar concentrations of ubiquitin were reported to be needed for CXCR4 activation, with the affinity for ubiquitin estimated to be approximately 100 nm, more than 10-fold lower than reported for the cognate ligand CXCL12 [35]. Subsequent work has reported ubiquitin to be a biased agonist of CXCR4, unable to activate β-arrestin signalling [36], and supporting the idea that each ligand stabilizes a subtly different CXCR4 conformation leading to different signaling outputs.

Using a panel of previously described CXCR1:CXCR3 chimeras [33], we found that the N-terminus of CXCR1 was required for chemotactic responses to both CXCL8 and SUMO-3. In terms of CXCL8 signaling, this agrees with the cryo-EM structure of the CXCL8:CXCR1 complex, which found the N-terminus of CXCR1 to bind to the core of CXCL8, forming the first of the chemokine recognition sites (CRS1) [37]. Modelling of the CXCR1:SUMO-3 interaction using AlphaFold 3 supported our observation that replacement of the CXCR1 N-terminus correlated with a loss of SUMO-3 signalling (Fig. 10a, b). V28, K34, and K41 of SUMO-3 are predicted to interact with T5, D24, and D26 of the CXCR1 N-terminus, with the N-terminus of SUMO-3 pointing away from the receptor. This is in stark contrast to CXCL8 activation of CXCR1, where the chemokine N-terminus inserts into a pocket composed of the transmembrane helices known as chemokine recognition site 2 or CRS2 [37]. This may explain why we were unable to displace CXCL8 from CXCR1 and CXCR2 with increasing concentrations of SUMO-3. As was the case with CXCR4:ubiquitin signalling [27], we observed that a small molecule chemokine receptor antagonist was able to block SUMO-3 signaling in CXCR2 transfectants, likely locking the receptor into an inactive conformation that impairs the conformational changes following SUMO-3 binding.

What then is the physiological relevance of SUMO-3 signaling at CXCR1 and CXCR2? We hypothesized that the release of SUMO-3 into the circulation would likely require cell death, which was supported by our observations that in vitro, necrotic cells readily release SUMO-3 into the extracellular milieu. In the absence of an ELISA specific for SUMO-3, we are unable to verify whether or not SUMO-3 is released in sufficient quantities to induce neutrophil migration. To our knowledge, there are no estimates of SUMO-3 concentrations in plasma, as is the case for ubiquitin [38]. Competitive-binding immunoassays have reported that serum levels of ubiquitin are increased 6-fold in patients with traumatic injuries and 5-fold in patients diagnosed with sepsis [39]. These increases significantly correlated with reduced TNF-α production, indicative of ubiquitin having anti-inflammatory properties. A follow-up study by the same group reported that the serum concentrations of ubiquitin in severe trauma and burns patients correlated with the degree of tissue damage and that elevated levels of serum ubiquitin in the first week of recovery were associated with increased survival rates [40]. Using ligand binding assays, we were unable to show that SUMO-3 could effectively compete with CXCL8 for binding at either CXCR1 or CXCR2, suggesting that any anti-inflammatory effects of released SUMO-3 are likely not directly attributable to receptor blockade. However, since we were able to show that SUMO-3 was chemotactic for neutrophils over a relatively broad concentration range, it suggests that nanomolar concentrations of free SUMO-3 would be sufficient to recruit neutrophils in vivo and influence the clearance of cellular debris. Again, this has parallels with previous work describing ubiquitin released into wound fluid as a chemoattractant for dendritic cells [41].

In summary, we have shown that SUMO-3 is able to activate CXCR1 and CXCR2, the principal chemokine receptors expressed by neutrophils. Given the phagocytic capacity of neutrophils, we postulate that in vivo, neutrophil recruitment into regions of tissue damage following SUMO-3 release may aid the resolution of tissue injury [1]. Given the obvious parallels with ubiquitin, which has documented anti-inflammatory effects in some models of injury and inflammation [42, 43], it may be fruitful to assess the potential for SUMO-3 modulation of CXCR1 and CXCR2 in inflammatory processes.
