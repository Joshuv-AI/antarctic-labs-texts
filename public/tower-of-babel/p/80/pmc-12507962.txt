From hive to laboratory – biotechnological potential of microorganisms from honey
Authors: Michał Pląder, Joanna Sękul, Anna Maria Kot, Katarzyna Pobiega
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHoney is a valuable source of microorganisms that may have potential applications in various fields of science and industry. The high sugar content and low pH inhibit the growth of most microorganisms; however, some are capable of adapting to these conditions. Moreover, the bioactive components of honey, such as hydrogen peroxide and flavonoids, exhibit antimicrobial activity, which limits the proliferation of pathogens while simultaneously promoting the selection of microorganisms with beneficial properties. These include bacteria from the genera Lactobacillus, Fructilactobacillus, Leuconostoc, Bacillus, and Gluconobacter; yeasts such as Saccharomyces, Schizosaccharomyces, Zygosaccharomyces, Candida, Rhodotorula, Yarrowia, Wickerhamomyces, Aureobasidium; and fungi such as Talaromyces, Aspergillus, and Epicoccum. The metabolic potential of these strains encompasses fermentation properties, probiotic microorganisms activity, and the ability to biosynthesize secondary metabolites such as erythritol, citric acid, kynurenic acid, lipids, carotenoids, and pullulan. These traits indicate the strong biotechnological potential of honey microbiota for industrial applications. However, compared to other environments, honey has not been extensively studied regarding the presence of beneficial microorganisms. This highlights the need for further research on its microbiota to identify and characterize microorganisms with potential applications in biotechnology, medicine, and the food industry.Keywords: Honey microbiota, Biotechnological potential, Probiotic microorganisms, Secondary metabolites, Industrial applications

Introduction

Bees (Apis melifera) produce honey for their own use, primarily as food during brood rearing, as well as in autumn and winter when access to other food sources is limited or unavailable (Brodschneider and Crailsheim 2010). Honey is consumed in many regions worldwide and is valued for both its taste and health-promoting properties. Its composition mainly includes sugars and water, along with smaller amounts of other compounds such as polyphenols, proteins, and vitamins. It has been proven that honey exhibits antimicrobial properties (Żebracka et al. 2022), however, it is not free from microorganisms (Xiong et al. 2023). Bacteria and fungi enter honey at various stages, from nectar collection by bees to the bottling of the final product (Kiš et al. 2019). Consequently, it is possible to isolate osmotolerant yeasts, molds, and bacteria from honey. The most commonly found species include Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Zygosaccharomyces rouxii, Debaryomyces hansenii, Zygosaccharomyces mellis, lactic acid bacteria, and Aspergillus niger (Sinacori et al. 2014). Many types of microorganisms isolated from honey have been described to date, and research has even been conducted into their biotechnological potential. However, these microorganisms are not yet used in industrial processes described in scientific publications. This may be due to their difficult access due to their deposition in local microbial collections. In the case of industrially isolated strains, they are not described in scientific articles but are usually patented, which limits access to data. This study describes the potential of microorganisms isolated from bee (Apis melifera) honey in terms of their ability to biosynthesize various compounds important for human and animal industries (Fig. 1).

Chemical composition of honey

Honey is a naturally sweet substance produced by honeybees and is considered a concentrated, supersaturated aqueous solution of sugars. Its composition is dominated by monosaccharides, mainly glucose (23–38%) and fructose (32–44%), with smaller amounts of disaccharides such as sucrose and maltose (Machado De-Melo et al. 2018; da Silva et al. 2016; Escuredo et al. 2013; Ruiz-Matute et al. 2010; Lazaridou et al. 2004). The final water content of mature honey is usually around 20% (Bell and Grainger 2023). Based on research results, a comparative table of water content and selected sugars in honey obtained from different regions of the world has been prepared (Table 1). Such high sugar concentrations, combined with low water activity, are key factors limiting microbial growth, allowing only osmotolerant microorganisms to survive.

In addition to sugars, honey contains organic acids, proteins (including enzymes), amino acids, minerals, and bioactive compounds such as polyphenols and flavonoids. These components contribute to its antimicrobial properties and further influence the survival of microorganisms. The precise composition depends on factors such as the botanical origin of nectar or honeydew, enzyme activity, and storage conditions (da Silva et al. 2016; Kaškoniene et al. 2010; Alqarni et al. 2014; Escuredo et al. 2013).

The crystallization of honey is a natural process that depends on its glucose and fructose content. The time of appearance of crystals depends on the ratio of mentioned monosaccharides (Laos et al. 2011; Gleiter et al. 2006). The rate of crystallization decreases when the fructose-to-glucose ratio exceeds 1.33, whereas ratios below 1.11 accelerate crystal formation (Smanalieva and Senge 2009). This is attributed to the comparatively lower aqueous solubility of glucose relative to fructose (Hamdan 2010). Due to the crystallization of honey, the glucose contentration decreases in the liquid phase and water activity (aw) rises. This allows osmophilic yeast cells to muliply and may cause spontaneous honey fermentation (Zamora and Chirife 2006).

Honey possesses several properties that influence the survival of microorganisms. Its antimicrobial activity, related to the presence of hydrogen peroxide, organic acids, and polyphenolic compounds, contributes to the inhibition of bacterial and fungal growth (Weis et al. 2022; Martinotti and Ranzato 2018). Additionally, the antioxidant potential of honey and its ability to reduce enzymatic browning or fat oxidation further limit microbial activity and spoilage (Chang et al. 2011; Shin and Ustunol 2004; Shin et al. 2003; McKibben and Engeseth 2001). These bioactive features, combined with its high sugar content and low water activity, make honey a challenging environment for most microorganisms, allowing only osmotolerant species to persist.

Honey contains numerous antioxidant compounds, including polyphenols, organic acids, enzymes, vitamin C, amino acids, and Maillard reaction products (Nagai et al. 2018; Pita-Calvo and Vázquez 2017; Erejuwa et al. 2012). These bioactive substances contribute to the antimicrobial properties of honey by creating an environment unfavorable for microbial growth and survival. However, it should be noted that the composition of honey, and consequently its properties, depend on various factors (i.e. floristic characteristics of the region, environmental conditions, apiculture practices, and climatic factors), meaning that the health benefits of two different types of honey may not be the same (Demir Kanbur et al. 2021).

The influence of honey composition on microbial activity

Many factors contribute to the antimicrobial properties of honey. These can be divided into three main groups: physical, chemical, and enzymatic (Żebracka et al. 2022). Physical factors primarily include low pH, high osmotic pressure, and low water activity. Chemical factors consist of phenolic compounds, organic acids, tannins, peptides, methylglyoxal, hydrogen peroxide, terpenes, and others. Enzymatic factors mainly include lysozyme and glucose oxidase (Żebracka et al. 2022; Majtan et al. 2021; Kiš et al. 2019; Pita-Calvo and Vázquez 2017) (Fig. 2).

Antimicrobial properties of honey

Water activity (aw) is defined as the ratio of the vapor pressure above a solution to the vapor pressure above pure water under constant pressure and temperature conditions (Yang et al. 2020b). This parameter ranges from 0.00 to 1.00. A reduced aw value is one of the factors that limit microbial growth and the progression of chemical reactions in food (Tapía et al. 2020). The water activity of honey ranges from 0.50 to 0.65. The low value of this parameter results from the fact that water is bound to saccharides through hydrogen bonds (Gleiter et al. 2006), making honey an unfavorable environment for microbial growth. The osmotic pressure in honey also negatively affects the growth of bacteria and fungi. It is approximately 500 Pa, making honey highly hygroscopic (Żebracka et al. 2022). This high value is due to the high carbohydrate content and low water content. Low water activity and high osmotic pressure create unfavorable conditions for microbial growth; however, osmophilic yeasts are capable of growing under such conditions (Buzzini et al. 2018; Sereia et al. 2017). Some species (i.e. Saccharomyces kudriavzevii, Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Zygosaccharomyces rouxii), in order to survive, reduce cell volume and membrane permeability while accumulating glycerol, trehalose, and fatty acids, which help mitigate cellular stress caused by high osmotic pressure (Wang et al. 2024a). Lipids, especially polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), are known for their abilities to act like antioxidants or other protective molecules. Microorganisms, especially yeast also are capable of accumulating hight amounts of glicerol, as mentioned, due to defense mechanisms helping to maintain water balance, restore cell turgor, and survive in difficult osmophilic environment, such as honey. Moreover, microorganisms can synthetize amino acids, like proline or arginine, and GABA, which also is crucial in osmo-adaptation processes (Segal-Kischinevzky et al. 2022).

High osmotic pressure in yeast activates mechanisms controlling glucose uptake (Horák 2013). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, glucose transport occurs either via facilitated diffusion at high sugar concentrations or via proton symport when glucose is scarce. The expression of specific glucose transporter genes is regulated by external sugar levels and osmotic stress, with the High Osmolarity Glycerol (HOG) pathway playing a key role in adaptation and directing glucose uptake towards glycerol synthesis (Dakal et al. 2014).

The preferred carbon source for Zygosaccharomyces rouxii yeast is fructose, and for this reason, the facilitated diffusion mechanism of glucose has not been sufficiently studied in this species. Nevertheless, two proteins, ZrFfz1 and ZrFfz2, have been identified, which, in addition to fructose, also transport glucose (Dakal et al. 2014). Furthermore, a fructose/H + symporter, ZrFsy1, has been detected and characterized, with its production increasing under low sugar concentration conditions in the extracellular environment (Leandro et al. 2013). These yeasts possess flexible sugar uptake mechanisms, enabling them to adapt to both fructose-rich and low-fructose environments. This ability to efficiently metabolize fructose and, to a lesser extent, glucose is crucial in fermentation processes occurring in high-sugar environments, such as honey, where osmotic conditions limit the growth of many other yeasts (Dakal et al. 2014; Leandro et al. 2013).

Water loss from the cell can be triggered by high osmotic pressure. To counteract this, yeast accumulates certain water-soluble chemical compounds inside the cell, such as glycerol, trehalose, and mannitol (Vustin 2021; Dakal et al. 2014). Glycerol is a small, uncharged molecule, allowing it to easily pass through the lipid membrane. Through passive diffusion, it is expelled from the cell, while water is taken in. This mechanism ensures that cell turgor is maintained despite the unfavorable conditions in which the microorganism exists (Duskova et al. 2015).

Honey exhibits an acidic pH, with an average pH value ranging between 3.2 and 5.5. The low pH is influenced by the presence of both organic and inorganic chemical compounds. Organic acids, particularly gluconic acid, which accounts for 70–90% of all organic acids found in honey, are considered the primary contributors to honey’s pH (Moreira et al. 2007). Other acids present include citric, acetic, fumaric, glycolic, glyoxylic, lactic, formic, malic, glutaric, and succinic acids (Majtan et al. 2021; Seraglio et al. 2021; da Silva et al. 2016). Inorganic compounds that lower the pH of honey include phosphates, sulfates, and chlorides (Moreira et al. 2007). Studies conducted by Bentabol Manzanares et al. (2011) indicate that honeydew honeys have a higher pH compared to nectar honeys. Most bacteria and molds grow in neutral to slightly alkaline environments, whereas yeasts prefer a pH range of 4.0-4.5 for optimal growth (Conti et al. 2007). However, this does not mean that acidic honey provides a favorable environment for yeast development, as factors other than pH, including the physicochemical properties of honey, also play a crucial role.

Chemical factors are also responsible for inhibiting the growth and development of microorganisms. These primarily include peptides, hydrogen peroxide, flavonoids, phenolic acid, and methylglyoxal (MGO) (Żebracka et al. 2022; Majtan et al. 2021; Pita-Calvo and Vázquez 2017). Defensin-1 is a peptide with antibacterial properties, exhibiting activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Bucekova et al. 2018). The exact mechanism of action of this peptide is not fully understood, but it is assumed that defensin-1 forms pores in the cell membrane, leading to cell death. Studies on defensin-1 have shown that it prevents biofilm formation (Combarros-Fuertes et al. 2020). The presence of hydrogen peroxide depends on the amount and activity of glucose oxidase in honey. The antibacterial properties of H₂O₂ are caused by the production of reactive oxygen species, including the superoxide radical and hydroxyl radical, which damage cellular DNA, proteins, enzymes, and fatty acids that make up cell membranes (Majtan et al. 2021). Methylglyoxal (MGO) alters the structure of fimbriae and flagella, thereby impairing bacterial adhesion and motility. Although MGO is primarily associated with manuka honey, studies have shown its presence in other types of honey, such as Finnish and Italian multifloral honeys and Portuguese honeydew honey (Combarros-Fuertes et al. 2020). Phenolic compounds, including flavonoids and phenolic acid, exert bactericidal effects by inhibiting nucleic acid synthesis, disrupting cytoplasmic membrane function, and negatively affecting cellular metabolism (Cushnie and Lamb 2005). The antimicrobial properties of honey result from the synergistic action of these chemical compounds (Combarros-Fuertes et al. 2020).

Enzymes that contribute to the antimicrobial properties of honey include lysozyme, glucose oxidase, and catalase (Żebracka et al. 2022; Machado De-Melo et al. 2018). Lysozyme causes the destruction of the bacterial cell wall. It hydrolyzes β−1,4-glycosidic bonds between N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetylglucosamine molecules, which are present in the peptidoglycan of the cell wall (Feknous and Boumendjel 2022). Glucose oxidase is responsible for producing two chemical compounds that influence the presence of microorganisms. Through the enzymatic oxidation of glucose, hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid are formed. Another enzyme that regulates the amount of H2O2 is catalase. This enzyme decomposes hydrogen peroxide into oxygen and water (Machado De-Melo et al. 2018), meaning that a high catalase content in honey may reduce its antimicrobial properties.

Honey as a source of microorganisms

Microorganisms enter honey through various pathways, which can be divided into two groups: primary and secondary. The primary source of contamination includes bacteria and fungi present in plant pollen, nectar, air, dust, soil, and those originating from bees, mainly from their digestive tract (Xiong et al. 2023). Secondary contamination of honey occurs during and after collection, and the factors contributing to contamination include smoking, the condition and cleanliness of equipment, transportation conditions, filtration, packaging, and storage (Grabowski and Klein 2017). It is difficult to control primary contamination, whereas contamination caused by human activity can be managed by following good manufacturing practices (Kiš et al. 2019).

Although honey, due to its physicochemical properties, is an unfavorable environment for the development of most microorganisms, among those that can survive in these conditions, some beneficial species can be isolated (Table 2and Table 3). These include Lactobacillus spp., Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Fructilactobacillus fructosus, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, Bacillus spp., Gluconobacter oxydans, Starmerella magnoliae, Starmerella sorbosivorans, Zygosaccharomyces rouxii, Zygosaccharomyces mellis, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Rhodotorula mucilaginosa, Aureobasidium pullulans, Yarrowia lipolytica, Aspergillus spp., and Penicillium spp. (Ziuzia et al. 2023; Echeverrigaray et al. 2021; Lashani et al. 2020; Sakandar et al. 2019; Kiš et al. 2019; Zarour et al. 2018; Begum et al. 2015; Sinacori et al. 2014). These microorganisms have the ability to biosynthesize compounds widely used in the food, chemical, medical, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries. Such compounds include lactic acid, citric acid, ethanol, erythritol, carotenoids, pullulan, and antibiotics (Kieliszek et al. 2017; Cheng and Yang 2016; Gänzle 2015; Cheng et al. 2011; Promnuan et al. 2009). Although microorganisms with a broad range of biotechnological potential are frequently isolated from honey and related bee products, their application has remained mainly at the experimental scale and hasn’t been used industrially yet. Lactobacillus and Lactiplantibacillus

Bacteria of the genus Lactobacillus belong to the order Lactobacillales, and family Lactobacillaceae. The cells of these microorganisms are rod-shaped, do not form spores, are Gram-positive, do not exhibit the ability to break down hydrogen peroxide, and are facultative anaerobes. Bacteria of the species Lactobacillus delbrueckii are considered typical representatives of the Lactobacillus genus. In 2020, based on comprehensive phylogenomic analyses, the traditional genus Lactobacillus was reclassified into 25 genera, which better reflect evolutionary relationships. As a result, many species previously classified as Lactobacillus were reassigned, including Lactobacillus plantarum, which is now named Lactiplantibacillus plantarum.

From honey samples, species such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Lactobacillus acidophilus have been isolated (Feizabadi et al. 2021; Lashani et al. 2020;). Lactiplantibacillus plantarum has also been isolated directly from the honey stomach of bees, indicating the bee gastrointestinal tract as an important source of these bacteria. Other potential routes of introducing Lactobacillus species into honey include floral nectar and pollen, as well as components of the hive environment (Meradji et al. 2023; Iorizzo et al. 2022; Endo and Salminen 2013; Olofsson and Vásquez 2008).

These bacteria belong to the group of lactic acid bacteria (LAB), making them highly valuable for use in the food industry, particularly in dairy production and fermentation processes (Wang et al. 2021; Todorov and de Melo Franco 2010). L. plantarum exhibits probiotic properties and is part of the human gastrointestinal microbiota (de Vries et al. 2006; Giraffa et al. 2010). It holds GRAS status granted by EFSA (Heo et al. 2022). It is most commonly isolated from dairy products, meat, and vegetables (Giraffa et al. 2010). L. plantarum possesses antimicrobial properties due to its production of bacteriocins and organic acids, primarily lactic acid and acetic acid (Rocchetti et al. 2021). It has the ability to synthesize an exopolysaccharide with thickening, antioxidant, and prebiotic properties (Sasikumar et al., 2017), as well as enzymes such as phytase and feruloyl esterase (Esteban-Torres et al. 2013; Sumengen et al. 2013). L. acidophilus also exhibits probiotic properties (Bull et al. 2013), is part of the intestinal wall microbiota, and can synthesize bacteriocins that inhibit the growth of pathogens (Anjum et al. 2014). Additionally, it is capable of producing the enzyme β-galactosidase, which is used to obtain galacto-oligosaccharides (Carević et al. 2018).

Fructilactobacillus

Bacteria of the genus Fructilactobacillus belong to the order Lactobacillales, and family Lactobacillaceae. The cells of these bacteria are Gram-positive, non-spore-forming, and non-motile. They are facultative anaerobes with a rod-shaped morphology (Oliphant et al. 2023). They do not produce catalase (Lin et al. 2022). Their optimal carbon source is fructose, and for this reason, they are classified as heterofermentative, fructophilic lactic acid bacteria (FLAB), which represent a non-conventional branch of lactic acid bacteria (Endo et al. 2018). They have been isolated from fructose-rich environments, including honey, fruits, flowers, as well as the gastrointestinal tract of herbivorous insects (De Simone et al. 2023; Pachla et al. 2018; Filannino et al. 2016). In particular, F. fructosus has been identified in the gastrointestinal tract of honeybees originating from an apiary in Poland (Pachla et al. 2018).

The most commonly identified species in honey is F. fructosus, reported in honey samples from Italy and Algeria (De Simone et al. 2023; Meradji et al. 2023; Sakandar et al. 2019). F. fructosus is capable of synthesizing mannitol from fructose (Behare et al. 2020). In addition to lactic acid, these bacteria also produce citric acid (De Simone et al. 2023) and synthesize exopolysaccharides. This species exhibits both proteolytic and lipolytic activities (Meradji et al. 2023).

Leuconostoc

Bacteria of the genus Leuconostoc belong to the order Lactobacillales, and family Leuconostocaceae. They are Gram-positive lactic acid bacteria forming cocci, facultative anaerobes, engaged in heterofermentative metabolism producing D-lactate, ethanol, and CO2 (Ramos et al. 2022; Müller et al. 2021; Holland and Liu 2011). They are commonly found in vegetables, grains, fruits, silage, wine, meat, and dairy products (de Paula et al. 2015). In addition, Leuconostoc species detected in honey likely originate from multiple ecological sources, including floral nectar, pollen, and the bees themselves, particularly from the bee’s gastrointestinal tract during nectar processing. Specifically, L. mesenteroides has been identified in honey produced by Apis mellifera suggesting that both plant-derived and bee-associated microbiota contribute to its presence in honey (Huang et al. 2021; Zarour et al. 2018; Grabowski and Klein 2017).

Leuconostoc can produce valuable compounds such as ethanol, oligosaccharides, acetic acid, antimicrobial agents, vitamins, and mannitol, a polyol used in the pharmaceutical industry and as a low-calorie ingredient in food products. Leuconostoc strains can also synthesize exopolysaccharides (EPS), such as dextran, a homopolysaccharide with various applications in the food, pharmaceutical, medical, and oil drilling industries (Mustar and Ibrahim 2022; Zarour et al. 2018).

Bacillus

Bacteria of the genus Bacillus belong to the family Bacillaceae and the order Bacillales. They are aerobic, Gram-positive rods that produce endospores (Zhao et al. 2013). They are widely distributed in the natural environment and can be found in soil, rocks, dust, airborne particles, water, food, and the gastrointestinal tracts of humans and animals (Hamdy et al. 2020). Importantly, members of Bacillus, such as Bacillus subtilis, have been found in the honey sac of honey bees (Apis cerana and Apis mellifera) and are likely transferred into honey during nectar processing (Wang et al. 2025). Bacillus spp. have been isolated from honey samples originating from various regions worldwide, including Sudan (Shin and O’Connor 2022), Japan (Okumura et al. 2022), Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, France (López and Alippi 2009), Poland (Grabek-Lejko and Worek 2024; Pajor et al. 2018), Greece (Tsadila et al. 2021), Romania (Pașca et al. 2021), Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Nigeria (Hamdy et al. 2017), and Libya (Esawy et al. 2011). The most commonly isolated species include B. subtilis (Hamdy et al. 2020; Sinacori et al. 2014; Esawy et al. 2011), B. megaterium (Sinacori et al. 2014; López and Alippi 2009), B. atrophaeus (Abd-Elaziz et al. 2020), B. amyloliquefaciens, B. cereus, B. thuringiensis, B. licheniformis, B. pumilus/altitudinis (Sinacori et al. 2014), B. safensis (Shin and O’Connor 2022), and B. paralicheniformis (Okumura et al. 2022). Pathogenic Bacillus species, such as B. cereus, which can cause food poisoning, have also been identified in honey (Alippi and Abrahamovich 2019; López and Alippi 2007).

Bacteria of the Bacillus genus are known for their ability to synthesize various enzymes, including α- and β-amylase, xylanase, levansucrase, galactosidase, protease, cellulase, and chitinase (Abd-Elaziz et al. 2020; Hamdy et al. 2017; Aizawa et al. 2010; López and Alippi 2009; Gote et al. 2004). Additionally, these bacteria are used in controlling other microorganisms due to their ability to produce antifungal peptides. The inhibitory effects of Bacillus spp. have been demonstrated against molds belonging to the genera Rhizopus, Trichoderma, Penicillium, Botrytis, and Fusarium (Zhao et al. 2013). For over 50 years, Bacillus species have been used in the food industry and dietary supplements due to their probiotic properties (Cutting 2011). Studies conducted by(Grabek-Lejko and Worek 2024) showed that B. pumilus/altitudinis isolated from honey is suitable for use as a probiotic, as well as other honey isolates, B. licheniformis, B. subtilis, B. coagulans and B. amyloliquefaciens (Pașca et al. 2021; Hamdy et al. 2020; Alippi and Abrahamovich 2019; López and Alippi 2007). Bacillus spp. which were isolated fom honey samples or bees were observed to synthetise levasucrase, an enzyme capable of hydrolising sucrose to glucose and fructose and simultaneously synthetize fructans. Fructans are polymers consists predominantly of β-(2,6)-linked polyfructan, extensively branched via β-(2,1) linkages, characteristic of levan, which is known from its beneficial properties for human health and prebiotic properties. In honey-derived Bacillus isolates, two strains exhibited particularly high levan-sucrase activity (94.1 and 81.5 U/mL) and produced 4.17 g/100 mL and 3.10 g/100 mL of levan, respectively (Xu et al. 2019; Hamdy et al. 2017), which may be relatively comparable but less than the results obtained with use of strain Bacillus subtilis var. natto, a well-known producer of this polysaccharide. For instance Literature data indicate levan concentrations ranging from 41.44 to 61 g/L. Similarly, with use of Bacillus licheniformis strain, levan was produced at the level of 53 g/L (Vieira et al. 2021; Gojgic-Cvijovic et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2013).

Gluconobacter

Gluconobacter is a Gram-negative, obligately aerobic bacterium with a rod-shaped or oval morphology, typically occurring singly or in pairs, and less frequently in chains (da Silva et al. 2022). Bacteria of this genus belong to the family Acetobacteriaceae, a group of acetic acid bacteria (Azar and Alemzadeh 2020). A typical representative of the genus is Gluconobacter oxydans, which is usually isolated from sugar-rich materials, flowers, fruits, alcoholic beverages such as cider, and non-alcoholic drinks, and less frequently from garden soil (Qin et al. 2021). G. oxydans has been primarily isolated from honey (Silva et al. 2017; Begum et al. 2015). Its presence likely stems not only from environmental sources such as floral nectar but also from bee-associated routes; as a member of Acetobacteriaceae, G. oxydans is closely related to bacteria that colonize the honey bee gut and the honey crop niche (Smith and Newton 2020; Corby-Harris et al. 2014; Good et al. 2014). These bacteria have a wide range of applications in biotechnology, particularly in acetic acid production. It has also been demonstrated that they can synthesize siderophores, which are multidentate iron-chelating organic ligands (Begum et al. 2015).

Saccharomyces

Yeasts of the genus Saccharomyces belong to the family Saccharomycetaceae and the order Saccharomycetales (Alsammar and Delneri 2020). Saccharomyces species (S. cerevisiae and S. unisporus) detected in honey likely originate from various ecological sources, including floral nectar, pollen, the bee’s body, and the gastrointestinal tract, especially of worker bees, during nectar processing (Luca et al. 2024; Machado et al. 2024; Viveros-Lizondo et al. 2024; Prestianni et al. 2022; Bitrus et al. 2020; Carvalho et al. 2010; Pereira et al. 2009). Studies of bee gut microbiota confirm the prevalence of Saccharomyces in the bee gut, particularly among worker bees, which suggests that bees may introduce these yeasts into honey through their digestive activities (Agarbati et al. 2024; Callegari et al. 2021; Yun et al. 2018).

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is widely used in the food, alcohol, and biotechnology industries. It holds GRAS status granted by the FDA (Jin et al. 2024), is well-known as ‘baker’s yeast’, and plays key roles in alcoholic fermentation and single-cell protein production (Parapouli et al. 2020). S. cerevisiae is known for its alcohol production and is therefore utilized in the fermentation of alcoholic beverages such as wine, beer, mead, cider, and other drinks. Yeasts of the genus Saccharomyces are also used for the bioutilization of food and agricultural industry waste to produce single-cell protein (SCP) (Gervasi et al. 2018). In the biotechnology industry, S. cerevisiae is considered a model organism and is widely used in research on aging, gene regulation and expression, the cell cycle, and many other topics (Karathia et al. 2011). Additionally, the potential of these yeasts for biofuel production, including higher alcohols, biodiesel, and sesquiterpenes, is well known (Buijs et al. 2013).

Schizosaccharomyces

Yeasts of the genus Schizosaccharomyces belong to the family Schizosaccharomycetaceae and the order Schizosaccharomycetales. This genus includes six species (Brysch-Herzberg et al. 2024), five of which have been isolated from honey: S. pombe, S. osmophilus, S. octosporus, S. japonicus, and S. lindneri (Prihartini et al. 2023; Brysch-Herzberg et al. 2022, 2023; Saksinchai et al. 2012a). Recent studies suggest that for species such as S. pombe and S. octosporus, honeybee honey constitutes their primary natural habitat, with these species frequently isolated from honey samples, more so than from fruits, soil, or other substrates (Brysch-Herzberg et al. 2022). This indicates that these yeasts likely originate from within the hive or are transferred via bees, rather than being solely introduced from external environments like floral nectar or fruit.

Yeasts of the genus Schizosaccharomyces are used in wine production. They regulate acidity and, due to their ability to perform malic-ethanol fermentation, contribute to a stable color, taste, and aroma of the final product (Loira et al. 2018; Domizio et al. 2018). The species S. pombe is widely used in experimental research as a model eukaryotic cel (Brysch-Herzberg et al. 2022). Additionally, these yeasts tolerate high concentrations of sugar and alcohol, which makes them suitable for bioethanol production using molasses-based substrates (Bakhiet and Abdalrhim Mahmoud 2015).

Zygosaccharomyces

Yeasts of the genus Zygosaccharomyces belong to the family Saccharomycetaceae and the order Saccharomycetales. This genus includes 13 species, some of which are responsible for food spoilage (Hulin and Wheals 2014). The most commonly found species in honey include Zygosaccharomyces rouxii, Z. mellis, Z. osmophilus, and Z. siamensis (Echeverrigaray et al. 2021; Matos et al. 2020; Sinacori et al. 2014; Saksinchai et al. 2012a). These species exhibit high resistance to the strong osmotic pressure present in honey. Research shows that Zygosaccharomyces yeasts are prevalent in bee-associated environments and are likely introduced into honey via both plant sources (such as nectar and pollen) and bee-associated routes. These osmotolerant species thrive in sugar-rich niches tied to bees, including floral nectar and stored honey (Iguchi and Watanabe 2025; Agarbati et al. 2024; Rutkowski et al. 2023; Xiong et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2022; Callegari et al. 2021; Echeverrigaray et al. 2021). Additionally, fungal genera including Zygosaccharomyces are commonly detected in the digestive tracts of honey bees, which suggests that bees contribute to the transfer of these yeasts into honey (Xiong et al. 2023).

Yeasts of the genus Zygosaccharomyces are used in the synthesis of organic acids such as malic acid and succinic acid (Taing and Taing 2007) as well as lactic acid (Solieri 2021). They are also employed in the production of certain enzymes, including lipases and glutaminases (Pramitasari and Ilmi 2021), and in bioethanol production (Mukherjee et al. 2017). The species Z. rouxii is capable of producing 4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2 H)-furanone (HDMF), an aromatic compound with a strawberry-like scent, which not only imparts a fruity aroma to food but has also been proven to possess antimicrobial properties (Schwab 2013; Wang et al. 2024a, b, c). Zygosaccharomyces species also play a role in the production of soy sauce and balsamic vinegar (Solieri 2021).

Candida and Starmerella

Yeasts of the genus Candida belong to the family Saccharomycetaceae and the order Saccharomycetales. Depending on the species, growth phase, and environmental conditions, the cells exhibit different shapes (oval, ellipsoidal, or elongated) and sizes (Kieliszek et al. 2017). In honey, species most commonly reported as Candida include Candida magnoliae (currently reclassified as Starmerella magnoliae) (Ziuzia et al. 2023; Carvalho et al. 2010;), C. sorbosivorans (Starmerella sorbosivorans) (Carvalho et al. 2010)d patagonica (Echeverrigaray et al. 2021). Other species such as C. lundiana and C. suthepensis have also been isolated (Saksinchai et al. 2012b). Candida in honey likely derives from both bee-associated microbiota and plant-associated environments such as pollen and nectar (Anderson and Mott 2023; Ziuzia et al. 2023; Xiong et al. 2023; Rodríguez-Andrade et al. 2019).

Yeasts of the genus Starmerella belong to the family Starmerellaceae and the order Saccharomycetales. They are closely related to Candida but have been taxonomically separated based on molecular phylogeny (Kurtzman et al. 2011). From honey, primarily in regions of Brazil and Canada, S. bombicola and S. meliponinorum have been isolated (Echeverrigaray et al. 2021; Silva et al. 2020). These yeasts are also frequently associated with stingless bees and their hive products, including pollen and propolis, indicating that they may enter honey through contact with bee foragers or via bee digestive tracts (Januário da Costa Neto and Benevides de Morais 2020). The species Starmerella magnoliae has the ability to biosynthesize xylitol, mannitol, glycerol, and erythritol (Kim et al. 2013; Khan et al. 2009; Tada et al. 2004). Starmerella sorbosivorans also synthesizes erythritol, and studies have shown that this compound can inhibit the growth and biofilm formation of Streptococcus (Saran et al. 2015). Yeasts of the species S. bombicola synthesize biosurfactants (sophorolipids) classified as surface-active glycolipids, which serve as a more environmentally friendly alternative to conventional chemical surfactants (Qazi et al. 2022). The addition of pure cultures of these yeasts, due to their increased production of glycerol and succinic acid, can be used in winemaking to create wines with distinctive organoleptic properties and reduced alcohol content (Canonico et al. 2021). The species S. meliponinorum can be used in brewing to produce beer with specific organoleptic characteristics and a low carbohydrate content, as these yeasts efficiently metabolize maltotriose and produce glycerol (Schinca et al. 2024).

Rhodotorula

Yeasts of the genus Rhodotorula belong to the family Sporidiobolaceae and the order Sporidiobolales. Their cells are spherical, ellipsoidal, or elongated and reproduce vegetatively through multilateral or polar budding. Residual pseudohyphae are observed only in some strains (Kot et al. 2016). The species Rhodotorula glutinis is considered the typical representative of this genus (Zhao and Li 2022). Yeasts of this genus are classified as red yeasts due to their ability to biosynthesize carotenoids, which give their colonies an orange, pink, or red coloration. Compared to other yeast genera, they are not frequently isolated from honey; however, the presence of osmophilic strains of R. mucilaginosa (Carvalho et al. 2010)d toruloides (Xue et al. 2023) has been detected in samples from Portugal and China. Significantly, R. mucilaginosa has been isolated from multiple bee-associated substrates, including pollen, beebread, forager bees, and hive debris. This suggests that the presence of Rhodotorula in honey likely derives from both plant-associated sources and bee-associated transmission (via foragers and hive materials) (Kieliszek et al. 2021; de Oliveira Scoaris et al. 2021; Nogueira et al. 2012).

Yeasts of R. mucilaginosa have attracted scientific interest as efficient lipid producers. Triacylglycerols synthesized by R. mucilaginosa mainly contain oleic and linoleic acids. Moreover, R. mucilaginosa can also be used as a producer of enzymes, particularly L-phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL, E.C. 4.3.1.24) and endo-β−1,4-glucanase (EC 3.2.1.4). This species also synthesizes carotenoids, mainly β-carotene, torulene, and torularhodin (Li et al. 2022). Additionally, some strains have the ability to inhibit the growth and activity of molds that cause post-harvest losses in plant materials. Antagonistic strains of R. mucilaginosa have been found to inhibit the growth of Penicillium expansum on apples (Yang et al. 2015; Cheng et al. 2016), Botrytis cinerea on strawberries (Li et al. 2011), and Penicillium digitatum on oranges (Ahima et al. 2019). Yeasts of the species R. toruloides have the ability to biosynthesize lipids (Reķēna et al. 2023) and carotenoids (Qi et al. 2020).

Yarrowia

Yeasts of the genus Yarrowia belong to the family Dipodascaceae and the order Saccharomycetales. The typical representative of this genus is Yarrowia lipolytica. These dimorphic yeasts exhibit spherical, ellipsoidal, or occasionally elongated cells, capable of both multipolar budding and the formation of septate hyphae or true mycelium (Jach and Malm 2022). Analyses of raw and monofloral honeys have identified Yarrowia lipolytica as a dominant yeast in certain samples, including Manuka and wildflower honeys (Xiong et al. 2023), suggesting adaptation to the highly osmotic conditions in honey – likely introduced via nectar or bee-associated vectors (Ziuzia et al. 2023).

Yarrowia lipolitica have been recognized as a “microorganism with a documented use in food” by the European Food and Feed Cultures Association (EFFCA) and the International Dairy Federation (IDF). They are also classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (Jach and Malm 2022). Y. lipolytica has the ability to biosynthesize large amounts of intracellular protein with a favorable amino acid profile (Drzymała et al. 2020), lipids (Carsanba et al. 2020), erythritol (Juszczyk et al. 2023), γ-decalactone (peach aroma) (Kothari et al. 2022), riboflavin (Wagner et al. 2018), and citric acid (Cavallo et al. 2017). Additionally, these yeasts can be used as biocontrol agents against Penicillium digitatum and P. italicum on mandarins (Zhu et al. 2019) and Fusarium proliferatum on asparagus (Hu et al. 2021).

Wickerhamomyces

Yeasts of the genus Wickerhamomyces (family Debaryomycetaceae, order Saccharomycetales) are characterized by multilateral budding, formation of pseudohyphae or true hyphae in some strains, and hat-shaped or oval ascospores in sexual reproduction (Chai et al. 2024). The most widespread species of this genus in natural environments is Wickerhamomyces anomalus (Nundaeng et al. 2021). Strains belonging to Wickerhamomyces sydowiorum (syn. Pichia sydowiorum) have been isolated from bee honeys (Jacinto-Castillo et al. 2022; Echeverrigaray et al. 2021). Wickerhamomyces anomalus (syn. Pichia anomala) has also been isolated from honey of bees in the Brazilian Tropical Dry Forest (Caatinga) (Barbosa et al. 2016). Notably, W. anomalus has been identified frequently in the digestive tracts of honey bees, and is believed to be associated with bee physiology (Tauber et al. 2019).

Strains of W. sydowiorum are being investigated for their potential use in the bioremediation of nitroexplosive wastewater (Kanekar et al. 2009). W. anomalus is recognized for its antagonistic activity against spoilage yeasts and phytopathogenic fungi, mediated through the production of killer toxins and volatile organic compounds (Amorim-Rodrigues et al. 2024; Comitini et al. 2004). This species has been applied as a biocontrol agent in winemaking and cider production to inhibit Brettanomyces and improve fermentation performance (Kuchen et al. 2023; Satora et al. 2014). Moreover, its enzymatic activities and flavor-enhancing metabolites have attracted interest for use in mixed fermentations with Saccharomyces cerevisiae in the wine industry (Padilla et al. 2018).

Wickerhamiella

The genus Wickerhamiella belongs to the family Dipodascaceae and the order Saccharomycetales (de Vega et al. 2017). These yeasts reproduce by budding and are widely distributed in the natural environment (Takei et al. 2024). A strain of Wickerhamiella versatilis was isolated from honey samples collected in southern Brazil. Studies on yeast diversity in stingless bee honey (Echeverrigaray et al. 2021) highlight pollen, nectar, and the bees digestive tract as probable sources of microbial inoculation in honey.

Wickerhamiella versatilis exhibit antagonistic potential in inhibiting the growth of Pectobacterium carotovorum subsp. carotovorum, a phytopathogen responsible for potato soft rot (Hassan et al. 2019). They can also be used in the production of soy sauces due to their ability to produce 4-ethylguaiacol (Wang et al. 2024b, 2022).

Aureobasidium

Aureobasidium is a yeast-like fungus belonging to the family Aureobasidiaceae and the order Dothideales (Di Francesco et al. 2023). Its life cycle includes yeast-like blastospores and filamentous hyphae, with some strains producing melanin-containing chlamydospores that darken the colony (Rensink et al. 2024). From honey samples, Aureobasidium pullulans has been primarily isolated (Echeverrigaray et al. 2021; Sinacori et al. 2014), as well as A. melanogenum (Jiang et al. 2018). Their presence likely reflects multiple sources, including floral nectar, bee digestive tracts, and plant or environmental contamination of foraging areas (Ullah et al. 2024; Agarbati et al. 2024).

Particularly, A. pullulans has the ability to produce various secondary metabolites. It synthesizes bio-products such as pullulan, melanins, poly(β-malic acid), β-glucan, gluconic acid, siderophores, and lactic acid (Guan et al. 2024). Many A. pullulans strains have also demonstrated the ability to produce industrially valuable enzymes, including amylases, cellulases, lipases, proteases, xylanases, and mannanases (Prasongsuk et al. 2017). This genus is also used in the production of the peptide aureobasidin A, which exhibits antifungal properties and is applied as a biological control agent in agriculture due to its strong antagonistic activity (Gostinčar et al. 2014). Furthermore, some strains of A. pullulans produce the antibacterial compound exophilin A, as well as liamocins, which may have applications as surfactants (Price et al. 2013). A very interesting metabolite of these fungi is siderophores, which have great biotechnological potential. These low-molecular-weight iron-chelating compounds are produced by microorganisms to capture this metal from the environment, enabling its absorption despite the low availability of Fe3+ ions at neutral pH. As a result, they can be used to stimulate plant growth. Additionally, they have antimicrobial activity against, for example, Monilinia laxa (which contributes to biocontrol properties) (Rensink et al. 2024; Di Francesco and Baraldi 2021; Chi et al. 2012).

Talaromyces

Fungi of the genus Talaromyces (family Trichocomaceae, order Eurotiales) are widely distributed in the environment, occurring in soil, plants, and marine habitats (Zheng et al. 2024). Species such as T. affinitatimellis, T. basipetosporus, T. brunneosporus, T. funiculosus, T. brasiliensis, and T. scorteus have been isolated from honeydew and multifloral honeys (da Silva et al. 2024; Roxo et al. 2023; Rodríguez-Andrade et al. 2019; Barbosa et al. 2018; Sinacori et al. 2014). Plants appear to be the primary source of these fungi (Sinacori et al. 2014).

Fungi of the genus Talaromyces are capable of synthesizing a variety of metabolites, including alkaloids, terpenoids, polyketides, and steroids (Zheng et al. 2024). These compounds have demonstrated a wide range of bioactive properties, such as herbicidal, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory effects (Lei et al. 2022). Additionally, these fungi produce a diverse array of pigments that have been found to be thermally stable, which is significant considering that dyes are often subjected to high temperatures in industrial processes (Morales-Oyervides et al. 2017).

Penicillium

Fungi of the genus Penicillium belong to the family Trichocomaceae and the order Eurotiales. Species such as P. apimei, P. mellis, P. meliponae, and P. polonicum are particularly frequent. Penicillium spores enter honey from plant sources, such as nectar and pollen, but also via insects (honey bees or stingless bees) that carry these fungi into the hive (Roxo et al. 2023; Barbosa et al. 2018).

Among the Penicillium species isolated from honey the best-documented biotechnological potential has been reported for P. polonicum. This species produces a wide range of hydrolytic enzymes, including endo-β−1,4-xylanase, CMCase, avicelase, pectinase, and mannanase, which are valuable for lignocellulose bioconversion and the development of enzymatic cocktails for food, feed, and biorefinery applications (de Camargo et al. 2022). Additionally, P. polonicum has been used for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) with antimicrobial activity and plant growth-promoting effects (Zhu et al. 2024). Generally, as a genus, Penicillium shows broad biotechnological potential. They can produce many different biologically active compounds, such as polyketides, terpenoids, alkaloids and non-ribosomal peptides, some of which have antimicrobial or anticancer properties. In addition, Penicillium species release strong enzymes that can break down plant materials, including lignocellulose and pectin (Akaniro et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2021; Assaf et al. 2020; Keller 2018).

Aspergillus

Aspergillus is a filamentous fungus belonging to the family Aspergillaceae and the order Eurotiales (Cairns et al. 2021). It is an aerobic microorganism with a broad ecological distribution, capable of colonizing various substrates such as soil, compost, plant debris, and food products. Species of this genus have also been detected in different types of honey, most frequently A. niger, as well as A. proliferans, A. spelunceus, and A. cibarius (Roxo et al. 2023; Xiong et al. 2023; Grabowski and Klein 2017; Sinacori et al. 2014).

The species A. niger has been widely applied in the biotechnology industry as a producer of various enzymes, organic acids, and secondary metabolites (Li et al. 2023). Aspergillus enzymes (cellulases, hemicellulases, and ligninases) are used to convert plant material into sugars, which are then utilized as substrates in biofuel production (Cairns et al. 2018).

Epicoccum

Epicoccum is a genus of ubiquitous mitosporic fungi belonging to the family Didymellaceae and the order Pleosporales. Most species inhabit plants, but they can also be isolated from honey due to the transfer of pollen to the hive by bees. The endophytic species exhibiting beneficial effects primarily include E. nigrum, E. layuense, E. dendrobii, E. mezzettii, and E. minitans, with Epicoccum nigrum being the most common (Luca et al. 2024; Khan et al. 2020; Braga et al. 2018).

The beneficial effects of Epicoccum are due to its ability to produce various metabolites (e.g., flavipin and epicolactone), which exhibit biological activity. These compounds are mainly used to protect plants from pathogens (Taguiam et al. 2021). Some substances secreted by Epicoccum are also useful in the treatment of various human diseases (Perveen et al. 2017). Additionally, they have the ability to produce pigmented compounds, such as epirodin (Alcock et al. 2015).

Mead

Mead is a traditional beverage that has been produced by humans for centuries. It is believed that mead was first created through the spontaneous fermentation of a mixture of honey and rainwater by wild yeasts, which are part of the natural microbiota of bee products. Mead is defined as an alcoholic beverage produced by fermenting honey must, a mixture of water and honey (Webster et al. 2025; García et al. 2024). During honey must preparation, specific honey-to-water ratios are used (1:0.5, 1:1, 1:2, or 1:3 (v/v), and the alcohol content of the final product typically ranges from 8 to 18%. The production of mead requires both fermentation and aging periods, which can last for months or even years. The recipes for mead vary considerably, with the common addition of fruits (such as apples and grapes), as well as spices and herbs (Benetole et al. 2021; Starowicz and Granvogl 2020). Yeast strains commonly used in winemaking and brewing are also employed in mead production due to their rapid fermentation rates, tolerance to high ethanol concentrations, and resistance to temperature fluctuations. Furthermore, these strains exhibit metabolic pathways directed toward the synthesis of numerous compounds responsible for the sensory characteristics of the final product, such as glycerol, which contributes to the perception of body and smoothness during consumption (Schwarz et al. 2020). However, mead production can also utilize yeasts that naturally inhabit the honey environment and may contribute to spontaneous fermentation. These primarily include strains from Saccharomyces, Schizosaccharomyces, and Zygosaccharomyces, as well as other yeast genera such as Debaryomyces, Hansenula, Lipomyces, Pichia, and Torulaspora (Jose-Salazar et al. 2024). Silva et al. (2025) isolated three yeast strains from honey and pollen, identifying them as Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The fermentation process was conducted for 35 days at 20 °C, followed by a 14-day aging period at 10 °C. The ethanol content in the resulting meads ranged from 6.6 to 8.5% (v/v), which was higher compared to meads produced using commercial yeast strains (5.9% (v/v). The fermented meads exhibited relatively low acidity and a rich profile of aromatic compounds, including acetaldehyde and ethyl acetate. These compounds, at appropriate concentrations, contribute to the characteristic and pleasant sensory attributes of alcoholic beverages. Sensory evaluation of the products demonstrated that they were acceptable to untrained tasters; however, no significant differences in aroma were detected during the conducted surveys (Silva et al. 2025). An attempt was made to produce mead using two yeast isolates from honey samples collected in Portugal, identified as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and compared to a commercial strain. The fermentation process and the quality of the resulting meads were similar between the isolates and the commercial strains selected for mead production. However, in the case of one of the isolates, an unpleasant odor was detected after fermentation, likely caused by phenolic compounds such as ethylphenol, ethylguaiacol, or hydrogen sulfide, which were not synthesized by the other isolate or the commercial strain. It was also demonstrated that the fermentation process was influenced more by the type of honey and the applied supplements rather than by the specific yeast strain used (Pereira et al. 2009). Saccharomycopsis fibuligera, isolated from honey in the Maranhão Amazon, was also shown to be suitable for mead production. This isolate was selected for mead production due to its fermentation capacity, pH and ethanol tolerance, and ability to produce β-glucosidase, which hydrolyzes glycoside precursors in honey. Although the strain demonstrated lower fermentation potential than commercial Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the resulting mead had a low ethanol content, which may be beneficial in designing new products, for example, for those seeking to limit their alcohol consumption. The resulting beverage also had a unique aroma profile due to the presence of a fruity compound (ethyl hexanoate) and low acetic acid content (Serra et al. 2025).

Probiotics

Probiotics are defined as microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, can have a positive effect on human health (Fijan 2014). These microorganisms are largely lactic acid bacteria, but certain yeast strains also exhibit probiotic properties (Abadi et al. 2023; Zahoor et al. 2021). Probiotic microorganisms are commonly isolated from milk and its derivatives or fermented plant products(Srinivash et al. 2023; Taye et al. 2021) however, they can also be obtained from hive environments, including honey and bees. A characteristic feature of probiotic microorganisms is the absence of hemolytic activity, resistance to bile salts, acids, and pepsin, as well as a defined sensitivity to antibiotics (Abadi et al. 2023). From honey samples collected in Bangladesh, 25 bacterial strains were isolated. Among them, three strains exhibiting probiotic properties were selected – two belonging to Pediococcus pentosaceus and one to Apilactobacillus kunkeei. These strains demonstrated strong antimicrobial activity, inhibiting the growth of pathogens such as Bacillus cereus, Staphylococcus aureus, Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella typhi, and Candida albicans. Tests also confirmed their high tolerance to low pH, bile salts, and phenol, indicating their potential to survive in the human gastrointestinal tract. Moreover, these bacteria exhibited strong autoaggregation, coaggregation, and adhesion to hydrophobic surfaces, suggesting their ability to colonize the intestines. Their high antioxidant activity, assessed based on their ability to scavenge DPPH free radicals, further highlights their health-promoting potential. Importantly, safety tests confirmed the absence of hemolytic activity and natural resistance to several antibiotics (Ferdouse et al. 2023). Roy and Mandal (2024) conducted research on the isolation and probiotic characterization of lactic acid bacteria from natural honey collected in the Malda region of India. Two of the most promising LAB strains were identified as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (LMEMh and LMEMh1). These strains exhibited resistance to low pH, high sodium salt concentrations, and bile salts, indicating their ability to survive in gastrointestinal conditions. Antibacterial activity assessment using the agar-overlay and agar-well diffusion methods demonstrated a broad spectrum of action against both Gram-negative (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii) and Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus, Enterococcus faecalis, Streptococcus pneumoniae) bacteria. The zone of inhibition (ZDI) for the agar-overlay method ranged from 13.67 to 31.67 mm, while for the culture filtrate at natural pH using the agar-well diffusion method, it ranged from 10.33 to 20.33 mm. Notably, the culture filtrate at neutral pH did not exhibit antibacterial activity. These results confirm that honey can be a source of bacteria with probiotic properties (Roy and Mandal 2024).

Bacillus spp. are gaining increasing attention as potential probiotics. Compared to LAB, these bacteria have the ability to form spores. These spore-forming forms are stable under unfavorable conditions, such as low pH or high temperature (Zulkhairi Amin et al. 2019). Therefore, they could potentially be more widely used in the food industry, especially in food production requiring high-temperature heat treatment (Grabek-Lejko and Worek 2024). Additionally, Bacillus spp. exhibit good storage stability at low temperatures (Elshaghabee et al. 2017).

Razmgah et al. (2016) conducted a study on the probiotic activity of bacteria isolated from Iranian honey. The strains were tested for resistance to low pH and bile salts, susceptibility to antibiotics, antimicrobial activity, and the presence of genotypic virulence determinants. Two of the 17 strains, belonging to the species Bacillus megaterium (TA008) and B. subtilis (TA049), were the most promising for use as probiotics. These strains demonstrated resistance to unfavorable environmental conditions. B. megaterium showed the greatest resistance to extreme acidic conditions (pH = 2) for more than 4 h. Of all the isolates tested, B. subtilis was the most resistant to bile salts. The isolates were susceptible to antibiotics, but B. megaterium showed the highest sensitivity to most of the antibiotics included in the study. Both Bacillus megaterium and B. subtilis showed antimicrobial activity against Streptococcus pyogenes, Enterococcus faecalis, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonia aeruginosa, Listeria monocytogenes, Escherichia coli and Bacillus cereus. B. subtilis also showed inhibitory effects on the growth of Streptococcus agalactiae and Shigella dysenteriae. Importantly, Bacillus megaterium and B. subtilis isolates appeared to lack virulence genes. Therefore, these two isolates could be considered as safe for use as probiotic.

In a study conducted by Grabek-Lejko and Worek (2024), the probiotic activity of Bacillus spp. strains isolated from Polish honeys was examined. The most promising strains belonged to the Bacillus pumilus/altitudinis species. Their ability to survive for 4 h at low pH (2.0) ranged from 60.67 to 76.66%, and in the presence of 0.3% bile salts was 96.59-107.97%. The tested strains also demonstrated antimicrobial activity against foodborne pathogens. The isolates were sensitive to most antibiotics included in the study, including gentamicin, erythromycin, ampicillin, and chloramphenicol. Further studies are needed to exclude the presence of virulence genes and to examine stability during food processing and storage. However, it can be considered that Polish honey is a promising reservoir of Bacillus spp. with probiotic potential.

Erythritol

Erythritol is a naturally occurring polyol composed of four hydroxyl groups, with the molecular formula C4H10O4. Small amounts of this compound are naturally present in various fruits, seaweed, and fungi. In the European Union, erythritol is classified as a food additive under the code E968 and has been granted GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) status, indicating its recognized safety for consumption. It has no caloric value and a glycemic index of zero, making it suitable for use in low-calorie food production, particularly for diabetics. Erythritol is not fermented by bacteria inhabiting the human oral cavity, which prevents it from contributing to tooth decay (Moon et al. 2010). Among the known erythritol-producing strains are microorganisms originally isolated from honey. An example is Moniliella sp., a yeast-like fungus isolated from Taiwanese honey. The wild-type strain synthesized 113 g/L of erythritol in media containing 40% glucose and 1% yeast extract. In the next stage, the strain was subjected to mutagenesis, and the resulting mutants were screened to select high-glucose-consuming strains. Out of six new strains, one was chosen for its high erythritol production capability. In pilot fermenters with capacities of 250 L and 2000 L, the mutant strain N61188-12 produced erythritol at levels of 152 g/L. Using a simplified fed-batch culture in a 2000 L fermenter increased the erythritol yield to 189 g/L after 10 days of fermentation (Lin et al. 2010). Other studies evaluated the erythritol biosynthesis potential of Yarrowia lipolytica, Candida magnolia, and Starmerella magnoliae strains isolated from Polish honey. Shake flask cultures were conducted at 28 °C in Liquid media containing glycerol as a carbon source. Among 15 tested strains, one belonging to Y. lipolytica was selected for further studies in a bioreactor. After 95 h of cultivation, the yeast produced 32.6 g of erythritol per liter of medium (Ziuzia et al. 2023). The findings of Lin et al. (2010) and Ziuzia et al. (2023) indicate that microorganisms isolated from honey exhibit potential for application in the industrial production of erythritol.

Citric acid

Citric acid (C₆H₈O₇) belongs to the group of tricarboxylic acids and is considered safe for human consumption. In the European Union, it has been classified as a food additive under the code E330 and can be used according to the quantum satis principle. Additionally, it holds the status of GRAS. The primary applications of citric acid include the food industry, where it functions as an acidity regulator and preservative. Citric acid is also widely used in the pharmaceutical, chemical, cosmetic, and textile industries (Ciriminna et al. 2017). This compound is synthesized by yeasts isolated from honey. Two Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains originating from Thai honey were inoculated into liquid media: YP (without glucose), YPD (with 2% glucose), and YPM (containing 5% cane molasses). The cultures were maintained at 30 °C for 48 h. The results indicated that the most efficient medium for citric acid production was YPM (containing 5% cane molasses). After 48 h of cultivation, citric acid concentrations in both strains were similar, reaching 15.46 g/L and 15.43 g/L, respectively (Zahoor et al. 2021). The ability of 15 strains belonging to the species Yarrowia lipolytica, Candida magnoliae, and Starmerella magnoliae, isolated from Polish honey, to produce citric acid was also analyzed. The tested strains were inoculated into Liquid media containing glycerol as a carbon source, and cultures were maintained at 28 °C. Based on the results from shake flask cultures, Y. lipolytica strain no. 3, which exhibited the highest citric acid production efficiency (5.7 g/L), was selected for further studies in a bioreactor. After 120 h of cultivation in the bioreactor, the citric acid concentration in the medium reached 75.7 g/L (Ziuzia et al. 2023). Depending on the composition of the culture medium and the applied Y. lipolytica strain, the amount of citric acid produced varies. For example, cultivation in a medium containing glucose, ammonium sulfate, and yeast extract yielded 49 g/L of citric acid, whereas the use of a medium composed of inulin, ammonium sulfate, and yeast extract resulted in 77.9 g/L of this chemical compound. When compared to the Y. lipolytica strain isolated from honey, these values are relatively high, indicating that this strain has potential for industrial citric acid production. Nevertheless, Aspergillus niger strains are still widely used industrially for the biosynthesis of citric acid, while research on Y. lipolytica strains remain on progress (Carsanba et al. 2019; Cavallo et al. 2017; Papanikolaou et al. 2009).

Kynurenic acid

Kynurenic acid (KYNA), with the molecular formula C10H7NO3, is an endogenous amino acid produced in the tryptophan metabolic pathway in the human body. Small amounts of this compound can also be obtained through diet, particularly from foods such as honey, broccoli, cauliflower, and potatoes (Walczak et al. 2020). Studies on the properties of kynurenic acid have demonstrated its strong neuroprotective effects. KYNA blocks receptors for excitatory amino acids, whose excessive activity may contribute to the development of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and Huntington’s disease. Additionally, scientific reports suggest that KYNA may exhibit antiproliferative activity against cancer cells. However, further research is necessary to fully determine the biological activity of this compound (Walczak et al. 2020; Kozłowska and Kozłowski 2017). This compound can be synthesized by yeasts isolated from honey. Yarrowia lipolytica strains were isolated, and after 168 h of cultivation, they produced KYNA at a concentration of 3.9 mg/L (Ziuzia et al. 2023).

Lipids and carotenoids

Microbial lipids (SCOs – single cell oils) are a promising alternative to traditional raw materials for biodiesel production. They are synthesized by oleaginous microorganisms, including yeasts, bacteria, filamentous fungi, and microalgae. However, yeasts exhibit the fastest growth and the greatest ability to metabolize various carbon sources. Despite their potential, high commercial production costs remain the main barrier to the development of this technology. Therefore, ongoing research focuses on identifying new strains capable of simultaneously producing lipids and other valuable metabolites (Mota et al. 2022; Abeln and Chuck 2021). Among the secondary metabolites synthesized by oleaginous yeasts, carotenoids are of particular interest. These are pigmented organic compounds with strong antioxidant properties, widely used in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries. Yeasts capable of carotenoid biosynthesis include species from the genus Rhodotorula, such as Rhodotorula glutinis, Rhodotorula mucilaginosa, and Rhodotorula toruloides (Tang et al. 2019). Particularly noteworthy is R. toruloides, which simultaneously accumulates large amounts of lipids and carotenoids, including torulene, torularhodin, and β-carotene (Qi et al. 2020). In the study conducted by Xue et al. (2023), yeast strains associated with honeycombs in China were isolated and characterized. A total of 96 yeast strains were isolated, among which a newly identified strain, Rhodotorula toruloides C23, was of special interest due to its ability to simultaneously produce lipids and carotenoids under high sugar concentration conditions. In shake flask cultures, R. toruloides C23 achieved the highest lipid accumulation level (69.79% of dry cell mass) and carotenoid production (6.22 mg/L) among all tested strains. Further experiments conducted in a bioreactor demonstrated that this strain could reach a Lipid biosynthesis production of 24.41 g/L and a carotenoid titre of 15.50 mg/L when utilizing 120 g/L of glucose. The obtained lipids were used to produce biodiesel with a high conversion efficiency (93.15%) and properties compliant with the EN14214 and ASTM D6751 standards.

Pullulan

Pullulan is an extracellular polysaccharide produced microbiologically, primarily by fungi such as Aureobasidium pullulans and Aureobasidium melanogenum, but also by Cytaria spp., Teloschistes flavicans, Rhodotorula bacarum, and Cryphonectria parasitica (Yang et al. 2020a; Chen et al. 2019a; Yildiz and Karatas 2018). This compound is an α-D-glucan composed of maltotriose units linked by α-(1–6)-glycosidic bonds. Depending on the source, the degree of polymerization of pullulan ranges from 100 to 5000 glucopyranoside units, with a molecular weight between 5 × 103 and 2 × 106 Da. The regular molecular structure and linear conformation of pullulan contribute to its two characteristic properties: structural flexibility and good solubility (Kraśniewska et al. 2019). Pullulan appears as a white, non-hygroscopic powder, devoid of taste and odor. It is highly soluble in water, both hot and cold, as well as in alkaline solutions, but it is insoluble in alcohol and other organic solvents except for dimethyl sulfoxide and formamide (Mujdeci et al. 2024; Hamidi et al. 2019). In 2002, pullulan received GRAS status from the FDA, and in 2004, it was approved by the European Union as a food additive, assigned the code E1204 (Kraśniewska et al. 2019; Singh et al. 2017). According to Directive 2006/52/EC of the European Parliament and the Council of July 5, 2006, concerning food additives other than colors and sweeteners, and Directive 94/35/EC on sweeteners used in foodstuffs, pullulan may only be used in breath-freshening confectionery in sheet form and in capsules for dietary supplements and tablets (Directive 2006/52/EC). Pullulan has found numerous applications in food biotechnology, medicine, and other fields. It is used for the production of molded forms, fibers, drug delivery materials, and edible films. An interesting application of pullulan is in food packaging, as it is a fully biodegradable, naturally derived polymer that can prolong food freshness by inhibiting microbial growth. It is an oxygen barrier, limiting the exposure of food products to microorganisms from the external environment. Fruits and vegetables sprayed or immersed in pullulan solutions retain their freshness longer, allowing pullulan to replace wax coatings traditionally used for this purpose (Pobiega et al. 2020). Another food-related application is the use of pullulan as a thickener in mayonnaise and dairy products such as yogurt or dairy desserts. It serves as a lower-calorie alternative to starch and acts as a gelatin substitute for vegans, as gelatin is animal-derived (Hermund et al. 2019). Isolates of Aureobasidium melanogenum from honey exhibit high productivity in pullulan biosynthesis due to their high tolerance to elevated concentrations of osmotically active substances in the culture medium. It has been demonstrated that Aureobasidium melanogenum TN1-2 can produce 97 g/L of pullulan from 140 g/L of sucrose in flask culture over five days and 114 g/L of pullulan in a 10-liter bioreactor over 134 h. The high pullulan biosynthesis capability of this strain was associated with high glucosyltransferase activity, elevated phosphofructokinase-2 activity, high intracellular glycerol content, and low glucose repression. The low molecular weight of the produced pullulan (1.42 × 105 g/mol) may be attributed to high α-amylase, glucoamylase, and isopullulanase activity (Jiang et al. 2018). Another isolate, Aureobasidium melanogenum TN3-1, exhibited pullulan synthesis activity at 110 g/L from 140 g/L of glucose during a five-day fermentation. It was observed that most cells in the TN3-1 culture formed arthroconidia, while in TN1-2, blastoconidia were predominant (Xue et al. 2019). This isolate was later subjected to genetic modifications, resulting in its classification as a hyperproducer of pullulan. The genes responsible for α-amylase and melanin synthesis were deleted from strain TN3-1, giving rise to the mutant strain AMY-PKS-11. This mutant produced 103.5 g/L of non-pigmented pullulan in a medium containing 140 g/L of sucrose during a 96-hour cultivation (Jia et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2019b, 2020).

Conclusions and future perspectives

Microorganisms present in honey exhibit biotechnological potential and may find applications in various industries. Frequently occurring groups include lactic acid bacteria, aerobic spore-forming bacteria, acetic acid bacteria, osmophilic yeasts, and molds capable of surviving high sugar concentrations and low pH. Applications investigated so far involve the use of honey-derived microorganisms in traditional mead production, isolation of probiotic lactic acid bacteria with pathogen-inhibiting properties, and utilization of yeast strains for the biosynthesis of erythritol, citric acid, kynurenic acid, lipids for biodiesel, carotenoids, and pullulan. Additional metabolites of interest include enzymes, organic acids, vitamins, and aromatic compounds, and some strains also demonstrate antagonistic activity against plant pathogens. Despite these advances, the species diversity of the honey microbiome remains insufficiently explored, and many metabolic capabilities have yet to be characterized. Future research should encompass the investigation of honey from diverse botanical and geographical origins, the use of multi-omics approaches to elucidate microbial diversity and functionality, the identification of novel bioactive compounds produced by honey-derived microorganisms, and the evaluation of the industrial scalability, safety, and consumer acceptance of products developed using these microbial strains. Such efforts will expand the understanding of honey microbiota and its potential applications in biotechnology, medicine, and the food industry.
