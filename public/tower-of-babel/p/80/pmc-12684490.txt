Circulating Cathepsin D Exacerbates Injury‐Induced Brain Damage by Promoting Neutrophil Infiltration Into the Brain
Authors: Yu Liang, Dan Zhao, Peng‐Fei Wu, Yong‐Qi Liang, Xue‐Wei Zhang, Jia Xing, Xue Sun, Ming‐Xin An, Jun‐Xiu Shi, Qing‐Hua Gao, Li Tian, Hong‐Zan Sun, Bin Xue, Li‐Bo Jiang, Yu‐Hua Chen, Wei‐Dong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunication between the vascular endothelium and peripheral leukocytes is an important process controlling inflammatory responses in the brain. Increased plasma levels of CTSD (cathepsin D) have been associated with vascular events, but whether circulating CTSD may affect the functional status of the endothelium remains unknown.MethodsTo resolve this issue, two groups of transgenic hCTSDhi mice with high levels of circulating CTSD were subjected to traumatic and ischemic injury, respectively. Then, single‐cell RNA sequencing was performed to characterize gene expression changes in the brains of hCTSDhi mice. Additionally, transgenic CTSDMono knockout mice with reduced plasma CTSD levels were generated to investigate whether CTSD downregulation confers protection against ischemic injury and traumatic brain injury‐induced brain damage.ResultsWe found the hCTSDhi mice showed increased brain neutrophil infiltration in response to the brain injury. Severe motor deficit and delayed behavioral recovery were observed in hCTSDhi mice after brain traumatic and ischemic injury. Mechanistically, the circulating CTSD was characterized as the nonenzymatic prototype CTSD, pro‐CTSD. We further found the circulating nonenzymatic pro‐CTSD was able to activate brain endothelium by upregulating VCAM‐1 (vascular cell adhesion molecule 1) expression, which is necessary for the transmigration of neutrophils into the brain. Furthermore, genetic reduction of CTSD effectively attenuated the ischemic injury‐induced neutrophil infiltration and motor deficits.ConclusionsTaken together, these results demonstrate that circulating prototype CTSD exacerbates the injury‐induced brain damage by upregulating brain endothelial VCAM‐1 and promoting neutrophil transmigration into the brain.Keywords: brain endothelial cells, brain injury, cathepsin D, neutrophil infiltration, VCAM‐1Subject Categories: Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke, Ischemic Stroke, Cell Biology/Structural Biology, Inflammation, Ischemia

The brain endothelium lining the cerebral vasculature is an important interface between the blood circulation and the central nervous system. 1 Under neuroinflammatory conditions induced by injury, infection, and neurological disorder, the brain endothelium plays important role in the communication between the peripheral immune system and central nervous system. 2 Given that neuroinflammation in the brain is considered as an important mediator of secondary brain damage, deciphering the endothelium‐mediated communication mechanism may provide a new target to develop intervention strategies for brain injury and disease.

The communication between the vascular endothelium and peripheral leukocytes, including neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes, is an important process controlling inflammatory responses. 3 The circulating leukocytes could transmigrate across the endothelium into brain parenchyma contributing to the neuroinflammation process, preceded by the adhesion of leukocytes to the endothelium. 4 , 5 The adhesion and transmigration of leukocytes are primarily mediated by the molecular interaction between leukocyte receptors including integrins and selectins and endothelial ligands including immunoglobulin superfamily members ICAMs (intercellular cell adhesion molecules), vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM‐1) and PECAM‐1 (platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule‐1, also known as CD31). 6 , 7 Within the circulation, the soluble chemoattractants and chemokines are actively involved in leukocyte adhesion and transmigration. 8 Elucidating the nature and mechanism of bioactive circulating factors involved in adhesion and transmigration of leukocytes may offer great potential in providing therapeutic targets to the neuroinflammation in brain.

CTSD (cathepsin D) is a lysosomal aspartic proteinase preferentially expressed in phagocytic cells including monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils. 9 , 10 CTSD was found to be secreted extracellularly as soluble form in certain cancer cells. 11 Elevated plasma CTSD levels were found in patients with vascular events including coronary disease 12 and endothelial dysfunction with chronic kidney disease. 13 Upregulation of CTSD in cancer cells exhibited a proangiogenic effect on cultured endothelial cells. 14 , 15 These findings implicated the potential effect of CTSD on endothelium dysfunction. Whether and how circulating CTSD may affect the functional status of brain endothelium remained unknown.

To address this issue, we used transgenic mice with a high level of circulating CTSD and we found the mice exhibited increased neutrophil accumulation, enhanced brain damage, and exacerbated motor deficits in response to the ischemic and traumatic injury. Mechanistically, the circulating CTSD was identified as the nonenzymatic prototype CTSD, pro‐CTSD. The circulating pro‐CTSD was able to upregulate brain endothelial VCAM‐1 for the transmigration of neutrophils into the brain. Furthermore, we employed 3 distinct disease models (diabetes, cerebral infarction, and traumatic brain injury [TBI]) to investigate the therapeutic potential of targeting CTSD. We found genetic reduction of CTSD significantly alleviated ischemic injury‐induced brain lesion and motor deficits in the diabetes condition and ameliorated traumatic injury‐induced neutrophil infiltration and neurological impairments.

Antibodies and Reagents

Animal Experiments

For all protocols and experiments related to animal subjects, we received the approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Ethics Committee of China Medical University (#CMU2023244). The mice were housed in a temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled environment with a 12‐hour light/dark cycle where they were kept in sterilized cages with autoclaved water, food, and bedding. Sample sizes and the number of animals per group were determined by power analysis using G‐Power 3.1 software. Specifically, the effect size was established based on preexperimental results to calculate the minimum number of animals required for each experiment to achieve a power >80%. The power value (1‐β) for each experiment was included in the Statistics Data Set (Data S1). The Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Studies protocols were followed for all the animal experiments. 16 Due to the certain number of groups involved in the study, we conducted separate experimental sessions with distinct cohorts of mice. Within each cohort, we evaluated the homogeneity of baseline performance and randomly allocated the animals to the experimental groups. Randomization was executed using a computerized random number generation method, independently of the study investigators. Throughout the study, all investigators remained blinded to the treatment conditions.

For the construction of hCTSDhi transgenic mice, we obtained the coding sequence of human CTSD (NM 001909.5, encoding 440 amino acids) by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction with the isolated human monocytes. Then we created the fusion construct containing 2.9 kb human CD68 promoter (hCD68), intervening sequence‐1, human CTSD, and bovine growth hormone polyadenylation signal. The construct was excised by MfeI and DrdI restriction enzyme and injected into the pronucleus of fertilized C57BL/6 mouse eggs (Cyagen Biosciences). The microinjected embryos were transferred to the uteri of pseudopregnant C57BL/6N foster mothers. We performed DNA genotyping with mouse tail biopsies using the 5′ primer (CACCTACTCAGACAATGCGAT) located at the hCD68 promoter and the 3′ primer (ACTGTGTTTGACCGTGACAAC) located within the sequence of human CTSD (Table S4) to identify the founders of hCTSDhi transgenic mice. The founder lines were then bred with C57BL/6 mice to generate F1 offspring, and the F1 progeny were then interbred.

The CTSDloxP/loxP mice were generated using gene‐targeting approach with C57BL/6N mouse embryonic stem cells (Cyagen Biosciences). The CTSDloxP/loxP mice were then bred with Lyz2‐Cre mice (Cyagen Biosciences) to produce CTSDloxP/+; lyz2‐Cre mice, named as CTSDMono knockout (mKO), with littermate CTSDloxP/+ mice as control. We performed DNA genotyping with mouse tail to identify the LoxP and Cre allele (Table S4).

Controlled Cortical Impact

The 3‐month‐old mice were subjected to TBI via controlled cortical impact as described previously. 17 Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with 1.5% to 3% isoflurane and placed in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). A 3 mm craniotomy was conducted over the right somatosensory cortex (S1) centered at −1.5 mm posterior from bregma, +1.5 mm lateral from the midline. The injury was performed by a Pinpoint PCI3000 Precision Cortical Impactor (Hatteras Instruments, NC, USA) equipped with a metal piston using the following parameters: 3 mm tip diameter, 0° angle, depth 1.0 mm from the dura, velocity 1.5 m/s, and 50 ms dwell duration. The sham‐operated mice were received identical anesthesia and craniotomy without the injury.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging was performed on isoflurane‐anesthetized mice using a 3T magnetic resonance system (Biospec; Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany) at D1, D3, and D7 after TBI injury. T2‐weighted images were acquired using a gradient echo sequence with parameters set at repetition time/echo time = 250/7 ms, a slice thickness of 1 mm, a field of view of 40 × 20 mm2, a matrix size of 256 × 256, and a number of excitations of 10. The total duration of the scanning procedure was 8 minutes. The volume of brain edema was quantified from hyperintense regions in the T2‐weighted images with OsiriX Lite software.

Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion Model

The middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model was established in mice aged 3 months as described previously. 18 In brief, the left common carotid artery was exposed through a midline incision. The external carotid artery was isolated and permanently ligated. The internal carotid artery was dissected and separated from the adjacent vagus nerve carefully. The selection of the nylon filament type was based on the weight of the mice. A 30‐mm length of silicon‐coated nylon filament (MSMC23B120PK50 [native diameter 0.12 mm; diameter with coating 0.22 ± 0.01 mm; coating length 3–4 mm] or MSMC21B120PK50 [native diameter 0.12 mm; diameter with coating 0.20 ± 0.01 mm; coating length 3–4 mm]; RWD Corporation, Shenzhen, China) was introduced into the external carotid artery lumen through a puncture. The silk suture around the external carotid artery stump was tightened to prevent bleeding. The nylon suture was then gently advanced from the external carotid artery to the internal carotid artery lumen until mild resistance was felt (~ 9 mm). For reperfusion, the suture was withdrawn 45 minutes after occlusion to restore blood flow. The infarct volume in the coronal brain slices were evaluated by staining with TTC (Sigma, #T8877) for 30 minutes at 37 °C. The infarct area was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA) by measuring the nonstained white area. The relative infarct volume was presented as the ratio of the infarct volume to the volume of the entire brain slice.

Type 2 Diabetes Mouse Model

A type 2 diabetes mouse model was induced by a high‐fat diet and streptozotocin. In brief, male C57BL/6 mice, aged 6 weeks, were fed a high‐fat diet (comprising 60% of calories from fat, H10060, Beijing HFK Bioscience Co.) for 42 days. Following a 6‐hour fasting period, the mice received intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (50 mg/kg) for 5 consecutive days. The control group was administered a standard diet and received an intraperitoneal injection of a vehicle solution. Body weight and blood glucose levels from the tail vein were measured at regular intervals. The successful establishment of type 2 diabetes models was confirmed when fasting blood glucose levels exceeded 16.67 mM.

India‐Ink Angiography

India‐ink angiography was performed to assess intracranial vessels, as previously described. 19 After anesthesia and cardiac catheterization, the circulatory system was perfused with 5 mL of PBS, followed by 15 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, a solution comprising 20% India ink dissolved in 5% gelatin was infused at a rate of 15 mL/min. The mouse carcasses were then stored at 4 °C for 6 hours to allow for tissue hardening. Following removal from the skull, the circle of Willis, basilar artery, and cortical vascular were photographed. The diameter of middle cerebral artery and the internal carotid artery were measured with ImageJ software. Vessel density was determined using Angio Tool software.

Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging

To assess the cerebral blood flow, laser speckle contrast imager (SIM BFI‐HR PRO, Simopto) was performed. Briefly, after inducing anesthesia with isoflurane (2.5% for induction and 1% for maintenance), a 1 cm incision was made in the scalp, and the skull was exposed to facilitate the detection of cerebral blood flow. Speckle imaging was performed with Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging software (Simopto) at 3 time points: before ischemia, during artery occlusion, and 30 minutes post reperfusion. The average blood fluxes were calculated in the region supplied by the middle cerebral artery.

Rotarod Test

Rotarod test was performed as described in our previous study. 20 Briefly, the mice were habituated to stay on the rotating rod (3‐cm diameter). Three days of a training course, 5 trials per day, was conducted for each mouse. The speed of the rod was increased to 20 rpm (acceleration rate, 1 rpm/s) and remained constant until the end of the trial. We recorded the time until the mice fell from the rod. The cutoff time was 300 seconds for TBI model and 600 seconds for MCAO model. The mice were allowed to rest for 60 minutes between 2 trials. 21 , 22 , 23 The investigators were kept blind to the treatment assignments.

Pole Test

Pole test was performed as described in our previous study. 20 A vertical wood pole (diameter of 2 cm, height of 50 cm) was placed inside the cage and was covered with tape to create a rough surface. Mice were trained 3 times daily for 3 days before the start of testing. The total time spent by the mouse to descend and reach the floor was recorded. The test consisted of 5 trials with 5‐minute intervals, and the data were expressed as the mean value of 5 trials. The assessments were performed by investigators blinded to both the mouse genotype and the experimental group allocation.

Neutrophil Migration Assay

The bEnd.3 cells were seeded on the upper side of the Transwell insert membrane (3.0 μm pore size, 6.5 mm diameter, Costar, USA) at a density of 2.5×105/well for 3 to 4 days until confluence. Then the neutrophils were isolated from the GFP (green fluorescent protein) transgenic mice (CAG‐GFP‐Tg, Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.). The GFP‐positive neutrophils were then loaded into the upper chamber of the Transwell. After incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 3 hours, the neutrophils were harvested from the lower chamber. The transmigrated GFP‐positive neutrophils were enumerated by flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence

Immunostaining was performed as described in our previous study. 24 In brief, the anesthetized mice were perfused transcardially with cold 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) followed by perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate buffer. The brains were collected and postfixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde. Then the coronal sections (100‐μm‐thick) were prepared using vibratome (Leica VT 1200, Germany). The slices were incubated in 5% donkey serum in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 1 hour at room temperature. Then the slices were incubated with the primary antibodies recognizing CD31 (1:200, AF3628, R&D system), MPO (myeloperoxidase; 1:200, ab9535, Abcam), NeuN (neuronal nuclear protein; 1:500, 26 975‐1‐AP, Proteintech), human CTSD (1:200, ab134169, Abcam), human CTSD (1:200, MA5‐29205, Thermo), Ly6c‐Alexa 488 (1:50, 53–5932‐82, ThermoFisher), mouse CTSD (1:200, ab52832, Abcam), VCAM‐1 (1:200, 39036S, Cell Signaling Tech.), VCAM‐1 (1:100, CBL1300, Sigma‐Aldrich), and ACTA2 (actin alpha 2; 1:200, 19245T, Cell Signaling Tech.), respectively, overnight at 4 °C. The slices were then incubated with the indicated fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:200, Invitrogen) for 3 hours at room temperature. After counterstaining with DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylin‐dole) for 5 minutes, the coverslips were mounted with Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Inc.) and visualized under confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM880).

For cellular staining, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes. The fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 and then blocked with 5% BSA in PBS. Then the cells were stained with antibodies against human CTSD (1:200, PA5‐80518, ThermoFisher), mouse CTSD (1:200, ab52832, Abcam), CD11b (1:50, 11–0112‐81, ThermoFisher), or CD115 (1:200, 16‐1152‐82, ThermoFisher). Then the cells were washed and incubated with secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa 488 or Alexa 555 (1:200 dilution, Invitrogen). Following DAPI staining, the coverslips were mounted and analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscopy. The images were captured using 20 × objective (Plan‐Apochromat 20×, N.A. 1.0, Zeiss) or 63 × objective (Plan‐Apochromat 63x/1.46 Oil Corr M27, N.A. 1.0, Zeiss). All images were taken in an unbiased manner. Image analysis was conducted using ZEN blue software (Zeiss).

Lentivirus Production and Infection

The cDNA of human CTSD was obtained by polymerase chain reaction and cloned into pLenti‐EF1a‐EGFP‐P2A‐Puro‐CMV‐3xFlag vector (OBiO Technology Corp., Shanghai, China). The construct was verified by DNA sequencing. The lentiviral constructs and packaging plasmids were cotransfected into HEK293T cells by Lipofectamine 2000 for lentivirus production. The viral supernatant was collected 24 hours after transfection and centrifuged at 1000g for 5 minutes followed by filtering with 0.45‐μm filter. The obtained viral solution was further concentrated by centrifugation at 100000g for 2 hours at 20 °C using a SW41 swinging rotor in ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter Optima‐L‐100 XP, Beckman, Fullerton, CA, USA). The THP‐1 monocytic cells were then infected with the concentrated lentivirus encoding human CTSD. Puromycin (2 μg/mL) was added to the media 48 hours later to screen the positively infected THP‐1 cells.

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

The bEnd.3 brain endothelial cell line was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD) and was grown in DMEM (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (ThermoFisher Scientific). The human monocytic leukemia cell line THP‐1 (ATCC) was maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS. The HEK293T cells (ATCC) were grown in DMEM with 10% FBS. All the cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2, 95% air humidified atmosphere.

Murine Leukocytes Isolation

Blood samples were collected from the anesthetized mice by cardiac puncture and centrifuged at 300g for 10 minutes to separate the plasma. The erythrocytes were lysed with Ammonium Chloride Solution (Stem Cell Tech.). Then the neutrophils were isolated using negative magnetic sorting with Mouse Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec Inc.) following manufacturer’s instructions.

Western Blot

The cells were washed with cold PBS and lysed with radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP‐40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate) supplemented with Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Pierce). The samples were subjected to SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membrane (Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA and then incubated with primary antibodies against CTSD (ab75852, Abcam), human CTSD (MA5‐29205, ThermoFisher), human CTSD (PA5‐80518, ThermoFisher), and VCAM‐1 (1:1000, 39036S, Cell Signaling Tech.) at 4 °C overnight. β‐actin (66009‐1‐Ig, Proteintech) was used as loading control. Then the blots were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:10000 dilution, Santa Cruz Biotech) for 1 hour at room temperature. The immunoreactive bands were visualized by Super Signal West Pico chemiluminescent substrate (ThermoFisher) using Tanon 5200 imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). For quantification, the protein band intensities of the western blot images were measured with ImageJ software.

ELISA

The blood samples were collected and moved to the anticoagulated tubes, then the tubes were centrifuged at 300g for 10 minutes to isolate the plasma. The concentration of CTSD in the plasma was measure with Mouse Cathepsin D Simple Step ELISA Kit (ab239420, Abcam) and Human Cathepsin D ELISA kit (ab119586, Abcam), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA Interference in the Brain Endothelium

We used AAV‐BR1, an adeno‐associated virus showing specificity to brain microvascular endothelial cells, 24 , 25 to knockdown VCAM‐1 in the brain endothelium of mouse. The GFP‐tagged AAV‐BR1 containing VCAM‐1‐specific shRNA was constructed by DNA recombination technology, with empty vector as control. The target sequence of the VCAM‐1‐specific shRNA was 5′‐ CACAGAAATCCAAAGTGTA −3′ (Table S5). The viral particles of recombinant AAV‐BR1 constructs were prepared, harvested, and injected into the tail vein of mice at a dose of 5 × 1011 genomic particles in a volume of 100 μL/mouse. The expression of VCAM‐1 in the brain endothelium were determined by immunostaining 8 weeks after virus injection.

scRNA Sequencing

Single Cell Isolation

Fresh brain tissues from mice were thoroughly rinsed 3 times with HBSS and subsequently subjected to enzymatic digestion using the sCelLive Tissue Dissociation Solution (Singleron Biotech, China) in conjunction with the PythoN Automated Tissue Dissociation System (Singleron Biotech) at 37 °C for 15 minutes. Following this, the GEXSCOPE red blood cell lysis buffer (Singleron Biotech) was applied, and the cells were incubated at 25 °C for 10 minutes to effectively lyse and remove red blood cells. The dissociated cells were then stained with trypan blue (Sigma) to evaluate cell viability via microscopic examination.

Library Preparation and scRNA Sequencing

Single‐cell suspensions were prepared at a concentration of 1×105 cells/mL and introduced into microfluidic devices using the Matrix Single Cell Processing System (Singleron Biotech). Subsequently, scRNA sequencing libraries were generated using the GEXSCOPE Single Cell RNA Library Kits (Singleron Biotech). The individual libraries were diluted to a concentration of 4 nM and pooled for sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 System (Illumina), using 150 base pair paired‐end reads.

Raw Sequencing Data Processing

The raw reads obtained from scRNA sequencing were processed to generate gene expression matrices through an internal pipeline. Initially, quality control of the raw reads was conducted using FastQC and Fastp (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk) to remove low‐quality reads, followed by the trimming of poly‐A tails and adapter sequences. Subsequently, cell barcodes and unique molecular identifiers were extracted. The STAR software was then used to align the sequencing reads to the reference genome GRCh38, employing the Ensembl version 92 annotation. Unique molecular identifier counts and gene counts for each cell were obtained using featureCounts from the Rsubread package, facilitating the generation of expression matrix files for further analysis.

Quality Control, Dimension Reduction, and Clustering

Cells were filtered by excluding those with unique molecular identifier counts <30 000, and gene counts ranging from 200 to 5000 were retained for analysis. Additionally, cells exhibiting >20% mitochondrial content were excluded. Post filtering, functions from the Seurat package (version 2.3) were applied for dimensionality reduction and clustering. Subsequently, the Normalize Data and Scale Data functions were employed to standardize and scale the gene expression data. The top 2000 variable genes were then subjected to principal component analysis using the Find Variable Features function. Using the top 20 principal components, cell clustering was performed using the Find Clusters function. The Harmony algorithm was applied to mitigate batch effects between samples. Finally, the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection algorithm was used to visualize the cells in a 2‐dimensional space.

Cell Type Annotation

The identification of cell types within each cluster was determined by examining the expression of canonical markers among the differentially expressed genes, as cataloged in the SynEcoSys database. Visualization techniques, including heatmaps, dot plots, and violin plots, were employed to illustrate the expression of these markers for cell type identification, using Seurat (version 3.1.2).

Differentially Expressed Genes Analysis

The differentially expressed genes were identified using the Seurat FindMarkers function, applying the Wilcoxon likelihood‐ratio test with default parameters. Genes expressed in more than 10% of the cells within a cluster, with an average log fold change greater than 0.25, were classified as differentially expressed genes. For cell type annotation within each cluster, the expression profiles of canonical markers identified among the differentially expressed genes were compared with markers reported in the literature. The expression levels of these markers across different cell types were visualized using heatmaps, dot plots, and violin plots. Cells identified as doublets, which exhibited the expression of markers from multiple cell types, were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses were conducted using the “cluster Profiler” R package. Pathways with a P value of <0.05 were considered significantly enriched. GO gene sets including molecular function, biological process, and cellular component categories were used as reference.

Statistical Analysis

We have assessed the normality using D’Agostino‐Pearson normality test for large samples (n>6) and Shapiro–Wilk normality test for small samples (n≤6). The results indicated that all data were normally distributed (P>0.05). The results of normality test were included in the Statistics Data Set (Data S1). The quantitative variables are expressed as mean±SEM. All statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Statistical differences were assessed by unpaired Student’s t test between 2 groups. One‐way ANOVA or 2‐way ANOVA was used with Bonferroni’s/Tukey’s post hoc test to compare multiple groups. For the Rotarod and pole tests, repeated measures 2‐way ANOVA was performed with Tukey’s post hoc test. Specific statistical tests and actual P values are stated in the figure legends and Statistics Data Set (Data S1). Two‐sided P values were used for all comparisons, and values <0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Transcriptional Activation of Endothelial Cells in the Brains of hCTSDhi Mice

To assess the effect of circulating CTSD in vivo, the transgenic hCTSDhi mouse with a knockin of the human CTSD gene driven by human CD68 promoter 26 established in our laboratory were used. Our previous work verified the expression of human CTSD in the peripheral monocytes by immunostaining and found the concentration of circulating CTSD in the plasma of hCTSDhi mice was significantly higher than that in the littermate wild‐type (WT) control mice. 26 The body weight and sex ratio were similar between the hCTSDhi and littermate WT control mice (Figure S1A,B). To investigate the potential impact of circulating CTSD on cerebrovascular network, vascular labeling by India ink was conducted and the results showed no significant differences in vascular density in the cortical surface between the hCTSDhi and littermate WT control mice (n=6, Figure 1A). To further assess the effect of high‐level circulating CTSD on vascular development, the brain slices were prepared for immunostaining using antibody against CD31, marker for endothelial cells of blood vessels. The results showed no significant difference between hCTSDhi and WT mice in the length, density, branching index, and diameter of brain microvessels (n=6, Figure 1B; Figure S1C).

Next, to determine whether the high‐level circulating CTSD could induce gene expression alterations in the brain vascular endothelial cells of hCTSDhi mice, scRNA sequencing was conducted to profile the gene expression changes in the brain of hCTSDhi mice, with littermate WT mice as control (Figure 1C). The distinct 15 cell clusters were assigned cell types (Figure 1C) using cell‐type markers (Table S1). Cell clustering analysis revealed that the ratio of brain endothelial cells was slightly changed in hCTSDhi mice (16.2%) compared with control (11.7%) (Figure 1D; Figure S1D). Then we performed differential gene analysis with the cluster of endothelial cells. The differentially expressed genes (Table S2) were subjected to GO analysis and we found the enrichment was associated with ameboidal‐type cell migration and cell adhesion (Figure 1E). The results of gene set enrichment analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in the term “positive regulation of leukocyte cell‐cell adhesion” (Figure 1F). These data suggested that the increased circulating CTSD resulted in transcription alterations in the brain endothelium likely involved in the adhesion and migration process of peripheral leukocytes.

Enhanced Neutrophil Infiltration in the Injured Brains of hCTSDhi Mice

Then we sought to assess the neutrophil infiltration in the brain of hCTSDhi mice with 2 different brain injury mouse models. First, using controlled cortical impact model, 27 the mice were subjected to moderate TBI producing traumatic insult to the cortex without affecting hippocampus (Figure S1E). After the injury, the mouse coronal brain slices were prepared after cardiac perfusion, and the brain slices were subjected to immunostaining with antibodies recognizing MPO (a neutrophil marker) and CD31 (Figure 2A). The results showed that the number of extravascular infiltrated neutrophils (arrows in Figure 2A) in the brain parenchyma of hCTSDhi mice were higher than that in WT littermate mice from day 1 (D1) to day 7 (D7) after injury, showing a prominent peak at day 3 (D3) (Figure 2A) (P<0.05, D1; P<0.001, D3; P<0.01, D7; n=6 for each group, top panel of Figure 2B). The brain slice from sham‐operated mice showed no neutrophils in the brain (Figure S2A). We found certain number of neutrophils located within the lumen of cerebral vessels after TBI injury (arrowheads in Figure 2A), which was likely to be the neutrophils adhered to the endothelium. We counted the intravascular neutrophils and found the alterations of intravascular neutrophils in hCTSDhi mice after TBI injury (Figure 2A) (P<0.01, D1 and D3; P<0.05, D7; n=6 for each group, bottom panel of Figure 2B) were in line with that of extravascular neutrophils. To assess whether the high‐level CTSD may affect the infiltration of monocytes into the brain, the brain slices were immunostained with Ly6c antibody to identify the brain‐infiltrating blood‐derived monocytes, together with CD31 antibody (Figure 2C). The results showed that the number of extravascular monocytes (arrows in Figure 2C) and intravascular monocytes (arrowheads in Figure 2C) in the brain of hCTSDhi mice was at similar levels to the WT mice at D1 and D3 after injury (n=6 for each group, Figure 2D). At D7 post injury, the extravascular infiltrated monocytes remained unchanged (top panel, Figure 2D), whereas the intravascular adhered monocytes were slightly increased in the brains of hCTSDhi mice compared with WT mice (P<0.01, D7; n=6 for each group, bottom panel of Figure 2D). These findings were verified by the immunostaining results using antibody against CCR2, another monocytes marker (Figure S2C). The body weight post injury showed no significant difference between hCTSDhi and WT mice (Figure S2B). These results indicated that high‐level circulating CTSD enhanced neutrophil infiltration into the brain at the early stage, from D1 to D7, after TBI injury.

To assess the size of brain lesion induced by TBI injury, magnetic resonance imaging was performed at D1, D3, and D7 after TBI injury. The T2‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging sequences were used to evaluate edema surrounding the injury site. The results showed that TBI induced significant cerebral edema from D1 to D7 post‐TBI (Figure 3A). We found the volume of brain edema in hCTSDhi mice from D1 to D7 post TBI was increased compared with that in WT littermate controls (P<0.01, D1 and D7; P<0.01, D3, n=6 per group, Figure 3A). Then the loss of neurons in the peri‐injury region (black squares, upper panel in Figure 3B) was assessed by immunostaining with antibody recognizing NeuN, a neuronal marker. The results showed that the number of neurons in the cortex surrounding the injury site was significantly reduced in hCTSDhi mice, at D1 and D3 post TBI, compared with WT controls (P<0.001, n=6 per group, bottom panel, Figure 3B).

hCTSDhi Mice Exhibited Aggravated Motor Impairment After Brain Injury

We performed behavioral tests to evaluate the recovery of motor function after the brain injury. The Pole test was performed after TBI to measure the time for each mouse to descend a vertical pole. The results of Pole test showed that the time necessary for hCTSDhi mice to descend the pole was significantly increased, from D1 to D7 post TBI, compared with WT littermate control mice (P<0.001, D1–D5; P<0.01, D7, n=12 per group, Figure 4A). The descending time for hCTSDhi mice was reduced to a similar level as in WT mice at D10 after TBI injury (Figure 4A). Then the Rotarod test was conducted and the average latency to fall from a rotating rod were recorded for each mouse. The results showed that the time for hCTSDhi mice to remained on the rod was reduced, from D5 to D14 post injury, compared with WT control mice (P<0.001, n=12 per group, Figure 4B). The remaining time of hCTSDhi mice was recovered to a similar level as in WT mice at D21 after TBI injury (Figure 4B). Consistently, in the condition of ischemic/reperfusion brain injury, the results of Pole test and Rotarod test showed that the motor performance of hCTSDhi mice was worse than the WT mice from D1 to D28 post injury (n=8 for each group, Figure 4D and E). These indicated that high‐level circulating CTSD exacerbated the motor coordination deficit after brain injury. Additionally, an increase in the neurological deficit score was observed in hCTSDhi mice 3 days after MCAO compared with WT mice (P<0.01, n=15 for each group, Figure 4C).

To investigate the molecular mechanism of the pro‐CTSD‐enhanced neutrophil transmigration, the upregulated genes of the differentially expressed genes in the endothelial cell subset of the scRNA data (Table S2) were subjected to GO enrichment network analysis. We found the upregulated GO networks were associated with terms like “leukocyte adhesion to vascular endothelial cell” and “positive regulation of cell adhesion” (Figure 5B). Among the identified hub genes, VCAM‐1 was connected to 10 nodes and was ranked first (Figure 5B). Violin plot analysis revealed that the transcriptional level of VCAM‐1 in the brain endothelial cells of hCTSDhi mice (5157 cells from 3 mice) was significantly higher than that in WT littermate controls (4003 cells from 3 mice) (P<0.0001, Figure 5C). Further scRNA‐seq analysis suggested that the increased expression of VCAM‐1 in the brain endothelial cell population of hCTSDhi mice is more prominent in the vein subpopulation (Figure 5D). Then the brain endothelial cell populations from the scRNA‐seq data were classified as artery, vein, and capillary endothelial cells, and the capillary endothelial cells were further classified as capillary‐venous, capillary, and capillary‐artery endothelial cells, by the corresponding markers (Figure S5A). Pseudo‐time expression analysis indicated that the differential expression of VCAM‐1 in the brain endothelial cells of hCTSDhi mice was particularly apparent in the subpopulations of venous endothelial cells compared with the WT controls (solid line, Figure 5E). In contrast, the expression of PECAM1 remained constant in the subpopulations of endothelial cells of hCTSDhi mice as in WT mice (dashed line, Figure 5E). To verify this, the brain slices were prepared for immunostaining with antibodies recognizing CD31, VCAM‐1, and arterial‐marker ACTA2, respectively. The results showed that VCAM‐1 expression in the cerebral venous endothelium of hCTSDhi mice was significantly increased compared with the WT controls (P<0.01, n=6 for each group, Figure 5F). These data demonstrated that pro‐CTSD upregulated endothelial VCAM‐1 to enhance the adhesion of neutrophils on brain endothelial cells (especially on vein endothelial cells) for their transmigration. We further identified that the intracellular NF‐kB (nuclear factor kappa B) signaling in brain endothelial cells was activated by pro‐CTSD to trigger VCAM‐1 expression (Figure S5B–F). To exclude the direct effect of pro‐CTSD on neutrophils, the neutrophils were preincubated with recombinant pro‐CTSD and then the neutrophils were collected by centrifugation and subjected to transmigration assay. We found the neutrophil transmigration remained unaffected by pro‐CTSD pretreatment (Figure S5G), indicating the effect of pro‐CTSD to promote neutrophil transmigration was achieved by the activation of endothelial cells but not by the activation of neutrophils.

The Exacerbated Neutrophil Infiltration and Motor Deficit in hCTSDhi Mice After Injury Was Ameliorated by In Vivo Silencing of Brain Endothelial VCAM‐1

To determine whether the increased VCAM‐1 in brain endothelium is required for the enhanced neutrophil infiltration in the brain of hCTSDhi mice after injury, the endothelial VCAM‐1 was downregulated by VCAM‐1‐specific shRNA encoded in AAV‐BR1 virus showing brain microvascular specificity without inducing neuroinflammation 24 , 25 followed by analysis of the TBI‐induced brain damage (Figure 5G). Immunostaining of the brain slices was performed 8 weeks after virus injection, and the results showed that the elevated VCAM‐1 expression in the brain microvascular endothelium of hCTSDhi mice was suppressed by the AAV‐BR1 virus containing VCAM‐1 shRNA (P<0.001, n=6 per group, Figure 5H). Further immunostaining results showed that the increased MPO+ neutrophils in the parenchyma and within the vascular wall in the brain of hCTSDhi mice after TBI injury were reduced by endothelial‐specific VCAM‐1 shRNA (P<0.001, n=6 per group, Figure 5I), suggesting that the enhanced neutrophil infiltration in the brain of hCTSDhi mice post TBI were attenuated by downregulation of endothelial VCAM‐1. To evaluate the neuronal damage, immunostaining of the brain slices with NeuN antibody were performed and the results showed that the neuronal loss in the brain of hCTSDhi mice after injury were recovered by the delivery of endothelial‐specific VCAM‐1 shRNA (P<0.01, n=6 per group, Figure 5J). Further results of behavioral analysis, including the Pole test and Rotarod test, showed that the aggravated motor deficit of hCTSDhi mice after TBI injury was ameliorated by downregulation of endothelial VCAM‐1 (n=12 per group, Figure 5K and 5L). These data collectively demonstrated that brain endothelial VCAM‐1 upregulation is responsible for the enhanced neutrophil infiltration and the exacerbated motor deficit in hCTSDhi mice after TBI injury.

To establish diabetic models, the mice were fed with a high‐fat diet combined with the streptozotocin injection, and then body weight and blood glucose were monitored accordingly (Figure S6C,D). Downregulation of Circulating CTSD Mitigates TBI‐Induced Neutrophil Infiltration and Motor Deficits

Previous research has not comprehensively elucidated the alterations in plasma CTSD levels following acute trauma, particularly in the context of TBI. To evaluate the plasma CTSD levels induced by TBI during the acute phase, ELISA was conducted on days 1, 3, and 7 post TBI. The findings indicated a significant increase in CTSD levels from day 1 to day 7, with a peak observed on day 3 (n=6 per group, Figure 6G). Subsequently, we investigated whether reducing circulating CTSD levels could confer protective effects against TBI‐induced brain damage. CTSDmKO mice were subjected to TBI, with CTSDloxP/+ mice serving as controls. ELISA results demonstrated that plasma CTSD levels were elevated in TBI mice compared with controls, and this elevation was significantly attenuated in CTSDmKO TBI mice through heterozygous knockout of CTSD (P<0.01, n=6 for each group, Figure 6H). Additionally, immunostaining of brain sections was performed to evaluate neutrophil infiltration following TBI. The findings indicated a significant reduction in the number of infiltrated neutrophils (arrows, left panel, Figure 6I) and neutrophils adhered to the vascular wall (arrowheads, left panel, Figure 6I) in the brains of CTSDmKO mice compared with their littermate CTSDloxP/+ control counterparts at day 3 post TBI (P<0.001, n=6 per group, right panel, Figure 6I). Correspondingly, behavioral assessments using the Pole test and Rotarod test demonstrated that TBI‐induced motor deficits were less pronounced in CTSDmKO TBI mice than in CTSDloxP/+ TBI mice (n=6 per group, Figure 6J and 6K). Collectively, these data suggest that the downregulation of circulating CTSD mitigates TBI‐induced neutrophil infiltration and brain damage and attenuates motor deficits during the acute phase.

DISCUSSION

Novel Regulatory Role of Circulating CTSD in Neutrophil Infiltration

CTSD is well known as a lysosome‐localized aspartic endopeptidase. Studies reveal the secretion of CTSD and the extracellular CTSD is emerging as a potent regulator of intercellular signaling. 30 The concentration of CTSD in the plasma of patients was reported to be correlated with diseases including breast carcinoma, 31 heart failure, 32 and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis. 33 Yet the exact function of circulating CTSD remained obscure. Here, using the transgenic mice harboring a high level of CTSD in the plasma, we found that circulating CTSD exacerbates the entry of neutrophils into the brain in response to TBI and ischemic brain injury. To our knowledge, this study represents the first evidence revealing the role of circulating CTSD in neutrophil infiltration. We noticed that a high range of circulating CTSD was found in myocardial infarction and 34 nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, 33 which share the characteristics of acute aseptic inflammatory responses. Thus, we thought the plasma CTSD is likely to promote the progression of diseases by exacerbating the early‐phase inflammatory responses dominated by neutrophils. In contrast, in Alzheimer’s disease 35 and Parkinson’s disease, 36 which are associated with chronic and prolonged inflammation in the brain, studies showed the plasma CTSD levels are negatively correlated with disease progression, indicating that the circulating CTSD may play distinct role rather than facilitating neutrophil infiltration in the condition of these chronic neurodegenerative disorders.

Circulating Pro‐CTSD Upregulates Brain Endothelial VCAM‐1

In response to the inflammation in the brain parenchyma, the peripheral neutrophils execute adhesion and migration events at the cerebrovascular endothelium. The adhesion molecules VCAM‐1, ICAM1/2, P‐selectin, and E‐selectin located at the vascular luminal surface are necessary for the adhesion of neutrophils to brain endothelium. 37 In this study, we found circulating pro‐CTSD upregulates endothelial VCAM‐1 in brain endothelium, and the increased VCAM‐1 is required for the enhanced adhesion and infiltration of neutrophils into the injured brain. The factors that have the potential to modulate endothelial VCAM‐1 expression, such as TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor alpha), 38 IL (interleukin‐1β), 39 IL4, 40 and CXCL1, 38 , 41 are mostly inflammatory cytokines. Here we present pro‐CTSD as an additional regulator of endothelial VCAM‐1 for neutrophil infiltration. Our study calls for further investigation of the proinflammatory activity of circulating pro‐CTSD in the context of endothelium‐related inflammation.

In the brains of hCTSDhi mice, the increase of endothelial VCAM‐1 is primarily restricted to the venous part of the brain vessels. It is known that circulating neutrophils are first deformed at the capillary bed, and then the amoeba‐shape allows neutrophils to cross the endothelium into the inflamed tissue primarily in the vein with low shear stress. 7 Thus, the circulating pro‐CTSD‐induced VCAM‐1 upregulation is compatible with the adhesion and migration behavior of neutrophils for their crossing through the vascular wall. The exact mechanism by which circulating pro‐CTSD regulates VCAM‐1 expression particularly at the venous part of the brain vasculature remains an interesting issue to be explored in the future.

The Nonenzymatic Function of Circulating Pro‐CTSD

CTSD is a well‐known ubiquitously expressed lysosomal protease involved in proteolytic degradation. Knockout of CTSD in mice primarily caused impairment in the autophagy‐lysosome system. 42 , 43 , 44 Studies from the past 2 decades revealed that CTSD could be secreted, in the form of prototype pro‐CTSD, into the extracellular environment in breast, prostate, and lung cancer cells. 45 Here we found that pro‐CTSD is released to the extracellular medium by the monocytes (Figure 5A, 5C and 5D). The extracellular pro‐CTSD has been reported to be active in both proteolytic and nonproteolytic manner. 30 In our study, the secreted pro‐CTSD is able to promote neutrophil transmigration by inducing VCAM‐1 expression in brain endothelium. The extracellular pro‐CTSD was reported to nonenzymatically promote growth of breast cancer cells 46 and invasive outgrowth of fibroblasts, 47 which is somewhat in line with our findings. Regarding the signaling cascades triggered by pro‐CTSD, we revealed the activation of intracellular NF‐kB signaling in brain endothelial cells following the application of pro‐CTSD (Figure S5E, F). To exclude the potential effect of pro‐CTSD on neutrophils, the neutrophils preincubated with recombinant pro‐CTSD were subjected to transmigration assay. The results demonstrated that neutrophil transmigration was unaffected by pro‐CTSD pretreatment (Figure S5G), suggesting pro‐CTSD does not directly affect neutrophil migration behavior. Additionally, to exclude the possibility that CTSD per se acts as a chemokine to promote transendothelial migration of neutrophils, we performed in vitro chemotaxis assay with the presence of pro‐CTSD (125 ng/mL) in the lower chamber of the transwell. The results showed that the transendothelial migration of neutrophils loaded into the upper chamber was not affected by the pro‐CTSD in the lower chamber (Figure S5I), indicating that pro‐CTSD alone did not have any chemokine functions. We further analyzed the upregulated genes encoding chemokines in the single‐cell sequencing results of cerebrovascular endothelial cells of hCTSDhi mice and we found upregulation of CXCL12 (Figure S5J), a chemokine contributing to the recruitment of neutrophils. 48 , 49 This suggests the possibility that plasma CTSD may stimulate endothelial cells to secrete CXCL12 to promote neutrophil infiltration. Thus, it could be proposed that pro‐CTSD may play a dual role by promoting VCAM1 expression and CXCL12 secretion in the brain endothelium to facilitate neutrophil infiltration into the brain after injury.

The Feasibility of Targeting CTSD Therapeutically for the Treatment of Acute Brain Injury

Elevated plasma CTSD levels have been documented in various pathological conditions, including diabetes, 26 obese, 50 alcohol‐related liver disease, 51 and osteoporosis. 52 Numerous studies have demonstrated that hyperglycemia 53 , 54 and obesity 55 , 56 exacerbate ischemic brain injury, leading to heightened neurological impairments. This aligns with our findings that elevated plasma CTSD levels contribute to the aggravation of ischemia–reperfusion injury in murine models. Furthermore, we explored the protective effects of downregulating plasma CTSD against ischemia‐induced brain damage in conditions characterized by elevated plasma CTSD levels, such as diabetes. Our findings indicated that the exacerbated cerebral infarction observed in diabetic mice was significantly ameliorated when plasma CTSD was downregulated. Consistently, we observed that a reduction in plasma CTSD attenuated neurological deficits and improved the survival rate of diabetic mice subjected to MCAO.

Our ELISA experiments have identified that TBI is associated with elevated CTSD levels in plasma. In addition, immunostaining analyses and behavioral assessments have demonstrated that the downregulation of circulating CTSD reduces neutrophil infiltration and alleviates motor deficits during the acute phase of TBI. These findings suggest that decreasing circulating CTSD exerts a protective effect against diabetes‐exacerbated ischemic brain damage and TBI‐induced brain damage during the acute stage. Therefore, therapeutically targeting CTSD may represent a viable strategy for the treatment of acute brain injury.

The Role of Monocytes in the Injury‐Induced Brain Damage of hCTSDhi Mice

The circulating monocytes are able to enter the brain during inflammation contributing to neuroinflammatory responses. 57 In contrast to the robustly increased neutrophil infiltration in the brain of hCTSDhi mice after injury, the infiltrated monocytes in the brain of hCTSDhi mice remains constant after TBI injury (Figure 2C and 2D; Figure S2C) as well as that after ischemic injury (Figure 2E and 2F). Thus, we thought the contribution of infiltrated monocytes to the exacerbated brain damage in hCTSDhi mice is limited. We noticed that, late at D7 after TBI injury, the intravascular adhered monocytes in the brain of hCTSDhi mice were slightly higher than controls (bottom panel, Figure 2D; Figure S2C), likely caused by the increased brain endothelial VCAM‐1 in hCTSDhi mice, supported by the facilitation of monocytes adhesion to endothelium by VCAM‐1. 58 Recent studies revealed the monocytes that preferably adhered to the microvasculature are involved in promoting the inflammatory response in the condition of rheumatoid arthritis 59 and lupus nephritis. 60 It will be interesting to further characterize the identity of the TBI‐induced adhered monocytes in hCTSDhi mice to determined its potential contribution to the injury‐associated inflammation in the brain. Using mice expressing human CTSD under human CD68 promoter, our study revealed the role of circulatory CTSD in neutrophil infiltration into the brain after injury. However, caution must be taken in the interpretation of these results due to the difference between human and mouse CTSD proteins as well as the stringency of human CD68 promoter though its activity was excluded in the brain at resting condition. 26

It has been reported that CTSD expression is regulated by estrogen in breast cancer cell line MCF‐7 expressing estrogen receptor, 61 implicating the potential sex‐related effect of circulating pro‐CTSD. To clarify this, the brain damage results from hCTSDhi mice were divided into 2 groups according to sex and subjected to further statistical analysis. We found that sex did not appear to affect the cerebral circulation (Figure S3A,B), brain damage (Figures S5H and S3D), and neutrophil infiltration in hCTSDhi mice (Figure S2D). These discrepancies may be due to different experimental systems used in different studies.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrated that high‐level circulating pro‐CTSD exacerbates the neutrophil infiltration and motor deficit in response to brain injury. The enhanced neutrophil transmigration was caused by the pro‐CTSD‐induced VCAM‐1 upregulation in brain endothelium. Reduction of circulating CTSD levels showed protective effect against the diabetes‐aggravated ischemic brain damages and the motor deficit.
