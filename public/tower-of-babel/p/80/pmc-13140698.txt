History‐indicated cerclage versus ultrasound cervical length screening in women with a history of one extremely preterm birth: A comparative observational cohort study
Authors: Mathilde Terral, Chloé Lieng, François Goffinet, Paul Guerby, Yoann Athiel, Lola Loussert
Published: 2026

Key message

In women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or extremely preterm birth suggestive of cervical insufficiency, history‐indicated cerclage is associated with a twofold decreased risk of delivery before 34 weeks compared with ultrasound cervical length screening.

INTRODUCTION

Preterm birth occurs in 6.8%–13.2% of deliveries worldwide, 1 7–10% in high‐income countries. 2 , 3 Complications of prematurity are the leading cause of death among children under 5 years old and are responsible for severe long‐term morbidity, with significant societal and economic costs. 4 Effective preventive measures are urgently needed.

Cervical cerclage is performed to prevent the recurrence of preterm delivery in women with cervical insufficiency. However, there is no consensus on the diagnostic criteria for cervical insufficiency. Therefore, the diagnosis is difficult and studies on cervical cerclage include heterogeneous populations. Two management strategies can be offered: history‐indicated cerclage in the first trimester or ultrasound cervical length screening, with cerclage for women whose cervical length shortens. For women with a history of one preterm delivery, the available literature supports ultrasound screening: studies have found no difference in rates of preterm delivery and ultrasound screening may reduce unnecessary surgical procedures. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, the population selection in previous studies is debatable. Indeed, some of the women included were at a low risk of cervical insufficiency: previous delivery up to 32 weeks, obstetric history suggestive of another causal condition for preterm delivery. Therefore, the efficacy of history‐indicated cerclage could have been underestimated. As a result, international guidelines regarding the management of women with a history of one preterm delivery are inconsistent. 4 , 9 , 10 , 11 Our objective was to compare history‐indicated cerclage with ultrasound cervical length screening in women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or extremely preterm birth suggestive of cervical insufficiency.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study comparing two centers with different management strategies for women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or extremely preterm birth suggestive of cervical insufficiency. This study was conducted from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2022 in two French tertiary care maternity units.

Cervical insufficiency is classically defined as the inability to retain a pregnancy in the absence of labor, in the second trimester. 12 Given the lack of consensual diagnostic criteria, we defined as evocative of cervical insufficiency any delivery before 28 weeks of gestation (WG), in the absence of labor and in the absence of any other identifiable pathology. Therefore, we included women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or spontaneous preterm delivery before 28 weeks and we excluded women who experienced one of the following complications in their previous pregnancy: intrauterine fetal death before labor, delivery after invasive prenatal testing, bleeding from placenta previa or placental abruption. We also excluded women with congenital uterine anomalies. Women who had an emergency cervical cerclage at the time of the first preterm delivery were excluded because the next pregnancy was managed with cervical cerclage in both centers. Women with a history of more than one second‐trimester loss or preterm delivery were also excluded, because both centers systematically offered cerclages to these women. Finally, we excluded women with multiple gestations for the index pregnancy as this is a major independent risk factor for preterm delivery.

In the first center, women were offered an elective cerclage in the first trimester (cerclage center). McDonald cerclage was performed using Mersilene tape under spinal anesthesia. Progesterone administration was not part of the protocol.

In the second center, women were offered an ultrasound screening, with cerclage only for women with cervical shortening (ultrasound center). Cervical length was assessed by transvaginal ultrasound every 2 weeks, between 14 and 24 WG. Cervical cerclage was performed only if the cervical length was 25 millimeters or less before 24 WG. A McDonald cerclage was performed using Mersilene tape, under spinal anesthesia. In addition, women received daily 200 milligrams of intravaginal progesterone from 16 to 36 weeks to prevent preterm delivery. 13 , 14

In both centers, vaginal bacteriological analysis was performed prior to cervical cerclage and antibiotic treatment was prescribed if indicated.

In both centers, cervical cerclage was removed around 37 weeks.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was delivery between 15 and 33 weeks + 6 days.

Secondary outcomes were delivery between 15 and 21 weeks, delivery between 22 and 27 weeks, delivery between 28 and 33 weeks, delivery between 34 and 36 weeks, and a composite outcome of neonatal morbimortality.

The composite outcome of neonatal morbimortality included necrotizing enterocolitis, intraventricular hemorrhage grade III–IV, neonatal death or second‐trimester fetal loss, in accordance with the EPIPAGE‐2 cohort study. 15

Statistical analyses

Sample size calculation was based on the assumption that preterm delivery before 34 weeks would occur in 15% of the women included in the cerclage center and 25% in the ultrasound center. 16 We estimated that the inclusion of 250 women in each group would provide 80% power to detect a difference in the main outcome at a two‐sided alpha level of 5%.

We compared the characteristics of the two centers, using Chi2 for categorical variables and the Student t‐test for quantitative variables. For the association analysis, we used univariable then multivariable logistic regression to estimate odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals. Covariates included in the multivariable analysis were risk factors for preterm delivery identified in the literature 17 : maternal age, ethnicity, smoking status and history of term delivery. Covariates were categorized as shown in Table 1. There were 284 (90.4%) women with no missing data for covariates included in the multivariable model. Multivariable analysis was performed on complete cases. Demographic characteristics, obstetric history and outcomes of the women with full data were similar to those of women with missing data (data not shown).

We performed a “per‐protocol analysis” after excluding women who did not undergo history‐indicated cerclage at the cerclage center and women who did not receive ultrasound cervical length screening at the ultrasound center.

We performed a sensitivity analysis after excluding women with a history of premature prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM). Indeed, some authors argue that preterm delivery in the context of PPROM is not indicative of cervical insufficiency.

We performed another sensitivity analysis excluding women with a history of delivery at 37 weeks or more. Indeed, these women might have a lower risk of cervical insufficiency.

In the absence of consensual diagnostic criteria, these two sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the impact of the diagnostic criteria on our results.

Finally, we rerun the analyses after stratification by several risk factors of preterm delivery: smoking status, chorioamnionitis in their previous pregnancy and delivery before 22 WG in their previous pregnancy.

All tests were two sided with p‐values ⩽0.05 defined as statistically significant. All analyses were performed with STATA 15.1.

RESULTS

During the study period, 41 591 women gave birth in the cerclage center and 39 310 gave birth in the ultrasound center. After exclusions, we included 165 women in the cerclage center and 149 in the ultrasound center (Figure 1).

Population selection. US, ultrasound.

Women of the cerclage center, compared with women of the ultrasound center, were older, less frequently from Europe, and more frequently from Subsaharan Africa. They more often had chronic hypertension, and they were less often smokers (Table 1). Regarding their previous pregnancy, women of the cerclage center had higher rates of chorioamnionitis and preterm prelabor rupture of membranes. Women from the 2 centers had a similar gestational age at delivery in their previous pregnancy (Table 1).

In the cerclage center, among the 165 women included, 155 (94%) underwent a history‐indicated cerclage (152 McDonald and 3 Shirodkar cerclages) and there was no per operative complication (Table 2). Ten women did not undergo a history‐indicated cerclage, including five women who underwent a therapeutic cerclage. In the ultrasound center, of the 149 women included, 140 (94.0%) underwent ultrasound cervical length screening and 9 (6.0%) received a history‐indicated cerclage. In the ultrasound center, 46 women (30.9%) had an ultrasound‐indicated cerclage (Table 2), at a median gestational age of 19 WG, and the median cervical length of these women was 19.9 millimeters. A cervical pessary was placed in 17 (11.4%) women of the ultrasound group, which was a deviation from the center protocol and reflected the choice of certain clinicians. The median gestational age at pessary placement was 24 WG and the median cervical length of these women was 25 millimeters. These 17 women did not receive cervical cerclage.

In the ultrasound center, 19 (41.3%) of the 46 women who underwent an ultrasound indicated cerclage, delivered before 34 WG. The median cervical length at cerclage placement was 18 millimeters in women who gave birth before 34 WG, vs. 21 millimeters in women who gave birth after 34 WG.

In the per‐protocol analysis, characteristics of the women and their previous pregnancy were similar to those in the main analysis (Table S1). As in the main analysis, the rate of delivery before 34 weeks was significantly lower in the cerclage center compared with the ultrasound center (19/155 (12.3%) vs. 38/140 (27.1%), p = 0.002). After adjusting for confounding factors, history‐indicated cerclage was associated with a decreased risk of delivery before 34 weeks compared with ultrasound cervical length screening (adjusted OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.13–0.65). (Table S2).

The sensitivity analysis after excluding women with PPROM included 193 women. Among women in the cerclage center, 13.4% (11/82) delivered before 34 weeks, compared with 24.3% (27/111) of the ultrasound center. The association analysis found similar patterns of association to the main analysis, although it did not reach statistical significance (adjusted OR 0.49 [0.19–1.25]) (Tables S3 and S4).

The sensitivity analysis excluding women with a history of delivery at 37 weeks or more included 258 women. Among women in the cerclage center, 13.1% (18/137) delivered before 34 weeks, compared with 27.3% (33/121) of the ultrasound center. The association analysis found similar patterns of association to the main analysis (adjusted OR 0.38[0.17–0.86]).

The subgroup analyses according to smoking status, preterm prelabor rupture of membranes and chorioamnionitis in previous pregnancy showed similar patterns of association to the main analysis. (Table S7) Notably, women with a previous delivery before 22 WG had a high risk of delivery before 34 WG in both groups (23.6% in the cerclage center and 24.6% in the ultrasound center).

DISCUSSION

In women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or extremely preterm birth suggestive of cervical insufficiency, history‐indicated cerclage was associated with a two‐fold decreased risk of delivery before 34 WG compared with ultrasound cervical length screening.

Ultrasound cervical length screening, allowed an important reduction in surgical interventions: cervical cerclage was avoided for 63.1% of the women.

The current literature does not provide robust evidence regarding the optimal management of women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or extremely preterm birth suggestive of cervical insufficiency.

A metaanalysis of four randomized trials compared cervical length screening to history‐indicated cerclage in women with singleton pregnancies and prior preterm delivery. 18 The authors concluded that both groups had similar rates of preterm delivery (PTD) before 34 weeks (17 vs. 23 percent, RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.48–1.20) and that ultrasound monitoring avoided cerclage in about 60 percent of women. Then, the authors concluded in favor of ultrasound screening and guidelines from most countries recommend performing ultrasound cervical length screening for women with a history of one preterm delivery 4 , 9 , 10 , 11 However, the population selection is a major limitation of this metaanalysis because some of the women included were at a low risk for cervical insufficiency. Two of the trials included women with a history of PTD between 28 and 34 WG 6 , 8 a group in which the likelihood of cervical insufficiency is considered to be low. 19 , 20 , 21 In addition, the obstetric history of some of the women included could be evocative of another causal condition for preterm delivery. Therefore, the metanalysis included some women with a low risk of delivery before 34 WG even without cerclage. This probably led to underestimating history‐indicated cerclage effectiveness. In contrast, in this study we included only women at a high risk of cervical insufficiency. Therefore, our study best targets women who would benefit the most from history‐indicated cerclage.

The major strength of this study is its population selection. We defined our criteria in order to focus on women who were at a high risk of cervical insufficiency. We included only women who had a history of delivery before 28 WG. We chose the threshold of 28 WG because it best targets women with cervical insufficiency, with a high risk of recurrence. 19 , 20 We excluded women whose previous preterm delivery was marked with intrauterine fetal death, delivery following invasive prenatal testing, bleeding from placenta previa, or placental abruption. Indeed, in these women, cervical insufficiency was unlikely to have caused their previous preterm delivery. Finally, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to focus exclusively on women with one single history of preterm delivery or second‐trimester fetal loss.

The main limitation of our study is its observational design. Therefore, the two groups differ on some characteristics such as maternal age and ethnicity. To limit this potential bias, we performed a multivariable analysis, with adjustment on risk factors for preterm delivery. In addition, because we compared women from two different centers, their management was different, beyond performing history‐indicated cerclage or ultrasound cervical length screening. At the cerclage center, only one woman received intravaginal progesterone, whereas most of the women at the ultrasound center did. However, the efficacy of progesterone in preventing preterm delivery recurrence is controversial. 12 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 If progesterone had an effect, we may have underestimated the difference between the 2 groups. Notably, 39.9% of women in the ultrasound center did not receive progesterone despite it being part of the center's protocol. This variability mainly reflected the discretion of the treating clinician. Another difference between the two centers is the mode of delivery, with fewer cesarean deliveries in the cerclage center than in the ultrasound center. We do not expect this difference to have affected our primary outcome, as all cesarean deliveries before 34 weeks were emergency procedures, performed either intrapartum or for urgent maternal/fetal indications, and thus did not influence the gestational age at delivery.

Another limitation is that, in the absence of consensual diagnostic criteria for cervical insufficiency, our criteria for population selection may be open to debate. For example, we chose not to exclude women whose preterm delivery occurred in the context of PPROM. Previous studies suggest that cervical insufficiency may predispose to PPROM. 26 , 27 The hypothetical pathophysiological mechanism is that cervical insufficiency could lead to abnormal colonization of the membranes by vaginal bacteria, with subsequent inflammation, which may promote PPROM. However, some authors argue that preterm delivery in the context of PPROM is not indicative of cervical insufficiency. To address this, we conducted a sensitivity analysis after exclusion of these women, which showed similar patterns of association to the main analysis. In addition, we did not exclude women with a history of delivery at 37 weeks or later. We considered that such history did not rule out cervical insufficiency (as it could be secondary to cervical trauma during labor for example 28 , 29 ). Nevertheless, given the potential controversy, we performed another sensitivity analysis excluding these women, which again showed similar patterns of result. These two sensitivity analyses reinforce the robustness of our findings.

Furthermore, as expected, there were deviations from the center protocol in both groups. In the cerclage center, 10 women did not undergo history‐indicated cerclage because their first consultation was after 15 weeks. In the ultrasound center, 9 women underwent a history‐indicated cerclage, against the center protocol, after discussion with their obstetrician. Our main analysis included the women who had protocol deviation because it reflects the reality that nonadherence occurs in real‐world practice. To explore the impact of these deviations on our results, we performed a per‐protocol analysis which supports our conclusion.

In addition, the size of our population was limited. Therefore, this study is underpowered to detect rare events, such as postoperative complications of cervical cerclage.

Finally, in the ultrasound center, cervical cerclage was performed if the cervical length was 25 millimeters or less before 24 WG. However, recent studies suggest that 30 mm may be a better threshold for identifying women at high risk of preterm delivery. 30 Therefore, offering cervical cerclage to all women with a cervical length of 30 millimeters, rather than 25, may have been more effective in preventing preterm delivery.

Our findings may help clinicians to inform women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or extremely preterm birth suggestive of cervical insufficiency about their treatment options and the risk–benefit ratio. Ultrasound cervical length screening avoids cerclage in more than 60% of women. However, the risk of delivery before 34 weeks is lower in women with a history‐indicated cerclage. This study should then enable informed shared decision making and improve the management of women with cervical insufficiency.

Further studies on this issue are needed to confirm our results. A randomized controlled trial including women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or extremely preterm birth suggestive of cervical insufficiency would help determine the optimal management for these women.

CONCLUSION

In this cohort study of women with a history of one second‐trimester loss or extremely preterm birth suggestive of cervical insufficiency, history‐indicated cerclage was associated with a two‐fold decreased risk of delivery before 34 WG, compared with ultrasound screening of cervical length. Therefore, history‐indicated cerclage appears to be a reasonable option to prevent the recurrence of preterm delivery in these women.
