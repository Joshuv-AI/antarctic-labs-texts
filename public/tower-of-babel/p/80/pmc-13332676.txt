Prosthetic Aortic Valve Vegetation
Authors: Justin Ortiz, Eric D. Saunders, Joshua M. Baker, Constantino S. Pena, Brian J. Schiro, Raul E. Herrera, Nish H. Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPercutaneous mechanical aspiration has been well described for the removal of vegetations involving valves of the right side of the heart. However, only limited cases describe its use for the aortic valve.Case SummaryAn 89-year-old man presented with methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus–positive infective endocarditis involving a bioprosthetic aortic valve and was determined to be high risk for surgery, He therefore underwent percutaneous mechanical aspiration to remove a vegetation.DiscussionInfective endocarditis is a life-threatening condition for which high-risk surgical candidates are at increased risk of related morbidity and mortality. This case highlights the potential important role of percutaneous mechanical aspiration in debulking vegetations in nonsurgical candidates. This case also discusses the use of cerebral protection to minimize the risk of procedure-related cerebral embolism.Take-Home MessagesPercutaneous mechanical aspiration offers a feasible treatment strategy for high-risk patients with aortic valve endocarditis in whom surgery may be precluded. However, additional studies are needed.

History of Presentation

An 89-year-old man presented with altered mental status, fever, nausea, and vomiting. He had a dental procedure performed 1 month ago for which he received amoxicillin perioperatively. The patient had rigors and fever and became altered, prompting presentation to an outside facility before transport to our facility. Blood cultures were positive for methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA). The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit because of hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressors.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of atrial fibrillation on Xarelto, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, pulmonary hypertension, and coronary artery disease. Past surgical history included transcatheter aortic valve replacement with a 29-mm Evolut Pro+ (Medtronic) for aortic stenosis 2 years before presentation. The patient denied a history of smoking or intravenous drug use.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for septic shock includes pulmonary infection, urinary tract infection, intra-abdominal infection, skin and soft tissue infection, and endovascular infection such as infective endocarditis (IE) or catheter-associated bacteremia. Given the patient’s history of transcatheter aortic valve replacement and MSSA-positive cultures, the most concerning differential was IE.

Investigations

A computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis and transthoracic echocardiogram were ordered to evaluate for IE (Figure 1). Imaging was indicative of pneumonia with associated pulmonary edema and bilateral pleural effusions as well as a moderate perivalvular leak and a suspected vegetation. Coronary computed tomography angiography and transesophageal echocardiography were performed the next day (Figure 2). These demonstrated a 1.9 × 0.4-cm mobile vegetation on the aortic surface of the bioprosthetic aortic valve attached at the 6 o’clock position, severe aortic regurgitation, and a redemonstrated moderate paravalvular leak. Management

The patient’s antibiotic regimen was escalated to cefazolin and rifampin given the presence of MSSA-positive cultures and aortic valve vegetation. Cardiothoracic surgery was initially consulted; however, the patient was determined not to be a surgical candidate given his Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk score of 80%. Given the persistent bacteremia despite intravenous antibiotics with a large mobile vegetation, interventional cardiology and interventional radiology were consulted and percutaneous mechanical aspiration was pursued.

Procedural Details

The procedure was performed in an advanced endovascular suite with the patient under general anesthesia. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography was used to visualize the vegetation (Figure 3). Thereafter, using bilateral 6-F radial access, dual Sentinel Cerebral Protection System devices (Boston Scientific) were positioned in the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries as well as the left subclavian artery origin (Figure 4). The device positioned at the left subclavian artery origin was advanced from a left radial approach, and only the proximal filter was deployed within the 11-mm-diameter vessel, with the articulating sheath and undeployed distal filter remaining within the aortic lumen. Right common femoral artery large-bore access was then obtained using a 17-F, 65-cm Element sheath (Penumbra), and a Flash 16 mechanical aspiration catheter (Penumbra) was manipulated toward the vegetation (Figure 5). The aspiration catheter was then engaged onto the vegetation using real-time transesophageal echocardiography. The vegetation was aspirated and removed in its entirety with <50 mL of blood loss (Figure 6). Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was hemodynamically stable and neurologically intact immediately after the procedure. Postprocedural blood and vegetation cultures were negative. Repeat transthoracic echocardiogram on postprocedure day 7 was notable for interval improvement of the paravalvular leak, and no vegetation was visualized (Figure 8). The patient’s course was complicated by aspiration pneumonia, acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, and recurrent encephalopathy. A brain magnetic resonance imaging was performed on postprocedure day 16 as part of the evaluation for the patient’s altered mental status, which was notable for multiple small acute and subacute infarcts of the left occipital lobe and left thalamus. Neurology ultimately determined that these were not clinically significant, and the patient’s mental status improved; however, the temporal relationship relative to the procedure could not be determined. The patient remained hospitalized for 3 months before ultimately being discharged home on oral cefadroxil for chronic suppressive therapy.Figure 8Transthoracic EchocardiogramTransthoracic echocardiogram on postprocedure day 5 depicts a well-seated bioprosthetic aortic valve without evidence of vegetation and noted perivalvular leak improvement.

Discussion

Medical therapy with or without surgery is the current standard of care for IE. Early surgery for left-sided IE is indicated in cases of valve dysfunction, identification of a highly resistant microorganism, persistent infection after antibiotic initiation, prosthetic valve with relapsing infection, recurrent emboli, and identification of a mobile vegetation, among others (Table 1). IE is associated with a mortality of 20% to 40% for which early surgery has been shown to be beneficial in a variety of presentations. Percutaneous mechanical aspiration has been well documented for the treatment of right-sided IE. One systematic review, published in 2021, identified 105 published cases of valve vegetation of which 102 were right-sided, emphasizing the sparsity of literature regarding left-sided IE.2 Importantly, left-sided IE carries a greater morbidity and mortality that is likely only compounded in high-risk, nonsurgical patients.3 The growing interest in using percutaneous mechanical aspiration has gained sufficient momentum that it is now included in the European Society of Cardiology guidelines for debulking of right-sided vegetations in selected patients who are high risk for surgery (Class IIb; Level of Evidence: C).4 Notably, there is no mention for use in left-sided vegetations and no mention at all in the 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines.1 Although outside guideline-supported practice, the authors concurred that the patient was likely to obtain greater benefit from an endovascular approach than from medical management alone, especially given the numerous features supporting early surgery.

To our knowledge, only 3 cases have been published describing the use of percutaneous mechanical aspiration for aortic valve IE, all of which happen to involve prosthetic valves, as was also encountered in our case.5, 6, 7 Each used unique techniques and various aspiration systems including the F18 AngioVac (AngioDynamics), CAT 8 (Penumbra), and CAT 12 (Penumbra). In our case, we successfully used a Flash 16 (Penumbra). In all 4 cases, the use of percutaneous mechanical aspiration played a central role in ameliorating each patient’s IE course.

Another important aspect of left-sided interventions is the use of cerebral protection devices to shield from iatrogenic distal embolization into the cerebral vasculature. In a case series by Akhtar et al8 describing 3 cases of percutaneous mechanical aspiration of a mitral valve vegetation, 1 patient experienced an ischemic stroke localized to the basilar artery on postprocedure day 2, ultimately transitioned to hospice, and died as a result. It is notable that this case did use cerebral protection within the brachiocephalic trunk and left common carotid artery but not the left subclavian or left vertebral. In another case, percutaneous mechanical aspiration of a mitral valve vegetation was complicated by distal embolization to the left axillary artery requiring surgical embolectomy.9 In the 3 cases describing aortic valve aspiration, 1 case did not use cerebral protection, 1 used a cerebral protection device for the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries, and 1 used the same cerebral protection device and intermittent balloon occlusion of the left subclavian artery during aspiration.5, 6, 7 No case reported an associated periprocedural stroke. In our case, we elected to use dual Sentinel devices to cover all 3 great vessels. Albeit off-label, this is regularly performed at our institution given data indicating that a clinically notable amount of iatrogenic embolization affects this traditionally unprotected, when using the Sentinel device, region arising from the left subclavian artery. A meta-analysis including pooled data from the MISTRAL-C and SENTINEL trials found that use of the Sentinel device was associated with a significantly lower volume of new ischemic lesions within protected territories while no significant difference in total infarct volume was observed.10 These findings support the theoretical benefit of cerebral protection while underscoring the limitations of incomplete (2-vessel) coverage and the importance of full 3-vessel protection. It is our opinion that the use of cerebral protection should be standard of care in left-sided percutaneous mechanical aspiration cases given the risk of embolic events; however, further research is needed to define the optimal protection strategy.10

In this case, because magnetic resonance imaging could not be obtained during the initial evaluation due to the patient’s altered mental status, it is uncertain when the small embolic strokes occurred and if they were related to the procedure. Given the high-risk patient presentation, it is plausible these emboli were secondary to the vegetation before intervention, although intraprocedural embolization is also a possibility. It is important to note that cerebral protection does not entirely negate intraprocedural distal embolization but does significantly reduce the volume of infarcted tissue and the rate of disabling stroke, consistent with this case.10,11

Conclusions

Percutaneous mechanical aspiration thrombectomy is a potential treatment strategy for high-risk surgical patients with IE involving the aortic valve. Additionally, the use of cerebral protection should be standard of care to protect from iatrogenic cerebral embolic events. Further studies are needed to establish the safety and efficacy of left-sided percutaneous mechanical aspiration; however, the use of percutaneous mechanical aspiration in high-risk surgical patients appears to be a promising alternative to early surgery to reduce IE-related morbidity and mortality.
