Sex-specific dynamic changes of serum short-chain fatty acids in rhesus monkeys during aging
Authors: Xiaopeng Chen, Songyuan Ye, Juncai Pu, Siwen Gui, Dongfang Wang, Xiaogang Zhong, Weiyi Chen, Yue Chen, Xiang Chen, Yanyi Jiang, Renjie Qiao, Yiyun Liu, Peng Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe potential association between short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and aging has gained increasing research attention. Although most studies have focused on fecal SCFAs, limited attention has been directed toward SCFAs in peripheral blood. Furthermore, this relationship remains insufficiently explored in non-human primate models.MethodsSerum samples were collected from eighty-five healthy Macaca mulatta. Serum SCFA concentrations were quantified using targeted gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. A subset of samples (19 males and 19 females) was further analyzed for reproductive and stress-related hormones using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits.ResultsSignificant age-related alterations in SCFA concentrations were identified. Isobutyric acid levels increased with age, whereas acetic acid, propionic acid, and valeric acid levels decreased. Moreover, the direction of SCFA alterations differed between males and females. Integrated analysis with hormone assays demonstrated that these sex-specific differences in SCFAs were closely associated with the activity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis.ConclusionSerum SCFA profiles varied significantly with both age and sex. The secretion of reproductive and stress-related hormones was highly correlated with these differences. These findings provide new insights into how aging influences sex-dependent SCFA metabolism and may contribute to understanding the mechanisms underlying age- and sex-related physiological changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02738-9.

Introduction

Aging represents a progressive and unavoidable pathophysiological process that exerts substantial threats to human health and vitality [1]. It is strongly associated with multiple age-related diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease [2], atherosclerosis [3], diabetes [4], and Parkinson’s disease [5]. To unravel the underlying mechanisms of aging, multiple theories have been put forward [6–8], including the free radical theory, mitochondrial DNA damage, genetic programming, and telomere shortening.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are metabolic byproducts generated when intestinal microorganisms ferment dietary fiber and indigestible carbohydrates [9], among which acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid are the most prevalent. The majority of SCFAs are quickly absorbed from the intestinal cavity into the circulatory system, whereas less than 5% are eliminated through feces [9]. Once absorbed, SCFAs undergo extensive metabolism mainly in the liver. Consequently, SCFAs in peripheral blood are abundant and relatively stable, making them suitable for systemic investigations. Acetic acid, for instance, is primarily utilized by the liver for energy and serves as a precursor for cholesterol, fatty acids, glutamic acid, and glutamine [10]. Additionally, acetic acid is capable of traversing the blood-brain barrier and supplying energy to astrocytes [11].

Growing evidence points to a potential connection between SCFAs and the aging process. SCFAs exert an impact on various physiological activities, such as the regulation of glucose homeostasis, immune function, and lipid metabolism [9, 12]. Research conducted on germ-free mice has shown that SCFAs regulate blood-brain barrier permeability, thereby influencing the extent to which circulating molecules enter brain tissue [13, 14]. Furthermore, SCFAs act on gut-brain communication pathways, which include immune, endocrine, vagus nerve, and direct humoral pathways [15]. These functions are closely related to key factors implicated in the aging process. However, research specifically addressing the role of SCFAs in aging remains limited, and most existing studies focus primarily on intestinal SCFAs [16–20].

Non-human primates represent a valuable translational model in medical research [20] because of their high genetic, anatomical, reproductive, developmental, cognitive, and social similarity to humans [21]. For instance, cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) share 39.49% of non-redundant gut bacterial genes with humans, compared with only 0.6% shared by mice [22]. Moreover, unlike humans, non-human primates can be maintained under controlled conditions, reducing confounding environmental influences. These features make them particularly suitable for studies of age-related physiological and pathological changes.

Building on these observations, we hypothesize that serum SCFA levels undergo significant age-related changes in non-human primates, and that these changes follow distinct sex-specific patterns. While previous studies have primarily focused on fecal SCFAs in rodent models, this study is among the first to examine dynamic changes in serum SCFAs in non-human primates across aging, alongside reproductive and stress-related hormone profiles. The results provide novel insights into sex-specific metabolic alterations during aging.

Materials and methods

Animals

The study population comprised 85 rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta), including 55 females and 38 males, aged between 2 and 30 years. The monkeys were bred at Suzhou Xishan Zhongke Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu Province, China) and maintained in captive breeding groups. Each group was housed in a separate enclosure (12 m × 4 m × 3 m) with 6–15 monkeys, typically including 1–2 adult males, females, and infants. Standardized compound feed was supplied two times per day, with the first feeding at 08:00 and the second at 17:00, and fresh fruits and vegetables were given at 12:00. Cages were cleaned daily at 09:00. Keepers and veterinarians conducted daily health checks, and microbial quarantine was performed twice yearly.

The monkeys were housed in semi-open environments designed to simulate natural habitats. Enclosures were equipped with recreational facilities (rings, metal balls, etc.) as well as climbing structures (posts and beams) to maintain natural activity and climbing behavior. Screening was conducted to minimize potential confounding factors, including caretaker variability and differences in cage environments.

Selection of monkeys was based on age and sex distribution, resulting in the final cohort of 85 healthy individuals. Monkeys were categorized into three groups based on developmental stage and approximate aging thresholds [23]: Young group: age ≤ 4 years, Adult group: 5 ≤ age ≤ 15 years, Old group: age ≥ 16 years.

Sample collection and Preparation

Sample collection was conducted during the non-mating season. Under anesthesia, venous blood was collected from each monkey using a blood collection needle inserted into a vein in the lower extremities. A volume of 5–6 mL of venous blood was drawn into a sodium Heparin anticoagulation tube, which was inverted several times and mixed before centrifugation at 4 °C (3000 rpm, 15 min). The upper supernatant was first collected, aliquoted, and finally stored at a temperature of −80 °C for subsequent experimental procedures.

Targeted metabolomics

For serum extraction: Mix 100 uL serum with 200 uL methanol, vortex 5 min, centrifuge at 12,000 rpm (4 °C, 10 min), take 1 uL supernatant for GC-MS. Use Agilent 8890GC-5977B MSD (HP-FFAP column), splitless injection. Inlet/transfer line/ion source: 200/230/250°C. Carrier gas: 99.999% Helium (1.0 mL/min). Oven: 80 °C to 160 °C (5 °C/min) to 200 °C (20 °C/min, Hold 2 min). Identify SCFAs via full-scan (m/z, vs. standards), quantify via target ions (Supplementary Table 1); analyze with MassHunter (external standard), express in ng/mL. The detailed methods for targeted metabolomics analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

ELISA

To measure the serum concentrations of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits produced by KALANG (Shanghai, China) were utilized. Standard curves were constructed based on absorbance readings at 450 nm, and the concentrations of these hormones were computed using these curves.

Statistical analysis

Serum SCFA and Hormone measurements were subjected to statistical analysis using SPSS 21.0 (Armonk, NY, USA), whereas GraphPad Prism 9.3.1 (La Jolla, CA, USA) served for graphic construction. Data are visualized as boxplots, incorporating mean values, extreme values, and mean ± Standard Error of the Mean (SEM). To verify the normality of the collected data, the Shapiro-Wilk test was adopted. Meanwhile, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlational relationships between the variables under investigation. When it came to comparing statistical differences across the three experimental groups, the adjusted Kruskal-Wallis test was selected as the analytical method. In this study, a P-value of less than 0.05 was defined as the threshold for determining statistical significance.

Results

Basic information about rhesus monkeys

After removing outlier data (defined as values that exceeded 1.5 times the interquartile range in the measurement of at least one SCFA [24]), a total of 85 rhesus macaques (males = 35, females = 50) were included in the study. Their age distribution is shown in Fig. 1, and body Weight, sex, and age are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Group distributions were as follows: Young group, 10 males and 10 females; adult group, 18 males and 27 females; old group, 7 males and 13 females. The mean age of male macaques was 9.6 ± 4.9 years, and that of females was 11.3 ± 6.3 years. No statistically significant distinction was found in the age distribution between male and female macaques (P = 0.250).

Profile of serum SCFA concentrations in rhesus monkeys

Concentration data for butyric acid and isovaleric acid were excluded because they were below the detection limit. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the total ion chromatogram for targeted metabolomics. Precision and accuracy data for SCFA assays are presented in Supplementary Table 3. The mean concentrations of isobutyric acid, acetic acid, valeric acid, and propionic acid across all samples were 294.012 ± 19.375 ng/mL, 240.354 ± 8.336 ng/mL, 1152.832 ± 741.063 ng/mL, and 282.301 ± 36.676 ng/mL, respectively.

Age-related fluctuations in serum SCFAs

Targeted metabolomics revealed age-related fluctuations in SCFAs. Isobutyric acid displayed a positive correlation with age (r = 0.227, P = 0.035). In contrast, valeric acid, acetic acid, and propionic acid declined with increasing age, with acetic acid showing the most significant reduction (r = − 0.236, P = 0.028) (Fig. 2A). Notable differences across groups were detected in the levels of valeric acid (P = 0.037), acetic acid (P = 0.030), and propionic acid (P = 0.012) (Fig. 2B).

Age-related differences in SCFAs were sex-specific

Age-related differences in SCFAs were identified following initial analysis. Subsequent stratification by sex revealed distinct patterns. In male monkeys, a notably positive correlation was found between isobutyric acid levels and age (r = 0.440, P = 0.008) (Fig. 3A). Compared with the young group (P = 0.011) and the adult group (P = 0.023), the old group exhibited markedly higher isobutyric acid levels (Fig. 3B). These findings were consistent with trends observed in the overall sample (P = 0.009). In contrast, no age-related variation in isobutyric acid was observed in females. Instead, valeric acid (r = − 0.390, P = 0.005), acetic acid (r = − 0.362, P = 0.010), and propionic acid (r = − 0.377, P = 0.007) displayed significant negative correlations with age (Fig. 4A). Correspondingly, concentrations of valeric acid (P = 0.029), acetic acid (P = 0.025), and propionic acid (P = 0.012) were highest in females of the young group (Fig. 4B).

Age-related and sex-related differences in reproductive and stress-related hormones

Sex differences in SCFAs during aging prompted further investigation of reproductive and stress-related hormones in a subset of samples (19 males and 19 females). In the entire samples, no statistically significant variations in hormone levels were detected across the three age groups(Fig. 5A). However, among males, the old group showed significantly elevated ACTH levels (P = 0.022). In contrast, LH concentrations in females declined significantly with age (P = 0.003) (Fig. 5B-C).

Correlation between hormones and SCFAs in aging

Correlations between SCFAs, hormones, and age were further evaluated (Fig. 6). No significant association was detected between isobutyric acid and hormones in either the overall or sex-stratified analyses. In males, stronger positive correlations were observed between SCFAs and hormones, particularly for valeric acid. By contrast, female monkeys displayed more pronounced negative correlations between hormones and age. These findings suggest that males and females follow distinct aging trajectories, with both SCFAs and hormones potentially contributing to sex-specific differences in aging.

Discussion

This study identified significant age-related alterations in serum SCFAs in rhesus monkeys. Overall, isobutyric acid levels increased with age, whereas acetic acid, propionic acid, and valeric acid levels declined. Sex-specific differences were evident, with males and females exhibiting distinct patterns. Furthermore, correlations between hormones and SCFAs suggest potential interactions contributing to sex-dependent trajectories of aging.

As a primary energy source, reduced acetic acid levels may impair normal physiological functions [25]. In addition to energy metabolism, acetic acid plays an important role in astrocyte-mediated immune functions within the brain [11]. Astrocytes are essential hubs for neurotransmission, contributing to the metabolism of glutamate and γ-aminobutyric acid and facilitating synaptic signaling [26, 27]. A decline in serum acetic acid may therefore contribute to the age-related deterioration of brain function.

Alzheimer’s disease research has long focused on preventing amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation and removing deposited plaques [28]. It has been documented that valeric acid and propionic acid can suppress the misfolding of Aβ40 polypeptides into neurotoxic aggregates, and this inhibitory effect follows a dose-dependent pattern [29]. In the present study, serum propionic acid levels declined with age, consistent with mechanisms implicated in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis. These findings suggest that peripheral SCFAs may influence the development of central nervous system lesions during aging. It is therefore hypothesized that maintaining optimal peripheral levels of acetic acid and propionic acid is essential for preserving brain immune function and sustaining a healthy intracranial environment. This hypothesis warrants further investigation to elucidate the mechanistic role of SCFAs in age-related neurological decline. Having established the potential link between fluctuations in SCFAs and aging, this study further examined whether these changes exhibit sex-specific patterns. In females, age-related decreases were observed in acetic acid, propionic acid, and valeric acid, whereas in males, only isobutyric acid demonstrated an age-related change. Different SCFA fluctuations likely exert distinct health impacts. For example, studies have reported that during the aging process, women’s gut microbiota shows decreased butyrate production capacity, leading to weakened histone deacetylase inhibition and exacerbating chronic inflammation [30].In male populations, elevated isobutyrate levels in elderly men are significantly associated with benign prostatic hyperplasia [31]. Moreover, isobutyric acid has the potential to increase glucose uptake and improve insulin sensitivity in patients with metabolic disorders [32]. These findings suggest that during aging, SCFAs may influence physiological processes not only indirectly, via modulation of sex steroid hormones through cholesterol metabolism [10], but also directly, through epigenetic regulation by histone deacetylase inhibition [32].

Mechanisms underlying SCFA fluctuations and their potential impacts require careful consideration. Previous research has shown that gut SCFA concentrations fluctuate with alterations in gut microbiota [17]. In elderly Chinese populations, marked changes in both gut microbiota composition and function have been observed with aging [33]. Given the strong influence of dietary diversity on gut microbiota, Chen et al. investigated the gut microbiome and metabolome of captive male rhesus monkeys across different age stages and reported significant age-related changes. In the α-diversity analysis, significant differences were observed among different age-groups [34]. Similarly, Wang et al. employed a multi-omics approach to explore intestinal aging in Macaca fascicularis and identified distinct sex-specific aging patterns [35]. These findings indicate that differences in gut microbiota composition and function between males and females may underlie the observed SCFA fluctuations during aging. Specific microbial taxa at the species or genus level may differentially influence SCFA metabolism. To date, no studies have specifically addressed this issue in relation to aging, highlighting an important avenue for future research.

Minor variations in reproductive and stress-related hormone concentrations were detected among age groups, both in the overall sample and in sex-stratified subgroups. However, correlation analyses suggested sex-dependent differences, with female hormone changes showing stronger associations with acetic acid and propionic acid. This aligns with previous findings: He et al. reported that acetic acid improved ovarian function in rat models of polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) [36], while other studies demonstrated a reduction in the LH/FSH ratio following acetic acid supplementation in PCOS rats [37]. Comparable outcomes were observed with flaxseed oil supplementation, which altered gut microbiota composition, increased plasma acetic acid levels, and ameliorated PCOS symptoms [32]. Additional studies have further confirmed strong associations among sex steroid hormones, inflammation, and gut or vaginal microbiota [38, 39]. Together with the current findings, these data suggest that SCFAs may influence female aging by modulating hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis function. The differences in SCFA-hormone correlations between males and females further support the notion that sex-specific patterns of aging exist, warranting deeper exploration of the role of SCFAs in these processes.

Strengths and limitations

This study employed cage-housed rhesus monkeys, thereby eliminating potential confounding factors such as dietary variability and environmental heterogeneity. This allowed for more precise investigation of age-related physiological and pathological changes. In addition, the study not only identified age-related alterations in serum SCFAs but also analyzed their associations with sex, reproductive hormones, and stress-related hormones, offering multidimensional insights into aging mechanisms.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size for hormone testing in some subgroups was relatively small. Second, the cohort did not include infants Younger than two years, Limiting coverage of the early Life stage. Third, biochemical parameters, particularly lipid profiles, were not assessed. Finally, although SCFAs are products of gut microbial metabolism, 16 S rRNA sequencing was not performed, preventing integration of microbiome data with SCFA dynamics.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that serum SCFAs in rhesus monkeys undergo significant age-related changes, with distinct sex-specific patterns. In females, age-related declines in acetate may contribute to deteriorating brain function with aging. Clinically, this finding supports the development of sex-specific dietary interventions or early monitoring of serum acetate levels as a strategy to preserve brain function. In males, the age-related increase in isobutyrate aligns with previous associations between isobutyrate and benign prostatic hyperplasia, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for assessing risk in elderly men. Furthermore, observed correlations between SCFAs and hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis hormones indicate that SCFAs may serve as mediators linking gut health to endocrine function during the aging process.
