Pre-Travel Consultation for the Prevention of Infectious Diseases among International Travelers from GCC Countries: Travel Medicine Recommendations for Clinicians
Authors: Mazin Barry, Jaffar A. Al-Tawfiq, Fayssal Farahat, Mohammed Alzunitan, Sultan Almaziad, Majid Alshamrani, Ziad A. Memish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Travel has been increasing globally since the COVID-19 pandemic ended, including travel from the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries to international destinations. This creates unique risks for travelers, who may return with various infectious diseases. Travel medicine is a well-established medical specialty worldwide; however, it has only recently been introduced within the GCC, with limited healthcare and public awareness of the importance of these services in preventing infectious diseases among international travelers. This document presents travel medicine recommendations for practicing clinicians in the GCC to help prevent these diseases through pre-travel consultation.

INTRODUCTION

Travelers are at an increased risk of exposure to infectious diseases outside their home country, which they might bring back with them. Recent examples of widespread outbreaks in diseases due to travel include COVID-19, Mpox, and Zika. Travel and tourism are aligned with the United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which aim to achieve various objectives by 2030, including economic growth, environmental sustainability, and social equality.[1] International travel has been expanding globally since the COVID-19 pandemic ended. The United Nations World Tourism Organization (UNWTO) reported 1,455.07 billion international tourist arrivals and an estimated international tourism expenditure of USD 27.69 billion in 2024.[2]

International travel poses a risk of acquiring infectious diseases that may not be epidemic in the traveler’s country of origin, and travelers often remain unaware of these risks. Several travel-related risk factors may contribute to this, including the traveler’s underlying health condition, whether their vaccines are up to date, the duration and season of travel, the purpose of travel, the mode of transportation, the traveler’s behavior, and the destination.[3] Fever in a returning traveler is a common presentation to healthcare providers and can be challenging to diagnose for unsuspecting clinicians. A wide variety of infectious diseases can be acquired during travel, including malaria, typhoid and paratyphoid fever, arboviruses such as dengue and Zika, leptospirosis, rickettsial diseases, and trypanosomiasis, as well as common viral respiratory illnesses and infectious diarrhea.

Networks that provide epidemiological data on international travelers, such as the GeoSentinel Global Surveillance Network and Global TravEpiNet, help collect and analyze data on travel-related illnesses, identifying risks of acquiring such conditions. In a large GeoSentinel network analysis of 42,173 ill returning travelers from 53 clinics worldwide, gastroenteritis was the most common presentation (34%). Overall, 23.3% of travelers presented with fever, of which 29% was attributed to malaria and 15% to dengue. At the same time, no specific cause was found in 40%.[4] A retrospective analysis of 53,432 ill returning travelers to the UK found that the most prevalent five pathogens were dengue, chikungunya, Zika, leptospirosis, and spotted fever group Rickettsia.[5] Another study evaluated the use of the Infection Tracking in Travelers mobile application, which collects daily self-reported travel-related illness symptoms along with geolocation and weather data of 470 tracked trips, 35% included symptom reports, with gastrointestinal and respiratory symptoms being the most common (19% and 17%, respectively). A longer travel duration, the winter season, and high humidity were significantly associated with symptom occurrence. The symptoms most disruptive to daily activities were diarrhea, headache, and nausea.[6]

The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries recorded a 10.2% increase in international travelers in 2024 compared to 2023, while the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) recorded an 83% increase in 2025 compared to 2019.[2] The Saudi Ministry of Health (MoH) publishes annual requirements and recommendations for travelers to KSA for the Hajj pilgrimage.[7] Various health risks for tourists traveling to KSA have also been addressed.[8] However, there are scarce travel medicine care facilities in the KSA that are affiliated with the International Society of Travel Medicine (ISTM), and there has been a call for travel medicine specialists and training within the KSA to address these deficiencies, especially with the expansion of tourism and the anticipated hosting of the FIFA World Cup in 2034.[9] Additionally, data on infectious diseases acquired by returning travelers to the GCC are scarce.[10]

Guideline development and objective

The current document was developed by a group of experts in travel medicine and provides the first Travel Medicine Specialized Professional Program (SPP) in the GCC, as part of an SPP by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties, valid for 2 years for travel medicine practitioners. The group of experts includes the authors MB, FF, MALZ, SA, and MALS. The recommendations were then reviewed by two independent expert reviewers, JT and ZM. The recommendations were developed, in part, in accordance with the Reporting Items for Practice Guidelines in Healthcare.[11] Evidence was identified through a formal literature review and expert consensus; recommendations were agreed upon in consensus meetings and presented as good practice points.

The aim is to address current deficiencies by providing recommendations for travelers from the GCC, including KSA, to help clinicians offer local guidance to travel medicine practitioners and provide pre-travel consultation to prevent infectious diseases. The vaccines recommended in this document are widely available at ISTM-registered and MoH-associated travel clinics, with a focus on diseases for which vaccines are available, those with regional relevance, or those with travel-associated risk.

VACCINE-PREVENTABLE DISEASES

Vaccine-preventable diseases are infectious illnesses for which effective vaccines exist, offering travelers protection against serious infections endemic to destination regions and forming a cornerstone of pre-travel health interventions.

Hepatitis A and hepatitis B

Hepatitis A virus (HAV) is a single-stranded RNA Picornaviridae of the Hepatovirus genus. Globally, the number of hepatitis A incident cases increased by 13.90% from 139.54 million in 1990 to 158.94 million in 2019.[13] It is transmitted by the fecal–oral route and is associated with poor hygiene and sanitation. In children aged <6 years, 70% of infections are asymptomatic and often serve as sources of infection for adults, especially because viral shedding begins 2 weeks before and continues for 1 week after symptom onset, if any. The disease presents in older children and adults with jaundice in 80% and is usually self-limited. However, HAV is the major cause of fulminant hepatitis and has been reported to account for 10% of liver transplants in children in France and 20% in Argentina.[13] In fact, hepatitis A is responsible for 10%–20% of fulminant hepatic failure globally because it is more prevalent than other types of hepatitis; however, only a small proportion (0.1%–0.5%) of patients with hepatitis A develop fulminant hepatic failure.[14]

Vaccination against HAV is recommended for all persons aged ≥6 months who are traveling to developing countries and areas of intermediate or high risk for HAV transmission. This includes all countries except the U.S. and Canada, as well as Western Europe, Scandinavia, Japan, Australia, and New Zealand. Serological testing for HAV is not routinely recommended in the GCC, and vaccination is considered safe even in previously infected or immunized individuals.

The vaccines are highly immunogenic, with 100% seroconversion 1 month after the second dose.[15] Seroprotection may last for 20 years in 97% of recipients and up to 40 years in 90% of vaccinees.[16] The vaccine is available in combination with hepatitis B; however, for travelers presenting <21 days before departure, the monovalent vaccine is preferred and can be administered up to 1 day before travel, with the series completed upon return [Table 1]. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a significant global health issue, especially in Asia and Africa. Transmitted via blood/body fluids, HBV is prevented by the HBV vaccine, which is recommended for travelers aged 19–59 who lack prior vaccination or immunity, and for older adults with risk factors. Standard risk avoidance (such as no needle sharing) should also be applied.

Typhoid fever

Typhoid fever is an acute, life-threatening illness caused by Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, Salmonella enterica serovar Paratyphi A, B, and C. Infection is acquired through fecal contamination of food and water. Worldwide, 22,000,000 cases occur a year, with 200,000 deaths.[17] It is endemic in the Indian subcontinent, with an incidence of 493 per 100,000 person-years.[18] Fluoroquinolone resistance is as high as 90% in Pakistan, which directly affects the GCC due to the large number of visiting travelers to and from the region. In addition to the emergence of cephalosporin-resistant strains in India, extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains that are resistant to ceftriaxone, ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) have been reported in returning travelers from Pakistan and Iraq.[19]

Vaccination is recommended for travelers to Sub-Saharan Africa, Asia (especially South Asia), the Pacific, and Latin America. Vaccine is particularly recommended for travelers visiting friends and relatives, longer-stay travelers, adventurous eaters, and travelers to mass gatherings. Both the length of stay and the number of dietary indiscretions increase risk and should be considered when counseling travelers on vaccination. Influenza

Seasonal influenza poses year-round risk, especially during winter and mass gatherings. Annual vaccination is recommended, particularly for high-risk groups. Travelers should receive the appropriate vaccine for the destination’s hemisphere and season, supplemented by personal hygiene and respiratory etiquette.

Coronavirus disease 2019

COVID-19 remains a global concern. Travelers should be fully vaccinated and consider updated boosters as recommended. Masking, distancing, and hand hygiene remain important in high-risk settings.

Poliomyelitis

Although eradicated from most of the world, wild and vaccine-derived polio still circulate in a few countries (notably Afghanistan and Pakistan), posing a risk for unvaccinated travelers.[1819] KSA travelers visiting these regions should confirm their polio immunization status. Adults who are unvaccinated should complete a three-dose course (IPV or OPV, per guidelines).

Tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis

Tetanus and diphtheria vaccines (Td or Tdap) should be up to date for all travelers, especially those at risk for wounds or exposure to healthcare environments. Tdap during pregnancy is strongly advised (27–36 weeks of gestation) to protect newborns. Standard wound care remains essential.

Meningococcal disease

Meningococcal disease is a major concern for pilgrims attending Hajj or Umrah in Mecca due to the risk of outbreaks in large crowds. To protect travelers, all pilgrims must receive a quadrivalent MenACWY vaccine (covering serogroups A, C, W, and Y) at least 10 days before arriving in Saudi Arabia. This mandatory vaccination policy has played a significant role in reducing meningococcal outbreaks associated with pilgrimages and mass gatherings. Additionally, people traveling to other high-risk areas or with certain risk factors should also consider receiving the vaccine.

FOOD AND WATERBORNE DISEASES

Food and waterborne diseases are infections caused by consuming contaminated food or drinking unsafe water, posing significant risks to travelers, especially in areas with poor sanitation, and include illnesses such as typhoid fever, traveler’s diarrhea, cholera, and hepatitis A.

Traveler’s diarrhea

One of the most common infectious diseases encountered during travel is gastroenteritis (GE), presenting as traveler’s diarrhea (TD), with an incidence of 10%-40% on a 2-week trip.[20] Acute GE is most often due to enteroaggregative (EAEC) or enterotoxigenic (ETEC) E. coli (60%), as well as Salmonella sp., Shigella sp., and Campylobacter sp. Freshwater exposure and consumption of raw seafood may cause GE by Aeromonas hydrophila and Plesiomonas shigelloides.[21] Other causes include norovirus and amebiasis. TD can be mild (i.e., tolerable, without interference with daily activities), moderate (i.e., distressing, interfering with activities), or severe (i.e., (incapacitating or dysentery). Chronic and/or persistent diarrhea is an illness that lasts for >14 days and is typically caused by Giardia sp., Cyclospora sp., and Cryptosporidium sp.[2223]

TD prevention includes non-pharmacologic prevention, e.g. food and water hygiene, proper hand hygiene, and clear risk communication, as well as pharmacologic prophylaxis with bismuth subsalicylate (BSS), which can be effective in preventing TD.[24] It is widely available in the GCC and is found over the counter in KSA. BSS is contraindicated for individuals allergic to salicylates or aspirin, those with active bleeding ulcers, with bloody or black stools, or bleeding disorders, including the use of anticoagulants, children due to risk of Reye’s syndrome, and in pregnancy in the late third trimester. Antimicrobial prophylaxis is not routinely recommended unless the traveler is a high-risk host with significant immunosuppression, advanced chronic kidney disease, decompensated liver cirrhosis, diabetes, congestive heart failure, or inflammatory bowel disease.

Self-treatment algorithms can be provided to the traveler. For mild TD, BSS or loperamide is usually effective. For moderate-to-severe TD, rifaximin, azithromycin, or a fluoroquinolone can be used. Rifaximin is a non-absorbable antibiotic of the rifamycin class that is effective in treating EAEC and ETEC, which cause TD, at a dose of 200 mg po q8h for 3 days. However, it is not effective against other pathogens including Shigella, Salmonella, and Campylobacter sp.[252627] Hence, it should only be given for non-dysenteric TD and certain destinations. Rifamycin SV is also indicated for TD due to non-invasive strains of E. coli in adults; however, it is currently not available in the GCC. Azithromycin can be given empirically as a first-line treatment for TD in Southeast Asia and other regions when fluoroquinolone-resistant bacteria are suspected, and it is the preferred treatment for dysentery or febrile diarrhea. Azithromycin 1000 mg orally once or 500 mg q24h for 3 days, with loperamide 4 mg once, then 2 mg after each loose stool, can be recommended.[28] However, loperamide should be used with caution, as it is contraindicated in acute dysentery or bacterial enterocolitis caused by invasive organisms (Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter) and in pseudomembranous colitis. Ciprofloxacin 750 mg, taken orally once, if symptoms are not resolved after 24 hours 500mg po q12h for 3 days should be initiated.[29] Levofloxacin 500 mg PO OD for 1–3 days can also be recommended. However, depending on the destination, particularly Southeast Asia, the rate of fluoroquinolone-resistant Campylobacter, Salmonella, and Shigella can be high.[30] In addition, fluoroquinolones have multiple concerns, including serious adverse effects such as aortic tears, hypoglycemia, QT interval prolongation, tendinitis, and tendon rupture; they also carry a high risk of Clostridioides difficile infection, which limits their use. Entamoeba histolytica is a relatively uncommon cause of TD; however, it can cause a variety of clinical disease states, including asymptomatic cyst passers that do not invade or cause symptoms such as diarrhea or mild to moderate dysentery. Severe complications include invasive intestinal amebiasis presenting with dysentery, colitis, appendicitis, toxic megacolon, amebomas, and invasive extraintestinal amebiasis presenting with liver abscess, peritonitis, pleuropulmonary abscess, cutaneous and genital amebic lesions.[31] Treatment is with metronidazole 500–750 mg po or IV q8h for 7 to 10 days, depending on severity. This is followed by cyst carriage treatment with paromomycin or iodoquinol; neither agent is available in the GCC. Alternatively, diloxanide furoate 500 mg po q8h for 10 days can be prescribed, and is available in the GCC.

Cholera

Cholera is endemic in 47 countries and infects 3-5 million people worldwide annually, with 21,000-143,000 deaths a year.[32] It is caused by Vibrio cholerae toxigenic strains serogroup 01 (biotypes Classical and El Tor) and 0139.[33] Seven cholera pandemics have occurred, with the most recent ongoing since 1961. In 2018-2019, a highly drug-resistant strain of the seventh pandemic Vibrio cholerae O1 El Tor (7PET), belonging to the AFR13 sublineage, emerged in Zimbabwe.[34] In 2019, the strain was also detected in Yemen and became more resistant after acquiring an incompatibility group C (IncC) plasmid pCNRVC190243.[35] In 2022, it was detected in Lebanon.[36] By 2023, it spread to eastern Africa, with reported cases in Europe from returning travelers.[37]

Cholera vaccines are recommended for humanitarian aid and refugee workers, including healthcare providers, when traveling to designated areas within countries with active cholera transmission or a humanitarian crisis. Vaccines are not recommended for individuals traveling to areas with stable, ongoing transmission, such as India, Bangladesh, and Indonesia.

The most widely available vaccine is Dukoral, by Valneva, an inactivated, oral, whole-cell, killed vaccine with the recombinant B subunit of cholera toxin that protects against V. cholerae 01 only and is administered in 2 doses, 1–6 weeks apart. The seroprotection rate is 78% at 1 year[38] and 49% at 5 years.[39] Vaxchora, by Emergent Biosolutions, is an oral live-attenuated vaccine containing the V. cholerae CVD 103-HgR strain, which protects against V. cholerae 01 only, and is given as a single dose of 100 mL at least 10 days before the risk of exposure. It provides a seroprotection of 90% by day 10, which decreases to 80% by day 90.[40] However, this vaccine is not widely available. ShanChol (Shantha Biotec) and Euvichol (EuBiologics) are bivalent O1 and O139 oral killed cholera vaccines, with 70% protection for 3 years after 2 doses,[4142] but are also not widely available.

VECTOR-BORNE DISEASES

Vector-borne diseases are infections transmitted to humans through the bite of infected arthropods such as mosquitoes, ticks, and fleas, and include illnesses such as malaria, dengue, chikungunya, yellow fever, and Japanese encephalitis, which are major causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, especially in tropical and subtropical regions. One of the primary methods of protection against vector-borne diseases is the use of personal protective measures. Clinicians should advise their travelers to wear long-sleeved shirts and long trousers, and to apply insect repellent containing 50% diethyltoluamide (DEET) or 20% picaridin to exposed skin. To spray aerosolized insecticides, such as those containing pyrethrins, in living and sleeping areas, especially at dusk, and to sleep in a screened or air-conditioned room, in addition to the use of bed-nets of good quality with small mesh that is not damaged and impregnated with permethrin, and to pretreat clothing with permethrin.[43]

Malaria

Malaria is endemic in 83 countries. In 2023, it caused 263 million cases worldwide, with 597,000 deaths.[44] Around 15,000 cases are imported by travel, though this is probably under-reported, and causes almost 60 deaths a year in travelers, mostly from sub-Saharan Africa. The disease mainly affects non-immune or semi-immune travelers who have lost or partially lost their immunity during stays of ≥6 months in countries without malaria. These travelers are at particular risk when traveling to malarious areas to visit friends and relatives.[45] Most cases of Plasmodium falciparum malaria in travelers occur because of poor adherence and/or inappropriate prophylactic malaria drug regimens, and failure to take adequate precautions against mosquito bites. Adherence to the latter and chemoprophylaxis can be improved if travelers are informed of the risk of infection and believe in the benefit of prevention strategies.[46] However, late-onset Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale malaria may occur despite effective prophylaxis. Chloroquine-sensitive malaria includes P. falciparum (in Central America and the Caribbean), P. vivax (in regions other than Papua New Guinea and Indonesia), P. ovale, Plasmodium malariae, and Plasmodium knowlesi. Chloroquine phosphate 500 mg or 300 mg base weekly at a similar timing to mefloquine can be used. Atovaquone/proguanil can be used as an alternative, as outlined in Table 4.

Self-initiated treatment is an option only if malaria is laboratory diagnosed while overseas, and reliable medical attention is not feasible. This is an option for those at high risk who refuse to use or are on sub-optimal prophylaxis. The traveler’s ability to self-diagnose using rapid diagnostic testing may also be considered. Options for self-initiated treatment include artemether-lumefantrine (20/120 mg tablets): 4 tablets (80 mg/480 mg) as a single dose, then 4 tablets again after 8 hours, then 4 tablets every 12 hours for 2 days. However, artemisinin resistance due to the Pfkelch13 variant is present in the Greater Mekong subregion of Asia and East Africa, including Eritrea, Rwanda, and Uganda.[47] Another option is atovaquone/proguanil 4 tablets (1000/400 mg) daily for 3 days; however, this should not be used if on atovaquone/proguanil prophylaxis.

There are two currently available malaria vaccines. The first is RTS, S/AS01 (trade-named Mosquirix), which is engineered from T-cell epitope in the pre-erythrocytic circumsporozoite protein of P. falciparum malaria parasite and the envelope protein of HBsAg with an adjuvant system (AS01). It has an efficacy of 25%–50% in infants and young children, reducing hospital admissions from severe malaria by 30% and toddler deaths by 15%. The WHO recommended it in October 2021 for children aged 5 to 17 months living in areas with moderate-to-high malaria transmission; it is not recommended for adults or travelers.[48] The second vaccine is R21/Matrix-M, which utilizes the same circumsporozoite protein antigen in a higher proportion and the same HBsAg-linked recombinant structure, without excess HBsAg. It has an efficacy of 78%, which fulfils the WHO target of at least 75%.[49] It was endorsed by the WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (SAGE) in October 2023 for children aged 5–36 months in endemic countries.[50] Similar to Mosquirix, it is not recommended for adults or travelers.

Dengue

Dengue virus (DENV) is a single-stranded RNA flavivirus with four serotypes: DENV1, DENV2, DENV3, and DENV4. Severe dengue disease, in the form of dengue hemorrhagic fever, with or without dengue shock syndrome, can occur following reinfection with a different serotype, thereby increasing the risk of severe complications due to antibody-dependent enhancement. Worldwide, 3.9 billion people are at risk of dengue virus infection, resulting in 390 million cases annually, with 96 million requiring medical attention. The case fatality ratio ranges from 2.5% to 5.9%.[51] It occurs in all tropical and subtropical countries and is common among travelers[52] [Figure 2]. It has become the most common cause of fever in returning travelers from all countries outside Africa.[53] The incidence of symptomatic dengue is approximately 6 cases per 1,000 unvaccinated travelers to endemic areas each month,[54] with 1.3% of these cases developing severe dengue.[55]

CYD-TDV (Dengvaxia®, Sanofi Pasteur) is a live attenuated tetravalent (DENV1-4) vaccine with yellow fever virus backbone. It has little or no effect against DENV-2, with an efficacy of 60%. It is indicated for persons aged 9–16 years, administered in 3 doses of 0.5 mL SC at 0, 6, and 12 months. It is only recommended for people with prior dengue infection in endemic countries and requires lab confirmation of prior infection due to possible antibody-dependent enhancement.[56] This vaccine is rarely used due to the need for pre-screening for dengue antibody, and it is not recommended for travelers; however, it is currently the only available vaccine in KSA.

TAK-003 (Qdenga®, Takeda Pharmaceuticals) is a recombinant chimeric attenuated vaccine with DENV1, DENV3, and DENV4 components on a DENV2 backbone. The predominant effect is against DENV-2; the efficacy against DENV-3 and DENV-4 remains unclear. SAGE recommended vaccination of children in endemic areas in October 2023. It is available in Europe, the UK, Brazil, Argentina, Indonesia, and Thailand, but not currently available in the USA, Canada, or KSA. It is administered subcutaneously in 2 doses 3 months apart. Protection starts 14 days after the first dose. Currently, no data is available on boosters. Reducing the dose interval should be avoided, and a delayed second dose should be administered at the first available opportunity. The vaccine exhibits good immunogenicity, with 62% efficacy against dengue infection and 83.6% efficacy against hospitalization after 3 years of vaccination.[57]

Qdenga® for travelers should be used with caution due to limited data in dengue-naïve adults; however, it can be administered. Proposed recommendations are for travelers with a previously known dengue infection; vaccination can be recommended before travel to an endemic country. For dengue-naïve travelers, vaccination may be considered for individuals aged 4 to 16 years, irrespective of travel duration. It may also be considered for travelers aged 17 to 60 years, but only for long trips related to the travel destination. Qdenga® should be avoided in individuals aged ≥60.[58] The WHO recommendations for travelers suggest that the greatest benefit occurs during an ongoing DENV2 or DENV1 epidemic at the destination. Previously infected seropositive travelers with any serotype may benefit from Qdenga® vaccination. Pre-vaccination serostatus screening is not required, but it may be considered to help assess the risks and benefits. The benefits of vaccination are lower for seronegative travelers. The protection afforded to seronegative individuals against DENV3 and DENV4 is uncertain, and circulating serotypes at any destination may change over time, underscoring the importance of matching vaccination strategies to circulating serotypes and travel destination epidemiology.

Tick-borne encephalitis

Tick-borne encephalitis (TBE) is caused by the TBE-virus, a positive-sense single-stranded RNA flavivirus, transmitted by woodland ticks such as Ixodes scapularis, Ixodes ricinus, and Ixodes persulcatus, which are abundant in woodland environments. It exhibits peri-urban transmission and is rarely spread in urban parks.[59] Additionally, it is less frequently transmitted through unpasteurized goat milk.[60] TBE is endemic across Western and Northern Europe, extending to Northern and Eastern Asia. It is highly prevalent in Baltic countries, the Czech Republic, Slovenia, southern Germany, Switzerland, and Austria. Recently, it has been emerging at low levels in England, the Netherlands, and Belgium. There are three distinct strains: European, Siberian, and Far Eastern. It follows a strict seasonal pattern from April to November, which is important for assessing travel risk.[61]

Vaccination is recommended for travelers to endemic areas with significant exposure to ticks based on planned outdoor activities and itinerary. Significant exposure can be determined by travel duration and exposure frequency, including short-stay travelers with <1 month of travel and daily or frequent exposure, as well as long-stay travelers with regular exposure to potential tick-infested environments. It may be considered for individuals who might engage in outdoor activities in areas likely to be tick-infested. The decision should be based on clinicians’ risk assessment of the itinerary and activity types, as well as risk factors for poor outcomes, including those aged >60 years, and the individual’s personal perception and tolerance of risk. High-risk activities include hiking, camping, cycling, hunting, fishing, birdwatching, or collecting berries and mushrooms. High-risk itineraries include visiting forested woodland areas, rural areas, during specific seasons, and for extended travel durations.[62] Tick checks are ineffective at preventing TBE, as the virus is transmitted within minutes of the tick’s attachment.

The vaccines are TicoVac®, FSME-Immun® (Pfizer), and Encepur Adults® (Bavarian Nordic/Valneva). They are whole-cell, inactivated virus vaccines containing the Neudoerfl strain, a Western European TBE virus subtype. However, they also exhibit strong reactivity against eastern strains. They are administered in 3 doses of 0.5mL IM at 0 days, 14–90 days, and 5–12 months after the second dose. The efficacy is 83%–99% after the second dose for 12 months in travelers who are unable to complete the full 3-dose series. Boosters are required every 3–5 years for frequent travelers.[63]

Yellow fever

Yellow fever is endemic in sub-Saharan Africa and Amazonian South America, with the highest prevalence in Brazil. It is a result of the increased urbanization of the susceptible population. It causes around 200,000 cases of severe illness and 50,000 deaths each year. The actual number could be up to 250 times higher,[64] and the case fatality rate for hospitalized cases is as high as 40%.[65] The disease is caused by the Yellow fever virus, a single-stranded RNA flavivirus. Disease transmission occurs from primates or humans. The main mosquito vector is Aedes aegypti. The risk is highest during the rainy season, so it is important to advise travelers to avoid mosquito bites and use mosquito preventive measures.

Vaccination [Table 5] is required for entry to several African countries, including Angola, Benin, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Democratic Republic of Congo, Côte d’Ivoire, Gabon, Ghana, Liberia, Mali, Niger, Rwanda, São Tomé and Príncipe, Sierra Leone, and Togo. In South America, it is required only for entry into French Guiana [Figure 3]. Proof of vaccination with an International Certificate of Vaccination must be obtained from the travel clinic and signed by a licensed physician. For travelers who are contraindicated from receiving the vaccine, a waiver form should be provided.

Japanese encephalitis

Japanese encephalitis (JE) is the most common cause of encephalitis in Southeast Asia and the Western Pacific and has recently emerged in Southeast Australia.[66] Approximately 4 billion people reside in regions where the disease is prevalent. Annually, there are about 68,000 symptomatic cases and around 17,000 deaths. Cases often appear in outbreaks, mainly affecting rural farming communities.[67]

The disease is caused by Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), an arbovirus, specifically a mosquito-borne flavivirus. It mainly infects children, although adults in endemic areas may have natural immunity. Nevertheless, individuals of any age can contract the infection. The virus is transmitted by Culex mosquitoes, and in tropical regions, transmission can occur year-round. The risk increases after it rains, as mosquito populations grow. Major outbreaks tend to happen every 2 to 15 years. The main Culex mosquito vectors are Culex tritaeniorhynchus, Culex gelidus, and Culex vishnui. They typically breed in flooded rice fields, drainage ditches, and other areas with standing water. Their biting pattern is biphasic, with peaks around 7 pm, a decline throughout the midnight hours, and another peak around 3 am. They readily feed on birds, swine, and humans.

Vaccination is advised for long-term travelers to endemic countries, those spending over a month in endemic areas during the JEV transmission season, frequent travelers to JE-endemic regions, visitors to active epidemic zones, short rural stays of less than a month during transmission season involving outdoor activities, and travelers with unclear plans in endemic areas. Vaccination is not recommended for short stays of less than a month travelers whose trip is limited to urban areas, day trips to typical tourist sites in rural areas, or times outside a clearly defined JEV transmission season.[68]

JE-VC is an inactivated Vero cell culture SA14-14-2 vaccine, which is administered in two 0.5 mL intramuscular doses on days 0 and either 7 (accelerated schedule) or 28 (standard schedule). Protective antibody levels reach about 100% within 7 days after the second dose. A single dose alone is not sufficient for protection. A booster dose is recommended 12 to 24 months after the initial vaccination, which provides 96% seroprotection. Mathematical modeling indicates that 75% of vaccinated individuals will stay protected for at least 10 years.[69]

Chikungunya

Chikungunya virus is a single-stranded RNA alphavirus that causes a self-limited illness characterized by fever, rash, and arthritis. However, arthritis may last for months or even years, leading to significant morbidity. It is an arbovirus transmitted by Aedes spp. mosquitoes. It has caused several epidemics on most continents, with explosive outbreaks in the Indian Ocean islands and the Caribbean.[70] Recently, multiple travel-related cases have been reported from Southeast Asia,[71] with an increased risk of infection transmission and further spread through international travelers.[72]

Two vaccines are licensed in North America and Europe. Ixchiq® is a live-attenuated virus vaccine given as a single intramuscular dose, providing nearly 99% seroprotection for 2 years. However, this vaccine poses a risk of inducing chikungunya-like illness in >11% of recipients.[73] Vimkunya® is a recombinant protein based on a virus-like particle, administered as a 0.8-mL single intramuscular dose, providing nearly 98% seroprotection and not causing chikungunya-like illness.[74] With recent safety concerns regarding Ixchiq in persons aged ≥60 years,[75] Vimkunya® would be a more favorable vaccine. Although neither vaccine is currently available in the GCC, they can be recommended to travelers going to regions with active chikungunya outbreaks or to long-term, frequent travelers to areas where chikungunya transmission occurred in the past 5 years.[76]

ZOONOTIC DISEASES

Zoonotic diseases are infections caused by pathogens such as viruses, bacteria, parasites, or fungi that are naturally transmitted between animals and humans, often through direct contact or vectors, and represent a significant proportion of emerging infectious diseases worldwide.

Rabies

Rabies is caused by Rabies lyssavirus, a neurotropic single-stranded RNA virus. Most human rabies cases are acquired from dogs, bats, monkeys, cattle, camels, wolves, cats, raccoons, and skunks. It is most commonly found in Asia, with 20,850 deaths per year in India and 6000 deaths per year in China.[77] Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is indicated for travelers with long stays of a month or more in high-risk destinations, especially those in rural areas, as well as for short-stay travelers to high-risk destinations and those with extensive outdoor exposure, whether occupational or adventurous, in high-risk destinations, even if the stay is short. PrEP does not eliminate the need for additional rabies vaccine doses after exposure, but it simplifies post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) and eliminates the need for rabies immune globulin (RIG). PrEP vaccination and PEP boosting have shown a 100% survival rate after a rabies exposure.[78]

There are two rabies vaccines, Rabavert, or Rabipur, by Bavarian Nordic, which are inactivated-virus purified chick embryo cell vaccines; however, this vaccine is not available in KSA. The second vaccine is available, which is Imovax Rabies, or Verorab, by Sanofi Pasteur, a rabies virus strain PM-1503-3M antigen, propiolactone inactivated virus, human diploid cell. It is given as two doses of 1 mL intramuscularly at 0 and 7 days. A booster dose is required for travelers with a frequent or sustained risk of exposure for >3 years after completing the primary series, or if their rabies antibody titer is <0.5 IU/mL.

Ebola

Ebolaviruses are single-stranded RNA filoviruses. Human diseases are caused by four strains: Bundibugyo ebolavirus, Sudan ebolavirus, Taï Forest ebolavirus, and Ebolavirus (formerly Zaire ebolavirus). Ebolavirus is the most serious, and their reservoirs are fruit bats. Infection often involves non-human primates, followed by sustained human-to-human transmission. It usually occurs in outbreaks in tropical regions of Sub-Saharan Africa. Thus far, 12 outbreaks have occurred; the most recent were in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda in 2022.[79] Ebola is highly contagious when it comes into contact with blood, body fluids, or contaminated personal protective equipment. Travel-related infections have been reported in many aid workers returning to the USA, the UK, and Spain.[80]

Ebola vaccines are indicated for active responders to Zaire ebolavirus outbreaks; they are not indicated for routine tourists or business travelers. They are reserved for outbreak response personnel and certain frontline workers in affected countries. Everbo (Merck) is a replication-competent, live recombinant virus vaccine with a vesicular stomatitis virus backbone with an inserted envelope glycoprotein of the Zaire ebolavirus (rVSVΔG-ZEBOV-GP), given as 1 mL intramuscularly within the deltoid region of the arm. It is only effective against the Zaire ebolavirus and not Sudan ebolavirus or other less common strains. During the 2016 Guinea outbreak, vaccine efficacy was 100% among all frontline workers who received the vaccine.[81] In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the efficacy rate was 99% among frontline workers after 30 days of vaccination.[82]

Zabdeno (Ad26.ZEBOV-GP), a replication-deficient adenovirus type 26 vector-based vaccine expressing Zaire ebolavirus glycoprotein, and Mvabea® (MVA-BN-Filo), a non-replicating, recombinant, modified vaccinia Ankara vector-based vaccine, encoding glycoproteins from Zaire ebolavirus, Sudan ebolavirus, and Marburg virus, and nucleoprotein from the Tai Forest virus. They are administered as a 2-dose prime-boost primary regimen: Zabdeno followed by Mvabea 8 weeks later, each at a dose of 0.5 mL IM. Zabdeno may be repeated once, 4 months after completion of the primary course, if the risk persists.[83] Common associated adverse effects include injection-site pain (70%), headache (37%), fever (34%), myalgia (33%), arthralgia (3-40%), and arthritis (0%–23%). Vaccine virus transmission may occur; recipients must be advised to avoid blood donation for at least 6 weeks; avoid sharing razors, utensils, and toothbrushes as well as intimate kissing for 2 weeks; to use effective barrier prophylaxis during intercourse for 2 months; and to avoid close association with high-risk and immunocompromised people for at least 6 weeks.[84]

OTHER RECOMMENDED VACCINES

All travelers should be fully vaccinated according to their age, underlying health conditions, and lifestyle factors. Vaccinations should be up to date, including HBV, seasonal influenza, COVID-19, poliomyelitis, tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis, measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella. Meningococcal disease is a concern for pilgrims traveling to Mecca for Hajj and Umrah due to large crowds and the risk of outbreaks.[858687] Therefore, pilgrims must receive the quadrivalent meningococcal vaccine (covering serogroups A, C, W, and Y) at least 10 days before arriving in KSA.[88] This has significantly mitigated outbreaks associated with pilgrimages. Travelers visiting other high-risk regions or those with additional risk factors should also be considered for vaccination. For older adults, especially those aged ≥50 years or with chronic medical conditions, pneumococcal vaccination, using the latest pneumococcal conjugate vaccines, such as PCV20 or PCV21, is strongly recommended to prevent serious pneumococcal disease. Additionally, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccines are recommended for adults aged ≥60 years, or for those with risk factors for severe RSV infection. Tailored vaccination ensures optimal protection for travelers against common and potentially severe infections encountered during travel.

GENERAL PREVENTION MEASURES AND OTHER TRAVEL HEALTH CONSIDERATIONS

Effective travel health strategies extend beyond vaccination to include comprehensive preventive measures tailored to individual risks and destinations. Travelers should receive personalized pre-travel consultations to assess their health status, immunization history, and itinerary, allowing for tailored advice on prophylactic medications, such as antimalarials, and on treatments for traveler’s diarrhea. Essential travel health kits, including prescription medications, insect repellents, and first aid supplies, should be prepared in advance. During travel, adherence to food and water safety precautions, use of protective clothing and vector repellents, and awareness of outbreak alerts are critical to minimizing infection risk. Post-travel monitoring for symptoms and timely medical consultation ensures early detection and management of travel-related illnesses.

CONCLUSION

Travel medicine requires an individualized approach that integrates destination-specific risks, traveler health status, and evolving epidemiologic trends. Vaccination remains a cornerstone of prevention, but comprehensive strategies encompassing self-care, environmental precautions, and timely medical consultation are equally vital. Tailoring advice to the traveler’s itinerary and health profile optimizes protection and reduces morbidity from travel-associated infections. Ongoing surveillance, education, and research will enhance global travel health outcomes, supporting safe and healthy journeys for diverse populations.
