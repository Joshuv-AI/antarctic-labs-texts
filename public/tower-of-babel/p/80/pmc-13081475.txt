Clinical efficacy and safety of recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide in the treatment of sepsis complicated by heart failure: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Wei Liu, Ruixiang Zeng, Zhilin Zhang, Xiangzhao Kong, Liheng Guo, Biao Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure is among the most prevalent organ dysfunctions in sepsis and carries a high mortality rate. Currently, no specific drugs are available. Recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide (rhBNP) has demonstrated efficacy in treating isolated heart failure. This study aims to evaluate the feasibility of using rhBNP in the management of sepsis complicated by heart failure (SCHF).MethodsEight databases were searched for qualified randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from inception to 19 July 2025, with no language restrictions. The primary outcomes included 28-day mortality and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). The secondary outcomes included total clinical effective rate (TCER), APACHE II scores, cardiac output (CO), and adverse events (AEs). The meta-analyses were conducted by R (version 4.5.0). We utilized the Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) software to perform TSA. Subsequently, the quality of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE framework (the Grading of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation system). This study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD 42023491344).ResultsTen Chinese RCTs involving 1,182 patients were included. Pooled analysis showed that rhBNP combined with conventional Western therapy (CWT) was associated with reduced 28-day mortality (RR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.58–0.92, p = 0.006) and APACHE II scores (MD = -4.18, 95% CI: -7.80 to -0.55, p = 0.024), and improved TCER (RR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.12–1.41, p < 0.0001), LVEF (MD = 4.91, 95% CI: 2.56–7.26, p < 0.0001), and CO (MD = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.21–0.74, p = 0.0005). However, rhBNP did not significantly reduce AEs (RR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.54–1.08, p = 0.13). The certainty of evidence was graded as low for CO and AEs, and very low for 28‑day mortality, LVEF, TCER, and APACHE II scores.ConclusionsAdjunctive rhBNP may offer cardiac and survival benefits in sepsis complicated by heart failure, but the evidence is of low certainty, non‑generalizable, and safety concerns persist. These hypothesis‑generating findings require confirmation by rigorously designed, multinational RCTs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05677-0.Keywords: Sepsis complicated by heart failure, Recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide, Meta-analysis, Systematic review, Trial sequential analysis

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Heart failure is a major component of sepsis-induced multiple organ failure, accounting for 18–65% of septic complications, with global mortality rates as high as 36–55% [2, 3]. Sepsis complicated by heart failure (SCHF) is diagnosed within the first three days in only 40–60% of cases, primarily due to the lack of a consensus definition [4, 5]. Currently, inotropic agents such as dobutamine and levosimendan are first-line treatments for septic cardiomyopathy, yet their efficacy remains suboptimal [6, 7]. Thus, novel therapeutic approaches for SCHF are urgently needed.

Natriuretic peptides, released primarily from the heart in response to increased wall stress and myocardial stretch, function to inhibit apoptosis and counteract fibrotic pathways. B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) is a cardiac hormone synthesized and secreted predominantly by ventricular cardiomyocytes in response to ventricular wall stress [8]. It is released as the prohormone proBNP, which is cleaved to yield the inactive N-terminal fragment (NT-proBNP) and the biologically active BNP. In the septic cardiomyopathy, hemodynamic instability leads to elevated plasma BNP and NT-proBNP levels, which serve as surrogate markers of fluid overload and early indicators of myocardial depression [9]. Elevated levels of both peptides correlate strongly with sepsis severity [10, 11], and are associated with increased myocardial dysfunction and mortality [12, 13]. These findings establish plasma BNP/NT-proBNP as reliable biomarkers for identifying SCHF and assessing disease severity [14, 15].

Recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide (rhBNP) is a synthetic analog of endogenous BNP with identical biological activity [16]. Preclinical studies indicate that rhBNP attenuates myocardial injury and dysfunction in septic models while reducing mortality [17]. However, its clinical efficacy in SCHF patients remains controversial. While some studies suggest that early administration of rhBNP may significantly reduce 28-day mortality and protect cardiac function [18, 19], others report no significant survival improvement [20, 21]. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate the efficacy and safety of rhBNP for SCHF based on evidence from RCTs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Statement (PRISMA 2020, Supplementary Table S1) [22]. The study was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD 42023491344) prior to the commencement of the literature search in December 2023. The protocol was subsequently amended in July 2024 to extend the literature search period, with the final search conducted in July 2025.

Protocol registration and amendments

The study protocol was prospectively registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) in December 2023, after which an initial literature search was conducted. Prior to the final data synthesis, the protocol was amended to extend the literature search cut-off date from July 2024 to July 2025. This amendment, which is documented in the PROSPERO record, aimed to capture any newly published RCTs released after the original search, thereby ensuring the inclusion of the most current evidence. All eligibility criteria and analytical plans remained unchanged. However, the updated search did not yield any new eligible RCTs beyond those identified initially.

Database for search

A comprehensive literature search was performed across eight electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), and the Wan-fang Database. The search encompassed all records from the inception of each database up to July 2025, with no restrictions on language or publication region.

Eligibility criteria for study selection

Participants (P)

Studies involving adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with SCHF based on recognized diagnostic criteria established at or before the time of publication were eligible. No restrictions were applied regarding nationality, ethnicity, sex, language, or disease duration.

Interventions and comparators (I and C)

The control group received conventional western therapy (CWT), encompassing infection source control, antimicrobial treatment, fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, corticosteroids, inotropic support, blood product transfusion, sedative and analgesic management, glycemic control, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, and nutritional support. The experimental intervention consisted of CWT and rhBNP.

Outcomes (O)

Primary endpoints included 28-day mortality and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Secondary outcomes comprised total clinical effective rate (TCER), APACHE II scores, cardiac output (CO), and adverse events (AEs).

Study designs (S)

Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded for the following reasons: (1) incomplete outcome data; (2) purely theoretical research; (3) case reports; (4) duplicate publications.

Search strategy

The systematic literature search was constructed using a combination of controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings, MeSH) and free-text keywords, structured around the core PICOS elements: Participants (sepsis and heart failure), Intervention (recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide, rhBNP), Comparator (conventional Western therapy), and Study design (randomized controlled trials, RCTs). Our primary search logic combined all five components (P + I+C + O+S), with “O” denoting the pre-specified primary and secondary outcomes. To ensure sensitivity, a secondary, more inclusive strategy using the P + I+S framework was employed if the initial search returned an insufficient yield, after which all retrieved records were manually screened for eligibility. The exact, reproducible search strings for each of the eight databases are available in Supplementary Table S2, provided in full compliance with PRISMA guidelines to guarantee methodological transparency.

Data extraction and outcome definitions

Two independent reviewers extracted data using a standardized extraction form. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or third-party adjudication. When multiple publications from the same research group were identified, authors were contacted to confirm non-overlapping datasets. Extracted information included: first author, publication year, country, SCHF diagnostic criteria, baseline patient characteristics (e.g., age, sex, etc.), infection sources, eligibility criteria, rhBNP dosing and treatment duration, and outcome measures.

TCER is a composite endpoint frequently used in the included regional trials. Response was typically classified into three tiers (marked improvement, improvement, no improvement) according to study-specific, non-standardized criteria, with TCER defined as the proportion of patients achieving any degree of improvement. The variability in category definitions across studies limits the comparability and interpretability of the pooled estimates.

AE data were extracted based on prespecified items, including event types and whether definitions or causality assessments were reported. However, inconsistent reporting across trials precluded uniform distinction between treatment-related and all-cause AEs, and prevented verification of standardized AE definitions. This limitation is further addressed in the Discussion.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment of the selected studies was performed independently by two reviewers employing pre-defined tools. Discrepancies in their judgments were settled by consensus. In the case of RCTs, the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB2), was employed for appraisal [23]. The evaluation specifically focused on several domains: the randomization process, missing outcomes, measurement of the outcome, deviations from the intended intervention and selection of reported results. Ultimately, each trial was categorized as having a “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” of bias according to the RoB2 guidelines.

The certainty of the evidence derived from the meta-analyses was graded using the GRADE approach [24]. Following the standard GRADE methodology, the evidence quality for each outcome commenced with a high-confidence rating for randomized trials. This initial rating was then subject to a structured evaluation, potentially leading to downgrading based on five key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The final ratings were categorized into four levels: high, moderate, low, or very low certainty.

Statistical analyses

We performed meta-analyses to calculate pooled effect estimates—relative risks (RRs) for binary outcomes and mean differences (MDs) for continuous outcomes, each presented with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Mantel-Haenszel method (for binary data) and the inverse-variance method (for continuous data) were employed [25–27]. Model selection (fixed- or random-effects) was based on the observed statistical heterogeneity, which was quantified using the I² statistic (categorized as low: 25%-50%, moderate: 50%-75%, high: >75%), τ², and Cochran’s Q test [28–31]. To test the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding each study. Pre-specified meta-regression and subsequent subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity [32]. Publication bias was assessed visually via funnel plots for meta-analyses containing fewer than ten studies, and statistically using Egger’s regression test for those with ten or more studies [33]. The trim-and-fill method was further applied to impute potentially missing studies and estimate adjusted effect sizes [34]. All analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.0.5).

To control risks of Type I and Type II errors, we performed Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) on meta-analysis results. The TSA software (version 0.9.5.10 beta) was used to recalculate confidence intervals by using primary outcome data, and to reevaluate the cumulative meta-analysis effects. A conclusive finding could be established without additional trials if the cumulative Z-curve crossed either the required information size (RIS) or the TSA monitoring boundary. TSA parameters were set with 80% power and 5% Type I error rate [35].

Results

Literature search

The literature search identified 544 records. After removing 78 duplicates and excluding 446 articles during title/abstract screening, 20 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Twenty papers were initially selected, but 10 studies [36–45] were excluded due to unreported primary outcomes, not being RCTs, no comparison between rhBNP and placebo, or unretrievable full text. Thus, 10 studies [18–21, 46–51] involving 1,182 SCHF patients were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Ten studies with 1,182 SCHF patients were incorporated in this meta-analysis, including 596 in rhBNP group (rhBNP + CWT) and 586 in placebo group (CWT). The proportion of male patients was similar between the groups (0.57 [95% CI 0.53–0.61] in the rhBNP group vs. 0.56 [95% CI 0.51–0.60] in the placebo group), pooled mean age was also similar (69.9 years [95% CI 66.9–72.9] vs. 70.1 years [95% CI 67.3–73.0]. All studies were Chinese single-center randomized controlled trials published between 2018 and 2023, with nine [18, 19, 21, 46–51] published in Chinese and one [20] in English. The sample sizes ranged from 80 to 250 in most studies [18–21, 46, 47, 49–51], while one study [48] had fewer than 80 participants. All studies diagnosed SCHF according to the Surviving Sepsis Campaign Guidelines for Management of Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock (Version 2.0 or 3.0). Assessment of potential data duplication

To address the potential risk of duplicate data inflation from single-center trials conducted within the same healthcare system, we performed a systematic assessment for overlapping patient cohorts across all included studies. This involved cross-examining the names of the participating clinical centers, the full authorship lists, and the patient recruitment periods as reported in each publication. No instances of shared centers or overlapping authorships were identified. Furthermore, the patient enrollment periods, where reported, did not indicate contemporaneous recruitment at the same institution. Based on this assessment, we concluded that the risk of duplicate patient data being included in our meta-analysis is low.

Risk of bias assessment

With the exception of one study [20], the other studies did not provide details on blinding. Nine trials [36–42] were judged to have “some concerns” regarding the randomization process because allocation concealment was not reported. Additionally, “some concerns” regarding outcome measurement were raised for these studies due to a lack of blinding information. All studies were judged to be at “low risk” of bias in the following domains: deviations from intended interventions and selective outcome reporting, as no issues were identified. Selective reporting risk was also deemed “low risk” as all RCTs had reported the pre-specified outcomes (Fig. 2).

Meta-analysis results

Primary outcome measures of 28-day mortality

Four studies [18–21] involving 551 patients reported data on 28-day mortality, and the methods of assessment varied across these studies (see Supplementary Table S3). The mortality was different between the rhBNP group and the placebo group (82 of 278 patients [29.5%] vs. 110 of 273 patients [40.3%], respectively). The Fixed-effects model was chosen due to low heterogeneity among studies (p = 0.45, I² = 0%). Compared to CWT alone, rhBNP plus CWT was associated with a reduction in 28-day mortality (RR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.58–0.92, p = 0.006; Fig. 3A), with very low certainty based on GRADE criteria (Supplementary Table S4). Visual inspection of the funnel plot indicated suspicion of publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S1). The trim-and-fill analysis imputed one missing study. The adjusted estimate (RR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.62–0.99, p = 0.045; Supplementary Table S5) also showed a lower 28-day mortality rate with rhBNP plus CWT versus CWT alone. The adjusted funnel plot showed no asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. S2). Subgroup analyses of the primary outcome—based on dosage, duration, study quality, sample size, average age, and diagnostic criteria—did not show significant differences in the treatment effect on 28-day mortality (Pinteraction > 0.05; Supplementary Table S6).

Forest plot of primary outcomes (A 28-day mortality, B LVEF)

Primary outcome measures of LVEF

Nine studies [19–21, 46–51] involving 1,057 patients reported the results of LVEF. The random-effects model was selected for meta-analysis due to high heterogeneity across studies (p < 0.0001, I 2 = 95.5%). The meta-analysis showed that rhBNP was associated with an increase in LVEF (MD = 4.91, 95% CI: 2.56–7.26, p < 0.0001; Fig. 3B), with very low certainty based on GRADE criteria (Table S4). Sensitivity analyses found no possible source of the high heterogeneity(Supplementary Fig. S3). Subgroup analyses of the primary outcome—based on dosage, duration, combination therapy with CRRT, study quality, sex ratio, sample size, average age, and diagnostic criteria—did not show significant differences in the treatment effect on LVEF (Pinteraction > 0.05; Supplementary Table S7). Univariable meta-regression indicated that the diagnostic criteria were a significant moderator of the effect size (p = 0.03; Supplementary Table S8, Fig. S4). Visual inspection of funnel plot showed no evidence of publication bias (Fig. S1).

Secondary outcome measures of TCER

Three studies [46–48] involving 242 patients reported the results of TCER. The TCER was different between the rhBNP group and the placebo group (113 of 121 patients [93.4%] vs. 90 of 121 patients [74.4%], respectively). The definitions and components of TCER varied across the three trials that reported this outcome, as summarized in Supplementary Table S9. Given this conceptual heterogeneity, pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution. The Fixed-effects model was chosen for meta-analysis as there had low heterogeneity among studies (p = 0.745, I² = 0%). Meta results showed that rhBNP plus CWT had a higher TCER than CWT alone (RR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.12–1.41, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4A), with very low certainty based on GRADE criteria (Table S4). Visual inspection of the funnel plot indicated suspicion of publication bias (Fig. S1). The trim-and-fill analysis imputed two missing studies. The adjusted estimate (RR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.09–1.31, p < 0.0001; Table S5) also indicated that rhBNP plus CWT had a higher TCER than CWT alone. The adjusted funnel plot showed no asymmetry (Fig. S2).

Forest plot of secondary outcomes (A TCER, B CO, C APACHE II scores, D AEs)

Secondary outcome measures of CO

Three studies [21, 47, 48] involving 252 patients reported the results of CO. The random-effects model was selected for meta-analysis due to moderate heterogeneity across studies (p = 0.08, I² = 60.7%). Compared to CWT alone, rhBNP plus CWT was associated with an improvement in CO (MD = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.21 to 0.74, p = 0.0005; Fig. 4B), with low certainty based on GRADE (Table S4). Visual inspection of funnel plot did not indicate suspicions for publication bias (Fig. S1).

Secondary outcome measures of APACHE II scores

Three studies [47, 49, 50] involving 349 patients reported the results of APACHE II scores. The random-effects model was selected for meta-analysis due to high heterogeneity across studies (p < 0.0001, I 2 = 94.4%). Compared to CWT alone, rhBNP plus CWT was associated with a reduction in APACHEⅡ scores (MD = -4.18, 95% CI: -7.80 to -0.55, p = 0.024; Fig. 4C), with very low certainty based on GRADE (Table S4). Visual inspection of funnel plot suggested possible publication bias (Fig. S1), while the trim-and-fill analysis indicated no missing studies (Table S5).

Secondary outcome measures of AEs

Six studies [21, 46, 48–51] involving 631 patients reported the results of AEs. The Fixed-effects model was selected for meta-analysis due to low heterogeneity across studies (p = 0.28, I² = 19.8%). Compared to CWT alone, rhBNP plus CWT did not significantly reduce AEs (RR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.54 to 1.08, p = 0.13; Fig. 4D), with low certainty based on GRADE (Table S4). Visual inspection of the funnel plot indicated suspicion of publication bias (Fig. S1). The trim-and-fill analysis imputed three missing studies. The adjusted estimate (RR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.41 to 0.92, p = 0.017; Table S5) suggested that rhBNP plus CWT might reduce AEs better than CWT alone. The adjusted funnel plot showed no asymmetry (Fig. S2).

The quality of the evidence

The certainty of evidence for all outcomes was evaluated using the GRADE methodology. Assessments were based on the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The overall certainty ratings ranged from very low to high. CO was graded as low certainty, downgraded for serious risk of bias (as most studies were at moderate risk) and serious inconsistency (50% < I² < 75%). For 28-day mortality and TCER, the certainty was rated as very low, owing to serious risk of bias (most studies at moderate risk), serious indirectness (the differences in outcome measurement), and strong suspicion of publication bias (as indicated by funnel plot asymmetry). LVEF was graded as very low certainty, downgraded for serious risk of bias and very serious inconsistency (I² > 75%). APACHE II scores were assessed as very low certainty based on serious risk of bias, very serious inconsistency (I² > 75%), and strong suspicion of publication bias. For AEs, the certainty was rated as low, owing to serious risk of bias (most studies at moderate risk) and strong suspicion of publication bias (as indicated by funnel plot asymmetry). These results are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Results of trial sequential analysis (TSA)

TSA was performed to assess the reliability of the meta-analytic findings (Supplementary Table S10). For the outcomes of TCER, LVEF, and CO, the cumulative Z-curves crossed both the conventional and TSA-adjusted boundaries for benefit, and the accrued sample sizes exceeded the required information size (RIS). These results suggest a potentially treatment effect of rhBNP plus CWT over CWT alone for these endpoints. Regarding 28-day mortality, while the Z-curve crossed the monitoring boundaries, the RIS was not achieved. Therefore, the result, although indicating a potential reduction in mortality, should be interpreted with caution and cannot be considered conclusive; the evidence remains insufficient to definitively confirm or refute an effect. For APACHE II scores, the RIS was met and the boundaries were crossed, indicating that the evidence for a beneficial effect is less likely to be a random error. Conversely, for AEs, neither the RIS was met nor did the Z-curve cross the benefit boundaries, indicating that the current evidence is inadequate to draw any conclusion regarding the impact of rhBNP on AEs, and more data are needed. Overall, where RIS was not met, the TSA primarily underscores the need for further research to reach firm conclusions, aligning with the inherent limitations of the available sample size and study quality.

Discussion

Recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide is widely used as an adjunctive therapy for SCHF in Chinese emergency and critical care settings. Our meta-analysis suggests several potential benefits. Compared to CWT alone, the addition of rhBNP was associated with a reduction in 28-day mortality and improvements in cardiac function, as reflected by increased LVEF and CO. It was also linked to a reduction in APACHE II scores and a higher TCER. However, it did not significantly reduce the incidence of AEs.

Our finding of reduced 28-day mortality aligns with previous RCTs on rhBNP in acute heart failure [52, 53]. However, this outcome was reported in only four [18–21] of the initially identified twenty studies [18–21, 36–51], involving 551 patients. We were concerned that the limited sample size could bias the results. To address this, we performed Trial Sequential Analysis. Although the Z-curve crossed the efficacy boundaries, the required information size (RIS) was not met; therefore, the finding remains inconclusive. Given the very low certainty of evidence, this conclusion may change with future high-quality trials. The assessment of 28-day mortality was inconsistent and may be incomplete, lowering certainty in the finding. The estimate suggests a benefit but comes from few trials with potentially incomplete follow-up, preventing firm conclusions.

Subgroup analyses of 28-day mortality based on treatment duration and dosing regimen showed no statistically significant interaction effects (P for interaction = 0.33 and 0.27, respectively), so we cannot definitively attribute effect modification to these factors. Nevertheless, numerically consistent trends were observed. Regimens incorporating a loading dose (40 µg: RR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.35–0.90; 1.5 µg/kg: RR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.49–0.99) yielded more favorable point estimates than the bolus-free regimen (RR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.62–1.36). Similarly, the shortest treatment duration (3 days) showed the lowest point estimate (RR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.43–0.89) compared to longer durations. These observations are pharmacologically plausible, as a bolus dose may rapidly achieve effective drug levels, and a shorter, intensive course might maximize early hemodynamic benefits [54]. The main limitation of these subgroup comparisons is their low statistical power, with each containing only one or two trials and wide, overlapping confidence intervals. Thus, while no definitive recommendations can be made, our analysis generates the hypothesis that an rhBNP regimen initiated with a loading dose and administered over a short course (e.g., 3 days) may optimize survival outcomes—a hypothesis requiring prospective validation.

Regarding LVEF, our results are consistent with previous studies [55, 56], which showed that rhBNP improved LVEF in patients with heart failure. Due to high heterogeneity, we performed sensitivity and subgroup analyses for LVEF. No significant interaction effects were found for treatment duration or dosing regimen (P = 0.16 and P = 0.44, respectively). Numerically, the greatest improvement was seen with courses < 7 days (MD = 6.16%, 95% CI: 3.83 to 8.48) and with the 1.5 µg/kg bolus dose (MD = 6.06%, 95% CI: 4.16 to 7.96), compared to other subgroups.In contrast, univariable meta-regression identified the diagnostic criteria of primary studies as a significant effect moderator (p = 0.03), indicating that variability in patient definitions contributed substantially to heterogeneity.Thus, while trends regarding shorter duration and specific bolus dosing are hypothesis-generating, the significant meta-regression underscores that standardized diagnostic criteria are critical in future trials to clarify rhBNP’s true effect on LVEF. TSA indicated that the accrued sample size reached the RIS and the Z-curves crossed the boundaries, suggesting a signal of benefit. However, given the very low certainty of evidence, results should be interpreted cautiously, and future high-quality RCTs are needed for validation.

Our findings on TCER agree with prior RCTs [57, 58] but are based on very low-certainty evidence. TCER is a non-validated, composite endpoint whose definition lacked standardization across trials, incorporating varying combinations of subjective and objective assessments. While its reporting reflects regional clinical practice, its subjective nature precludes it from being considered a robust, internationally generalizable efficacy measure. Therefore, we treated it as an exploratory outcome, underscoring the need for future trials to prioritize internationally recognized, patient-centered hard endpoints.

The safety profile of rhBNP, also known as nesiritide, requires critical appraisal beyond the quantitative synthesis presented in this meta-analysis. Nesiritide has historically been associated with important safety concerns in the settings of acute heart failure and sepsis. Pivotal investigations such as the ASCEND-HF trial and the ADHERE registry have documented an elevated risk of hypotension and renal impairment following its administration [59, 60]. These observations led to formal safety communications from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and continue to shape conservative prescribing practices in cardiovascular medicine [61]. The risk–benefit equilibrium of this agent in critically ill populations thus remains a subject of ongoing debate.In the present analysis, adverse event data were extractable from six included studies. The pooled effect estimate indicated no statistically significant difference in the overall incidence of adverse events between patients receiving rhBNP plus CWT and those receiving CWT alone (RR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.54–1.08, p = 0.13). TSA demonstrated that the required information size (n = 2,704) was not attained, and the cumulative Z-curve failed to surpass either the conventional or the TSA‑adjusted monitoring boundaries. These findings collectively indicate that the current body of evidence is inadequate to support definitive conclusions regarding the safety of rhBNP in this clinical context. Several methodological limitations merit acknowledgment. First, definitions of AEs varied across trials, and none attributed events specifically to the investigational drug, precluding causal inference. Second, low event numbers and wide confidence intervals limited estimate precision. Third, follow-up was largely restricted to the in-hospital period, leaving longer-term outcomes—particularly renal function—unexamined. Although no excess overt AEs were observed, this finding does not constitute evidence of safety, especially given the well-documented risks of hypotension and renal injury from prior cardiovascular trials. Future studies should adopt standardized AE definitions, prospectively adjudicate treatment relatedness, and incorporate prespecified monitoring for renal function and hemodynamic compromise. Until such evidence emerges, the use of rhBNP in SCHF warrants cautious hemodynamic and renal surveillance.

The interpretation of our findings is subject to several important limitations. Primarily, the evidence base consists exclusively of small-scale, single-center randomized controlled trials conducted within the Chinese healthcare system and published in regional journals. This geographic and methodological homogeneity raises significant concerns regarding the generalizability of the results to other populations, where patient demographics, clinical practices, and drug formulations may differ. Furthermore, the methodological rigor of the included trials was variable; common shortcomings included the lack of sample size calculations, inadequate reporting of allocation concealment and blinding, and infrequent use of trial registration or adherence to CONSORT guidelines. Substantial clinical heterogeneity, particularly in rhBNP dosing regimens and treatment durations, further complicates the synthesis. Although we verified no overlap in clinical centers or authorship to mitigate duplicate publication risk, the overall certainty of evidence, as assessed by the GRADE framework, remains low to very low for most outcomes. Regarding endpoint timing, this meta-analysis used 28-day mortality as the primary endpoint. While standard in sepsis trials, 28 days may be too short to capture the full course of septic cardiomyopathy. Injury and recovery often extend beyond this window. Thus, our findings reflect mid-term survival differences but lack data on longer-term outcomes. Longer follow-up is needed to better assess treatment effects. Consequently, our results, while highlighting a potential signal of benefit for rhBNP in this specific context, must be interpreted with caution and are not yet suitable for uncritical extrapolation. Definitive conclusions await future large-scale, multinational trials designed with rigorous methodology.

Future trials should address the core limitations of the current evidence. Multicenter, multinational studies with adequate power and extended follow-up are required to validate the generalizability of rhBNP’s effects beyond the Chinese healthcare setting. Methodological rigor must be improved through mandatory adherence to CONSORT guidelines, prospective registration, sample size justification, and robust blinding. Standardized protocols for rhBNP dosing and treatment duration are essential to reduce heterogeneity. Given the very low certainty of existing evidence, all efficacy and safety outcomes—particularly mortality and renal function—require reassessment in well-designed trials before definitive conclusions can be drawn.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis suggests that adjunctive rhBNP may improve cardiac function and reduce disease severity in SCHF, with a potential but inconclusive survival benefit. However, the evidence is derived predominantly from small, single-center Chinese trials with low to very low certainty, and well-documented safety risks—particularly hypotension and renal impairment—remain unresolved. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and are not generalizable. Definitive conclusions require adequately powered, multinational RCTs with standardized protocols and patient-centered hard endpoints.
