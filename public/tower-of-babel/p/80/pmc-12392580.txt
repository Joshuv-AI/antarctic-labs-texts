Impact of frailty on in-hospital outcomes in nonagenarian ICU patients: a binational multicenter analysis of 8,220 cases
Authors: Je Min Suh, Nattaya Raykateeraroj, Raelynn Tong, David Pilcher, Dong-Kyu Lee, Laurence Weinberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs global populations age, the number of nonagenarians admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) is rising. Frailty, a multidimensional syndrome marked by diminished physiological reserves, has been associated with adverse outcomes in older ICU patients. However, evidence remains limited regarding its prognostic significance in nonagenarians, who represent a unique and rapidly growing subset of critically ill patients. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of frailty on in-hospital mortality and length of stay among nonagenarian ICU patients in Australia and New Zealand.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the ANZICS Adult Patient Database, including nonagenarians admitted to 211 ICUs between 2017 and 2023 with documented Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) scores. Patients were classified as frail (CFS ≥ 5) or non-frail (CFS < 5). Propensity score matching (1:1) was applied to adjust for confounders including age, sex, illness severity, admission type, and comorbidities. Outcomes included ICU and hospital mortality, and ICU and hospital lengths of stay (LOS). Statistical analyses included multivariable Cox regression, log-transformed logistic regression, and Fine Gray competing risks models.ResultsAmong 16,439 nonagenarians, 8220 patients were propensity matched. In the matched cohort, frailty was independently associated with increased hospital mortality (adjusted HR 1.352, 95% CI 1.192–1.534, p < 0.001) and ICU mortality (adjusted HR 1.242, 95% CI 1.044–1.440, p = 0.017). Each one-point increase in CFS score was associated with a 9% increase in the odds ratio of ICU mortality (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.01–1.18, p = 0.026) and a 19% increase in the odds ratio of hospital mortality (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.10–1.28, p < 0.001). Frailty was not associated with ICU LOS after adjustment (p = 0.739) but predicted prolonged hospital LOS (adjusted β = 1.051, 95% CI 1.033–1.070, p < 0.001).ConclusionsFrailty is a strong, independent predictor of hospital mortality and prolonged hospitalization in critically ill nonagenarians, even after adjusting for illness severity and comorbidities. These findings support the incorporation of frailty assessment into early risk stratification and clinical decision-making in ICU settings, to facilitate goal-concordant care and optimize resource allocation for the very elderly.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05612-3.Keywords: Nonagenarians, Intensive care unit, Critical illness, Geriatrics, Aging

Introduction

Advancements in healthcare and improved living conditions are driving global population aging, with the number of elderly individuals (aged 65 years and above) expected to increase from 800 million in 2025 to 2.2 billion by 2080 [1]. Within this growing demographic, the number of nonagenarians is projected to reach 30.9 million by 2030 [2], resulting in a corresponding increase in demand for specialized and intensive care for patients in this age group [3].

Frailty is a multidimensional geriatric syndrome characterized by increased vulnerability to stressors due to reduced physiological and cognitive reserves [4]. It is commonly assessed using the well-validated Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), which scores patients from 1 to 9, with scores of 5 or above considered frail [5]. The CFS has been used to predict short- and long-term mortality outcomes in various intensive care settings [6–9]. Given that intensive care unit (ICU) patients are critically ill, their pre-existing level of frailty may be particularly salient in determining their likelihood of surviving the admission.

Although research has explored the relationship between frailty and outcomes in critically ill geriatric patients, most studies have used a minimum age cut-off of 80 years [6, 10–13]. While one European study found similar 30-day mortality for octogenarian and nonagenarian ICU patients [14], and one study comparing nonagenarians and centenarians admitted to ICUs, using data from the very old intensive (VIP1 and VIP2) care patient and COVIP registries, found that nonagenarians had a higher 30-day mortality risk [15], research on the impact of frailty on short-term outcomes for critically ill nonagenarians remains limited. To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective study to investigate the impact of frailty on in-hospital mortality and length of stay in nonagenarians admitted to the ICU.

Methods

Data sources, study population, and variables

The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [16]. De-identified patient data were obtained from the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Adult Patient Database. This resource includes data on patient demographics, clinical information, interventions, and outcomes from 98% of Australian and 68% of New Zealand ICUs [17], collected by the ANZICS Centre for Outcome and Resource Evaluation.

The present study included all nonagenarians (aged 90 to 99 years) admitted to the ICU between 2017 and 2023 who had a documented Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) score at the time of admission. In accordance with the most recent version of the CFS, updated in 2020, but modified in this study to an 8-point scale (rather than the original 9-point scale), patients were categorized as either frail (CFS score ≥ 5) or non-frail (CFS score < 5). Demographic variables included age, sex, admission type, admission source, discharge destination, and ICU and hospital admission dates. Clinical data included frailty, assessed using the modified CFS, and illness severity, and measured using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) III-J score. Comorbidities were recorded, including immunosuppression, malignancy, and chronic respiratory, cardiovascular, liver, and renal disease. Missing data are summarized in Supplementary Table 1 and were excluded from the analysis without imputation.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were ICU mortality, defined as death during ICU admission, and hospital mortality, defined as death before hospital discharge. Secondary outcomes included ICU and hospital lengths of stay (LOS), measured from the date of admission to the date of discharge. To account for variations in illness severity, APACHE III-J scores were analyzed as a predictor of both mortality and LOS. This facilitated risk-adjusted analyses, including survival models and competing risks regression. These selected outcomes enabled the assessment of the short-term impact of frailty on critically ill nonagenarians, taking into account competing risks and time-dependent effects on mortality.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). To achieve covariate balance between frail and non-frail patient groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the MatchIt package. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression models incorporating age, sex, APACHE III-J score, admission type, and pre-existing comorbidities. Various matching algorithms with varying calipers and matching ratios were systematically evaluated to determine the optimal approach. Matching quality was assessed by visual inspection of propensity score distributions and quantified using standardized mean differences (SMDs), visualized as Love plots. Ultimately, 1:1 nearest neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1 was selected, achieving optimal covariate balance while maintaining an adequate sample size. Detailed balance diagnostics are presented in Fig. 1.

Love Plot: Standardized mean differences before and after propensity score matching (PSM) with calipers 0.1–0.2 and ratios 1:1–2:1

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Skewed continuous outcomes, such as ICU and hospital LOS, were log10-transformed to normalize distributions and improve model stability; results were subsequently backtransformed for clinical interpretation.

Survival outcomes, including ICU and hospital mortality, were initially evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression was employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeldt's residual tests, confirming its validity for both ICU and hospital mortality models. Multivariable Cox regression models were constructed, incorporating clinically relevant covariates and variables that were significant at p < 0.2 in the univariable analyses. Model discrimination and calibration were assessed using Harrell’s C-statistic and visual inspection of residual plots.

We conducted univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses for ICU and hospital mortality to evaluate the association between binary mortality outcomes and frailty, age, and illness severity. Frailty was modeled as a continuous variable after confirming model fit using likelihood ratio testing. This involved comparing nested models, treating frailty as a categorical versus continuous predictor, with the continuous specification providing equivalent or superior fit while preserving parsimony and degrees of freedom in both ICU and hospital mortality models. This approach enabled more interpretable effect estimates while minimizing the risk of overfitting. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, admission type, comorbidities, and APACHE III-J score.

For ICU and hospital LOS, linear regression analyses were conducted on log10-transformed outcomes. Variables with a significance of p < 0.2 in univariable models were considered for inclusion in multivariable models using forward stepwise selection. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with a VIF > 4 were excluded from the final models.

Competing risks regression using the Fine Gray subdistribution hazard model was applied to account for the competing risk of death in time-to-discharge analyses. Subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) were calculated to estimate cumulative incidence functions.

Results

Over the study period (2010–2023), the proportion of critically ill nonagenarian admissions steadily increased across 211 ICUs in New Zealand and Australia, rising from 1.22% of total admissions in 2010 to a high of 1.93% in 2019, before slightly declining to 1.62% in 2023. ICU mortality decreased from 9.98% in 2010 to 6.63% in 2023, with hospital mortality also demonstrating a downward trend from 21.69% in 2010 to 15.23% in 2023 (Fig. 2).

After excluding patients with missing data and those receiving palliative care, the final analysis included 16,439 nonagenarians admitted to the ICU between 2020 and 2023. Of these patients, 6459 were classified as non-frail, and 9980 as frail. The overall ICU and hospital mortality rates were 6.19% and 14.49%, respectively. PSM resulted in a matched cohort of 8220 patients (Tables 1 and 2).

Baseline characteristics of original and propensity-matched cohorts

Tables 1 and 2 present the baseline characteristics of the original and propensity-matched cohorts. In the original cohort, frail patients were significantly older, more often female, and had a higher prevalence of chronic respiratory, cardiovascular, and renal comorbidities than non-frail patients (all p < 0.001). Elective surgical admissions were less frequent in the frail subgroup, consistent with their higher acuity burden. Median APACHE III-J scores were also significantly higher in frail patients (64 [54–76]) compared to their non-frail counterparts (60 [51–72]; p < 0.001), reflecting a greater overall burden of illness at ICU admission, including age, comorbidities, and physiological disturbance.

PSM resulted in a well-balanced distribution of baseline variables between frail and non-frail patients. All SMDs were below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.1, indicating successful covariate adjustment. In the matched cohort, median APACHE III-J scores were comparable between frail and non-frail patients (62 [53–73] in both), confirming appropriate matching on illness severity and baseline risk.

Impact of frailty on ICU and hospital mortality

Tables 1 and 2 present ICU and hospital mortality outcomes in both the original and propensity-matched cohorts, stratified by frailty status. In the original cohort, ICU mortality was significantly higher in frail patients than non-frail patients (6.5% vs. 4.2%, p < 0.001). This disparity was more pronounced for hospital mortality, with the incidence in frail individuals nearly twice that in their non-frail counterparts (15.9% vs. 9.3%, p < 0.001). These findings highlight the profound vulnerability of frail patients admitted to the ICU, with an elevated mortality risk that extends beyond the acute phase of care.

Following PSM, which was designed to minimize confounding by balancing key covariates such as age, comorbidities, admission type, and illness severity, mortality differences were still observed. While ICU mortality remained elevated in frail patients (5.2% vs. 4.7%), this difference was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.416), suggesting partial attenuation of risk following adjustment. However, hospital mortality remained significantly higher in the frail group (13.8% vs. 10.6%, p < 0.001), indicating the persistent and substantial influence of frailty on outcomes beyond the ICU, despite matched baseline risk profiles.

These results underscore the prognostic significance of frailty for both short-term ICU outcomes and overall hospital survival. The persistence of mortality disparities post-matching emphasizes the independent contribution of frailty to adverse outcomes, beyond traditional predictors such as age and illness severity scores.

Table 3 presents the results of univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models evaluating predictors of ICU and hospital mortality in the propensity matched cohort. In the multivariable model for ICU mortality, frailty (defined as CFS ≥ 5) was independently associated with increased hazard of death (adjusted HR 1.242, 95% CI 1.044 to 1.440, p = 0.017), even after controlling for admission type and illness severity. APACHE III-J score remained a strong predictor of ICU mortality (adjusted HR 1.045 per unit increase, 95% CI 1.041 to 1.049, p < 0.001). Interestingly, non-elective surgical admissions carried a substantially higher mortality risk compared to medical admissions (unadjusted HR 7.184, 95% CI 5.425 to 11.412, p < 0.001); however, this variable was not retained in the final adjusted model due to multicollinearity.

Frailty again emerged as a significant independent risk factor for hospital mortality (adjusted HR 1.352, 95% CI 1.192 to 1.534, p < 0.001). Female sex was associated with a lower risk of hospital mortality than male sex (adjusted HR 0.826, 95% CI 0.728 to 0.937, p = 0.003). Neither age nor admission category remained a significant predictor in the final multivariable hospital mortality model.

These findings establish frailty as an independent and robust predictor of both ICU and hospital mortality among critically ill nonagenarians, even after adjusting for illness severity and other confounders. Frailty’s stronger association with hospital mortality highlights its enduring impact beyond the acute ICU phase.

Frailty, when modelled as a continuous variable, was a strong and independent predictor of both ICU and hospital mortality, as demonstrated by multivariable logistic regression models adjusting for age, sex, admission type, comorbidities, and APACHE III-J score in the propensity matched cohort (see Tables 4 and 5). Specifically, each one-point increase in frailty score was associated with a 9% increase in the odds of ICU mortality (adjusted OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.18, p = 0.026) and a 19% increase in the odds of hospital mortality (adjusted OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.28, p < 0.001). These associations remained statistically significant after adjusting for key clinical covariates and were further supported by likelihood ratio testing, which confirmed the appropriateness of treating frailty as a continuous variable. Taken together, these findings underscore frailty’s prognostic value as a quantitatively meaningful and clinically actionable indicator of mortality risk in critically ill nonagenarians.

Impact of frailty on ICU and hospital lengths of stay

Table 1 illustrates that, in the original cohort, frail patients experienced significantly longer ICU and hospital stays compared to their non-frail counterparts. While frail patients experienced a moderately longer median ICU LOS (1.66 vs. 1.45 days, p < 0.001), the difference in their hospital LOS was more pronounced, reflecting an 18% relative increase (10.6 vs. 8.97 days, p < 0.001). Following PSM, the difference in ICU LOS was no longer statistically significant (1.6 vs. 1.5 days, p = 0.571), suggesting that the initial disparity was attributable to confounding factors such as comorbidity burden and illness severity. In contrast, the difference in hospital LOS persisted post-matching (10.7 vs. 9.1 days, p < 0.001), indicating an independent association between frailty and prolonged hospitalization.

This observation was further supported by multivariable linear regression modelling in the propensity matched cohort (Table 7), which demonstrated no significant association between frailty and ICU LOS in the adjusted model (β = 0.997, 95% CI 0.982 to 1.013; p = 0.739), confirming the absence of a meaningful effect on ICU duration. However, frailty remained independently associated with extended hospitalization, with a 5.1% relative increase in total hospital LOS (β = 1.051, 95% CI 1.033 to 1.070; p < 0.001) after adjusting for age, sex, admission type, comorbidities, and APACHE III-J score. This likely reflects increased care complexity and delayed recovery trajectories among frail patients, rather than differences in ICU-level interventions.

As demonstrated in Table 6, the Fine–Gray competing risks regression confirmed that extended LOS in frail patients was primarily a consequence of excess in-hospital mortality, rather than delayed recovery. Frailty was associated with a significantly higher sub-distribution hazard of hospital mortality, suggesting that frail patients’ prolonged hospitalization predominantly reflected increased time spent in hospital prior to death, rather than delayed discharge among survivors. This finding reinforces the interpretation of frailty as a marker of physiological vulnerability and progressive clinical deterioration.

Additional covariates were also independently associated with LOS outcomes. Higher APACHE III-J scores predicted longer ICU stays (β = 1.004, 95% CI 1.004 to 1.005; p < 0.001), consistent with established evidence linking illness severity with ICU resource utilization [19]. Female sex was associated with shorter hospital LOS (β = 0.973, 95% CI 0.948 to 1.000; p = 0.048), aligning with prior studies [18] that found sex-based differences in physiological resilience and recovery among older adults.

Combined, these findings demonstrate that, after adjusting for clinical severity, frailty does not prolong ICU stay but is independently associated with extended hospitalization and increased in-hospital mortality. This underscores the value of integrating frailty assessment into ICU triage, care planning, and discharge strategies, particularly in very elderly populations with complex post-acute needs.

Discussion

Key findings

Frail patients exhibited significantly higher ICU and hospital mortality than their non-frail counterparts, even after propensity score matching. The greater risk associated with frailty manifested early in the ICU stay and persisted throughout admission, as evidenced by both Cox regression and Fine–Gray competing risks analyses. Hospital mortality also remained substantially elevated among frail patients, indicative of their increased vulnerability beyond the ICU setting.

Although both groups had similar ICU LOS, frail patients experienced significantly longer hospital stays. This was primarily attributable to delayed recovery and higher rates of in-hospital complications rather than prolonged intensive care. Multivariable models confirmed frailty’s role as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes, reinforcing its prognostic value.

Higher APACHE III-J scores were independently associated with increased mortality and longer ICU LOS. However, they were paradoxically linked to shorter hospital LOS, likely reflecting the confounding influence of early in-hospital deaths. Additionally, male sex was independently associated with higher hospital mortality, possibly reflecting a greater burden of comorbidities and baseline illness severity.

Relationship of findings to the literature

In 2024 and 2025, a multicenter international study and a Polish study, treating frailty as a categorical variable (CFS ≥ 5), yielded statistically significant results. The first study, a meta-analysis of ICU patients aged ≥ 65 years, found a hazard ratio of 1.34 (p < 0.0001) [12], and the other found higher ICU mortality (p < 0.03) in patients aged ≥ 80 years [13]. Our finding of a hazard ratio of 1.242 (p < 0.017) for frailty in nonagenarians’ ICU mortality aligns with these more recent results. Increased frailty has also been associated with higher ICU mortality when using other frailty measures (the Edmonton Frail Scale [20], Hospital Frailty Risk Score [21], and Frailty Index [22]). To date, this study represents one of the largest retrospective analyses of ICU outcomes in nonagenarian patients.

In addition to ICU mortality, hospital mortality was the other primary outcome of this study. A bi-national Australia and New Zealand study on ICU patients aged ≥ 80 years demonstrated an association between increased CFS score and significantly higher hospital mortality (p < 0.001) [10], which is consistent with our finding of a hazard ratio of 1.352 (p < 0.001) for frailty in critically ill nonagenarians’ hospital mortality. Although the reporting of hazard ratios is scarce, odds ratios of 1.87 [95% CI, 1.65–2.11] and 2.15 [95% CI, 2.05–2.25] have been reported by multicenter Australian and New Zealand studies on patients 80 years and older [10, 23] and aged 65 years [24], respectively. A 2021 meta-analysis of studies investigating frailty measured using the CFS and the in-hospital mortality of patients aged ≥ 65 years found a risk ratio of 1.73 [95% CI, 1.55–1.93] [25]. Collectively, these results support the increased likelihood of in-hospital mortality in frail compared to non-frail critically ill elderly patients.

ICU and hospital LOS were important secondary outcomes considered in the present study. A 2019 multicenter study of ICU patients aged ≥ 80 years in Australia and New Zealand found that frail patients experienced slightly, although insignificantly, longer ICU and hospital stays than non-frail patients [10]. A 2020 single-center Spanish study of critically ill patients aged ≥ 70 years found that the frail group had slightly shorter ICU LOS and slightly longer hospital LOS, although both results were statistically insignificant due to overlap between the two groups [11]. These results are similar to our findings of insignificantly shorter ICU LOS (β-coefficient 0.997, p < 0.7) and significantly longer hospital LOS (β-coefficient 1.051, p < 0.001) in frail compared to non-frail nonagenarians. A potential explanation for this difference could be the higher likelihood that frail patients die early on in their ICU admission and that, if they do survive, they require extended inpatient care.

Looking into ICU and Hospital outcomes, in comparison to existing literature, a Dutch national observational study reported ICU and hospital mortality rates of 13.8% and 26.1% [26], respectively, while a Brazilian study found rates of 17.8% and 27.5% [27]. Our findings—showing ICU mortality below 10% and hospital mortality below 20%—are slightly lower, likely reflecting differences in healthcare resources, ICU admission thresholds, and care practices in Australia and New Zealand.

Clinical implications

Frailty was independently associated with significantly increased ICU and hospital mortality among critically ill nonagenarians, with the excess risk most pronounced during the early stages of ICU admission. This time-dependent pattern of mortality suggests a more favorable long-term trajectory for patients who survive the initial critical period. These findings highlight the need for ongoing, dynamic reassessment of treatment goals in response to the evolving clinical status, rather than relying solely on static pre-admission decisions. Frailty should not be viewed as a binary determinant for care escalation or limitation, but rather as a marker that informs the trajectory and intensity of care required.

The robust association between APACHE III-J score and adverse outcomes underscores the importance of early illness severity scoring as a critical tool in ICU triage and decision-making. Timely risk stratification facilitates targeted monitoring, early escalation of supportive therapies, and efficient use of critical care resources. Integrating objective physiological assessment with frailty status enables a more nuanced understanding of prognosis and can support anticipatory multidisciplinary care planning from the point of admission.

Crucially, a combination of frailty and severity scoring should inform structured discussions around treatment limitations, which must be grounded in evidence-based prognostication and sensitively adapted to the patient’s evolving condition, personal values, and longer-term goals of care. Considering factors such as sociocultural background, religious beliefs, and jurisdictional frameworks for end-of-life care is essential to ensure ethically aligned, person-centered decision-making.

Ultimately, these findings underscore the importance of embedding frailty assessment into routine ICU workflow, not as a determinant of futility, but as a tool to guide proportionate care. It is critical to acknowledge the heterogeneity of outcomes among the very elderly, particularly those who survive the initial ICU phase. Thoughtful, evidence-informed care strategies that incorporate frailty, severity of illness, and evolving clinical status can promote alignment between therapeutic interventions and the outcomes that matter most to patients and their families.

Directions for future research

ICU and hospital LOS should be investigated further to clarify the relationship between frailty, time spent in the ICU, and the mortality outcomes of patients who survive their ICU admission. Furthermore, elective and non-elective admissions warrant further investigation, as they represent distinct clinical trajectories that may differentially influence prognosis, resource utilization, and ICU decision-making. Additionally, future studies should also examine the role of specific ICU interventions such as mechanical ventilation, vasopressor therapy, and renal replacement therapy and how their utilization may modify outcomes among nonagenarian patients.

Strengths and limitations

Drawing on 13 years of comprehensive data from the ANZICS Adult Patient Database, encompassing a diverse ICU population across Australia and New Zealand, this study represents one of the largest cohort analyses of frailty among critically ill nonagenarians. Our application of propensity score matching ensured the robust balancing of baseline characteristics between frail and non-frail patients to minimize confounding influences and enhance the internal validity of our comparisons. Methodological rigor was further strengthened through the use of multivariable Cox regression, time-dependent survival modelling, and Fine–Gray competing risks analysis to address proportional hazard violations and better characterize mortality and LOS outcomes. The incorporation of standardized severity adjustment using APACHE III-J scores enhances the reproducibility and reliability of our findings across institutions.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design inherently limits causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables may persist despite matching and multivariable adjustment. Second, while the ANZICS database offers rich clinical detail, its restriction to a single regional registry may limit the generalizability of our results to other healthcare systems globally. Third, the exclusion of patients with missing data and those receiving palliative care prior to ICU admission may introduce selection bias, potentially underrepresenting the most vulnerable or highest risk individuals. Fourth, our definition of frailty relied on registry-based classification rather than prospectively applied clinical frailty assessments, which may lead to misclassification or the underestimation of its effect. Additionally, the dataset lacks information about any treatment modifications introduced during the ICU stay, which could have influenced both escalation decisions and eventual outcomes. We also lacked data on critically ill nonagenarians who were not admitted to the ICU, limiting insights into system-level triage decisions or care received in alternative settings. Additionally, our study was conducted exclusively in Australian and New Zealand ICUs, which are high-resource settings. As such, the findings may not be fully generalizable to health systems with differing ICU structures, resource availability, or therapeutic practices, including the use of mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drugs, or renal replacement therapy.

Finally, our study was limited to short-term in-hospital outcomes and did not evaluate long-term survival, post-discharge functional status, or quality of life. The absence of data on patient preferences, advance care planning, or resuscitation orders further restricts the interpretation of how individual goals of care and shared decision-making may have influenced treatment intensity or clinical trajectories.

Conclusion

Frailty was independently associated with significantly increased ICU and hospital mortality among critically ill nonagenarians, even after adjusting for illness severity and baseline characteristics. Frailty was independently associated with increased risk of ICU and in-hospital mortality among nonagenarians and centenarians, underscoring its prognostic significance across the continuum of critical care. These findings support the routine incorporation of frailty assessments into early risk stratification and ongoing clinical decision-making to inform treatment intensity and align care with individual prognosis. The APACHE III-J score remained an independent predictor of mortality, highlighting its continued value as a complementary tool for physiological risk assessment.

Although frail and non-frail patients had comparable ICU lengths of stay, hospital stays were significantly more prolonged in frail patients, reflecting delayed recovery and greater care complexity. These findings emphasize the need for integrated geriatric care models and anticipatory resource planning within acute care settings. Future research should focus on long-term outcomes, including survival, functional and cognitive trajectories, and post-discharge quality of life. Economic evaluations of frailty-informed care pathways may further guide cost-effective, patient-centered strategies for managing the growing population of older adults requiring intensive care.
