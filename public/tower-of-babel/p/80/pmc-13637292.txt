Association between depression and all-cause, cardiovascular, and non-cardiovascular mortality in the Spanish population: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Jesús Godino-Cruz, Elvira Lara, Francisco Félix Caballero, Beatriz Olaya, Joan Domènech-Abella, Josep Maria Haro, Marta Miret, José Luis Ayuso-Mateos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is among the most prevalent mental disorders worldwide and is associated with an increased risk of mortality from all causes and from specific causes (cardiovascular disease or suicide). The aim of this study was to analyze whether depression is a risk factor for all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality in a representative sample of the Spanish population, considering also the competing risk of depression in both CVD mortality and other causes that are not CVD mortality.MethodsWe used the data of 4,753 individuals from the Edad con Salud study, recruited between 2011 and 2012, and followed up on three occasions. We included sociodemographic, healthy lifestyle, and chronic conditions variables. The association between depression and all-cause mortality was assessed using hazard ratios obtained from Cox regression, and the association between depression with CVD and non-CVD mortality was assessed using sub-hazard ratios from Fine-Gray models for competing risks.ResultsA total of 18% of the sample had depression. Of the 279 participants who died during the study and had their causes of death recorded, 57 died from CVD. After adjustment for the main potential confounders, depression was significantly associated with all-cause (HR = 1.81; 95% CI = 1.23–2.65) and CVD mortality (sHR = 2.01; 95% CI = 1.08–3.72), but was not significantly associated with non-CVD mortality (sHR = 1.32; 95% CI = 0.88–1.99).ConclusionsDepression was associated with an increased risk of all-cause and CVD mortality, but was not significantly associated with non-CVD mortality. This may be due to the heterogeneity of the causes of death of the participants comprising this subgroup. One significant limitation is the small number of cases involving CVD mortality and depression (n = 13). One potential implication of this study is the need to adopt a comprehensive approach to treating depression to address not only mental health, but also actively promote healthy behaviors that reduce the risk of chronic disease and mortality.Trial registration numberNCT03343886 (Last Public Release: 10/10/2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13690-026-02010-1.Keywords: Depression, Mortality, Cardiovascular disease, Cohort study, Competing risk

Contributions to the literature• This study provides valuable insights into the association between depression and the risk of death from all causes and from specific causes such as cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular disease in a cohort of adults in Spain.• It highlights the importance of studying depression comprehensively, considering factors such as physical activity or chronic conditions, to reduce the risk of mortality.• The assessment of cardiovascular mortality risk in people with depression may be overestimated if a competing risks perspective is not used.

Background

Depression is among the most prevalent mental disorders worldwide, affecting approximately 3.8% of the population and 5.7% of adults, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. Furthermore, depression is considered one of the most disabling diseases, surpassing other chronic diseases such as angina, arthritis, asthma, and diabetes in terms of its impact on daily life and productivity [2]. In recent years, the incidence of depression has increased significantly in Spain [3, 4] and worldwide [5].

Several meta-analyses have found that depression is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, with mortality rates approximately twice as high among individuals with depression compared to those without [6–8]. How depression is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality [9], and CVD mortality [10–12] has also been extensively studied. Individuals with depression face a heightened risk of natural mortality compared to those without depression, and it is also a significant risk factor for suicide [6]. Other studies have reported that depression is a critical risk factor for multiple causes of death related to disease, beyond CVD and suicide [9, 10]. Other studies analyzed how depression is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality [11], and CVD mortality [12–14]. These results are consistent with those found in other meta-analyses, in which depression was associated with an increased risk of CVD mortality and non-fatal CVD [15, 16]. The association between depression and CVD is bidirectional, with depression contributing to CVD and CVD-related mortality, and patients with CVD showing higher rates of depressive symptoms than those without CVD [17]. However, research on mortality risk tends to be limited to studying subgroups of patients with specific physical morbidities, such as CVD [8, 18], cancer [19, 20], or diabetes mellitus [21, 22].

Although depression is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality, this effect varies greatly depending on the variables used for adjustment, such as age and chronic conditions, especially for CVD mortality, suggesting possible confounding and overadjustment [23, 24]. Some studies suggest that the presence of chronic conditions (diabetes, hypertension, etc.) may influence the risk of mortality in people with depression [25, 26], especially in people over 60 [27]. For example, people with depression who have chronic conditions such as diabetes have an approximately 50% higher risk of CVD-related mortality and all-cause mortality [28–31]. Prior research has also found that depression is associated with an increased risk of non-CVD mortality in patients with diabetes [29]. Depression is a risk factor for mortality in both genders, but its impact appears to be stronger in men [32], despite women having a higher prevalence of depression. Other lifestyle behaviors, such as smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and poor adherence to depression treatment, are key variables in the relationship between depression and mortality risk [33–35]. Several studies have shown that depression correlates with elevated CVD and non-CVD mortality rates across diverse sociodemographic settings and populations, including urban environments [36, 37], hospitalized patients [17, 29], and individuals with specific medical conditions such as stroke [15, 24] and diabetes [29, 30].

Although depression and mortality show a nominally significant association across all settings and populations evaluated, this effect weakens when structured interviews are used or when adjusting for chronic conditions or health behaviors. This attenuation may reflect overestimation due to publication bias or because most studies do not assess the severity of depression [7]. Therefore, despite the fact that depression and mortality are strongly associated, it cannot be said that depression is the sole cause of mortality, as it may also be due to other health factors, lifestyle, or chronic conditions [38]. Furthermore, most of the previously mentioned studies are predominantly focused on CVD mortality and often neglect to consider mortality from other causes. To avoid biased results, it is necessary to investigate the longitudinal impact of depression on CVD mortality, as well as its effect on mortality from other causes [39]. Our main hypothesis is that, after adjusting for sociodemographic variables, health behaviors, and chronic diseases, depression will be significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. Additionally, we hypothesize that people with depression are at increased risk of mortality from CVD and other causes, but this effect may be attenuated when adjusting for sociodemographic variables, health behaviors, and chronic conditions.

Methods

Sample and procedure

We used data from the Edad con Salud (EcS) cohort study, which includes a nationally representative sample of adults from Spain. Participants were initially assessed between July 2011 and May 2012 and subsequently interviewed on three additional occasions (December 2014–June 2015; April–November 2018; July–December 2022). At baseline, 4,753 participants were interviewed, from which data were extracted for those who died during the study (n = 645) and compared with those who did not (n = 3533). Cases with missing data for any study variable were excluded from all analyses (n = 232), and for the Fine-Gray competing risk models, cases with missing data on cause of death (n = 343) and proxy respondents were also excluded—even though it was known that they had died—to avoid a classification error. Further information on the study design can be found in the article by Lara et al. [40].

Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews using Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing at respondents’ homes. Participants answered a questionnaire initially adapted from the WHO Study on Global Ageing and Adult Health (SAGE) [41], which was translated from English into Spanish using the WHO translation guidelines for assessment instruments. Quality control procedures were implemented during fieldwork [42]. At the beginning of the interview, the interviewer assessed whether the selected person had cognitive limitations that prevented them from correctly understanding the survey questions. From the second wave onwards, the Mini-Mental State Examination [43] screening questionnaire was used to assess these limitations. In such cases, a proxy respondent —someone familiar with the intended participant who answered on their behalf —completed a shortened version of the interview questions. These proxy respondents were excluded from the analyses because not all variables were measured in these participants.

Variables

Depression

We assessed 12-month depression at baseline by asking participants a series of questions about possible depressive symptoms (depressed mood; a marked decrease in interest or pleasure; loss of appetite; insomnia; psychomotor agitation; fatigue or loss of energy; feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt; difficulty thinking or concentrating; and suicidal thoughts, attempts, or plans). Participants were considered to meet the criteria for depressive disorder if they had five or more symptoms lasting for more than two weeks, present most of the day, nearly every day, and included at least one of the core symptoms (depressive mood, markedly diminished interest or pleasure). Participants also self-reported whether they had ever been diagnosed with depression by a medical doctor and when (age). We used the depression module of the WHO Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI) Version 3.0 (CIDI 3.0) [44] at Waves 2, 3, and 4. In these waves, participants reported the presence of a new major depressive episode since the previous interview using the same version of the CIDI. Reappraisal interviews have shown good concordance between the CIDI and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID) in DSM-IV diagnoses [45]. Depression was coded as a time-varying dichotomous variable, such that each participant was initially classified as “no depression” unless a depressive episode was documented at baseline or at a follow-up visit. From that point on, the exposure status was automatically updated to “depression” for subsequent intervals until the final event or censoring [46, 47]. This time-based approach was used to accurately reflect the longitudinal nature of the exposure and avoid immortal survival bias, ensuring that participants at risk were correctly assigned to the appropriate exposure status [48].

Mortality

Data were cross-referenced with the Spanish National Death Index and the National Institute of Statistics, where information was obtained on which participants had died and the causes of death associated with their deaths, following the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes. Vital status was ascertained through the Spanish National Death Index. The cause of death was determined by a nosologist based on the death certificate and classified according to the ICD-10 [49]. Deaths from CVD (n = 74) were classified in group I00-I99, while all other causes of death (infectious and parasitic; tumors; blood/hematopoietic; endocrine/metabolic; mental and behavioral; nervous system; respiratory system; digestive system; genitourinary system; musculoskeletal and connective tissue; and external causes/suicide) were categorized as non-CVD mortality (n = 228). Information on the date and cause of death was obtained from an agreement with the National Statistics Office, and has been previously shown to be complete, accurate, and reliable [50, 51].

Covariates

Sociodemographic variables at baseline included age (continuous, 18–100 years), sex (men/women), and highest educational attainment (less than secondary school/secondary school or higher). Healthy lifestyle variables, such as smoking status (current or former smoker/non-smoker), alcohol consumption (current or former drinker/never drinker), physical activity (high/moderate/low), and chronic conditions (none/one/two or more) were also included in the analyses.

Physical activity was assessed with the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire developed by the WHO for physical activity surveillance. The questionnaire comprises 16 questions and collects information on physical activity participation in three settings as well as sedentary behavior. The domains are activity at work, travel to and from places, and recreational activities. Physical activity was categorized as follows: high physical activity (vigorous-intensity activity on at least 3 days achieving a minimum of 1500 MET [metabolic equivalent] minutes per week or 7 or more days of any combination of walking, moderate- or vigorous-intensity activities achieving a minimum of 3000 MET minutes per week); moderate physical activity (3 or more days of vigorous-intensity activity for at least 20 min per day, 5 or more days of moderate-intensity activity or walking for at least 30 min per day, or 5 or more days of any combination of walking, moderate- or vigorous-intensity activities achieving a minimum of 600 MET minutes per week); and low physical activity (a person not meeting any of the above mentioned criteria).

Participants indicated whether they had received a medical diagnosis for any of the following chronic physical conditions: angina, hypertension, arthritis, asthma, and diabetes both at baseline and during follow-up assessments. Additional questions were included to assess symptoms experienced over the previous 12 months, aiming to identify potential undiagnosed cases. In these cases, algorithms were applied, and a person was considered to have one of these conditions if they met at least the self-reported diagnosis criteria or the diagnostic criteria made according to the symptom criteria [52]. The variable was recoded into three categories according to the number of chronic conditions suffered (No chronic conditions/One chronic condition/Two or more chronic conditions).

Statistical analyses

All analyses in this study were conducted using RStudio version R.4.3.2 [53]; the code and datasets are available upon request to the principal investigators (Contact – Edad con Salud). For the analyses, the proportions and frequencies of the participants’ baseline characteristics were calculated separately for individuals with and without depression. To study statistically significant differences between both groups and considering the categorical nature of the covariates, the chi-square test for categorical variables and the t-test for continuous variables were used for comparing proportions in both groups. Cramer’s V (for categorical variables) and Hedges’ g (for continuous variables) were also reported to provide an effect size measure. In general, values below 0.1 were considered to indicate no association, values between 0.1 and 0.3 suggested a weak association, values between 0.3 and 0.5 were considered moderate, and values above 0.5 indicated a strong association [54].

To determine the risk of all-cause mortality among participants with and without depression, a Kaplan-Meier curve was used to estimate and visualize the probability of survival over time, along with the log-rank test to determine whether the difference between the two groups was statistically significant. To analyze the effect of covariates on the relationship between mortality and depression, Cox proportional hazards models were used to study the risk of all-cause mortality associated with the presence of depression, after adjusting for all covariates. The analyses regarding CVD mortality were performed with the Fine-Gray model for competing risks to assess the marginal probability of the sub-distribution as sub-hazard ratio (sHR), considering non-CVD causes of death as competing events [55]. For the all-cause mortality model, the entire sample of deceased individuals (n = 762) was used, whereas for the Fine-Gray competing risk models, only those deceased individuals with a recorded cause of death were included (n = 378), to avoid classification bias resulting from the overrepresentation of non-CVD mortality cases. To analyze the longitudinal and time-varying nature of depression, both models were used with a time-dependent depression variable [46], in which depression was defined as a figure with entry and exit time points, allowing the exposure status to be updated at each wave. The equation for the hazard function from the Cox proportional hazards model (Appendix 1: S1) and the cumulative hazard function for CVD versus non-CVD mortality from the Fine-Gray model (Appendix 1: S2), adjusted for depression, age, sex, educational level, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and chronic conditions, can be found in the Supplementary Material.

The risk ratios adjusted for all covariates and depression are presented with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The sHRs obtained from the Fine-Gray model describe the relative effect of the covariates on the subdistribution hazard function. Therefore, covariates in these models can also be interpreted as having an effect on the cumulative incidence function or on the probability of events occurring over time [56]. The general formula for the hazard ratio is similar to that of the Cox model, except that the betas in the Cox model were replaced by gammas in the Fine and Gray model. Consequently, gammas can be interpreted similarly to betas estimated from a Cox model, except that the Cox model estimates the effect of covariates in the presence of competing events [57]. The proportional-risk assumption was tested using Schoenfeld’s residual method, which revealed no violation of that assumption (p-value > 0.05) for the all-cause mortality model (Appendix 2: Table S1). For the competing risks models, this assumption was violated (p-value > 0.05) for age and sex in the CVD model (Appendix 2: Table S2 and Figure S1), and for physical activity in the non-CVD model (Appendix 2: Table S3 and Figure S2). However, upon examining the graphical representation of the residuals, it can be seen that that the deviation is very slight and the total residual is not significant; therefore, we can assume that they do not bias the model results [58]. The stability of the model was also verified by examining potential multicollinearity among the variables using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for continuous variables and the Generalized VIF (GVIF) for categorical variables (Table S4). However, no VIF values above 3 were detected, so the existence of potential multicollinearity was ruled out [59]. The linearity of the continuous variables was assessed by visually examining the dispersion of the residuals using the Martingale plot (Appendix 2: Figure S3) [60].

Results

The baseline characteristics of the sample were examined using descriptive analyses for comparing those with and without depression, as shown in Table 1. Compared with individuals without depression (n = 3715), participants with depression (n = 463) had a higher average age (52.54 > 46.62), were more likely to be female (71.92%), had a primary school education or lower (81.64%), smoked more (55.39%), drank less alcohol (63.57%), had a higher level of physical activity (39.97%), and more frequently had two or more physical conditions (52.25%). All comparisons conducted with the χ2 test and t-test were statistically significant, except for smoking status (p-value = 0.25) and alcohol consumption (p-value = 0.69), but the effect sizes were relatively small (Cramer’s V and Hedges’ g values ranged from 0.09 to 0.15).

For all-cause mortality, depression showed a significant direct relationship with mortality (HR = 1.81; 95% CI = 1.23–2.65). Furthermore, the Fine-Gray competing risk model (Table 2) showed that depression was significantly associated with CVD mortality (sHR = 2.01; 95% CI = 1.08–3.72). For non-CVD mortality, depression was not significantly associated with non-CVD mortality (sHR = 1.32; 95% CI = 0.88–1.99). The power of the Cox regression model for all-cause mortality was 61.3%, whereas in the competitive risks model, the power was 45.2% for CVD mortality and 13.4% for non-CVD mortality.

Discussion

The main findings of this study indicate that depression is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality in the Spanish adult population. Previous studies have reported that depression is associated with a 50–80% increase in the risk of all-cause mortality in the Spanish population [61, 62]. These data support the results obtained in this study, which found that depression was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality by 81% compared to not having depression. We can therefore conclude that depression is associated with an increased risk of premature mortality, possibly through its association with adverse health behaviors or substance use [63]. Although depression has been reported to double the risk of CVD mortality, most studies estimate this increased risk to be in the range of 40–60% [23, 34, 64]. The higher estimates observed in our analyses may be inflated, potentially due to the relatively small number of participants with CVD in our sample [65]. This significantly elevated risk of CVD mortality in people with depression may be explained by a link between risk behaviors and certain inflammatory factors found in patients with depression, which are associated with an increased risk of CVD and CVD mortality [64].

However, depression was not found to be a significant risk factor for non-CVD mortality, which may be explained by the high heterogeneity of causes included in the non‑CVD mortality category. Nonetheless, studies specifically focusing on individuals with depression—rather than on general population samples such as ours—have reported an increased risk of mortality from non-CVD causes, such as suicide or other external causes [6, 66].

Regarding which treatments or guidelines can be used to reduce mortality associated with depression, several studies have found that treatment with antidepressants and electroconvulsive therapy can reduce the risk of all-cause mortality and suicide mortality [67]. Psychotherapy has also been shown to improve depressive symptoms and reduce the risk of all-cause mortality, CVD [68], and suicide [69]. Similarly, among coronary patients with depression, those who completed a cardiac rehabilitation program were found to have fewer and less severe depressive symptoms than those who did not complete the program, which led to a reduction in mortality among patients with depression [70]. Furthermore, there are digital health intervention programs that have shown moderate effect sizes in reducing depressive symptoms [71, 72], particularly when these programs were hybrid in nature, combining technology-based applications with face-to-face interventions led by mental health professionals [73].

The strengths of this study are the use of a large, nationally representative sample of adults, a longitudinal design that allowed for the examination of temporal relationships, and the use of competing risk models to account for long-term mortality, which in turn allow for a more accurate exploration of the relationship between different factors and depression than traditional survival methods [56]. Furthermore, all the variables in the study were measured using validated and standardized instruments.

However, this study has several limitations. First, for the all-cause mortality model, the statistical power did not reach the acceptable threshold of 75–80%, although it was moderately close to it. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, as there is only a 61.3% probability of detecting a true effect. In addition, for the competitive risks models, the power for CVD mortality was very low (45.2%) and for the non-CVD model it was negligible (13.4%), which may be due to the limited number of CVD deaths among participants with depression in our study (n = 13) and the high variance in causes of non-CVD death. Therefore, the results of the competitive risk models should be interpreted with great caution, as there is a very high risk of committing a Type II error in both models. Second, healthy habits, depression, and chronic conditions were self-reported by participants, which may imply recall bias. However, previous evidence has shown that self-reported health measures can effectively assess mortality risk in comparison with objective health measures [74]. Thirdly, differences in the severity of depression were not controlled for in these analyses, which may compromise the interpretation of the results. Fourthly, this study did not account for the effects of variables such as loneliness and social support [75], which are associated with worsening depressive symptoms and an increased risk of mortality [76]. Finally, the inverse association between the number of comorbidities and mortality suggests that the variable for chronic conditions does not capture the actual clinical complexity, partly because the study did not account for whether participants were receiving treatment for those chronic conditions. This suggests the need to promote new longitudinal studies on the association between depression and all-cause and specific-cause mortality in representative samples. Such studies should incorporate further clinical and environmental factors to better understand the links between depression and mortality risk. There is also a need to advance consensus on the measures to be used in population studies to assess mental disorders, mortality, and their risk factors.

These findings might have several implications. The relationship between depression and mortality decreases considerably after adjusting for other factors, so more complex, multifactorial models may be necessary to examine it. All of this reinforces the need for a comprehensive approach to managing patients with depression, one that not only addresses mental health but also actively promotes positive health behaviors to reduce the risk of a wide range of chronic diseases and mortality from various causes. Further research should employ a larger sample size, with more homogeneous mortality groups to ensure greater statistical power and compare individuals with clinical depression to a control group to examine the impact of depression on all-cause and cause-specific mortality under greater experimental control.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this representative sample of the adult population in Spain, depression was associated with an 81% increased risk of all-cause-mortality. Depression also was associated with a 101% increased risk of cardiovascular mortality, while in the case of non-cardiovascular mortality depression did not show a significant association. However, given the small size of certain subgroups in competing risk models and the limited statistical power of this sample, these findings should be interpreted with caution and not as a definitive causal relationship.
