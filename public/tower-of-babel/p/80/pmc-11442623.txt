Effects of passion fruit peel (Passiflora edulis) pectin and red yeast (Sporodiobolus pararoseus) cells on growth, immunity, intestinal morphology, gene expression, and gut microbiota in Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus)
Authors: Anisa Rilla Lubis, Nguyen Vu Linh, Orranee Srinual, Camilla Maria Fontana, Khambou Tayyamath, Supreya Wannavijit, Punika Ninyamasiri, Toungporn Uttarotai, Wanaporn Tapingkae, Yuthana Phimolsiripol, Hien V Van Doan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the effects of dietary supplementation with passion fruit peel pectin (Passiflora edulis) and red yeast cell walls (Sporidiobolus pararoseus) on growth performance, immunity, intestinal morphology, gene expression, and gut microbiota of Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus). Nile tilapia with an initial body weight of approximately 15 ± 0.06 g were fed four isonitrogenous (29.09–29.94%), isolipidic (3.01–4.28%), and isoenergetic (4119–4214 Cal/g) diets containing 0 g kg−1 pectin or red yeast cell walls (T1 - Control), 10 g kg−1 pectin (T2), 10 g kg−1 red yeast (T3), and a combination of 10 g kg−1 pectin and 10 g kg−1 red yeast (T4) for 8 weeks. Growth rates and immune responses were assessed at 4 and 8 weeks, while histology, relative immune and antioxidant gene expression, and gut microbiota analysis were conducted after 8 weeks of feeding. The results showed that the combined supplementation (T4) significantly enhanced growth performance metrics, including final weight, weight gain, specific growth rate, and feed conversion ratio, particularly by week 8, compared to T1, T2, and T3 (P < 0.05). Immunological assessments revealed increased lysozyme and peroxidase activities in both skin mucus and serum, with the T4 group showing the most pronounced improvements. Additionally, antioxidant and immune-related gene expression, including glutathione peroxidase (GPX), glutathione reductase (GSR), and interleukin-1 (IL1), were upregulated in the gut, while intestinal morphology exhibited improved villus height and width. Gut microbiota analysis indicated increased alpha and beta diversity, with a notable rise in beneficial phyla such as Actinobacteriota and Firmicutes in the supplemented groups. These findings suggest that the combined use of pectin and red yeast cell walls as prebiotics in aquaculture can enhance the health and growth of Nile tilapia, offering a promising alternative to traditional practices. Introduction

Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) is cultured worldwide; its appeal lies in its rapid growth, resilience, exceptional flavour, and its market demand1. Its global production reached 4.47 million tonnes in 2020 and is projected to rise to 7.3 million tonnes by 2030, driven by advancements in intensive farming methods2. Tilapia aquaculture aims to produce eco-friendly and robust products3; however, the sustainability is jeopardized by practices such as higher stocking densities and the overuse of antibiotics4. Increased stocking densities can result in increased levels of stress through competition for nutrients and space, which can result in stunted growth, an increased predisposition to bacterial infection, and an increased probability of significant financial losses for producers5. To manage and control infections, antibiotics are frequently applied, which, if used promiscuously, can result in harm to aquatic life and lead to antibiotic resistance6. These issues highlight the need to explore alternative approaches for sustainable Nile tilapia farming.

Prebiotics are “a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit”7. They are important in maintaining health as they can be fermented by the beneficial intestinal microflora of the host8. Prebiotics serve as food for beneficial bacteria like bifidobacteria and lactobacilli9. The fermentation of prebiotics by these bacteria results in the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which play a crucial role in improving gut health, enhancing immune function, and reducing inflammation 10,11. In this context, agricultural by-products, which are abundant in dietary fibre, are recognized as functional substances capable of mitigating diseases associated with alterations in intestinal microflora12,13. Utilizing these by-products for industrial purposes not only addresses the challenge of proper waste disposal but also provides farmers with an additional source of revenue and create jobs opportunities14. Additionally, the yeast cell wall emerges as another promising prebiotic15,16. Constituting 10–25% of the dried yeast cell mass and rich in β-glucans and chitin, the yeast cell wall has been identified as a potential prebiotic with significant health benefits15,17.

Passion fruit (Passiflora edulis Sims) is extensively grown in tropical and subtropical climates18, with production of 1.5 million tons per annum19. The passion fruit peel constitutes approximately 50–55% of the entire fruit, serving as its primary by-product20. Peels from passion fruits are abundant in important nutrients, including fibre, pectin, carotenoids, polyphenols, and ascorbic acid, which can be used to produce functional food and feed additives21. A naturally occurring polysaccharide complex termed pectin, which is well-known for its prebiotic properties, can be detected in the pith of the peel of passion fruit22,23. Numerous health benefits, including the ability to decrease cholesterol and blood sugar, prevent cancer, bind metals, and promote wound healing, have been documented24,25. Therefore, utilizing passion fruit peel not only helps in reducing agricultural waste but also provides an opportunity to create value-added products with significant health benefits26. This approach supports sustainable practices and contributes to the development of functional foods and feed additives. Specifically, extracting pectin from passion fruit peel and using it as a feed additive for Nile tilapia can enhance the nutritional quality of the fish diet, potentially improving growth performance and overall health, while also promoting environmentally friendly aquaculture practices27.

The yeast cell wall is highly valued in aquaculture for its unique composition and biological properties28. It is rich in β-glucans, mannoproteins, and chitin, which have strong immunostimulatory effects29,30. These components boost the immune response of fish and shrimp, increasing their resistance to diseases and reducing the need for antibiotics31. Additionally, they improve gut health by modulating intestinal microflora, leading to better nutrient absorption and overall growth performance. For example, optimal level of yeast cell wall (YCW) in gibel carp (Carassius auratus gibelio) was 0.4% 31. In tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), the dietary inclusion of 0.2% YCW boosts non-specific immune responses, upregulates immune-related gene expression, and enhances disease resistance32. However, there is currently no information available regarding the effects of red yeast cell wall on the growth and immune response in fish. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the “Effects of passion fruit peel (Passiflora edulis) pectin and red yeast (Sporodiobolus pararoseus) cells on growth, immunity, intestinal morphology, gene expression, and gut microbiota in Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus).

Materials and methods

Production of red yeast cell

Preparation of red yeast

The red yeast cell walls (YCWs) used in this study was prepared following the methods of Chaiyaso and Manowattana33. Red yeast (S. pararoseus KM281507) with the purity of 100% was acquired from the Division of Biotechnology, Faculty of Agro-Industry, Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai, Thailand. It was cultured in a medium containing 0.10% of yeast extract, 5.50% of crude glycerol, 0.55% of potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4), 0.53% of ammonium sulphate ((NH4)2SO4), 0.37% of dipotassium hydrogen phosphate (K2HPO4), 0.05% of magnesium sulphate heptahydrate (MgSO4.7H2O), 0.02% of manganese (II) sulphate monohydrate (MnSO4. H2O), and 0.05% of sodium chloride (NaCl). The starting pH was set to 5.63 before sterilisation at 121 °C for 20 min. The yeast was grown at incubator shaker ((LSI-3016R, Labtech, Gyeonggi, Korea) at 25 °C for 5 d, followed by storage at 4 °C for 14 d for autolysis. The red yeast was then mixed with 16% (v/v) maize starch, dried at 60 °C for 24 h, resulting in the powdered prebiotic supplement.

Red yeast cells production (RYCs)

RYCs were prepared using the method of Tapingkae, et al.34. The cultured medium with RYCs was refrigerated at 4 °C for 14 d to facilitate autolysis and settling. The supernatant from the settled RYCs was then discarded. Spray-dried RYCs were produced by introducing phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) to the RYCs for glucan content analysis. The 1,3:1,6-β-glucan content of the spray-dried RYCs (without the addition of maltodextrin) was measured using an enzymatic assay kit (β-glucan assay kit: yeast and mushroom, Megazyme Ltd., Wicklow, Ireland) following the manufacturer’s instructions (Mushroom and yeast β-glucan assay procedure, K-YBGL 08/18, Megazyme Ltd., Wicklow, Ireland). The red yeast cell was found to contain 5.73% (w/w) β-glucan34.

Preparation of pectin from passion fruit peel

Passion fruit peel was procured from a market in Chiang Mai province, Thailand, for pectin extraction. Upon arrival, the peel underwent oven-drying at 60 °C for 48 h, followed by grinding and filtration through a 40-mesh sieve. Passion fruit peel powder was kept at 4 °C until next step. Pectin isolation from the passion fruit peel followed the method of Van Doan and colleagues35 with some modifications. In a Pyrex beaker with a volume of 500 mL, 25 g of powdered peel was meticulously mixed with 422.5 mL of distilled water, which was then adjusted with sulfuric acid to achieve a pH of 1.4. The blend was microwaved for 169 s at 422 W power, subsequently cooled down and aliquots were centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 3 min in a 50 mL tube. The resultant supernatant was collected and mixed with an equivalent volume of 95% (v/v) ethanol, leading to the precipitation of pectin. The pectin was then subjected to three washes with 95% ethanol. Ultimately, the pectin was dried at 50 °C until a constant weight was attained. Purity of pectin (80%) was checked at the Faculty of Agro-Industry, Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai, Thailand. The prebiotic activity of pectin was determined following the method of Wongkaew, et al.36.

Preparation of experimental diets and biofloc water

Basal feed was formulated according to the method proposed by Van Doan, et al.37 (Table 1). In addition to the standard ingredients, two supplements, pectin derived from passion fruit peel and red yeast powder, were included to the basal diet. Four isonitrogenous (29.47% crude protein), isolipidic (3.37% crude lipid), and isoenergetic (4.17 kcal/g) diets were prepared using different rates of supplementation as follows: T1, 0 g kg−1 (control); T2, 10 g kg−1 pectin; T3, 10 g kg−1 red yeast powder; and T4, 10 g kg−1 pectin and 10 g kg−1 red yeast powder. The amount of supplements used was modified from previous research38,39.

The feed ingredients were finely ground and thoroughly blended with soybean oil and water to form a dough. This dough was then extruded into floating pellets using equipment from Siam Farm Services Co., Ltd. The floating pellets were subsequently dried at 50 °C until achieving a moisture content of 10% and then stored in airtight bags at 4 °C for up to 2.5 further use40.

The floc medium for each tank was prepared three weeks before the experiment, adhering to established protocols41. Initially, each tank received coarse salt (400 g), molasses (5 g), dolomite (5 g), and control feed (2 g) to stimulate floc formation. Throughout the study, the C: N ratio was maintained at 15:1 by regularly adding molasses (40% carbon) as the primary carbon source, following previous procedure42. Molasses was added every two days, two hours post-feeding. The C: N ratio was monitored by measuring the residual nitrogen levels in the tanks and analysing the carbon and nitrogen content of the feed 41,31.

Proximate analysis of experimental diets

The nutritional composition of each diet was analysed using standardized methods outlined by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists43. The dry matter (DM) content was determined by oven-drying the samples at 105 °C for six hours (AOAC 2001.12). The nitrogen content was quantified using the Kjeldahl titrimetric digestion method, and crude protein was subsequently calculated by multiplying the nitrogen content by 6.25 (AOAC 954.01). Ash content analysis involved incinerating the samples in a muffle furnace at 550 °C (AOAC 923.03). Fiber content was measured using the enzymatic-gravimetric method (AOAC 978.10). Lipid content was determined according to the acid hydrolysis method, followed by Soxhlet extraction (AOAC 920.85). The gross energy (GE) was calculated as 5.72*CP + 9.50*EE + 4.79*CF + 4.17*NFE from the analysed content (g kg−1 DM) of CP, EE, CF and NFE44.

Experimental design

Mono-sex Nile tilapia fingerlings for this feeding experiment were purchased from Chiang Mai Aquatic Development Farm Co., Ltd., Chiang Mai, Thailand. The fish underwent a 2-week acclimatisation period to adapt to the experimental conditions before the commencement of the feeding trial. Subsequently, Nile tilapia fingerlings (n = 320) with an average weight of 15.19 ± 0.06 g were randomly distributed into 16 glass tanks with a capacity of 150 L (n = 20). The tanks were arranged in a completely randomised design (CRD) with 4 replications per treatment. The fish were fed two times daily (09:00 and 16:00) at a rate of 3% of their body weight over the course of 8 weeks.

Growth performance

The weight of all fish in each tank was recorded on day 0, week 4, and week 8 of the feeding trial. The fish were fasted for 24 h prior to each weighing. Growth parameters were calculated using the methodology outlined in a previous study40, as follows:

Innate immune response

Sample collection

At the 4-week and 8-week marks, eight fish per treatment were randomly selected for the sampling of skin mucus and blood serum. Skin mucus was collected after administering anaesthesia with 5 mL L-1 of clove oil, following the method described by Ross, et al.45. Briefly, the fish were placed in a plastic bag containing 10 mL of 50 mM NaCl (Merck, Germany) and gently massaged for 1 min. The mucus was collected in 15 mL Falcon tubes and then centrifuged at 1,500 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of supernatant was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Serum was sampled following the method of Van Doan, et al.37. Briefly, 1 mL of blood was drawn from the caudal vein of each fish using a 1 mL syringe. The samples were then placed into new, sterilised tubes without anticoagulants. After collection, the blood samples were left at room temperature for one hour before being refrigerated at 4 °C for four hours. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The serum was then collected with a micropipette and stored at -80 °C until further use. After sample collection, the fish were gently returned to their designated tanks.

Lysozyme activity

The activity of skin mucus and serum lysozyme was analysed following the methods described by Parry Jr, et al.46. Serum samples (25 µL) and skin mucus samples (100 µL) samples were aliquoted into 96-well plates in triplicates. Then, 175 µL of Micrococcus lysodeikticus (0.3 mg mL−1 dissolved in 0.1 M citrate phosphate buffer, pH 5.8) was added to each well. The turbidity was monitored by reading absorbance at 540 nm every 30 s for 5 min at 25 °C using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA). Lysozyme activity was determined by analysing the decline in optical density (OD) and comparing it to a standard curve created using defined concentrations of hen egg-white lysozyme (ranging from 0 to 20 µg mL−1, Sigma Aldrich, USA). The lysozyme activity was expressed as µg mL−1 serum.

Peroxidase activity

The quantification of peroxidase activity was performed following the method described by Quade and Roth47. Briefly, 5 µL of either skin mucus or serum was added to a 96-wells plate, followed by 45 µL of Hank’s balanced salt solution without Ca2+ or Mg2+, in triplicate. Subsequently, 100 µL of a reaction mixture, consisting of 40 mL distilled water, 10 mL hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (30% - Sigma Aldrich), and one tablet of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (Sigma Aldrich) was added to each well. The reaction was stopped upon colour development by adding 50 µL of 2M H2SO4. The optical density (OD) was then measured at 450 nm using a plate reader, with control wells containing neither serum nor skin mucus. One unit of peroxidase activity was defined as the amount causing a 1.0 absorbance increase at 450 nm. Peroxidase activity was expressed as U mL−1.

Alternative complement pathway activity (ACH50)

The activity of the alternative complement pathway (ACH50) was assessed using the method described by Yano48. Rabbit red blood cells (R-RBCs), generously supplied by the Department of Animal and Aquatic Sciences at Chiang Mai University, were washed with 1X PBS and 0.01 M ethylene glycol tetra-acetic acid-magnesium-gelatine veronal buffer (0.01 M-EGTA-Mg-GVB). The R-RBCs were resuspended in 0.01 M EGTA-Mg-GVB at a concentration of 2 × 108 cells mL−1. To prepare the hemolysate, 100 µL of R-RBC suspension was mixed with 3.4 mL of distilled water, and the absorbance was measured at 414 nm. Adjustments were made to the suspension to achieve an absorbance of approximately 0.740, using distilled water as a blank.

In the ACH50 assay, 100 µL of serum was subjected to successive two-fold dilutions in 0.01 M-EGTA-Mg-GVB and kept on ice. Following this, 100 µL of R-RBC suspension was added to each dilution, and the mixture was incubated at 20 °C for 1.5 h with intermittent shaking. The reaction was stopped by adding 3.15 mL of cold normal saline solution, followed by centrifugation at 1600×g for 5 min. After centrifugation, 100 µL of the supernatant from each tube was transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 414 nm. Haemolysis assessed by calculating the ratio of the adjusted 414 nm absorbance value to that of the 100% haemolysis control. The degree of haemolysis and serum volume were then plotted on log-log paper. The serum volume required to lyse 50% of the RBCs was used to determine the ACH50 unit activity, calculated as: ACH50 (units/mL) = 1/K x r x ½, where K is the serum volume at 50% haemolysis, r is the serum dilution factor, and ½ is the correction factor.

Gene expression analysis by quantitative PCR (qPCR)

To evaluate immune and antioxidant gene expressions, 8 fish per treatment were randomly selected. The fish were anaesthetised with clover oil before approximately 0.5 g samples of the gut and spleen were collected. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Life Technologies) and the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit. RNA quantity and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer at A260/280 nm. Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis with the iScriptTM cDNA Synthesis Kit from BIO-RAD49. The qPCR analysis and thermal cycling conditions were performed as previously described50. Target gene mRNA expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method51, with β-actin serving as the internal control reference. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Table 2.

Histology

For histology analysis, 8 fish per treatment were randomly selected following the method of Emam, et al.52. The guts were dissected and preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin. The tissues were then processed automatically, undergoing a series of steps including progressive dehydration in ethanol, clarification in xylene, and embedding in paraffin. Sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm using a microtome (Leica Biosystems, Germany) and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), following the fixation and staining procedures described by Bancroft and Gamble53. The slides were examined under a compound microscope (CX21, Olympus Cooperation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital video camera (Motic MC 2000) at 20x magnification. Villus height (µm), villus width (µm), crypt depth (µm), muscularis layer (µm), and the villus height to crypt depth (VH: CD) ratio were measured in 4 randomly selected fieds per fish using ImageJ software v.154d (NIH)54.

Intestinal microbiota analysis

Gut sample

Intestinal microbiota analysis was conducted using intestinal samples from 8 fish per treatment. The fish were euthanised with clove oil, and the gut was carefully removed. A segment of approximately 2 cm, containing intestinal content, was collected55. The intestinal segments were placed into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube filled with 1X PBS, kept on ice, and sealed with parafilm. The samples were then sent to Gibthai Co., Ltd., situated in Bangkok, Thailand, for DNA extraction. The QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA) was used for extraction, following the manufacturer’s instructions. After extraction, the DNA from each sample was subjected to sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene, specifically targeting the V3–V4 regions. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform by Macrogen (Macrogen Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea)56.

Bioinformatics analysis

Bioinformatics analysis of the microbiome was conducted using QIIME 2 version 2022.2 57. Raw sequence data was demultiplexed using the q2-demux plugin and reads with a maxEE > 3.0 were filtered out through DADA2 (via q2‐dada2) denoising. Alpha and beta diversity metrics, along with Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) plots, were generated using q2‐diversity, with rarefaction applied to 31,357 reads per sample. Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were taxonomically classified using the classify‐sklearn naive Bayes taxonomy classifier, based on the Greengenes 99% OTUs reference sequences. Alpha diversity was statistically evaluated using Kruskal-Wallis test, while beta diversity was analysed using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. The STAMP software58 was utilised to identify genera with significant changes in relative abundance due to the treatments. Statistical significance of these differences using a two-sided Welch’s t-test. Venn diagrams were generated using InteractiVenn (http://www.interactivenn.net/).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was first assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Parameters including growth performance, immunological responses, intestinal morphology, and gene expression levels were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA. Statistical analyses were conducted using Origin Pro (Version 2023b, OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Significant differences among group means were identified using Fisher’s LSD test. Results are presented as the mean ± SEM with statistical significance recognised at P < 0.05.

Results

Growth performance

The growth performance results indicated the positive effects of adding red yeast cell walls (RYCs) and passion fruit-derived pectin to the tilapia diet compared to the control (Table 3). By the 4-week mark, the final weight (FW) showed a significant increase in groups T2 and T4 compared to the control group (P = 0.0006). At week 8, all treatment groups exhibited significantly higher weights than the control, with the mixed prebiotic T4 group showing the most favourable outcome. Similar trends were observed for weight gain (WG). At week 4, T4 group achieved the highest specific growth rate (SGR), significantly surpassing both the control and T3 group, with the T2 group also outperforming the control (P = 0.0006). By week 8, all treatment groups displayed superior SGR compared to the control group (P < 0.001), with the mixed prebiotic groups outperforming those supplemented with single prebiotic.

Innate immune response

Lysozyme activity

The results shown in Fig. 1 demonstrated increased lysozyme activities in the skin mucus and blood serum of Nile tilapia after 4 and 8 weeks. Discussion

In this study, supplementing juvenile Nile tilapia diets with prebiotics, specifically red yeast cell walls (RYCs) and passion fruit-derived pectin, led to significant improvements in growth performance metrics, such as final weight (FW), weight gain (WG), specific growth rate (SGR), feed conversion ratio (FCR), and survival rate (SR) compared to the control. The benefits observed may be attributed to the unique properties of pectin, as soluble fibre known for producing short-chain fatty acids like butyrate, propionate, and acetate during fermentation59. These metabolites serve as an energy source, promote protein synthesis, and support anabolic processes that enhance growth60. On the other hand, red yeast is rich in bioactive carotenoids, β-glucan, and mannan-oligosaccharides (MOS)54, which has been shown to improve growth performance and feeding efficiency in tilapia38. The combination of these prebiotics elicited synergistic effects, yielding more significant growth improvements than individual supplements11, which aligns with previous research indicating that prebiotics can positively influence growth in various aquatic species61–66. When analysing the results across the 4-week and 8-week time points, it becomes evident that the dose and duration of supplementation play crucial roles in the observed outcomes. By week 4, the T4 group, which received a combination of prebiotics, already exhibited superior growth metrics, suggesting that even at an early stage, the combined prebiotics had a potent effect on growth67. However, by week 8, all treatment groups showed significant improvements over the control, with the T4 group consistently outperforming others. This suggests a potential dose-dependent effect, where the combination of prebiotics not only sustains but also amplifies the growth benefits over time. The significant improvement in FCR by week 8 further supports the hypothesis that prolonged supplementation enhances feed utilization efficiency, contributing to the sustained growth observed in the treatment groups68,69.

The immune response, assessed through lysozyme, peroxidase, and ACH50 activities, also demonstrated notable enhancements in the treatment groups. Lysozyme, a key component of the innate immune system70, showed increased activity in both skin mucus and serum across all treatment groups at both time points, with the T4 group displaying the most pronounced effects. The sustained elevation of lysozyme activity in the T4 group, particularly in serum by week 8, indicates a prolonged immune-stimulatory effect of the combined prebiotics. This suggests that the synergistic action of pectin and red yeast not only enhances innate immune defence mechanisms but may also provide longer-lasting protection against potential infections.

Peroxidase activity followed a similar trend, with significant increases observed in both skin mucus and serum at weeks 4 and 8. The T4 group again demonstrated superior peroxidase activity, particularly in serum by week 8, highlighting the effectiveness of the combined prebiotics in bolstering antioxidant defences over time. The ACH50 activity, representing the alternative complement pathway, was significantly elevated in all treatment groups by week 4, with the T4 group maintaining the highest levels by week 8. This consistent enhancement across multiple immune parameters suggests that the combined prebiotics exert a cumulative effect on immune function, potentially through the activation of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors and dectin-1, which are known to recognize β-glucan and other bioactive components in red yeast71. On the other hand, pectin can interact with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as galectin-3 and Toll-like receptors 2 and 4 (TLRs)72,73, thereby modulating innate immunity. Our findings align with previous studies conducted on Caspian trout (Salmo trutta caspius)61, shabout (Tor grypus)63, and hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus ♀ × Epinephelus lanceolatus ♂)66.

The glutathione defence system eliminates H2O2 through coordinated actions of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and glutathione reductase (GSR)74. Glutathione peroxidase converts H2O2 into water by oxidising glutathione (GSH) to glutathione disulfide, while glutathione reductase subsequently regenerates GSH by oxidising NADPH75. Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) are detoxification enzymes that conjugate xenobiotics, aiding in their elimination from the body76. In this study, the combination of pectin and red yeast cell walls significantly upregulated intestinal antioxidant genes, whereas the individual supplements downregulated GSTα and IL8, respectively. No changes were observed in the spleen. Our findings are consistent with those reported by Xu, et al.67 who found that prebiotic mixtures increased the expression of GSTα, GPX, and GSR in Nile tilapia. This suggests that prebiotic mixtures are more effective in modulating the glutathione system, offering enhanced protection against oxidative stress over the feeding trial77. Cytokines are immune signalling molecules that recruit phagocytes to clear infections through inflammation78. The pro-inflammatory cytokine IL1 plays a crucial role in modulating innate immunity and inflammatory cascades79, while IL8, produced by macrophages, stimulates chemotaxis and phagocytosis80. Lipopolysaccharide binding protein (LBP) is essential for innate responses against Gram-negative bacteria81. In this study, mixed prebiotics significantly upregulated immune genes, although the effect on LBP was not statistically significant. Notably, supplementation with the diet with only red yeast cell walls alone resulted in the downregulation of IL8. This suggests that the combined prebiotics positively impact the immune system of tilapia by promoting the upregulation of genes associated with immune function. Supporting our findings, previous studies have shown that yeast-derived compounds can both upregulate38,82 and downregulate cytokines83. Pectin has been shown to modulate antioxidant and anti-inflammatory signalling via the Nrf2 (nuclear factor-erythroid 2 p45-related factor 2) and NF-κB (nuclear factor-kappa B) pathways84, a capability also observed with β-glucan85. NF-κB regulates DNA transcription, cytokine expression (including IL1 and IL8) and various cellular processes86, while Nrf2 is involved in maintaining cellular GSH homeostasis and managing oxidative stress mechanisms87. Overall, the combination of the two prebiotics appears to activate key immune and antioxidant pathways, which are known to be individually stimulated by pectin and red yeast cell walls compounds.

Maintaining the morphological integrity of the gut is essential for preserving its normal functions88. Dietary prebiotics play a vital role in enhancing gut health in cultured fish by improving nutrient utilisation, mucosal epithelium condition, and overall intestinal function89. This study demonstrated that supplementation with pectin and red yeast cell walls significantly increased the height and width of intestinal villi. Similarly, S. pararoseus supplementation has been shown to improve villus height, width, muscularis thickness, and crypt depth following a mycotoxin challenge90. Yeast supplementation has also been reported to increase villus height in broiler chickens91. Additionally, mannan oligosaccharides have been found to positively affect various parameters of intestinal morphology in several fish species92–94. Although the increase in the villus height-to-crypt depth (VH: CD) ratio observed with dietary prebiotics was not statistically significant, it is associated with improved nutrient digestion, absorption and growth performance95. Therefore, the diets supplemented with the two prebiotics in this study led to enhancements in Nile tilapia intestinal morphology, contributing to improved growth.

Increased gut microbiota diversity is indicate of a more robust and resilient microbial community96, which can enhance diet assimilation97. Our investigation showed that both alpha and beta diversity improved with the administration of mixed prebiotics compared to the control group. Pectin has been shown to influence gut microbiota diversity and composition98, promoting homeostasis by stimulating growth, activity, and epithelial attachment72, mediated through fermentation and the production of short-chain fatty acids99. Yeast supplementation also increased alpha diversity in Nile tilapia microbiota100, suggesting that both prebiotics effectively modulate the gut microbial community. However, it is the enrichment of beneficial species, rather than diversity alone, that has a more significant impact101. The findings from this study indicate that the dominant phyla were Actinobacteriota, Fusobacteriota, Proteobacteriota, Firmicutes, and Planctomycetota, consistent with the observations of Kathia, et al.102 and Abakari, et al.103, although Proteobacteriota and Bacteroidetes were found to be more dominant in biofloc systems104,105. Actinobacteriota plays a crucial role in floc formation and pathogen protection106. Its increased abundance in the treated groups suggests pectin and red yeast cell walls support the growth of beneficial gut bacteria. While the prevalence of Fusobacteriota declined with supplementation, it remained highest in the control group, consistent with previous reports of its dominance in tilapia97,107 and control group108, indicating its natural occurrence. Planctomycetota, which also prevalent in biofloc system, play key roles in carbon and nitrogen cycling104. Their increased presence in the fish fed the T4 diet suggests improved water quality. Additionally, Verrucomicrobiota, known for their ability to degrade polysaccharides109; showed increased abundance with supplementation, indicating enhanced metabolism and growth compared to the control.

STAMP analysis reveals subtle changes in microbial communities that may not be apparent in broader abundance plots. In this study, pectin supplementation led to an increase in Nakamurella, a polysaccharide-accumulating bacterium capable of utilising various sugars for growth110,111. The provision of carbohydrates, such as molasses in biofloc systems112, likely contributed to the enrichment of Nakamurella with pectin supplementation. Hyphomicrobium, a bacterium associated with denitrification113, increased in the T3 and T4 groups, possibly due to the presence of methanol in crude glycerol used for yeast production114,115. This increase may have contributed to improved water quality through enhanced denitrification processes. The abundance of Mycobacterium significantly increased in the T4, likely due to higher dietary lipid intake. Previous studies have shown that cholesterol-rich diets can lead to an increase in Mycobacterium in tilapia116, which is linked to cholesterol and bile acid catabolism117. The T4 group also exhibited elevated levels of beneficial bacteria such as Bacillus and Paenibacillus, both of which are known to enhance tilapia growth and immunity118. Overall, pectin and red yeast cell walls modified the microbiota by promoting favourable microbes, leading to improvements in water quality, growth performance, and immune status in the fish.

Conclusion

In summary, the combined supplementation of 10 g kg−1 pectin and 10 g kg−1 red yeast cell walls has proven to be beneficial biofloc-cultured Nile tilapia. This supplementation enhances growth performance, boosts innate immunity, upregulates antioxidant and immune-related gene expression, and includes positive changes in intestinal morphology and gut microbiota.
