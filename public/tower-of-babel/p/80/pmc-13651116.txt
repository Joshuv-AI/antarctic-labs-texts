Phenological synchrony, parental contribution, and effective pollen dispersal patterns affect the genetic composition of Cupressus funebris dwarf seed orchard progenies
Authors: Hao-bo Zhao, Sen Cao, Zhen Zhang, Guo-qing Jin, Zhong-ping Feng, Zhi-chun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCupressus funebris is a pivotal conifer species for timber production and ecological restoration in subtropical regions. While dwarf seed orchards (DSO) created via apical pruning facilitate intensive management and cone harvesting, their impact on the reproductive success and genetic integrity of offspring remains poorly understood. This study evaluated the interplay between flowering phenology, parental reproductive success, and effective pollen dispersal dynamics in a DSO of C. funebris.ResultsOur multi-year observations reveal that phenological asynchrony in DSO is driven by both clonal genotypic variations and stochastic climatic fluctuations (temperature and precipitation). High-resolution paternity analysis using SSR markers demonstrated a significant deviation from the idealized random mating model, characterized by unbalanced gametic contributions (approx. 24% of clones dominating > 50% of the progeny pool). Notably, while the dwarf architecture did not significantly restrict horizontal effective mating distances, it vertically compressed the canopy, creating a denser zone of reproductive structures. This structural condensation is associated with an increased probability of geitonogamy (self-pollination), corresponding to a higher selfing rate in the dwarfed orchard (27.81%) compared to the un-dwarfed counterparts (18.30%), as determined using a relaxed 80% confidence interval to maintain sufficient spatial modeling resolution. Despite these imbalances, the progeny population maintained a high level of allelic richness (Na), albeit with a detectable increase in homozygosity.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that while dwarfing optimizes logistical efficiency, it is associated with increased inbreeding risks, potentially related to altered canopy aerodynamics and flower positioning. Future management should prioritize optimized clonal deployment and supplemental mass pollination to mitigate genetic erosion in DSO.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09497-4.

Background

Seed orchards, special - purpose tree populations for seed production, are the “output delivery system” of breeding populations [1–3]. For conifers lacking clonal propagation materials, seed orchards link genetic improvement with artificial forest establishment. They contain the best individuals (breeding values), and theoretically, their offspring should have high genetic gain and diversity to enhance major economic traits or adaptability. This is crucial for global seed production programs [4, 5].

To produce seeds in a predictable manner, in open - pollinated seed orchards, balanced mating probabilities between all parents, low - level inbreeding, and random mating are expected. Each parent should contribute equally to the offspring, ensuring a broad genetic base and the expected genetic value [6, 7]. This requires several conditions: (a) all parents provide equal numbers of male and female gametes for seed production; (b) female flower fertilization timing is synchronized with effective pollen dispersal; (c) the seed orchard parents have sufficient genetic diversity to pass on to the offspring; and (d) the seed orchard is isolated from natural stands of the same species to prevent external pollen contamination. As observed in many tree breeding programs, these expectations for seed orchards are rarely fully met [8–12]. Parental phenology, pollen quantity, and seed yield in seed orchards can vary significantly among parent sources and genotypes. Parental contributions to the genetic pool of the offspring are often unbalanced, potentially leading to a loss of genetic diversity [13, 14]. When selecting parents for seed orchards, it is essential to optimize a large number of parental materials and evaluate offspring genetic traits to maximize gains while maintaining high diversity levels [15–17].

Phenology is a key part of a plant’s life history and a major fitness factor that affects reproductive success [18–21]. Differences in phenological variation and flowering synchrony can impact the frequency of gene exchange between parents and the genetic composition of seeds in seed orchards. These differences can also reduce effective population size, cause year - to - year changes in mating systems, increase self - pollination probabilities, and raise the risk of external pollen contamination [22, 23]. These issues are particularly evident in conifer seed orchards where wind is the main effective pollen dispersal agent [2, 3, 24]. In conifers, 34% − 52% of parents typically contribute to 80% of the offspring in seed orchards, showing a clear imbalance [25, 26]. Effective pollen dispersal and female cone flower pollination usually occur within two to three weeks. During this time, factors such as rainfall, wind direction, and wind speed can become critical, alter random mating in seed orchards, and increase pollen contamination [27, 28].

In recent years, tree dwarfing has been used to address the problem of tall trees in seed orchards and difficulties in seed collection. It’s an important part of modern intensive seed orchard management [29, 30]. Methods like topping, pruning, girdling, and hormone induction are used to suppress apical dominance and control upward growth. This makes it easier to harvest cones and conduct artificial pollination. However, it may affect the downward movement of female and male cones [31]. But tree - breeding programs rarely focus on flower - related management in dwarf seed orchards. It’s still unclear whether pollen and mating patterns change in these orchards. This information is crucial for making genetic management decisions in seed orchards, such as dwarfing, thinning, artificial pollination, and clone arrangement [28, 32].

Cupressus funebris is a key afforestation and ecological tree species in southern China. It has excellent wood quality, strong adaptability, and can tolerate drought and poor soils, making it highly effective for afforestation and ecological restoration in barren mountains [33]. However, significant clonal differences in reproductive capacity and flowering phenology have been observed in Cupressus funebris. Factors such as multiple branches, poor ventilation and light penetration in the inner canopy, and adverse conditions like temperature drops and rainfall during the flowering period may affect mating probabilities among parents in dwarfed seed orchards [26, 34]. To ensure seed quality for afforestation and develop rational genetic management strategies for dwarfed seed orchards, long - term monitoring of reproductive phenology and flowering synchrony within seed orchards is essential. It is also crucial to evaluate the impact of these factors on seed production, as well as effective pollen dispersal patterns and mating systems among parents. The objectives of our study are to: (1) Characterize differences in flowering synchrony and identify major climatic factors in dwarfed Cupressus funebris seed orchards. (2) Assess parental contributions to the genetic pool of offspring. (3) Examine changes in mating mechanisms and effective pollen dispersal patterns following tree management practices. Our findings offer new insights into the mating mechanisms of dwarfed seed orchards and provide valuable references for flower - related management practices.

Results

Reproductive capacity

During the three observation years, in the dwarfed seed orchard, 51.85% to 66.67% of clones contributed 85% of female cones, and 44.44% to 51.85% of clones contributed 85% of male cones. The parental contribution curves for female and male cones deviated from the ideal (Fig. 1a), showing unbalanced potential gamete production capacity. Paternal contribution analysis (Fig. 1b) showed that in the dwarfed plot, paternal contribution ranged from 0.99% to 14.85%, with 23.81% of fathers producing over 50% of the offspring. In the non - dwarfed plot, paternal contribution was between 0.87% and 13.04%, with 24% of fathers producing over 50% of the offspring. Overall, a small number of clones produced a disproportionately large number of male strobili, indicating a highly skewed potential for pollen fecundity during reproduction.

Mating pattern

The dwarfed seed orchard is mainly characterized by outcrossing, with an outcrossing rate of 72.19%. Among the offspring, 101 individuals (70.89% identification rate) matched with parental clones, and 8 offspring-specific alleles (Na) were found. The pollen contamination rate was 5.80%. In contrast, the non-dwarfed seed orchard had a higher outcrossing rate of 81.70%, with 115 offspring (76.67% identification rate) matching the parentals and 12 offspring-specific alleles detected (Table 1). A higher selfing rate in the dwarfed orchard (27.81%) compared to the un-dwarfed counterparts (18.30%) (P < 0.05).

Effective pollen dispersal patterns

Pollen propagation among seed orchard clones is random without a specific pollination direction. The highest proportion of offspring occurs when effective pollen dispersal is 30–40 m (Fig. 2). In non - dwarfed plots, the average distance between mother trees and pollen donors was 35.03 m. In dwarfed plots, the maximum pollination distance was 60.00 m, the average pollination distance was 33.35 m, and 75.76% of effective pollination occurred within 40 m (Fig. 2). A Mann-Whitney U test confirmed that the mean effective pollen dispersal distances were not significantly different between the dwarfed and non-dwarfed plots (P > 0.05).

Parental contribution to the offspring gene pool

In the parental population of the dwarfed seed orchard, the number of observed alleles (Na) ranged from 3 to 11, with an average of 6.000, and the average number of effective alleles (Ne) was 3.561. Among 540 offspring, Na ranged from 4 to 11, averaging 6.636, which is 10.60% higher than the parental average. Effective allele numbers were 12.86% higher. Genetic diversity was comparable to the parental population, indicating no reduction in offspring genetic diversity (Table 2). This study also reveals that the offspring’s average observed heterozygosity (Ho = 0.576) is lower than the parents (Ho = 0.616), however, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed that this difference was not statistically significant (Z = -1.45, P = 0.147). Offspring largely retained parental genetic diversity. Both parental and offspring populations had positive fixation indices (F), 0.026 and 0.145, respectively. This suggests potential excess homozygosity and inbreeding, but with relatively low heterozygote deficiency.

Flowering synchrony index

Due to clonal differences, male and female cone flowering durations vary. Over three observation years, pollen shedding lasted 5–11, 9–14, and 10–12 days, respectively. The female cone pollination period was 5–12, 9–13, and 9–11 days (Fig. 3). The full flowering period accounted for the largest proportion, with male cone full flowering comprising 36.36% − 66.67% of the total flowering period and female cone full flowering comprising 37.50% − 71.42% (Fig. 4a).

Climatic impact factors

Factorial analysis of major meteorological factors during the flowering period and flowering phenology shows that rainfall and temperature are key drivers of the flowering synchrony index (Fig. 6). Interestingly, in years with good weather, the shorter full - flowering period leads to a lower synchrony index. Conversely, in years with rainy and cold conditions, the synchrony index rises. Among the three observed years, two with low temperatures and rainfall showed similar increases in the flowering synchrony index (Fig. 7). During the flowering period, the frequency of rainfall events showed no significant correlation with floral duration (P > 0.05); however, cumulative precipitation was positively correlated with prolonged anthesis (P < 0.01). Additionally, Daily mean temperature (DMT) inversely modulates floral longevity, demonstrating a significant negative correlation (P < 0.05), indicating that elevated thermal regimes shorten the flowering window.

Discussion

Imbalanced parental contribution to the offspring gene pool

Compared to other conifer seed orchards, the genetic diversity of Cupressus funebris parental populations is slightly higher or similar, indicating a proper selection intensity of high - gain parents from the first - generation seed orchard for the improved one. This study reveals that the offspring’s overall observed heterozygosity (Ho = 0.576) is lower than the parents’ (Ho = 0.616) [35], and the fixation index (F) increased substantially from 0.026 in parents to 0.145 in the offspring. Previously, we attributed this decrease in heterozygosity primarily to imbalanced parental contributions. However, a more rigorous population genetics assessment indicates that the primary driver of this excess homozygosity is the high selfing rate (27.81%) observed in the dwarfed orchard. Under assumptions of equilibrium, the expected inbreeding coefficient based on the observed selfing rate can be modeled as F = SR/2-SR. the expected F is approximately 0.164. Notably, after accounting for technical artifacts, our corrected observed F (0.145) aligns closely with this theoretical expectation. (As confirmed by our Micro-Checker analysis, locus F015 possessed a high frequency of null alleles which artificially inflated the initial locus-specific F to 0.822; this locus was therefore excluded to provide an unbiased estimate). Because the biologically negligible discrepancy between the corrected observed F (0.145) and the expected F (0.164) shows no severe positive deviation, it confirms that the high selfing rate (27.81%) is the overwhelming primary driver of the excess homozygosity in the dwarfed orchard. While phenological asynchrony exists, its contribution to further heterozygote deficit (the Wahlund effect) appears minimal compared to the direct structural impact of the dwarfed canopy promoting self-pollination [36, 37]. In addition, significant imbalance in reproductive structure production exists: 51.85% − 66.67% of clones contribute over 85% of female cones, and 44.44% − 51.85% contribute over 85% of male cones [1]. While we did not directly test absolute pollen volume or viability, this skewed cone production heavily implies an underlying imbalance in potential gamete availability. This may lead to a few parents dominating the offspring gene pool, reducing genetic diversity. Similar cases have been reported in other conifer seed orchards [24].

Effective pollen dispersal patterns and paternal reproductive success

When interpreting these mating patterns, it is crucial to distinguish between the physical dispersal of pollen and realized paternal reproductive success. While physical pollen dispersal is primarily driven by aerodynamics and weather conditions (wind speed, precipitation), paternal reproductive success—which is what our SSR-based progeny analysis inherently measures—is the culmination of the entire reproductive process. This includes not only the physical arrival of pollen on a receptive female strobilus but also pollen viability, successful germination, pollen tube competition, fertilization, and seed maturation. As observed in our data, an individual clone might theoretically release a massive volume of pollen over a wide area, yet exhibit a low paternal contribution in the progeny pool. This discrepancy can arise from biological factors such as phenological asynchrony, low pollen vigor, genetic incompatibility (e.g., inbreeding depression associated with embryo abortion), or mere chance. Therefore, the ‘dispersal distances’ and ‘parental contributions’ reported in our study represent effective pollen dispersal and realized gene flow, highlighting the clones that successfully navigated both aerodynamic transport and post-pollination biological barriers.

The mating system affects the reproductive level and genetic structure of parent trees in seed orchards. The extent of effective pollen dispersal within seed orchards is limited, and pollen from external unknown sources has a wide dispersion range, leading to imbalanced paternal contributions [10]. Funda and El-Kassaby indicated that 20% of parental clones in seed orchards can produce up to 80% of the cones [1]. In this study, over three years of investigation in the seed orchard, a smaller number of clones were found to contribute a larger amount of pollen during reproduction. The parental balance curves for female and male cone quantities deviated significantly from the ideal state (Fig. 1). The offspring parentage analysis results showed that in the dwarfed plots, the pollen source could be determined for 70.89% of the offspring, and in the non-dwarfed plots, the pollen source could be determined for 76.67% of the offspring. The range of the number of offspring produced by the mother tree and the candidate father tree was 1 to 11, with the majority producing only one offspring. The parental reproductive contribution rate ranged from 3.57% to 42.31%, indicating an imbalance in paternal contributions within the seed orchard, especially in the dwarfed plots, where the frequency of gene exchange was reduced.

The effective contribution of the paternal candidate clones decreased with the increase of the spatial distance between the mother tree and the candidate ramet. While our spatial analysis focused solely on Euclidean distance and did not incorporate prevailing wind direction, it is highly probable that canopy aerodynamics also drive these asymmetric mating successes. As demonstrated in other wind-pollinated tree species, prevailing wind vectors often skew the physical pollen cloud, creating directional biases in paternal contributions [3]. The low pollination efficiency and asynchronous flowering among clones are the main reasons for the imbalance in paternal reproductive output within the seed orchard. Gene flow through the dynamic dispersal of pollen and seeds helps the genetic variation of plant populations [7, 12, 38]. The effective pollen dispersal range in Cupressus funebris seed orchards is relatively wide, with a pollination pattern similar to that of other wind-pollinated tree species, such as Pinus massoniana, Pinus tabuliformis, Pinus roxburghii and Abies [39]. In dwarfed seed orchards, the widespread pattern of effective pollen dispersal is likely due to the significantly higher plant density in the seed orchard compared to natural populations, and the lower position of male and female cones on dwarfed trees compared to non-dwarfed trees. Crucially, our statistical analysis revealed no significant difference in the mean effective pollen dispersal distances between the dwarfed and undwarfed plots. This indicates that the dwarfing treatment does not severely restrict the horizontal aerodynamic transport of the pollen cloud across the orchard. Therefore, the significantly higher selfing rate observed in the dwarfed plots (27.81% vs. 18.30%) is likely associated with structural modifications to the canopy rather than horizontal dispersal limitations. By suppressing apical dominance, dwarfing vertically compresses the canopy, potentially causing male and female strobili to concentrate within a lowered, relatively dense zone. We hypothesize that this spatial condensation provides a plausible structural framework that may increase the probability of geitonogamy (mating between different flowers on the same ramet), as pollen shed from upper branches is more likely to be intercepted by receptive female cones on the same tree before being caught by prevailing winds for wider cross-pollination.

Studies show that the outcrossing rate in Cupressus funebris seed orchards is lower than in other conifers. This suggests that the genetic diversity of parental clones in the seed orchard isn’t being effectively and stably transmitted. Even with a fixed - clone design, there’s a risk of inbreeding and pollen contamination. The flowering asynchrony among parents exacerbates this [2, 40]. The dwarfing treatment is associated with reduced effective pollen dispersal and an increased self-pollination risk. This implies that more attention should be paid to parental clone arrangement and spacing in dwarfed seed orchards. Effective pollen dispersal and paternal reproductive success are key for seed orchard management [41]. To boost seed yield and quality, a “wide - row, close - plant” spacing is more suitable for conifers [4]. After dwarfing, the fruit - bearing layer shifts downward, and male/female cones enter a “relatively dense zone.” So, more pollination space is needed to reduce inbreeding risk.

Effects of flowering synchrony and climatic factors

During flowering, synchrony among individuals impacts gene flow and may be influenced by changing climatic conditions [28, 42]. Low synchrony may reduce resource competition among clones but decreases pollination efficiency and affects offspring genetic gain [39]. High synchrony can enhance pollination efficiency through resource sharing [2]. However, over the three - year observation period, overall synchrony in the seed orchard was low. Differences in flowering synchrony among clones and between years were observed, indicating that varying flowering times among clones is common in forest tree seed orchards. This asynchrony can reduce effective parental mating combinations, increase self - pollination and external pollen contamination, and thus lower the genetic gain of seeds [43]. While we quantified the flowering synchrony index (Wij), we did not directly regress these pairwise values against the number of realized offspring assigned in the paternity analysis. We acknowledge this as a limitation; future studies should explicitly model the relationship between Wij and realized mating success.

The flowering synchrony index is closely related to environmental factors such as temperature, rainfall, and altitude. In years with low temperatures and high rainfall (e.g., 2024 and 2025), the flowering synchrony index was higher. This is because adverse weather conditions slowed the flowering process of the clones. Those clones that were ready or already in the flowering stage experienced a longer flowering period due to the impact of the weather. The cold and rainy weather typically lasted for 5–8 days. Once the weather improved, the male and female strobili of the clones opened in a more concentrated manner, leading to a relatively higher flowering synchrony index. In contrast, in sunny years (e.g., 2021), the flowering synchrony was lower. This is attributed to the fact that under favorable weather conditions, clones capable of pollen shedding or female flower pollination completed their flowering process in a shorter time frame, making clonal differences more pronounced. As observed from our meteorological correlations, elevated thermal regimes shorten the flowering window, while cumulative precipitation prolongs it. Consequently, when weather conditions are highly variable, these genetically mediated clonal effects interact with climatic fluctuations to further diminish overall phenological synchrony. This finding differs from studies on trees like Pinus radiata, which generally suggest that high temperatures and humidity reduce synchrony [36, 44]. Therefore, the flowering management of Cupressus funebris seed orchards should be tailored to the local climate. Monitoring meteorological data during the flowering period, forecasting changes in synchrony, and implementing measures such as assisted pollination or flowering induction can enhance pollination efficiency [45].

Conclusions

In Cupressus funebris dwarfed seed orchards, gamete contributions are unbalanced. The genetic composition of offspring is jointly influenced by flowering synchrony, parental contributions, and realized mating patterns (effective pollen dispersal), with clonal differences playing a leading role. Climatic factors also have complex effects on flowering synchrony. Although the dwarfed plots did not exhibit significantly altered effective mating distances between successful parents, the dwarfing practice inherently lowers the fruit-bearing layer, which is associated with male and female cones entering a “relatively dense zone.” While our operational experimental design limits strict causal inference due to potential block-level confounding variables, the observed association between this condensed canopy architecture and the increased risk of inbreeding underscores the necessity of optimizing clonal deployment (e.g., maximizing the distance between ramets of the same clone) to facilitate outcrossing and mitigate the increased risk of inbreeding within this condensed canopy. Consequently, in Cupressus funebris dwarfed seed orchards, clone selection should be optimized based on cone quantities, phenology, and parent origins. Furthermore, spatial management must be addressed at two levels. Physically, appropriate spacing and branch management among all individual trees (ramets) must be maintained to enhance canopy ventilation and light penetration. Genetically, the spatial deployment must maximize the distance between ramets belonging to the same clone to minimize geitonogamy (self-pollination). Combined with flowering-period management, such as assisted pollination and phenological regulation, these comprehensive measures can improve the genetic gain and stability of dwarf seed orchards.

Materials and methods

Study area and material sources

The study materials were obtained from a Cupressus funebris seed orchard located in Chun’an County, Zhejiang Province (119°03’57″ E, 29°32’24″N). The orchard covers an area of 37, 000 m², with an altitude ranging from 250 to 300 m. It is situated on a gentle slope with a gradient of less than 15°, facing south - southeast. The soil conditions of the seed orchard were determined, with mountain red soil (pH 4.74) containing 20.08 g/kg organic matter, 1.12 g/kg total nitrogen, 0.49 g/kg total phosphorus, 95.87 mg/kg alkaline - hydrolyzed nitrogen, 283.92 mg/kg available phosphorus, 93.36 mg/kg available potassium, 212.90 mg/kg exchangeable calcium, and 108.55 mg/kg total boron.

The improved seed orchard was established in 2015. After genetic testing of the parental materials from the first - generation seed orchard (established in 1990) with 45 parents, 27 genotypes were retained to form this improved clonal seed orchard. In this context, a ‘clone’ refers to a group of genetically identical trees (ramets) vegetatively propagated from a single selected parent tree. The seed orchard is divided into five blocks, with a total of 1,680 trees planted. The clones are arranged in a random spatial arrangement within the orchard. Each clone is repeated 10–15 times within a plot, and the distance between ramets of the same clone is over 20 m, with a tree spacing of 4 m × 4 m. To minimize external background pollen contamination, a spatial isolation zone of over 300 m surrounds the seed orchard, within which no other conspecific Cupressus funebris stands are present. The seed orchard first flowered and produced cones in 2018. In 2020, the mother trees in blocks 2–5 underwent topping to facilitate cone harvest and pollination management, forming the Dwarfed Seed Orchard (DSO). Specifically, the top 1–2 whorls were cut, with the cutting height controlled at 2.5 m, with no further treatment (Fig. 8). Block 1 was non-dwarfed seed orchards (USO). The management of both seed orchards remained consistent.

Phenological observation

Phenological observations were conducted during the flowering stage in 2021, 2024, and 2025. For each clone, five ramets were selected. The following method was used: one first - order branch in the middle - upper part of the canopy was chosen in each of the four directions (east, south, west, and north). These branches were observed daily until the end of the flowering period [2]. The flowering phases were determined as follows: (1) When 0% − 30% of female cone scales crack or 0% − 30% of male cones shed pollen, it’s the onset of flowering. (2) When 30% − 75% of female cone scales crack or 30% − 75% of male cones shed pollen, it’s full flowering. (3) The end of flowering for female cones is when 75% − 100% of female cone scales close, thicken, and turn dark purple - red. For male cones, it’s when 75% − 100% of pollen is shed, and the flower branches turn yellowish - brown and wilt. The entire flowering period, which included the beginning, full, and end of flowering phases, was recorded. At the end of the flowering period, the number of female cone flowers and male cone flower spikes on each observed branch were recorded [46, 47]. It is important to note that male strobilus counts were used as an observational proxy for potential male fecundity; absolute pollen yield and viability were not directly quantified.

Samples of mating systems

Sample collection was carried out in two parts. In the first part, in May 2023, we collected the current year’s needles from 27 parents in the dwarfed seed orchard and stored them in a -80 ℃ freezer for DNA extraction. During the cone maturation period, we collected cones from all clones and processed the seeds for sowing and seedling cultivation. When the seedlings reached a height of 20 cm, we randomly selected 20 seedlings from each clones, forming a total of 540 offspring individuals for genetic diversity analysis between parental and offspring generations in the seed orchard.

The second part involved selecting five identical clones from both non - dwarfed and dwarfed plots. We marked the locations of the mother trees and collected current - year needles. Upon cone maturation, we collected cones from the middle - upper part of the tree, air - dried them in the laboratory, and gathered over 1,000 seeds per tree. After sowing and seedling cultivation, 30 offspring seedlings were randomly chosen from each mother tree, forming 150 offspring individuals. Young tissues were collected and stored in a -80 ℃ freezer for DNA extraction. These samples were used for paternal analysis and effective pollen dispersal pattern research in dwarfed and non - dwarfed seed orchards.

DNA extraction, primer screening, and SSR-PCR amplification

DNA was extracted from parent trees and seedling progeny in the seed orchard using an improved CTAB plant genomic DNA extraction kit (Aidlab, Beijing, China), and DNA quality and concentration were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. For parent - offspring relationship analysis and genetic diversity assessment, 12 SSR markers were used, with primer information listed in Table S1. PCR amplification and detection followed previously described methods [35]. Parent and offspring DNA samples were amplified with primers, and the amplified products were separated by electrophoresis on a Qsep100™ automatic nucleic acid protein analysis system (BIOptic, Taiwan).

Genetic diversity parameter calculation

After exporting and converting the genotyping data from the Qsep100TM system, genetic diversity parameters were calculated using GenAlex 6.5. These parameters included the number of alleles (Na), effective number of alleles (Ne), Shannon’s diversity index (I), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), fixation index (F). Polymorphism information content (PIC) for microsatellite loci were assessed using Cervus 3.0. Prior to population genetic and paternity analyses, the SSR dataset was screened for potential genotyping artifacts, including large allele dropout, stuttering, and null alleles. Loci exhibiting a high frequency of null alleles that could severely bias the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium and fixation index estimates were excluded from the descriptive genetic diversity calculations.

Paternity analysis

Using the codominant nature of the SSR markers, the software compares the known diploid genotype of the mother tree with the diploid genotype of the offspring to identify the obligate paternal allele at each locus. It then evaluates the diploid genotypes of all candidate clones in the seed orchard (derived from their needle tissue) to determine the most likely pollen donor based on Mendelian inheritance probabilities. Paternity Identification Rate (PIR), outcrossing rate (OCR), selfing rate (SR), Number of Unique Alleles in Progeny (NUAIP) and pollen contamination rate by foreign pollen (PCFP) were calculated for non - dwarfed and dwarfed plots. Because pollen undergoes meiosis and contributes a haploid genome to the offspring, paternity cannot be established by direct full-genotype matching. Instead, CERVUS 3.0 was used to conduct parentage analysis. To determine the critical LOD and ∆ scores for paternity assignment, a simulation was performed prior to the analysis. The simulation was set to generate 10,000 offspring using the following exact parameters: 27 candidate fathers, a 0.95 proportion of candidate fathers sampled (set to 0.95 rather than 1.0 to conservatively account for the estimated ~ 5% external background pollen contamination), a 0.98 proportion of typed loci, and a genotyping error rate of 0.01. Based on these parameters, to balance the tradeoff between assignment accuracy and sufficient sample size for spatial modeling, the primary paternal contributions and effective pollen dispersal distances were determined using a relaxed confidence interval (CI) of 80%. Additionally, to quantify the potential impact of false-positive assignments, a parallel analysis was conducted using a strict CI (95%). Mating system parameters (SR, OCR) derived from the strict CI dataset were compared against the relaxed dataset to ensure the robustness of the population-level estimates.

Because SSR markers identify the paternal clone rather than a specific paternal ramet (individual tree), effective pollen dispersal distances were estimated using spatial coordinate analysis. The spatial coordinates (X, Y) of all ramets within the sampled plots were mapped. Upon successful paternal assignment by CERVUS, the Euclidean distance between the specific mother tree (seed parent) and all individual ramets of the assigned paternal clone was calculated. Following standard assumptions in clonal orchard studies [13], we applied the nearest-neighbor model, assuming the pollen most likely originated from the paternal ramet closest to the mother tree. Thus, the effective pollen dispersal distance for each mating event was recorded as the minimum linear distance between the mother tree and the nearest ramet of the assigned pollen-donor clone. These values were then used to determine the average and maximum pollination distances and to model the paternal contribution rates across different distance intervals (Fig. 2).

Collection and analysis of climatic data

A micro-automatic weather station was used to record weather factors during the flowering period. To avoid the confounding effects of the understory microclimate, the station was installed in an open, unobstructed area within the seed orchard Approximately 15 m away from the nearest tree canopy, with sensors positioned at a height of 2.5 m above the ground. DMT stands for daily average temperature. Rainfall amount (RA) was the sum of daily rainfall. Average wind speed (AWS) was the mean of wind speeds, and average humidity (AH) was the mean of humidity levels during the flowering period.

To evaluate the relationship between flowering phenology and meteorological factors, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to assess the linear correlation degree between each meteorological factor and the reproductive phenological indicators in pairs. The analysis included phenological phases, the flowering synchrony index (Wij), and major meteorological factors (RA, AH, AWS, DMT). Data were standardized to zero mean and unit variance prior to analysis to account for different measurement units. The calculation of the correlation coefficient matrix is accomplished using the basic statistical functions of the R language, while the visualization of the correlation heatmap is carried out by using the corrplot package.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Categorical data, such as selfing rates between the dwarfed (DSO) and undwarfed (USO) plots, were compared using a Chi-square test. To assess differences in genetic diversity metrics (e.g., observed heterozygosity) between the parental and progeny populations, a paired t-test was employed for normally distributed data, while a non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was utilized for non-normally distributed locus-level data. The effective pollen dispersal distances between DSO and USO were compared using a non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, given the non-normal distribution of spatial mating distances. Furthermore, to evaluate the relationships between flowering phenology and meteorological factors, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted. Variables were standardized prior to PCA to account for varying measurement units. A significance level of P < 0.05 was applied for all hypothesis testing.
