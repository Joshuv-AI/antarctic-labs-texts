Gender Discrimination Against Women in Surgery Is Due to Implicit Bias Rather Than Quantifiable Differences in Clinical Performance

Introduction

Multiple publications from the last decade have demonstrated that gender discrimination in the field of surgery still remains a problem. 1–3 Published datasets have clearly shown that the discrimination is aimed disproportionately at younger surgeons or residents who are women. 4–6 The authors theorize that the bias is not due to a real difference in clinical competence, but that the gender discrimination is due to implicit bias: a subconscious prejudice that traditionally assigns women to roles as a homemaker and/or nurturer. 2,5,7 The authors designed this study to determine whether gender discrimination was due to implicit bias or a quantifiable difference in clinical acumen and/or performance between genders in surgical specialties among a professional association of surgeons.

Methods

After securing approval from the St Augustine Campus of The University of the West Indies' Ethics Committee (SA2035/03/2023), the authors carried out a questionnaire study to investigate the prevailing attitudes among a professional association of surgeons in the Anglophone Caribbean. Data were collected using a specially designed 28-item questionnaire that evaluated differing aspects of gender bias. The self-administered questionnaire was delivered electronically to all members of the Professional Association of Surgeons and remained active for data collection over a period of 3 months between September 1 and November 30, 2025.

The first part of the questionnaire sought information on respondent demographics and to determine whether respondents witnessed gender discrimination, based on the definition as “a person receiving different treatment based solely on their gender.” Respondents’ perceptions of clinical performance were also collected using 4 parameters, defined as follows:

In this study, the authors included medical doctors who were members of a professional surgical association, regardless of gender, employment status, and level of training. The authors excluded members of allied professions in medicine and doctors who did not consent to participation. The data collected were collated into groups separated by generation, gender, time from medical school graduation, and postgraduate training levels to facilitate comparisons. Attending-grade and surgical resident staff in postgraduate years 4 and greater were classified as senior-level staff. Interns and surgical residents in the first 3 postgraduate years were considered junior-level staff. From the generation perspective, the authors classified millennials as persons ≥ 40 years of age and premillennials as those < 40 years of age.

The authors used the z score test for population proportions to determine whether groups differed significantly based on gender alone. Chi-square tests were used to evaluate the associations between categorical variables. The authors considered a P value < .05 as being significant.

Results

Of 140 questionnaires distributed, 95 (68%) respondents observed discrimination in the workplace based on gender alone. Surgeons who were women were statistically more likely to be victims of discrimination in this cohort (64.2% vs 4.2%; z −8.7165; P < .0001). The data collected from the respondents who witnessed gender discrimination in the workplace were analyzed more closely, in order to determine whether there was tangible difference in clinical performance between the genders.

Table 1 outlines the responses of persons who had witnessed gender discrimination as it related to differences in clinical performance. There was no statistical difference in clinical judgment or surgical skill based on gender alone. However, based on gender alone, there was a statistically significant difference in thoroughness and time to complete work-related tasks. Interestingly, women outperformed men in these categories.

Discussion

Multiple authors have demonstrated that, despite improved awareness and reduced prevalence, gender discrimination still exists within surgical fields. 1–6 These results supported these statements by revealing that 68% of respondents witnessed gender discrimination, significantly skewed against surgeons who were women. This must be corrected if society wishes to encourage women to pursue surgical training.

The authors were unable to demonstrate a difference in clinical acumen or performance between genders, and no logical basis for explicit bias could be found. This suggests that the discrimination is likely due to implicit bias, which is a prejudice that is present but not consciously recognized. 2 Understanding this requires a holistic and historic perspective.

Several prior authors have discussed the historic assignment of gender norms, with homemaker roles allocated to women and breadwinner roles to men. 5,7 These roles have been deeply ingrained into the psyche of society, especially in premillennial generations with formative social development years that coincided with the era when these ideologies were most prevalent. 5,7 The authors believe that the unconscious pervasiveness of these historic norms created the implicit bias that drives gender discrimination, and it was also responsible for the difference between generations.

The authors do recognize that societal gender norms have evolved over the past 3 decades. At the turn of the 21st century, women became more educated and increasingly joined the professional workforce, 1 reducing their active roles within the home. Goldscheider et al 8 coined the term “gender revolution” to describe these evolving changes, which they theorized occurred in 2 phases. In the first phase, women began to advance outside of the home. This created the unintended results of increasing family instability, declining fertility, and mounting resistance driven by society’s desire to maintain traditional norms. Society witnessed the reflex emergence of gender equality and gender rights movements that was required to maintain the momentum. 9 These changes paved the way for increasing numbers of women to pursue careers in medicine and surgery.

In the second phase of Goldscheider’s 8 gender revolution, societal views began to change, and a more egalitarian view was adopted. In this view, the new norm was for women to have equal financial and logistic contributions toward day-to-day running of the household, as well as professional input into the workplace. In this phase, women were no longer active homemakers, and all aspects of daily living were shared equally in partnership between genders. 8 Many modern societies are still navigating the second phase of the gender revolution. The authors believe that the local surgical community studied has made an incomplete transition through the second phase of gender revolution. The authors also believe that this accounts for the pervasion of implicit bias in this setting.

In a way, the authors understand why surgeons have navigated this second phase less elegantly than other professions, because surgery has traditionally been a heavily biased, competitive, time-consuming, and man-dominated area. This is evidenced by historic injustices where Margaret Ann Bulkley had to pretend to be a man in order to practice surgery, 10 and Mary Edwards Walker experienced prejudice from surgeons who were men in order to be a surgeon in the military. 11 Surgeons must make a conscious effort to navigate this second phase of Goldscheider’s 8 gender revolution. The realization that implicit bias drives surgeons’ gender discrimination in this setting is important, because it informs the surgical community and forms the foundation upon which interventions for change can be designed.

A significantly greater proportion of surgeons who were women in this study were victims of gender discrimination. This is in keeping with data from other recent publications. 2,3,5,8,12–15 This finding is important to acknowledge, because the profession creates the chance for alienating women from pursuing surgery as a career in this setting if this remains uncorrected. Negative experiences for surgeons who are women may shape their subsequent decisions about persistence in surgery and subsequent specialization. 12,14,16 It is true that some women may persevere in surgical specialties despite gender discrimination, but it undoubtedly creates mental anguish, workplace dissatisfaction, and melancholy for the victims. 1–3,5,6 This has a ripple effect, because these women cannot be effective mentors to younger women who may have been considering careers in surgery. The net result is that younger women in this setting may be pushed toward other fields in medicine that they perceive may offer more rewarding lifestyles and better work-life balance.

Other studies in the scientific literature have also suggested implicit bias as a cause for gender discrimination. 2,5,7 Salles et al 7 published a study in which they measured health care professionals’ reaction times to the Implicit Associaton Test, a validated tool to identify mental associations, to explore the existence of gender-based bias. This was an interesting study, as it utilized a validated tool to identify mental associations shaped by one’s environment and influencing interactions with others. The authors were able to show that health care professionals exhibited stronger implicit bias by associating career and surgery with men, while associating women with family. 10 The authors should view the results of this study as a wake-up call, because if society continues to impede full participation by women, it may undermine expansion of the workforce needed for the next generations’ surgical care.

The results revealed that surgeons who were women significantly outperformed surgeons who were men in thoroughness and task completion times. Although the nature of the study did not provide insight on the reasons for this observation, the results added to the existing data in the medical literature. When Wallis et al 17 examined the association between surgeon gender and clinical postoperative outcomes in a cohort of 1,165,711 patients, they demonstrated that patients treated by surgeons who were women were less likely to experience complications, death, and overall adverse outcomes after surgery. Similar findings were reported by other authors. 17–22 Some have suggested that the gender-based effect on outcomes may be due to differences in communication, practice styles, relationships between patients and practitioners, and patient selection. 6,8,17 Others suggest that women operate more slowly and take more time during procedures, 17,20 are more likely to adhere to guidelines, 18–20 collaborate better with nursing teams, 17,22 and are generally more risk-averse. 17

This study did have some limitations. First, the authors used a survey design, and this may have introduced the potential for respondent bias. Second, there was an unequal number of responses by each gender, with a 1.6:1 man to woman ratio, which may also have introduced bias. Additionally, the authors did not capture data on nationality, ethnicity, level of training/experience, practice environment, or other parameters that may also have impacted surgeons’ perspectives. Finally, the authors only included surgeons in the study, while anesthesiologists, nurses, and other members of the health care team who made important contributions to health care delivery were not included.

Conclusion

Unfortunately, gender discrimination is still prevalent in surgical disciplines as the second quarter of the 21st century begins. The authors have demonstrated that surgeons who are women are substantially more likely to be the victims, and this is due to implicit gender bias. The surgical community must address this with urgency, because impeding full participation by women creates the chance for undermining the expansion of the workforce needed for the next generations’ surgical care.
