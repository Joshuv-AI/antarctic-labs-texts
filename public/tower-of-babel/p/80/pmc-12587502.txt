Research on the expression, prognostic value, and regulatory effects on immune microenvironment of NETO2 in oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Jinhang Wang, Zifeng Cui, Kaicheng Yang, Yalin Zhang, Shixiong Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNETO2 is a gene with potential prognostic value in various cancers, but its role in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remains unclear. This study investigates NETO2 expression, clinical significance, and its association with the tumor immune microenvironment in OSCC.MethodsNETO2 expression in OSCC tissues was analyzed using public databases and correlated with prognosis via Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. A nomogram incorporating NETO2 and clinical factors was developed to predict survival, and its performance was assessed using ROC curves. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyzed cell types and NETO2 expression across different cell populations. The relationships between NETO2 expression, tumor mutation burden (TMB), immune microenvironment, and drug sensitivity were explored, and molecular docking assessed NETO2's binding affinity with small-molecule compounds. The functional role of NETO2 in OSCC cell invasion, proliferation, and migration was validated experimentally.ResultsNETO2 was significantly overexpressed in OSCC tissues, and high expression correlated with shorter overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). The nomogram model, developed using TCGA and GEO cohorts, demonstrated good predictive performance, with AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates above 0.66 in both cohorts. scRNA-seq revealed elevated NETO2 expression in T cell clusters and significant associations with key immune signaling pathways, such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions and T cell receptor signaling. The high NETO2 expression group exhibited higher immune cell infiltration, increased potential for immunotherapy response, and differential sensitivity to various anticancer drugs. Molecular docking showed strong binding of NETO2 with compounds like ruxolitinib, paclitaxel, and docetaxel. Additionally, NETO2's role in OSCC cell invasion, proliferation, and migration was experimentally confirmed.ConclusionNETO2 is a significant prognostic biomarker in OSCC, potentially influencing tumor progression through modulation of the immune microenvironment. Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a significant health concern, with symptoms that may not be apparent in the early stages. However, as the disease progresses, clinical manifestations may include persistent oral ulcers or masses, loose teeth, pain, difficulty in chewing and swallowing, restricted tongue movement, and even weight loss or persistent bleeding [1]. Epidemiological studies have shown that the incidence of OSCC is closely associated with multiple factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, and poor dietary habits. Additionally, there are significant geographical, gender, and age-related differences in the incidence of OSCC [2–4]. The molecular mechanisms underlying OSCC are complex, involving the abnormal expression of various genes and signaling pathways. Important molecular targets include TP53 mutations, PD-L1, p53, and the Bmi1 gene.

NETO2 (neuronal activity-related pentatricopeptide repeat-containing protein 2) is a gene encoding a single-pass transmembrane glycoprotein. Initially believed to primarily function in neurons, recent studies have shown that its abnormal expression in various cancers is closely related to tumor progression. NETO2 is significantly upregulated in pancreatic cancer tissues, correlating with tumor staging, invasiveness, and patient survival rates. It promotes the proliferation, invasion, and migration of pancreatic cancer cells by activating the STAT3 signaling pathway. Knockdown of NETO2 inhibits cell proliferation and migration, while overexpression enhances these functions [5]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma patients, NETO2 is also significantly upregulated, correlating with clinical staging and lymph node metastasis status. It promotes tumor progression through the PI3K/AKT and ERK signaling pathways, and its knockdown inhibits cell proliferation and migration [6].

In summary, the abnormal expression of NETO2 and its role in promoting tumor progression have been widely demonstrated in various cancers. However, the specific mechanisms of NETO2 in OSCC remain unclear. Given the importance of NETO2 in other tumors and the complex molecular characteristics of OSCC, in-depth investigation of NETO2 expression patterns, biological functions, and interactions with the tumor microenvironment in OSCC is of great significance for elucidating the pathogenesis of OSCC and identifying new diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Therefore, this study aims to systematically explore the role and potential mechanisms of NETO2 in OSCC, with the expectation of providing new strategies and theoretical basis for the prognosis prediction and treatment of OSCC.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

In this study, we collected RNA-seq data from 330 OSCC patient samples and 32 normal tissue samples from the TCGA-HNSC cohort through The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Corresponding clinical and survival information were obtained from the UCSC Xena database (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). Additionally, we collected transcriptomic data from 40 normal and 40 OSCC samples from the GSE37991 dataset in the GEO database. We also obtained 270 head and neck cancer samples from the GSE65858 dataset, with tissues located in the Cavum Oris, Oropharynx, Larynx, and Hypopharynx. The OSCC samples from the TCGA-HNSC cohort and the GSE65858 dataset were used as the training and testing sets, respectively. Detailed clinical information of the samples can be found in Table S1. Furthermore, we collected single-cell RNA-seq data from six OSCC samples from the GSE172577 dataset.

Pan-cancer analysis

We explored the expression landscape of NETO2 across cancers using TIMER2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/) and conducted a pan-cancer analysis of NETO2 copy number variation (CNV) using the GSCA database (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/GSCALite/).

Cox regression analysis and nomogram model construction

We performed a univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis using the coxph() function from the survival package in R. Hazard ratios (HR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using log–log plots of survival times and statistical tests. The model's predictive performance was assessed using the concordance index (C-index). Forest plots were generated using the ggforest() function from the survminer package to visually display the HR and CI for each variable. To construct a nomogram model for predicting survival outcomes, we used clinical information and NETO2 expression levels of patients. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was built using the cph() function from the rms package in R, with survival time and status as dependent variables, to assess the combined impact of these variables on survival outcomes. Based on the linear predictor values from the Cox model, we calculated the 3- and 5-year survival probabilities using the survival() function and developed a nomogram using the nomogram() function to visually illustrate the contribution of each variable to the overall survival prediction.

Expression landscape and enrichment analysis of NETO2

We classified patients into a high-expression group if their NETO2 expression was above the mean value across all patients, and into a low-expression group if their expression was below the mean. Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using the Limma algorithm with a threshold of |logFC|> 0.5 and p < 0.05 to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package in R. Metascape analysis of DEGs was performed using the Metascape database (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html). We assessed the enrichment of hallmark pathways in the high and low expression groups using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA). GSVA scores reflecting pathway activity were calculated for each sample based on hallmark pathway gene sets and gene expression matrices. Differential analysis of pathway activity between clusters was performed using the Limma package, and significant pathways were identified based on adjusted p-values. Additionally, we conducted GSEA between the high and low expression groups to identify differentially enriched pathways, with log2 fold changes (logFC) calculated between the two groups. Enrichment analysis was performed using KEGG pathway gene sets, and pathways with p < 0.05 were selected. The top enriched pathways in each group were visualized using gseaplot2. Finally, we used the NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/NetworkAnalyst/) and selected miRNAs interacting with NETO2 from the miRTarBase (v9.0) database for analysis.

Drug sensitivity and molecular docking

We conducted drug sensitivity analysis between the high and low expression groups. Drugs with significant differences in IC50 values (p < 0.05) between the two groups were selected using the “oncoPredict” package in R and the GDSC2 database. The 3D structures of three small-molecule compounds were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and protein structure data for the gene were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Molecular docking analysis was performed using the CB-Dock2 online platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php).

Analysis of scRNA-seq data

We preprocessed the scRNA-seq data of OSCC using the “Seurat” package in R. The data were normalized using the LogNormalize(·) method with a normalization scale factor of 10,000, and high-variance features were identified by PCA to reduce data dimensionality. Cell types were annotated using the SingleR package. To investigate the expression characteristics of NETO2 in scRNA-seq data, cells were scored and divided into high and low expression groups based on the median expression level of the gene using the AddModuleScore(·) function. GSEA analysis was performed using the fgsea package in conjunction with gene sets from the MSigDB database (C2 collection) to explore pathway involvement between the two groups. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the FindAllMarkers(·) function from the Seurat package to identify marker genes associated with high and low expression clusters. GSEA analysis was performed based on these differentially expressed genes, and significant pathways were highlighted using bar plots to display NES. Additionally, to investigate intercellular communication networks among immune cells, we used the “CellChat” package in R. Immune-related cell types were filtered from the Seurat object, and expression matrices and cell type annotations were extracted to create a CellChat object. Using the human CellChat database (CellChatDB.human), we mapped overexpressed genes and ligand-receptor pairs to the human protein–protein interaction network. Communication probabilities between cells were inferred, and interactions supported by at least three cells were filtered out. The intercellular communication network was visualized using circular network plots and bubble plots. Finally, to study the regulatory networks in the high and low expression groups, we obtained human regulatory modules (regulons) with confidence levels A, B, and C using the “dorothea” package in R. Viper scores were calculated using the viper package to quantify the activity of these regulatory modules in cells. Viper scores were summarized by cell populations, and the top TFs with the highest variance were selected based on variance filtering.

In vitro functional assays

Tissue sample collection

From December 2023 to July 2024, we enrolled 16 patients with OSCC who underwent surgical treatment at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University. During resection, tumor specimens and matched non-tumorous mucosa were obtained; the latter was taken ≥ 3 cm from the gross margin and confirmed histologically to be free of malignant involvement. Eligibility required histopathologic confirmation of primary OSCC on resection specimens, no preoperative antineoplastic therapy (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy), adequate cardio-cerebro-pulmonary status to tolerate surgery, and no prior or concurrent malignancy. Exclusion criteria were: not meeting diagnostic pathology standards; any tumor-directed therapy before surgery; serious cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, pulmonary, or other systemic conditions incompatible with anesthesia or operation; and other malignancies or active systemic infection, autoimmune disorders, psychiatric illness, or severe metabolic disease. Immediately after excision, tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. 2022KT211).

Cell culture and transfection

The normal human oral keratinocyte cell line HOK was obtained from Yaji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), while the OSCC cell lines Tca8113, SCC4, and SCC25 were sourced from Yaji Biotechnology and the American Type Culture Collection, respectively. All cell lines were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Life-iLab, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Biosharp, China). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ (Thermo Scientific, USA).

For transfection, SCC25 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were plated in 6-well culture plates. Upon reaching approximately 60% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with serum- and antibiotic-free DMEM for a 4-h pre-incubation. Transfection was then conducted using Lipofectamine™ 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Eight hours post-transfection, the medium was changed to complete DMEM containing 10% FBS, and the cells were allowed to continue growing. After 48 h, the transfected cells were harvested for evaluation of transfection efficiency by Western blot analysis. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting NETO2 and a non-targeting negative control (si-NC) were synthesized by GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The sequences were as follows: si-NETO2#1: 5'-CGCCAAAUUAUCCUGACUCAUTT-3', si-NETO2#2: 5'-CCUCCUCAUUAUGAACUGUUUTT-3'.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using Trlquick Reagent (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Reverse transcription and cDNA synthesis were performed using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The qPCR was conducted with Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Gene-specific primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). GAPDH was used as the internal normalization control, and relative gene expression was quantified using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences were as follows: NETO2 (5’ → 3’), Forward, CCACCAAACAAGGAGTGTATCT; Reverse, GGCTTTTCACGCCACAGTAAC. GAPDH (5’ → 3’), Forward, ATGGGTGTGAACCATGAGAA; Reverse, GTGCTAAGCAGTTGGTGGTG.

Western blot

For tissue lysates, specimens were gently rinsed with ice-cold PBS, blotted and weighed, and homogenized at 4 °C using a tissue homogenizer; RIPA buffer was added at a fixed weight-to-volume ratio, and all steps were performed on ice. Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China), and protein concentrations were determined with a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, China). Protein samples were denatured by heating at 100 °C for 10 min, separated via 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Germany). Following transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-NETO2 (1:1000 dilution; Cat#127,661, Zenbio) and anti-β-Actin (1:10,000 dilution; Cat#R380624, Zenbio). The next day, membranes were washed and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000 dilution; Cat#511,203, Zenbio) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; MCE, USA) and captured using a gel documentation system (Gene, UK).

Cell proliferation assay (CCK-8)

To evaluate cell proliferation, transfected cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well in 100 μL of complete growth medium. Proliferation was monitored at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after transfection. At each designated time point, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, China) was added to each well, followed by incubation in the dark for 4 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (Biotek, USA) to quantify cell viability.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in 2 mL of complete culture medium. The medium was refreshed every 48 h. After 10 to 14 days of incubation, visible colonies containing more than 50 cells were observed under a microscope. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 15 min. After thorough rinsing with PBS and air drying, images of the stained colonies were captured, and the number of colonies was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54).

5-Ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight at 37 °C/5% CO₂. A 2 × EdU working solution (20 μM; 1:500 in DMEM) was mixed 1:1 with complete medium to yield 10 μM EdU, and cells were incubated for 2 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS containing 3% BSA, and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min. The click-reaction cocktail (Beyotime, China) was then applied for 30 min in the dark, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst for 10 min, and images were acquired by fluorescence microscopy.

Wound healing assay

Cells were plated into 6-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 90% confluency. A straight-line scratch was created across the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. The wells were gently rinsed twice with PBS to remove cell debris, and the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM to inhibit cell proliferation. Images of the wound area were taken at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany). The wound area at each time point was measured with ImageJ software, and the cell migration rate was calculated using the formula: Migration rate (%) = [(wound area at 0 h − wound area at 24 h)/wound area at 0 h] × 100%.

Cell invasion assay (Transwell)

Transwell inserts (LabSelect, China) were pre-coated with Matrigel (Beyotime, China) to establish a uniform extracellular matrix barrier. A total of 2 × 105 cells suspended in 200 μL of serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with 600 μL of complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS to serve as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, non-invading cells remaining on the upper membrane surface were carefully removed with a sterile cotton swab. The inserts were then rinsed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 15 min. Cells that had invaded through the Matrigel to the underside of the membrane were imaged under a microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare differences between groups, while the Spearman correlation coefficient was used to evaluate associations between variables. Western blot results from 16 paired tissue samples were analyzed using a paired t test. For in vitro functional experiments, data from the CCK-8 assay were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA. Group differences in colony formation, EdU assay, wound healing, and invasion assays were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

NETO2 as a pan-cancer biomarker

Figure 1 presents the technical roadmap of this study. We first evaluated the expression levels of NETO2 in normal tissues and OSCC tissues. The expression of NETO2 was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 2A-B). We further analyzed the relationship between NETO2 expression and patient overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated that patients with high NETO2 expression had significantly lower OS and PFS compared to those with low expression (Fig. 2C-D), suggesting that high NETO2 expression may be associated with poorer prognosis. We also assessed the expression levels and survival impact of NETO2 in pan-cancer settings. We further explored the impact of NETO2 copy number variation (CNV) on patient survival. The results showed that NETO2 CNV was significantly associated with survival prognosis in multiple cancers (Fig. 2E). Finally, we analyzed the expression levels of NETO2 across different cancer types and subtypes (Fig. 2F). The results revealed significant differences in NETO2 expression among various cancer types and subtypes. For example, in breast cancer (BRCA), NETO2 expression varied among different subtypes (Basal, Her2, LumA, LumB), indicating that NETO2 may play different roles in these subtypes. A total of 44 miRNAs predicted to interact with NETO2 were identified through the miRTarBase database, and detailed information of the network is provided in Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S1.

Clinical significance of NETO2

To evaluate the prognostic value of NETO2 in OSCC, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis of clinical factors in the TCGA and GEO cohorts. In the TCGA cohort, the hazard ratios (HR) for age, gender, tumor stage, T stage, and N stage were 1.019 (95% CI: 1.002–1.038), 0.905 (95% CI: 0.592–1.384), 1.640 (95% CI: 1.253–2.148), 1.450 (95% CI: 1.172–1.794), and 1.524 (95% CI: 1.217–1.907), respectively (Fig. 3A). The corresponding p-values were 0.033 for age, 0.645 for gender, < 0.001 for stage, < 0.001 for T stage, and < 0.001 for N stage. In the GEO cohort, the HR for age, gender, tumor stage, T stage, and N stage were 1.046 (95% CI: 0.617–1.771), 1.615 (95% CI: 1.204–2.168), 1.512 (95% CI: 1.222–1.872), 1.415 (95% CI: 1.129–1.774), and 1.027 (95% CI: 1.006–1.048), respectively (Fig. 3B). The corresponding p-values were 0.001 for age, 0.003 for gender, < 0.001 for stage, 0.013 for T stage, and 0.868 for N stage. These results indicate that age, tumor stage, T stage, and N stage were significant prognostic factors in both cohorts, while gender was only significant in the GEO cohort.

Subsequently, we constructed a nomogram model based on NETO2 expression levels and clinical factors with significant prognostic value in the TCGA cohort to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates of OSCC patients (Fig. 3C). The nomogram integrated age, tumor stage, T stage, and N stage. The calibration curve of the nomogram demonstrated good consistency between predicted survival probabilities and actual observations (Fig. 3D). The predictive performance of the nomogram was evaluated by ROC analysis, with AUC values of 0.664, 0.757, and 0.793 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates, respectively (Fig. 3E). Similarly, we constructed a nomogram model based on NETO2 expression levels and clinical factors with significant prognostic value in the GEO cohort (Fig. 3F). The calibration curve of this nomogram also showed consistency between predicted and actual survival probabilities, indicating accurate predictive ability (Fig. 3G). The predictive accuracy of the nomogram was further evaluated by ROC curves, with AUC values of 0.736, 0.754, and 0.758 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates, respectively (Fig. 3H). These results further confirmed the robustness of the nomogram in predicting the survival outcomes of OSCC patients across different cohorts.

Furthermore, we divided all OSCC samples in the TCGA cohort into high- and low-expression groups based on NETO2 expression levels. Differential expression analysis was performed between the two groups, identifying 176 DEGs (Table S3). The expression patterns of the top 50 DEGs between the two groups were analyzed (Fig. 4A). To explore the potential roles of these DEGs in biological processes, we performed KEGG and Metascape enrichment analyses (Fig. 4B-C). The results showed that these genes were mainly enriched in several key biological processes and pathways, such as cell adhesion, extracellular matrix-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, cellular senescence, small cell lung cancer, and Staphylococcus aureus infection. GSVA was used to assess differences in gene set expression between the high- and low-expression groups (Fig. 4D). GSEA identified significant enrichment differences in multiple pathways between the two groups (Table S4). We focused on two KEGG pathways related to OSCC progression identified by GSEA (Fig. 4E-F). Specifically, the apoptosis and DNA replication pathways may play key roles in OSCC progression, and their dysregulation may be closely associated with tumor proliferation, drug resistance, and poor prognosis.

NETO2 and the TMB landscape and immune microenvironment in OSCC

In this section, we first explored the mutation frequencies of different genes in high and low expression groups. The mutation types included missense mutations, frameshift insertion mutations, splice site mutations, frameshift deletion mutations, nonsense mutations, in-frame deletion mutations, and translation initiation site mutations. In the high expression group, the genes with the highest mutation frequencies were TP53 (71%), TTN (32%), and FAT1 (20%), while in the low expression group, the genes with the highest mutation frequencies were TP53 (72%), TTN (32%), and FAT1 (27%). The mutation frequencies of other genes also differed between the two groups, suggesting that these two groups may have distinct genetic characteristics (Fig. 5A-B). Figure 5C analyzed the relationship between TMB levels and patient survival probability using a Kaplan–Meier survival curve. The KM survival curve showed a significant difference in survival probability between the high TMB (H-TMB) group and the low TMB (L-TMB) group (p = 0.012), with patients in the high TMB group having a lower survival probability. Figure 5D further investigated the combined effect of TMB levels and NETO2 expression on patient survival. The survival curves were divided into four groups based on TMB status and NETO2 expression level: high TMB with high NETO2 expression, high TMB with low NETO2 expression, low TMB with high NETO2 expression, and low TMB with low NETO2 expression. The log-rank test results showed p = 0.075.

Figures 5E-H analyzed the differences in immune landscape between the high and low-risk groups using the IOBR software package, including immune cell types in the TME, immune suppressive features, immune exclusion features, and immunotherapy biomarkers. Specifically, Fig. 5E compared the feature scores of different immune cell types between the two groups. For example, the scores of T cells CD4 memory and NK cells were higher in the high-risk group, indicating more active immune cell infiltration in this group. The analysis of immune suppressive features showed that the score of T cells regulatory immune suppressive feature was higher in the low-risk group, suggesting more significant immune suppression in this group (Fig. 5F). In terms of immune exclusion features, the scores of CAFs family member receptors were higher in the high-risk group, indicating a more immunosuppressive immune microenvironment in this group, which may hinder immune cell infiltration (Fig. 5G). Regarding immunotherapy biomarkers, the TME score and mismatch repair score showed significant differences between the two groups, with higher scores in the high-risk group, indicating that this group may have a more favorable response to immunotherapy (Fig. 5I-L). Figures 5I-L demonstrated the differences in immunotherapy response between high and low-risk populations. The results showed that in the four scenarios of ips_ctla4_neg_pd1_neg, patients in the high and low-risk groups exhibited significant differences in response to immunotherapy.

Expression landscape of NETO2 at single-cell resolution

The analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data revealed the presence of multiple cell types in OSCC tissues, including B cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, keratinocytes, monocytes, neutrophils, NK cells, T cells, and tissue stem cells (Fig. 6A). The proportions of these cell types varied across different OSCC samples, suggesting the heterogeneity of the OSCC microenvironment (Fig. 6B). The constructed intercellular interaction network uncovered extensive communication relationships between T cells and other cells, such as B cells, monocytes, neutrophils, and NK cells (Fig. 6C-D). Key signaling pathways included ANXA1-FPR1, CCL5-CCR1, IFNG-(IFNGR1 + IFNGR2), LGALS9-CD44/CD45, and MIF-(CD74 + CD44) (Fig. 6E-F). Among them, the communication probabilities of the IFNGR and TNFRSF1B pathways were high with p < 0.01, indicating their significance in intercellular communication. The IFNG pathway promotes immune cell activation and is involved in anti-tumor immunity, while the LGALS9 and MIF pathways may be associated with tumor invasion and immune evasion. The TNF pathway may regulate immune cell infiltration and tumor cell apoptosis.

Drug sensitivity and molecular docking of NETO2 in high and low expression groups

Figure 8A-I shows the differences in IC50 values of multiple drugs between the high- and low-risk groups (Table S5). Western blot analysis of 16 paired OSCC and normal tissues further demonstrated elevated NETO2 protein levels in tumors, supporting its overexpression at the tissue level (Fig. 9A). As shown in Fig. 9C, transfection with either si-NETO2#1 or si-NETO2#2 markedly reduced NETO2 protein expression. Discussion

This study comprehensively investigated the potential role of NETO2 in the occurrence and progression of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). Initially, pan-cancer analysis revealed the expression levels and prognostic value of NETO2 in OSCC and various other cancers. High expression of NETO2 in tumor tissues was significantly associated with poor overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Moreover, the copy number variation (CNV) of NETO2 was significantly correlated with survival outcomes in multiple cancers, and its differential expression across cancer subtypes suggests diverse roles in tumorigenesis and progression. Based on the miRTarBase database, hsa-miR-203a-3p, which interacts with NETO2, may function as a tumor suppressor in OSCC by regulating the CDK1 gene. Reduced expression of hsa-miR-203a-3p may lead to high CDK1 expression, thereby promoting the malignant progression and poor prognosis of OSCC [7].

In OSCC, the expression level of NETO2, together with several clinical factors (e.g., age, tumor stage, T stage, and N stage), collectively impacts patient prognosis. By constructing a nomogram model, we successfully integrated NETO2 expression and clinical factors to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival rates of OSCC patients. The calibration and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve results of the nomogram demonstrated good predictive accuracy and robustness in both TCGA and GEO cohorts. Additionally, KEGG and Metascape enrichment analyses of DEGs between high- and low-NETO2 expression groups revealed its potential involvement in key biological processes, such as cell adhesion, extracellular matrix-receptor interaction, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. Specifically, KEGG enrichment analysis identified three pathways associated with OSCC progression: PI3K − Akt signaling pathway, focal adhesion, and ECM-receptor interaction. Triphala can mediate the metabolic activity, migration, invasion, and proliferation of OSCC cell lines by inactivating the PI3K/Akt pathway, serving as a potential anti-tumor agent [8]. Cheng et al. found that target genes involved in these pathways (e.g., PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, focal adhesion, and ECM-receptor interaction) may contribute to the occurrence and development of OSCC through circRNA mechanisms [9]. Metascape enrichment analysis further identified two pathways: regulation of keratinocyte proliferation and formation of the cornified envelope. The regulation of keratinocyte proliferation is closely related to the occurrence and progression of OSCC. For instance, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway regulates keratinocyte proliferation and apoptosis, leading to uncontrolled proliferation of keratinocytes in OSCC when this pathway is overactivated [10]. The cornified envelope, a product of terminal keratinocyte differentiation, is also closely associated with OSCC and may serve as a target for developing therapeutic strategies against OSCC [11]. Tyrosine metabolism can activate multiple pathways, including the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway [12], which is crucial for inhibiting apoptosis in OSCC cells [13]. Therefore, investigating the molecular mechanisms of tyrosine metabolism pathways may enhance our understanding of OSCC progression. Finally, GSEA analysis identified two pathways: APOPTOSIS and DNA REPLICATION. Abnormalities in the apoptosis pathway often result in cancer cells resisting apoptosis and promoting tumor growth. Inducing apoptosis may thus be an important therapeutic strategy for OSCC [14]. Aberrant DNA replication affects cell cycle regulation, leading to uncontrolled proliferation. Han et al. discovered that XPO1, a nuclear export protein facilitating the transport of various substances, can serve as a poor prognostic marker in OSCC by mediating DNA replication-related pathways [15].

This study further explored the relationship between NETO2 expression and tumor mutation burden (TMB) as well as the immune microenvironment. Differences in the most frequently mutated genes (e.g., TP53, TTN, and FAT1) between high- and low-NETO2 expression groups suggest that NETO2 may be associated with specific genetic features. The significant correlation between TMB levels and patient survival probability further underscores its prognostic importance in OSCC. Moreover, combined analysis of NETO2 expression and TMB levels indicated that patients with high TMB and high NETO2 expression may have a poorer prognosis. Immune microenvironment analysis revealed more active immune cell infiltration but a more pronounced immunosuppressive phenotype in the high-NETO2 expression group, suggesting that NETO2 may influence OSCC progression by modulating immune cell functions and the composition of the immune microenvironment. Specifically, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) may either promote or inhibit the proliferation, invasion, and migration of OSCC cancer cells [16]. M1 macrophages, classically activated macrophages, may inhibit OSCC progression by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines and inducing inflammatory responses [17]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) play a crucial role in immune regulation. In the tumor microenvironment, Tregs can suppress anti-tumor immune responses and promote tumor growth. Gan et al. demonstrated that targeting Treg metabolism in OSCC could be an important strategy for enhancing anti-tumor immunity [18].

This study also investigated the relationship between NETO2 expression and drug sensitivity. Results showed significant differences in sensitivity to various drugs (e.g., Bortezomib, Cisplatin, and Docetaxel) between high- and low-expression groups, suggesting that NETO2 may serve as a potential predictive biomarker for drug response. Molecular docking experiments further revealed the binding capabilities of NETO2 with three small-molecule compounds: Ruxolitinib, Paclitaxel, and Docetaxel. Previous studies have confirmed the potential of these three drugs in treating OSCC. Low-dose Bortezomib, which inhibits NF-κB, in combination with Paclitaxel and Cetuximab, may become an important research direction for treating OSCC by enhancing anti-tumor efficacy [19]. Docetaxel, a taxane antimicrotubule drug, can inhibit tumor cell division and achieve better therapeutic effects when combined with other drugs for OSCC treatment [20]. Ruxolitinib can effectively inhibit the phenotype of OSCC cells by disrupting BTK/CD133 signaling, serving as an important therapeutic strategy for chemoresistant OSCC [21].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis revealed the heterogeneity of various cell types and extensive intercellular communication in OSCC tissues. The high expression proportion of NETO2 in T cell clusters suggests its potential role in regulating T cell function and the immune microenvironment. GSEA analysis further confirmed the enrichment of NETO2-related DEGs in multiple immune-related pathways, such as ENDOCYTOSIS and CYTOKINE-CYTOKINE RECEPTOR INTERACTION pathways. Liu et al. investigated the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylome of OSCC at the mRNA level and found that genes with downregulated m6A modification sites were significantly associated with endocytosis, highlighting the potential link between OSCC and m6A modifications [21]. Chemokine signaling pathways play crucial roles in immune cell migration, inflammatory responses, and disease occurrence. Recent studies have shown that cell fusion between tumor cells and macrophages can promote metastasis in OSCC patients by activating chemokine signaling pathways [22]. Moreover, transcription factor regulation analysis based on the DoRothEA database revealed correlations between NETO2 expression levels and the activities of multiple transcription factors, further emphasizing its complex role in cell function regulation.

This study has certain limitations in the external validation process. In the GSE65858 dataset, only a limited number of OSCC samples were available, and the small number of cases with 5-year survival data made it difficult to construct a stable nomogram model restricted to OSCC. Therefore, we expanded the validation to include other subtypes of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) to assess the robustness and generalizability of the model. It should be noted, however, that HNSC arising from different anatomical sites may exhibit heterogeneity in molecular features, risk factors, and prognostic patterns, which could partially affect the specificity of the validation results. Future studies incorporating larger, independent OSCC cohorts will be essential to provide more targeted validation and to ensure the clinical applicability of our findings. In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were not performed to further validate the dynamic stability of the protein–ligand complexes. We acknowledge this as a key limitation of the study and plan to incorporate MD simulations and related functional experiments in future work, once sufficient computational resources become available, to further strengthen the reliability and translational relevance of our findings.

Despite the comprehensive multiomics analysis supporting the potential prognostic value and biological roles of NETO2 in OSCC, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size is limited and largely derived from public repositories, which may constrain generalizability and statistical power. Second, key bioinformatic outputs, including miRNA target prediction, gene set/pathway enrichment, and drug screening prioritization, are computational and have not yet been experimentally validated. Accordingly, these results should be regarded as hypothesis generating rather than definitive evidence of therapeutic efficacy. Future work will validate the prognostic value of NETO2 in larger independent cohorts and delineate its mechanisms in OSCC initiation, progression, and the immune microenvironment through in vitro and in vivo experiments and pharmacologic perturbation, for example dose response assays and pathway modulation.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the expression levels, prognostic value, relationship with the immune microenvironment, and potential functional mechanisms of NETO2 in OSCC and other cancers. High expression of NETO2 was significantly associated with poor prognosis and may influence the progression of OSCC by modulating the immune microenvironment and cellular signaling pathways. Moreover, the correlation of NETO2 with drug sensitivity and its binding capacity to small-molecule compounds provide new insights for future targeted therapies. However, this study has some limitations, such as the lack of in vitro and in vivo experimental validation of the functional role of NETO2. Future research should further explore the specific molecular mechanisms of NETO2 in OSCC and other cancers and experimentally verify its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target.
