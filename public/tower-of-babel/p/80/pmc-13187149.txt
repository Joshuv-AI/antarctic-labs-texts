Biological characteristics of transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean and its competitive ability against weeds for survival
Authors: Shijia Dong, Yunfei Gao, Liu Xin, Wei Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetically modified crops have the advantages of high yield, strong resistance and low cost, and are currently cultivated in many countries around the world. During our research, we discovered a type of semiwild soybean that emerged due to genetic drift at a transgenic test site. However, the potential risks of genetically modified crops in the process of environmental release, such as gene drift, weeding, impact on soil ecological environment, have raised concerns whether genetically modified glyphosate-resistant soybeans can be widely promoted. This study investigated the biological characteristics of genetically modified glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean from various perspectives. Based on this, the study further evaluated the survival competitiveness of genetically modified glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean against weeds. These results indicate that this transgenic material retains some characteristics of wild type soybean while exhibiting unique advantages in physiological functions and adaptability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45274-x.Keywords: Glyphosate resistance, Semiwild soybean, Biological characteristics, WeedsSubject terms: Biotechnology, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Glyphosate resistant transgenic soybeans were first introduced for large scale commercial cultivation in 1996. Their robust herbicide resistance, high yield, elevated oil content, and ease of mechanized management facilitated rapid adoption, resulting in a consistent annual increase in the global area dedicated to transgenic soybean cultivation1. In comparison to conventional soybeans, glyphosate-resistant transgenic varieties demonstrate enhanced oil content, superior quality characteristics, suitability for extensive agricultural practices, reduced production costs, and certain traits that confer stress resistance2.

Nevertheless, gene flow emanating from glyphosate-resistant transgenic crops has triggered genetic modifications in wild soybean relatives within adjacent regions3. These genetic alterations stem from the target genes of transgenic soybeans, which disrupt the genetic architecture of wild soybean relatives and may confer upon them advantageous transgenic traits, thereby potentially giving rise to ecological consequences4. The sexual hybridization of glyphosate-resistant transgenic crops ensues when their pollen disperses to nearby wild relatives, resulting in hybrid progeny harboring glyphosate-resistant genes5. The study by Kang et al. demonstrated that high-oleic-acid transgenic soybean showed comparable survival competitiveness with non-transgenic soybean in wasteland conditions, exhibiting no competitive advantage over weeds6. Wang et al. investigated the survival competitiveness of DREB3-transgenic drought-resistant soybean against velvetleaf (Abutilon theophrasti) and redroot pigweed (Amaranthus retroflexus) under drought stress, with results showing superior competitiveness of the weed species7. Li et al. found that transgenic soybean displayed weaker survival competitiveness than both non-transgenic soybean and weeds in wasteland environments8. However, other reports have indicated that glyphosate-resistant transgenic soybean possesses stronger survival competitiveness and environmental adaptability than non-transgenic varieties under natural conditions9. Furthermore, the biological characteristics of these hybrid semiwild glyphosate-resistant transgenic soybeans remain largely unexamined in existing research10–12.

On the flip side, the most significant risk of cultivating glyphosate-resistant transgenic soybeans lies in their potential to turn weedy13. The weediness of glyphosate-resistant transgenic soybeans can be classified into two types: The first type happens when transgenic soybean seeds are shed and form self-sustaining populations in the wild, which can disrupt natural ecosystems14. Moreover, after obtaining resistance genes, soybeans might develop enhanced characteristics like winter hardiness or seed shattering15. These traits allow them to encroach upon and take over farmland, posing a threat to biodiversity and bringing about substantial economic and ecological ramifications16.

The second type is from the spread of glyphosate-resistance genes to weeds, giving rise to “superweeds”. Research has revealed that glyphosate-resistant weeds exhibit a level of resistance that is multiple times greater than that of ordinary weeds17. In Canada, rapeseed weeds resistant to three different herbicides have also appeared18. When transgenic crops hybridize with wild relatives or weeds, new resistant weeds can be produced. For example, herbicide-resistant rapeseed can crossbreed with wild radish to generate resistant weeds19–21. These results imply that hybrid semiwild glyphosate-resistant transgenic soybeans may have a competitive edge over weeds22.

This study conducted a comparative analysis of agronomic traits, physiological indices, and reproductive capacity among glyphosate-resistant semiwild transgenic soybean, glyphosate resistant soybean cultivar Hujiao 698, conventional cultivated soybean Dongnong 50, and wild soybean. The research particularly focused on investigating the competitive ability for survival between glyphosate-resistant semiwild transgenic soybean and weeds. These investigations contribute to elucidating the potential impacts of glyphosate resistant transgenic soybean gene flow on soil environmental safety and biodiversity in China, while providing new scientific insights and fundamental theories in this research field.

Materials and methods

Materials

In this study, we utilized four soybean materials: glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean (DT-1), glyphosate-resistant cultivated soybean variety Hujiao698 (HJ698), conventional cultivated soybean variety Dongnong50 (D50), and wild soybean (WS-1). The genetically modified (GM) soybean material, which exhibits glyphosate resistance through expression of the synthetic CP4-EPSPS gene, was kindly provided and maintained by the Pesticide Research Laboratory of Northeast Agricultural University (Harbin, China). Notably, this GM soybean contains the transgenic CP4-EPSPS insert from event GTS-40-3-2 which has obtained regulatory approval for cultivation and use in numerous countries worldwide. All wild soybean and cultivated soybean seeds are currently preserved at the Pesticide Research Laboratory of Northeast Agricultural University. The glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean (DT-1) seeds used in this study were collected by our research team from the transgenic test base of Northeast Agricultural University in Harbin City, Heilongjiang Province, China (45° 45′ N, 126° 43′ E). The glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean seeds are currently preserved at the Pesticide Research Laboratory of Northeast Agricultural University.

To confirm the transgenic status of all materials, PCR amplification was conducted using genomic DNA from various soybean lines as the template. DNA was extracted using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0. To specifically determine the presence of the transgenic CP4-epsps gene, a pair of primers unique to the CP4-epsps sequence was employed (Table S1). The PCR reaction was prepared according to the components and volumes specified in Table S2. The PCR reaction program was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 7 min; followed by 5 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 63 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; then 32 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Following amplification, the PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel for 20 min. The gel electrophoresis results are provided in the supplementary material file (Fig. S1).

Glyphosate treatment and plant height measurement

At the three-leaf stage, glyphosate-isopropylamine (41% active ingredient) was applied to glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean and Hujiao 698. A flat-fan nozzle (TeeJet 8002VS) was used for glyphosate application. The spray system was calibrated to maintain a constant pressure of 40 psi (276 kPa). The nozzle was fixed 50 cm above the average canopy of the soybean seedlings, and the spray boom traveled at a constant speed of 1.0 m/s to ensure uniform spray deposition across the target area. Determination of biological characteristics of soybeans

This study systematically investigated the agronomic traits (plant height, biomass), quality attributes (protein, fat, and isoflavone content), physiological characteristics (photosynthetic parameters, chlorophyll concentration, antioxidant enzyme activity), and reproductive capacity of transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybeans and control varieties (Hujiao 698, Dongnong 50, and wild soybeans) at the seedling (SS), flowering (FS), podding (PS), and maturity stages (MS). Additionally, evaluations were conducted on pollen viability, yield components, and overwintering germination characteristics to comprehensively assess their biological traits.

At the seedling (SS), flowering (FS), pod-setting (PS), and maturity (MS) stages, five uniformly growing plants of each soybean type, transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean (DT-1), Hujiao 698 (HJ698), Dongnong 50 (D50), and wild soybean (WS-1), were selected for phenotypic characterization and biomass measurement. At each stage, plant height and stage-specific traits were recorded: leaf shape at SS; flower color at FS; podding habit at PS; and plant architecture, pod shattering tendency, seed shape, seed color, and hilum color at MS. The fresh weight of the whole plant and the root system was measured immediately after sampling. All plant materials were then oven-dried at 80 °C to constant weight, and the dry weight was determined.

The protein and fat contents were determined using near-infrared spectroscopy23, while the isoflavone content was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography24. During the flowering stage (FS), pod setting stage (PS), and grain filling stage (TS) of soybean, photosynthetic parameters were measured on sunny days between 8:00 and 10:00 AM using an ADC BioScientific LCpro-SD System (Serial No. 34285). The instrument was set with a flow rate of 200 μmol s−1, fixed red-blue light source, and a photosynthetic active radiation (PAR) of 869 μmol m−2 s−1.

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) were analyzed as standard physiological indicators. These enzymes constitute core components of the plant antioxidant system, responsible for scavenging reactive oxygen species and maintaining cellular redox homeostasis25,26. Fresh soybean leaf samples (0.1 g) were collected at the seedling (SS) and pod-setting (PS) stages. The midribs were removed, and the leaf tissue was cut into small pieces and rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. The treated samples were homogenized in an appropriate extraction buffer, and the resulting supernatants were measured using commercially available assay kits (Suzhou Keming Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) following the manufacturer instructions. All spectrophotometric analyses were performed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. The activity of nitrogenase in rhizoma was determined by the acetylene reduction method27.

Pollen viability was determined using I2-KI staining28.

Evaluation of competitive ability in survival of glyphosate resistant transgenic semi wild soybean

The field competition trial was conducted at the experimental station of Northeast Agricultural University, Harbin, China (45° 45′ N, 126° 43′ E). The soil at the site is classified as a typical black soil (Mollisol) with the following properties prior to sowing: pH 6.5, organic matter content 3.8%, available nitrogen 125 mg/kg, available phosphorus 32 mg/kg, and available potassium 150 mg/kg. No herbicide or pesticide was applied during the trial period to allow for natural weed infestation. A random block design was adopted, with three replicates. Each plot measured 4 m × 4 m. The materials tested included glyphosate resistant transgenic semi wild soybean and Dongnong 50, a conventional cultivar. Two sowing methods were employed: surface broadcasting and conventional row sowing. Seeding densities were established at 100,000 plants/hm2 and 400,000 plants/hm2. Sowing dates included May 2, May 10, June 25, and July 1. No cultivation management was implemented following the sowing process. Surveys were carried out at 1 month, 2 months, and 3 months after soybean planting to record the following parameters: weed species composition, weed density (plants per unit area), and percentage of weed coverage. Weed coverage was assessed using a standardized visual estimation method within fixed quadrats30.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 22.0, IBM, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD, and significance was accepted at P < 0.05 (or P < 0.01 for highly significant differences). Figures were generated using Origin (Version 2021, OriginLab, USA).

Results

Glyphosate resistance analysis

Relative plant height declined with increasing glyphosate concentration in both DT-1 and HJ698 (Fig. 1). At the recommended application rate (T2), relative height remained above 91% in both materials (HJ698: 91.33%; DT-1: 91.73%), corresponding to a reduction of 8.67% and 8.27% from the untreated control (T1 = 100%), respectively. At higher concentrations, inhibition became pronounced: at T3, relative height decreased to 76.16% in HJ698 and 72.47% in DT-1; at T4, values further declined to 68.36% (HJ698) and 67.80% (DT-1). Within each material, treatment groups differed at the 5% level as indicated by letter groupings (Tables S3).

Analysis of the biological characteristics of transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean

Phenotypic traits vary among the different soybean varieties30,31. Dongnong 50 has lanceolate leaves, purple flowers, round yellow seeds with light yellow hilum, semi-determinate growth habit, non-shattering pods, and an erect plant architecture. Hujiao 698 exhibits lanceolate leaves, white flowers, elliptical yellow seeds with light brown hilum, semi-determinate growth habit, non-shattering pods, and an erect growth form. Wild soybean features ovate leaves, purple flowers, flattened elliptical black seeds with black hilum, indeterminate growth habit, shattering pods, and a vining plant stature. The transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean has elliptical leaves, purple flowers, ellipsoidal black seeds with dark brown hilum, indeterminate growth habit, shattering pods, and a vining growth pattern. Across qualitative morphological descriptors, DT-1 shared multiple characteristics with wild soybean, while cultivated varieties differed in several domestication-related traits.

In natural soybean populations, the interaction between genotype and environment is a key determinant of plant height related traits32. Plant height increased progressively from seedling to full maturity in all materials (Fig. 3). At the seedling stage (SS), heights were comparable among materials (approximately 14–15 cm). Divergence became evident from flowering onward: at flowering (FS), DT-1 (68.17 ± 13.82 cm) and WS-1 (73.83 ± 5.30 cm) were both higher than HJ698 (48.33 ± 5.51 cm) and D50 (43.23 ± 1.91 cm). This pattern persisted through pod setting (PS) and maturity (MS), with WS-1 remaining tallest at maturity (139.33 ± 23.86 cm), followed by DT-1 (130.33 ± 6.25 cm), while HJ698 (75.33 ± 0.58 cm) and D50 (72.67 ± 11.02 cm) were shorter.

Across soybean growth stages (SS, FS, PS, and MS), fresh weight, dry weight, and root weight generally increased as development progressed in all materials (Fig. 4). For fresh weight, the transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean showed significant differences from Dongnong 50 (D50) and HJ698 at the seedling (SS) and maturity (MS) stages, but did not differ significantly from wild soybean; in contrast, at the flowering (FS) and podding (PS) stages, its fresh weight differed significantly from all three other soybean types. Overall, fresh weight ranked as HJ698 > D50 > transgenic semiwild soybean > wild soybean.

Differences in the fresh, dry, and root biomass of different soybean varieties.

The results for dry weight and root weight followed similar trend as fresh weight. At SS, PS, and MS, the transgenic semiwild soybean differed significantly from D50 and HJ698, but showed no significant difference relative to wild soybean; at FS, it differed significantly from all three other types. For dry weight, except at the seedling stage (where the transgenic line was slightly higher than wild soybean), the overall ranking across the remaining stages was HJ698 > D50 > wild soybean > transgenic semiwild soybean. For root weight, the transgenic line was higher than wild soybean at SS and MS, but lower than wild soybean at FS and PS; accordingly, root weight ranked as HJ698 > D50 > transgenic semiwild soybean > wild soybean at SS/MS, and HJ698 > D50 > wild soybean > transgenic semiwild soybean at FS/PS.

Analysis of physiological characteristics

The transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean exhibited a higher photosynthetic rate compared to wild soybean across all growth stages, though no significant difference was found between the transgenic and cultivated varieties (Dongnong 50 and Hujiao 698) (Fig. 5a). The smaller leaf size of wild soybean may account for its lower photosynthetic rate33,34.

The differences in physiological indicators of different soybeans at different times.

Chlorophyll content, including total chlorophyll and chlorophyll a and b, increased in the transgenic and wild soybean from seedling to podding stages, while it decreased in Dongnong 50 and Hujiao 698, indicating reduced photosynthetic capacity in the cultivated varieties. Hujiao 698 showed the highest chlorophyll levels at the seedling stage but declined later (Fig. 5b).

Figure 5c–e shows that only CAT activity is significantly higher in roots of DT-1 and WS-1, but not SOD and POD activities.

The transgenic semiwild soybean showed significantly higher nitrogenase activity at the seedling stage, which was intermediate in later stages compared to the wild soybean and Hujiao 698, indicating superior early nitrogen fixation (Fig. 5f).

Analysis of quality and output characteristics

Soybean protein and oil content are key traits for evaluating soybean quality, which are influenced by both genotype and environmental factors35. This study demonstrates that the transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean possesses advantageous traits compared to both the cultivated variety Hujiao 698 and wild soybean (Fig. 6). Specifically, it shows significantly higher protein and isoflavone contents compared to Hujiao 698, as well as a higher fat content than wild soybean.

Yield components differed among materials (Fig. 7). DT-1 produced 149.33 ± 17.01 number of pods per plant and 477.67 ± 25.32 number of seeds per plant, exceeding D50 (87.67 ± 9.07 pods; 284.00 ± 17.00 seeds) and HJ698 (49.33 ± 9.50 pods; 194.33 ± 29.02 seeds), but remaining below WS-1 (197.67 ± 25.15 pods; 580.67 ± 33.61 seeds). Discussion

In our study, the glyphosate-resistant transgenic semiwild soybean exhibited a consistently higher photosynthetic rate than the wild soybean across growth stages, while being comparable to the cultivated varieties. This agrees with reports that glyphosate-resistant soybean can show cultivar-dependent photosynthetic responses, and that photosynthesis may be reduced under certain glyphosate regimes rather than being universally unaffected36. In roots, only CAT activity was significantly higher (DT-1 and WS-1), which is consistent with studies showing that antioxidant enzyme responses to glyphosate are selective and genotype-dependent37. Across developmental stages, DT-1 exhibited a biomass pattern consistent with the general trend observed between cultivated and wild soybean germplasm, where cultivated varieties tend to accumulate greater biomass than their wild relatives38. Under unfavorable environmental conditions, hybrid performance often converges toward the female wild parent, indicating that environmental stress may influence the apparent vigor and competitive potential of hybrids39.

We observed significant differences in pods per plant, seeds per plant, and 100-seed weight between the transgenic semiwild soybean and both the cultivated varieties (D50 and HJ698) and wild soybean. Yield per plant also differed significantly from D50 and HJ698; however, no significant difference was detected relative to wild soybean. These patterns are consistent with literature showing that early-generation crop–wild hybrids often exhibit reduced plant size, biomass, and yield components compared with their wild progenitors, reflecting genetic incompatibilities, segregation effects, or resource allocation trade-offs during hybrid formation40. At the same time, other studies report that hybrid descendants can display partial recovery of fitness-related traits over generations, with some indices improving relative to wild progenitors while remaining comparable for emergence, pollen germination, and yield components41. Our finding that the transgenic semiwild soybean resembles wild soybean in key yield-related traits is aligned with this “trait recovery” framework in later generations, suggesting that the transgenic semiwild line may not experience a strong yield penalty relative to wild soybean under the tested conditions. Some studies have reported that hybrids may produce higher seed yields than their GM parental lines despite reduced seed size10, whereas other studies found that many F1 hybrids show reduced yield components and composite fitness relative to wild progenitors42. Together, these findings indicate that fitness outcomes are highly dependent on the specific cross and environmental conditions.

In this study, the transgenic glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean produced abundant pollen, but its overall pollen viability was low. This indicates that, compared with cultivated and wild materials, its male reproductive performance may be limited mainly by viability rather than pollen quantity. Previous studies reported that some GM crops show no obvious changes in pollen viability or morphology relative to conventional varieties43, whereas our results suggest that pollen traits can differ in semiwild materials, emphasizing the need to evaluate reproductive traits in the specific genetic background. This study demonstrates significant differences between the DT-1 hybrid soybean progeny plants and their wild progenitors. Similarly, another study also reported that the F2 and F3 hybrids of wild soybean populations exhibited markedly higher emergence rates than their wild progenitors44.

Conclusion

The glyphosate-resistant semiwild transgenic soybean exhibits high morphological and biomass similarity to wild soybean, with plant height intermediate between wild and cultivated varieties. Key differentiating traits include an enhanced photosynthetic rate (without altered chlorophyll content), improved seedling stage nodulation and nitrogen fixation, stable antioxidant enzyme activity, increased pollen production (though with reduced viability), and significantly reduced seed shattering coupled with higher overwintering survival compared to wild soybean. Nutritional profiling revealed intermediate characteristics, with protein and isoflavone contents markedly higher than the conventional cultivar Dongnong 50. Ecologically, the transgenic line had no significant effect on weed biodiversity. Under optimal sowing conditions, it outperformed Dongnong 50 in weed competition, though neither cultivar held a competitive advantage under suboptimal conditions. These findings collectively suggest that the glyphosate-resistant semiwild soybean combines beneficial wild and cultivated traits, offering agronomic potential while maintaining ecological compatibility.
