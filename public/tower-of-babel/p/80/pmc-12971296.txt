G9a inhibition reduces stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking in both male and female mice: Implications for safe and effective pharmacological therapeutics for alcohol use disorder
Authors: Deltrice Holmes, Marcelo F. Lopez, Howard C. Becker, Christopher W. Cowan, Ethan M. Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlcohol use disorder (AUD) is a chronic, relapsing disease that is difficult to treat, and current therapies are not effective for many patients. Clinical studies show that stress increases heavy alcohol consumption and that stress is a predominant factor in rates of relapse. As such, pharmacotherapies that reduce stress‐related alcohol drinking might be beneficial clinically. We recently showed that viral vector‐mediated reduction of the epigenetic enzyme G9a (also known as euchromatic histone‐lysine N‐methyltransferase 2 or EHMT2) in the nucleus accumbens blocks stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking. In addition, repeated systemic administration of the G9a inhibitor UNC0642 also reduces stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking, suggesting that G9a inhibitors might be effective for treating AUD.MethodsHere, we further investigated the potential for UNC0642 to be used clinically to treat AUD. We first tested the effective dose of UNC0642 in multiple models of alcohol consumption in both male and female mice. We also tested half‐life, oral bioavailability, maximum tolerated dose, and many measures of behavioral toxicity.ResultsWe found that UNC0642 is effective at reducing stress‐potentiated alcohol consumption in both male and female mice at lower doses than we previously reported, and we re‐confirmed the anxiolytic‐like effects of UNC0642. We also found that UNC0642 has a long half‐life in vivo, is orally bioavailable, is within an acceptable toxicity range for therapeutic use, and does not produce behavioral inhibition in many standard preclinical assessments, including locomotor activity, open field, Y‐maze, rotarod, fear conditioning, sucrose self‐administration, or sucrose‐seeking.ConclusionsTogether, our studies suggest that G9a inhibitors could be effective, safe, and novel AUD therapeutics that reduce stress and dependence‐related high levels of alcohol drinking in AUD patients.

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a chronic, relapsing disease that is difficult to treat. Approximately 13.9% of adults in the United States in 2015 met AUD criteria in the past year (Grant et al., 2015) and in 2018, an estimated 16.6 million were heavy drinkers (SAMHSA, 2018). In addition, the economic burden has been estimated at >$200 billion each year (Sacks et al., 2015), and AUDs are also a leading cause of preventable deaths in the United States (Mokdad et al., 2004) Despite these issues, there are only three drugs currently approved in the United States to treat AUDs: disulfiram, naltrexone, and acamprosate. However, these AUD medications are not effective for many patients (Ryan et al., 2017) as most people with AUD do not want to quit drinking alcohol entirely, even if they recognize their alcohol problem and would like to reduce alcohol intake.

One reason AUDs are a pervasive problem is that alcohol is often consumed during times of stress. Alcohol can reduce stress when taken acutely (Becker, 2017); however, continued alcohol use over time actually increases the reactivity of the brain's stress systems. This can cause individuals to increase alcohol drinking to compensate, which propagates a vicious cycle known as “the dark side of addiction” (Koob & Mason, 2016). Since AUD clinical studies show stress increases heavy alcohol drinking (Dawson et al., 2005; Russell et al., 2017) and that stress is a predominant factor in rates of relapse (Blaine et al., 2016; Milivojevic & Sinha, 2017), pharmacotherapies that can reduce the risk of stress‐induced heavy alcohol consumption could be helpful in combating the chronic relapsing nature of AUD. A second reason for the pervasiveness of AUD is that heavy alcohol drinking can produce alcohol dependence, and alcohol dependence further dysregulates the body's stress systems (Becker, 2012) to increase alcohol drinking. Therefore, targeting dependence‐ and/or stress‐related alcohol drinking clinically could greatly reduce “heavy drinking” in AUD patients, potentially halt the downward spiral of “the dark side of addiction,” and reduce stress‐related relapse in abstinent patients. Since many AUD patients present with co‐morbid psychiatric diseases related to stress (Moss et al., 2010), targeting stress‐related alcohol drinking could be particularly useful.

One key mechanism underlying behaviors associated with AUD and/or substance use disorder (SUD) is epigenetics (Anderson et al., 2023; Anderson, Penrod, et al., 2018; Anderson & Taniguchi, 2022). Epigenetics refers to changes in nuclear chromatin structure that can alter programs of gene expression, and these epigenetic changes can be very long‐lasting. Chronic drug and alcohol abuse can produce enduring changes in brain function, and several important epigenetic enzymes, including G9a (also known as euchromatic histone‐lysine N‐methyltransferase 2 or EHMT2) are regulated in addiction‐relevant brain areas, including the nucleus accumbens (NAc). In some cases, the alcohol/drug‐induced changes support addiction‐related behavior (Anderson, Penrod, et al., 2018); however, in other cases, the AUD/SUD‐related changes in epigenetic activity are compensatory and thus reduce AUD/SUD‐related behavior. G9a is an example of the latter, a compensatory change that can reduce AUD/SUD‐related behavior. G9a catalyzes di‐methylation on lysine 9 of histone H3 (H3K9me2) (Anderson, Penrod, et al., 2018; Maze et al., 2010), and H3K9me2 is frequently associated with condensed chromatin and repression of nearby genes. Both cocaine and opioids regulate G9a expression levels in the NAc (Maze et al., 2010, 2014; Sun et al., 2012) and G9a expression levels have bi‐directional effects on stress‐induced cocaine seeking in a preclinical model of stress‐triggered relapse following intravenous cocaine self‐administration (Anderson, Larson, et al., 2018, Anderson, Sun, et al., 2019). Similar to cocaine and opioids, we recently discovered that G9a and H3K9me2 levels in the NAc are both reduced by chronic alcohol exposure (Anderson et al., 2021). Based on these observations, we examined the function of NAc G9a for AUD‐related behaviors in mice. Viral‐mediated reduction of G9a levels—via AAV2‐G9a shRNA—in the NAc blocked stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking (Anderson et al., 2021), which suggested that G9a inhibitors might reduce alcohol drinking. Using systemic administration of UNC0642, a highly selective and brain‐permeable G9a inhibitor (Liu et al., 2013), we observed that 10 days, but not 1 day, of UNC0642 administration (4 mg/kg; i.p.) blocks pharmacological stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking (Anderson et al., 2021). In addition, we recently showed that repeated systemic administration of UNC0642 (4 mg/kg; i.p.) reduces escalated alcohol drinking following a chronic intermittent ethanol (CIE) vapor exposure model of dependence (Lopez et al., 2025). Also, UNC0642 reduces escalated alcohol drinking in a mouse model of combined dependence plus forced swim stress (FSS) (CIE + FSS) (Lopez et al., 2025). These findings suggest that G9a inhibitors might be effective AUD treatments, thus further efficacy and safety studies are warranted.

Here, we present new findings showing that repeated administration of UNC0642 can significantly reduce stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking in both male and female mice at lower doses (2 mg/kg, i.p.) than we previously reported (Anderson et al., 2021). Also, UNC0642 blocks escalated alcohol drinking in a model of combined stress plus alcohol dependence, but does not alter binge‐like alcohol drinking. Using an effective dose of UNC0642 (2 mg/kg, i.p.), we failed to detect behavioral toxicity in many common behavioral assessments. Finally, we performed several pharmacokinetic measures and found that UNC0642 has a long half‐life, is orally bioavailable, is within an acceptable toxicity range therapeutically, and produces no changes in weight even after 7 weeks of repeated dosing. Together, these new data show that repeated, systemic administration of UNC0642 reduces stress‐escalated and dependence‐escalated heavy alcohol drinking, supporting the use of G9a inhibitors as a safe therapeutic for AUD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal care

Adult male and female C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME) were singly housed in a climate‐controlled environment (21°C) on a 12‐h light–dark cycle. Animals were habituated to the housing environment for at least 7 days prior to use in experiments and had food (Diet #2918; Harland Teklad, Madison, WI) and water ad libitum. All drinking and behavioral experiments were performed during the dark cycle. All experiments were approved by the MUSC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) in facilities accredited by the American Association for the Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) or by Touchstone Biosciences. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines established by the National Institutes of Health and the National Research Council.

Experiment 1: Test whether systemic UNC0642 administration reduces stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking and anxiety‐like behavior

Two‐bottle choice ethanol drinking and kappa opioid receptor agonist‐induced stress

Mice received 3 weeks (2 h/day; 5 days/week) of baseline ethanol drinking using a two‐bottle choice design (i.e., 15% [v/v] ethanol vs. water) 3 h into the dark cycle as described previously (Anderson et al., 2021). In general, ethanol intake values are presented as the 5‐day average. During baseline drinking, all mice were given vehicle intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections approximately 30 min before the drinking session. Mice were then matched for average ethanol consumption (g/kg) and assigned to four treatment groups: 0, 1, 2, or 4 mg/kg UNC0642 (i.p.). Mice had 10 days of i.p. injections at 30 min before the drinking sessions and then received 2 days of the kappa opioid receptor (KOR) agonist, U50,488 (5 mg/kg; i.p.), injected 30 min before 2 h, two‐bottle choice drinking sessions.

Elevated plus maze

Next, these same mice were tested on the elevated plus maze (EPM) with open arms set at 120/127 lux. At 30 min postinjections of UNC0642 or vehicle, mice were placed in the center of the arms, and entries/duration in each arm, open and closed, were recorded from above by ANYMaze (Stoelting Co, Wood Dale, IL) video‐tracking system for 5 min.

Experiment 2: Test whether systemic UNC0642 suppresses escalated alcohol drinking in a mouse model of combined dependence plus stress

We next tested the effects of UNC0642 in model of combined dependence produced by CIE exposure plus FSS. Mice were given limited access (1‐h/day) to a single bottle of alcohol (15% v/v) in their home cage 5 days/week for 4 weeks to establish stable alcohol intake. Alcohol access was presented 3‐h after onset of the dark cycle. Mice were weighed weekly. After establishing stable baseline intake, mice were divided into two groups (vehicle vs. UNC0642, balanced for baseline alcohol intake). Mice were then injected with 2 mg/kg UNC0642 or vehicle 30 min prior to the start of ethanol drinking each day. After another 2 weeks (10 days) of alcohol drinking and UNC0642 or vehicle injections, mice were split into an additional 8 groups based on ethanol consumption using a 2 × 4 design (UNC0642 vs. vehicle; CTL, FSS, CIE, CIE + FSS). Mice that received CIE exposure (i.e., CIE and CIE + FSS groups) were exposed to alcohol vapor (16‐h/day for 4 days) in inhalation chambers. Immediately prior to being placed in inhalation chambers, CIE‐exposed mice received an intraperitoneal injection of alcohol (1.6 g/kg) and the alcohol dehydrogenase inhibitor pyrazole (1 mmol/kg), administered together in a total volume of 0.02 mL/g of body weight, whereas mice in the CTL and FSS groups received an injection of pyrazole only. Alcohol concentration in the inhalation chambers was monitored daily, and air flow was adjusted to maintain exposure within a range that yielded stable blood‐alcohol levels within the target range (180–250 mg/dL) throughout each weekly exposure cycle. Blood samples were collected from the retro‐orbital sinus immediately upon removal from the inhalation chambers and assayed using an Analox Instrument analyzer (Lunenburg, MA, USA). CTL and FSS groups were similarly handled, but maintained in control (Air) inhalation chambers. After an 80‐h abstinence period, test drinking sessions resumed for five consecutive days (as during baseline). Mice in the FSS and CIE + FSS groups experienced brief (10‐min) FSS exposure 4‐h prior to each of the test drinking sessions. Water was replaced before each subject, and it was maintained at 23–25°C. Upon completion of each FSS session, mice recovered in their home cage on a heating pad for 5‐min and then promptly returned to colony room. The remaining nonstressed mice (CTL and CIE groups) were left in their home cage undisturbed. Alcohol consumption during baseline and test drinking sessions was determined by weighing drinking bottles before and after the sessions.

Experiment 3. Determine whether UNC0642 reduces binge drinking in mice

After acclimation, mice were given access to a single bottle containing 20% ethanol solution for 2 h for 3 days in a “Drinking in the Dark” paradigm (Rhodes et al., 2005) starting 3 h into the dark cycle. To model a binge session on the 4th day, access to the ethanol solution was increased to 4 h, and the bottles were measured at both 2 and 4 h. After 1 baseline week (2 h/3d, 4 h/1d, 4 days/week) the subjects were assigned to groups (Vehicle or 0.2 mg/kg UNC0642) based on the baseline ethanol intake. The mice were then given a 3‐day period of no drinking but received vehicle or treatment 5 days a week for 10 days. Binge drinking was assessed for 2 weeks while mice received vehicle or UNC0642 (2 mg/kg; i.p.).

Experiment 4. Determine whether UNC0642 produces behavioral inhibition

Subjects were assigned to treatment groups (vehicle or 2 mg/kg UNC0642) based on weight. Mice first received 10 days of vehicle or UNC treatments (2 mg/kg; i.p.) before undergoing behavioral toxicity assays. Mice received an injection 30 min prior to each assay, like in our drinking studies above. Before each test, mice were acclimated after transport for at least 30 min prior to testing.

First, locomotor activity was recorded in a cage filled with a thin layer of bedding and covered with a lid. Test cages were placed inside black Plexiglas chambers (22.5″ × 14.5″ × 9.4″) equipped with photobeam arrays (San Diego Instruments, San Diego, CA) under red light room illumination. Activity was collected as beam breaks for 1 h via eight evenly spaced beams across the cage length and four across the width.

Next, mice were tested in an open field for 5 min. Sessions were video recorded, tracked, and analyzed using the video‐tracking software (AnyMaze).

Then, mice were tested on the Y‐maze test, which leverages the natural tendency of mice to explore the arm of the maze not most recently visited and is a measure of spatial working memory. Mice were allowed to explore all three arms in a Y‐maze for 5 min. The sessions were analyzed using the video‐tracking software (AnyMaze), and the order of entries (80% of mouse in arm) is calculated. “Correct” alternations, meaning that they entered the arm not recently visited, were counted when the mouse entered all three arms without re‐entering a previously explored arm.

Next, mice were accessed on a four‐chamber rotarod (Ugo Basile Apparatus #47600). The rotarod contains a rotating rod, 3 cm in diameter, elevated to a height of 16 cm above the instrument floor, and suitably machined to provide grip for the mice with six flanges dividing the rod into five equally spaced lanes (5.7 cm lane width). For the training day, mice were placed on the rotating rod with a 2‐min run rotating at four revolutions per minute (RPM), and 4–6 h later, mice were placed back on the rotarod at eight RPM. Mice were replaced on the rotating rod if they fell off during the 2‐min run. The following day, test mice were returned to the roller, and the speed steadily increased from 4 to 40 rpm over 5 min. Each animal received a total of four testing sessions: two morning and two afternoon sessions. Time to fall was recorded.

Lastly, learning and memory were measured with contextual fear conditioning. Mice received a training day where they were placed in a fear conditioning chamber (Med Associates, Fairfield, Vermont) and allowed to explore the arena for 2 min, after which a loud auditory stimulus (30 s; ~70 dB) that co‐terminates with a 2‐s mild foot‐shock (0.5 mA) was presented to the animal. The mice were exposed to three tone/shock pairing with a 1‐min interval separating each tone/shock. Twenty‐four later, the animals were returned to the chamber and behavior (freezing) in the context and in a new context was recorded with a video‐tracking system (Video Freeze V2.7; Med Associates). Forty‐eight hours after the training day, mice were placed back in the chambers with the audible tone played in a new context. The number of seconds each mouse was immobile was recorded.

Experiment 5. Test whether UNC0642 alters sucrose‐taking or sucrose‐seeking behavior

Mice were first injected with vehicle or UNC0642 (2 mg/kg, i.p.) once daily for 10 days. Injections of either vehicle or UNC0642 (2 mg/kg) also occurred 30 min before the start of each sucrose self‐administration, extinction, and cue‐induced reinstatement session. All self‐administration experiments occurred in standard operant chambers with two nose‐poke detectors, a house light, and a cue‐light and cue‐tone generator (Med Associates, Fairfield, Vermont). All sucrose‐paired nose pokes, including those during the timeout, were recorded and are reported as “paired nose pokes.” No fasting occurred during the experiment. During the 1‐h sessions, mice were trained to nose‐poke in the paired side on a fixed‐ratio 1 (FR1) schedule with a 15‐s timeout period after delivery of a single sucrose pellet. Concurrent with the pellet delivery, a cue tone and cue light (which is located immediately above the paired nose‐poke port) are activated. All mice had 10 sessions. Next, following a 7‐day period of forced abstinence in the home cage, all mice began extinction training for 6 days (a model of context‐associated sucrose‐seeking behavior). During these 2‐h sessions, nose pokes in either port resulted in no sucrose‐pellet delivery nor presentation of the light and tone cues. Finally, light/tone cue‐induced reinstatement behavior (a model of discreet cue‐induced sucrose seeking) was tested 1 day after the 6th extinction session in a single 2‐h session. Mice were allowed to nose‐poke for light and tone cues without receiving pellets.

Experiment 6. Test the maximum tolerated dose and pharmacodynamic/pharmacokinetic profile of UNC0642

In vivo maximum tolerated dose procedure

Mice were fed a standard laboratory rodent diet and housed in individual cages on a 12‐h light and 12‐h dark cycle with room temperature maintained at 22 ± 3°C and relative humidity at 50 ± 20%. Food and water were provided ad libitum throughout the study. Animals were dosed via injection in the lower right quadrant of the abdomen for i.p. injections at 0, 10, 20, and 30 mg/kg (10 mL/kg) once‐a‐day for 5 days. Animal body weights were recorded daily, and clinical observations were performed once in the morning and once in the afternoon each day.

In vivo PK/TD procedures

Adult mice were housed in individual cages on a 12‐h light and 12‐h dark cycle with room temperature maintained at 22 ± 3°C and relative humidity at 50 ± 20%. Animals were fasted overnight before dosing, with food returned after the 6‐h blood samples were obtained. Water was provided ad libitum. Three animals were dosed via gavage needle for oral administration at 10, 30, and 100 mg/kg (10 mL/kg) and via tail‐vein injection for intravenous administration at 2.5 mg/kg (2.5 mL/kg). All blood samples (25–30 μL per sample) were taken via appropriate vein (saphenous or submandibular vein) at 5, 15, 30 min, and 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 24 h. Blood samples were collected in Greiner MiniCollect K2EDTA tubes, placed on ice, and within 30 min, centrifuged at 15,000 g for 5 min to obtain plasma samples. All plasma samples were stored at −70°C until analysis.

Bioanalysis of PK/PD samples

Plasma samples were prepared as follows. Three volumes of acetonitrile containing internal standard were added to one volume of plasma to precipitate proteins. Samples were centrifuged (3000 g for 10 min) and supernatant was removed for analysis by LC‐MS/MS. Calibration standards and quality controls were made by preparation of a 1 mg/mL stock solution and subsequently a series of working solutions in methanol: water (1:1, v/v), which were spiked into blank plasma to yield a series of calibration standard samples in the range of 1 ng/mL to 10 μg/mL and quality control samples at three concentration levels (low, middle, and high). All incurred PK/PD plasma samples were treated identically to the calibration standards and quality control samples. LC‐MS/MS analysis was performed utilizing multiple reaction monitoring for detection of characteristic ions for each drug candidate, additional related analytes, and internal standard.

Pharmacokinetic data analysis

Plasma concentrations were measured as described above to determine a concentration vs. time profile. The area under the plasma concentration vs. time curve (AUC) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal method. Fitting of the data to obtain pharmacokinetic parameters was carried out using noncompartmental analysis. Key PK parameters reported following intravenous administration were as follows: terminal half‐life (t 1/2), initial plasma concentration (C 0), area under the plasma concentration vs. time curve (AUC), volume of distribution at steady‐state (V ss), total plasma clearance (CLp), and mean residence time (MRT). Key PK parameters reported following extravascular administration were as follows: terminal half‐life (t 1/2), maximum plasma concentration (C max), time to reach maximum plasma concentration (t max), area under the plasma concentration vs. time curve (AUC), mean residence time (MRT), and bioavailability (F).

Statistics

For the CIE + FSS experiment in Figure 2, average weekly alcohol intake (g/kg) served as the primary dependent variable. Two‐way ANOVAs, or Repeated Measures (RM) three‐way ANOVAs were used to analyze behavioral data. Newman–Keuls' post hoc tests were used following significant group differences. These statistics were performed with Statistica (Tibco Software Inc.; Palo Alto, CA) and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

For all remaining data, one‐way, two‐way, or three‐way ANOVAs were used to analyze behavioral data. Repeated measures ANOVAs were used where appropriate. Fisher's LSD post hoc tests were used following significant ANOVAs. A Grubbs outlier test was applied where appropriate. All statistics were performed with GraphPad Prism 8 and p < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical results, including outliers are reported in Table 1.

RESULTS

Experiment 1: Systemic UNC0642 reduces stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking and reduces anxiety‐like behavior

Since UNC0642 (4 mg/kg; i.p.) blocked stress‐potentiated ethanol drinking and dependence‐escalated ethanol drinking in male mice in our previous studies (Anderson et al., 2021; Lopez et al., 2025), we first tested whether this effect was dose‐dependent and whether it was effective in both sexes. As shown in Figure 1A, wild‐type mice (both male and female) were allowed to drink ethanol for 3 weeks prior to repeated, daily injections of vehicle alone or UNC0642 (1–4 mg/kg; i.p.) given 30 min prior to the 2‐bottle choice sessions (Figure 1B). No changes were observed in alcohol drinking during the 2 weeks of daily UNC0642 injections (Figure 1C). However, compared with vehicle‐only control mice, repeated administration of UNC0642 suppressed U50,488‐potentiated ethanol drinking at both the 2 and 4 mg/kg doses as compared to their baseline, but there was no effect at the 1 mg/kg dose (Figure 1D). These data demonstrate a dose‐dependent effect of UNC0642 on stress‐escalated alcohol drinking. Importantly, UNC0642 suppressed U50,488‐potentiated alcohol consumption similarly in male and female mice, suggesting no obvious sex differences in UNC0642 efficacy.

Systemic administration of UNC0642 reduces stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking and anxiety. (A) Experimental timeline. (B) Average two‐bottle choice ethanol drinking at baseline before UNC0642 injections. (C) Average two‐bottle choice ethanol drinking during UNC0642 injections. (D) Average two‐bottle choice ethanol drinking following U50,488 injections ± UNC0642 injections. (E) Time spent in the open arms, (F) number of entries into the open arms, and (G) latencies to enter the open arm of an elevated plus maze. N = 10–13 per group. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

The same mice were next tested on a 5‐min elevated plus maze (EPM) protocol at 1–3 days after their final ethanol drinking session. Thirty to sixty minutes following treatment with vehicle or UNC0642 (1, 2, or 4 mg/kg; i.p.) less anxious mice typically explore the open arms more quickly and spend more time exploring the open arms. No significant differences were observed with any dose of UNC0642 on time spent in the open arms (Figure 1E) or the total number of entries into the open arms (Figure 1F). However, all three doses of UNC0642 significantly reduced the latency to explore the open arms (Figure 1G), consistent with an anxiolytic response to UNC0642. Importantly, this effect was observed in both male and female mice, suggesting no obvious sex differences. Taken together, these results suggest that UNC0642 has anxiolytic‐like properties in addition to the ability to reduce stress‐ and dependence‐potentiated alcohol drinking.

Experiment 2: Systemic UNC0642 administration suppresses escalated alcohol drinking in a model of combined dependence plus stress

One well‐established preclinical model of dependence that causes increased alcohol drinking in mice is CIE exposure (Becker & Lopez, 2004, Griffin 3rd et al., 2009, Lopez et al., 2017, Lopez & Becker, 2005). While the CIE‐dependence model produces dysregulation of the stress systems (Becker, 2012, 2017), humans that are alcohol‐dependent can still increase alcohol drinking in response to stress. So, we next examined whether UNC0642 can reduce alcohol drinking under conditions of both CIE‐dependence and chronic external stress (chronic FSS) as CIE + FSS increases alcohol drinking above the levels produced by CIE alone (Anderson et al., 2016a; Lopez et al., 2016). As illustrated in Figure 2A, mice had 2 weeks of baseline alcohol drinking with a single bottle access, then were split into two even groups based on ethanol intake. We injected mice with vehicle or UNC0642 (2 mg/kg, i.p.) 30 min prior to 10 additional alcohol‐drinking sessions. Notably, there was a reduction in intake in all mice during the first week of injections, possibly due to injection‐related stress. Since we did not observe a similar reduction in Experiment 1 (Figure 1B,C), this could be due to drinking protocol differences in one vs. two‐bottle choice, or could be due to the 1 versus 2 h drinking periods. We then split the mice into eight treatment groups using a 2 × 4 design (i.e., UNC0642 vs. vehicle; CTL, FSS, CIE, CIE + FSS) before having two cycles of CIE + FSS. In this analysis, Group (CTL, FSS, CIE, CIE + FSS) and Drug (vehicle, UNC0642) were the main between‐condition factors and Time (Baseline 1–4) was the repeated factor in this factorial design. The ANOVA indicated only a main effect of Time [F(3,186) = 31.32, p < 0.001] due to a lower overall alcohol intake during Baseline 3–4 compared with baseline Weeks 1–2, possibly due to daily i.p. injections during that period, but all groups consumed similar amounts of alcohol (Figure 2B). Importantly, no differences between the groups were detected during the final week of baseline drinking (Figure 2C). Following the first CIE + FSS, we observed no effects of UNC0642, CIE, or FSS, nor was there a significant interaction (Figure 2D). However, following the second week of CIE + FSS, we detected a significant effect of UNC0642. The analysis of alcohol consumption following the second test cycle indicated a significant interaction between the Group and Drug factors [F(3,62) = 5.26, p = 0.003]. Post hoc comparisons showed a significantly elevated ethanol intake in CIE + FSS vehicle group relative to the CTL vehicle group (p < 0.05), consistent with previous reports that CIE + FSS increases alcohol consumption (Anderson et al., 2016a, Lopez et al., 2016). Importantly, another post hoc test showed that UNC0642 blocked the CIE + FSS‐induced increase in ethanol intake (p < 0.01) (Figure 2E). Of note, CIE‐exposed mice had similar average BACs during the CIE exposure, suggesting all mice had similar levels of dependence during the CIE exposure sessions (Figure 2F). Together, these data suggest that UNC0642 can significantly reduce multiple forms of stress‐escalated alcohol drinking.

Experiment 3. G9a inhibition has no effect on binge drinking in mice

Since UNC0642 blocked stress‐potentiated ethanol drinking in mice, we next tested whether UNC0642 could alter binge‐like ethanol drinking using the well‐established “drinking in the dark (DID)” design (Anderson, Lopez, et al., 2019; Haun et al., 2020; Rhodes et al., 2005). As illustrated in Figure 3A, mice were given a single bottle of 20% (v/v) ethanol in their home cage for 2 h per day for 3 days. On the 4th day, access to the bottle was extended to 4 h to model a binge‐drinking session. After the first week, mice were divided and injected 30 min prior to each alcohol‐drinking session for 10 days with UNC0642 (2 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle. Mice were tested for changes in baseline drinking and binge drinking for 2 weeks. We observed no changes in alcohol drinking during the baseline 2‐h sessions (Figure 3B), or the 4‐h binge sessions (Figure 3C). These data suggest that UNC0642 does not alter binge alcohol drinking and corroborate our prior findings that UNC0642 does not alter baseline alcohol drinking (Anderson et al., 2021; Lopez et al., 2025).

Systemic administration of UNC0642 has no effect on alcohol drinking in a binge‐drinking model. (A) Experimental timeline. (B) Average one‐bottle choice ethanol drinking at baseline and after UNC0642 injections began during the 2 h/day drinking sessions three times a week. (C) Average one‐bottle choice ethanol drinking at baseline and after UNC0642 injections began during the 4 h/day drinking sessions once per week. N = 8 per group. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM.

Experiment 4. Repeated UNC0642 treatment does not produce behavioral inhibition

Since the effects of UNC0642 on stress‐ and dependence‐potentiated alcohol drinking could be caused by a reduction of general behavior (e.g., a general sedative or toxic effect), we next examined the effects of UNC0642 on a battery of standard rodent behaviors. As illustrated in Figure 4A, we first injected mice with UNC0642 (2 mg/kg, i.p) or vehicle once daily for 10 days and then continued these injections once daily, 30 min before the following five behaviors: novelty‐induced locomotor activity, open‐field exploration, Y‐maze, rotarod, and fear conditioning. We did not detect any effects of UNC0642 on body weight (Figure 4B), but we did detect a significant UNC0642‐induced increase in locomotor behavior (Figure 4C) that was largely due to increased activity in the last 30 min of the 1‐h test (Figure 4D), suggesting a possible reduction in chamber habituation. Anxiolytic drugs can increase locomotor behavior (Crabbe et al., 1998; Thompson et al., 2015), so the observed effect might be related to UNC0642's anxiolytic properties (Berkel et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018) (Figure 1G). Importantly, this increase in activity is the opposite that we would expect for a sedative drug or a toxic drug (Ramaekers, 1998). Next, we tested behavior in the open‐field test for 5 min, but failed to detect any influence of UNC on time spent in the center (Figure 4E). Often, repeated tests on the open field show diminished anxiolytic responses (Dai et al., 2016), so this could explain the lack of significant findings here. We also failed to detect effects of UNC0642 on: spatial working memory in the Y‐maze test (Figure 4F), motor coordination in the rotarod assay (Figure 4G), or contextual or cued fear learning and memory (Figure 4H–J). Together, these data indicate that UNC0642, at an effective dose that suppresses stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking, does not produce obvious negative effects on behavior.

Experiment 5. UNC0642 Does not alter sucrose self‐administration or sucrose seeking

In principle, UNC0642 could block stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking by influencing general rewarding behavior, so we tested its effects on sucrose self‐administration. As shown in Figure 5A, we first injected mice with vehicle or UNC0642 (2 mg/kg, i.p) once daily for 10 days. Thirty minutes after the 10th dose, they began the first 2‐h sucrose self‐administration session. The UNC0642 and vehicle injections occurred 30 min before the start of each sucrose self‐administration, extinction, and reinstatement session. Mice were weighed each week, and we detected no differences produced by UNC0642 even after 31 doses (Figure 5B). UNC0642 had no effect on the number of sucrose pellets earned (Figure 5C) suggesting no major impact of UNC0642 on self‐administration behavior. While there was no overall effect of UNC0642 on sucrose‐paired nose pokes, there was a significant interaction between the self‐administration day and the UNC0642 treatment (Figure 5D), suggesting that UNC0642 slightly increased paired nose pokes in female mice. However, UNC0642 had no effects on operant discrimination (Figure 5E), a measure of reward‐reinforced learning and memory. These findings strongly suggest that UNC0642's ability to suppress stress‐ or dependence‐potentiated alcohol drinking (in both sexes) is unlikely due to a general deficit in reward learning and memory or appetitive behavior.

Systemic administration of an effective dose of UNC0642 does not produce any effect on sucrose self‐administration or sucrose‐seeking behavior. (A) Experimental timeline. (B) Average weights across the study. (F) Paired nose pokes during extinction training. (G) Paired nose pokes during cue‐induced reinstatement of sucrose seeking. N = 7–8 per group. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM.

Following a 7‐day period in the home cage, these same mice began extinction training for 6 consecutive days to model context‐associated sucrose‐seeking behavior and extinction learning. During these 2‐h extinction sessions, nose pokes in either port did not result in sucrose‐pellet delivery or light and tone cues. UNC0642 had no effects on sucrose seeking or the rate of extinction learning (Figure 5F), or cue‐reinstated sucrose seeking (Figure 5G), indicating that UNC0642 does not alter any aspect of sucrose self‐administration or sucrose‐seeking behaviors.

Experiment 6. The maximum tolerated dose and pharmacodynamic/pharmacokinetic profile of UNC0642 are within acceptable ranges

To examine the parameters of UNC0642's in vivo toxicity, we tested the maximum tolerated dose by injecting mice once daily for 5 days with either vehicle, 10, 20, or 30 mg/kg UNC0642 (i.p.). No changes in weight were observed for the treatment with vehicle, 10, or 20 mg/kg UNC0642 groups; however, mice treated with 30 mg/kg UNC0642 stopped gaining weight and weight began to decline in all mice (Figure 6A). This weight‐loss effect appeared in both male (Figure 6B) and female mice (Figure 6C). Of note, clinical symptoms (e.g., reduced mobility and labored breathing), were only observed with 30 mg/kg UNC0642 (data not shown). These results suggest that the maximum tolerated dose of UNC0642 is between 20 and 30 mg/kg (i.p.). Since the effective dose is around 2 mg/kg (Figure 1), this suggests UNC0642 is only toxic at 10–15 times the effective dose, which suggests an acceptable therapeutic window.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that the selective G9a inhibitor, UNC0642, reduced several measures of stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking and anxiety‐like behavior, but it did not alter volitional or binge‐like alcohol drinking in male or female mice. We also tested the minimal effective dose, maximum tolerated dose, oral bioavailability, pharmacokinetic profile, and several preclinical models of behavioral toxicity. We found that UNC0642 appears to be a safe pharmacotherapy that reduces stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking with an acceptable therapeutic index and acceptable oral bioavailability. These results suggest that systemic G9a inhibitors could be effective AUD treatments by slowing or reversing the exacerbating effects of stress and curbing high levels of alcohol drinking.

We showed here that G9a inhibition can selectively reduce stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking in two distinct preclinical AUD models. Similar to our previous studies (Anderson et al., 2021; Lopez et al., 2025), UNC0642 dose‐dependently reduced stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking caused by the KOR agonist, U50,488. Here, however, we found that a lower dose of UNC0642 (2 mg/kg; i.p.) reduced stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking produced by U50,488. We also found that the 2 mg/kg UNC0642 dose was effective at reducing stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking in a commonly used model of combined alcohol dependence plus stress (Anderson et al., 2016a, Anderson et al., 2016b, Becker & Lopez, 2004, Griffin 3rd et al., 2014, Lopez et al., 2016, Lopez et al., 2020, Lopez & Becker, 2005). It is notable that G9a inhibition reduced stress‐potentiated drinking in two distinct paradigms, both U50,488 and FSS plus dependence, since these two paradigms differ in several ways, including the timeframe (1 h vs. 2 h of alcohol drinking), choice (two‐bottle choice vs. one‐bottle limited access), and dependence state (nondependent vs. vapor‐exposed CIE). We chose these two different paradigms based on our previous results (Anderson et al., 2021, Lopez et al., 2025) and due to the limited number of stress‐inducible behaviors that show increases in drinking in mice, since there are limited models of stress‐induced increases in alcohol drinking in mice (Becker et al., 2011; Noori et al., 2014). Though these two behavioral paradigms are distinct, it is likely that they are working through a converged mechanism surrounding alcohol‐induced changes in KOR function and/or activation. Forced swim stress increases KOR function (Bruchas et al., 2008; Flaisher‐Grinberg et al., 2012; Varlinskaya et al., 2020) plus previous results show that U50,488 can mimic at least some of the effects of FSS on cocaine conditioned place preference (McLaughlin et al., 2006), learning/memory (Carey et al., 2009), and increasing ethanol consumption (Anderson et al., 2016b). Combined, our findings in these two different models suggest that G9a inhibition likely targets the dynorphin/KOR system to reduce stress‐induced increases in alcohol drinking. Since many patients have AUD and co‐morbid stress disorders (Ersche et al., 2012; Jakuszkowiak‐Wojten et al., 2015; Voon & Dalley, 2016), these data suggest that G9a inhibitors might be particularly useful these patients. Interestingly, UNC0642 does not reduce all forms of alcohol drinking, since there were no effects on baseline or binge‐like alcohol drinking, suggesting a selective effect to blunt stress‐related alcohol drinking (Lopez et al., 2025). This low‐level, baseline alcohol drinking is typically viewed as “volitional” or “nonproblematic” alcohol drinking, akin to humans without AUD drinking alcohol moderately or recreationally (Heyman, 2013; Kwako et al., 2019; Luscher et al., 2020; Nieto et al., 2021). Combined, these results suggest that UNC0642 does not suppress all forms of alcohol drinking, but it targets stress‐, dependence, and anxiety‐linked pathways that escalate alcohol consumption (Anderson et al., 2021, Lopez et al., 2025). This is unlike some medications used for AUD currently (e.g., naloxone) that does reduce volitional alcohol drinking in mice, presumably by blocking the rewarding effects of alcohol. This suggests that G9a inhibition has the potential to reduce high levels of problematic alcohol consumption observed in AUD patients that are dependent and/or suffer from high levels of stress. Since individuals with early‐life stress are much more likely to develop AUD (Lee et al., 2018), G9a inhibition could be helpful for this population of patients.

Though we focused on alcohol drinking in the current study, in the future it would be interesting to test whether UNC0642 might have a broader impact on polysubstance abuse in patients suffering from SUD, since combining the use of both drugs and alcohol is very common (Gipson et al., 2025). NAc G9a reduction is a neuroadaptation common to all major classes of abused drugs, including chronic cocaine (Maze et al., 2010, 2014), alcohol (Anderson et al., 2021), and morphine (Sun et al., 2012). UNC0642 might show therapeutic benefits in cocaine‐ or opioid‐related behaviors, particularly since stress is a major trigger of relapse in CUD and OUD. Of note, stress‐reinstated cocaine seeking was reduced by viral‐mediated G9a knockdown in the NAc (Anderson, Sun, et al., 2019). A similar G9a mRNA knockdown approach in mice also blocked stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking (Anderson et al., 2021), suggesting that the systemic UNC0642 effects might function via a NAc‐specific effect. As such, it will be interesting in the future to examine the effects of G9a inhibitors on stress‐provoked cocaine seeking. Since many individuals with AUD also have co‐occurring SUD (Althobaiti & Sari, 2016; Bierut et al., 2008; Taira et al., 1986), G9a inhibitor therapies could be especially therapeutic for these patients.

In addition to reducing stress‐induced alcohol drinking, we also found that the G9a inhibitor UNC0642 has anxiolytic‐like effects in the EPM test, which is consistent with prior reports in mice (Berkel et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018). This anxiolytic‐like effect agrees with our previous studies on the role of NAc G9a in anxiety (Anderson, Larson, et al., 2018; Anderson, Sun, et al., 2019), suggesting a NAc‐selective role for UNC0642 on anxiety‐like behavior. Interestingly, in the present study, we did not find significant effects on time spent in the EPM open arms or the number of entries into the open arms, but rather a reduction in latency to enter the open arms. Together, these findings suggest a potential anxiolytic mechanism of action (MOA) of UNC0642 that could contribute to—or even be entirely responsible for—the reductions in alcohol drinking during times of stress. In addition, our findings of increased locomotor behavior following UNC0642 also suggest an anxiolytic effect (Crabbe et al., 1998; Thompson et al., 2015). Surprisingly, we did not detect an anxiolytic effect the following day in the open‐field test. This was unexpected as the open field is a well‐established measure of anxiety, and UNC0642 has been shown to have effects on other measures of anxiety, like the light/dark box (Berkel et al., 2019) and the elevated plus maze (Wang et al., 2018). All these anxiety measures rely on a conflict between two contrasting motivations in mice, the motivation to explore a novel environment versus the motivation to stay in a safe, well‐protected, dark space. If the novelty of a box is reduced, then this likely reduces motivation to explore, thus limiting the ability to detect anxiolytic or anxiogenic treatments. Indeed, as shown previously, repeated anxiety tests can lead to reductions in exploration over time (Andreatini & Bacellar, 2000; Cnops et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2016; Varga et al., 2025), likely due to habituation and thus a lack of novelty. Since our locomotor boxes and open‐field chambers were similar in size and the 60‐min open‐field test was performed only 1 day after the locomotor assay in Figure 4, we think it is likely that some interference could have occurred. In contrast, the EPM in Figure 1 was a very different environment that the mice were exposed to for the first time, and for only 5 min. Since we did detect some anxiolytic behavior, it remains possible that UNC0642 blunts stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking, at least in part, due to an anxiety‐related MOA, but we found that a 1 mg/kg dose of UNC0642 had no significant effect on reducing stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking, yet in the same mice, this dose did produce an anxiolytic effect on the elevated plus maze. In contrast, both the 2 and 4 mg/kg doses effectively reduced both anxiety and reduced stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking. This behavioral dissociation, in the same mice, at the same dose, suggests that these two effects of UNC0642 may not share a common MOA. In any event, our data suggest that G9a inhibitors could reduce both anxiety and stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking in patients suffering from AUD. As such, reducing anxiety could still be an effective strategy for some patients suffering from co‐occurring AUD plus anxiety disorders (Ersche et al., 2012; Jakuszkowiak‐Wojten et al., 2015; Voon & Dalley, 2016).

To assess potential nonspecific effects of G9a inhibitors on behavior, we tested many standard mouse behavioral assays, but we found no suggestions of behavioral toxicity. Earlier studies had shown that UNC0642 has a reasonable toxicity: function ratio in cell culture (Liu et al., 2013), but in vivo testing was still needed since it is possible that G9a inhibition could produce sedative effects or other toxic effects that could influence alcohol consumption. Thus, we ran several control experiments following at least 10 daily injections of an effective 2 mg/kg (i.p.) dose of UNC0642. We found no evidence of behavioral inhibition in any of our assays, including locomotor behavior, open field, Y‐maze, rotarod, fear conditioning, sucrose self‐administration, or sucrose‐seeking behavior. While we found no behavior phenotypes suggestive of behavioral toxicity, we did instead find an increase in locomotor behavior following UNC0642 in both male and female mice, suggesting an anxiolytic‐like effect of G9a inhibition (Crabbe et al., 1998, Thompson et al., 2015), or modest motor activating effect. Importantly, if sedative or toxic effects occurred, we would expect a reduction in locomotor behavior just like we observed with higher doses of UNC0642 in our maximum tolerated dose studies (further discussed below). In fact, if behavioral inhibition was occurring, we would expect a reduction in baseline alcohol drinking as well. Since we found that UNC0642 produced no differences in baseline consumption in any of the three alcohol‐drinking experiments herein, this strongly suggests that behavioral inhibition and/or general toxicity are unlikely explanations for the effects of UNC0642 on stress‐potentiated drinking.

Though G9a inhibitors can be effective in reducing stress‐potentiated alcohol drinking, all drugs are toxic at high enough doses (Paracelsus, 1538). For a drug to be safe clinically, there must be an acceptable therapeutic index. The therapeutic index of a drug is the ratio of the toxic dose for 50% of the population divided by the effective dose for 50% of the population (TI = TD50 / ED50). We measured both weight changes and clinical observations following 0 (vehicle), 10, 20, and 30 mg/kg (i.p.) injections over 5 days. We observed clinical symptoms of toxicity only at the 30 mg/kg dose, including reduced mobility and labored breathing, though no mice died from these 5 daily doses. Also, mice in the 30 mg/kg group stopped gaining weight, as opposed to all other groups of mice that continued to gain weight over the 5‐day study. Therefore, our acutely toxic dose is higher than 30 mg/kg (i.p.). In contrast, our effective dose is between 1 and 2 mg/kg, shown by the U50,488 studies. Though more doses and a larger population are needed to establish the exact TD50 and ED50, the therapeutic index is greater than 15 based on these values. Since a therapeutic index of 10 is acceptable for novel pharmacotherapies (Tamargo et al., 2015), G9a inhibitors might be a safe pharmacotherapy. Since weight loss is a toxic sign for UNC0642 at 30 mg/kg, and because toxicity could also build over time, it is important to note that we saw no evidence of weight loss from repeated doses of our effective 2 mg/kg dose in any study above. For instance, in our sucrose‐pellet self‐administration study, we injected mice for 7 weeks for a total of 33 injections, but these mice continued to gain weight normally as compared to vehicle controls. Though these analyses suggest safety, more detailed analyses are needed in the future. Still, repeated treatments with UNC0642 might prove to be safe in humans.

As a candidate therapeutic, UNC0642 has an acceptable half‐life, pharmacokinetic profile, pharmacodynamic profile, and is orally bioavailable. We tested several oral doses (10, 30, and 100 mg/kg), one i.v. dose (2.5 mg/kg), and our effective i.p. dose (2.0 mg/kg). All doses had a normal pattern of blood plasma concentrations over time. The i.p. and i.v. doses had an almost immediate peak and slowly cleared over time. The oral doses had a slower onset, all peaked between 2 and 4 h, and then cleared slowly over time. Interestingly, in most doses, some level of UNC0642 was still found 24 h later, demonstrating a long in vivo half‐life. Since the PK/PD profile shows normal clearance rates, time curves, and steady‐state distribution, this suggests that UNC0642 behaves as a typically prescribed pharmaceutical drug. Oral delivery of UNC0642 is an ideal route of administration for most patients and physicians. We found that the bioavailability of our effective 2 mg/kg i.p. dose of UN0642 is about 67%. Compared with the 2.5 mg/kg i.v. dose, the oral bioavailability was found to increase from 10 (2%) to 30 (15%) to 100 (25%). Since we are only trying to achieve the plasma concentrations of the effect i.p. dose of 2 mg/kg, this suggests that an oral dose between 10 and 30 mg/kg will be sufficient to achieve the therapeutic effects of UNC0642. Of note, no significant sex differences were suggested by these tested parameters.

Since there are well‐documented effects of sex on alcohol behaviors and AUD vulnerability (Agoglia et al., 2020; Ait‐Daoud et al., 2019; Finn et al., 2010; Peltier et al., 2019), these studies were designed to account for potential sex differences in UNC0642 effects, but none were apparent. The underlying reasons for sex differences in AUD are poorly understood, but considering that women have rapidly increasing rates of AUD, develop AUD more quickly, have more vulnerability to alcohol‐induced medical consequences, are less likely to seek help, and have increased likelihood to drink in response to stress (Agabio et al., 2017; Peltier et al., 2019; Tucker et al., 2020), it is important to develop AUD therapies that are beneficial for both sexes. In prior studies testing UNC0642 effects on stress‐potentiated and dependence‐escalated alcohol drinking, we examined only male mice (Anderson et al., 2021; Lopez et al., 2025). In contrast, our current study was appropriately powered to examine sex effects, but no notable sex differences in UNC0642 were detected. However, we observed the well‐documented sex effect that female mice drink more ethanol than males in Figure 1 using two‐bottle choice, but not in Figure 2 with limited access (data separated by sex not shown) nor in our binge‐drinking study in Figure 3. We typically do detect these sex differences as shown by studies from our group (Haun et al., 2020; Lopez, Doremus‐Fitzwater, & Becker, 2011, 2017; Lopez, Grahame, & Becker, 2011) that replicate findings from other groups in the field (Eriksson & Pikkarainen, 1968; Rivera‐Irizarry et al., 2023; Sneddon et al., 2019), so it is unclear why we did not observe this effect across all studies, though our group has observed a lack of sex differences before (Lopez et al., 2020). Nonetheless, UNC0642 was able to reduce stress‐potentiated drinking in both male and female mice regardless of the level of baseline intake.

Our new findings suggest that targeting epigenetic mechanisms to treat AUD could be a fruitful new treatment approach. Importantly, G9a inhibition and epigenetic targeting is a novel MOA for an AUD therapeutic, so UNC0642 represents a unique treatment approach from any of the currently approved AUD medicines. Although more studies need to be performed, the combination of a G9a inhibitor with another drug with a different MOA could act synergistically to reduce excessive drinking in humans. Alternatively, and as suggested by these results alone, G9a inhibitors alone could also be effective at reducing alcohol drinking in humans that suffer from AUD.

In summary, we find that the G9a inhibitor, UNC0642, may be an effective, safe, and novel pharmacological therapeutic for patients that suffer from AUD. UNC0642 reduces potentiated alcohol drinking caused by both pharmacological stress (KOR agonist) and FSS, but it does not alter baseline levels of volitional alcohol drinking or binge‐like alcohol drinking. UNC0642 also has anxiolytic effects in our mice, but it does not lead to general behavioral inhibition or behavioral toxicity on numerous measures of reward, locomotor activity, balance, coordination, learning, and memory. Lastly, UNC0642 is orally bioavailable, has a long half‐life, has an acceptable therapeutic index, and has an acceptable PK/PD profile. As such, while additional safety studies using UNC0642 are needed, human clinical trials examining UNC0642 are needed to test its potential effectiveness as a novel AUD therapeutic.
