Massively parallel in-sensor skinomorphic computing
Authors: Yixiang Li, Yuekun Yang, Cong Wang, Yitong Dai, Xiunan Yan, Dehe Kong, Zhengwei Liao, Shuang Wang, Gong-Jie Ruan, Pengfei Wang, Bin Cheng, Shi-Jun Liang, Feng Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReal-time sensing and processing of a large amount of tactile information is essential for intelligent robotics and wearable technology. However, physical separation between sensors and processors in the traditional tactile sensing scheme makes these functionalities inaccessible, posing a major roadblock to the rapid advance of skinomorphic electronics. Here, we propose a massively parallel in-sensor skinomorphic computing scheme and demonstrate its promising applications in intelligent tactile perception. This scheme allows for achieving parallel sensing and processing of tactile information directly within sensor. We implement this proposed scheme by fabricating a 32×32 flexible capacitive pressure sensors array with excellent uniformity and endurance, and by cascading the sensors array with a memristive crossbar array. We experimentally demonstrate that the broken pressure patterns of the letter ‘NJU’ loaded on the sensors array can be sensed and restored in parallel, which is inaccessible with previously reported tactile technologies. Moreover, by networking the pressure sensors array with two memristive crossbar arrays, we show that textural features of the loaded complex pressure patterns can be directly extracted in a parallel manner and the tactile information can thus be compressed. Our work opens up an avenue for developing intelligent skins capable of real-time and high-throughput tactile perception.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Sensors

Introduction

With the development of wearable technology and flexible edge intelligence1–9, there is a pressing need to realize parallel sensing and processing of tactile information, enabling real-time and high-throughput tactile perception10–13. Traditionally, tactile sensors and processors are physically separated14. The tactile data are sensed in a serial way, and the sensed analog information is converted into digital data suitable for information processing and analysis in a digital processor. In this sense, separate sensing and processing of a large amount of tactile data would lead to huge energy consumption and latency, which is undesirable for intelligent wearable technology and flexible edge artificial intelligence (AI)15–19. Therefore, a new computing paradigm is demanded for realizing energy-efficient and real-time tactile sensing and perception.

Bio-inspired in-sensor computing, as an alternative paradigm, can surmount the challenges associated with energy efficiency and latency in sensing and computing20–27. Previous findings in neuroscience suggest that the Merkel cell, one main type of tactile end organ in biological skin, is capable of sensing gentle touch and is essential for sophisticated sensory tasks, including perception of edges and textures of pressure28–31. Similar to retina-inspired retinomorphic computing32–37, the development of skinomorphic computing inspired by Merkel cells may not only overcome the aforementioned challenges of traditional pressure sensors, but also provide a radically different avenue for the future development of intelligent wearable technology and intelligent flexible edge AI.

In this work, we propose a massively parallel in-sensor skinomorphic computing scheme inspired by Merkel cells of skin receptors for intelligent tactile perception. This proposed scheme is based on frequency division multiplexing (FDM) that employs continuous-time analog reading signals across multiple frequencies for simultaneously sensing and processing38–40. For hardware implementation of this proposed scheme, we network a 32 × 32 flexible capacitive pressure sensor array with a memristive crossbar array, and experimentally demonstrate one-shot sensing and processing of both loaded perfect and broken pressure patterns ‘NJU’. Furthermore, we show that connecting the capacitive sensor with two memristive crossbar arrays enables the extraction of textural features from the loaded complex pressure patterns in a parallel manner. The large-area tactile information can thus be significantly compressed within the sensor. Our work provides an alternative tactile sensing paradigm desired for the future development of intelligent robots and wearable technologies.

Results

Massively parallel in-sensor skinomorphic computing

Figure 1 schematically illustrates information processing in human skin and the skin-inspired skinomorphic in-sensor massively parallel computing. In human skin, Merkel discs and neurons play an important role in sensing and processing tactile information, respectively. The Merkel disc is a specialized tactile end organ formed by the synaptic connection between a Merkel cell and an Aβ-afferent ending41–44. As a key mechanoreceptor in the skin, the Merkel disc is responsible for sensing gentle touch and playing an essential role in complex sensory tasks such as perceiving edges and textures. Meanwhile, functioning as a slowly adapting type I (SAI) receptor, the Merkel discs encode mechanical pressure of spatial features such as edges and textures into bioelectrical signals. Subsequently, the encoded information is processed by the neurons in a massively parallel manner (Fig. 1a). It is such massively parallel sensing and processing capabilities of the skin that make human beings able to achieve real-time external pressure perception. Inspired by the capability of highly efficient information processing in human skin, we propose a massively parallel in-sensor skinomorphic computing paradigm based on a flexible capacitive pressure sensors array (Fig. 1b). Taking full advantage of the physical attributes of the capacitive crossbars, the pressure patterns (e.g., ‘NJU’) loaded on the sensors array can be sensed and processed simultaneously with high parallelism in the full analog domain.

Massively parallel in-sensor skinomorphic computing.

a Schematic of tactile sensing and processing inspired by Merkel discs. b Illustration of skinomorphic in-sensor parallel computing by using a flexible capacitive sensor array. The capacitance change (ΔCij) of each sensor in the array corresponds to its own loading pressure (P). c, d The frequency division multiplexing (FDM) scheme implemented in the capacitive sensor array. The in-sensor parallel processing is essentially a matrix-matrix multiplication (MMM): Vkm·ΔCmn = Ikn, which can be expressed as a series of equations ∑i=1mVikΔCij=∫0TIjkdt, where Vik is the voltage amplitude of the kth frequency in the ith row, ΔCij is the capacitance change in the ith row and jth column, Ijk is the output current amplitude of the kth frequency in the jth column. The frequency spectrum of the input voltage signals of the array constitutes the transform matrix Vkm (c), while the frequency spectrum of the integration of the output current signals represents the processed result matrix Ikn (d). The parallel sensing and processing of a pressure pattern (ΔCmn) is achieved within the physical implementation of MMM based on the FDM scheme.

The mechanism for parallel sensing and processing of the pressure pattern can be understood as follows. Firstly, the whole pressure pattern is applied to the capacitive sensor array. The pressure loaded on the capacitor in the ith row and jth column of the array alters the distance between its upper and lower electrodes, resulting in a corresponding change of its capacitance (ΔCij), as shown in the red box of Fig. 1b. The capacitance change in each capacitor of the array is directly related to its applied pressure, and thus the entire pressure pattern is sensed and converted into the capacitance change matrix (ΔCmn) of the sensors array, in which m and n represent the number of rows and columns of the array, respectively. Next, we employ the frequency division multiplexing (FDM) approach to achieve one-shot readout and processing of the whole ΔCmn matrix, which represents the sensed pressure information. The FDM scheme takes advantage of data representation in a continuous-time analog signal and the physical attributes of the capacitive sensor array to process data in a continuous-time domain. By employing the capacitance law (i = Cdu/dt) and Kirchhoff’s current law, any individual sinusoidal voltage signals with different frequencies can be simultaneously fed into rows and processed by the sensor array. To achieve one-shot readout and processing, continuous-time signals by linear combinations of sinusoidal voltage signals are fed into rows of the capacitor array. Such a continuous-time signal can be transformed into a frequency spectrum with multiple peaks at different frequencies (Fig. 1c). These input continuous-time voltage signals of multiple frequency components can be used for reading and processing the sensed pressure information of the capacitor array to output the continuous-time current signals of multiple frequency components (Fig. 1d). The one-shot readout and processing of the whole pressure pattern loaded on the sensors array are accomplished while these continuous-time signals flow through the sensors array.

The operations of readout and processing of a pressure pattern can be described by the matrix-matrix multiplication (MMM), that is, a weight matrix (Vkm) multiplies the pressure pattern matrix (ΔCmn): Vkm·ΔCmn, in which k denotes the number of frequencies of the signal input into each row. The readout or processing mode is performed depending on whether Vkm is an identity matrix. This MMM can be implemented by using the continuous-time FDM scheme based on the capacitive sensor array. The matrix Vkm is represented by m voltage signals applied simultaneously to the m rows of the capacitive crossbar array. The voltage signal of the ith row consists of k frequency components with different amplitudes (Vik) (Fig. 1c). The full set of voltage signals (V1k,V2k,…,Vmk) forms the matrix Vkm. Following the capacitance law (Cu = ∫0Tidt) and Kirchhoff’s current law, the integration of the output current from the jth column over a time segment (0–T) also consists of k frequency components (Fig. 1d). As for the kth frequency from the jth column, it can be expressed by the equation in the dashed box between Fig. 1c and Fig. 1d. By varying all the k frequencies and n columns in this equation, the resulting k×n equations constitute the MMM: Vkm ΔCmn = Ikn. The processed result, denoted as matrix Ikn is represented by the k frequency components with different amplitudes from the integrations of the output current signals in each column, that is Ikn = (∫0TI1kdt,∫0TI2kdt,…,∫0TInkdt). In particular, if Vkm is an identity matrix, the parallel processing degrades into parallel sensing and readout. Our scheme achieves one-shot sensing and processing of the pressure pattern using the FDM approach. In contrast, the conventional tactile sensor array performs pressure pattern readout sequentially from each sensor, followed by one-by-one analog-to-digital conversion, and then sends it to the backend for processing. This results in significant latency and power consumption, especially for large-area and high-throughput tactile perception tasks. Note that for an N × N pixel array, our scheme enables parallel readout for all pixels in the array by common row lines and column lines with only 2 N ports. Whereas conventional architecture requires individual wiring of each pixel and needs N2 ports to achieve simultaneous readout of all pixels in parallel. Our scheme significantly reduces the complexity of wiring and layout for massively parallel computing compared with conventional architecture. This advantage in scalability is much more pronounced when the array size becomes larger.

Flexible capacitive pressure sensors array

For the hardware implementation of the skinomorphic in-sensor parallel computing scheme, we fabricated a flexible 32 × 32 capacitive sensors array with wide-range, robust, uniform and fast pressure-sensing capabilities (Fig. 2). The structure of each capacitive sensor in the array consists of a polyimide (PI) substrate, a gold (Au) bottom electrode, a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) dielectric layer with columnar microstructure, a poly (3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT: PSS) top electrode and a top PI packaging layer (Fig. 2a). The morphology of the PDMS dielectric layer with its well-defined columnar microstructure is shown in Fig. 2b. The columnar microstructures have a width of 10 μm, a height of 30 μm, and a periodicity spacing of 30 μm. The large-scale periodicity and well-defined columnar microstructure enable robust and uniform pressure-sensing performance across the tactile capacitive sensor array. To achieve better flexibility of the sensor array and facilitate adhesion between the electrode and the PDMS layer, the conductive polymer PEDOT: PSS is used as the electrode material. A detailed fabrication process is provided in the ‘Methods’ and Supplementary Fig. S1.

We conducted pressure response measurements of the flexible capacitive sensor array to evaluate its response range, uniformity, endurance, and speed. The relationship between capacitance change and the applied pressure is fundamental for pressure pattern sensing and processing. We measured the sensor’s capacitance change (ΔC = C − C0) under different applied pressures (Fig. 2c) (C is the capacitance under pressure load and C0 is the baseline capacitance without pressure). The correlation is nearly linear at low pressure loads, as shown in the inset of Fig. 2c. The effective pressure response range spans from 0.155 kPa to 32.875 kPa, enabling the sensor array to effectively detect both light and heavy pressure loads. We also measured the sensor by applying alternating-current (AC) voltages with different frequencies under periodic pressure loading (Supplementary Fig. S2). The sensor can successfully sense the loaded pressure and convert the signal into identical capacitance changes over a broad frequency range from 0 to 5 MHz. Moreover, uniformity and robustness are also critical for in-sensor parallel computing implementation and real-world applications. To assess uniformity, we measured the capacitance responsivity (ΔC/C0) of the 1024 devices within the capacitive sensor array, and the statistical experimental results present high uniformity across the sensor array (Fig. 2d). In terms of robustness, we tested the endurance of the pressure sensor by repeatedly applying and removing pressure over an extended period. The results reveal that there is no sign of degradation within the measurement window (4000 s) (Fig. 2e), demonstrating excellent long-term durability. More characterization tests of the sensor parameters, such as response time, linearity, sensitivity, detection limit, and hysteresis during loading and unloading cycles, are displayed in Supplementary Figs. S3–S6. These results highlight the excellent performance of the fabricated flexible capacitive sensor array, providing a reliable hardware platform for the implementation of skinomorphic in-sensor parallel sensing and processing.

Hardware implementation of skinomorphic in-sensor parallel computing

We experimentally implemented the skinomorphic in-sensor parallel sensing and processing with the capacitive crossbar sensor array (Fig. 3). Figure 3a schematically shows the physical implementation of the MMM in experiments. The conductance values of the memristors in k × m one-transistor-one-memristor (1T1R) array are configured to represent the weight matrix Vkm. Continuous-time sinusoidal voltage signals with k frequencies and a unified amplitude are simultaneously input into the m rows of the 1T1R array as carrier waves. The voltage signal with frequency fi is applied to the ith row of the 1T1R array. The amplitudes of these k frequencies from the m output current signals correspond to the conductance values of the 1T1R array, that is, the matrix Vkm. These currents are then converted into voltages as matrix signals and fed into the m rows of the capacitive array for multiplication with the ΔCmn matrix. To implement this scheme, we connected the 32 × 32 flexible capacitive pressure sensor array with a 32 × 32 1T1R array, along with an automatic pressure loading setup and the peripheral circuits (Fig. 3b). More detailed performance data for the memristor and its array, including uniformity and frequency characteristics, are shown in Supplementary Figs. S7–S12. In conjunction with the frequency characteristics of the capacitive pressure sensor (Supplementary Fig. S2), our integrated system can be operated over a wide frequency range from 0 to 5 MHz, which sufficiently fulfills the requirement of practical deployment in hardware. We employed a 3D-printed relief Teflon pattern with the letters ‘NJU’ (inset of Fig. 3b) and pressed it onto the sensor array through the automatic pressure loading setup. More details about readout circuits and operations are provided in the ‘Methods’ and Supplementary Fig. S13. By programming the conductance of the 1T1R array, which represents the weight matrix Vkm, we demonstrated two distinct operations of skinomorphic in-sensor parallel computing: parallel sensing and parallel processing.

We performed in-sensor parallel sensing by configuring the conductance of the 1T1R array into an identity matrix. In this condition, the voltage matrix signals that are fed into the capacitive sensor array contain only one distinct frequency per row, all with a unified amplitude. We conducted parallel sensing by using a perfect ‘NJU’ pressure pattern loaded on the sensor array. The output signals from the capacitive sensor array indicate that the ‘NJU’ pattern can be accurately captured (Fig. 3c). The experimental results are consistent with the simulation in the frequency spectrum (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. S14), further validating the accuracy and reliability of the parallel sensing and readout process. Moreover, the defective ‘NJU’ pattern loaded on the sensors array (Fig. 3e) can be restored and fixed by configuring the 1T1R array with a specific restoration weight matrix (Fig. 3f). In this case, the voltage matrix signals applied to each row of the capacitive sensors array contain k frequency components, with amplitudes corresponding to the restoration weight matrix. As a result, the broken loading pattern can be successfully reconstructed (Fig. 3g). Note that the restoration matrices for the two similar defective patterns are also quite identical, illustrating that only a small portion of the weights needs to be updated instead of rewriting all weights of the memristor array for a new pressure pattern, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S15. The real-time training of memristor arrays is also feasible and practical, as the on-chip learning of memristor arrays has been demonstrated in the previous work45. Comparisons of our work with recently reported state-of-the-art tactile sensory systems are displayed in Supplementary Note I. The in-sensor parallel restoration of a defective pressure pattern highlights the processing capability of our proof-of-concept skinomorphic sensory system, which is inaccessible with previously reported flexible strain or pressure sensors.

Parallel textural feature extraction of complex pressure patterns

The proposed in-sensor skinomorphic computing scheme can be further used for perceiving complex pressure patterns in the frequency domain while simultaneously compressing the sensory data. The parallel MMM implemented by the capacitive sensor array enables textural feature extraction and tactile information compression directly within the front-end sensor for large-area complex pressure patterns, which typically contain lots of redundant information, e.g., periodic spatial structure. In theory, the textural feature of a pressure pattern can be extracted through a two-dimensional Discrete Fourier Transform that involves a three-matrix multiplication: T = G1‧C‧G2, where C represents the capacitance matrix reflecting the input loading pressure pattern, G1 and G2 are complex transform matrices. T is the final output complex matrix representing the spatial frequency spectrum (textural feature) of the input pressure pattern. As for a square capacitive sensor array, C is also a square matrix and thus G1 = G2 = A + Bi, where A and B are real matrices, and i is the imaginary unit. In this condition, the multiplication can be written as: T = (A + Bi)‧C‧(A + Bi) = (ACA − BCB) + i(ACB + BCA). Since the real part of the textural feature T of the pressure pattern is only considered, T = ACA − BCB can be defined, in which

This operation of matrix multiplication can be completed with high parallelism by using a similar mechanism described in Fig. 3. To implement this task, we networked the capacitive sensors array with two 1T1R memristive arrays, as schematically displayed in Fig. 4a. The photograph of the whole integrated system, including the tactile sensors array and memristor arrays, is shown in Supplementary Fig. S16. Firstly, the pressure pattern is loaded onto the capacitive sensor array, and the conductance values of both memristive arrays are configured proportionally to matrix A. Carrier waves with frequencies f1, f2, …, f32 are applied to the rows of the left memristive array for matrix readout and multiplication. The frequency spectrum of the output signals from the columns of the right memristive array represents the multiplication result of ACA, which is then stored in a buffer memory. Next, the conductance values of both memristive arrays are reconfigured to represent the matrix B. The multiplication of BCB is performed in the same manner under the continuous loading of the pressure pattern on the sensor array, yielding the final result of the textural feature T of the pressure pattern.

We demonstrated two types of textural feature extraction for both periodic pressure patterns and non-periodic pressure patterns, with the corresponding results shown in Fig. 4b and Fig. 4c, respectively. We first performed textural feature extraction for periodic pressure patterns (Figs. 4b-i and 4b-iii). The output textural feature consists of discrete points on the frequency plane (Figs. 4b-ii and 4b-iv). The sparsity of the output spatial frequency spectrum indicates that the tactile information has been significantly compressed compared with the original input pressure pattern. Notably, the spacing between the points on the frequency plane increases as the spatial period of the original pressure pattern decreases, which is consistent with the principle of the two-dimensional Fourier transform. We next conducted textural feature extraction for non-periodic pressure patterns (Figs. 4c-i and 4c-iii). The output textural feature appears quasi-continuous on the frequency plane (Figs. 4c-ii and 4c-iv). As the pressure-loading area decreases, the region of non-zero frequency components expands, which also aligns with the Fourier transform theory. These results validate the feasibility of implementing textural feature extraction based on our capacitive sensor array with high parallelism. Note that the extracted textural features are significantly sparser than the original pressure patterns, enabling accurate tactile pattern recognition with reduced data volume, which is significant for deployed edge intelligent agents. Furthermore, we have performed the recognition task based on the dataset comprising both periodic pattern classes and non-periodic pattern classes. The original pressure patterns and the corresponding spatial-frequency patterns after two-dimensional Fourier transform are displayed in Figs. 4d and 4e, respectively. Neural network recognition details are provided in the ‘Method’ section. According to the results shown in Fig. 4f, the recognition accuracy based on spatial-frequency information is comparable to that of original images, which demonstrates the robustness and effectiveness of pattern recognition based on spatial-frequency information.

Discussion

In summary, we have proposed and experimentally demonstrated skinomorphic in-sensor parallel computing by using a flexible capacitive pressure sensor array in the full analog domain. We achieve one-shot sensing of a perfect pressure pattern loaded on the sensor array and intelligent reconstruction of a broken pressure pattern with massive parallelism. Furthermore, textural features of complex pressure patterns can be extracted directly within our pressure sensor array networked with two 1T1R arrays, with drastically reduced data volume. These findings show that the proposed skinomorphic in-sensor massively parallel computing paradigm is promising for the future development of intelligent embodied robots and wearable devices.

Methods

Fabrication of the PDMS dielectric layer with columnar microstructure

Preparation of the PDMS microstructure dielectric layer was carried out after the hydrophobic treatment of the Si-mold (Supplementary Fig. S1)46,47. The Si-mold was patterned using photolithography, and then the photoresist was used as a mask for plasma etching by inductively coupled plasma (ICP). The result, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S1a, shows that the columnar microstructures were fabricated with a length and width of 10 μm, a depth of 30 μm, and a periodicity of spacing of 30 μm. A 1:10 (wt%) mixture of curing agent to PDMS elastomer (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) was prepared and stirred for 1 h. The prepared solution was then placed in a vacuum for 4 h to remove bubbles. The solution was then spin-coated onto the Si-mold at a speed of 500 rpm for 40 s. Subsequently, the PDMS with the Si-mold was baked at 90 °C for 4 h, and then peeled off from the Si-mold by thermal release tape (TRT) (Supplementary Fig. S1d). Molded PDMS films were then transferred onto a flexible PI substrate printed with strip-shaped bottom electrodes.

Fabrication of the flexible capacitive pressure sensors and memristor array

The sensors array fabrication

The flexible capacitive pressure sensors were composed of a typical sandwich structure. A flexible 32 × 32 capacitive pressure sensor array was fabricated using a crossbar structure with 32 conductive PEDOT:PSS top electrode strips (~2.0 mm wide) for flexible electrodes, a molded PDMS film with a microstructure as the dielectric layer, and 32 bottom electrode strips (~2.0 mm wide, Cu/Au) preprinted on the PI substrate. Finally, the flexible sensor was encapsulated by the PI tape.

The memristors array fabrication

The memristive devices were fabricated according to our previous report39. The device has a typical sandwich structure (Pd/Ta/HfO2/Pd) that consists of the bottom/top electrodes layer Pd (50 nm thick), the HfO2 switching layer (6 nm thick), and the Ta capping layer (80 nm thick). All the above layers were patterned by utilizing standard photolithography, etching, and deposition processes. The feature line width of the device is 2 μm.

Characterization of the flexible capacitive pressure sensors and memristor array

The sensors array characterization

Morphologies of the molded PDMS film with columnar microstructures were investigated by a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, FEI Company). The capacitance of the sensor array was measured using an Agilent B1500A semiconductor characterization system (5 kHz frequency, 2 V bias). Pressure was loaded through employing various 3D-printing pattern molds onto the flexible sensors. Cyclic loading tests of the flexible sensors with different increasing pressure levels were performed at 0.155 kPa to 10.0 kPa.

The memristors array characterization

The electrical characterization of the memristive devices was measured by using an Agilent B1500A semiconductor characterization system, including measurements of basic electrical properties such as uniformity, stability, multi-resistance states, and frequency characteristics. The multi-resistance states of the memristor determine the number of weights that can be used for computing. These results are shown in the Supplementary Figs. S7–S12.

Operations of the pressure sensor array and memristors array

To reliably realize the conductance configuration of the memristor crossbar array, we gradually programmed the conductance values of the memristive devices by applying control signals from an MCU and programming voltage pulses through an Agilent B1530 pulse generator, where the control signals are used for selecting the individual memristors by switching the transistor, and the voltage pulses are employed for tuning the memristor conductance gradually. Accordingly, we programmed the memristor conductance into a defined range using a closed-loop writing method implemented with a script in LabVIEW: if Gread < (Gtarget – ∆G), a positive pulse would be applied on the memristive device; else if Gread > (Gtarget + ∆G), a negative pulse would be applied. The deviations of the programmed conductance values are controlled within about 5.0 μS for each conductance level. Note that the conductance variation can be further reduced by reducing appropriate initial parameters for ∆G used in the closed-loop writing method.

In the hardware implementation, continuous-time voltage signals with identical amplitude and different frequencies are generated by the voltage-controlled oscillators. These carrier wave signals are simultaneously fed into the rows of the memristor crossbar array in parallel. The output signals of the memristor array are then fed into the rows of the capacitive sensor array for multiplication through the amplifying circuit. To obtain the frequency components from the output signals of the sensor array, we employ a digital oscilloscope equipped with a Fourier transform function, which can clearly distinguish different frequencies and read them out.

The amplitudes and frequencies of the input signals are chosen based on the experimental equipment and memristive hardware, so that the parallel matrix multiplication in the frequency domain can be implemented in a reliable way. In this way, the amplitudes of the input voltage signals are constrained below half of the memristor’s switching threshold voltage to ensure precision and accuracy. In experiments, an amplitude of 0.1 V was employed for the first memristor crossbar array. The amplitude applied to the second memristor crossbar array is determined by both the actual pressure pattern and the capacitance parameter of the amplifying circuit. We selected the capacitance value of the amplifying circuit comparable to that of the flexible capacitive pressure sensor. Thereby, the resulting input voltage amplitudes of the second memristor array also maintain below half of the memristor’s switching threshold voltage, avoiding the unintended resistance-state switching.

As for the choice of frequency parameter, the highest frequency component is selected to remain below the analog bandwidth limit of the overall system. Previous studies have demonstrated that memristors can exhibit bandwidths exceeding tens of gigahertz48. Thus, the practical bandwidth limitation arises from the amplifying circuit rather than the memristor devices in our system. The operational amplifier used in our setup (OPA891, Texas Instruments) provides a gain–bandwidth product of approximately 180 MHz, allowing us to flexibly select carrier frequencies well within this range for reliable frequency-division multiplexing operation.

Implementation of massively parallel readout in a flexible pressure sensor array

Signal generators, trans-impedance amplifiers (TIAs), and an oscilloscope with a built-in frequency analyzer were employed to demonstrate massively parallel sensing and processing in the flexible 32 × 32 capacitive pressure sensor array. Signal generators with 32 channels were used as various frequency signal sources. TIAs were used to convert the current signals into voltage signals for measurement. A frequency analyzer was used to measure the output results from the flexible pressure sensor array. In sensing mode, the individual-frequency sinusoidal voltage signals were input as carrier signals into each row of the flexible pressure sensor array. In processing mode, the above-mentioned frequency signals were input into different rows of the memristive devices array with different weights (conductance). The output current-carrying weighted information was converted into a voltage before being fed into the flexible capacitive pressure sensor array. The output signals of the flexible capacitive pressure sensor array in both modes mentioned above were measured by the oscilloscope with a frequency analyzer.

Simulations of the two-dimensional Fourier transform and pattern recognition

The textural feature extraction through two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform in Fig. 4 is to basically carry out three-matrix multiplications: T = ACA − BCB. The above matrix multiplications are performed by the Python NumPy toolbox, and the resulting matrix T is displayed through the Python Matplotlib toolbox. The simulation parameters of the matrices A and B are set according to the matrix’s values shown in the main text. The simulation parameters of the matrix C are set by the pressure pattern with values of each element normalized into the scope of 0–1. Note that the operational principles in hardware implementation of the above three-matrix multiplications are the same as the two-matrix multiplication, which has already been demonstrated in experiments as shown in Fig. 3.

To better demonstrate the potential and usability in real applications, the downstream pattern recognition process following the textural feature extraction is performed. The dataset comprises five classes, including periodic patterns, stripes, and grids, and nonperiodic patterns, circles, rectangles, and triangles. Each class has 200 different binarized images for training and 40 different binarized images for testing. The recognition is based on the original images and the transformed spatial-frequency information of the original images after the two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform, respectively. The recognition is performed by a simple two-layer neural network, and the training and testing processes are achieved through PyTorch.
