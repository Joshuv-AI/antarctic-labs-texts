Prognostic value of radiological and pathological complete response following immune-based conversion therapy in patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (GUIDANCE004)
Authors: Da-Long Yang, Yi-He Yan, Yong-Cheng Lai, Ming-Song Wu, Xiao-Feng Dong, Yao-Zhi Chen, Wen-Feng Li, Fu-Quan Yang, Yong-Yu Yang, Teng-Meng Zhong, Guo-Dong Wang, Qing-Qing Pang, Kang Chen, Ning Peng, Jun-Liang Nong, Ze Su, Ya-Qun Yu, Lin Ye, Fan-Jian Zeng, Shao-Ping Liu, Xue-Yao Wang, Hong-Bing Yao, Chuang Qin, Ying-Yu Zhang, Jie Liu, Mian-Jing Li, Yong-Rong Liang, Pei-Sheng Wu, Fu-Xin Li, Xian-Shuang Mao, Shu-Chang Chen, Jun-Jie Ou, Min Luo, Si-Cong Lu, Zhi-Cheng Li, Shen Huang, Jia-Yong Su, Huan Chen, Yi-Ge Zhang, Xiu-Mei Liang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & aimsIt is unclear whether radiological complete response (rCR) and pathological complete response (pCR) after immune-based conversion therapy are associated with each other and with the prognosis of patients with initially unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC).MethodsData were retrospectively analyzed from 1,262 patients with uHCC who received immune-based conversion therapy at 22 medical centers across China between 2019 and 2023. Survival was compared among patients showing different responses through conventional analysis of all patients, conventional analysis of propensity score-matched subgroups, and landmark analyses at 5 and 7 months after treatment.ResultsConversion therapy led to tumor downstaging in 343 patients (27.2%) and upstaging in 91 (7.2%) based on modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) criteria. The 254 patients who achieved rCR showed significantly better overall survival at 3 years than the 1,008 who did not (79.4% vs. 34.0%), as well as significantly longer progression-free survival (hazard ratio [HR] 0.22, 95% confidence interval 0.19–0.26). These survival benefits were confirmed in landmark analyses at 5 and 7 months and in propensity score-matched subgroups. pCR was associated with better overall survival (HR 0.29) and recurrence-free survival (HR 0.40). Among those undergoing hepatectomy after conversion therapy, rCR was strongly associated with higher pCR rates (67.1% vs. 13.8% in non-rCR patients; Φ = 0.55). Multivariate analysis identified the following baseline predictors of rCR: Child–Pugh A grade, α-fetoprotein <400 ng/ml, solitary tumors, no macrovascular invasion, and absence of extrahepatic metastasis. The only preoperative predictor of pCR was rCR (odds ratio 16.65).ConclusionsBoth rCR and pCR may be reliable indicators of prognosis after immune-based conversion therapy in patients with uHCC. These metrics and their predictors may be useful for selecting which patients can benefit from conversion therapy.Impact and implicationsIt is unclear whether radiological and pathological complete responses after immune-based conversion therapy are associated with each other and with the prognosis of patients with initially unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma. This large-scale retrospective study demonstrates a strong correlation between radiographic and pathologic complete responses, both of which are associated with significantly improved survival outcomes. These findings provide valuable clinical insights, indicating that radiological and pathological complete responses may serve as reliable prognostic indicators following immune-based conversion therapy in this patient population. Furthermore, these response metrics could be used to identify patients most likely to benefit from such therapeutic approaches.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Immunotherapy, Conversion therapy, Complete response

Introduction

Most patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are diagnosed when the disease is already in intermediate or advanced stages, normally too late for potentially curative hepatectomy.1 The so-called ‘conversion therapy’, particularly when based on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), can lead to tumor downstaging that enables some of these patients to benefit from surgery or transplantation,2,3 leading to better survival than with purely palliative treatments.4,5 For example, work from our group and others has suggested that the combination of ICIs with transarterial chemoembolization and anti-angiogenic agents may be quite effective at converting initially unresectable disease.[6], [7], [8]

Consensus guidelines do not recommend how to select patients who have undergone conversion therapy for subsequent hepatectomy or transplantation, so research is needed on what factors would be helpful for this purpose. One possible factor is radiological complete response (rCR), assessed according to modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) criteria.9 This response has been strongly associated with longer overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS),10 but its reliability is controversial:11 some patients may experience early recurrence after achieving rCR,12 whereas some may fail to show rCR and yet go on to experience tumor shrinkage or disease stabilization.13 Another prognostic factor may be pathological complete response (pCR), which has been associated with better OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS).14 A better prognostic indicator may be pCR because in patients who achieve rCR after conversion therapy and subsequently undergo hepatectomy, viable tumor cells are detected in 42.9–59% of patients based on pathological examination.2,15 The problem with pCR is that it can be determined only after complete resection. If rCR can be shown to correlate with pCR after immune-based conversion therapy, it would help establish rCR as a useful preoperative factor for guiding decisions in the treatment and management of patients with initially unresectable HCC. Such correlation has been suggested in a handful of studies involving relatively small cohorts,16 but it remains to be confirmed in a large cohort.

To address these two research gaps, the present study retrospectively examined a cohort of patients with unresectable HCC who underwent immune-based conversion therapy at 22 medical centers across China. It assessed the usefulness of rCR and pCR after conversion therapy for predicting prognosis in the longer term, and it examined whether the two therapeutic milestones correlate with each other. It also explored whether clinical characteristics at baseline may be useful for predicting rCR and pCR.

Patients and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital (approval number KY2024386) and by the institutional review boards of participating centers. In addition, this study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06405321) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.17 The committees waived the requirement for informed consent because patients, at the time of admission, had consented to the analysis and publication of their anonymized medical data for research purposes. The data for this study were extracted from the ‘Guangxi Liver Cancer Clinical Study Alliance (GUIDANCE)’ registry, curated by the Liver Cancer Committee of the Guangxi Anticancer Association.5,8,18

Patients at each of the 22 participating medical centers in China were eligible for enrollment if they met the following criteria: 18–75 years old; initially unresectable HCC, confirmed by a multidisciplinary board according to Chinese guidelines (Supplementary method S1);19 score of 5–7 points on the Child–Pugh scale; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 0–1; and completion of at least two cycles of immune-based conversion therapy (e.g. PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors combined with anti-angiogenic agents with or without locoregional therapies) between 1 January 2019 and 30 June 2023.

Patients were excluded if they had a history of other primary malignancies within 5 years before enrollment; their medical data were incomplete; they had hypovascularized HCC, which can complicate assessment of mRECIST criteria;9 or they were followed up for less than 6 months from the start of conversion therapy. Otherwise eligible patients who died or progressed within 6 months from the start of therapy were included in our cohort to ensure that the analysis would capture early treatment failure and early mortality.

Immune-based conversion therapy

Immunotherapy was recommended for patients with unresectable HCC in accordance with the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) guidelines and the China National Liver Cancer guidelines,19,20 following multidisciplinary discussion. All patients received at least one of the following ICIs intravenously once every 3 weeks: tislelizumab (BGB-A317; BeiGene, Beijing, China), 200 mg; sintilimab (IBI308; Innovent Biologics, Suzhou, China), 200 mg; camrelizumab (SHR-1210; Jiangsu HengRui Medicine, Jiangsu, China), 200 mg; toripalimab (triprizumab or JS001; Shanghai Junshi Biosciences, Shanghai, China), 240 mg; or atezolizumab (Tecentriq; Roche, Basel, Switzerland), 1,200 mg. The decision to combine ICIs with anti-angiogenic agents or locoregional therapies was ultimately taken by the patient or their family, who considered the recommendations of the multidisciplinary team, financial factors, and possibly other considerations.

Conversion therapy was conducted until the patient was judged potentially eligible for curative local therapy, until the disease progressed, or until an intolerable adverse event occurred, after which subsequent treatment was decided on a case-by-case basis. Those whose disease progressed were usually advised to switch to second-line therapy or to start taking first-line drugs that had not previously been used. Other options included a combination of the existing therapy with other modalities or participation in a suitable clinical trial. The final decision for subsequent therapy was made by the patient based on recommendations from clinicians, who considered underlying liver disease, prior treatments, and characteristics of tumor progression when making their recommendations.

Patients chronically infected with the hepatitis B or C virus received routine oral antiviral therapy as part of their overall care.21

Hepatectomy

Hepatectomy was the only curative treatment given to selected patients after immune-based conversion therapy; none of the patients in our cohort underwent liver transplantation. A multidisciplinary team reassessed whether patients were eligible for potentially curative hepatectomy according to Chinese guidelines.19 To be considered resectable, HCC had to meet all the following criteria:22 (1) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1; (2) sufficient remnant functional liver volume after excision of all nodules, defined as a remnant liver volume ≥30% of the standard liver volume in patients without cirrhosis or ≥40% of the standard liver volume in patients with cirrhosis; (3) indocyanine green retention rate <15% within 15 min; (4) Child–Pugh liver function score of 5–7; and (5) no cancer thrombus involving the main portal vein or portal vein branches on the lobe opposite the primary focus and no cancer thrombus involving the inferior vena cava. Ultimately, the choice to undergo hepatectomy was made by the patient or their legal guardians, who considered the recommendations of the multidisciplinary team, financial aspects, and perhaps other factors.

When performed after conversion therapy, hepatectomy followed the final transarterial chemoembolization cycle by at least 4 weeks, the last ICIs by approximately 4 weeks, and the last anti-angiogenic agents by 1 or 2 weeks.19 The clinicians performing surgery at each study site had more than 10 years of professional experience. Surgery targeted intrahepatic tumors and, if patients had extrahepatic metastases, also included hilar lymph nodes.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from the start of conversion therapy until death from any cause or the date of last follow-up. Secondary endpoints were PFS, defined as the time from the start of conversion therapy until tumor progression based on mRECIST guidelines,9 or death from any cause; RFS, defined as the time from the date of hepatectomy until recurrence (emergence of new intrahepatic lesions or extrahepatic metastases based on mRECIST guidelines), death from any cause, or last follow-up if recurrence or death did not occur; objective response rate, defined as the number of patients who showed complete or partial response after conversion therapy, based on vein contrast-enhanced computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging and mRECIST criteria; and rCR, defined as absence or complete disappearance of any arterial hyperenhancement after conversion treatment, as stipulated in mRECIST and confirmed by two radiological evaluations at least 4 weeks apart. In patients who underwent hepatectomy after conversion therapy, pCR was defined as the absence of residual viable tumor in successive pathology sections of all surgical resection specimens, whereas major pathological response (MPR) was defined as a reduction of at least 90% in the number of viable tumor cells within the tumor bed, based on successive thin sections of intrahepatic tumor specimens and, if present, thin sections of extrahepatic metastases in lymph nodes or elsewhere.14,19,23 Details about the determination of pathological response are provided in Supplementary method S2.

Follow-up

All patients underwent rigorous follow-up, initiated with visits every 6–8 weeks after the first cycle of conversion therapy. Patients undergoing hepatectomy were evaluated every 2–3 months during the first 2 years after surgery, followed by semi-annual assessments thereafter.

Each follow-up comprised physical examinations; blood analyses, including assay of ɑ-fetoprotein; and imaging studies, such as contrast-enhanced venous computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. Assessments were carried out during outpatient clinic visits or following readmission; survival data were collected via telephone if necessary. The final follow-up was conducted on 15 June 2024.

All imaging was interpreted by one senior radiologist at each participating center, as well as by one senior radiologist at the leading center, Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital. All interpreting radiologists had at least 10 years of professional experience, and the radiologist at the leading center was blinded to clinical outcomes. Treatment response was assessed according to mRECIST and RECIST version 1.1 criteria,24 with discrepancies resolved by a second radiologist at the leading center (Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital), who also considered clinical symptoms and ɑ-fetoprotein levels during the assessment of response to treatment.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed statistically using SPSS 28.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Analysis, Vienna, Austria). Results associated with a two-tailed p <0.05 were considered significant. Continuous data were reported as mean ± SD if normally distributed or as median (IQR) if skewed, whereas categorical data were expressed as n (%). Inter-group differences in continuous variables were assessed for significance using Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences in categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

OS and PFS were calculated using Kaplan–Meier curves, which were then compared between patients who achieved rCR and those who did not after conversion therapy using the log-rank test. In subgroup analyses, survival was compared between patients who achieved rCR and those who did not after they had been stratified according to known risk factors for poor survival. Variables associated with poor survival in the present cohort were identified through univariate Cox regression, and those with p <0.05 were entered into multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression. When appropriate, risk was quantified in terms of hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95% CIs.

In some analyses, survival was compared between subsets of patients who achieved rCR and those who did not and who had been matched at a ratio of 1:2 based on propensity scores that accounted for the standardized mean differences in all 17 clinical variables in Table 1.25 During matching, there were no retractions, and the caliper width was 0.2.

To verify the robustness of observed associations between survival and either rCR or pCR, landmark analyses were conducted in the entire cohort and across subgroups in different BCLC stages.26 For rCR, the landmark times were chosen to be 5 and 7 months after the start of conversion therapy because 75% and 90% of patients in our cohort, respectively, had achieved rCR by these time points.27 For pCR, analyses were performed at landmark times of 3 and 6 months after surgery. In a landmark analysis of OS, patients who died or were lost to follow-up before the landmark time were excluded. In a landmark analysis of PFS, patients who died, experienced disease progression, or were lost to follow-up before the landmark time were excluded. In a landmark analysis of RFS, patients who died, experienced disease recurrence, or were lost to follow-up before the landmark time were excluded.

Predictors of rCR and pCR in our cohort were identified by conducting ‘forced-entry’ multivariate logistic regression on variables that were significantly associated with these outcomes in univariate analysis (p <0.05) as well as on the following variables that seemed likely to be relevant based on the literature and clinical experience: HBV infection, Child–Pugh liver function score, α-fetoprotein level, maximum tumor diameter, number of tumors, macrovascular invasion, and extrahepatic metastasis. The strength of correlation between rCR and pCR was assessed using the phi (Φ) coefficient.

Results

Patient characteristics at baseline and response to conversion therapy and subsequent hepatectomy

Of 2,372 patients with initially unresectable HCC who were screened for enrollment, 1,262 were considered to fulfill all inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria (Fig. 1), with nearly equal numbers presenting with early/intermediate or advanced disease at baseline (Table S1). The 254 patients (20.1%) who achieved rCR after immune-based conversion therapy, compared with the 1,008 patients (79.9%) who did not, were less likely at baseline to be in an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 1, Child–Pugh class B, or BCLC stage C or to have α-fetoprotein level ≥400 ng/ml, multiple tumors, macrovascular invasion, or extrahepatic metastasis (Table 1). Conversely, they were more likely to receive transarterial chemoembolization. Patients who achieved rCR showed significantly smaller maximum tumor diameter and median platelet count at baseline. These differences became negligible between propensity score-matched subgroups of 240 patients who achieved rCR and 480 who did not, for whom standardized mean differences were below 0.1 for all baseline variables that we examined.

Flowchart of patient selection and analysis. HCC, hepatocellular carcinoma; pCR, pathological complete response; rCR, radiological complete response.

The median duration from the commencement of conversion therapy to the attainment of rCR was 3.3 (IQR 2.1–4.5) months, and patients who experienced rCR received a median of 5 (IQR 3–8) cycles of ICIs, compared with a median of 3 (2–5) cycles in patients who did not experience rCR. Just over half of all patients (54.7%) received tislelizumab, whereas smaller proportions received sintilimab (23.0%), camrelizumab (19.8%), toripalimab (1.7%), or atezolizumab (0.8%) (Table S2). In addition to ICIs, 76.1% of patients received tyrosine kinase inhibitors, whereas 82.3% underwent transarterial chemoembolization.

The rate of rCR was higher based on mRECIST criteria than based on RECIST version 1.1 criteria, whether across the entire cohort (20.1% vs. 2.5%) or among those who did not undergo hepatectomy after conversion therapy (11.9% vs. 2.1%) (Table S3). Among patients who did not achieve rCR after conversion therapy, just over one-third experienced progressive disease, a similar proportion or more achieved partial response, and the remainder achieved stable disease. The rate of rCR after conversion therapy did not vary significantly with the etiological subtype of HCC (Fig. S1A).

Based on Sankey diagrams of the entire cohort, tumors after conversion therapy were downstaged in 343 patients (27.2%) but upstaged in 91 (7.2%) according to mRECIST criteria (Fig. S2A and B). Among the patients who failed to achieve rCR, 201 (58.6%) subsequently underwent hepatectomy, all of whom achieved at least partial response after conversion therapy, whether based on mRECIST or RECIST version 1.1 criteria. Substantially more patients in BCLC stage A/B at baseline underwent hepatectomy than patients in BCLC stage C (40.4% vs. 17.3%, p <0.001; Fig. S3C).

The rate of rCR was significantly higher among those who received transarterial chemoembolization (21.6–22.5%) than among those who did not (8.6–10.8%) (Fig. S4A). In contrast, among the 356 patients who underwent hepatectomy after conversion therapy, the rate of pCR did not vary significantly with the conversion therapy regimen (Fig. S4B).

Comparison of survival between patients who achieved rCR and those who did not after conversion therapy

Follow-up lasted a median of 23.5 (IQR 22.5–24.7) months after the start of conversion therapy. During follow-up, 31 of the 254 patients (12.1%) who achieved rCR after conversion therapy passed away, compared with 395 of the 1,008 patients (39.2%) who did not achieve rCR. The rate of OS was significantly higher among those who achieved rCR, whether across the entire cohort (79.4% vs. 34.0% at 3 years, p <0.001; Fig. 2A), in the subset of patients matched to each other through propensity scoring (HR 0.29, 95% CI 0.22–0.39; Fig. 2B), or in subgroups of the entire cohort stratified according to clinical variables at baseline (Fig. S5A), including most etiological types of HCC (Figs. S1B, D, F, and H) and BCLC stages (Fig. S6A and C). Similarly, the rate of PFS was significantly higher among those who achieved rCR, whether across the entire cohort (87.9% vs. 48.4% at 1 year, 74.4% vs. 25.4% at 2 years, and 59.7% vs. 14.7% at 3 years; HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.19–0.26; Fig. 2C), in the subset of patients matched to each other through propensity scoring (Fig. 2D), or in subgroups of the entire cohort stratified according to clinical variables (Fig. S5B), including most etiological types of HCC (Figs. S1C, E, G, and I) and BCLC stages (Fig. S6B and D).

Neither OS nor PFS varied significantly with the ICI used, except in the case of camrelizumab, which was associated with worse survival than tislelizumab (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.54–0.86, p <0.05; Fig. S4C and D). In contrast, both OS and PFS were significantly better when ICIs were combined with both tyrosine kinase inhibitors and transarterial chemoembolization than with neither of these adjunct therapies or with only one of them (Fig. S4E and F).

Landmark analyses confirmed the positive associations described above between survival and rCR. Patients who achieved rCR showed significantly better OS and PFS at the landmarks of 5 and 7 months than those who did not achieve rCR, whether in the overall cohort (p <0.001; Fig. 3) or in subgroups stratified by BCLC stage (all log-rank p <0.001; Fig. S7).

Comparison of survival between patients who achieved pCR and those who did not after conversion therapy

Among the 356 patients who underwent hepatectomy after conversion therapy, pCR was associated with significantly higher rates of OS at 1 year (95.9% vs. 92.8%), 2 years (92.7% vs. 72.7%), and 3 years (89.2% vs. 62.2%), indicating significant OS benefit (HR 0.29, 95% CI 0.17–0.49; Fig. S8B). Similarly, pCR was associated with significantly higher rates of RFS at 1 year (87.8% vs. 72.4%) and 3 years (71.8% vs. 38.7%), indicating significant RFS benefit (HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.27–0.59; Fig. S8C). In fact, both OS and RFS were significantly better in patients who achieved pCR after conversion therapy than in those who failed to achieve major pathological response (p <0.001; Fig. S8D and E). In addition, RFS (log-rank p = 0.036), but not OS (p = 0.467), was significantly better in those who achieved pCR than in those who achieved major pathological response.

Landmark analyses confirmed the positive associations described above between survival and pCR. Patients who achieved pCR showed significantly better OS and RFS at the landmarks of 3 and 6 months than those who did not achieve pCR (all log-rank p <0.05; Fig. S9).

Comparison of survival between patients who underwent hepatectomy and those who did not after conversion therapy

Among patients who achieved rCR after conversion therapy, subsequent hepatectomy was associated with significantly higher OS (HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.18–0.74) but with similar RFS (HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.68–1.98) compared with other downstream treatments (Fig. S10).

Predictors of survival after conversion therapy

Multivariate analysis identified the following independent predictors of better OS and PFS (Table 2): rCR after immune-based conversion therapy, Child–Pugh liver function grade A, maximum tumor diameter <10 cm, single tumor, absence of macrovascular invasion, and absence of extrahepatic metastasis. Variables that independently predicted better PFS, but not better OS, were ɑ-fetoprotein <400 ng/ml and absence of liver cirrhosis. An Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group score of 0 independently predicted better OS, but not better PFS.

Hepatectomy after conversion therapy was associated with significantly longer median OS (67.0 vs. 24.9 months, p <0.001) and PFS (32.7 vs. 12.0 months, p <0.001) (Fig. S3A and B). Among patients who underwent hepatectomy, rCR after conversion therapy was associated with significantly longer OS (HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.16–0.44) and RFS (HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.25–0.59) (Fig. 4). Multivariate analysis identified two independent predictors of better OS among patients who underwent hepatectomy (Table S4): prothrombin time within 13 seconds and achievement of rCR after conversion therapy. It identified several independent predictors of better RFS among those patients: absence of extrahepatic metastasis, conversion therapy that included transarterial chemoembolization, and achievement of rCR after conversion therapy.

Among patients who achieved rCR, hepatectomy was associated with significantly higher OS (HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.18–0.74) but not RFS (HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.68–1.98) (Fig. S10). Among the 108 patients who achieved rCR but did not undergo hepatectomy, OS and PFS were similar between those who continued ICIs and those who did not (Fig. S11). Nearly all recurrences in patients who achieved rCR after conversion therapy were intrahepatic, regardless of whether patients underwent hepatectomy (Table S5).

Correlation between rCR and pCR after conversion therapy

Among the 356 patients who underwent hepatectomy, 127 (35.7%) attained pCR. The rate of pCR after conversion therapy was much higher among those who achieved rCR than among those who did not achieve rCR (67.1% vs. 13.8%; Fig. S8A). In contrast, the rate of major pathological response was similar (11.0%) between those who achieved rCR and those who did not. The outcomes rCR and pCR correlated strongly with each other, based on the chi-square test (χ2 = 106.7, df = 1, p <0.001) and the Φ coefficient (0.55, p <0.001).

Predictors of rCR and pCR after conversion therapy

Univariate logistic regression identified several clinical factors at baseline that were associated with rCR after conversion therapy, of which Child–Pugh liver function grade B, ɑ-fetoprotein ≥ 400 ng/ml, multiple tumors, macrovascular invasion, and extrahepatic metastasis turned out to independently predict a lower likelihood of rCR (Table S6).

Univariate analysis identified age ≥60 years and rCR as associated with pCR, but only rCR emerged as an independent predictor of pCR (Table S7).

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study offers vital perspectives on the prognostic implications of rCR and pCR following immune-based conversion therapy in unresectable HCC. Our results indicate that attaining rCR, as determined by the mRECIST criteria, is strongly associated with longer OS and PFS, even after adjusting for baseline confounding factors through propensity score matching. Our results provide evidence from a relatively large cohort confirming the prognostic value of rCR in patients with initially unresectable HCC, and they provide evidence of such prognostic value after immune-based conversion therapy. They also establish a strong correlation between rCR and pCR after conversion therapy. The large cohort permitted extensive subgroup analyses, showing, for example, that the survival benefit of rCR applies to patients in early or advanced disease stages at baseline. Several preoperative factors were identified that may be useful for identifying patients more likely to benefit from immune-based conversion therapy and subsequent hepatectomy.

Our results confirm those of a smaller study that showed a 3-year OS rate of nearly 80% for patients who achieve rCR (Table S8).16 Indeed, these results are consistent with several studies highlighting the prognostic value of early tumor response to systemic therapies in patients with initially unresectable HCC.2,10,28 The combination of durvalumab with both an anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody and transarterial chemoembolization was associated with favorable OS and PFS in the EMERALD-1 trial, as was the combination of pembrolizumab with both lenvatinib and transarterial chemoembolization in the LEAP-012 trial.6,7 In our own previous research, such triple conversion therapy led to rCR in ∼25% of patients, based on mRECIST criteria.8 Indeed, rCR emerged from the present study as an independent predictor of survival. The durability of rCR-associated survival benefits was confirmed in our study through landmark analyses, which reinforce rCR as a robust intermediate endpoint for clinical decision-making.

Subgroup analyses of our cohort indicated that the triple combination of ICIs with tyrosine kinase inhibitors and transarterial chemoembolization was associated with a higher rate of rCR and better survival than double combinations or ICI monotherapy. Nevertheless, we did not detect significant differences in rates of pCR across the conversion therapy regimes, nor did we detect significant heterogeneity among the different types of ICIs, except in the case of camrelizumab.

In this cohort, α-fetoprotein protein ≥400 ng/ml, multiple tumors, and lack of transarterial chemoembolization during conversion therapy were identified as several factors that predicted worse PFS, which is consistent with previous work.29 Our results link rCR and subsequent hepatectomy to better survival, also consistent with previous studies.15,30 Among our patients who achieved rCR, OS but not RFS was better for those who subsequently underwent hepatectomy, similar to previous studies.15,31

We caution that comparing outcomes between patients who underwent hepatectomy and those who did not after conversion therapy may be confounded by bias, given that those selected for hepatectomy might present more favorable tumor biology as well as better treatment response. Indeed, the process of selection in our study involved multidisciplinary discussions that took a comprehensive account of the patient’s situation, in alignment with Chinese guidelines and clinical practices from many countries. As a result, patients in our cohort who underwent surgery after conversion therapy responded better to the therapy than those who did not undergo surgery. It may be time for the development of consensus guidelines that standardize criteria for selecting candidates for surgery following conversion therapy. In this regard, our findings mark an important milestone by comparing prognosis with or without hepatectomy after achievement of immune-mediated tumor control, because prognosis may differ from the traditional situation of tumor control achieved through anti-angiogenic agents.

The present study provides evidence from a large cohort supporting the emerging paradigm of ‘conversion–consolidation’ therapy to treat initially unresectable HCC, in which successful immune-based conversion therapy should be followed by hepatectomy whenever possible. Our findings suggest significantly better OS and PFS when rCR is followed by hepatectomy than when it is not. At the same time, rCR is no guarantee that resection will prevent recurrence. One third of patients in our cohort who achieved rCR and then underwent hepatectomy suffered recurrence during follow-up, and other studies have shown that up to 59% of patients achieving rCR harbor residual tumors after resection.2,15 In contrast, achievement of pCR after conversion therapy was associated with an excellent 3-year OS of 89.2% in our cohort. These findings highlight the greater prognostic accuracy of pathological than radiological response, as reported in other studies,14,15 even though the two indices correlated strongly in our cohort. The superior performance of pathological response may reflect its ability to detect immune-mediated tumor necrosis that imaging cannot because such necrosis does not cause structural collapse or fibrotic ‘scarring’.32 Because assessing pathological response requires surgery, future research should identify reliable non-invasive surrogates for pCR, such as radiomic indices or levels of tumor DNA in circulation.

Maintaining ICI treatment longer than 6 months in patients who achieve rCR has been shown to prolong survival,16 but we did not observe a survival benefit of ICI maintenance in our cohort. This discrepancy between the studies may reflect differences in type, duration, or dosage of ICIs.

This study excluded patients with a follow-up period of less than 6 months, while retaining those who died or progressed within 6 months. Early progression or early death may be associated with a delayed response to immunotherapy (pseudoprogression).33 Excluding these patients with early mortality could artificially inflate survival outcomes and underestimate the true failure rate of conversion therapy. Our inclusion of early deaths in the present study is unlikely to have affected our results, given that landmark analyses at 5 and 7 months supported the OS and PFS benefit of rCR observed in survival analyses of the overall population.

Despite being assigned BCLC stage A at baseline, 228 patients in our study were deemed initially unresectable because their tumors were large; these tumors lay adjacent to important blood vessels, requiring the removal of multiple liver lobes; or residual liver volume after hepatectomy was expected to be insufficient. The importance of prothrombin time reflects that hepatic functional reserve is a critical determinant of survival, and the present study showed that a prothrombin time of ≤13 s was an independent predictor of better OS among patients who underwent hepatectomy.

Our identification of Child–Pugh B liver function, elevated α-fetoprotein protein ≥400 ng/ml, multinodular tumors, macrovascular invasion, and extrahepatic metastasis as independent negative predictors of rCR aligns with established HCC prognostic models.[34], [35], [36] These factors collectively indicate enhanced tumor aggressiveness and diminished hepatic reserve, potentially impeding immune effector cell infiltration or persistence.35 The inverse correlation between tumor burden and the likelihood of rCR aligns with preclinical evidence suggesting that a high antigenic load overwhelms T cell exhaustion thresholds, whereas macrovascular invasion and metastases imply systemic immune evasion.37 Transarterial chemoembolization induces the release of tumor antigens, increases the abundance of CD8+ T cells, and upregulates PD-L1, all of which are associated with greater ICI efficacy.38 Our findings provide a framework for personalizing the management of initially unresectable HCC, including the intensification of therapies when appropriate. Nevertheless, the generalizability of our findings remains to be established because although all the ICIs in our study have proven effective in Chinese populations,[39], [40], [41], [42] most are not currently recommended by the EASL or by the European Society for Medical Oncology owing to the lack of global multicenter randomized controlled trials confirming their efficacy.

This study has several limitations that are intrinsic to its retrospective, multicenter design. Radiological response was not assessed through entirely centralized review, which may introduce inter-observer bias, although all images from study sites were independently analyzed centrally and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the sites. Although we attempted to minimize confounding resulting from baseline variations using propensity score matching, we cannot exclude confounding arising from baseline heterogeneity for which we lacked data or from heterogeneity across centers in the ICIs and other components of conversion therapy, in the selection of patients for subsequent hepatectomy, and in non-surgical management after achievement of rCR. Nevertheless, our large sample allowed extensive subgroup analyses to explore the survival benefits of rCR in different types of patients. At the same time, the generalizability of our findings to patients outside Asia remains to be established, given that just over 80% of our cohort had HCC related to chronic infection with HBV, whereas patient populations with initially unresectable HCC in the West tend to have other etiological types of the cancer. Even so, neither the frequency nor survival benefit of rCR in our cohort depended on etiological type.

Despite these limitations, our large multicenter study suggests that although assessment of pathological tumor response through surgical biopsies remains a more accurate predictor of prognosis, rCR can be a reliable, pragmatic endpoint for predicting the prognosis of patients with initially unresectable HCC who have undergone immune-based conversion therapy. In addition, our work strongly suggests that achievement of rCR, together with other patient characteristics, can guide the selection of patients for subsequent hepatectomy. Independent predictors of rCR in our cohort included Child–Pugh status, tumor burden, and the extent of metastatic spread, which may be useful for personalizing treatment and management. Large, prospective studies in the future should validate and extend our findings in patient populations outside Asia while exploring novel biomarkers that can reliably predict rCR and pCR non-invasively.
