Primary Treatment of Pelvic Organ Prolapse in Women Aged Over 80 Years: Pessary Versus Surgery
Authors: Qianlan Yu, Ming’e Li, Ying Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and HypothesisThis study compared functional outcomes of pessary and surgery as the primary treatment for symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse (POP) in elderly women.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, 148 women aged 80 and above with symptomatic POP were treated at our institute between November 2013 and May 2023 with either surgery (n = 23) or pessary (n = 125). Treatment was based on patient preference. A long-term follow-up of over 1 year was conducted. The primary outcome was subjective patient-reported improvement measured with the Patient Global Impression of Improvement scale. Secondary outcomes included symptom remission and adverse events. The ability of self-management of pessary and the discontinuation of pessary use were also analyzed.ResultsOne hundred thirty-two patients completed at least 1 year of follow-up (median 696 days); 94.7% women (18/19) in the surgery group and 87.6% women (99/113) in the pessary group reported subjective improvement (risk difference −7.1% [2-sided 95% CI, −16.3 to 12.9]; p value, .70), showing no statistical difference. Among pessary users, 68.3% of ring pessary users and 50.0% of Gellhorn pessary users were able to independently manage the pessary (p = .21). Reasons for discontinued pessary use were mainly perceived inconvenience, pain or discomfort, and decreased daily activity or negligible POP symptoms. Four patients switched from pessary to surgery.ConclusionsPessary is an effective and safe primary treatment for symptomatic POP in elderly women and can be managed independently in the majority of elderly users.Keywords: Colpocleisis, Pelvic organ prolapse, Pessary

Background

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP), defined as the downward displacement of pelvic organs, is a common condition in postmenopausal women and often leads to bothersome urinary, bowel, sexual, and pelvic symptoms [1–3]. Its prevalence increases with age [2, 3], and as life expectancy continues to rise, the number of very elderly women living with POP is expected to grow. Determining the optimal treatment for this population has therefore become increasingly important.

Both surgical and conservative approaches are effective treatment options for POP [4–6]. In elderly and frail patients, obliterative surgeries such as Lefort colpocleisis are often favored because they are generally safe and provide reliable anatomical and symptomatic relief [7–9]. However, many women in this age group have cardiovascular or other chronic comorbidities that increase the risks of general anesthesia and surgical intervention. Consequently, not all patients are suitable surgical candidates or willing to undergo an operation.

Pessary use offers a noninvasive alternative and is widely recommended as first-line conservative therapy [4, 11–13]. Pessaries can effectively support the prolapsed organs, relieve symptoms, and have been reported to avoid surgery in more than 70% of women [13]. Despite these benefits, pessary use requires ongoing care, such as regular removal, cleaning, and reinsertion, which must be performed by the patient or a caregiver. These practical demands raise concerns of clinicians and patients about the feasibility of long-term pessary management in advanced age. Younger pessary users also worry about whether they can continue using pessary for POP treatment as they age.

To our knowledge, no studies have specifically evaluated treatment outcomes in women over 80 with POP. This prospective cohort study was therefore designed to compare the long-term effectiveness of surgery and pessary in women aged 80 and above with symptomatic POP, aiming to generate evidence that can support individualized counseling and help guide treatment decisions for this rapidly growing patient population.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a prospective cohort study of women aged 80 years and older who received treatment for symptomatic POP at our institute between November 2013 and May 2023. All participants provided written informed consent.

Eligible patients included women who (1) presented with stage II, III, or IV symptomatic POP, (2) were over 80 years of age at the start of treatment, and (3) either received pessary management following a successful pessary fitting trial, or underwent surgical treatment. Clinical staging was based on the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) system. Patients with a positive response other than “none” and “not at all” to question 2 (“Do you usually experience heaviness or dullness in the pelvic area?”) or question 3 (“Do you usually have a bulge or something falling out that you can see or feel in your vaginal area?”) of the 20 item Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory (PFDI-20), were considered symptomatic.

All patients were evaluated by experienced urogynecologists. Demographics and medical history, including age, parity, smoking status, comorbidities, history of gynecologic surgery, body mass index, and presenting symptoms, were collected. Physical examination included POP-Q staging and measurement of vaginal introitus width horizontally across the posterior fourchette.

Patients diagnosed with symptomatic ≥ stage II POP were offered surgical and pessary treatment options, and were treated according to their preference. For patients choosing pessary treatment, pessary fitting trials were conducted with a ring with a support pessary fitted initially. If the ring with the support pessary failed to stay in place or caused persistent discomfort despite size adjustment, a Gellhorn pessary was tried. Once a comfortable fit was achieved, patients were educated on how to manage the pessary. They were advised to remove and clean their pessary at bedtime and reinsert it the next morning at least once a week. A follow-up visit was scheduled 2 weeks after pessary fitting. If discomfort or pessary expulsion was reported, an alternative pessary size or type was offered, followed by another 2-week follow-up visit. This process was repeated up to three times before pessary fitting was deemed unsuccessful. Patients who chose surgical treatment were offered a range of surgical options, including both reconstructive and obliterative procedures, with or without transvaginal or laparoscopic hysterectomy, depending on clinical requirements.

After the treatment, all patients received a short-term follow-up of 3 months and a long-term follow-up of over 1 year. The subjective improvement of patients, symptoms after treatment, and adverse events were asked, and vaginal inspection was performed. For pessary users, the ability to self-manage the devices was recorded. Patients who did not return in person were contacted via telephone to collect follow-up information.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the subjective patient-reported improvement measured with the Patient Global Impression of Improvement scale (PGI-I), a 7-point questionnaire ranging from “very much worse” (−3 points) to “very much better” (3 points). A score of 2 or 3 (“very much better” and “much better”) was defined as subjective improvement. Secondary outcomes included remission of prolapse-related symptoms and adverse events. Symptoms before and after the treatment of each patient were compared, where symptoms present before but absent after treatment were defined as remission, while symptoms absent before but present after treatment were defined as de novo (adverse events). We also analyzed the ability of self-management of pessary and the discontinuation of pessary use.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics and outcomes were compared between the pessary and surgery groups. Quantitative variables were described using mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, and qualitative variables were described using counts and percentages. Students’ t test and Mann–Whitney test were used in regard to the normally and non-normally distributed continuous data, respectively. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test were used for categorical variables. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p values < 0.05 considered as statistically significant. No equivalence testing was designed.

Results

A total of 165 patients aged over 80 years with stage II–IV symptomatic POP sought treatment at our institute during the study period. Among patients who initially attempted pessary fitting, 17 of 142 (<12.0%) were unable to be successfully fitted with a pessary and did not receive further treatment at our institute, and were therefore not included in the study. Ultimately, 148 patients received pessary (n = 125) or surgical (n = 23) treatment were enrolled (Fig. 1). Among those treated surgically, 22 underwent Lefort colpocleisis, and one underwent total colpocleisis. In the pessary group, 88.0% patients (110/125) were treated with ring pessaries and 12.0% (15/125) with Gellhorn pessaries.

Adverse events and treatment alteration

No recurrence was observed in the surgery group. Twenty-three patients (20.4%) discontinued pessary use, including 28.6% (4/14) of patients using Gellhorn pessaries and 19.2% (19/99) of ring pessary users (p = 0.48). The main reasons for discontinuation were perceived inconvenience (34.5%, 8/23), pain or discomfort (34.5%, 8/23), and decreased daily activity or negligible POP symptoms (26.1%, 6/23). Four patients switched to surgery, and the remaining 18 did not received further treatment.

In the pessary group, 68.3% of patients (69/101) using ring pessaries and 50.0% of patients (6/12) using Gellhorn pessaries were able to independently manage the pessary, showing no significant difference (p = 0.21). For patients unable to self-manage their pessaries (31.7% of ring pessary users and 50% of Gellhorn pessary users), continuation of pessary use was facilitated by caregiver assistance, most commonly provided by spouses or adult children.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that pessary treatment is an effective and feasible option for managing symptomatic POP in women over 80, offering long-term subjective improvement and symptom relief comparable to surgery, with the majority of patients able to adhere to and self-manage the pessary.

Long-term continuation is central to pessary effectiveness. In our cohort, 79.6% (90/113) of women continued pessary use for at least 1 year, including 80.8% (80/99) of ring pessary users and 71.4% (10/14) of Gellhorn pessary users. These rates are relatively high compared with the reported range of 46.6% to 86.6% in previous studies [10, 14–23], supporting the recommendation of pessary treatment for elderly POP patients. Although age has been discussed as a factor influencing pessary adherence, existing evidence remains conflicting and inconclusive [14, 18, 19, 24, 25]. Patients of older age may be more hesitant to undergo surgery and thus more motivated to try and maintain pessary use.

Nonetheless, in elderly patients, concerns about their ability to self-manage pessaries often influence treatment decisions. Self-management is known to reduce adverse events and is strongly associated with pessary continuation [25, 26]. Several factors contribute to successful self-management. First, the type of pessary is one important factor. Ring pessaries and Gellhorn pessaries are the most commonly used types [27, 28]. Our previous research showed that the Gellhorn pessary is more difficult to manage, leading to lower self-management and continuation rates than the ring pessary [20, 21, 27]. The present findings align with this pattern, although the difference was not statistically significant, possibly due to the relatively small number of Gellhorn pessary users. Second, patient education also plays a crucial role. At our institute, a team of doctors and nurses specialized in urogynecology provides outpatient education for patients and their caregivers, ensuring they understand the pessary treatment and master the essential management techniques. The education includes face-to-face instruction on pessary insertion, removal, cleaning, and monitoring for complications, with educational leaflets and instructional videos provided for reference. Most pessary users or their caregivers were able to master the pessary management in a single outpatient instruction session lasting less than 30 min. In addition, individualized guidance is available via a hotline and interim visits to address problems arising during pessary use, and is reinforced at each follow-up visit based on patient-specific questions or difficulties. Such education appears to support adherence and helps mitigate the impact of advanced age. In this study, only two patients discontinued treatment due to inability to self-manage, and the self-management rate was only slightly lower than that of our earlier cohort with a mean age of 70 years, in which 84.3% of ring pessary users and 58.3% of Gellhorn pessary users managed their pessaries independently [27]. These results underscore that pessary treatment remains a practical option even for patients over 80, bolstering confidence among clinicians and patients in choosing pessaries for POP treatment, and helping to alleviate concerns among younger patients that advancing age alone may limit the feasibility of continued pessary use. These findings also support a stepwise treatment strategy: using ring pessaries as the first-line option and reserving Gellhorn pessary as an alternative for cases in which ring pessaries are unsuitable or ineffective, which is more common in severe and complicated POP [15, 20, 29, 30].

Our results may also help explain discrepancies in continuation rates observed in prior studies. In a study by Dykes et al. focusing on elderly patients over 75 years old [24], the continuation rate was only 21% at a mean follow-up of 3.2 years, substantially lower than that in this and other previous studies. Besides the long follow-up duration, limited patient education likely contributed to the difference. Dykes’ study included many patients from non-English speaking countries and 38.4% of patients required translation services [24], suggesting that language barriers may have impeded the adequate communication and education, and hindered patients’ understanding of pessary function and management.

The reasons for discontinuation in our study were mainly perceived inconvenience and pain or discomfort, which was consistent with prior reports [17]. However, we had an interesting finding that 26.1% of patients stopped using the pessary because their symptoms no longer affected their daily lives. On the one hand, with the aging of the elderly, their daily activity intensity decreased; on the other hand, some patients reported that the prolapse no longer protruded beyond the vaginal opening even after stopping pessary use. This raises the possibility that long-term pessary support may contribute to partial prolapse regression, consistent with several previous reports of improved anatomical structure after pessary use [31–33]. This intriguing observation merits further investigation.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to focus specifically on treatment outcomes in POP patients aged 80 years and older, and included a relatively large sample size of this age group. All patients were evaluated, treated, and followed-up in the same urogynecology center using standardized clinical management and follow-up procedures, enhancing consistency and comparability of the data.

This study has several limitations. Treatment allocation based on patient preference may have introduced selection bias, as patients choosing pessary or surgery may differ in self-care ability and treatment expectations. Surgical management was limited to colpocleisis, which may limit the generalizability of the surgical outcomes. The relatively small surgical sample size may reduce statistical power. Furthermore, only two types of pessaries were available, and women with unsuccessful fittings who did not proceed to surgery were not included, which limits comparisons across clinical settings. Future studies with longer-term follow-up are warranted to further evaluate the durability of treatment efficacy, as well as adherence to pessary use.

Conclusion

Pessary is an effective and safe treatment for symptomatic POP in elderly women with no significant difference in subjective improvement and symptom remission compared with surgery. More than half of elderly POP patients were able to manage the pessary independently.
