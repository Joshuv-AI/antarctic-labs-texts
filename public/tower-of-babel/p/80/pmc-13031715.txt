Association between opium consumption and sarcopenia in middle-aged and older adults: findings from the Fasa Adults Cohort Study (FACS)
Authors: Sina Bazmi, Fatemeh Masrour, Mojtaba Farjam, Reza Homayounfar, Mohammad Mehdi Naghizadeh, Nematollah Jaafari, Bahareh Fakhraei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia, a condition linked to disability, frailty, and mortality, is influenced by multiple lifestyle and metabolic factors, but its association with opium consumption has not been evaluated. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of adults aged 35–70 years in the Fasa Adult Cohort Study (FACS). Sarcopenia was defined as skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) in the lowest two quantiles (lowest 40%), and opium use, including dose, was measured using standardized questionnaires. Logistic regression models estimated adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with subgroup analyses performed across demographic, dietary, and clinical factors. Opium use was associated with 76% higher odds of sarcopenia (OR = 1.76, 95% CI 1.31–2.36, p < 0.001). The association was consistent across physical activity, socioeconomic status, and protein intake strata, but significant only in males (OR = 1.69, 95% CI 1.22–2.34, p = 0.002). It was also evident among participants with low dietary inflammatory index, low alcohol intake, and without major chronic conditions. Within users, higher opium dose showed a modest dose-related relationship with sarcopenia risk (OR = 1.01 per mesghal/year, 95% CI 1.00–1.01, p = 0.040). These findings demonstrate a strong and independent association between opium use and sarcopenia, especially in men and otherwise healthy individuals, underscoring the need for routine muscle assessment in opium users and integration of sarcopenia prevention into cessation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40992-8.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is now recognized as a muscle-strength–led disease with confirmatory low muscle quantity/quality (European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People; EWGSOP2), a framework that standardizes diagnosis and underpins epidemiologic estimates and risk stratification1. Globally, pooled prevalence varies widely by criteria and region, roughly 10–27% in adults ≥ 60 years, with higher estimates in Oceania and lower in Europe when EWGSOP2 is applied, highlighting substantial population burden2. Clinically, sarcopenia is consistently associated with greater risks of falls and fractures in older adults, underscoring downstream morbidity and healthcare utilization3. Across diverse clinical settings, sarcopenia and low lean mass nearly double all-cause mortality risk, cementing its prognostic significance beyond musculoskeletal outcomes4. Health-economic analyses show sarcopenia and frailty jointly drive higher hospitalization and healthcare expenditures, quantifying the system-level burden5. Cost-effectiveness modeling further suggests that proactive sarcopenia management can be economically favorable for health systems6. In Iran, early work (SARIR) and subsequent reviews report notable prevalence among community-dwelling elders in Tehran, emphasizing local public-health relevance and the need for region-specific determinants7. Socioeconomic gradients in Tehran cohorts further correlate with sarcopenia prevalence, indicating modifiable social determinants in Middle Eastern settings8. Iranian adults also exhibit measurable sarcopenic-obesity prevalence with adverse metabolic profiles, reinforcing cardiometabolic implications of low muscle mass in this region9. Against this background of high prevalence, adverse outcomes, and economic impact, there is a compelling need to examine regionally prevalent, potentially modifiable exposures that may accelerate muscle loss.

Opium, the plant-derived prototype of the opioid class, has been used for centuries10, and modern medical and non-medical opioid exposure carries well-recognized endocrine and musculoskeletal consequences via central suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. In Iran, where opium remains culturally and epidemiologically prominent, contemporary cohort data highlight substantial substance use burdens in urban populations, with opium commonly reported among the primary drugs used11; provincial surveys similarly document notable current and lifetime opium use, underscoring its public-health relevance12. Globally, the rising burden of opioid use, across both pharmaceutical opioids and traditionally consumed opiates, has heightened awareness of the broad spectrum of opioid-related morbidity and mortality13. Mechanistically, chronic opioid exposure induces opioid-induced androgen deficiency (OPIAD) by inhibiting GnRH/LH secretion, leading to lower testosterone, reduced protein synthesis, and unfavorable body-composition shifts14,15; randomized trials in opioid-treated hypogonadal men consistently show increased lean mass and reduced fat mass with testosterone therapy, supporting a causal link between androgen suppression and muscle loss16,17; these body-composition effects occur without adverse metabolic derangements over short durations18. Preclinical work with fentanyl analogs demonstrates µ-opioid receptor–mediated decrements in grip strength and hypotonicity reversible with naloxone, suggesting direct myoneural and neuromotor impacts that could exacerbate sarcopenic pathways19. Collectively, these mechanistic data and epidemiologic evidence on related musculoskeletal and metabolic outcomes make opium exposure a biologically and clinically plausible correlate of sarcopenia through endocrine hypogonadism, potential direct skeletal-muscle effects, and downstream reductions in activity, aligning with and helping to contextualize the associations we observed.

Existing literature on sarcopenia has largely focused on aging, chronic diseases, malnutrition, and physical inactivity, with limited exploration of substance use as a risk factor. While opioids are known to affect endocrine, inflammatory, and metabolic pathways that influence muscle mass, no prior study has directly examined the relationship between opium use and sarcopenia. This gap is particularly notable in regions with high opium prevalence, where such an association may have substantial public health implications, an evidence void our study aims to address. Given the high prevalence of opium use in Iran and the absence of prior studies on its relationship with sarcopenia, we aimed to examine the association between opium consumption and sarcopenia in middle-aged and older adults participating in the Fasa Adults Cohort Study. We hypothesized that the strength of the association may differ across population subgroups such as sex and comorbidity status.

Methods

Study settings and population

This cross-sectional study, designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines (Supplementary Table 1). was performed within the framework of the PERSIAN cohort and included 10,138 adults aged 35–70 years from the Fasa region in southern Iran20. Eligible participants were permanent residents of Sheshdeh and its surrounding rural villages, recruited through the local primary health care network. Standardized protocols were applied to obtain demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, dietary, and medical history data, along with anthropometric and physical activity assessments. The baseline phase of FACS was carried out between March 2015 and October 2016, during which all demographic, lifestyle, clinical, and body composition data were collected. All participants provided informed written consent, and data were recorded in a secure online system following standardized cohort protocols. For the current analysis, individuals were excluded if they had missing data on sarcopenia status (n = 5910) or opium use (n = 142), as these were the main outcome and exposure variables. Additionally, participants with conditions that could independently and strongly affect muscle mass or confound the association were excluded, including multiple sclerosis (n = 1), cancer (n = 24), pregnancy (n = 38), chronic systemic corticosteroid use (n = 58), and antipsychotic medication use (n = 33). Subjects with a history of stroke (n = 0), myocardial infarction (n = 0), hepatitis B (n = 2), or hepatitis C (n = 1) were then excluded, to minimize bias from chronic conditions known to cause muscle wasting or alter drug metabolism. These exclusions ensured a more homogenous study population for investigating the association between opium use and sarcopenia. The summarized study flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Outcome variable assessment

Body composition assessment

Bioelectric impedance analysis (BIA) was employed to assess body composition utilizing the Tanita BC-418 MA Segmental Body Composition Analyzer (Tanita, Japan). This is a single-frequency BIA device featuring eight polar electrodes and a single-point load cell weighing system on the scale platform, capable of delivering distinct body mass measurements for different segments, including the right arm, left arm, trunk, right leg, and left leg. The impedance of the subject’s tissues is measured using receiver electrodes following the application of a predetermined signal through injector electrodes. All measurements are conducted at 50 kHz with a constant current of 0.8 mA sine wave. The FM percentage is computed using an algorithm that considers impedance, age, and height. The quantities of fat mass, fat-free mass, and fat percentage in the hands, trunk, legs, and overall body were documented individually21. Among all participants in the Fasa PERSIAN Cohort Study, 4289 individuals underwent BIA assessment, and all of them were eligible for sarcopenia evaluation in the present study. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) was not used for body composition assessment in this cohort.

Appendicular lean mass (ALM)

ALM was calculated as the sum of fat-free mass (FFM) from the four limbs (arms and legs) measured by BIA. This parameter reflects the lean soft tissue of the extremities, effectively capturing skeletal muscle mass excluding central trunk components22.

Skeletal muscle index (SMI)

We normalized ALM for body size by dividing by the square of standing height (in meters), yielding SMI (kg/m2), analogous to body mass index (BMI). This index, also referred to as the appendicular lean mass index (ALMI) or skeletal muscle mass index, is a widely used measure to account for physiological anthropometric differences when assessing muscle mass23.

Definition of sarcopenia

In line with our population-based methodology, sarcopenia was operationally defined as having an SMI that falls within the lowest two quintiles (i.e., the bottom 40%) of the study population’s SMI distribution9. This approach, using relative cut-offs based on quintiles, is epidemiologically valid in cross-sectional analyses where standardized external thresholds may not apply or may lack relevance in diverse ethnic or demographic groups. This definition reflects a muscle mass—based sarcopenia phenotype, commonly applied in large-scale epidemiologic studies when direct measures of muscle strength or physical performance are unavailable.

Opium consumption (main independent variable)

Opium use was assessed through a structured face-to-face questionnaire adapted from validated instruments used in prior epidemiologic studies within Iranian populations. Participants were classified as opium users if they reported consuming opium at least once per week for a minimum of 6 months24. The questionnaire captured multiple dimensions of opium exposure, including the total years of opium consumption over the life course, and the cumulative exposure quantified in dose-years, calculated as the number of years of opium use multiplied by mesghal consumed per day24. This approach allows for both dichotomous classification (user vs. non-user) and quantitative analysis of exposure intensity and cumulative dose. As with other long-term behavioral exposures, this measurement may be subject to recall error; however, the questionnaire used has been applied consistently across the PERSIAN Cohort. Opium consumption was analyzed in separate regression models as (1) a categorical variable (ever vs. never use) to assess the primary association with sarcopenia, and (2) a continuous variable (cumulative dose in mesghal/year) to explore dose–response trends. These exposure definitions were not included simultaneously in the same multivariable model, thereby avoiding multicollinearity.

Covariates

A comprehensive set of demographics, lifestyle, clinical, and nutritional factors was considered as covariates. Covariate selection for multivariable models was guided by a priori clinical and biological knowledge of sarcopenia and substance use, supplemented by epidemiologic evidence. Demographic variables included age (years), gender, marital status (single, married, widowed, divorced), and socioeconomic status (asset index). Lifestyle variables included smoking status (current smoker vs. non-smoker), non-cigarette tobacco use Tobacco use defined as using Naas, Hookah, Pipe, or Chopogh once per week for at least 6 months, alcohol intake (milligrams/day), physical activity level expressed as metabolic equivalents (METs), and total daily calorie intake (kcal). Clinical covariates included the presence of hypertension, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD; defined as estimated GFR by CKD-EPI 2021 < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2), end-stage renal disease (ESRD; GFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), rheumatic diseases, thyroid disease, depression, other psychiatric disorders, osteoporosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD; defined using the Fatty Liver Index with validated sex-specific cut-offs for the Iranian population), and movement disability, defined as any history of limb movement impairments (stiffness, slowness) or tremors lasting more than 1 week. History of hip or femur fracture, and traumatic head injury were also recorded.

Medication-related covariates included statin use (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, or simvastatin), proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use (omeprazole, esomeprazole, or pantoprazole), anticonvulsant use, antidepressant use, diuretic use, and polypharmacy, defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications. Nutritional factors included daily intake of protein, lipids, carbohydrates, calcium, and the energy-adjusted Dietary Inflammatory Index (E-DII). E-DII was calculated from food frequency questionnaire data using 34 dietary parameters encompassing macro- and micronutrients, bioactive compounds, and food items. For each parameter, intake values were standardized using global reference means and standard deviations, multiplied by their respective inflammatory effect scores, and summed to obtain the overall E-DII score, with higher values indicating a more pro-inflammatory diet25. E-DII reflects the inflammatory potential of an individual’s diet, which can influence muscle mass and function26. Adjusting for energy intake ensures that the inflammatory potential is assessed relative to caloric consumption, reducing bias from variations in overall energy intake.

Anthropometric measures included height (m) and fat mass index (FMI), with obesity defined as FMI > 9 kg/m2 for men and > 13 kg/m2 for women. Laboratory-derived markers included the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were described as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. Given the large sample size, normality of continuous variables was assumed based on the Central Limit Theorem, and parametric descriptive statistics were considered appropriate. Group comparisons were assessed using independent t-tests for continuous data and chi-square tests for categorical data. Logistic regression models were applied to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between opium use and sarcopenia, with adjustments made for relevant demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, dietary, and medical covariates. Subgroup analyses were conducted by sex, lifestyle factors (physical activity, socioeconomic status, dietary protein intake, alcohol intake, dietary inflammatory index), and comorbid conditions (e.g., thyroid disorders, COPD, CKD, rheumatologic diseases, depression, NAFLD, diabetes, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, obesity), as these factors are biologically plausible effect modifiers of the relationship between opium use and sarcopenia. To assess the robustness of findings to different modeling strategies, additional models were constructed using alternative covariate selection approaches, including Wald-based variable selection and exclusion of selected covariates (e.g., NAFLD). Additionally, dose–response analyses were performed among opium users to assess the impact of opium dose on sarcopenia risk. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Of the 3990 participants included in the study, 1,602 (40.2%) had sarcopenia. Among participants with sarcopenia, 1,234 (77.0%) were women and 368 (23.0%) were men (p < 0.001). This reflects the sex distribution within the sarcopenic group and does not represent sex-specific prevalence rates. Participants with sarcopenia were older (49.79 ± 9.59 vs. 46.92 ± 8.79 years, p < 0.001) and more frequently single (p < 0.001), unemployed (p < 0.001), and from lower socioeconomic backgrounds (p < 0.001) compared to those without sarcopenia. Lower physical activity levels (p = 0.005) and multiple comorbidities, including thyroid disease (p < 0.001), ischemic heart disease (p = 0.005), other psychiatric disorders (p = 0.003), NAFLD (p < 0.001), and osteoporosis (p < 0.001), were associated with sarcopenia prevalence.

Nutritionally, sarcopenia was associated with lower intake of protein, lipid, carbohydrate, and calcium (all p < 0.001) and a lower energy-adjusted dietary inflammatory index (E-DII) score (p < 0.001). Sarcopenic individuals also had lower fat-free mass in all limb segments and lower skeletal muscle index (SMI) (p < 0.001). Interestingly, opium use was less prevalent in the sarcopenia group (16.5% vs. 27.5%, p < 0.001), but among users, the mean annual opium dose was higher in those with sarcopenia (32.01 ± 25.87 vs. 24.64 ± 27.58 mesghal/year, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

In multivariable logistic regression, opium use was independently associated with higher odds of sarcopenia in the total population (OR = 1.76, 95% CI 1.31–2.36, p < 0.001) after adjusting for age, gender, marital status, smoking, hypertension, NAFLD, thyroid disease, E-DII score, calcium intake, and SIRI. Stratified analyses revealed that this association was significant in males (OR = 1.69, 95% CI 1.22–2.34, p = 0.002) but not in females (OR = 2.11, 95% CI 0.54–8.30, p = 0.283) (Table 2).

Subgroup analyses showed that the positive association between opium use and sarcopenia persisted across most strata, including those with low and high physical activity, low and high socioeconomic status, and low protein intake (all p < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the association between opium use and sarcopenia was robust across multiple analytical strategies (Supplementary Table 1). When sarcopenia was defined without gender-specific cutoffs, opium use was consistently associated with higher odds of sarcopenia in fully adjusted models (OR range: 1.79–1.83; all p < 0.001). Similar effect estimates were observed when models were adjusted using Wald-selected covariates, including analyses excluding NAFLD from the adjustment set.

When gender-specific sarcopenia cutoffs were applied, the association between opium use and sarcopenia remained statistically significant in the overall population across all models (OR range: 1.73–1.86; all p < 0.001), indicating that the findings were not driven by sex-related differences in body composition distribution.

In sex-stratified analyses, opium use was consistently associated with higher odds of sarcopenia among men across all models and outcome definitions. In contrast, although point estimates were generally elevated among women, associations did not reach statistical significance, and confidence intervals were wide, suggesting limited statistical power in female subgroups. Overall, these sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the observed association between opium use and sarcopenia under varying model specifications and outcome definitions. Effect estimates also remained stable across models adjusted using different covariate selection strategies, including literature-based full models and reduced Wald-selected models.

Discussion

In this large population-based study, opium use was found to be independently associated with a higher likelihood of sarcopenia, even after accounting for a wide range of demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. This association was evident in men but not in women and remained consistent across most subgroups, regardless of physical activity level, socioeconomic status, or protein intake. The relationship was particularly pronounced in individuals without major chronic diseases and in those with lower alcohol consumption or lower dietary inflammatory scores. Furthermore, among opium users, a higher cumulative dose was linked to a greater probability of having sarcopenia, suggesting a dose–response relationship.

Opioids potently suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, producing opioid-induced androgen deficiency (OPIAD), a highly prevalent and often under-recognized consequence of chronic opioid use that manifests with loss of muscle mass/strength, adverse body composition, and skeletal fragility16. Long-term opioid therapy commonly induces hypogonadism in both sexes with clinical sequelae that include reduced muscle strength and mass; routine endocrine monitoring has been urged15. Across observational and interventional data, OPIAD is frequent (20–80%) and negatively impacts bone density and body composition14. Contemporary focused reviews reaffirm GnRH/LH suppression as the central mechanism and note that testosterone therapy in unequivocally hypogonadal men improves body composition, with emerging anti-nociceptive signals27. Among methadone-treated patients, low bone mineral density is common, underscoring a musculoskeletal vulnerability milieu28. Young opioid-dependent women show inferior bone quality versus controls, highlighting that opioid-related musculoskeletal harms extend to females even when muscle has been less studied29. Body-composition disturbances (lower fat, altered water/lean compartments) are documented in opiate-dependent cohorts, consistent with catabolic or malnourished states30.

In randomized trials among opioid-treated hypogonadal men, transdermal testosterone over 14 weeks reduced fat mass and increased lean mass versus placebo, directly linking opioid-related androgen depletion to unfavorable body composition that is reversible with replacement16. Similar trials show improved body composition without deterioration in metabolic or inflammatory markers, suggesting potential cardiometabolic benefit18. Over 24 weeks, intramuscular testosterone increased lean body mass and reduced fat mass in this population17. Notably, increased lean mass did not translate into measurable gains in muscle function or gait performance over 24 weeks, cautioning that mass and function can be dissociated in chronic pain/OPIAD31. Preclinical work with fentanyl analogs demonstrates µ-opioid receptor–mediated, naloxone-reversible reductions in grip strength and hypotonia, offering a mechanistic bridge from opioid exposure to impaired skeletal muscle performance beyond endocrine pathways19. In older men with persistent musculoskeletal pain, opioid users had worse baseline physical performance, though longitudinal decline did not differ after adjustment, suggesting selection and confounding are substantial in human cohorts32. Cross-sectional data in advanced knee OA link opioid use (independent of dose) to slower gait speed, consistent with functional compromise33. During methadone treatment, percent muscle mass falls while fat mass and BMI rise, echoing an adiposity-shift phenotype rather than true myoanabolism34. Additionally, the ergonomics of opium smoking correlate with reduced cervical muscle strength and range of motion, indicating behavioral/positional contributors to musculoskeletal impairment12. The relationship may also be bidirectional: clinically coded sarcopenia predicts higher long-term postoperative analgesic, including opioid, use35.

The male-restricted significance we observed may be partly explained by sex-specific endocrine effects of chronic opioid use15. Opioids suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis via inhibition of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), leading to reduced luteinizing hormone (LH) and downstream sex steroid production. In men, this manifests as lower testosterone levels, a well-recognized consequence of long-term opioid exposure15,36. Lower testosterone may contribute directly to muscle mass loss and sarcopenia, given androgen’s anabolic effects on skeletal muscle. Several clinical and experimental studies have documented opioid-induced hypogonadism, muscle weakness, and reduced bone and muscle mass in men15. In contrast, data on sex-hormone suppression in women are less consistent: some studies report minimal or no significant reductions in circulating sex hormones in female opioid users37. Moreover, hormonal milieu in women is influenced by menstrual status, menopause, and cyclical fluctuations, potentially buffering or masking opioid-associated muscle-mass loss in cross-sectional measurements. Additionally, behavioral or cultural differences in patterns of opium use (dose, duration, route) between sexes may contribute: men may consume higher cumulative doses or longer duration compared with women, augmenting endocrine and musculoskeletal effects. Finally, given that sarcopenia was defined using SMI without muscle strength parameters, sex-specific differences in fat–lean mass distribution and body composition might differentially influence the detectability of sarcopenia by BIA in men versus women. Taken together, these hormonal, biological, and sociocultural factors may underlie the sex-specific association observed in our cohort. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional design and lack of direct hormone measurements limit our ability to draw definitive conclusions; future studies should include endocrine profiling (e.g., testosterone, estrogen, gonadotropins) and stratified dose–response analyses by sex to clarify mechanisms.

The persistence of association across strata of physical activity, SES, protein intake, and alcohol suggests the effect is not fully explained by lifestyle or nutritional confounding, aligning with endocrine and direct µ-receptor effects16. The finding that association emerged only with low dietary inflammatory index (E-DII) may indicate that when systemic inflammatory load is lower, opioid-endocrine mechanisms are more detectable, whereas higher inflammatory burden could obscure or dominate sarcopenia risk19. Conversely, null associations within participants bearing major chronic conditions (e.g., COPD, CKD, diabetes, NAFLD, obesity, IHD) likely reflect competing sarcopenic pathways, treatment effects, and index-event bias that attenuate incremental opium effects34. Although, the wide confidence intervals observed in several comorbidity subgroups suggest substantial statistical imprecision, raising the possibility of Type II error; therefore, these null findings should not be interpreted as evidence of no association. Collectively, these data situate opium use as a plausible, novel, and modifiable risk factor for sarcopenia through converging endocrine (OPIAD), neuromotor, behavioral, and nutritional mechanisms, filling a critical gap where no prior study directly assessed opium–sarcopenia links.

Opioids may be linked to muscle loss through endocrine suppression and downstream myocellular pathways14,15. Chronic opioid exposure inhibits hypothalamic GnRH with secondary reductions in LH/FSH and testosterone, opioid-induced androgen deficiency (OPIAD), a well-described effect in both sexes but most consequential for male skeletal muscle14,15,27. In men, androgens drive muscle protein synthesis via androgen-receptor–Akt/mTOR signaling and stimulate satellite-cell activation and myonuclear accretion; conversely, androgen deficiency shifts the balance toward proteolysis16,17,38. Mechanistically, testosterone administration increases mixed-muscle fractional synthesis and fiber hypertrophy and activates satellite cells39. Reviews detailing the opioid-HPG suppression pathway support a dose–response relation, consistent with the observed 1% higher odds per unit of opium dose and the male-only signal40. Androgens also counter-regulate inflammatory/NF-κB signaling implicated in muscle catabolism, so OPIAD may unmask cytokine-driven ubiquitin–proteasome and FoxO pathways even when background diet-derived inflammation is low, cohering with the stronger association we saw in participants with a low E-DII score41. Taken together, endocrine suppression provides a biologically specific explanation for the robust association across socioeconomic status, protein intake, alcohol, and physical-activity strata, its restriction to men, and the dose–response. The observed dose–response pattern reflects a statistically detectable but relatively small incremental effect per unit of annual exposure, consistent with the expectation that long-term cumulative use, rather than minor yearly variations, drives clinically relevant muscle mass loss.

Beyond hormones, opioids have direct neuromuscular and systemic effects that can accelerate muscle loss. µ-opioid agonists acutely reduce skeletal muscle strength and induce hypotonia in animals, an effect reversed by naloxone, and interfere with cholinergic signaling19,42,43. These central/peripheral actions can diminish habitual loading and precipitate disuse atrophy, matching human data linking opioid use with slower gait and lower performance32,33. Opioids also promote mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress (e.g., altered respiratory chain activity, ROS generation), which bias muscle toward proteolysis and impaired bioenergetics44,45. Nutritionally, methadone cohorts show rising fat mass with relative declines in muscle percentage despite weight gain, suggesting an anabolic/catabolic repartitioning unfavorable to lean tissue34. Finally, your null findings among participants with chronic inflammatory or metabolic diseases likely reflect “ceiling” catabolic loads, pre-existing cytokine/NF-κB–FoxO activation from conditions like COPD, CKD, NAFLD, diabetes, and IHD may dominate muscle turnover, leaving less detectable incremental effect from opioids41. In contrast, among relatively healthier participants (no major comorbidity, low E-DII), opioid-specific endocrine and neuromuscular pathways stand out, aligning with our overall 70% higher odds of sarcopenia and the consistency across lifestyle subgroups14,16,17,27. Given the number of subgroup analyses performed, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. The subgroup results are therefore hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Our findings position opium consumption as a potentially modifiable, under-recognized risk factor for sarcopenia, with clinically actionable implications for screening, prevention, and patient counseling in both general and high-prevalence settings. Given the observed dose–response relationship and male predominance, clinicians should incorporate targeted muscle-mass assessment, such as bioimpedance analysis or DXA, into routine evaluations of male patients using opium, even in the absence of overt comorbidities, and consider early interventions including resistance training, adequate protein intake, and, where appropriate, endocrine evaluation for OPIAD. In settings with high opium use, integrating sarcopenia risk assessment into primary care and addiction treatment programs could enable earlier detection and mitigate downstream outcomes such as frailty, falls, and disability. Future studies should extend these findings by employing longitudinal designs to clarify causality and temporal relationships, using gold-standard muscle quality measures (e.g., MRI, CT muscle cross-sectional area), and exploring mechanistic biomarkers such as testosterone, LH/FSH, inflammatory cytokines, and muscle turnover markers to disentangle endocrine from direct opioid effects. Stratified analyses in women, diverse ethnic populations, and in patients with varying inflammatory burdens could refine understanding of subgroup-specific risks, while interventional studies, ranging from opioid cessation to androgen replacement and targeted exercise/nutrition programs, are needed to determine the reversibility of opioid-associated muscle loss and to inform evidence-based clinical guidelines.

This study offers several important strengths that set it apart from all prior literature. It is the first to evaluate the association between opium use and sarcopenia, addressing a critical gap in the evidence base. The large sample size, drawn from a geographic region with a high prevalence of opium use, provides both statistical power and real-world relevance. Rigorous exclusion criteria, eliminating participants with conditions or treatments that independently influence muscle mass (e.g., multiple sclerosis, cerebrovascular attack, cancer, chronic corticosteroid use), enhanced internal validity by reducing potential confounding. The study also benefited from comprehensive adjustment for an unusually broad range of demographic, lifestyle, dietary, medication, and comorbidity covariates, as well as the ability to quantify opium dose, enabling dose–response analyses not previously attempted. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference; however, the observed biologically plausible and dose-dependent association supports the likelihood of a true relationship. Another limitation is the reliance on observational data without biomarker or imaging confirmation of mechanistic pathways, which future prospective studies should incorporate through serial hormonal, inflammatory, and muscle quality assessments. A major limitation of this study is the substantial exclusion of participants due to the absence of BIA-based body composition measurements. Nearly 60% of the original cohort did not undergo BIA evaluation, which may have introduced selection bias, as individuals who agreed to body composition assessment could differ systematically from those who did not. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to the entire cohort population and should be considered when interpreting the results. Moreover, sarcopenia was initially defined using a population-specific relative SMI threshold (lowest two quintiles) rather than internationally recommended sex-specific absolute cutoffs. While this approach enables internal ranking within the cohort and is commonly used in large epidemiologic studies, it may limit comparability across populations. To address potential sex-related misclassification, we conducted sensitivity analyses using sex-specific SMI cutoffs, which yielded results highly consistent with the primary analyses. These findings suggest that the observed associations were not driven by the choice of cutoff; nevertheless, sex-specific results should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the cross-sectional design. Furthermore, the cumulative opium dose was based on participants’ long-term recall of daily use patterns, which introduces the possibility of recall bias. Such misclassification is likely non-differential with respect to sarcopenia status and would generally bias dose–response associations toward the null, suggesting that the true relationship may be stronger than observed. Moreover, because multiple subgroup analyses were conducted without formal correction for multiple testing, the risk of type I error inflation is acknowledged, and statistically significant subgroup findings should be viewed as exploratory. Another key limitation of this study is the absence of direct measures of muscle strength or physical performance, which are integral components of contemporary clinical definitions of sarcopenia. Consequently, sarcopenia in this study was defined solely based on low muscle mass. While this approach is widely used in population-based studies and allows for etiologic investigation, it may overestimate sarcopenia prevalence and limits comparability with clinical diagnostic criteria. Finally, while the study population’s high baseline opium use enhances detection power, it may limit generalizability to regions with lower prevalence, an issue that can be addressed in future multi-center or multinational cohorts.

Conclusion

In this large population-based cohort, opium use was significantly associated with higher odds of sarcopenia, with a modest dose-related trend and a stronger association observed in men and individuals without major comorbidities, independent of an extensive range of demographic, lifestyle, dietary, and medical confounders. Conducted in a high-prevalence region and leveraging strict exclusion criteria, detailed covariate adjustment, and quantified opium dose, our work implies that opium may be a modifiable, overlooked risk factor for muscle loss, although causality cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional design. These findings carry direct clinical implications, supporting targeted sarcopenia screening and early preventive interventions in opium users, especially in endemic areas. Future research should employ longitudinal and interventional designs, integrate mechanistic biomarker profiling, and expand to diverse populations to confirm causality, elucidate underlying pathways, and test strategies, such as opioid cessation, hormonal evaluation, and tailored exercise–nutrition programs, to mitigate opioid-related muscle decline.
