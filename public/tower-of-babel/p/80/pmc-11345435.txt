Synergistic collaboration between AMPs and non-direct antimicrobial cationic peptides
Authors: Zifan Ye, Lei Fu, Shuangyu Li, Ziying Chen, Jianhong Ouyang, Xinci Shang, Yanli Liu, Lianghui Gao, Yipeng Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Non-direct antimicrobial cationic peptides (NDACPs) are components of the animal innate immune system. But their functions and association with antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are incompletely understood. Here, we reveal a synergistic interaction between the AMP AW1 and the NDACP AW2, which are co-expressed in the frog Amolops wuyiensis. AW2 enhances the antibacterial activity of AW1 both in vitro and in vivo, while mitigating the development of bacterial resistance and eradicating biofilms. AW1 and AW2 synergistically damage bacterial membranes, facilitating cellular uptake and interaction of AW2 with the intracellular target bacterial genomic DNA. Simultaneously, they trigger the generation of ROS in bacteria, contributing to cell death upon reaching a threshold level. Moreover, we demonstrate that this synergistic antibacterial effect between AMPs and NDACPs is prevalent across diverse animal species. These findings unveil a robust and previously unknown correlation between AMPs and NDACPs as a widespread antibacterial immune defense strategy in animals.

Introduction

The immune system of animals comprises both innate and adaptive immunity, with the former serving as the primary defense mechanism against microbial infections1. Innate immunity-derived antimicrobial agents, such as bioactive peptides, contribute significantly to the immune response against pathogens2–4. Among these bioactive peptides, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are considered one of the most important families5. AMPs can be categorized, based on their structure, into amphipathic α-helical6, β-sheet7, and random coils with specific amino acids8. Most AMPs exhibit direct and potent antimicrobial activity that is essential for innate immunity9,10. The identification of cecropins11, drosocin12, and melittin13 has advanced our understanding of the pathogen-specific innate immunity in animals. So far, hundreds of AMPs have been identified in various human tissues14. This includes LL-37 and defensins, which are secreted by immune cells in response to pathogen invasion or other immune triggers6,15.

During the mining and characterization of AMPs, many cationic peptides or proteins have been identified that are co-expressed and co-localized in animal tissues. These molecules exhibit physical and structural similarities to AMPs but lack antimicrobial activity. For example, the human histone H2A protein meets the aforementioned criteria and lacks antimicrobial activity under physiological conditions16. However, it has been confirmed to play a crucial role in innate immunity17. In this study, we categorized peptides that possess significant positive charges and demonstrate limited antimicrobial activity as non-direct antimicrobial cationic peptides (NDACPs). Over the past two decades, numerous peptides meeting this definition have been discovered16,18–22. These peptides primarily originate from histone-derived peptides, histone variants23, and the AMP families24. NDACPs consist of holo-histone proteins H2A, H3, and H4 and their hydrolyzed fragments from human extracellular traps (ETs)25. In addition, they include the cathelicidin and defensin families found in different animals, such as koala-derived PhciCath26 and oysters-derived Cg-pro27. Many studies have mistakenly categorized NDACPs as AMPs owing to their structural characteristics. These studies primarily aimed to explore the potential of these drugs to directly eliminate pathogens. However, the limited direct antibacterial efficacy of NDACPs has led to a lack of research on their anti-infection functions, resulting in poor understanding of them.

Amphibians, especially frogs, are an important reservoir of naturally occurring active peptides, with more than 2000 secreted peptides identified so far24. In this study, we identified a representative co-expressed combination of AMP/NDACP in the frog (Amolops wuyiensis), a species where the cathelicidins family AMPs had never been previously found or reported. The AMPs, categorized as α-helix cathelicidins with potent antimicrobial properties, were designated AW1, whereas the NDACPs, lacking such abilities and exhibiting a random coil secondary structure were designated AW2. Our study used a combination of in vitro and in vivo antimicrobial characterization tests, transcriptomic analysis, and molecular dynamics simulations to unravel the comprehensive synergistic interaction between the two peptides in combating bacterial infections. In addition, we investigated their multifaceted synergistic antibacterial mechanisms, including membrane disruption, targeting of intracellular components, and ROS generation. Our findings revealed that the combination of AMPs and NDACPs and the underlying innate immune synergistic mechanisms are potentially ubiquitous across various animal species.

Results

Identification and characterization of AW1 and AW2

Two novel peptides, designated AW1 and AW2 (GenBank Accession Numbers: OM243850-OM243851 for AW1 and AW2, respectively), were cloned from the synthesized second-strand cDNAs of the skin of A. wuyiensis. As depicted in (Supplementary Fig. 1), cDNAs encoding AW1 and AW2 had lengths of 459 bp and 593 bp, respectively. The AW1 peptide consisted of 24-amino acids with eight positive charges (KKCKFLCKVKKKLKSVGLQISMGI). In contrast, the AW2 peptide comprised 34 amino acids and had 11 positive charges (RRAGRSRGRSRGRPPGRPRLPGLNNPIAGIPGRK). Sequence alignment revealed that the amino acid structures of both two peptide precursors conformed to the structural characteristics of the cathelicidin family AMPs. Under normal conditions, the transcription of the AW1 and AW2 genes resulted in the production of their respective mature peptide products across diverse organs. Notably, analysis of cDNA levels indicated co-localized of these peptides within the skin and intestine (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In addition, AW1 and AW2 expression in A. wuyiensis was significantly upregulated following Aeromonas hydrophila bacterial infection. The specific mRNA level of AW1 and AW2 in the skin was quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR). The transcription level of both peptides exhibited a time-dependent enhancement after continuous exposure to pathogens for 3 and 6 h, with AW2 levels being 10-fold higher than that of AW1 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). These findings suggested the involvement of both peptides in the immune response against infections in the frog A. wuyiensis.

The secondary structures of AW1 and AW2 peptides were predicted based on their amino acid sequences using Alphafold2 software (Fig. 1a), an AI system developed by DeepMind28. The top-ranked conformations were then filtered, revealing that AW1 predominantly adopts an α-helical conformation with a disulfide bond at the N-terminus, whereas AW2 adopts a random coil structure. These structures were subsequently selected for molecular simulations to analyze the mechanism of peptide-DNA interactions. The two peptides were chemically synthesized using the sequences provided in (Supplementary Table 1). Their structures were subsequently determined using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Fig. 1b). These findings indicated that neither AW1 nor AW2 exhibited typical secondary structures when dissolved in ddH2O. AW1 acts as a typical membrane-damaging AMP

Both the AW1 and AW2 peptides exhibited characteristic structures and significant positive charges (Supplementary Table 1). These characteristics were consistent with the attributes commonly observed in AMPs. First, we investigated their antimicrobial activity against ten Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains. Notably, AW1 exerted broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) ranging from 2.58 to 16.96 μM. In contrast, AW2 did not demonstrate direct antimicrobial activity against any of the tested bacteria even at concentrations as high as 50 μM (Supplementary Table 2). In addition, we evaluated the cytotoxicity exhibited by both peptides in mammalian and amphibian cell lines, as well as their hemolytic activity toward human and mouse erythrocytes. Across varying concentrations (0–50 μM), the values of hemolytic rate were below 3%, as evidenced by Supplementary Fig. 3a, b. AW1 displayed a favorable safety profile at concentrations conducive to physiological antimicrobial activity, with cell survival rates surpassing 50% for concentrations under 6.25 μM (Supplementary Fig. 3c–h). Notably, AW2 exhibited significantly lower cytotoxicity compared to AW1. These results indicated the excellent cell compatibility and safety of AW1 and AW2, supporting their functional effectiveness. Consequently, a series of further assays were performed to validate the antimicrobial efficacy of AW1 in a safe concentration range. Confocal imaging results revealed that treatment with AW1 at concentrations ranging from 1 × to 5 × MIC resulted in a significant reduction (40–60%) in K. pneumoniae ATCC43863 and S. aureus CMCC26003 within 45 minutes. The percentage of K. pneumoniae dead cells reached 61.76% at a concentration of 5 × MIC, while for S. aureus, it reached 55.80% (Fig. 1c). Moreover, time-killing kinetic assays revealed that AW1 was able to eliminate all bacteria within less than 1 h at high concentrations (5 × MIC), showing its superior antibacterial efficacy compared to polymyxin-B at the equivalent concentration (Fig. 1d). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of bacteria treated with AW1 showed morphological characteristics similar to those induced by membrane-disrupting AMPs. This was evidenced by significant bacterial cytolysis and distinct damage to the bacterial cell membrane within a short time period, accompanied by rapid aggregation of AW1 and disruption of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus membrane (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, the antimicrobial stability of AW1 was found to be excellent, as evidenced by a maximum 8-fold change in MIC values following the exogenous addition of metal cations or co-incubation with serum for 18 h (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Taken together, these findings suggested that AW1 was a potent AMP.

To elucidate the antimicrobial mechanism of AW1, we investigated alterations in bacterial membrane properties caused by incubation with AW1. Membrane damage was assessed using the membrane-sensitive dye Laurdan to measure the changes in membrane fluidity. Compared to the untreated group, the exposure of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus cells to AW1 (0.5–5 × MIC) for 30 min resulted in different levels of increase in Laurdan GP values, suggesting alterations in membrane fluidity (Fig. 1f). In addition, alterations in membrane rigidity could disrupt bacterial homeostasis, resulting in depolarization of the cytoplasmic membrane. Therefore, we used 3,3′-dipropylthiadicarbocyan iodide [DiSC3(5)] to detect depolarization of the bacterial membrane after AW1 treatment. DiSC3(5) accumulates in the cytoplasmic membrane and emits fluorescence upon an imbalance in the transmembrane potential29. The depolarization changes in the cytoplasmic membranes of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus induced by AW1 were found to be proportional to the peptide concentration for 1 h. Eventually, depolarization reached a level comparable to that of polymyxin-B (10 × MIC), a prototypical peptide antibiotic known to aggregate and disrupt bacterial membranes (Fig. 1g). Based on the membrane phenomenon experiments, we tentatively concluded that the mechanism of action of AW1 was consistent with that of membrane-disrupting AMPs.

Subsequently, we identified the precise target of AW1 in the bacterial membrane. Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and lipoteichoic acids (LTAs) serve as the principal components of the outer membranes of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively30. In addition, bacterial membranes are characterized by a rich presence of four distinct acidic phospholipids: phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and cardiolipin (CL). These phospholipids are known to potentially facilitate the accumulation of the hydrophobic regions of AMPs on the membrane surface31. The AW1/bacteria mixture was supplemented with LPS, LTAs, and the aforementioned phospholipids at varying concentrations. Our results showed that (Fig. 1h) increasing LPS concentration from 0 to 128 μg/mL resulted in an 8- to 16-fold increase in the MIC of AW1 against K. pneumoniae. Similarly, a dose-dependent relationship was observed between PG and PE, with PG exhibiting the most pronounced ability to increase the MIC values of AW1 by up to 4-fold at the same concentrations of PE. Exogenous additions of PE and CL had an impact on AW1’s efficacy against S. aureus, whereas LTAs appeared to have a negligible effect on AW1’s activity. Consequently, the interaction between LPS and AW1 warranted further investigation, given LPS’s pronounced influence on AW1’s antibacterial activity. Isothermal calorimetry (ITC) revealed a strong binding affinity between AW1 and LPS, with a dissociation constant (KD) of ~ 4.4 μM. This could be attributed to the presence of robust electrostatic interactions (Fig. 1i). Based on these findings, we concluded that AW1 selectively targeted membranes and interacted with LPS and acidic phospholipids, which are more abundant in Gram-negative bacteria. Subsequently, it disrupted and permeated the bacterial cell membranes, thereby exhibiting potent bactericidal activity.

AW2 enhances the antimicrobial activity of AW1 in vitro

Although the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of AW1 against various bacteria was confirmed, AW2, despite its structural similarity to AMP, did not exhibit any antimicrobial activity (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4). However, both peptides were found to be co-expressed in A. wuyiensis. Notably, the expression level of AW2 in the skin tissue exposed to bacteria was 2- to 10-fold higher than that of AW1 (Supplementary Fig. 2). These findings suggested a potential synergistic antibacterial effect between them. As shown in (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), both K. pneumoniae and S. aureus exhibited growth inhibition when exposed to AW1 at 1 × MIC for 4 h. This resulted in a significant reduction in bacterial colony counts from 1000-2000 to below 100. After an additional 4 h of co-incubation, bacterial counts increased. However, the addition of increasing doses of AW2 significantly inhibited the growth of all the bacteria. Bacterial colonies were reduced by 1- to 3-fold compared to those in the groups treated with AW1 alone. This reduction was particularly notable for K. pneumoniae as the bacteria were completely eradicated. Furthermore, we observed a significant reduction in the growth rate of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus after incubation with AW1 and AW2 for 18 h (p < 0.0001). This was evident from the lower optical density compared to that of both the untreated and AW1-treated groups (Fig. 2b). In addition, similar synergistic effects were observed in A. hydrophila, a prevalent amphibian aquatic pathogen (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Checkerboard broth microdilution assays were conducted to evaluate the antibacterial activity of AW1 and AW2. The objective was to determine whether the combined activity against K. pneumoniae and S. aureus was synergistic or additive. The concentration range for AW1 spanned from 1/4 × MIC to 16 × MIC, while for AW2, it ranged from 0 to 50 μM. Given the absence of antibacterial activity exhibited by AW2, the MIC was set at 50 μM, which was the highest detectable concentration. Results indicated that the combination of AW1 and AW2 exhibited a fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index of 0.3125 against K. pneumoniae and 0.375 against S. aureus. These values, both below 0.5, suggest a synergistic effect of AW1 and AW2 against the tested bacteria (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, this study aimed to elucidate whether the antimicrobial efficacy of the AW1 and AW2 combination resulted from monomer synergy or conjugation, similar to the previously documented scenario involving MG2 and TP132. To achieve this, comparative tests were conducted on the monomers AW1 and AW2 at equivalent concentrations in both H2O and SDS solutions. These solutions were chosen to mimic the bacterial membranes. Based on the DLS data, prominent intensity peaks of AW1 and AW2 were observed at 2.3 nm, 1000–1250 nm, and 5200 nm in the SDS solution. When AW1 and AW2 were mixed in equal proportions (1:1 v/v), the resulting peaks appeared within the same range (2.3, 1200, and 5300 nm). This finding aligned with the individual peaks of AW1 and AW2, and no additional peaks were observed (Fig. 2d). To evaluate peptide aggregation in aqueous solution, we used molecular dynamics simulation with GAROMACS 5.1.4 software and AMBER 14SB force field. In addition, we determined the aggregation state of peptides using HPLC under H2O conditions. Consistent with the DLS results, no aggregation was observed between AW1 and AW2 (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Thus, we concluded that the individual actions of the two peptides on the bacterial membrane exhibited synergistic effects as monomers rather than as polymers.

To comprehensively understand the synergistic mechanisms underlying the combination treatment of AW1 and AW2, we further conducted a transcriptional analysis of K. pneumoniae following exposure to both AW1 and AW2. The results of this analysis revealed that the combination treatment group had 1047 upregulated and 907 downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) compared to the control group (Fig. 2e, f), In contrast, only a limited number of DEGs were observed when either AW1 or AW2 were used individually for treatment (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Subsequently, representative genes were subjected to GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses. GO pathway analysis demonstrated that the upregulated DEGs were primarily associated with the ribosome and peptide metabolism fields. In contrast, downregulated genes were associated with oxidative stress and membrane transport (Fig. 2g). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that 14 pathways had the highest number of upregulated and downregulated DEGs. Among these pathways, ribosomes, ABC transporters, and oxidative respiration exhibited the highest percentage of DEGs (Fig. 2h). These findings suggested that the mechanism underlying the combination treatment of AW1/AW2 was more complex and diverse than that of the individual treatments alone.

Synergistic antimicrobial effects of AW1 and AW2 in vivo

We investigated the combined efficacy of AW1 and AW2 in vivo. The therapeutic potentials of AW1 and AW2 were evaluated in a murine model of local skin wounds. S. aureus, a bacterial pathogen responsible for a range of infections, including mild skin and soft tissue infections as well as severe invasive infections, was used in this model33. As shown in (Fig. 3a), the dorsal skin of BALB/c mice (7-week-old female) was curved to create symmetrical wounds. The wound was infected with S. aureus (1 ×1 07 CFU/mL). Subsequently, the mice were divided into six groups (n = 8 for each group) and smeared with either AW1 (2 mg/kg), AW2 (2 mg/kg), or a combination of both peptides at 2 mg/kg for each peptide, totaling three applications. Vancomycin (2 mg/kg) served as the positive control, while the sham and vehicle controls were also included34. The progress of healing was documented through photographs, and wound area measurements were taken every three days. Our findings revealed that the wounds treated with a single peptide remained crusted on day 12 (Fig. 3b) The average remaining wound area for the AW1 and vancomycin groups were 10.49% and 8.21%, respectively (Fig. 3c). In contrast, wounds treated with the combined therapy demonstrated nearly complete healing by day 12, indicating a high rate of wound recovery after the combined treatments. In addition, we collected and tested residual bacteria from the skin tissue on day 4 after three administrations to assess the efficacy of the bacterial load reduction. The AW1/AW2 combination treatment resulted in a significant 10-fold reduction in bacterial load compared to the vancomycin or AW1 alone groups (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3d). To evaluate tissue damage, we measured the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α cytokines in the skin tissue on day 4, as they are important proinflammatory cytokines associated with the inflammatory response35. ELISA revealed an experimental trend similar to that of bacterial loads. The combination treatment of AW1/AW2 significantly decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine production and reduced tissue damage compared to the peptide alone groups (Fig. 3e). AW2 demonstrated potential anti-inflammatory activity, as evidenced by the reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the AW2 treated alone group compared to those in the vehicle group and the group treated with AW1 alone. These findings suggested that AW2 played a specific role in combating bacterial challenges in vivo. Collectively, AW1/AW2 combination treatment exhibited effective antibacterial properties both in vitro and in vivo. We propose a mechanism by which AW2 might assist AW1 in combating pathogens through their distinct modes of action. AW2 effectively assists AW1 in combating bacterial resistance and biofilm formation

Previous studies have demonstrated a relationship between alterations in membrane phospholipid composition and potential induced by AMPs and the development of bacterial resistance to drug36. As depicted in (Fig. 4a), analysis of DEGs associated with multidrug efflux pumps in K. pneumoniae revealed that most genes were downregulated when treated with a combination of AW1 and AW2 peptides compared with untreated or single peptide-treated samples. The efflux pumps of K. pneumoniae primarily comprise the outer membrane channel protein, inner membrane transporter protein, and periplasmic protein37. AcrAB-TolC and OqxAB38 are representative proteins found in these pumps distributed across the membrane. We hypothesized that K. pneumoniae down-regulated the expression of outer membrane channel protein genes (such as Omp3639) and efflux transport protein genes (such as MdtB and MdtC 40) in response to survival pressure from AW1 and AW2. Based on this hypothesis, we conducted resistance development assays (Fig. 4b) using sub-MIC concentrations of AW1 in the presence or absence of AW2, along with two different mechanisms of antibiotics as controls. The findings indicated that the combination treatment of AW1/AW2 resulted in a lower increase in MIC during the 10–45 generations in K. pneumoniae and 5–25 generations in S. aureus, compared to the AW1 treatment alone. The expression of efflux pumps and porin proteins has consistently been associated with biofilm formation. Correlations between antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation have been demonstrated in K. pneumoniae41,42 and S. aureus43 with multiple membrane genes co-expressed in both biofilms and efflux pumps. We further examined the potential anti-biofilm activity of the AW1/AW2 combination at high concentrations, considering its efficient synergistic antibacterial and resistance reduction effects against K. pneumoniae and S. aureus. To precisely determine the synergistic activities, the test concentration of AW1 was set at a low level because of its potent concentration-dependent anti-biofilm effect (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Following incubation with the combination treatment (AW1, 1 × MIC; AW2, 25 μM), the crystal violet-stained biofilm exhibited a significant reduction with eradication rates of 81.8% and 78.7% and inhibition rates of 90.8% and 92.3% for K. pneumoniae and S. aureus, respectively. However, the rate in the individual peptide-treated groups was below 40% (Fig. 4c). Moreover, the results of the TTC assays supported these findings, indicating that the presence of biofilm may be determined by the occurrence of red formazan precipitate44. The residual amount of precipitate after exposure to the AW1/AW2 combination treatment was markedly lower than that after the AW1 treatment alone, with a statistically significant difference indicated by a p-value of 0.0005 and 0.0354, respectively (Fig. 4d). The combination-treated group showed a nearly transparent color when assessed with crystal violet and TTC, suggesting a significant reduction in the residual biofilm (Fig. 4e). In addition, the combination of AW1 and AW2 significantly reduced the number of viable bacteria in the established biofilms of K. pneumoniae (p < 0.0001) and S. aureus (p = 0.0308). The reduction was 0.4 and 0.3 orders of magnitude, respectively, compared to the treatment with AW1 alone (Fig. 4f). Furthermore, persister cells found within biofilms play a critical role in evading antibiotics and immune components, thereby contributing to biofilm resistance45. To assess the efficacy of AW1 and AW2 against persisters, biofilms of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus were exposed to rifampicin (100 × MIC) for 24 h, resulting in the survival of ~ 106-107 CFU/mL bacterial viable cells as persisters. Remarkably, within 2 h, the AW1/AW2 combination treatment group exhibited significant persister cell-killing efficacy. Approximately 99.9% of persister cells in K. pneumoniae and 97.2% in S. aureus were eradicated. In contrast, the individual peptide-treated group only achieved a viability reduction of at least 50% for persisters (Fig. 4g). These findings suggest that the AW1/AW2 combination exhibited strong anti-biofilm activity.

We conducted peptide cellular uptake assays in K. pneumoniae and S. aureus. Propidium iodide (PI) was used to assess the membrane integrity in the bacterial solutions. PI can enter damaged cytoplasmic membranes and bind to DNA, resulting in fluorescence emission48. The results in (Fig. 5b) show that AW2 alone exhibited minimal permeation through the cytoplasmic membrane. The fluorescence intensities of the AW1/AW2 combination-treated groups were comparable to those of groups treated with AW1 alone. However, no increase in the fluorescence intensity was observed with increasing doses of AW2. This proved that AW2 had a negligible effect on the cytoplasmic membrane permeability. Furthermore, we tested the permeability of the bacterial outer membrane using 1-(N-phenylamino) naphthalene (NPN), a lipophilic dye that can penetrate the bacterial outer membrane only if the integrity of the membrane is damaged or compromised29. In contrast to the PI results, AW2 alone significantly increased the permeability of the outer membrane of K. pneumoniae and E. coli CMCC44102 compared with the control groups (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5c). The pronounced NPN fluorescence observed in the AW1/AW2 combination treatment group suggested a potential concentration-dependent effect between both peptides in the outer bacterial membrane. Moreover, AW2 induced notable changes in the membrane potential and fluidity of K. pneumoniae cell membranes but had a minimal effect on S. aureus (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). This differential impact may be attributed to AW2’s disruptive properties on the outer membrane. Similar conclusions can be inferred from the Gram-negative strains, E. coli ATCC25922 and A. baumannii ATCC19606 (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). In summary, AW1 could traverse both bacterial outer and inner membranes, whereas AW2 preferred the outer membrane over the inner membrane. The combination of these two peptides results in a synergistic membrane-disrupting effect.

We investigated whether AW2 could enter the cytoplasm using AW1’s ability to traverse both the inner and outer cell membranes. FITC-labeled AW2 was used to monitor the possible intracellular localization. The treatment of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus with FITC-AW2 alone resulted in minimal or negligible intracellular fluorescence (Fig. 5d). However, the fluorescence intensity of FITC-AW2 for both bacteria increased in a dose-dependent manner upon the addition of AW1. Confocal microscopy revealed the co-localization of AW2 with the cytoplasm, as evidenced by Hoechst 33342 (blue) and FITC (green) staining (Fig. 5e). Flow cytometric analysis also supported these conclusions. Following the exogenous addition of AW1, significant intracellular co-localization of AW2 was observed in over 99.5% of K. pneumoniae and 77.6% of S. aureus (Fig. 5f). These results indicated that AW1 facilitated the intracellular uptake of AW2. We illustrated our perspectives using a schematic representation, wherein AW2 affects the outer membranes while AW1 can directly penetrate both membranes. In addition, AW1 promoted the intracellular uptake of AW2. These findings highlighted the distinct mechanisms underlying peptide transport in diverse bacterial types (Fig. 5g).

AW2 interacts with bacterial DNA and inhibits protein production

The intracellular localization of AW1 and AW2 was investigated owing to AW2’s ability to translocate into the cytoplasm with the assistance of AW1. We conducted a comprehensive screening of DEGs associated with intracellular targets including DNA and ribosomes. This analysis involved integrating the findings from the GO and KEGG analyses (Fig. 2e, f). The AW1/AW2 combination treatment exerted an inhibitory effect on DNA replication, as evidenced by the downregulation of genes involved in helicase (such as HepA, DinG, and RecG), ligase (LigA), and polymerase subunit (Polα, and Polδ) expression. In addition, the gene expression of repair system components, such as DNA topoisomerase (Topos), exonuclease (ExoV), and mismatch repair protein (MutL, RadC) was subsequently upregulated. To counteract the risks posed by AW1 and AW2, compensatory upregulation of genes associated with ribosome synthesis (the 30S and 50S subunits) and transcription/translation factors were observed (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 10). Previous studies have suggested that some AMPs can bind to genomic DNA, thereby inhibiting replication or transcription and leading to bacterial death49. To investigate the targeting of bacterial DNA by AW1 and AW2, we examined their binding affinity towards the genomic DNA of E. coli, since replication is the initial stage of gene expression. The gel retardation assay revealed a distinct band corresponding to the genomic DNA lane (adjacent to the marker lane). As the concentration of each peptide was increased, the genomic DNA gradually remained in the starting position within each agarose gel electrophoresis sample. This suggested the formation of stable peptide-DNA complexes. Notably, AW2 required a significantly lower concentration (25 μg/mL) for complex formation than AW1, which was three times higher (Fig. 6b). This observation was confirmed by ultraviolet spectra assays and ethidium bromide (EB) competitive binding assays. The UV absorption peak of DNA was observed at 260 nm, whereas the embedded EB in the double-strand emitted fluorescence at ~ 600 nm. As shown in (Fig. 6c), an interaction between the peptides and the double helix structure of DNA was evident, as indicated by the reduction in intensity of the maximum DNA peak with increasing peptide concentration. Similarly, the fluorescence emitted by EB bound to DNA decreased as the peptide/DNA weight ratio decreased from 20 to 0.65 (Fig. 6d). In addition, substituting proline and glycine in AW2 with alanine resulted in three AW2 variants: one with alanine replacing proline (AW2-m1), another with alanine replacing glycine (AW2-m2), and a third with alanine replacing both proline and glycine (AW2-m3). These modifications led to changes in the secondary structure of the variants compared to the original AW2. Subsequent UV absorption and EB experiments demonstrated that the DNA binding capacity of these AW2 variants was diminished (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b). Overall, the spectroscopy-based assays demonstrated that both peptides interacted with bacterial DNA. However, AW2, owing to its extended secondary structure and the presence of substantially charged residues, exhibited a significantly stronger DNA binding capability compared to AW1.

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed to gain a comprehensive understanding of the interactions between the peptides and DNA. We selected unmethylated class C CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG-C ODN) with TCGTCGTTTTCGGCGCGCGCCG base pairs as our model DNA fragment. CpG-C ODN is more frequent in bacterial genomes than in vertebrates50 and the smallest unit having an immunostimulatory effect, whose structure was predicted by Web 3DNA 2.0 (Fig. 6e). The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) histograms of the peptides (Supplementary Figs. 11a, b,13f, g) indicated that the peptide remained stable during the binding process with DNA fragments in our simulation. Two representative snapshots extracted from the MD trajectories (Fig. 6e) revealed that AW2 had a stronger association with DNA than AW1. The extended and flexible features of AW2 facilitated the adjustment of its conformation to effectively bind to DNA. In contrast, AW1 exhibited a helical structure and increased rigidity (Fig. 6f), hindering its binding to DNA. The binding disparities of AW1 and AW2 with DNA were effectively demonstrated through the probability distribution of the contact number and hydrogen bond count. As shown in (Fig. 6g), only 95–125 atoms of AW1 could contact DNA, whereas 125–180 atoms of AW2 could contact DNA. AW2 formed approximately 25 hydrogen bonds with DNA, whereas AW1 formed only 10 (Fig. 6h). In addition, the penitential of mean force profiles revealed that the dissociation energy for AW1 from DNA was 31.2 kcal/mol, whereas for AW2 it was as high as 57.6 kcal/mol (Fig. 6i). At higher peptide concentrations, such as 6 AW1, 6 AW2, or 3 AW1 + 3 AW2 interacting with 1 DNA fragment, AW2 also exhibited a higher affinity for DNA than AW1 (Supplementary Fig. 12a–e). In addition, we performed the MD simulation to investigate the DNA binding affinity of the AW2 variants (Supplementary Fig. 13c–e). As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 13h, AW2-m1, AW2-m2, and AW2-m3 exhibit contact numbers with DNA ranging from 125 to 165, 85 to 145, and 65 to 85 atoms, respectively. However, 125 to 180 atoms of AW2 can contact with DNA. The number of contacts of the mutant peptide is all lower than that of AW2. Meanwhile, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 13i, the number of hydrogen bonds in AW2-m1 and AW2-m2 remained unchanged from AW2. This consistency may be attributed to the mutated proline and glycine residues, which seemingly had no impact on the breaking and formation of hydrogen bonds. Conversely, the decrease in hydrogen bonds observed in AW2-m3 could be attributed to its apparent helical conformation, reminiscent of AW1. These results suggested that both peptides could bind to CpG-C ODN derived from bacteria, with AW2 exhibiting a particularly strong binding affinity for this DNA fragment. This heightened affinity is attributed to AW2’s inclusion of flexible amino acids, such as glycine, which enhance its structural adaptability upon interacting with bacterial DNA.

The strong binding affinity between AW2 and bacterial DNA could potentially affect the subsequent transcription and translation processes. To validate this hypothesis, we investigated the inhibitory effects of AW1 and AW2 in E. coli lysate-based in vitro coupled transcription/translation assays. This kit facilitated the simulation of the bacterial environment by providing essential components for transcription and translation. This enabled the expression of target genes into functional proteins by adding DNA templates51. The effects of the peptides were evaluated by quantifying EGFP fluorescence. Gentamycin and rifampicin were used as positive controls in this assay to represent antibiotics with distinct mechanisms of action that target transcription and translation. As depicted in (Fig. 6j), the results demonstrated a contrasting trend between the two peptides. AW2 significantly suppressed EGFP protein (> 70% inhibition, p < 0.0001) production compared to that in the control treatment group, resembling the effects observed in the two positive controls. In contrast, AW1 exhibited minimal inhibitory activity with no significant EGFP fluorescence at the same concentration of AW2 (10 μM) (p = 0.3062). Moreover, SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed the presence of the target protein EGFP and showed results consistent with the fluorescence detection assay (Fig. 6k). Therefore, we hypothesized that the increased activity of AW2 in suppressing downstream protein synthesis could be attributed to its high-affinity binding to DNA. These findings suggested that AW1 did not primarily target the intracellular replication or transcription/translation processes. Conversely, the entry of AW2 into bacterial cells, facilitated by AW1, enhanced its ability to bind bacterial DNA, thereby exerting strong inhibition of bacterial protein synthesis.

AW1 and AW2 synergistically induce intracellular oxidative stress

In addition to the genes involved in membrane transport and intracellular targets that were previously mentioned, our analysis of DEGs related to cellular respiration and oxidative stress in K. pneumoniae exposed to AW1 and AW2 revealed a consistent downregulation pattern associated with the tricarboxylic acid cycle, NADH depletion, and peroxisomes (Fig. 7a). Previous studies reported that exposure of bacteria to stressful conditions can disrupt the delicate balance between the oxidative and antioxidant systems, leading to an increase in ROS52. Upon exposure to AW1 or AW2 alone, a typical upregulation of DEGs was observed, which was similar to the response observed for AMPs and antibiotics53,54. In contrast, the combination-treated groups exhibited a contrasting pattern with the down-regulation of most DEGs related to oxidative stress. This disruption would inhibit the activity of key enzymes in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (fumarate reductase and aconitate hydratase), NADH depletion (NADH dehydrogenase), and peroxidase activity (catalase and superoxide dismutase). This hypothesis was corroborated by the expression of the aforementioned genes. The redox-sensitive transcriptional regulator SoxR (Fig. 7a), associated with oxidative stress and resistance55, was found to be inactive, resulting in the decreased activity of specific effluent pumps (Fig. 4a). Subsequently, we evaluated the effects of the combination treatment of AW1/AW2 on the bacterial respiratory chain by analyzing NADH and FAD levels. NADH and FAD are primarily metabolized in respiratory chain complexes I and III. Autofluorescence in Gram-negative bacteria is typically associated with FAD (Ex/Em: 488/525 nm) and NADH/NADPH (Ex/Em: 355/450 nm)56. FAD levels were determined by scanning the fluorescence bands corresponding to the green channel. A commercial kit was used instead of the autofluorescence assay because of the similarity in channel signals produced by NADH and NADPH. Our findings indicated that the combination of AW1/AW2 depleted NADH levels and increased FAD synthesis. This suggested an enhancement in respiratory chain activity (Fig. 7b, c). A similar trend was observed when polymyxin-B (1 × MIC) was used in combination with AW2 instead of AW1. Moreover, we determined the activities of three key enzymes involved in oxidative responses: superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPX). SOD converts O2– into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). GPX facilitates the reaction between H2O2 and reduced glutathione, producing oxidized glutathione and H2O. CAT effectively prevents oxidative damage by converting excess H2O2 into H2O and O257. The results revealed that the AW1/AW2 combination significantly inhibited the activities of all three enzymes, in contrast to the promotion trend observed in the treatment-alone group (Fig. 7d). Since the inhibition of antioxidant response-related genes was detected in the transcriptome analysis of the combination treatments, we speculated that AW1 and AW2 may induce a high accumulation of ROS. To test this hypothesis, the total ROS levels in K. pneumoniae and S. aureus were measured using DCFH-DA. The results indicated that the total accumulation of ROS depended on the doses of AW1 and AW2 (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). At relatively low concentrations of AW1 (0.5× MIC), there was no significant difference in the total ROS levels between the combination treatment group and the untreated group. However, at high concentrations (AW1, 1-5 × MIC), the combination treatment of AW1 and AW2 significantly increased the total ROS levels in both K. pneumoniae and S. aureus. Based on these findings, we concluded that relatively low concentrations of ROS did not significantly affect the antimicrobial activity of AW1. Conversely, increasing AW1 concentrations during co-incubation led to a significant increase in ROS levels, ultimately resulting in bacterial death. Excessive ROS accumulation can exacerbate oxidative stress-related toxicity leading to DNA breakage, protein carbonylation, and membrane damage58.

To investigate the role of ROS in the AW1/AW2 combination treatment, we introduced N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and antioxidant thiourea (Tu) into our study. NAC is known to scavenge ROS, whereas Tu acts as an antioxidant and can directly neutralize hydroxyl radicals generated by the Fenton reaction30,59. The results revealed an increase in ROS upon the incubation of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus with AW1 (1 × MIC) and AW2 (25 μM) for 30 min. However, the presence of scavengers inhibited this trend (Fig. 7e). Excessive ROS production can attack fatty acids and nucleic acids in the bacterial membrane. This attack leads to lipid peroxidation (LPO). Ferrous ions participate in the Fenton reaction. Ferroptosis is a reliable marker of LPO60. Therefore, we assessed the LPO levels in K. pneumoniae and S. aureus using C11-BODIPY (581/591). The ROS scavenger dipyridyl (DP), a ROS scavenger that efficiently chelates free Fe2+, was introduced to reverse LPO progression. Consistent with the trend observed in the ROS production assay, the level of LPO increased after 30-min treatment with the combination groups (AW1, 1 × MIC; AW2, 25 μM) and polymyxin-B (1 × MIC). However, LPO levels decreased after pre-incubation with DP (Fig. 7f). The intracellular ATP levels showed a similar trend when incubated with AW1/AW2 combination treatment, suggesting the involvement of the respiratory chain. In addition, the synthesis of ATP could be enhanced by substituting AW1 with polymyxin-B and combining it with AW2. This suggested potential synergistic effects of AW2 and specific antibiotics (Fig. 7g). We used two fluorescent dyes, CellRox Green (known to detect O2•– and •OH) and AmplexTM Red (to detect H2O2) to detect the specific components of the generated ROS. CellRox Green is known for detecting superoxide (O2•–) and hydroxyl radical (•OH), whereas AmplexTM Red is designed for detecting hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)61. Confocal images demonstrated increased fluorescence of CellROX Green and AmplexTM Red after 30-min of treatment with the AW1/AW2 combination. This suggested a higher quantity and diversity of ROS compared to the peptide treated alone or no treatment groups. Notably, K. pneumoniae exhibited a comprehensive spectrum of ROS, including O2•–, •OH, and H2O2. In contrast, S. aureus showed minimal H2O2 production, consistent with the observed trend in the total ROS generated (compared to control groups) (Fig. 7h).

Furthermore, we conducted antimicrobial experiments (Fig. 2a) with the exogenous addition of NAC and Tu to evaluate the effect of ROS on the antibacterial activity of the peptides. After incubation with the combination of AW1 (1 × MIC) and AW2 (25 μM), the growth of both K. pneumoniae and S. aureus was restored by the introduction of ROS scavengers. This was evident as the bacterial density started to increase 4 h post-treatment (Fig. 7i). Subsequently, the concentration of AW1 was increased to 5 × MIC while maintaining consistent experimental conditions. The mixture of peptides and bacteria was added to 96-well plates to evaluate bacterial growth. Absorbance measurements at 600 nm revealed minimal variation in bacterial density among the AW1/AW2 combination treatment groups, irrespective of the presence or absence of Tu or NAC. Exogenous supplementation with Tu and NAC offered limited protective benefits and did not reverse the trend of bacterial death, as evidenced by the inhibition rate remaining consistently > 80% for 18 h (Fig. 7j). These findings validated that ROS played a significant role in the antimicrobial activity of AW1 and AW2. At low concentrations of AW1 (0.5-1 × MIC), the synergistic effect between both peptides primarily relied on two mechanisms: membrane disruption (AW1) and intracellular target binding effects (AW2), which ultimately resulted in the generation of ROS. Furthermore, at high concentrations, rapid generation of ROS led to irreversible bacterial death. The involvement of ROS in the antimicrobial process is illustrated in a schematic diagram (Fig. 7k). Both peptides triggered a stress response that enhanced the oxidative respiratory chain, leading to increased ATP production. This process involved the transfer of O2 to O2•– by Complexes I and III, conversion of NADH to NAD+ by Complex I, and conversion of FADH2 to FAD by Complex II. At the same time, AW2-targeted intracellular effects suppressed the expression of antioxidant stress transcription factors SoxR and OxyR, consequently downregulating peroxidase activity and increasing ROS levels.

The synergistic antibacterial effects of AMPs and NDACPs are widespread in animals

Considering the widespread presence of NDACPs in different animal species, it is probable that similar synergistic antimicrobial effects exist between AMPs and NDACPs in various animal taxa. We identified several specific peptide combinations derived from amphibian skin secretions based on our previous research in the field of AMP discovery. The peptides included PN-1 and PN-2 peptides from the black-spotted frog (Pelophylax nigromaculata)21, OL-1 and OL-2 from the odorous frog (Odorrana livida)18, and RC-1 and RC-2 from the Bullfrog (Rana catesbeiana)22. Notably, these peptide combinations adhere to a consistent search strategy, wherein one peptide exhibits antimicrobial activity while the other, though cationic, lacks such antimicrobial properties. As shown in (Supplementary Table 5), PN-1, OL-2, and RC-1 exhibited strong antimicrobial activity, as evidenced by their effective inhibitory activity against eight standard strains of pathogenic bacteria at concentrations ranging from 1 to 20 μM. However, PN-2, OL-1, and RC-2 exhibited minimal or no antimicrobial activities against these bacteria. Based on their structural characteristics, including a significant number of cationic charges and their respective antimicrobial properties (Supplementary Table 1), these two classes of peptides can be classified as AMPs and NDACPs, respectively. To assess the effect of peptide combination treatment, four bacterial cultures were supplemented with exogenous PN-1/PN-2, OL-1/OL-2, and RC-1/RC-2. Bacterial growth density was monitored after a 2-h incubation period. The combination treatment with PN-1/PN-2 effectively inhibited all four pathogens, with over 80% inhibition against E. coli and S. aureus. This inhibition was significantly higher than that in the PN-1 alone treatment group (Fig. 8a). In addition, the combination treatment of OL-1/OL-2 and RC-1/RC-2 showed potent activity against E. coli and A. baumannii, resulting in an inhibition range of 20–60% (Fig. 8b c). Furthermore, we identified similar combinations of AMPs and NDCAPs in various animal species. Three pairs of peptides were introduced: AS-2 and AS-4 from crocodiles (Alligator sinensis)19, PS-1 and PS-4 from turtles (Pelodiscus sinensis)20, and H2A and LL-37 from humans (Homo sapiens)16. AS-4 and PS-4 have robust antimicrobial properties and membrane-disrupting capabilities20,62. LL-37 is a well-established human-derived AMP, known for its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity15. In contrast, AS-1 and H2A were devoid of antibacterial capabilities, while PS-1 showed only minimal antimicrobial activity against the four tested pathogens (Supplementary Table 5). Consistent with the findings in amphibian species, the combined administration of AS-1/AS-4 showed efficacy in targeting K. pneumoniae, E. coli, and S. aureus. This treatment significantly reduced bacterial growth to < 40% (Fig. 8d). PS-1/PS-4, and LL-37/H2A treatment exhibited a broad-spectrum inhibitory effect against K. pneumoniae, E. coli, S. aureus, and A. baumannii (Fig. 8e, f). The growth of the four test bacteria was reduced by 2- to 3-fold in both the PS-1/PS4 and LL-37/H2A treatment groups compared to that in the groups treated only with PS-4 and LL-37. Notably, S. aureus had a survival rate of < 20% in all three synergistic groups, implying that different animals may achieve effective resistance against bacterial infections through synergistic mechanisms. In addition, our research did not reveal a synergistic antimicrobial effect between BNP-32, a peptide derived from humans conforming to the characteristics of NDACPs, and LL-37 (Supplementary Fig. 15). The absence of evidence for their co-secretion under specific conditions leads us to propose that the synergistic antimicrobial activity observed between AMPs and NDACPs likely transpires at identical bodily sites. This mechanism serves as a universal, conserved strategy within the innate immune response, aimed at combating infections across various animal species.

Discussion

Cationic peptides, ubiquitously produced by all animals, are crucial components of innate immunity against infections63. Previous studies have identified cationic peptides possessing direct microbicidal activity as AMPs64. However, limited research is available on the non-direct antimicrobial capacity of other cationic peptides, referred to as NDACPs. The relationship between AMPs and NDACPs in innate immunity remains elusive and poses a challenge to our current understanding. Our investigation revealed a pair of novel AMP/NDACP peptides, AW1 and AW2, derived from the amphibian A. wuyiensis. Although the AW1 peptide exhibits potent antimicrobial abilities, the AW2 peptide lacks similar capabilities. Independent tests conducted exclusively with AW1 revealed that exogenously added AW2 effectively increased antibacterial activity both in vivo and in vitro. This enhancement closely resembled the physiological concentration ratios of AW1 and AW2. Moreover, AW2 exhibited significantly lower cytotoxicity towards host cells than AW1. Combination therapy involving AMPs and NDACPs shows great potential for mitigating toxicity, while preserving high efficacy (Fig. 9).Fig. 9A comprehensive characterization protocol for the identification of peptide pairs, namely AMPs and NDACPs, with potent synergistic activities against bacteria and wide distribution among animal specie.

AW1 and AW2 exhibit distinct membrane-disrupting abilities. AW1 is identified as a membrane-disrupting AMPs, such as Melittin13 and Magainin II65. In contrast, AW2 cannot enter the cytosol independently and is limited to perturbing the outer membrane rather than the inner membrane. In contrast to AMP thanatin, which specifically targets Gram-negative bacterial LPS66, AW2 exclusively modulates the outer membrane permeability. Due to the absence of membrane receptor protein expression and assistance67, AW2 lacks the ability to traverse the membrane and impede bacterial survival significantly. However, upon exogenous addition of AW1, we observed intracellular co-localization of AW2. The remarkable synergistic antimicrobial effects of the peptides suggest that AW2 may exert microbicidal activities through mechanisms beyond membrane disruption. In addition, the combination treatment with AW1 and AW2 represents two distinct membrane-targeting models in Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively. AW2 specifically targets the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. To the best of our knowledge, this unique synergistic membrane-disrupting mechanism has not been extensively investigated.

Upon entering into the cytosol, AW2 robustly interacts with bacterial DNA, effectively inhibiting replication and impeding downstream transcription-translation processes. Similar to the structure of non-lytic AMPs, such as PR-3968, Buforin II69, and Bac78, AW2 contains an arginine/proline-enriched sequence and exhibits significant positive charges. These properties of AW2 allow for extended and flexible features, strong binding to bacterial DNA through electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and embedding interactions, as well as a multi-target antimicrobial role that contributes to the reduced occurrence of bacterial resistance12. AW1 exhibits a positive charge and is responsible for binding to intracellular DNA. However, it does not affect the expression of regular bacterial proteins. AW1 relies on lysine residues (K1-2, K4, K8, K10-12, and K14) to preserve ionization interactions. Unlike AW1, AW2 contains proline residues (P14-15, P18, P21, P26, and P31) that induce and/or strengthen DNA/peptide kinks. In addition, AW2 has flexible amino acids, including glycine (G4, G8, G12, G16, G22, G29, and G32), which contribute to its structural versatility upon binding with bacterial DNA70. Based on the findings indicating the minimal inhibitory activity of AW1 on bacterial DNA replication and downstream processes, we propose that the efficacy of peptides in targeting bacterial intracellular targets is influenced not only by specific electrostatic binding forces but also by their secondary structure and amino acid characteristics.

The combination treatment with AW1 and AW2 elicits a cascade of bacterial responses, including membrane alterations, attenuated bacterial resistance, and inhibition of intracellular metabolic activities71. AW1-induced damage results in increased levels of bacterial repair systems and oxidative respiration. In contrast, AW2 specifically targets bacterial intracellular targets to inhibit the activity of the transcription repair factors OxyR, SoxR, and PerR, consequently facilitating the rapid production of ROS. ROS-induced damage caused by a surge exceeding the threshold leads to irreversible bacterial demise58. However, treatment with AW1 or AW2 alone does not induce noticeable oxidative stress. We propose that the ROS-induced cell death pathway is a potential mechanism of action. ROS are primarily produced as by-products of cellular respiration at low concentrations. However, they become important when present at higher concentrations, as they can cause damage to cell membranes and DNA. Our data indicate that under the influence of a similar membrane pore-forming agent, polymyxin B, bacteria display comparable oxidative stress activity. In summary, ROS generation is primarily dependent on AW2’s ability to localize in the cytoplasm.

NDACP was initially classified as part of the AMP family due to its structural similarities to AMPs, characterized by a high content of basic amino acids and secondary structures typical of AMPs. Extensive research on NDACPs has been undertaken across various animal species, with amphibians, particularly frogs, receiving significant attention. In these creatures, over 2000 skin-secreted peptides have been identified, and many of these peptides have been thoroughly characterized24. Recent examples of retrievable accounts include PopuCATH derived from tree frogs (Polypedates puerensis)72, Cathelicidin-NV derived from plateau frogs (Nanorana ventripunctata)73, and RK-13 derived from South and Central American hylidae (Agalychnis calcarifer)74. Furthermore, similar peptides have been identified in other vertebrates and invertebrates, such as B-type Natriuretic Peptide (BNP-32)75 and peptide YY76 derived from humans, PhciCath5 derived from Koala26, CATHPb derived from the Burmese python (Python bivittatus)77, and pl14a derived from snails (Conus planorbis)78. The synergistic effects of AMP/NDACPs, particularly between LL-37 and H2A16, and combinations like AW1/AW2, highlight their prevalence across the animal kingdom. During the early stages of AMP excavation, researchers did not notice many cationic peptides because they did not exhibit antimicrobial properties. Consequently, there are limited direct reports on the antibacterial activity of NDACPs. However, they are frequently mentioned as by-products of AMPs that possess antioxidant79,80 or immunomodulatory properties81. Re-evaluation of a subset of cationic peptides previously identified in our laboratory revealed the ubiquity of synergistic interactions between AMPs and NDACPs in amphibians, mammals, and reptiles. However, not all peptides classified as NDACPs demonstrated the anticipated synergistic antimicrobial effects when combined with AMPs. This observation highlights the need for a deeper understanding of the co-secretion dynamics of AMPs and NDACPs within the same bodily organs of species. The co-expression of such peptides in the same host species may function as an innate defense mechanism against bacterial infections in different organisms. AMPs generally exhibit a higher bactericidal potency, whereas NDACPs exhibit reduced toxicity towards host cells. Cytotoxicity assays highlighted that the toxicity of AW1 towards African clawed toad kidney cells (ACTK-1) was 3- to 5-fold higher than that of AW2 at elevated concentrations. Conversely, at lower concentrations aligned with natural physiological conditions, AW1 does not demonstrate notable cytotoxic effects. This demonstrates the presence of well-balanced cytotoxic and bactericidal mechanisms in animals, indicating their evolutionary adaptation. Peptides consistent with the definition of NDACPs have been found in insects82,83, microorganisms84, and plants85,86. This suggests that the synergistic phenomenon is widespread and requires further investigation and conceptualization. Furthermore, considering the remarkable synergistic antimicrobial capabilities of AMP/NDACP peptide combinations, pursuing targeted discovery of these pairs is anticipated to yield novel approaches to combating in vivo bacterial infections, particularly those involving drug-resistant strains.

Methods

Animals and ethical statement

No permits were required for the study’s sampling sites/activities following the regulations and guidelines of the Ethics Review Committee of Soochow University. Moreover, this study did not involve endangered or protected species. All animal testing protocols were conducted in compliance with Animal Welfare Act regulations and the 1996 National Research Council’s Guide for Laboratory Animal Care and Use. Female frog (A. wuyiensis) specimens weighing 20–40 g were collected from the Huangshan Mountain, Anhui Province, China. Female C57BL/6 J and BALB/c mice (7-week-old, 18–20 g) were purchased from China Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Animals were provided with unrestricted access to food and water during a 12-h light/dark cycle, maintaining a temperature range of 20–26 °C and relative humidity between 40–70%. Frogs were housed in dechlorinated tap water and underwent daily water changes to ensure optimal living conditions.

Molecular cloning and characterization of peptide-encoding cDNAs

The A. wuyiensis skin tissues were pulverized into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, USA). A tissue cDNA library was generated using an In-Fusion SMARTerTM Directional cDNA Library Construction Kit (Takara, Japan). SMARTScribeTM MMLV Reverse Transcriptase and the kit-provided primers SMART IV Oligonucleotide and CDS III/3′ PCR Primer were used for the reverse transcription of first-strand cDNA. The long-distance PCR method was used to amplify the second-strand cDNA using the 50 × Advantage 2 Polymerase Mix and two primers provided in the kit: 5′ PCR Primer and CDS III/3’ PCR Primer. The synthesized second-strand cDNAs were stored at − 80 °C and used as a template for subsequent gene cloning procedures21.

A degenerate antisense primer (5′-WSCRCAGRYCTTCACCTCC-3′) was specifically designed based on known nucleotide sequences of conserved cathelin structural domains in amphibian cathelicidin genes21. This primer was paired with a complementary sense primer (5′-AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGTG-3′) specifically designed using the SMARTer V Oligonucleotide primer provided in the kit. It was used to screen for 5′ cDNA fragments encoding cathelicidins. The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 57 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min and 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified PCR product was purified by gel electrophoresis and subsequently cloned into the pMD19-T vector (Takara, Japan) for sequencing analysis.

Subsequently, a degenerate primer for the sense strand (5′-ATGAAGGTCTGGCAGTGTGTGCT-3′) was designed to target the 5′coding region of the cDNAs. The primer was paired with a 3′ antisense primer (5′- ATGGAGRTCTGGCAGTGTGTG-3′) based on the CDS III/3′ PCR Primer provided in the kit to screen full-length cathelicidin cDNAs. The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1.5 min and 72 °C for 10 min. The pMD19-T vector (Takara, Japan) was used for sequencing after gel electrophoresis and cloning.

Peptide synthesis and purification

Peptides, including FITC-labeled (N-terminal) peptides, were synthesized by GL Biochem (Shanghai) Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The purity of the peptides was confirmed to be higher than 95% by RP-HPLC and MALDI-TOF MS analysis.

Circular dichroism analysis and structural prediction

Peptides AW1 (KKCKFLCKVKKKLKSVGLQISMGI) and AW2 (RRAGRSRGRSRGRPPGRPRL-PGLNNPIAGIPGRK) were initially modeled using the homology modeling technique in Alphafold2 software to determine their three-dimensional (3D) structures. The secondary structures of the peptides were determined by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy on a Jasco J-715 spectrophotometer (Jasco, Japan). Peptides were dissolved in distilled water or SDS (30–120 mM) at a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. The CD spectrum was recorded under the following conditions: wavelength range, 190–260 nm at 298 K; path length cell, 0.1 cm; bandwidth, 1 nm; response time, 1 s; and scanning speed, 100 nm/min. The spectra were scanned three times consecutively and averaged. Subsequently, the CD of the solvent was subtracted to obtain the final peptide.

RP-HPLC assay

The chromatographic experiment was conducted using an Agilent 1260 series HPLC system with UV detection at 215 nm and a manual 20 µL injector. Individual peptides (10 µg) were loaded onto a MultoHigh 100 RP 18 column (CS-Chromatographie Service GmbH, Germany) measuring 250 mm × 3 mm. The column was subsequently balanced using a 0.1% (v/v) TFA solution in water. Elution was performed by gradually increasing the concentration of acetonitrile linearly at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min.

Tissue expression profiles and bacteria challenge assay in A. wuyiensis

Three frogs (A. wuyiensis) were dissected to extract total RNA from the skin, lungs, liver, and intestine using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, USA). As shown in Supplementary Table 3, the primers used for AW1 amplification were as follows: forward primer (5′-GAGAATTCGCGCAGCAAGAG -3′) and reverse primer (5′-TGATGGCGCGGTGATTTACT-3′). The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min and 72 °C for 5 min. For AW2 amplification, the forward primer used was (5′- CACATTGGAGGTGGCTCACT-3′) and the reverse primer was (5′-TATGCCTGCAATGGGGTTGT-3′). Control PCR was performed using GADPH-specific primers (forward primer, 5′-GTCTGTGGTTGACCTGACCT-3′ and reverse primer, 5′-GCCTTCACTACGGCTTTGATG-3′) under identical cycling conditions as previously described. After amplification, RNA integrity was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Each reaction product was electrophoresed on 1% agarose gel at a constant voltage of 90 V for 30 min. Bands were visualized by ethidium bromide staining.

To assess changes in gene expression during bacterial infection, A. wuyiensis specimens were divided into two groups (n = 3) and subjected to 1 cm scratches on their dorsal surface. Subsequently, they were inoculated with Aeromonas hydrophila A22 (107 CFU/mL). After exposure for 3–6 h, RNA samples from the skin were pulverized into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen. The pulverized samples were then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each qPCR system consisted of 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (10 μL, Invitrogen, USA), cDNA template (1 μL), mixed primer (2 μL), and ROX dye ii (0.4 μL, Invitrogen, USA), with ddH2O added to make the total volume up to 20 μL. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 3. The assay was performed on a CFX96-C1000 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) with three replicate wells.

Antimicrobial assay

All strains used in this research are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The standard strains were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and the National Center for Medical Culture Collections (CMCC). Clinical strains were isolated and characterized by the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University and were then supplied for this research (Supplementary Table 3). In addition, Aeromonas hydrophila was generously donated by Ningbo University. The antimicrobial activities of the peptides were evaluated following established protocols87. A total of 10 Gram-negative and 10 Gram-positive bacterial strains were used for the assay. The bacteria were cultured in Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) at 37 °C until they reached the exponential growth phase (OD600 ≈ 0.2), and subsequently diluted to a concentration of 2 × 106 CFU/mL. The peptides were serially diluted in 96-well microtiter plates and combined with an equivalent volume of the microbial inoculum. The plates were shaken in a THZ-100 constant-temperature culture shaker (Yiheng, China) at 37 °C and 110 rpm for 18 h. The absorbance of the plates was measured at 600 nm using a Tecan Infinite Spark microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). The concentrations at which microbial growth was absent were identified as the MIC values. Polymyxin-B and vancomycin were used as the positive controls in this study. Two standard bacterial strains, K. pneumoniae ATCC43863 and S. aureus CMCC26003 were used as representative strains of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively, for subsequent assays. A. hydrophila YD17, E. coli ATCC25922, and A. baumannii ATCC19606 were used in complementary assays.

Hemolytic and cytotoxic assays

The hemolytic assay was performed according to a previously described method88. Erythrocytes were obtained from C57BL/6 J mice and a healthy human population. Cells were washed with PBS (Gibco, USA) three times, and incubated with gradient concentrations of AW1 or AW2 at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm using a Tecan Infinite Spark microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). The hemolysis rates were determined by comparing them to a blank solution. The blank solution consisted of mouse erythrocyte solutions with either 100% or 0% hemolysis achieved by the inclusion or exclusion of 1% Triton X-100 (v/v), respectively. The in vitro cytotoxicity of AW1 and AW2 was evaluated using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) method on RAW 264.7 (murine macrophage), 4T1 (murine breast cancer), B16 (murine melanoma) cell lines, HEK 293 T (human embryonic kidney), HaCaT (human keratinocytes) and ACTK-1 (African clawed toad kidney)89. The four cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (HyClone, Australia), while the remaining cells were cultured in DMEM medium (HyClone, Australia) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (PAN Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany), or in F12K medium (HyClone, Australia) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum, and 100 U/mL streptomycin and penicillin (HyClone, Australia). Subsequently, different concentrations of the peptides were added and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Each well was treated with a solution containing 5 mg/mL MTT (20 µL/well) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and cultured for an additional 4 h. The supernatant was removed from each well, and DMSO (200 µL per well) was added to dissolve the formazan crystals formed by the viable cells. 0.1% Triton X-100 treated cells were used as the positive control group. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 570 nm using a Tecan Infinite Spark microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland).

Time-dependent killing assay

K. pneumoniae ATCC43963 and S. aureus CMCC26003 bacterial strains were cultured in MHB at 37 °C and 200 rpm in a THZ-100 constant-temperature culture shaker (Yiheng, China) overnight until they reached the exponential growth phase. The resulting bacterial suspension was diluted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL in fresh MH broth. The AW1 peptide was diluted to a final concentration corresponding to the MIC gradient and incubated with bacteria at 37 °C for 0, 15, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min. Polymyxin-B was used as the positive control90. In the synergy-testing experiments, the time intervals were adjusted to 2 h. Each gradient dilution was meticulously applied and uniformly spread onto MH agar plates, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 18 h. Bacterial colonies on the plates were quantified using a SupcreG10 colony counting and measuring instrument (Shineso, China), which marked single colonies in green.

The antibacterial efficacy of the peptides at different concentrations was evaluated using a Tecan Infinite Spark microplate reader at 600 nm. In summary, the bacterial concentration was determined to be 3 × 107 CFU/mL following overnight culture. Subsequently, different peptide concentrations were added to the bacterial solution in a 96-well plate. The absorbance of the bacterial solution at 600 nm was monitored continuously for 18 h.

Bacterial confocal microscopy scanning

SYTO™9 (Invitrogen, USA) and PI (Invitrogen, USA) fluorescence dyes were used to distinguish between dead and live bacteria induced by AW1 based on their distinct fluorescence intensities30. ROS fluorescence probes, CellRox Green (Invitrogen, USA) and AmplexTM Red (Invitrogen, USA), were used to detect different types of ROS91. CellRox Green exhibited higher sensitivity towards O2- and •OH at an excitation/emission wavelength of 485/520 nm, while AmplexTM Red enabled the detection of hydrogen peroxide in vitro at an excitation/emission wavelength of 571/585 nm. FITC-labeled AW2 and Hoechst 33342 DNA dye reagent (MCE, China) were used for bacterial membrane testing.

K. pneumoniae ATCC43963 and S. aureus CMCC26003 bacterial strains (in the logarithmic growth phase) were washed three times and resuspended in PBS (Gibco, USA). Subsequently, the samples were diluted until OD600 ≈ 0.1 and then exposed to various MIC concentrations of AW1 or control groups for a 45-minute incubation period. SYTOTM9 (0.87 mM, 3 μL) and PI (5 mM, 3 μL) were added to each sample at a final volume of 1 mL at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Different types of ROS were identified using CellRox Green (2 mM, 2 μL) and AmplexTM Red (10 mM, 1 μL) following 30-min incubation with AW1 (1 × MIC) and AW2 (25 μM). The cellular uptake rate of intracellularly ingested AW2 (25 μM) was quantified by measuring the fluorescence of FITC emitted after the addition of AW1 (1 × MIC, 2 × MIC) for 30 min. The stained bacteria were fixed with 50% glycerin, and fluorescent images of the stained bacteria were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (AIR HD25, ABI, USA). The fluorescence intensity of each image was quantified using the Image J software.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted following an established protocol with slight modifications92. The bacterial culture was incubated with the peptides (at a final concentration of 1 × and 5 × MIC) using the previously mentioned method, at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, bacterial solutions were centrifuged, and the resulting bacterial pellets were collected for SEM sample preparation following the manufacturer’s recommended standard procedure93. The prepared samples were analyzed using an S-4800 SEM instrument (Hitachi, Japan).

Membrane fluidity assay

The membrane-permeable fluorescent probe Laurdan (Aladdin, China) demonstrated spectral sensitivity towards the phospholipid phase of bacterial membranes. The experimental procedure outlined in previous literature was followed, along with the selection of a concentration gradient of peptides corresponding to 0.5–5 × MIC94. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay

Peptide-LPS affinity was assessed through calorimetric experiments at 25 °C using a Malvern MicroCal instrument (USA)30. Experiments were performed using a diluted solution of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 150 mM NaCl (ITC buffer). The experiment involved sequential injections of LPS from E. coli 055:B5 (Solarbio, Beijing) into a calorimetric cell filled with AW1 at a 10:1 ratio. The injection process was repeated 20 times at equilibration intervals of 200 s. The obtained data were processed using Origin 5 software to generate fitting curves to calculate the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD). Data were analyzed using the single-site binding model in the Origin 7.0 package (Malvern MicroCal, USA) or the independent model in the NanoAnalyze software package (TA, Inc.).

Membrane potential assay

The fluorescent probe 3,3’-dipropylthiadicarbocyanine iodide [DiSC3(5)] (AAT Bioquest, USA) was used to evaluate the effects of peptides on the bacterial membrane potential95. Bacterial cells were incubated until they reached the logarithmic growth phase, then washed and resuspended in HEPES buffer (5 mM, pH 7.2). The cell suspension was subsequently incubated with 0.5 μM DiSC3(5) for 30 min to stabilize fluorescence. Subsequently, five different concentrations of AW1 corresponding to the MIC gradients were added to the bacterial suspensions. Polymyxin B (5 × MIC) was used as a positive control due to its mechanism of targeting and disrupting the bacterial cell membrane, leading to potential disturbances. Fluorescence measurements were recorded at each time point using a Tecan Infinite Spark microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 620 and 670 nm, respectively.

Antimicrobial stability assay

The antimicrobial stability of AW1 was determined in the presence of physiological concentrations of metal ions and serum96. Briefly, 50 μL of AW1 containing various salts (300 mM NaCl, 9 mM KCl, 12 μM NH4Cl, 2 mM MgCl2, 4 mM CaCl2, and 6 μM FeCl3) (Aladdin, China) and 50% mouse serum (Yuanye Bio-Technology, China) were prepared in a sterile 96-well plate. Subsequently, 50 μL of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus cultures grown to logarithmic phase and diluted to 2 × 105 CFU/mL were added to each sample. The subsequent steps mirrored those of the MIC assay. At predetermined time points (0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 18 h), a portion of the sample was withdrawn, and the MIC was determined.

In addition, LPS (0–128 μg/mL) from E. coli O111:B4, LTA (0–128 μg/mL) from S. aureus, and four distinct acidic phospholipids: phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and cardiolipin (CL) (0–16 μg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was exogenous added to tested the antibacterial activity of AW1 using checkerboard microdilution experiments. The experimental procedure closely mirrored that of the metal ions and serum assay, and the MIC values were recorded after incubating different concentrations of lipids with AW1.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) combined with ability test

DLS experiments were performed following a previously established protocol32. The peptides were dissolved in ddH2O and the experiment was divided into three groups: AW1, AW2, and a mixture of AW1 and AW2 at equal concentrations. K. pneumoniae ATCC43863 was cultured in MH broth at 37 °C, 200 rpm in THZ-100 constant temperature culture shaker (Yiheng, China) until it reached the logarithmic growth phase. Furthermore, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted to identify the enriched differentially expressed genes in various pathways and phenomena.

Mouse skin infection model

The mouse skin infection model was induced with S. aureus CMCC26003 following previously established protocols with certain modifications35. BALB/c mice (7-week-old female, n = 8) were anesthetized using isoflurane in an animal anesthesia machine (RWD, Shenzhen, China). A symmetrical curved wound (10 mm in diameter) was created on their dorsal surfaces. Subsequently, the wound was carefully smeared with 50 μL of bacterial suspension (107 CFU/mL). At 24, 48, and 72 h time points, one group (n = 8) received smear treatment with 50 μL of PBS (Gibco, USA), while the other groups were treated with 50 μL of AW1 (2 mg/kg), AW2 (2 mg/kg), or a combination of both peptides after infection. Vancomycin (2 mg/kg) was used as the positive control. Cutaneous wound healing was monitored at three-day intervals, and the wound areas were quantified using Image J software. An additional group of BALB/c mice (7-week-old female, n = 8) underwent the same procedure as described earlier. After a 72-h administration period, mice were euthanized to collect skin tissue abrasives for bacterial counts and determination of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) using ELISA kits (R&D Systems, USA).

Antimicrobial resistance development assay

The bacterial strains K. pneumoniae ATCC43863 and S. aureus CMCC26003 (in the logarithmic growth phase) were diluted 1:1000 into 1.5 mL of fresh MHB. The metabolic dye triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used for the antibiofilm assay with slight modifications, as outlined in a previous study102. Bactericidal activities against established biofilms and persister cells

The experimental procedures for bacterial incubation and peptide supplementation in the established biofilm assay were conducted following a modified version of the TTC assay protocol. Following a 24-hour incubation period with the peptide, the bacterial suspension was washed three times with PBS (Gibco, USA). Subsequently, the adherent bacteria were detached by sonication of 96-well plates for 5 min. Viable bacteria in the established biofilms were quantified using serial dilutions of the bacterial suspension and plated onto an LB solid medium.

The effect of peptides on persister cells in bacterial biofilms was assessed following established protocols described in previous publications103. Log-phase bacteria at a concentration of 107 CFU/mL were inoculated into 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Following three washes with PBS, 100 μL of fresh MHB containing rifampicin (4.9 μg/mL for S. aureus and 234 μg/mL for K. pneumoniae, 100 × MIC) was added to the bacteria to remove planktonic bacteria from the biofilms over a 24 h period. Bacteria were exposed to a combination of AW1 (1 × MIC) and AW2 (25 μM), as well as individual treatments, in 100 μL PBS for 3 h. The bacterial suspension was serially diluted and plated onto an LB solid medium.

LB solid medium was incubated in an inverted position for 18 h at 37 °C in a constant-temperature incubator. Bacterial colonies on the plates were quantified using a colony counting and measuring instrument (SupcreG10, Shineso, China).

Parameters and protocols of molecular dynamics simulations of AW1 and AW2

We used the AMBER14SB force field108 and GROMACS 5.1.4109,110 to conduct atomistic MD simulations. The DNA sequence used in our study was unmethylated class C CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG-C ODN) with TCGTCGTTTTCGGCGCGCGC-CG base pairs. The three-dimensional structure of this sequence was obtained from Web 3DNA 2.0 (http://web.x3dna.org/), a website that specializes in the analysis, visualization, and modeling of 3D nucleic acid structures. Two sets of systems were investigated by MD simulations. The first set consisted of a single peptide (1 AW1 or 1 AW2) that interacted with a DNA fragment. The second set included six peptides (six AW1, six AW2, or 3 AW1 + 3 AW2) interacting with the same DNA fragment. A box measuring 10.0 nm in the x, y, and z dimensions was constructed for all the systems. The peptides were positioned 2.5 nm from the center axis of the DNA fragment using the Packmol software111. The box was solvated using the TIP3P water model and neutralized by randomly adding Na+ and Cl− ions, resulting in a total of ~ 100000 atoms. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm until the maximum force reached a threshold of 1000.0 kJ/mol/nm. The systems were equilibrated in constant-number, constant-volume, constant-temperature (NVT), and constant-pressure (NPT) ensembles, and production runs were conducted in the NPT ensemble for 300 ns with a time step of 2 fs and periodic boundary conditions. The temperature was maintained at 310 K using a V-rescale thermostat112, with a coupling constant of 2 ps. The pressure was set to 1.0 bar using a Parrinello-Rahman barostat113 with a time coupling constant of 5 ps. Van der Waals interactions were addressed using the force-switching technique, which involved decaying the forces to zero within the range of 1.0 and 1.2 nm. Short-range electrostatic interactions were truncated at a distance of 1.2 nm. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald decomposition algorithm with a Coulomb cutoff of 1.2 nm and a mesh spacing of 0.12 nm. Three independent simulations were conducted for each system to collect the statistical data. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was calculated using the GROMACS 5.1.4 software suite. Umbrella sampling simulations were performed to calculate the potential of mean force (PMF) for separating AW1 and AW2 from DNA. The structural landscapes were generated using VMD114.

Parameters and protocols of molecular dynamics simulations of AW2 and its variants

We used the AMBER99SB-disp force field115 to perform the systems of position-restrained peptide solvated by TIP4P water. For the systems, a box with an initial size of 6.0, 6.0, and 6.0 nm in the x, y, and z dimensions was built. Peptides were placed in the center of the box. Then, the box was solvated with the TIP4P water model and Na+ and Cl- ions which were randomly added to neutralize the system, resulting in ∼ 30000 atoms in total. The simulation parameters are the same as peptide binding with DNA systems. After full relaxation of 100 ns of water and ions, we remove the position restriction on the peptide. REST3 enhanced sampling of 8 replicas was performed 100 ns by GROMACS-2022.5-mpi-plumed_2.9.0116 to obtain the conformation of each peptide. After obtaining the stable conformation of the peptides, we performed the molecular dynamics of peptide interaction with DNA. The parameters and settings are the same as in the simulation of AW1 and AW2 systems interacting with DNA. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was computed using standard utilities included in the GROMACS software suite.

Analyses

Helicity

The helicity of the peptide in the simulation was measured by monitoring the secondary structure using the gmx do_dssp tool, which was interfaced with DSSP117. Helicity was calculated by dividing the number of helical residues by the total number of residues.

Probability distribution of contact number

Atoms of a peptide within a distance of 4 Å from any atom of the DNA fragment were considered to be atoms in contact with DNA. The number of contacts was counted by considering up to 100 samples with a time separation of 1 ns from a single trajectory in the last 100 ns. The probability was then calculated using a bin size of 10 units for the contact number.

Number of hydrogen bonds

The number of hydrogen bonds between the peptide and DNA was determined using the gmx_hbond function in the GROMACS software. Increased hydrogen bonding between the peptide and DNA fragments implied stronger intermolecular interactions. In this study, we monitored the number of H bonds as a function of time using the MD trajectory over the last 100 ns.

Potential of mean force

The potential of mean force (PMF) for dissociating AW1 or AW2 from DNA was determined using umbrella sampling118 and the weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM)119. Starting from a structure where a peptide is tightly bound to a DNA fragment, we aligned the principal axes of the system along the z-coordinate axis. We then performed steered MD using a harmonic force of 5000 kJ/mol/nm2 to separate the peptide from the DNA, resulting in the generation of a series of initial configurations. Then, 26 configuration windows were screened at intervals of 0.2 nm in the direction of DNA normal (x-axis). In each window, MD simulations were conducted by restraining the center of mass of the peptide using a constant harmonic force of 1000 kJ/mol/nm. Up to 50 ns of simulation was performed at 310 K to ensure that the root mean square deviation (RMSD) could approach equilibrium. The trajectory in the last 30 ns was used to generate PMF profiles using WHAM with the g wham tool in GROMACS. Statistical errors were estimated using a bootstrap analysis with 100 bootstraps120.

ROS-related indicator measurement assay

The antioxidant enzyme activity was assessed using established protocols121. K. pneumoniae ATCC43863 and S. aureus CMCC26003 suspensions were incubated with the peptides and cultured for 45 min. The bacterial solution was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the bacterial solution was resuspended in 800 μL of PBS (Gibco, USA) and added to the centrifuge tube. The bacteria were then crushed using an ultrasonic crusher (Scientz, Ningbo, China) at 35 kHz (5 s, 6 s intervals, 3 min) in an ice bath. The supernatant was collected and the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, cellular glutathione peroxidase, ATP, and NADPH were measured using SOD (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Catalase (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Cellular Glutathione Peroxidase (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), ATP (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and AmpliteTM Colorimetric NADPH (AAT Bioqest, USA) Assay Kits. Synergistic antibacterial effects of AMPs and NDACPs from other animal species

The synergistic antimicrobial effects of AMPs and NDACPs were investigated using peptides PN-1 and PN-2 from the black-spotted frog (Pelophlax nigromaculata)21, OL-1 and OL-2 from the odorous frog (Odorrana livida)18, RC-1 and RC-2 from Bullfrog (Rana catesbeiana)22, LL-37 and wild-type H2A from humans (Homa sapiens)16, AS-2 and AS-4 from the crocodile (Alligator sinensis)19, and PS-1 and PS-4 from the turtle (Pelodiscus sinensis)20. Peptide concentrations were determined to be 25 μM for non-antimicrobial peptides and 1 × MIC for AMPs. The absorbance of the bacterial suspension was measured at 600 nm using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Bacterial growth rates were determined based on the data obtained using formula (4). A single MH broth blank culture medium was used as a blank control, while a bacterial culture medium without peptides served as the positive control. After exposure to peptides for 2 h, bacterial samples were diluted to a concentration of 105 CFU/mL, and subsequently meticulously applied, uniformly spread in solid MH broth. The cultures were inverted and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Colony morphology was assessed using a colony-counting instrument (Shineso, SupcreG10, China).
