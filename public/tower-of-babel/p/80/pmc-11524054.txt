Head and neck cancer mortality in the United States: Regional differences in hospice use and place of death
Authors: Melissa C White, Julia E Canick, Tariq M Omer, Justin M Barnes, William T Reed, Rebecca L Rohde, Dina K Abouelella, Eric Adjei Boakye, Katherine Ramos, Russel R Kahmke, Nosayaba Osazuwa-Peters
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith more than 15,000 annual deaths from head and neck cancer (HNC), an important aspect of end-of-life care for these patients is place of death. Recent evidence suggests an increasing preference for home/hospice at end of life; however, it is unclear whether there is variation in home/hospice use based on region or urban status. We described differences in the place of death of HNC patients based on their region and urban status.MethodsUsing the CDC WONDER (Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research) database for HNC mortality (1999 to 2019), place of death was dichotomized as home/hospice vs. other, by Health and Human Services (HHS) region (Regions 1-10), and by urbanization status. Multivariable logistic regression analyses estimated odds of place of death being home/hospice, and being urban/metro, adjusting for both clinical and nonclinical variables.ResultsOver the study period, there were 260,630 deaths, 47.4% of which were at home or hospice. Compared to patients in New England/Region 1 (CT, ME, MA, NH, RI, and VT), HNC patients were more likely to die at home/hospice in the Pacific Northwest/Region 10 (AK, ID, OR, WA) (aOR (1.73; 95% CI: 1.64, 1.83) and less likely in the Eastern Section/Region 2 (NJ, NY, PR, VI) (aOR 0.93; 95% CI: 0.89, 0.97). Further, large central metro areas had significantly lower rates of dying at home/hospice than did all other settings.ConclusionPatients in moderately urban areas were reported to have the greatest use of hospice services and at-home death; patients in the HHS Region representing Alaska, Idaho, Oregon, and Washington were also found to have the highest rates of use. These differences in hospice care should be considered when clinicians counsel patients on their end-of-life options.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) accounts for 4% of all cancers in the United States [1], and is diagnosed in about 66,000 people annually [2]. Despite advancements in diagnostic technique and locoregional control of disease, long-term outcomes for HNC remain complex to manage and are often lethal, with a five-year overall survival rate of 66% [1]. In addition to having a high mortality rate, HNC has a significant negative impact on quality of life [3], compromising patients’ ability to carry out functions fundamental to daily living, including breathing, chewing, swallowing, and speaking [2, 4, 5]. These morbidities negatively affect patient anxiety and distress [6–8], and have been shown to increase the risk of depression and suicide [9–12].

With nearly 15,000 annual deaths from HNC [1], an essential consideration in the treatment of HNC is palliative, hospice, and end-of-life (EOL) care. EOL care encompasses the treatment of symptoms, and improving the quality of life of those with serious illness [13]. It can be provided in the home or in the healthcare setting. In recent years, research describing EOL care of patients with HNC has grown, with the concept of a “good death” rapidly evolving and growing more popular over recent decades [14, 15]. While “successful dying” is a nebulous concept whose definition lacks precision and varies widely among most individuals [16], the larger question of how to help a patient fulfill a dignified death has prompted discussion among doctors and patients alike. One rising theme in EOL research is the preference for location of death [17], with the majority of patients preferring to die at home and surrounded by loved ones if given the option [18] rather than in a healthcare setting such as the hospital. While there is evidence of recent increases in EOL care at home or in hospice [19], this preference for death at home is, unfortunately, not always fulfilled for decedents of HNC [20]; furthermore, female patients, elderly patients, and patients of racial/ethnic minority groups have been found to be less likely to die at home or in hospice [21]. It is unclear whether these differences vary based on whether patients live in urban or rural settings, or whether there are regional differences in the EOL care of patients with HNC. An understanding of the place of death preferences based on factors such as geographical location can help clinicians tailor the options they offer to different patients to support them in their final days.

Previous studies have found that rural communities are the least likely to have access to Medicare-certified hospice services [22], and that inpatient HNC patient palliative care services are underutilized and are often restricted to the elderly, uninsured, and those with high mortality risk [23]. Additionally, Black patients with HNC, especially those living in rural areas, have decreased survival rates [24]. No study, however, has examined how urban-rural and regional differences interact with race in EOL considerations of HNC patients. Whether a patient with end-stage HNC lives in an urban or rural setting may contribute to the disparity between the number of HNC patients who stand to benefit from hospice services and the number of patients who actually utilize them, which has already been shown in patients with hematologic malignancies [25]. This is especially important given that patients in rural communities are less likely to have a Medicare-certified hospice, rendering them unable to access the services available at EOL [22]. Understanding these factors might help optimize the quality of life for these patients in their final days, tailor their EOL care, and offer them the opportunity to die a “good death.” Building on previous work [19, 21, 26], we sought to describe urban-rural and regional differences in EOL care of patients dying from HNC.

Materials and Methods

Data source

The CDC WONDER (Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research) database is a publicly available resource that offers a range of public health information about patients whose deaths were reported by their healthcare providers to the CDC. The Institutional Review Board of Duke University determined that the study did not require additional review as data were deidentified and publicly available.

Cohort selection

The sample space for this study was modeled after that of Stephens et al. [21]. The CDC WONDER Underlying Cause of Death database was queries for people who died of HNC between 1999 and 2019 in the United States; these data were first grouped by place of death, then grouped according to urban vs. rural status, as well as region. Similar to the prior study, the place of death was categorized as home/hospice (search criteria: decedent’s home or hospice facility) vs. other (search criteria: medical facility – inpatient, medical facility – outpatient or ER, medical facility – dead on arrival, medical facility – status unknown, nursing home/long-term care, other, or place of death unknown). These data could then be categorized by state, census region, or Health & Human Services (HHS) region [27]; this study utilized HHS Regions. In addition, the place of death data was sorted by urbanization status, as categorized by the 2013 NCHS Urban-Rural Scheme for Counties (Appendix A); this classification system assigns locations a label of “large central metro,” “large fringe metro,” “medium metro,” “small metro,” “micropolitan (nonmetro),” and “noncore (nonmetro)” [28].

Aggregate patient data were obtained and analyzed from 1999 to 2019 (inclusive). Individual data were also available from the CDC by request, but this did not contain further information on the place of death.

Statistical analysis

Similar to the work of Stephens et al. [21], available patient information was summarized with counts and percentages for all patients, and for patients whose place of death was home or hospice. Sample proportions, differences in proportions, and associated 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed assuming normality. Multivariable logistic regression analyses (MLR) were also performed to further assess the relationship between place of death with HHS Region and urbanization, controlling for demographic factors. Estimated odds ratios (OR) and associated 95% CI were obtained.

Finally, the percentage of HNC patients dying in home/hospice settings across the study period was plotted using the aggregate data; these plots were created for each HHS Region and urbanization setting. The Coefficient of Determination (R2) value from a linear regression of each trend line was also calculated.

Results

Over the 20-year study period, there were 260,630 deaths from HNC, 47.4% of which occurred at home or in hospice. There was considerable variability by HHS Region and urbanization, with the percentage dying at home/hospice ranging from 30.1% to 58.3% (Table 1). Significant differences in the percentage of patients dying at home/hospice by HHS Region were found (Table 2). The lowest rate of patients dying at home/hospice was observed in HHS Region 2 (OR 0.93, 95% CI = 0.89–0.97, p = 0.003; estimate compared to HHS Region 1). The highest rate of patients dying at home/hospice was observed in HHS Region 10 (OR 1.73, 95% CI = 1.64–1.83, p < .001; estimate compared to HHS Region 1). There were also differences in percent dying at home or hospice by urbanization, with large central metropolitan locations having significantly lower rates than all other settings. Nonmetropolitan places, including micropolitan (OR 1.22, 1.18–1.25) and noncore (OR 1.14, 1.10–1.17) regions had generally lower rates of individuals dying at home or hospice than did other metro areas including large fringe metro (OR 1.31, 1.28–1.34), medium metro (OR 1.30, 1.27–1.33), and small metro (OR 1.27, 1.23–1.31) regions. These findings are detailed in Tables 2 and 3. Over the study period, there were increases in the proportion of individuals dying at home across all subgroups (R2 0.299 to 0.796; see Figs. 1a–i and 2a–f).

Discussion

This study found that nearly half of HNC patients utilize hospice or home services for end-of-life care and that this usage has been increasing over the past several years. Enrollment in hospice was observed to increase across all subgroups when data were stratified by HHS Region, though likelihood of hospice usage differed significantly among various regions. In addition, there was substantial variation in places of death based on urban-rural status. Patients in the most urban and most rural locations were less likely to utilize these services, while patients in moderately urban areas were more likely to use them.

Over the study period, 260,630 deaths occurred from HNC, nearly half (47.4%) of which were at home or hospice; these numbers have been steadily increasing since 2000 (Figs. 1 and 2). These increases in the proportion of individuals dying at home were observed across all subgroups (R2 = 0.299 to 0.796; see Figs. 1a–i and 2a–f), mirroring the prevalent literature findings that utilization of hospice services is increasing [29, 30], and that it is not uncommon for patients with a terminal diagnosis to prefer a home death [31]. Hospice care is known to be especially beneficial for HNC patients by reducing invasive medical interventions and healthcare expenditures [32] in patients with highly morbid diseases. These services are especially important for HNC patients, who face unique challenges in their EOL care; such difficulties include airway and upper digestive tract obstruction, which can make breathing and eating painful and demanding [33]. The utilization of hospice services also aids patients in avoiding aggressive treatments that might be futile and costly [34]. The present observation of an increase in hospice use over the years might suggest that more HNC patients and providers are appreciating the benefits of this type of care as the end-of-life approaches.

Urban-rural status was found to have a significant association with home death and hospice use. Interestingly, patients in large central metro locations had rates of home deaths that were significantly lower than those of patients from any other urbanization status. Prior studies have found that patients in urban settings are more likely to utilize home death and hospice services, rather than dying in the hospital [35]; the present findings suggest that, though urban areas see more use of hospice services, this effect might be weaker in urban areas that are highly centralized, though it is unclear at this time why this is. It has long been known that rural hospitals rely more heavily on Medicaid disproportionate-share hospital (DSH) funding than do urban hospitals, and that the urban-rural gap in hospital profitability has grown in recent years [36].

One might expect that central metro area hospitals with established funding have better-equipped facilities, which might make a hospital death more appealing than a home death [37]. However, when comparing micropolitan and noncore areas to metro areas, patients in the most rural locations used hospice and home services less than did patients in more populous places. It is possible that rural patients might not utilize home services and hospice care due to lack of information, the undesirability of travel (for both palliative workers and patients), the challenges of providing rural hospice care, or the scarcity of Medicare-certified hospice services in rural areas [22]. Indeed, rural hospice workers often carry multiple, overlapping roles in aiding patients, and often struggle with the tension of defining and describing their roles in communities whose residents often share years of history and might be resistant to outsiders [38].

This U-shaped distribution of hospice service use and at-home deaths, with patients in moderately rural locations exhibiting the greatest use, has several potential interpretations. One plausible explanation is that rural locations (which might have the least funding) have lower rates of hospice use due to a lack of availability, moderately urban locations might have higher rates of hospice use due to more availability, and central urban locations (which might have the most funding) have hospitals where patients might stay longer, be more comfortable, or pursue more aggressive and curative treatments. Another possibility is that the distribution of tertiary medical centers might influence the treatment options and EOL decision-making for HNC patients. For example, the cancer resources at academic medical centers, usually in large, urban locations, are extensive; these hospitals often house interdisciplinary teams of surgical oncologists, radiation oncologists, medical oncologists, and other specialists who might be primed to offer more aggressive treatment that community doctors might not. Doctors in moderately rural locations might therefore provide hospice services without offering intense treatment modalities.

Further, location as defined by the United States Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Region was associated with variation in home deaths and hospice care. More specifically, the lowest rate of patients dying at home/hospice was observed in HHS Region 2 (OR 0.93, 95% CI = 0.89–0.97, p = 0.003; estimate compared to HHS Region 1); this region contains New York, New Jersey, and the U.S. territories in the Caribbean. The geographic composition of this region poses several challenges in analysis--namely that it is composed of two densely populated mainland states as well as territories in the English-speaking Caribbean. It has been reported that healthcare systems in the Caribbean, and in other middle-income nations, do not prioritize or make available hospice, palliation, and pain relief services [39]. Inclusion of this area in HHS Region 2 might account for some of this finding. Further, the highest rate of patients dying at home/hospice was observed in HHS Region 10 (OR 1.73, 95% CI = 1.64–1.83, p < 0.001; estimate compared to HHS Region 1); this region includes Alaska, Idaho, Oregon, and Washington—states that are known to have large rural populations. This finding is surprising, given the aforementioned challenges that are specific to providing hospice services in rural areas, including staffing shortages and low funding [40]. However, in addition to these areas, this HHS Region also has densely populated cities, including Portland and Seattle. Urban-rural status cannot wholly account for the increased hospice use observed in these HHS Regions, though it may be one contributing factor. Further investigation involving sub-stratification of these pre-defined regions is warranted to further elucidate whether HNC patient enrollment in hospice is associated with specific regional distinctions separate from urban-rural status. Future research might also disentangle the differences between preferences for at-home death, often considered to be more comfortable, as compared to death in a hospice facility, the latter of which might be perceived as “waiting to die”.

This study has several limitations. First, regional analysis was based on HHS Region assignment, which often groups areas that are heterogeneously composed and geographically separated (e.g., Region 2 has New York, New Jersey, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands). Given that these data were reported specifically at this regional level, we were unable to make further observations based on subdivisions of these regions or re-group the areas included. Further, we did not analyze the rate of change in hospice and at-home death utilization among different areas; future investigations might explore this variable, so as to identify locations that are more rapidly increasing in the use of these services. Additionally, we were unable to stratify the data by insurance status, prohibiting us from making conclusions on how insurance can impact hospice use. Finally, as noted above, deaths involving home care and those in hospice facilities were analyzed as one subgroup; this might have prevented more nuanced findings about the regional differences between the preferences for these two settings.

Despite these limitations, this study has significant implications. There is an urban-rural disparity in the use of hospice care, with patients in the most rural areas utilizing these services less often. However, patients in the very most urban locations use this service the least of any group, illustrating a bimodal pattern of utilization. Our findings suggest that more resources must be put toward making hospice and home resources available for all. Less than half of our cohort died at home or in hospice, which is not congruent with previous research that shows that most patients would like to die at home. While urban-rural status should be considered, it is unclear at this time how it mediates the relationship between hospice use and place of death. Future studies might correlate hospital funding to hospice use and location specifically, in order to assess whether these three qualities are linked.

Conclusion

In general, the proportion of HNC patients dying at home or in hospice is increasing, even when stratified by HHS Region. Patients in the most and least rural areas had significantly lower rates of home death and hospice use than did patients in moderately urban areas. HHS Region was also significantly associated with differences in place of death. Future studies should consider the importance of urban-rural status in the consideration of the place of death preferences and also might continue to sub-stratify regions to pinpoint more specific locational factors that affect rates of home at hospice deaths. This additional work may inform future policies that address the differences between hospice use in urban and rural areas.
