Letter regarding amide proton transfer imaging reveals cerebral metabolic alterations associated with cognitive impairment in type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Binghan Guo, Xiaotao Zhang, Zian Wang, Yu Song
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We are writing to commend the recent article by Jiang et al., titled “Amide proton transfer imaging reveals cerebral metabolic alterations associated with cognitive impairment in type 2 diabetes mellitus [1]”, published in the Journal of Translational Medicine. We employ amide proton transfer imaging to study cerebral metabolic changes in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). We found that reduced APT signal in the temporal lobe is associated with poorer cognitive performance and imaging markers of CSVD. We provide a noninvasive imaging biomarker for early detection of diabetic cognitive decline and help us understand metabolic mechanisms of T2DM brain injury.

The main strength of this paper is translational, linking advanced MRI technology to clinical neuropsychological assessment. Finding that APT values in left-timbre white matter and right-timbre gray matter are significantly lower in T2DM-MCI patients and correlate with specific cognitive domain scores such as attention and recall, and the integration with AI-quantified CSVD markers offers potential for early and objective diagnosis.

This work is interesting and methodologically robust, but needs further work in order to maximize its impact and inform future research. First, even though cross-sectional design finds a good correlation, it does not establish causality or detail the evolution of APT changes. Longitudinal studies of APT and cognitive changes in T2DM patients would be useful to determine whether baseline APT predicts the transition from normal cognition to MCI or dementia [2].

Second, while the authors are interested in whether reduced APT is related to protein homeostasis and microenvironment, direct pathological effects of altered APT signal in diabetic brain are still unknown. Future studies integrating APT imaging with multimodal assessments (e.g. PET imaging for amyloid/tau, MR imaging for neurochemical profiles, or histopathological correlations, when feasible [3]) would help clarify whether changes show mainly glycemic protein modification, neuroinflammation, vasculopathy, or combination of these.

The clinical use of APT imaging is worth exploring more deeply. While promising, it is important to evaluate its diagnostic value in comparison with other MRI measures (such as structural volumetry and diffusion tensor imaging) [4]. Cost-effectiveness studies and reproducibility studies of APT measurements across scanner platforms and sites are essential steps to incorporate APT imaging into routine practice or large-scale cohort studies.

We thank the authors for their work and the Journal of Translational Medicine for the publication of this very important neuroimaging study. We expect future studies to extend these findings to explore the metabolic dynamics of the diabetic brain and to develop APT imaging as a reliable tool for personalized risk assessment and treatment in T2DM.

Sincerely.
