Health-Related Quality of Life in Survivors of Stroke Post Inpatient Rehabilitation Discharged to a Home Setting
Authors: Siqi Xiang, Bradley Smith, Margaret A French, Heather A Hayes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine if personal factors, stroke severity, social determinants of health, and functional status are associated with health-related quality of life (HRQOL) in individuals poststroke who have completed their inpatient rehabilitation facility (IRF) stay and have returned home. We hypothesized that functional status would explain a significant portion of variability in HRQOL after accounting for covariates, including personal factors, stroke severity, and metrics of social determinants of health.DesignThis study was a secondary analysis of data from a prospective, longitudinal cohort study.SettingThe IRF to the community.ParticipantsIndividuals (N=72) poststroke (mean age ± SD, 64.1±14.4y, 58.3% men) who returned home after completing rehabilitation in an IRF.InterventionsNot applicable.Main Outcome MeasuresThe HRQOL in Stroke Patients-26 (HRQOLISP-26) was captured after individuals were discharged to the home setting.ResultsFunctional status explained an additional 40% of the variance in HRQOLISP-26 after controlling for personal factors, stroke severity, and metrics of social determinants of health (R2=0.63, ΔR2=0.40, F=6.21, p<.001). The specific functional measures that were significantly associated with HRQOLISP-26 were basic mobility (β=0.38, p=.01), applied cognition (β=0.56, p<.001), and depression (β=−4.58, p=.03).ConclusionsWe found that functional status, including mobility, cognition, and depression, is implicated in affecting HRQOL in individuals with subacute stroke who have completed their IRF stay and have returned home. Thus, underlying the importance of rehabilitation is the consideration of not only the progression of physical and cognitive function but also mood.KEYWORDS: Health-related quality of life, Inpatient rehabilitation, Rehabilitation, Stroke, Subacute care

Stroke is the main cause of long-term disability, with many individuals experiencing persistent impairment in motor function, cognition, speech and language, and emotional function (eg, depression,anxiety).1,2 These deficits negatively affect the ability of individuals to carry out daily activities, maintain social relations, and engage in meaningful occupations,1 contributing to a significant decline in health-related quality of life (HRQOL) after stroke.3 HRQOL measures the effect of health status on quality of life and is comprised of several unique dimensions, including physical, emotional, pain/discomfort, self-care, and social well-being.4 Individuals poststroke experience a significant decline in their HRQOL compared with prestroke levels.4 The European Quality of Life 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) index is a standardized tool for assessing HRQOL. It generates a utility score based on 5 dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Scores typically range from below 0 (worse than death) to 1 (full health).4 Among survivors of stroke, mean EQ-5D scores have been reported as 0.61 at 3 months poststroke and 0.76 at 12 months poststroke compared with a normative population value of 0.94.4 While the EQ-5D 5-Level Scale (EQ-5D-5L) is widely used, it may not fully capture stroke-specific issues. Therefore, this study employed the HRQOL in Stroke Patients-26 (HRQOLISP-26), a validated multidimensional measure developed for stroke populations and not previously applied in the United States inpatient rehabilitation settings.5

Most individuals poststroke receive postacute rehabilitation care in the United States, with a goal of promoting functional recovery, managing medical conditions, and improving independence.6 Up to 25% of individuals poststroke will receive postacute rehabilitation in an inpatient rehabilitation facility (IRF).6 The IRFs provide intensive multidisciplinary care to improve physical function and independence, with a primary goal of helping individuals return to the community.7, 8, 9 We know little about how this care translates to HRQOL once patients return home, as past work has primarily focused on functional outcomes as opposed to HRQOL. Given the high costs associated with IRFs,10 this knowledge gap is concerning and creates a need to understand the HRQOL after discharge from an IRF.

Additionally, understanding the factors that influence HRQOL after discharge can help rehabilitation professionals optimize care strategies to improve patient outcomes and resource utilization. While personal factors (eg, age, sex) and functional status (eg, physical function, depression) seem to be associated with HRQOL in individuals with chronic stroke, the effect of these factors on HRQOL in the subacute phase remains unclear.

Our purpose was to examine the relationship of personal factors, stroke severity, social determinants of health (SDOH), and functional status with HRQOL in individuals poststroke who are discharged from an IRF to the home setting. We hypothesized that after discharge home from an IRF, functional status would explain a significant portion of variability in HRQOL after accounting for personal factors, stroke severity, and SDOH.

Methods

Participants

This work was a secondary analysis of a larger, prospective, longitudinal cohort study performed at the University of Utah. In the primary study, we included adults (aged >18y) who were admitted to the hospital for an acute ischemic stroke (International Classification of Diseases–9th Revision: 433-434 and 436; International Classification of Diseases–10th Revision: I63.0-I63.9) and had a score on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Individuals were enrolled during their acute care hospital stay. For individuals agreeing to participate, they signed a consent form approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Utah (IRB_00114762). In this secondary analysis, participants were recruited via convenience sampling from individuals enrolled between January 2020 and March 2024 and discharged from the acute care hospital to an IRF and then to a home setting. We excluded individuals if they (1) were readmitted to the acute care hospital at any time during the IRF stay, (2) had an IRF length of stay (LoS) ≥49 days, (3) if the primary outcome measure (HRQOL) was collected outside of the desired time window (7-60d after discharge from the IRF), representing individuals who had been in the home 7-60 days, or (4) were missing key independent variables (ie, education, household income). Seventy-two individuals met all inclusion criteria and comprised this study sample.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome, HRQOLISP-26, was assessed between 7 and 60 days postdischarge to capture the early subacute phase of stroke recovery, typically defined as 7-90 days poststroke.11 This range was selected to reflect a period of rapid functional improvement and the early challenges that individuals may experience when first returning home.11 We administered the HRQOLISP-26 via telephone. Patients or their designated alternative contact, who could answer on the patient’s behalf, were contacted to complete the questionnaire. Each call lasted approximately 20-30 minutes. HRQOLISP-26 assesses overall HRQOL and specific domains of HRQOL that are particularly relevant to individuals poststroke and has good internal consistency and test–retest reliability.5 The questionnaire includes questions related to the physical, psycho-emotional, cognitive, and ecological-social domains of HRQOL.12 To calculate the total score, the score for each domain is transformed to a scale of 0-100, and then those transformed scores are averaged. The maximum score is 100, with higher scores indicating better HRQOL.

Independent variables

We grouped our independent variables into 4 categories: (1) personal factors, (2) stroke severity, (3) SDOH, and (4) functional status. Personal factors included age, sex, and race. These data were captured through chart review at the time of enrollment in the study. We chose variables in these categories because they are associated with the prevalence, progression, and management of different health-related conditions.13

Stroke severity was assessed via chart review and included LoS in the IRF and the admission NIHSS score obtained during the acute hospitalization. A longer LoS often correlates with more severe stroke or more rehabilitation needs.14 The NIHSS is used to measure stroke severity by assessing 15 items, each scored on a 3- or 4-point ordinal scale. The total score ranges from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating more severe stroke symptoms.15 The IRF LoS was captured through a retrospective chart review after discharge from the IRF.

The SDOH metrics that we included were education level, household income, and assistance at home. Educational level was included because it influences a range of health outcomes and behaviors.16 We categorized education into 3 categories: “High School,” “Some College and Associate’s Degree,” and “Bachelor’s and Above.” We included income because it is a key indicator of socioeconomic status, which is known to affect a wide range of health outcomes and life opportunities.17 Family income was categorized as low (<$20,000), middle ($20,000-$75,000), and high (>$75,000), following a stratified approach adapted from prior epidemiologic research to examine socioeconomic variation.18 The variable “home assistance” reflects prestroke living arrangements, categorized as living alone or with family/others, based on the Continuity Assessment Record and Evaluation (CARE) Tool criteria.19 Although it does not capture formal in-home services, it represents a structural aspect of social support increasingly linked to poststroke outcomes. Living alone has been associated with a higher risk of accident and emergency attendance and hospital readmission, underscoring the value of assessing home support during discharge planning.20, 21, 22 All SDOH measures were self- or proxy-reported at enrollment, with participants permitted to withhold information.

Our functional status metrics included measures of mobility, activities of daily living, and cognitive function, as measured by the Activity Measure for Postacute Care (AM-PAC). Within each of these functional domains, individuals were asked to report how much difficulty they have doing specific tasks related to that domain. For example, in the basic mobility domain, individuals were asked about tasks such as getting out of bed. In the daily activity domain, they were asked about tasks such as putting on and taking off clothing. In the applied cognition domain, they were asked about cognitive functions, including comprehension and memory.23 The Computerized Adaptive Test (CAT) was developed for use in both acute and subacute care to measure these 3 domains. The CAT version of the AM-PAC customizes item selection based on a patient’s functional abilities and has good validity.24 The AM-PAC CAT results in a T-score for each domain, which provides a standardized metric for assessing functional abilities within that domain. The AM-PAC T-scores range from 0 to 100, with norm-based scores standardized to a mean of 50 and an SD of 10.25 We also included the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and the “Anxiety/Depression” subcomponent of the EQ-5D-5L as additional metrics of functional status.26,27 Although the latter reflects emotional well-being, it was conceptualized as part of health-related functioning because of its relevance to daily life and perceived quality of life.28 The mRS identifies activity limitations or difficulties in the performance of activities poststroke and ranges from 0 to 6 (independent of death).26 We categorized the mRS into 2 groups: (1) “favorable outcome,” defined as an mRS score<2, and (2) “unfavorable outcome” for mRS scores>2.29 We measured anxiety and depression with the “Anxiety/Depression” subcomponent of the EQ-5D-5L. Previous research has demonstrated that the “Anxiety/Depression” subcomponent of the EQ-5D-5L can be reported and analyzed independently.28,30 In this subcomponent, participants rate their level of anxiety or depression using a 5-point Likert scale (1: “I am not anxious or depressed” to 5: “I am extremely anxious or depressed”). In this study, we categorized “Anxiety/Depression” into 2 groups: (1) “no symptoms of anxiety/depression,” defined by a score of 1, and (2) “symptoms of anxiety/depression,” defined as a score of 2-5.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included frequency distributions and percentages for categorical variables and central tendency (mean ± SD) for continuous variables in the secondary analysis of this cohort.

To test our hypothesis, we performed a hierarchical regression model to test the relationship between HRQOL and personal factors, stroke severity, SDOH, and functional status. The model included 13 predictors that were entered in 4 sequential blocks. The first block included personal characteristics; the second block included metrics of stroke severity; the third block included metrics of SDOH; the fourth block included functional status. Prior to conducting the analysis, several key assumptions were assessed, including linearity between the predictor variables and the outcome variable, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals. We assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factor values.31 We present the R2 of the entire model, a change in R2 (ΔR2) with the addition of a new block, and the p value for the model R2 and ΔR2. R2 and its p value reflect whether the model, with all of the entered blocks, is significant; ΔR2 and its p value represent whether the newly added block explains a significant portion of variability after accounting for the previously entered blocks. We also present the standardized coefficients of the model with all the blocks entered. Significance was set at p<.05. Analysis was performed using Stata 18 software.32,a

A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1b (f2=0.25, α=0.05, 13 predictors) for a multiple linear regression model. The achieved power was 0.71, indicating moderate power to detect medium to large effects.33

Results

There were 194 individuals discharged from acute care to an IRF. After applying exclusion criteria, 72 individuals were included in this secondary analysis. The most common reasons for exclusion were discharge to settings other than the home, data were not collected within the designated timeframe, and extended LoS in the IRF (fig 1). Personal factors, stroke severity, SDOH, and functional status of the cohort are shown in table 1. Briefly, we included individuals with a mean age ± SD of 64.10±14.40 years, 58% whom were men, and those who had an average LoS in the IRF of 15.8±8.2 days. The HRQOLISP-26 was ultimately collected at a mean of 23.14 days (SD, 10.12) post-IRF discharge, ranging from 8 to 53 days.

The results of the hierarchical linear regression are shown in table 2. The first block, ie, personal factors, explained a small and nonsignificant portion of variability in HRQOLISP-26 (R2=0.02, p=.67). The addition of the second block, ie, indicators of stroke severity, explained a significant portion of variability in HRQOL after accounting for personal characteristics (ΔR2=0.17, p=.001) and resulted in a significant model (R2=0.19, p=.01). This indicates that metrics of stroke severity explained an additional 17% of the variance after accounting for personal characteristics. The addition of metrics of SDOH in block 3 explained an additional 4% of the variability in HRQOLISP-26, which was not significant (ΔR2=0.04, p=.7). The addition of functional status in the fourth block explained an additional 40% of the variability in HRQOL after accounting for personal factors, indicators of stroke severity, and metrics of SDOH (ΔR2=0.40, p≤.001). The final model, which included all 4 blocks, explained 63% of the variability in HRQOL and was significant (R2=0.63, p<.001).

Discussion

This study investigated the effect of different factors on HRQOL, as measured by HRQOLISP-26, in individuals with stroke discharged home from an IRF. Consistent with our initial hypotheses, functional status explained a significant portion of variability in HRQOL above and beyond the variability that was explained by personal factors, stroke severity, and SDOH. Additionally, we found that symptoms of anxiety/depression significantly contributed to the variability in HRQOL.

HRQOLISP-26

The HRQOL is often measured using instruments such as the EQ-5D and Short Form-36 Health Survey, which are not stroke-specific and may overlook poststroke deficits, including cognitive, communicative, and endurance impairments. This study used the HRQOLISP-26, a validated stroke-specific measure covering physical, psychological, and social/spiritual domains, not previously used in US studies.5

Personal factors and SDOH

In this study, neither personal factors (age, sex, race) nor the selected metrics of SDOH (education level, household income, home assistance) were significantly associated with HRQOL among individuals discharged home poststroke from an IRF. While our SDOH metrics were informed by prior literature, they may not fully capture the complexity or adequacy of social and economic support. Our findings differ from prior research that has linked lower HRQOL to aging, living arrangements, sex, and racial disparities.34, 35, 36, 37, 38 Our smaller, less diverse sample (N=72; 86.1% White) limited stratified analyses. Prior research indicates poorer functional outcomes among Black survivors of stroke39 and lower poststroke HRQOL among Mexican Americans than non-Hispanic Whites.40 These differences are likely because of our focus on the early discharge period from an IRF, whereas previous studies typically examined longer-term outcomes (1 and 5 years poststroke), and the lack of racial diversity in our sample, given the demographics of the region.41 The SDOH metrics selected in this study did not appear to be related to HRQOL. However, future research should examine a broader set of SDOH domains, as additional forms of heterogeneity may influence poststroke outcomes.

Stroke severity

Factors of stroke severity (LoS in an IRF, NIHSS at acute admission) explained a significant portion of variability in HRQOL after accounting for the first block of personal factors. However, neither variable showed a significant individual association, suggesting their contribution may reflect a combined rather than an independent effect. Stroke severity is a key determinant of HRQOL; however, the NIHSS primarily reflects acute neurologic status and may not adequately capture functional outcomes at the subacute stage assessed. Moreover, the total NIHSS score may mask the effect of specific deficits, such as aphasia, visual field loss, or neglect, which can disproportionately affect HRQOL. Domain-level analysis of the NIHSS may yield more detailed insights into these associations in future research.42 Additionally, LoS in an IRF may reflect the potential severity of a stroke, but LoS can also be influenced by hospital policies and the health care system. Thus, LoS may not be relevant to HRQOL after an individual is discharged home.

Functional status

The findings indicated that basic mobility, applied cognition, and depression/anxiety were significant contributors to HRQOL during early poststroke recovery, whereas daily activity and mRS were not. This contrasts with studies identifying the Barthel Index and mRS as key predictors,43,44 but aligns with evidence emphasizing mobility and cognition as central to functional independence.45,46 Given that depression affects approximately 25% of survivors of stroke within 1-5 years, its association with reduced HRQOL further reinforces the need to incorporate mental health screening and targeted interventions into rehabilitation.47 Although rehabilitation focuses on physical recovery, psychological effects such as depression, anxiety, irritability, and apathy are common and often underrecognized.48

Study limitations

This study used a convenience sample from a single university IRF, limiting generalizability. No a priori power analysis was conducted; a post hoc analysis indicated 71% power to detect medium to large effects, suggesting the study may be underpowered for smaller differences. Thus, nonsignificant findings should be interpreted with caution. While key factors influencing HRQOL were assessed, comorbidities,49 sleep,50 and fatigue51 were not. The early postdischarge focus provides short-term insight but lacks long-term follow-up. The sample was modest and predominantly White (86.1%), limiting subgroup analysis. Broader SDOH, including access to care, transportation, and employment, were not assessed. Although AM-PAC domains were assessed, the absence of functional threshold reporting limits clinical interpretability.

Conclusions

This study suggests that functional status related to impaired mobility, cognition, and anxiety and depression was most associated with worse HRQOL in individuals with subacute stroke who have completed their rehabilitation in an IRF and have returned to the home setting. The primary goal of an IRF is to relearn skills and regain independence; however, after a stroke, mobility and cognitive concerns may still be impaired, making the return to a home environment challenging. Addressing both physical and psychological needs early in rehabilitation may improve postdischarge outcomes. Future research should examine additional factors and identify targeted interventions to support reintegration into the home environment.
