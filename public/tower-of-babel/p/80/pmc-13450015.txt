Total pancreatectomy friend or foe?
Authors: Emmanuel Devant, Mircea Chirica
Published: 2026

The initiative to define reference values for a rare and heterogeneous procedure such as total pancreatectomy (TP) is, in theory, relevant. However, the quality of a benchmark depends less on cohort size than on the appropriateness of indication stratification, timing of decision-making, and technical choices. On these key points, Müller et al.’s (1) study, based on a multicenter cohort of 994 patients operated on between 2017 and 2023 in 25 expert centers, presents several limitations that merit careful consideration.

Some methodological considerations arise from comparisons that are not directly supported by the available data. The authors compare survival after TP to published benchmarks for pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD), despite the absence of a PD cohort in the study and without adjustment for key confounding factors such as surgical indication, tumor stage, or patient selection. Once comparisons extend across different procedures, this no longer qualifies as strict benchmarking, which requires comparable procedures and risk profiles. Comparing distinct “best-case scenarios” is informative but ultimately reflects a contextual comparison rather than true benchmarking and may induce substantial selection bias. Repeated direct or implicit comparisons between TP and PD outcomes are conceptually debatable, as the two procedures follow different surgical logics. This may help explain why the present findings appear discordant with a recent meta-analysis reporting comparable postoperative mortality (~6%) between TP and PD (2).

Additionally, the authors discuss endocrine outcomes of TP in a way that suggests comparison with PD, although the cohort does not include PD patients with prospectively collected endocrine data. In the absence of a contemporaneous comparison group, any assessment of the relative severity of postoperative diabetes remains indirect and should be interpreted with caution. Recent literature suggests that metabolic outcomes after TP can be better managed today thanks to modern glycemic monitoring strategies and tailored insulin regimens—for instance, contemporary cohorts report HbA1c levels and insulin requirements comparable to those in insulin-dependent diabetes, sometimes with lower than expected insulin doses (3). Systematic reviews on functional outcomes after TP also indicate no diabetes-related mortality in the modern era, and endocrine morbidity remains manageable, although requiring specialized care (4). Therefore, extrapolating endocrine comparisons without PD data, and without fully accounting for recent advances in post-resection diabetes management, may extend beyond what the presented data can robustly support and should be interpreted with caution when considering TP’s relative position to PD in contemporary practice.

A substantial portion of the discussion aims to argue that TP performed to avoid a high-risk pancreatic anastomosis is dangerous and should not be performed, despite this subgroup representing only a minor fraction of the cohort. This view contrasts with the relatively high frequency of intraoperative totalization decisions reported (408 patients, 51% of the series), which may warrant further clarification in the context of expert centers. Among these patients, TP was performed for oncological reasons in 307 cases and prophylactically to avoid a high-risk pancreatic anastomosis in 46 cases, while no clear indication was provided for 55 patients, which may limit the interpretability of the data with respect to the stated goal of defining reliable reference values. This high proportion of intraoperative decisions may suggest areas for improvement in preoperative planning, as the possibility of TP (oncological or prophylactic) would ideally be anticipated in specialized centers. Intraoperative decisions expose surgeons to constrained technical choices in suboptimal conditions, clearly increasing morbidity and mortality. This decision-making drift is directly reflected in the reported outcomes. The 11% mortality observed in patients totalized to avoid a high-risk anastomosis is high and contradicts contemporary high-volume center literature (2,5). Prophylactic TP, when considered in carefully selected patients, can be performed with very low, or even zero, mortality and represents a viable alternative option to PD (5-7). The authors identify gastric venous congestion as a major contributor to morbidity after TP, although the practical implications are not fully elaborated. This complication is often considered predictable and potentially preventable with appropriate technical anticipation, including preoperative assessment of the left gastric vein, vein-preserving strategies, or pylorus-preserving resections (7). These technical aspects are not discussed in detail, although they may contribute to a proportion of the observed mortality. Overall, some of the reported complications may be related at least in part to limitations in anticipation rather than solely to the intrinsic risk of TP.

Methodologically, the groups defined by the authors (LR-TP, HR-TP, TP for high-risk anastomosis, TP with vascular resection) are not independent, as patients can belong simultaneously to multiple categories. This structuring renders any direct comparison fundamentally biased. Although the authors refrained from inappropriate statistical tests, the side-by-side presentation of results induces misleading comparative reasoning; for example, claiming that mortality in TP for high-risk anastomosis or with vascular resection is almost double that of LR-TP is scientifically unjustified.

Conclusions

Despite its multicenter and international nature, allowing for a large cohort, Müller et al.’s study (1) has several important methodological limitations. The reported morbidity and mortality rates may reflect, at least in part, late intraoperative decision-making and limited preoperative anticipation rather than solely the intrinsic risk of TP. Published in a high-impact journal, these findings should therefore be interpreted in context to avoid overly generalizable conclusions that could influence clinical decision-making, particularly regarding the role of planned, technically optimized TP in expert centers.
