Sequence patterns in RNA replication via the virtual circular genome mechanism
Authors: Paul G Higgs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe ask whether functional RNA sequences could be stably copied nonenzymatically at the time of the origin of life. We show that random sequence mixtures spontaneously reduce to short overlapping sequence sets that have been called virtual circular genomes (VCGs). VCG patterns can be classified as irreducible sets of words. Short VCG sequences replicate stably if they cannot reduce to shorter cycles, but longer VCG sequences tend to collapse to shorter sequence patterns, a problem we call scrambling. The VCG mechanism works for relatively short sequences when the error rate is small. Using stochastic computer simulations, we demonstrate successful replication of sequences with a potentially useful function, including a hairpin capable of loop-closing ligation, and an autocatalytic ligase of length 20. However, a hammerhead ribozyme sequence of length 44 is found to be barely stable even for optimistic parameter choices, suggesting that this is an upper limit to the length that could be encoded on a VCG. The recent discovery of short polymerase ribozymes of only length 45 gives hope that nonenzymatic replication might begin to approach what is required for origin of a polymerase.

Introduction

At the time of the origin of life it is envisaged that RNA sequences could replicate nonenzymatically without a polymerase ribozyme or protein. Primer extension reactions have been studied in the laboratory [1–8] and have been shown to work inside lipid vesicles [9–12]. We have also shown [13] that primer extension reactions in a protocell satisfy the requirements for being a metabolism by themselves—i.e. an autocatalytic reaction is maintained in a nonequilibrium state inside the cell, but the reaction does not initiate spontaneously outside—without requiring an additional (unknown) autocatalytic cycle to function as the metabolism.

RNA World scenarios propose that polymerase ribozymes catalyzed RNA replication [14–17]. In the laboratory, polymerases have been synthesized by in vitro selection [18–24]. The first polymerase would require a second complementary strand to be a template. It is difficult to see how this could arise in a mixture of sequences synthesized only by random polymerization, whereas if nonenzymatic replication was occurring, the polymerase and its complement would naturally arise together in multiple copies. We therefore ask whether functional ribozymes could emerge in an oligomer mixture undergoing nonenzymatic templating.

It has been shown that ribozymes can be synthesized from shorter fragments by ligation [25, 26], and that ligases [27] and recombinases [28] can assemble autocatalytically from their own fragments. These experiments demonstrate the assembly of fragments into longer sequences but do not show replication of the fragments. For these assembly processes to work prebiotically, there would also need to be nonenzymatic replication of the fragments.

The idea that oligomers encoding a ribozyme might be copied nonenzymatically has been proposed under the name of the virtual circular genome (VCG) [29]. A mixture of oligomers is envisaged containing overlapping linear subsequences taken from a circular sequence and its complement. In this case all sequences would all have a template on which to grow. We tested this idea by computer simulations [30] and found that it is difficult to maintain VCGs of sufficient length to encode ribozymes due to a problem we call scrambling. The desired sequence usually contains short repeated words of a few bases. Sustained replication relies on cycles of melting and reannealing double strands. When there are short repeated words, fragments can reanneal in more than one way. This allows creation of sequences not contained in the desired VCG sequence, and leads to the loss of sequence information. Whether a VCG sequence can be maintained without scrambling depends on the length of the sequence, the length of repeated words, and the reaction rates in the templating process. This paper aims to determine the conditions in which VCG sequences are stable.

The error-threshold problem [31] is the loss of sequence information due to accumulation of copying errors. The error-threshold theory assumes that a new sequence is a copy of a single template. However, in the VCG mechanism there is continual separation and reannealing. A sequence is not copied from a single template, but is copied a few bases at a time from each of the templates that it is temporarily paired with. Scrambling occurs when sequences reanneal in incorrect positions. This happens even when there is no copying error. Initially we need to determine which sequence patterns are stable in the limit of zero error. When the error rate is nonzero, errors also create words that are not present in the VCG sequence. For a sequence that is stable at zero error rate, there is an error threshold i.e. an error rate above which the sequence information is lost. The simulations in this paper build on those used in our previous papers [30, 13]. Simulations of nonenzymatic replication have also been carried out by other authors [32–35].

Materials and methods

Generators and irreducible sets

We first consider sequences with only two possible bases. We call these A and U, but the patterns are equivalent for C and G. The simplest patterns with A and U are poly-A plus poly-U, and the pattern consisting of AU repeats. These two patterns frequently arise in previous simulations [13, 32]. These patterns are stable because if only sequences of this type are present and if there are no mismatch errors, then no sequences other than these can be synthesized. In the poly-A plus poly-U mixture, a poly-A sequence must pair with a poly-U template, so it can only be extended by a further A. Similarly, for alternating AU sequences, each sequence can only be extended in the same alternating pattern.

We now generalize this idea to produce a classification of all possible patterns in terms of irreducible sets (ISs). Sequences belonging to an IS allow continued synthesis of sequences in the set but do not create sequences outside the set when there are no sequence errors. We write sequence patterns as a base sequence in square brackets that we call a generator, such as [AAUU]. The brackets mean repeat this sequence multiple times and take subsequences of all lengths from within the long sequence. The generator [AAUU] creates a mixture that includes AU, AAU, UUAA, AAUUAAU, etc. This generator is self-complementary i.e. sequences from this generator are templates for one another. If a generator is not-self complementary, we require a pair of mutually complementary generators, such as [AAU]+[UAA].

For any generator we can construct a graph where the nodes are words of length n that occur in the sequence, and the links are words of length n + 1 representing two consecutive n-mer words. A graph is an IS if no word can be removed from it without preventing replication of the whole set and if it contains no smaller IS within it. The level of an IS is the minimum word length required to make the graph irreducible. The [A]+[U] pattern and the [AU] pattern are level 1 and can be drawn as graphs of 1 letter words (Fig. 1A and B). The [AAUU] pattern is level 2 (Fig. 1C). The pattern [AAU]+[AUU] is level 3 (Fig. 1D). Figure 1A–D are cycles where each word has only one input link and one output link. We call an IS that is a cycle an irreducible cycle (IC). If the [AAU]+[AUU] pattern is drawn with two-letter words, as in Fig. 1E, it is not a cycle because some words have two input or two output links, and it is not irreducible because it contains the smaller cycle [AU] within it. Therefore this pattern is level 3 not level 2.

The cyclic word graphs in Fig. 1 are known as De Bruijn graphs in mathematical graph theory [36]. They have been used in molecular biology for assembling genomes from overlapping sequence reads [37]. The noncyclic graphs in Fig. 1H and I are extensions of De Bruijn graphs that we propose here. These are also examples of connected components in directed graphs.

Simulation methods

We simulate replication of sequences in a compartment representing either a protocell or a rock pore in which strands are trapped by thermophoresis [38, 39]. The program stores the base sequence of each strand and uses the Gillespie algorithm [40] to follow reactions between strands. The simulation proceeds in cycles of growth and separation. The length of time of the growth and separation phases are each set to 1 (arbitrary time units). During the growth phase, the allowed reactions are entry and leaving of an activated nucleotide from the cell, formation and separation of double strands, primer extention reactions which extend a paired sequence by one base, and nucleation events in which a new trimer is initiated by pairing monomers with an existing strand. At the end of the growth phase all double strands are immediately separated. It is assumed that double helix formation cannot occur in the physical conditions of the separation phase. During the separation phase, the allowed reactions are entry and leaving of single nucleotides, breakdown of strands to shorter strands and single unactivated nucleotides, and dilution (i.e. random loss of strands from the mixture).

When this occurs, a random nucleotide is selected. If this matches the template, the extension reaction occurs. If it does not match the template, the extension reaction is still pernitted with a probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\varepsilon $\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\varepsilon $\end{document} is the mismatch error rate. If an incorrect base is added, the the matching length does not increase. The last base then no-longer matches the template, and we assume that no further growth steps are possible (i.e. there is complete stalling after the first mismatch).

Nucleation of new trimers can occur when monomers interact with an unpaired strand, as in Fig. 2(iii). This occurs at rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{\mathrm{ nuc}}}{{L}_{\mathrm{ un}}}N_{\mathrm{ nuc}}^3/{{V}^4}$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{L}_{\mathrm{ un}}}$\end{document} is the total length of unpaired strands. If this occurs, a single strand is chosen with probability proportional to its length. A window of 3 is chosen within the strand and three matching nucleotides are paired with this strand to initiate a new trimer.

During the breakdown phase all strands are unpaired. Double strand formation and primer extension cannot occur. Entry and leaving of nucleotides occurs as in the growth phase. Strand breakdown occurs at rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $b$\end{document} per bond, as in Fig. 2(iv). Strands break at a random position along their length, creating two shorter strands. If either of the the shorter strands is a single base, this is treated as an unactivated base which cannot cause primer extension. It is assumed to exit the cell, and is thrown out of the simulation. If either of the shorter strands is a dimer, then it cannot grow again because we assumed a minimum pairing length of 3 for helix formation. Therefore any dimers are immediately thrown out of the simulation. If the strands formed by breaking are at least length 3 they are retained and we assume that both can grow during the next growth phase. In fact, breakage of a 5′–3′ bond in RNA forms a cyclic 2′–3′ phosphate on the end of the upstream fragment, and this could potentially prevent subsequent primer extension reactions on this end. We ignored this point, and assumed that the cyclic phosphate will decay fairly rapidly so that subsequent regrowth of this fragment is possible.

By ‘dilution’ we mean the escape of a random strand from the reaction mixture. This occurs at rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{\mathrm{ dil}}}$\end{document} per strand during the separation phase. This would apply if the compartment represents a pore in a rock. If the compartment represents a protocell then loss of strands should be impossible if oligomers cannot pass through the membrane. However, in a growing protocell dilution of concentration will occur as the cell grows, and division will halve the number of strands. The outcome will be similar to the case where strands are lost one at a time.

We therefore consider simulations in two regimes—a breakdown-limited regime B in which strand length and number are limited by strand breakdown only and there is no dilution, and and dilution-limited regime D in which breakdown rate is slower and the length and number of strands in the mixture are principally limited by dilution. Standard parameters for the B conditions are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{\mathrm{ ext}}} = 100,\ {{r}_{\mathrm{ on}}} = 1000,\ \mu = 0.001,$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{\mathrm{ nuc}}} = 0,\ {{k}_{\mathrm{ dil}}} = 0,b = 0.005$\end{document}. The off rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{r}_{\mathrm{ off}}}$\end{document} is variable and is stated separately for each case in the results. As breaking increases the number of strands, and as we assume that both fragments can grow, it is possible to ignore nucleation events in B conditions. Therefore we set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{\mathrm{ nuc}}} = 0$\end{document}.

The standard parameters for the D conditions are as for B conditions except that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu = 0.005,\ {{k}_{\mathrm{ nuc}}} = 0.1,\ {{k}_{\mathrm{ dil}}} = 0.02,b = 0.001.$\end{document} With these rates a chain is much more likely to be lost by dilution than to undergo strand breakage. In D conditions it is necessary to include a nonzero nucleation rate in order to generate new strands, and a higher permeability for incoming nucleotides \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu $\end{document} is necessary to compensate for the loss of nucleotides by dilution.

In a general sequence mixture there may be cycles of more than one length, so it is useful to determine the strongest cycle. To do this we construct a connection matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{C}_{ij}}$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $i$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $j$\end{document} are pentamers, setting element \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{C}_{ij}} = 1$\end{document} if the hexamer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ij$\end{document} that links them is present in at least \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} copies. If the threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} is too high, there are no connected cycles. We start with a high c and progressively reduce it until a connected cycle appears. The strongest cycle is the one which appears for the highest threshold c, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{L}_{{\mathrm{ strong}}}}$\end{document} is the length of this cycle. When a connected cycle appears, we check that for every connected word, the reverse complement is also connected. This ensures that both halves of a two component cycle are connected. If this is not true, we continue to reduce the threshold until it becomes true. For runs that resulted in a single IS, the GC content of the pattern is usually exactly 1/2 (e.g. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $[ {\textit{ACAAGGUGCACCUUGU}} ]$\end{document}) or close to 1/2 (e.g. 8/14 for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $[ {\textit{UUCGCAGCUGCGAA}} ]$\end{document}). In cases where more than one IS remains in the mixture at the end of the run, the strongest cycle usually has a GC content that differs considerably from 1/2 (e.g. 3/4 for [ACCC]+[GGGU], and 12/18 for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $[ {\textit{CGGGUGGAAUUCCACCCG}} ]$\end{document}). In these cases another unconnected IS with a GC content biased in the opposite direction remains in the mixture at the same time, so that the GC content of all the sequences in the mixture remains close to 1/2. In the extreme cases where the strongest cycle contains only AU bases or only GC bases, the strongest cycle is balanced by another cycle containing bases of the opposite type. Thus in general, if the supply contains all four bases, then all four bases remain in the sequence mixture, either because an IS is formed with GC content close to that of the supply, or because two independent ISs are formed with balancing GC content. A caveat here is that we have assumed equal rate constants and stacking energies for formation of GC and AU base pairs, whereas in reality GC pairs are much more stable and might form faster. We expect that allowing for this might result in selection of ISs with very high GC content.

The dynamics of sequence scrambling

In this section we intiate the mixture in a desired IS and ask whether it is maintained without scrambling. We first consider a length 12 sequence that we call 12A (see Fig. 5A). This contains repeated three letter words CAA and UUG, hence the level is 4. The number of tetramers is 24. The number of pentamers is also 24 (red arrows). The blue arrows are pentamers that are not contained in the generator, but which can be created by primer extension. Scrambling occurs if the blue pentamers become too frequent. The y and z links can be formed if primer extension occurs when there is a match length of 3 between primer and template but not if the match length is 4 or more. For example, if the primer ends UCAA, as in Fig 5B, there is only one tetramer UUGA that must be in the template, and the only possible base that precedes this is U (red in Fig. 5B), so the only base that can be added is A. However, if primer extension occurs when there is a match length of 3, then the preceding base in the template can also be C (as in Fig. 5C), and this allows the addition of a G base, which creates the y link UCAAG.

When a double stranded configuration exists with the 3′ end of the primer paired with the template the rate of attempted addition of a base is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{\mathrm{ ext}}}{{C}_{\mathrm{ Nuc}}}$\end{document}. If it does not match, then we still accept the addition with a small probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\varepsilon $\end{document}. More realistically, however, other parameters should vary at the same time as the off rate because all the rates should depend on the stacking free energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta G$\end{document}.

However, more realistically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{r}_{\mathrm{ off}}}$\end{document} should depend on the stacking free energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta G$\end{document}. If we want \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{r}_{\mathrm{ sep}}}( 3 ) = 500$\end{document} when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta G/kT$\end{document} = 3, as we chose initially, then we need \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{r}_0} = 500{{e}^6}$\end{document}. If there is some rate of bond formation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{\textit{bond}}}$\end{document} when the monomer is in position, then the net primer extension rate should be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{\mathrm{ ext}}} = r_{\mathrm{ on}}^{\mathrm{ mon}}{{k}_{{\mathrm{ bond}}}}/( {r_{\mathrm{ off}}^{\mathrm{ mon}} + {{k}_{{\mathrm{ bond}}}}} )$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r_{\mathrm{ on}}^{\mathrm{ mon}}$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r_{\mathrm{ off}}^{\mathrm{ mon}}$\end{document} are the on and off rates for monomers and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{k}_{{\mathrm{ bond}}}}/( {r_{\mathrm{ off}}^{\mathrm{ mon}} + {{k}_{{\mathrm{ bond}}}}} )$\end{document} is the probability that the bond forms before the monomer separates from the template. We will assume that the monomer on rate is the same as that for double strand formation, and the monomer off rate is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{r}_0}{\mathrm{exp}} ( { - \frac{{2\Delta G}}{{kT}}} )$\end{document}. The factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $2\Delta G$\end{document} would apply if the monomer were actually a bridged dimer (as in [7]), in which case there is one stacking interaction between the dimer and the primer and one between the two bases of the dimer. The real rates may not exactly match these assumptions, but the essential point is that the primer extension rate decreases when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta G$\end{document} is reduced. By changing physical conditions in the growth phase such that stacking is less strong, we can increase the off rate, which reduces scrambling, but only at the expense of reducing the primer extension rate. If the primer extension rate becomes too small, templating reactions cannot be sustained at all and the system breaks down to just monomers.

For longer oligomers which are correctly paired according to the VCG sequence, there are multiple ways of initiating a double strand and the resulting match length is long because of zipping. Can VCGs encode sequences that fold to a secondary structure?

We now ask whether VCGs could encode a sequence with a secondary structure that might have a useful function. A secondary structure makes scrambling worse because it introduces repeated words. For example, the sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ACUCUUCGGAGU$\end{document} forms a hairpin (Fig. 11A) with the bases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $UUCG$\end{document} in the loop. This loop sequence is a tetraloop that is able to perform loop-closing ligation [41], which is potentially a constructive function in the RNA world. The structure has a stem of length 4, and the two sides of the stem are complementary. Words in the stem are repeated in the complementary sequence of the VCG; hence this cannot be a two-component IC. We argued previously [30] that any sequence with a secondary structure can never be encoded on a VCG. However, this argument was too simplistic. The original VCG idea is a two-component IC, but the possibility of ISs that are not cycles makes it easier to encode sequences that have a secondary structure. This tetraloop sequence and its complement form a level 3 IS shown in Fig. 11B. As it is only level 3, it is quite stable in simulations. Figure 13C shows a hammerhead ribozyme of length 44. The unpaired base on the 3′ end was added so that the two halves of the outer stem do not form a word of length 8 in the circular permutation. With this extra base included, the sequence is a two-component IC of level 5. We generated many trial hammerhead sequences with the conserved bases required for the cleavage reaction fixed (shown in red) and the remaining bases free to vary with the constraint that paired bases in the stems formed a Watson–Crick pair. All these trial sequences were level 5 or above i.e. there are no hammerhead sequences of level 4. Thus, to encode a hammerhead ribozyme on a VCG it is necessary to find parameters where a length 44, level 5 pattern is stable, and this is not easy. We find (Table 4) that this hammerhead sequence is unstable in B conditions and stable at zero error rate in D conditions, but becomes unstable at even the smallest nonzero error rate that we tried (0.5%). We conclude that the hammerhead structure is on the borderline of what might be possible to replicate by the VCG mechanism with an optimistic choice of parameters. We allowed a high off rate without a corresponding decrease in the primer extension rate, whereas it would actually be more realistic to decrease the primer extension rate if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{r}_{\mathrm{ off}}}$\end{document} is increased, as discussed above. We also ignored the stability difference between GC and AU pairs. We assumed that the error rate is equal for each combination of template base and incorrect base, whereas experiments show that error rates are much higher for some combinations than others [1, 3]. We expect that all these things would make the scrambling problem worse than the case simulated.

We also considered the tRNA clover-leaf structure with sequence length 76. Although there is a small probability (∼1.5%) that a random length 76 sequence is level 5 (Fig 3), when we add the additional constraints of base pairing in the positions of the stems of the clover-leaf, we find all sequences are at least level 6. We did not attempt to simulate a level 6 sequence because level 5 sequences are already at the margin of what is stable in our simulations. We argue that it would be impossible to encode a tRNA cloverleaf using the VCG mechanism.

Discussion

The VCG idea was proposed as a mechanism by which sequence information could be encoded in a mixture of overlapping oligomers [29]. This mechanism works if the encoded sequence is short and the error rate is small. We have found it relatively easy to replicate sequences encoding a single hairpin loop, but sequences longer than this become rapidly quite difficult. Sequences of the size of a hammerhead ribozyme are on the borderline of what is likely to be possible even for the most optimistic choice of parameters. The original VCG idea corresponds to a two-component IC. We demonstrated the possibility of ISs that are not simple cycles, and if these ISs have a low level, they may be stably replicated by the VCG mechanism. The algorithm for determining the level can be rapidly run for many trial sequences. This allows us to prove that all sequences folding to the hairpin plus internal loop structure (Fig. 13A) must be at least level 4, all sequences folding to the hammerhead ribozyme structure must be at least level 5 and all sequences folding to the tRNA cloverleaf must be at least level 6.

Viewed optimistically, the VCG provides a mechanism for sequence heredity and the beginning of Darwinian evolution. Random mixtures of sequences converge to many different ISs, so in a population of protocells with different VCG sequences, the possibility of natural selection exists. The tetraloop performing loop-closing ligation (Fig. 11A) and the autocatalytic ligase (Fig. 13B) both have the ability to construct longer sequences. Simple functions like this might evolve into ribozymes with more complex and general functions, as has been envisaged by several authors [44, 45]. We should remember, however, that selection would need to act at the level of the protocell, not at the level of the molecule. It is not immediately obvious why a VCG that encoded a short ligase would confer an advantage to the cell.

If a polymerase ribozyme could be constructed that was able to copy a general template sequence as well as its own sequence, this would open the way for evolution of ribozymes with other functions that could be copied by the polymerase. For some time, known polymerases have had lengths around 200 [18–21, 45], which leaves a large gap between what is achievable by the VCG and what is required for a polymerase. However, the recent discovery of the QT45 polymerase [24] of only length 45 makes this gap much narrower. QT45 is comparable in size to the hammerhead ribozyme we considered here, so it might just about be replicable by the VCG mechanism, which would then allow evolution from nonenzymatic to ribozyme-catalyzed replication.

We assumed that primer extension adds a single monomer, as In Fig. 14A. The case where the primer extends by addition of a dimer (Fig. 14B) was not included in these simulations, but there is no reason why this should not occur if there is an in situ method of activation of dimers. Dimer addition has been demonstrated experimentally [8, 46], and extension by dimers is more efficient than by monomers in some cases. In situ activation has also been considered in the context of the VCG [47]. Addition of a dimer to a primer that has a long match with the template (as in Fig. 14B) does not make the scrambling problem worse than it already is for monomer addition. In contrast, if a long primer binds with only two base pairs and is then extended (Fig. 14C), this creates a serious scrambling problem that prevents replication of patterns with level >2.

(A) The simulations presented in this paper use a virtual circle mechanism where primer extension adds a single monomer. (B) A virtual circle mechanism could also work by adding dimers if activated dimers were available. Adding dimers to a primer-template with a long match does not make scrambling any worse. (C) Allowing a primer to extend when the match length is only two would create serious scrambling problems and needs to be avoided. (D) An alternative picture in which linear templates are replicated by ligation of monomers, dimers, and trimers without the need for a log primer. This would completely eliminate the scrambling problem.

These points are relevant in another recent computational study of the VCG mechanism [33]. This reference distinguishes between “feedstock” oligomers that are too short to specify a unique location on the genome and VCG oligomers that are long enough to specify a unique location. If dimers are activated, then dimers would be feedstock by this definition. It was argued that scrambling is mostly caused by ligation of two VCG oligomers and not by ligation of a VCG oligomer to a feedstock molecule. We agree that ligation of two long VCG oligomers would be bad for scrambling, but this situation was already excluded from our simulations because only monomers were assumed to be activated. Our simulations clearly show that scrambling occurs when there is addition of a feedstock monomer to a VCG oligomer, and the problem does not go away if only monomers are activated, contrary to what is suggested in [33]. Having activated dimers as feedstock will not prevent this problem either, because incorrectly positioned VCG oligomers will be ligated to the dimer feedstock. Ref. [33] concludes that the VCG is a promising scenario for prebiotic replication, but we feel this is based on study of a rather easy case. They studied an example sequence of only length 16 that is only level 3. We tested this same sequence in our simulations and found that it is indeed quite stable—with an error threshold of 6% in B conditions with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{r}_{\mathrm{ off}}} = 500$\end{document} (similar to the other level 3 sequences in Table 4). But as we have shown, longer sequences with interesting secondary structures necessarily have higher levels, and this makes them much more prone to scrambling.

The VCG mechanism has both advantages and disadvantages. In experiments, it is easier to study extension of a pre-existing primer on a template than to observe the nucleation of a new strand on a template without a primer. It has therefore tended to be assumed that nucleation is more difficult than primer extension. By proposing a circular arrangement of overlapping strands that can reanneal, the VCG ensures that every template has a primer, so this mechanism can work even if nucleation of new strands is very slow. Furthermore, in most previous experiments, primer extension is inefficient and addition of only a small number of bases is observed. This is not a problem for the VCG mechanism because a strand may grow over multiple cycles. If strands reanneal in a partially overlapping fashion (Fig. 14A), the growing strand can be longer than the template, so the VCG mechanism has no problem in creating longer strands. However, the VCG relies on reannealing of oligomers, and when reannealing occurs in the wrong place, scrambling occurs. Thus, although the VCG mechanism solves several problems, it introduces the scrambling problem that has been the focus of this paper.

The difficulty of avoiding scrambling unless the VCG sequence is very short leads us to consider possible mechanisms that do not have this problem, for example, a linear replication mechanism in which a template is copied by binding a mixture of monomers, dimers, and trimers with in situ activation and subsequent ligation (Fig. 14D). If the monomers, dimers, and trimers bind strongly to the template, a new strand can be nucleated easily without the need for a long primer. Recent experiments with in situ activated dimers [46] show that dimers bind strongly to the template, and cover most of the template prior to ligation. If this is true, scrambling cannot occur at all because the monomers, dimers and trimers do not bring sequence information that is not in the template (corresponding to ‘feedstock plus feedstock’ of [33]). We call this mechanism linear because there is no requirement that templates should form an overlapping virtual circle. Any number of different sequences could be replicated simultaneously, so there is no problem of accumulating large amounts of sequence information, whereas a VCG is limited to a single sequence (unless there are two extremely short sequences with no words in common). Sequence errors would still occur in the linear mechanism, so accurate copying would still require a small error rate, but sequence errors are different from scrambling, which occurs even when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\varepsilon = 0$\end{document}.

Linear replication has other problems, however. Physical conditions would need to cycle between growth and separation phases. Reannealing of existing complementary strands is usually fast compared to assembly of new strands; hence, reannealing of complementary pairs inhibits subsequent replication cycles [4]. Our own modelling work [48] showed that the concentration of strands is limited to a very low level if reannealing is fast. But the inhibition caused by reannealing can be avoided if reannealing is slowed by a viscous solution [49, 50]. Also, in a system of replication with triplets [23], it was found that the triplets bind rapidly to a template and prevent it reannealing with its complement. Furthermore, triplet binding was strong enough to compete with the internal secondary structure of the template, which opens up the folded sequence and makes it accessible to replication. These experiments used a polymerase ribozyme [23], but these advantages of triplets would also apply to nonenzymatic replication. We therefore imagine a prebiotic linear replication system in which a mixture of monomers, dimers and trimers covers the template and is subsequently ligated (Fig. 14D). There would need to be a high probability that the full complementary strand was formed in a single cycle, otherwise strands would inevitably get shorter every cycle. To balance the tendency to shorten, there would have to be some probability that a dimer or trimer could extend beyond the end of the template (as in Fig. 14D). In contrast, for the VCG, extending beyond the template is not necessary as growth occurs on different templates over multiple cycles.

Computational studies are useful because they allow us to test things that are not accessible in experiment. Although some aspects of the VCG model can be tested in experiment [47], scrambling takes thousands of cycles to occur in the simulations, and RNA replication experiments sustained over long periods of time are not yet feasible. The simplest stable patterns with four bases are two base repeats, like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $[ {AG} ] + [ {CU} ]$\end{document} or four base repeats like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $[ {\textit{ACGU}} ]$\end{document}, which cannot scramble to shorter patterns, but even so, to sustain replication primer extension must be efficient. As far as we are aware, sustainable long-term nonenzymatic replication of even these simple patterns has still not been demonstrated experimentally. So it is perhaps premature to worry about scrambling of longer patterns. Nevertheless, our results show that for the VCG to avoid scrambling, there must be weak pairing between primer and template so the off rate is large, and this inevitably means that the primer extension reaction will be slow. In contrast, experiments in which linear replication occurs with dimers and trimers can use conditions (e.g. ice [23]) in which there is strong pairing of the dimers and trimers with the template. In this case there is no opposition between fast replication and maintenance of sequence information. At this point, it is still unclear which of these approaches is more likely to be successful under prebiotic conditions.
