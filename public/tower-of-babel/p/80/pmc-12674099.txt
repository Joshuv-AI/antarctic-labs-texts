High‐Resolution and High‐Conductivity Electrodes via Two‐Step Annealing‐Enhanced Solvent‐Free Patterning of Silver Nanoparticles
Authors: Sangmok Kim, Dong‐Joon Won, Dongwoo Yoo, Joonwon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatterning of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) for high‐resolution and high‐conductivity electrodes is essential for high‐performance flexible electronic devices. AgNPs with sinster‐bridged conductive networks maintain high electrical conductivities even at fine features owing to their constant (i.e., size‐independent) percolation threshold, thereby enabling the fabrication of high‐resolution, high‐conductivity electrodes. However, patterning AgNP electrodes with high density using a facile, high‐quality, and highly efficient approach remains challenging. Therefore, this study developed two‐step annealing‐enhanced solvent‐free patterning of AgNPs using a UV‐curable pressure‐sensitive adhesive (PSA) film. The first annealing induces interfacial fusion between the AgNPs and the poly(1,1,2,2‐tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE)‐coated substrate, converting point contacts into surface contacts while increasing the electrical conductivity through metallic bonding via sinter bridge formation. The second annealing allows the prepolymer adhesive to infiltrate voids between annealed AgNPs, creating mechanical interlocking after UV curing that significantly increases the work of fracture of the composite. This approach enables the formation of AgNP patterns without residues or loss, achieving critical dimensions down to 10 µm with excellent pattern fidelity. Moreover, the developed patterning technique simultaneously creates complementary patterns on both rigid and flexible films in a single process. Consequently, this study demonstrates applicability to flexible electronic devices, such as flexible heaters and a capacitive sensor array.

Introduction

Flexible electronics have attracted significant attention owing to their application potential in various fields, including flexible displays,[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] flexible transistors,[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] electronic skin (E‐skin),[ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] and healthcare monitoring systems.[ 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] In recent years, flexible electronic devices have been miniaturized to enhance their integration densities.[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] As the electrode width decreases and the length increases, the aspect ratio increases, while the demand for arbitrarily shaped patterns increases the geometric complexity. Consequently, more intricate pattern designs are required for flexible electronic devices. These structural changes, including the increasingly intricate electrode geometries induced by miniaturization and the arbitrary design requirements, inevitably increase the electrode resistance. This higher resistance increases both the power consumption and the resistive‐capacitive (RC) delay of the flexible electronics.[ 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] In addition, high resistance degrades the electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding performance, thereby compromising device stability.[ 34 , 35 , 36 ] Furthermore, the high resistance of source and drain electrodes in field‐effect transistors, which are integral components of various wearable devices such as sensors and displays, reduces the drain current, thereby degrading the sensitivity and responsivity.[ 37 , 38 , 39 ] Consequently, there is a growing demand for flexible electrodes that maintain high electrical conductivities even at fine critical dimensions (CDs).

Various materials have been explored for the preparation of miniaturized flexible electronic devices, including conductive polymers such as poly(3,4‐ethylenedioxythiophene)–poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS),[ 40 , 41 ] 1D nanomaterials such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs),[ 42 , 43 ] and silver nanowires (AgNWs),[ 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 ] and 0D nanomaterials like silver nanoparticles (AgNPs).[ 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 ] In particular, PEDOT:PSS suffers from inherently poor electrical conductivity and is vulnerable to humidity,[ 31 , 52 , 53 ] whereas 1D nanomaterials exhibit progressively higher percolation thresholds as the feature size decreases because of their high aspect ratios, thereby limiting the fabrication of high‐conductivity, high‐resolution patterns.[ 46 ] Among these, 0D‐AgNP‐based flexible electrodes are particularly promising because of their porous structure, which provides mechanical reliability during bending.[ 54 ] They also exhibit a consistent percolation threshold regardless of the line width, which is attributed to their 0D morphology, along with solution processability and high electrical conductivity.

Conventional approaches for patterning nanomaterials, such as AgNPs, include inkjet printing,[ 55 , 56 ] gravure printing,[ 57 , 58 ] shadow‐mask printing,[ 59 , 60 ] and photolithography employing a photoresist (PR).[ 61 , 62 , 63 ] Inkjet printing enables direct, maskless deposition onto various substrates but suffers from poor pattern uniformity and a limited resolution because of the coffee ring effect,[ 64 ] as well as nozzle clogging and subsequent printing failure.[ 65 ] For both gravure and reverse‐offset printing, the ink composition, rheological properties, and process parameters must be intricately optimized based on the substrate characteristics, and they also rely on costly and sophisticated printing systems. Additionally, shadow‐mask printing faces several inherent limitations. In particular, the fabrication of shadow masks involves complex processes, including photolithography and wet/dry etching, which require the use of toxic chemicals. Furthermore, any modification to the shadow‐mask design necessitates the repetition of these complex fabrication processes. Moreover, the fabrication of fine, high‐aspect‐ratio patterns is challenging because it requires thin‐film shadow masks with demanding structural characteristics, which inherently render them highly susceptible to tearing and mechanical failure. Shadow‐mask printing is also fundamentally limited in producing closed geometries such as “B,” “D,” “O,” “P,” “Q,” “0,” and “6.” Additionally, capillary infiltration of the solution between the mask and the substrate degrades the line‐edge roughness (LER), which can adversely affect the device performance, particularly in terms of its power consumption. Photolithography employing a PR enables the high‐resolution and designable patterning of AgNPs. However, this approach requires costly equipment and complicated fabrication procedures, and it incurs substantial manufacturing expenses. In addition, the use of organic solvents and etchant solutions, along with soft or hard baking processes at temperatures above 90 °C, is known to limit substrate selection. Moreover, the infiltration of etchant solutions during wet etching or PR removal can damage the AgNP networks, thereby degrading the pattern quality.

To overcome these limitations, a solvent‐free patterning technique was previously developed for the high‐resolution patterning of nanomaterials.[ 46 ] This approach entails attaching a selectively UV‐cured pressure‐sensitive adhesive (PSA) film to a nanomaterial‐coated substrate. Upon further UV irradiation of the patterned PSA film attached to the nanomaterial‐coated substrate, the nanomaterials located in the non‐photocured regions become securely anchored through hardening and volume shrinkage. Subsequently, after peeling off the PSA film, the anchored nanomaterials are transferred onto the patterned adhesive surface. This approach offers a non‐toxic, cost‐effective, and highly process‐efficient approach for achieving the high‐resolution patterning of 1D nanomaterials, such as AgNWs, without the need for toxic solvents, expensive equipment, or complex processing steps. In contrast, the conventional solvent‐free patterning technique embeds only the upper portion of the nanomaterial coating layer into the PSA adhesive, limiting its applicability to thin coating layers of 1D nanomaterials.

In such thin layers of 1D nanomaterials, the high aspect ratio of the nanomaterials allows sufficient anchoring even when only the upper portion is embedded, enabling complete transfer without residues on the substrate. Nevertheless, the conductivities of such 1D‐nanomaterial‐based electrodes rapidly decline as the pattern dimensions decrease owing to drastic increases in the percolation threshold. Therefore, to realize high‐conductivity fine electrodes, a high‐resolution patterning strategy is required for 0D nanomaterials, such as AgNPs, that maintains a consistent percolation threshold irrespective of the pattern width. However, establishing stable current pathways using 0D nanomaterials requires a higher material density and thicker coating layers than those that can be achieved using 1D nanomaterials. Moreover, because 0D materials exhibit extremely low aspect ratios, nanomaterials located in the lower region of the coating layer cannot be effectively embedded into the PSA film. Consequently, incomplete transfer occurs, ultimately leaving residue on the substrate.

In this study, we demonstrate two‐step annealing‐enhanced solvent‐free patterning of AgNPs to fabricate high‐resolution and high‐conductivity electrodes. A UV‐curable PSA film is selectively photopolymerized through a photomask; then, the patterned film is placed onto an AgNP‐coated substrate, and a two‐step annealing is performed to ensure the complete transfer of AgNPs to the PSA film. The first annealing step enhances the adhesion between the AgNPs and the poly(1,1,2,2‐tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE)‐coated substrate, simultaneously improving the electrical conductivity through metallic bonding via sinter bridge formation. Subsequently, the second annealing step allows the prepolymer adhesive to flow into the voids between the annealed AgNPs in the transfer region. Through this approach, high‐quality AgNP patterns with CDs down to 10 µm are achieved, demonstrating an excellent pattern fidelity for various shapes, including iso‐lines, line‐space structures, and arbitrary curved patterns. Moreover, this technique enables the simultaneous fabrication of a positive AgNP pattern on the substrate and its complementary, precisely inverted negative pattern on the PSA film in a single process. To demonstrate the applicability of the developed patterning technique, flexible heaters and a capacitive sensor array are fabricated. The grid pattern embedded in the flexible PSA film operates as a mechanically robust flexible heater without the requirement for additional encapsulation processes, demonstrating the application potential for mechanically robust flexible electronic devices. Additionally, the unique compatibility of this approach allows the capacitive sensor array to simultaneously produce positive and negative patterns on the substrate and the PSA film, respectively, thereby enabling the fabrication of the upper and lower electrodes of the sensor array in a single process, resulting in a high process efficiency.

Results and Discussion

Figure 1 illustrates the two‐step annealing‐enhanced solvent‐free patterning process employed for the high‐resolution patterning of AgNPs using UV‐curable PSA films. The process begins with the selective photopolymerization of a UV‐curable PSA film, which consists of a polyolefin base film, a UV‐curable acrylic prepolymer PSA layer, and a polyethylene terephthalate (PET) release film. Upon UV exposure (365 nm, 7 mW cm−2) through a photomask, the photopolymerization initiator generates radicals in the PSA layer, initiating a crosslinking reaction. This transforms the exposed regions of the adhesive prepolymer PSA into hardened non‐adhesive polymer regions while maintaining adhesivity in the masked regions (Figure 1a‐i). Specifically, UV curing significantly decreases the adhesion strength from 1900 to 10 gf/ 25 mm (Figure S1, Supporting Information). After peeling off the PET release film, a patterned PSA film remains, which is ready for attachment (Figure 1a‐ii). In addition, a pristine AgNP‐coated substrate was prepared by spin‐coating an ethanol‐based AgNP solution onto a PTFE‐coated glass substrate pretreated with air plasma to enhance its hydrophilicity (Figure S2, Supporting Information). A first annealing step was performed by heating the substrate at 200 °C for 1 min 30 s to promote interfacial fusion between the PTFE and AgNP layers. This first annealing step increased adhesion strength by converting point contacts into surface contacts, while also enhancing cohesion via sintering‐induced metallic bonding, thereby robustly attaching the AgNP layer to the PTFE‐coated substrate (Figure 1a‐iii). Subsequently, the patterned PSA film was attached to the first‐annealed AgNP‐coated substrate using a roller with a force of ≈ 20 N to ensure uniform contact between the adhesive regions of the PSA film and the AgNP layer (Figure 1a‐iv). A second annealing step was then performed at 50 °C for 30 min, which is above the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the adhesive but below that of the non‐adhesive polymer. This second annealing step caused the prepolymer adhesive to flow downward owing to gravity, filling voids within the AgNP coating layer and entirely embedding the AgNPs within the transfer regions. The higher fluidity of the prepolymer adhesive compared with that of the non‐adhesive polymer region enabled this embedding process, ensuring that the AgNPs were completely integrated into the adhesive layer (Figure 1a‐v). A final UV exposure step across the entire PSA film induced further crosslinking in the transfer regions, resulting in volume shrinkage and hardening. This generated strong mechanical interlocking between the embedded AgNPs and the polymer network (Figure 1a‐vi). Upon peeling off the PSA film, the AgNPs in the transfer regions were transferred onto the PSA film, forming negative patterns, while the AgNPs in the remaining regions remained on the substrate, forming positive patterns (Figure 1a‐vii). The photographic and optical microscopy images presented in Figure 1b,c, respectively, show the positive patterns on the rigid glass substrates, whereas those in Figure 1d,e show the negative patterns on the flexible PSA films. The positive patterns exhibit line widths of 200 µm with spacings of 50 µm (Figure 1c), corresponding to the pattern defined by the open regions of the photomask. In contrast, the negative patterns show inverted dimensions, i.e., line widths of 50 µm and spacings of 200 µm (Figure 1e). These results demonstrate that the complementary patterning technique enables the simultaneous fabrication of positive and negative patterns in a single process. This indicates its potential for application in flexible electronic devices while minimizing material usage.

Figure 2 demonstrates the necessity of the first annealing step and its critical role in stabilizing the patterning mechanism, thereby expanding on the selective photopolymerization process presented in Figure 1. As shown in Figure 2a, the open and closed patterns of the photomask define the adhesive (prepolymer) and non‐adhesive (polymer) regions on the PSA film, corresponding to the remaining region “a” on the substrate and the transfer region “b” on the PSA film. This ensures that the photomask design corresponds directly to the target line and space widths for patterning. However, as shown in Figure 2b, without performing the first annealing step, AgNPs are lost in the remaining region owing to the weak adhesion between the pristine AgNPs and the PTFE layer. Figure 2c illustrates the loss mechanism of AgNPs in the remaining region during the patterning process. This phenomenon can be physically explained by considering the van der Waals forces (FvdW) acting between the particles.[ 66 , 67 , 68 ] The magnitude of FvdW is proportional to the Hamaker constant (AH), which represents the total strength of the dispersion interactions. For a spherical particle and a flat plate, FvdW is given by:[ 66 ] (2) FvdW,AgNP−PTFE=−AH,Ag−PTFE·R6D2 where R = 25 nm is the nanoparticle radius, and D ≈ 0.2 nm[ 67 ] is the typical separation distance between the contacting surfaces. Using experimentally reported values of AH, Ag‐Ag = 38.2 × 10−20J[ 68 ] and AH, Ag‐PTFE = 13.7 × 10−20 J,[ 68 ] the resulting forces are FvdW, AgNP‐AgNP ≈ −20 nN and FvdW, AgNP‐PTFE ≈ −14 nN, confirming that the AgNP–AgNP interactions are 1.4 times stronger than the AgNP–PTFE interactions. This cohesive dominance explains the experimentally observed behaviors of the two regions. Specifically, when the PSA film is UV‐crosslinked and then peeled off, it preferentially removes AgNPs from the remaining region (“a”) along with adjacent particles from the transfer region (“b”). The strong Ag–Ag interparticle cohesion overcomes the relatively weak Ag–PTFE adhesion, resulting in the loss of AgNPs from the substrate surface, as shown in Figure 2c. This differential interaction strength, as evidenced by the difference in the calculated van der Waals forces (FvdW, AgNP‐AgNP ≈ 1.4 × FvdW, AgNP‐PTFE ), comprehensively explains the loss of AgNPs from the remaining region. During the patterning process, the prepolymer in the PSA film strongly adheres to the upper layer of AgNPs owing to volume shrinkage and hardening during UV‐induced polymer crosslinking (black arrows, Figure 2c). In addition, cohesion occurs between the AgNPs (yellow arrows), contributing to the structural connectivity between the upper and lower layers. Meanwhile, the lower layer of AgNPs becomes weakly adhered to the substrate owing to the relatively weak FvdW, AgNP‐PTFE between the lower AgNPs and the PTFE‐coated substrate with a low surface energy (red arrows). Consequently, when the PSA film was peeled off from the substrate, the AgNPs in the remaining region (“a”) were transferred to the PSA film along with the adjacent AgNPs in the transfer region (“b”) because the van der Waals forces acting between the AgNPs are substantially stronger than those between the lower AgNP layer and the PTFE‐coated substrate. To address this problem, the first annealing step was introduced to enhance adhesion by inducing interfacial fusion between the AgNP layer and PTFE (Figure 2d‐i). Interfacial fusion increases the contact area from point contacts to surface contacts, thereby improving the adhesion. To ensure interfacial fusion during the first annealing step, the first annealing temperature (Ta1) must exceed both the glass transition temperature of PTFE (Tg_T) and the sintering temperature of the AgNPs (Ts_A) (i.e., Ta1 > Tg_T and Ta1 > Ts_A). Surpassing Tg_T increases the fluidity of this component, promotes facial contact with the AgNPs, and enhances adhesion. Meanwhile, AgNP sintering reduces the contact resistance between particles by forming sinter bridges, thereby enhancing the electrical conductivity. To optimize the first annealing conditions, the effects of the main process parameters were systematically characterized. Initial experiments conducted at 180 °C (above Tg_T, i.e., 170 °C)[ 69 ] for 1 min 30 s failed to induce sufficient interfacial fusion between the AgNPs and the PTFE‐coated substrate, allowing a significant amount of AgNPs to transfer to the film, with no AgNPs being present in the remaining region of the substrate (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Thus, to enhance interfacial fusion, the annealing temperature was increased to 200 °C while maintaining the annealing time at 1 min 30 s. Figure 2d–ii and 2d–iii show the cross‐sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images without and with the first annealing step, respectively (obtained using focused ion beam milling), to verify interfacial fusion and metallic bonding. Figure 2d‐ii shows that the AgNPs do not undergo sintering and the PTFE layer does not undergo interfacial fusion with the AgNPs, resulting in a flat surface with only point contacts between PTFE and the AgNPs. In contrast, Figure 2d‐iii demonstrates that the first annealing step promotes metallic bonding of the AgNPs through sintering, while the PTFE layer undergoes interfacial fusion with the AgNPs, resulting in a bumpy surface with surface contacts between PTFE and the AgNPs. As a result, the first annealing step effectively promotes robust interfacial fusion between the AgNPs and the PTFE substrate, enabling the retention of AgNPs in the remaining region and demonstrating enhanced interfacial bonding along with excellent adhesion to the substrate (Figure 2e‐i). In contrast, annealing times of 30 s and 1 min at 200 °C, both of which are shorter than the optimal 1 min 30 s, led to a loss of AgNPs in the remaining region, thereby reflecting insufficient interfacial fusion (Figures S4a,b, Supporting Information). Therefore, an annealing temperature and time of 200 °C and 1 min 30 s, respectively, were selected as the optimal conditions to appropriately enhance the adhesion between the AgNP and PTFE layers, while minimizing the contact resistance between the AgNPs, and ensuring a high conductivity. Figure S5 (Supporting Information) further supports this finding by showing that the sheet resistance reaches saturation at 2.55 Ω sq−1 following a first annealing step performed for 1 min 30 s. Figure 2e‐ii illustrates the patterning results and phenomena observed after the first annealing step, during which no AgNPs are lost from the remaining region during patterning. Despite these improvements, random residues were observed in the transfer region after patterning (Figure 2e‐i). This residue formation can be explained by considering an imbalance in the work of adhesion and cohesion during peel‐off (Figure 2e‐ii). In the transfer regions, W1 represents the work of adhesion between the PSA film adhesive and the upper layer of annealed AgNPs, W2 represents the work of cohesion between the annealed AgNPs due to metallic bonding, and W3 represents the work of adhesion between the lower annealed AgNPs and PTFE caused by interfacial fusion (Figure 2e‐ii). Residues form because the PSA adhesive contacts only the upper layer of the AgNPs without engaging the lower layers. Specifically, during the peel‐off process, W1 enables strong bonding only between the PSA and the upper layer of the AgNPs after UV crosslinking. Therefore, to achieve complete transfer without residue formation, W2 must overcome W3. If W2 is not consistently greater than W3, residues form on the substrate. As shown in Figure 2e‐i, these residues form in the transfer region because of the Gaussian size distribution of the AgNPs. Since the sizes of the sinter bridges also follow a Gaussian distribution owing to the variation in the AgNP sizes, the strengths of these bridges vary according to their size. Consequently, when W2 is smaller than W3 owing to the variation in the sinter bridge strength, cohesive failure occurs, forming residues on the PTFE‐coated substrate. As shown in Figure 2e, the random formation of residues in the transfer region can be attributed to this region, where W2 < W3. Therefore, to completely remove the residue, additional work greater than W3 must occur between the lower layer and the upper layer of AgNPs.

Figure 3 illustrates the purpose and mechanism of the second annealing step. Specifically, the primary objective of the second annealing was to allow the uncured prepolymer adhesive to flow into the voids between the annealed AgNPs, ultimately embedding the AgNPs within the polymer network to form a mechanically interlocked structure. As shown in Figure 3a, thermomechanical analysis (TMA) was conducted to determine the Tg of the non‐adhesive polymer (Tg_A), which was identified as 66.7 °C at the intersection between the extrapolated glassy and rubbery regions in the thermal expansion curve.[ 70 ] Based on this analysis and a previously reported study, the second annealing temperature (Ta2) was set at 50 °C. At this temperature, the prepolymer adhesive exhibits a low viscosity and a high fluidity, while the rigidity of the non‐adhesive polymer is maintained. At this temperature, as illustrated in Figure 3b‐i, the prepolymer adhesive flows downward under gravity during annealing at 50 °C for 30 min, filling voids between the annealed AgNPs in the transfer region and embedding them within the adhesive. Following this step, the prepolymer was subjected to UV curing to generate a crosslinked polymer network and form mechanical interlocking between the polymer matrix and the annealed AgNPs (Figure 3b‐ii). This mechanical interlocking formed a composite structure comprising the polymer matrix and the AgNP layer. As a result, the work acting on the lower layer of annealed AgNPs during transfer corresponded to the work of fracture of the composite (W6), which is the sum of W2, the work of cohesion of the polymer (W4), and the work of adhesion of the interface between the annealed AgNPs and the polymer, which was strengthened by mechanical interlocking (W5). Since W6 was greater than W2 and W3, complete transfer of the AgNPs from the substrate to the PSA film was achieved without any residues (Figure 3b‐iii). This mechanical interlocking prevented the upper and lower layers of the annealed AgNPs from separating during transfer. Consequently, a stable patterning performance was achieved without residues in the transfer region, further improving the pattern fidelity and quality.

Figure 4 presents the characterization and verification results for the first and second annealing steps, focusing on their influence on the patterning quality through a detailed analysis of key parameters such as AgNP loss, residue formation, the CDs, and the LER. The optimization criteria for the annealing times were defined as follows: 1) prevention of AgNP loss in the remaining region to avoid open circuits, 2) elimination of residue formation in the transfer region to prevent short circuits, 3) conformity of the patterned CD to the target CD, and 4) achievement of a low LER to ensure a high pattern fidelity. Figure 4a shows the changes in the CD and LER according to the first annealing time. As shown in Figures S4 and S6a (Supporting Information), an insufficient first annealing time (i.e., less than 1 min 30 s) resulted in incomplete sintering between the AgNPs and insufficient interfacial fusion between the AgNPs and the PTFE‐coated substrate, leading to AgNP loss in the remaining region owing to weak adhesion. Conversely, an excessive first annealing time of 5 min caused excessive sintering and interfacial fusion, leading to residue formation in the transfer region after the second annealing step (Figure 4b). In contrast to the optimal first annealing time of 1 min 30 s (Figure S6b, Supporting Information), a prolonged first annealing time of 5 min caused excessive sintering between the AgNPs, which led to an enlarged void size, thereby reducing the extent of mechanical interlocking between the prepolymer adhesive and the annealed AgNPs (Figure S6c, Supporting Information). Simultaneously, excessive interfacial fusion between the AgNPs and the PTFE‐coated substrate strengthened adhesion to the substrate, which lowered the transfer efficiency of the AgNPs to the PSA film, reducing the CD values (Figures 4a; Figure S6c, Supporting Information). Excessive sintering also caused the voids to become larger and more unevenly distributed, resulting in higher LER values (Figure 4a). These findings indicate the critical need to optimize the first annealing time to balance sintering, interfacial fusion, and adhesive interaction, ensuring target CD (i.e., 50 µm) with high‐fidelity patterning (Figures S7a,b, Supporting Information). Based on these results and considering that the sheet resistance also reached saturation at a first annealing time of 1 min 30 s (Figure S5, Supporting Information), this condition was identified as optimal, as it ensured sufficient adhesion without residue formation, an excellent conformity to the target CD, the lowest LER, and the highest electrical conductivity. For the second annealing step (Figure 4c), an annealing time of <30 min at 50 °C led to incomplete penetration of the prepolymer adhesive into the voids between the annealed AgNPs, resulting in residue formation in the transfer region. The SEM and optical microscopy images confirm that after the second annealing step (50 °C for 30 min), the adhesive fully infiltrated these voids, embedding the lower AgNP layers within the polymer matrix and preventing residue formation (Figures 3b‐iii; Figure S8a, Supporting Information). Figures 4d‐i and 4d‐ii present the depth profile analysis results, which verify the effects of the first and second annealing steps on the interfacial fusion and adhesive infiltration. After annealing, secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) was performed on the positive and negative AgNP patterns on the substrate and the PSA film, respectively. As shown in Figure 4d‐i, the SIMS depth profile of the positive AgNP pattern on the substrate indicates that the PTFE layer infiltrated the AgNP layer after the first annealing step. The gradual increase in the fluorine intensity upon moving from the PTFE layer to the upper and lower AgNP layers confirmed that PTFE infiltrated upward, demonstrating successful interfacial fusion and enhanced adhesion between the AgNPs and the substrate. As shown in Figure 4d‐ii, the SIMS depth profile of the negative AgNP pattern of the PSA film indicates that the adhesive infiltrated the voids between the annealed AgNPs, leading to stepwise increases in carbon from the adhesive in the lower AgNP layer after the second annealing step. A comparison of Figures S8a,b (Supporting Information), which show the first annealed AgNPs with and without a second annealing step, respectively, confirms that this step is necessary for the adhesive to infiltrate the lower layer of AgNPs, thereby enabling mechanical interlocking after UV curing. To further confirm that residues were absent in the transfer region and that AgNP loss was prevented in the remaining region, energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) was conducted (Figure 4d‐iii,iv). In Figure 4d‐iii, no Ag signals appear in the transfer region of the substrate, confirming the absence of residual AgNPs after patterning, as corroborated by the SEM image. Similarly, Figure 4D‐iv shows that no Ag is present in the remaining region of the PSA film, as also evidenced by the SEM image, indicating that AgNP loss was prevented during transfer. These findings confirmed that the optimized annealing conditions (first annealing: 1 min 30 s, second annealing: 30 min) effectively prevented both residue formation in the transfer region and AgNP loss in the remaining region. The combined SIMS and EDS analyses demonstrated that interfacial fusion during the first annealing step and adhesive infiltration during the second annealing step were therefore essential for achieving stable, high‐quality patterns without defects. As presented in Figure S9 (Supporting Information), atomic force microscopy (AFM) observations showed that the surface roughnesses of the pristine AgNPs, the positive pattern of AgNPs on the substrate (i.e., the positive electrode), and the negative pattern of AgNPs on the PSA film (i.e., the negative electrode) were 6.4, 6.0, and 2.2 nm, respectively. These results indicate similar roughness characteristics for the pristine AgNPs and the positive electrode (Figures S9a,b, Supporting Information). However, the roughness of the positive electrode, which was composed of sintered AgNPs, was slightly reduced. This decrease in roughness can be attributed to the partial merging of particles during sintering, which forms sinter bridges and reduces the size of the voids, resulting in a smoother surface. In the case of the negative electrode, the roughness was further reduced by ≈33% compared with that of the positive electrode. This additional reduction was attributed to the second annealing step, during which the PSA infiltrates and fills the voids between the particles, leading to a smoother surface (Figures 4d‐ii; Figures S8a and S9c, Supporting Information).

Figure 5 presents the results of the AgNP electrode patterning on both the substrate and the PSA film using the proposed patterning technique, demonstrating its versatility and precision. Figure 5a–c show microscopy images of the iso‐line, line‐space, and iso‐space patterns, respectively, all exhibiting a consistent CD of 50 µm. These results indicate that the proposed technique achieves a uniform patterning performance regardless of the pattern density, with a CD of 50 µm. To demonstrate the feasibility of applying the iso‐space patterns fabricated with high‐resolution AgNP electrodes to TFT fabrication, we fabricated a bottom‐contact top‐gate (BCTG) TFT structure using our patterning technique (Figure S10a, Supporting Information). Figure S10b (Supporting Information) presents the device architecture of the BCTG‐based TFT fabricated using our patterning technique. In the first patterning process, AgNP source/drain (S/D) electrodes were fabricated on the glass substrate. We fabricated devices with a channel length (L) of 15 µm and a width of 1500 µm (Figures S10c,d, Supporting Information). Subsequently, a 300 nm‐thick hydrogenated amorphous silicon (a‐Si:H) channel layer was deposited on the glass substrate with patterned S/D electrodes using plasma‐enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD), followed by the deposition of a 50 nm‐thick Al2O3 gate dielectric layer on the a‐Si:H channel layer via atomic layer deposition (ALD). After stacking the semiconductor and gate dielectric layers, the gate electrode was formed through the second patterning process. As shown in the SEM image in Figure 5d, the maximum resolution limit for iso‐line patterns fabricated using the proposed patterning technique is 10 µm. The minimum achievable pattern size is fundamentally limited by the resolution of the photomask, and thus, it is technically feasible to fabricate electrodes with line widths smaller than 10 µm using our method. However, when the relative value with respect to the line width (i.e., LER/line width [%]) is considered, the absolute LER remains approximately constant regardless of the line width. As a result, the relative impact of the LER becomes more significant as the line width decreases, potentially compromising the pattern fidelity and overall device reliability. For this reason, a minimum line width of 10 µm was selected, which ensures a reproducible and reliable electrical performance. Table S1 (Supporting Information) provides a systematic comparison of recently reported AgNP patterning techniques and their characteristics. The optical microscopy images in Figure 5e–h confirm that high‐aspect‐ratio patterns with arbitrary shapes, such as sinusoidal curves and line patterns, were successfully fabricated on both the substrate and the PSA film. Additionally, Figure 5i demonstrates the successful patterning of the word ‘POSTECH’ with a line width of 90 µm, highlighting the ability to fabricate closed shapes such as ‘P’ and ‘O,’ which are challenging to achieve using conventional shadow mask‐based techniques. Furthermore, regardless of the electrode width, the AgNP electrodes fabricated using this technique exhibited constant conductivities owing to the constant percolation threshold characteristics of the AgNPs (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Consequently, the AgNP electrodes maintained high conductivities even in fine electrodes, unlike in the case of 1D nanomaterials, such as AgNWs or CNTs, wherein the percolation thresholds drastically increase as the electrode width decreases.[ 46 ] Furthermore, the fabricated AgNP electrode exhibited an electrical conductivity of (2.87 ± 0.036) × 106 S m−1, which is higher than that of the conventional PEDOT:PSS (102–105 S m−1).[ 71 ] This suggests that AgNPs are highly suitable for applications in microelectronic devices requiring high‐resolution and high‐conductivity electrodes.

Wearable devices, which represent flexible electronic devices, frequently interact with the external environment and are therefore often exposed to mechanical contact. To ensure the durability of such devices, their flexible electrodes must possess excellent mechanical properties, including resistance to bending and abrasion. However, conventional direct or indirect patterning techniques fabricate electrodes that remain exposed on the surface, rendering them vulnerable to mechanical stresses and thereby generally requiring additional encapsulation processes.[ 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 ] In this study, AgNP‐based negative patterns on a PSA film (i.e., the negative electrodes) were fabricated by utilizing the unique advantages of this technique, which enable the firm embedding of AgNPs into the polymer matrix of the PSA film in a single process, eliminating the requirement for an additional encapsulation process. As shown in Figure 6 , durability tests were conducted to evaluate the applicability of the negative electrodes in wearable devices. Since the heater integrated into the wearable devices is frequently exposed to mechanical contact, 90° peel tests were conducted using 3M scotch tape 351 with an adhesive strength of 85.4 N/100 mm to evaluate the adhesive strengths of the negative and positive electrodes (Figure 6a‐i,ii). The positive electrode lost its conductivity after a single 90° peel test, whereas the negative electrode maintained its normalized resistance (R/R0) even after the tenth cycle of the peel test (Figure 6a‐i,ii, b). This was attributed to the fact that the AgNPs in the negative electrode were firmly embedded within the polymer matrix via the second annealing and additional UV curing process. Consequently, the adhesive strength of the negative electrode was significantly higher than that of the positive electrode, where the AgNPs were exposed on the substrate. In addition, a bending test was conducted to evaluate whether the negative electrode maintained a consistent electrical performance under repetitive bending. As shown in Figure 6c, the normalized resistance change (ΔR/R0) of the negative electrode was measured over 1000 bending cycles with a radius of curvature (ROC) of 2 mm. The results revealed that the ΔR/R0 values at the initial and 1000th cycles were nearly identical, indicating that the developed electrode is suitable for use in flexible electronics applications (Figure 6d). Furthermore, as a proof‐of‐concept demonstration, a flexible heater was fabricated using the negative electrode. The grid design (pitch: 600 µm, line width: 300 µm; Figure 6e) was selected because of its optimal electrical properties. The Joule heating performance was evaluated by measuring the temperature changes under varying applied voltages using an IR camera (Figure 6f). The results indicate that the temperature increased proportionally with the applied voltage, demonstrating efficient heat generation following Joule's law (T ≈ P = V2/R, where T is the temperature, P is the supplied power, V is the applied voltage, and R is the resistance of the specimen). Finally, to demonstrate its practical applicability, the heater was integrated into clothing, wherein heat was successfully generated even under mechanical deformation (Figure 6g), confirming the potential of this heater for use in wearable devices.

The proposed patterning technique is a complementary process that enables the simultaneous fabrication of both positive and negative electrodes in a single process, resulting in a high process efficiency. Utilizing this approach, the upper and lower electrodes of a capacitive sensor array were fabricated to demonstrate the applicability of the developed process through the fabrication of a sensor array (Figure 7 ). The sensor array was constructed by forming a microstructured polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) dielectric layer between the positive and negative electrodes (Figure 7a); the detailed fabrication steps are outlined in Figure S12 (Supporting Information). The cross‐sectional schematic in Figure 7b shows how microstructures were formed in the PDMS layer using PTFE‐coated sandpaper (400 grit) to reduce the surface energy and ensure the residue‐free transfer of the sandpaper texture to the dielectric layer (Figure S13, Supporting Information). The sensitivity was analyzed (Figure 7c) by applying pressures ranging from 0 to 1000 kPa using a printed stamp, and the capacitance changes were measured using an LCR meter. The sensitivity values were calculated as S1 = 3.38 × 10−3 kPa−1 (linearity = 90.15%) in the first linear region and S2 = 1.04 × 10−3 kPa−1 (linearity = 98.36%) in the second linear region. The pressure range for the linear region S1 (≈0–220 kPa) corresponds to the range where the fabricated capacitive sensor exhibits a high sensitivity while maintaining a linearity of >90%. Meanwhile, the pressure range for the linear region S2 (≈220–1000 kPa) represents the range where the sensor maintains a linearity >98% with a relatively lower sensitivity. In other words, S1 corresponds to a region of relatively lower linearity but higher sensitivity, whereas S2 corresponds to a region of relatively higher linearity but lower sensitivity. The higher sensitivity observed for S1 was attributed to significant changes in the air gaps within the microstructure under low pressures, which had a greater influence on the permittivity changes. In contrast, at higher pressures, the air gaps were compressed and eventually disappeared, resulting in a lower sensitivity (S2). Repeatability tests (Figure 7d) demonstrated a consistent output under repeated loading at pressures of 150, 350, and 700 kPa, confirming the reliability of the sensor. Finally, the multi‐touch functionality was evaluated using a measurement system equipped with an LCR meter and a scanning circuit (Figure S14, Supporting Information). A 5 × 5 sensor array successfully detected multi‐touch inputs from printed ‘H,’ ‘O,’ ‘P,’ and ‘E’ stamps under uniform pressure (900 kPa), as shown in Figure 7e. The resulting pressure distributions matched these patterns with a standard deviation of only 3.03%, confirming the excellent inter‐unit consistency and reliable multi‐touch sensing capability. These results confirm that the proposed patterning technique enables the scalable fabrication of advanced and reliable devices such as flexible heaters and capacitive sensors, which exhibit high‐performance attributes and are suitable for use in practical applications.

Conclusion

In this study, a two‐step annealing‐enhanced solvent‐free patterning approach was developed for the preparation of AgNP‐dense fine features, which enabled the fabrication of high‐conductivity, high‐resolution electrodes. This approach incorporated the selective photopolymerization of a UV‐curable PSA film and a two‐step annealing process. The first annealing step (200 °C, 1 min 30 s) critically enhanced the interfacial fusion between the AgNPs and the PTFE‐coated substrate, improving the electrical conductivity through metallic bonding via sinter bridge formation. The second annealing step (50 °C, 30 min) enabled the prepolymer adhesive to infiltrate the voids between the annealed AgNPs, creating mechanical interlocking after UV curing, which significantly increased the work of fracture of the composite. This synergistic approach led to completely residue‐free patterns with no AgNP loss, achieving CDs as small as 10 µm and an excellent pattern fidelity across various geometries. The first advantage of this technique is the simultaneous fabrication of complementary patterns on both rigid substrates and flexible films in a single process, eliminating the need for separate electrode fabrication processes. The second advantage is the fabrication of mechanically robust flexible electrodes via a single process, without the requirement for additional encapsulation processes. The practical utility of this technique was demonstrated through two applications, namely a capacitive sensor array with multi‐touch capability and flexible heaters that efficiently generate heat under deformation. This approach offers significant advantages, including nontoxicity, process simplicity, cost‐effectiveness, and high precision, thereby providing a versatile platform for next‐generation flexible electronic devices.

Experimental Section

Materials

Commercially available AgNPs (DGP 40LT‐15C; Advanced Nano Products) with an average particle diameter of 40 nm were used. The Ag NPs were not coated with a specific capping layer and were dispersed in an ethanol‐based solution containing triethylene glycol monomethyl ether (30–35 wt.%). The UV‐curable PSA film (AUG5 model, AMC) consisted of a polyolefin base film, a UV‐curable acrylic prepolymer PSA layer, and a PET release film. To prepare a 0.2 wt.% PTFE solution, PTFE powder (AF 1600, DuPont) was diluted with FC‐40 (KEM‐40, Kemis). PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) with a 10:1 base/curing‐agent ratio was used as a dielectric layer for the capacitive sensor array. Sandpaper (400 grit) coated with PTFE was used to create microstructures in the PDMS layer.

Preparation of the Pristine AgNP‐Coated Substrate

The glass substrates were cleaned via sequential ultrasonication in acetone, isopropyl alcohol, and deionized water for 15 min each, followed by nitrogen drying. To adjust the surface energy and form an interfacial fusion layer, a 0.2 wt.% PTFE solution was spin‐coated onto the glass substrate at 700 rpm for 40 s. Annealing was subsequently conducted at 70 °C for 5 min on a hot plate to evaporate the solvent, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 15 min to remove any residual solvent, and at 300 °C for 15 min to enhance adhesion to the substrate. Prior to coating with the AgNPs, air plasma treatment (50 W, 30 s) was performed to temporarily increase the surface energy. The AgNP solution was then spin‐coated at 1500 rpm for 45 s onto the plasma‐treated PTFE‐coated substrate, and the solvent instantly evaporated during the process.

Patterning Process Using the UV‐Curable PSA Film

The UV‐curable PSA film was exposed to UV light through a photomask with the desired pattern. This was performed using a mercury lamp (HBO 350 W/S, OSRAM) for an optimized duration of 55 s.[ 46 ] After UV exposure, the PET release film of the UV‐curable PSA film was removed, and the patterned film was attached to the AgNP‐coated substrate. A roller was used to apply a force of ≈20 N to ensure uniform contact between the film and the substrate. After attachment, the substrate was subjected to the first annealing step.

Two‐Step Annealing

The first annealing step was conducted on a hot plate at 200 °C for 1 min 30 s. This process enhanced the cohesion between the AgNPs, while also increasing the adhesion between the AgNPs and the PTFE‐coated substrate in the remaining region. The second annealing step was performed at 50 °C for 30 min, allowing the prepolymer adhesive to flow into the voids between the annealed AgNPs in the transfer region. After the second annealing step, the film was additionally exposed to UV irradiation for 55 s to photopolymerize the AgNP‐embedded adhesive. Finally, the UV‐curable PSA film was peeled off the substrate, resulting in a positive pattern of AgNPs on the substrate and a negative pattern on the PSA film.

Fabrication of the Flexible Heaters

The AgNP electrodes for the flexible heaters were fabricated using the proposed patterning technique. The UV‐curable PSA film was exposed to UV light through a patterned photomask using a mercury lamp for an optimized duration of 55 s. The first annealing step was performed by heating this substrate at 200 °C for 1 min 30 s. Subsequently, the patterned PSA film was attached to the first‐annealed AgNP‐coated substrate using a roller with a force of ≈20 N. The second annealing step was then performed at 50 °C for 30 min, followed by additional UV exposure for 55 s. Finally, grid‐patterned AgNP electrodes (negative pattern) were successfully formed on the UV‐curable PSA film by peeling it off the substrate.

Fabrication of the Capacitive Sensor Array

The AgNP electrodes for incorporation into a capacitive sensor array were fabricated simultaneously using the proposed patterning technique. For this purpose, a UV‐curable PSA film was exposed to UV light through a patterned photomask (mercury lamp) for an optimized duration of 55 s. The first annealing step was performed by heating this substrate at 200 °C for 1 min 30 s. Subsequently, the patterned PSA film was attached to the first‐annealed AgNP‐coated substrate using a roller with a force of ≈20 N. The second annealing step was then performed at 50 °C for 30 min, and additional UV exposure was conducted for 55 s. After irradiation, the AgNP electrodes (positive and negative patterns) for the capacitive sensor array were fabricated simultaneously on the UV‐curable PSA film and the substrate. A microstructured PDMS dielectric layer was subsequently formed between the positive AgNP pattern on the substrate and the negative AgNP pattern on the PSA film. To create the microstructures in this PDMS layer, PTFE‐coated sandpaper (400 grit) was prepared by spin‐coating a 0.2 wt.% PTFE solution at 700 rpm for 40 s, followed by heating at 70 °C for 15 min. The PDMS prepolymer was then spin‐coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s onto the positive AgNP pattern on the substrate. Subsequently, the sandpaper was placed on the PDMS prepolymer, followed by curing at 70 °C for 2 h. After peeling off the sandpaper, the negative pattern on the PSA film was placed on the microstructured PDMS dielectric layer. The detailed fabrication process is shown in Figure S12 (Supporting Information).

Characterization and Measurements

The surface morphologies and elemental compositions of the patterned AgNPs and microstructured PDMS specimens were characterized using field‐emission SEM (FE‐SEM, SU6600, Hitachi) and EDS (SU6600, Hitachi). SIMS (IMS 6F, Cameca) was used to analyze the interfacial fusion between the PTFE layer and the AgNPs after the first annealing step, and also to examine adhesive penetration into the AgNP layers after the second annealing step. The Tg of the photopolymerized non‐adhesive polymer in the PSA film was measured by TMA (Q400, TA Instruments), and the characteristics of the AgNP pattern, such as the CD and LER, were measured using optical microscopy (MX51, Olympus) and FE‐SEM, respectively, in combination with MATLAB‐based software. The line resistance of the AgNP pattern and the capacitance of the capacitive sensor array were measured using an LCR meter (4230 B, Wanyne Kerr). A direct current power supply (LP‐303TP, Digital) was used to apply voltages in the range of 1.5–5 V to the flexible heaters. The corresponding temperature changes were monitored in real time using an infrared thermal imaging camera (226GRD, Fortric) under various applied voltages. The force applied to the sensor was repeatedly loaded and unloaded using an SLA‐printed stamp attached to a load cell (DBBP‐20, BONGSHIN). The applied force was measured using the load cell equipped with a digital indicator (BS‐3520, BONGSHIN). The load cell was mounted on a motorized Z‐positioner with a displacement resolution of 5 µm, which moves in the Z‐axis direction to apply vertical force to the sensors. The output voltage was converted into a digital signal using a data acquisition board (DAQ, USB‐6216, National Instruments) and transferred to a PC for analysis. To measure the capacitance values of all 25 sensors in the capacitive sensor array, a multitouch measurement system was designed, incorporating an LCR meter and a scanning circuit. The scanning circuit enables each individual sensor in the array to be sequentially connected to the LCR meter via two orthogonally controlled multiplexers (CD4053BE, Texas Instruments) operated by a microcontroller unit (Mega, Arduino). The capacitances of the connected sensors were measured using the LCR meter and subsequently transmitted to a PC for analysis.
