Dual-pocket inhibition of Nav channels by the antiepileptic drug lamotrigine
Authors: Jian Huang, Xiao Fan, Xueqin Jin, Liming Teng, Nieng Yan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceDissecting the mechanism of action of clinical drugs on human Nav channels will aid future drug discovery for the treatment of neurological and psychiatric disorders. The high-resolution cryo-EM structures of Nav1.7 in complex with two medications, lamotrigine (LTG) and riluzole (RLZ), reveal the working mechanism of these drugs. While RLZ binds to a conventional site within the central cavity of the pore domain to directly obstruct ion conduction, LTG observes a dual-pocket inhibition mode that is reminiscent of lacosamide and cannabidiol on Nav channels. One determinant for subtype-specific inhibition of Nav1.8 by the LTG-related linear 6,6 series was identified via structure-guided docking and mutational analysis. These studies have afforded an advanced understanding of the structural pharmacology of Nav channels.

Voltage-gated sodium (Nav) channels are critical for various physiological processes such as muscle contraction, sensory perception, and neurotransmitter release (1–4). These channels are composed of a pore-forming α subunit, including an ion-conducting pore domain (PD) and four voltage-sensing domains (VSDs), as well as auxiliary β subunits that regulate channel function and localization (5, 6). Dysfunction of Nav channels has been implicated in a variety of cardiovascular, neurological, and psychiatric disorders, such as epilepsy, arrhythmias, neuropathic pain, and autism (7–12). Consequently, a variety of Nav inhibitors have been developed and clinically used as local anesthetics, antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), antiarrhythmics, and pain killers (13–17). However, these agents are often of moderate potency and relatively nonselective across different Nav subtypes, leading to unwanted side effects (18, 19). In recent years, Nav pharmacology has shifted toward developing new-generation blockers with improved efficacy and subtype selectivity (20, 21).

Lamotrigine (LTG), sold under the brand name Lamictal®, is an FDA-approved medication used as a first-line treatment for epilepsy and bipolar disorder (22–24). Riluzole (RLZ), also known as Rilutek®, is another medication prescribed to individuals with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and other motor neuron diseases (25). Despite extensive studies of LTG and RLZ as nonselective Nav blockers, the binding details of these drugs to Nav channels remain unclear (22, 26). Reasoning that high-resolution structures of the Nav channel in complex with these clinical drugs would provide insight into the chemical basis underlying their mechanism of action (MOA) and aid in the development of next-generation AEDs and pain killers, we employed the Nav1.7 channel complex, in the presence of the β1 and β2 subunits, as a scaffold for cryo-EM analyses of the MOA of representative Nav-targeting drugs (6, 27).

Results

Cryo-EM Analysis of Nav1.7 Treated with Riluzole or Lamotrigine.

In our preliminary trials, we attempted to include RLZ and LTG throughout the purification process. While the protein behavior and cryosample quality appeared to be suitable for structural analysis in the presence of 1 mM LTG (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), that with RLZ was significantly compromised as assessed from the size exclusion chromatography profiles. Therefore, we incubated RLZ with the purified Nav1.7 complex right before sample vitrification (28). Following a well-established protocol for purification and cryo-EM analysis (6, 27), we obtained three-dimensional (3D) EM reconstruction of Nav1.7 in the presence of RLZ at a resolution of 2.9 Å (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Table S1), wherein a density that can be well fitted with the drug molecule was evidently discernible in the central cavity of the PD (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This structure will be referred to as Nav1.7-RLZ.

We also used the same sample preparation procedure for LTG by incubating the protein sample with a range of titrated LTG concentrations, including 0.1 mM, 1 mM, and 3 mM, prior to cryosample preparation. However, no density corresponding to LTG could be found in any of these reconstructions. Nevertheless, their overall conformation and local features at the central cavity and intracellular gate align with the previously reported apo-state structure (EMD-32368, SI Appendix, Fig. S3A) (27). Of note, pooling the three titration datasets for 3D reconstruction improved the resolution of apo-like Nav1.7 to 2.1 Å (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This high-resolution structure, which will be referred to as Nav1.7-Apo, serves as a reference for analysis of drug binding.

Considering the moderate potency of LTG on Nav channels (22), the lack of LTG binding might be attributed to the interference of detergents during protein extraction and purification. We thus included LTG at a concentration of 1 mM throughout the entire purification process. In the final 3D EM reconstruction at 2.7 Å resolution, additional densities that can be assigned to LTG were unambiguously identified (SI Appendix, Figs. S1–S4 and Table S1). This structure will be referred to as Nav1.7-LTG.

LTG Molecules at Site C and Site BIG.

Compared to the EM maps of our present and previously reported Nav1.7-Apo (EMDB: EMD-32368) (27), two additional densities were observed in the 3D EM reconstruction of Nav1.7-LTG, one situated in the central cavity of PD and the other beneath the intracellular gate (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4A). Following our recently proposed nomenclature for druggable sites on Nav channels (29), these two sites will be described as site C and site BIG, respectively. While the central cavity represents a conventional residence for multiple drugs in different channels (30, 31), site BIG was only recently identified with high-resolution cryo-EM structures to be a common receptor site for various clinically used drugs, including bupivacaine (BPV), lacosamide (LCM), and carbamazepine (CBZ) (29).

There is only one RLZ in the 3D EM reconstruction, located at site C. It inhibits the channel activity by directly blocking ion flow (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). We will hereafter mainly focus our analysis on the Nav1.7-LTG complex to elaborate on its dual-pocket inhibition mechanism. To simplify the depiction, we will use LTG-1 and LTG-2 to refer to the drug molecules bound to site BIG and site C, respectively.

Site BIG Correlates with α→π Transition on the S6IV Segment upon LTG Binding.

The structures of Nav1.7 in the presence of LTG or RLZ both represent an inactivated state with a closed intracellular gate. However, there are minor conformational differences between these two structures. Whereas the S6IV segment in Nav1.7-RLZ retains a conformation nearly identical to the apo state, LTG induces an α→π transition in the middle of the S6IV beginning from Val1751. In addition, slight inward movements of S6II and S6III contribute to a tighter gate and the formation of site BIG (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Red arrows indicate the structural rearrangement of S6IV in the presence of LTG. Representative S6IV residues that undergo conformational changes are shown as sticks. (B) Coordination of LTG-1 at site BIG. Surrounding residues within a 5-Å cutoff distance from LTG-1 are shown as sticks.

LTG-1 is primarily surrounded by hydrophobic cytosolic residues on the S6 tetrahelical bundles at site BIG, including Leu398, Ala402, Met403, and Glu406 from S6I, Leu964, Leu967, and Leu968 from S6II, Ile1457 and Asn1461 from S6III, and Leu1760 from S6IV (Fig. 2B). By occupying site BIG, LTG tightens a closed intracellular gate of the channel.

Different Binding Poses of LTG and RLZ to Site C.

LTG-2 and RLZ both bind to site C, situated below the selectivity filter (SF). But they display different binding poses. Whereas RLZ is coordinated primarily through hydrophobic interactions, the recognition of LTG-2 involves more polar interactions (Fig. 3A). LTG belongs to the linear 6,6 series, all of which contain two directly linked six-membered aromatic rings. The entire molecule of LTG-2 is positioned toward repeats III and IV. Its phenyl ring is nestled in a hydrophobic pocket enclosed by residues Phe391, Ser1697, Phe1748, Val1751, and Val1752 (Fig. 3B). The unique triazine ring of LTG-2, with two substituted amido groups, is anchored by Lys1406, Ser1445, and the backbone carbonyl group of Ala1403 through hydrogen bonds (Fig. 3B).

LTG-2 and RLZ, shown as spheres, are colored magenta and blue, respectively. (B) Coordination of LTG-2 in the central cavity. RLZ’s core benzothiazole ring is embedded in a pocket similar to LTG-2’s phenyl ring, surrounded by residues Gln360, Phe391, Thr1404, Thr1695, Thr1696, Ser1697, Ile1744, and Phe1748 (Fig. 3C). Although they adopt distinct binding poses, LTG-2 and RLZ are both recognized by Phe1748, a conserved residue that has been reported to engage in local anesthetic (LA) coordination (22).

Structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies on LTG derivatives.

Over the past decades, numerous linear 6,6 series have been developed as potential antiepileptic and antihyperalgesic drug candidates, using LTG as a starting skeleton. Chemical modifications to the heterocyclic core and aryl substitution patterns have led to compounds with enhanced potency, such as the pyrimidine BW-4030W92 (22), and improved subtype selectivity on Nav channels, such as the N-methyl amide PF-01247324 (Fig. 4A) (33). To investigate the structure–activity relationships (SARs) of these linear 6,6 series on Nav channels, we employed in silico molecular docking simulations based on the structure of the Nav1.7-LTG complex. Due to the internal conformational changes upon LTG binding, we first carried out homology modeling to reconstruct the 3D model of Nav1.8 to ensure the proper docking results.

(E) The Nav1.8-unique residue Gly1406, which is replaced by Ser in other subtypes, accounts for Nav1.8’s higher sensitivity to PF-01247324. Swapping the corresponding residues between Nav1.8 and Nav1.7 alters these channels’ subtype-specific sensitivity to PF-01247324 accordingly.

The binding poses of these linear 6,6 series appear nearly identical on different Nav subtypes at site C, but slight differences arise from specific chemical modifications. In the Nav1.7-LTG structure, an additional hydrophobic pocket next to the triazine ring is constituted by residues Leu1449, Ile1453 from S6III, and Phe1748 and Val1752 from S6IV (Fig. 4 B and C). This pocket is occupied by the monofluoromethyl group of BW-4030W92, consistent with its more than 20-fold potency increase on Nav1.7 than LTG (Fig. 4D) (15). For PF-01247324, the N-methyl amide group fits well within site C in Nav1.8. However, the spatial compatibility would be compromised in other subtypes due to a spatial clash with Ser, which occupies the corresponding locus of Gly1406 in Nav1.8 (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that the Nav1.8-unique residue Gly1406 could be a determinant for Nav1.8-selective recognition of PF-01247324.

To test this hypothesis, IC50 values of PF-01247324 against human Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 variants were measured using whole-cell patch-clamp electrophysiological recordings. While PF-01247324 inhibited Nav1.8-WT with an IC50 value of 0.55 ± 0.09 μM, its potency on Nav1.8-G1406S was reduced by approximately 50-fold, with an IC50 of 27.69 ± 4.93 μM. Of particular note, introducing S1445G to Nav1.7 increased the inhibitory efficiency of PF-01247324 from 24.89 ± 1.93 μM to 0.23 ± 0.03 μM, reaching a level nearly identical to that against Nav1.8-WT.

These characterizations confirmed the importance of Gly1406 in Nav1.8 as a key residue for maintaining selectivity. It is noted that we previously found Gly1406 to be a determinant for the subtype-specific inhibition of Nav1.8 by A-803467 (34). However, the direct spatial effect on the selectivity of PF-01247324 recognition observed in this study differs from the allosteric effect on A-803467 sensitivity (34).

Discussion

Pharmacology targeting Nav channels has proved to be of enormous value for treating a wide range of pathogenic conditions, and the development of new drugs targeting Nav channels is an active area of research in medicinal chemistry (2, 14, 20). One particular challenge in drug discovery is to precisely target specific subtypes or states of Nav channels to minimize unintended off-target effects. We aim to grapple with this challenge by elucidating high-resolution structures of various human Nav channels bound to prototypical drugs.

Our present study reveals the different Nav inhibition mechanisms by LTG and RLZ. Unlike RLZ, which directly blocks the pore domain through binding to the central cavity, LTG inhibits Nav channels through a dual-pocket mechanism. The α→π transition on the S6IV segment, along with local conformational changes, facilitates binding of LTG to site BIG. In addition, its binding pose at site C provides atomic details for understanding the structure–activity relationships of linear 6,6 series on different Nav channels with increased efficacy and specificity. Our identification of Gly1406's role in maintaining the selectivity of the linear 6,6 series against Nav1.8 here via direct spatial compatibility and previously through allosteric effect (34) highlights the significance of this locus in developing subtype-specific Nav blockers.

The recent structural advances of eukaryotic Nav channels interacting with clinically used drugs unveil the remarkable diversity of drug binding sites on Nav channels (29). Unexpectedly, a dual-pocket inhibition pattern emerges as a common mechanism among different types of Nav-targeting compounds with diverse chemical skeletons. Lacosamide (LCM), another AED, and cannabidiol (CBD), one major nonpsychoactive component isolated from cannabis, are also found to impede Nav channels through two distinct binding sites. Similar to LTG, two molecules of LCM bind to the PD of Nav, one at site C and the other at site BIG (29). CBD stabilizes the channels in their inactivated states by both occupying one fenestration (site F) and strengthening binding of the fast inactivation motif (site I) (35). Our findings not only explain the molecular basis for the distinct MOAs of various clinical and investigational drugs but also establish the framework for structure-based rational design of the next-generation AEDs and nonaddictive painkillers with improved efficacy and selectivity.

Materials and Methods

Transient Expression of Human Nav1.7-β1-β2 Complexes in HEK293F Cells.

The procedure for expressing human Nav1.7-β1-β2 complexes follows a similar protocol as described in our recent study (35). Briefly, the cDNAs encoding human Nav1.7 (Uniprot Q15858), β1 subunit (Uniprot Q07699), and β2 subunit (Uniprot O60939) were separately incorporated into the pCAG vector. For Nav1.7, the amino terminus was combined with Twin-Strep and Flag tags in a tandem arrangement. HEK293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R79007) were maintained in SMM 293T-II medium (Sino Biological Inc.) at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. A plasmid mixture containing 1.5 mg for Nav1.7 and 0.5 mg each for both β1 and β2 was preincubated with 4 mg polyethylenimines (Polysciences) and then added into a one-liter cell culture with a cell density of ~2.0 × 106 cells per mL to facilitate the transient expression.

Protein Purification of Human Nav1.7 Bound to Different Drugs.

For the Nav1.7-LTG complex.

Following a similar protocol (35), a total of 20 L cells were collected around 48 h after transfection via centrifugation (3,600 g, 10 min) and then resuspended in Tris-HCl buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 and 150 mM NaCl) with additional 1 mΜ lamotrigine (MCE) and protease inhibitor cocktail (Selleckchem). The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 45 min before adding n-dodecyl-β-D-maltopyranoside (DDM) and cholesteryl hemisuccinate Tris salt (CHS) to final concentrations of 1% and 0.1% (w/v). Following an additional 2-h incubation at 4 °C, the supernatant collected by centrifugation (16,000 g, 45 min) was incubated with anti-Flag M2 resin (Sigma) for affinity-based purification. The resin was washed five times with buffer W (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, protease inhibitor cocktail, 0.06% GDN, and 1 mΜ lamotrigine) and eluted using buffer W supplemented with an additional 0.2 mg mL−1 Flag peptide. The eluent was applied to Strep-Tactin Sepharose resin (IBA) and allowed to flow through via gravity. After washing by buffer W, the target proteins were eluted using buffer W with an additional 2.5 mM desthiobiotin (IBA) and concentrated for size-exclusion chromatography using pre-equilibrated Superose 6 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mΜ lamotrigine, and 0.02% GDN. The peak fractions were pooled and mixed with an additional 2 mM lamotrigine for another 45-min incubation at 4 °C and then concentrated for making cryogirds.

For the Nav1.7-RLZ complex.

21 L transfected HEK293F cells were used for purifying the apo Nav1.7 protein using a similar protocol to the one used for preparing the Nav1.7-LTG complex, except that no drug was included during the purification process. The concentrated apo Nav1.7 protein was subjected to a 45-min incubation at 4 °C with 350 μM riluzole (MCE) prior to making cryogrids.

For the Nav1.7-LTG titration.

The concentrated apo Nav1.7 protein was subjected to incubation at 4 °C for 45 min with lamotrigine (MCE) concentrations of 0.1 mM, 1 mM, or 3 mM, before making cryogrids. To simplify the illustration, the titration samples will be referred to as Nav1.7-0.1LTG, Nav1.7-1LTG, and Nav1.7-3LTG, respectively.

Cryo-EM Sample Preparation and Data Acquisition.

QuantiFoil Au (R1.2/1.3 300 mesh, Quantifoil) and UltrAuFoil (R1.2/1.3 300 mesh, Quantifoil) grids underwent glow discharge using easiGlow (PELCO) at 15 mA for 15 s under a pressure of 0.37 mBar. A protein sample of 3 μL was initially placed on the grid’s surface in the Vitrobot Mark IV chamber (maintained at 10 °C with 100% humidity). Subsequently, it was blotted for a duration of 4 s before being rapidly frozen in liquid ethane. Cryogrids were transferred to a 300-kV Titan Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher) for automated data collection by EPU (Thermo Fisher). Movies in EER format were captured using the Falcon IV camera (Thermo Fisher) operating in EC mode, with a nominal magnification set at 75,000× and a pixel size of 1.036 Å. Movies were subjected to a 6-s exposure, accumulating a total electron dose of approximately 40 e−/Å2. Subsequently, the movie stacks were aligned, dose-weighted, and averaged in cryoSPARC live (36).

Cryo-EM Data Processing.

In total, 7,530/2,295 cryo-EM micrographs in EER format were collected for the Nav1.7-LTG and Nav1.7-RLZ complexes, respectively. Patched CTF estimation was performed during cryoSPARC live preprocessing. For the Nav1.7-LTG dataset, 62,290 particles from 50 micrographs were picked to generate 2D class averaged templates. In total, 9,341,066 particles were picked by the selected 2D templates with template picker. With 3 split subsets of bin-4 particles, 2D classification (K = 100) was performed to select good classes. A total of 1,473,664 particles were re-extracted in bin-2 and applied to heterogeneous refinement (K = 2), following by 2D classification for each class. After removing duplicates, 414,490 particles were extracted without binning. Three rounds of heterogeneous refinement were performed with higher frequency cutoff. The best 294,629 particles after the last step were sent to nonuniform refinement and yielded a 2.7 Å reconstruction of Nav1.7-LTG complex. The same processing workflow was also applied to the Nav1.7-RLZ dataset, which generated a reconstruction at a resolution of 2.9 Å from 125,461 final particles. Local resolutions were estimated in cryoSPARC in BlocRes mode.

For Nav1.7-LTG titration series, 5,553/7,258/7,262 EER movies were collected for Nav1.7-0.1LTG, Nav1.7-1LTG, and Nav1.7-3LTG, respectively. Following the standard processing workflow described above, resolutions of 2.5 Å/2.4 Å/2.4 Å have been reached for Nav1.7-0.1LTG/Nav1.7-1LTG/Nav1.7-3LTG with 248,024/384,591/370,254 final particles. The merged 20,073 movies from three subsets were reprocessed from motion correction with twofold EER upsampling and Fourier space cropping (0.75 F-crop factor) in cryoSPARC, yielding a updated pixel size of 0.6907 Å for each summed micrograph. Similar data processing workflow as above with more rounds of heterogeneous refinement was performed before the final NU refinement. The final 2.1 Å reconstruction was generated from 1,557,991 particles.

Model Building and Refinement.

The model building of Nav1.7-LTG and Nav1.7-RLZ applied apo-state Nav1.7 (PDB: 7W9K) as the initial model. This initial model was positioned and saved according to the obtained reconstruction in UCSF Chimera (32). The adjusted model was tuned manually using COOT (37). Subsequently, refinement against the relevant map was conducted using PHENIX's Real-space Refinement tool (38). Information regarding the statistics in model refinement can be found in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Homolog Modeling and Molecular Docking Simulations.

The human Nav1.8 model under an appropriate state for linear 6,6 series binding was constructed based on the structure of Nav1.7 bound to LTG on the SWISS-MODEL web server (39). Following this, the docking of two small molecules BW-4030W92 and PF-01247324 against Nav1.7 or the Nav1.8 model was performed using Schrödinger Suite 2018-1. The LigPrep program (40) with the OPLS3 force field (41) was employed to generate and refine the initial 3D configurations of the small molecules. Default settings were used for processing the protein structures of the Nav1.7-LTG complex and the generated Nav1.8 model. Molecular docking simulations were then performed with the Glide program in an extra precision mode (Glide XP), and the most favorable poses were selected for further analysis.

Whole-Cell Electrophysiology.

The protocols we used were similar to the ones in the previous publication with minor modifications (34). Nav1.8 cannot be well recorded in HEK293T cells due to small peak currents. In our study, Nav1.8 variants were all recorded in ND-7/23 cells (National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures). As ND-7/23 cells have a high background of endogenous TTX-sensitive Na+ currents, Nav1.7 variants were recorded in HEK293T cells.

Human Nav1.7 or Nav1.8 variants were transfected into HEK293T cells or ND7/23 cells together with eGFP using lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

Whole-cell voltage clamp recordings were obtained using an EPC10-USB amplifier with Patchmaster software v2*90.2 (HEKA Elektronic), digitized at a rate of 50 kHz after passing through a low-pass Bessel filter setting of 3 kHz. The extracellular solution contained the following (in mM): 140 NaCl, 4 KCl, 10 Hepes, 10 D-glucose, 1 MgCl2, and 1.5 CaCl2, pH 7.4, with NaOH. 400 nM TTX was added to the bath solution during recording of the ND-7/23 cells in order to completely block endogenous TTX-sensitive sodium current. The pipette solution contained the following (in mM): 105 CsF, 40 CsCl, 10 NaCl, 1 EGTA, and 10 Hepes, pH 7.4, with CsOH. Patch pipettes had a resistance of 2–4 MΩ when filled with pipette solution.

To determine inhibition of the inactivated state by PF-012473248, cells were held at −70 mV (for Nav1.7 variants) or −60 mV (for Nav1.8 variants). This was followed by a recovery step to −120 mV for 20 ms and a test pulse to 0 mV for 50 ms. Drugs were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The final concentration of DMSO was less than 0.1%. PF-012473248 with different concentrations perfused to the recording chamber for up to 10 min to get to the maximal inhibition using a multichannel perfusion system (VM8, ALA). Recording was initiated for 5 min equilibration period to establish stable peak current after achieving whole-cell configuration and then drug perfusion. Concentration–response curve was fitted with Y = Bottom + (Top - Bottom)/(1 + 10^((LogIC50-X) * Hill Slope)), where IC50 is the concentration of PF-012473248 that blocks 50% of the current and X is log of indicated concentration, and Hill Slope is slope factor. All electrophysiological data were analyzed using Origin (OriginLab) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Results are presented as mean ± SEM, and n is the number of experimental cells. Statistical significance was assessed using the extra-sum-of-squares F test.
