A probabilistic detection-based approach to skin and freckle segmentation
Authors: Yeong-Su Lim, Myeong Jin Ju, Hee-Jae Jeon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate freckle segmentation is essential for dermatological assessments and cosmetic applications, but existing lesion detection techniques are primarily designed for well-defined skin abnormalities such as melanomas and tumors, making them less effective at capturing subtle features like freckles. In this study, we present an automated freckle segmentation framework that integrates the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) and the Viola-Jones algorithm for skin segmentation, coupled with an energy map-based approach for freckle detection. The process begins with image is clustered using GMM, followed by facial region detection with the Viola-Jones algorithms. A post-processing step then segments the selection of the skin region. Subsequently, an energy map is generated by combining the blue and saturation channels, while Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) and morphological operations enhance freckle contrast. The final segmentation is achieved through binarization and additional post-processing techniques. Quantitative evaluations demonstrate that the proposed method surpasses conventional approaches in recall, Intersection over Union (IoU), and Dice coefficient, highlighting its effectiveness in accurate freckle detection and segmentation. These findings indicate that, with further refinement, the proposed framework holds significant potential for applications in both clinical dermatology and cosmetic science.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20275-4.

Introduction

Accurate assessment and analysis of skin diseases and pigmentation play a crucial role in evaluating facial features and appearance, making them important research topics in dermatology and the cosmetics industry1–4 Facial skin lesions are of significant interest due to their dual impact on both medical diagnostics and aesthetic considerations. However, detecting and classifying these lesions is inherently challenging because of their high variability in presentation5,6. They can differ greatly in color, shape, and texture among individuals, and their subtle nature often makes it difficult to distinguish from the surrounding skin. Additionally, low contrast between lesions and skin tone, coupled with variations in lighting conditions, further complicates accurate identification7.

Traditional skin lesion diagnosis methods include visual inspection by physicians and histopathological examination8–10. While visual assessment remains a widely used approach11,12it is inherently subjective, leading to variations in diagnostic outcomes among examiners. Moreover, small lesions often blend with the surrounding skin due to subtle color differences, making them prone to misdiagnosis or oversight. Histopathological examination, though more definitive, is an invasive procedure that requires significant time and cost, limiting its practicality for routine screenings. To overcome these limitations, automated skin lesion diagnosis techniques utilizing computer vision and artificial intelligence have been actively studied to enhance accuracy, efficiency, and consistency in lesion detection9. As an image processing approach for skin lesion segmentation13–15freckle detection and segmentation have been explored through methods that integrate texture, shape, and color-based features to improve detection accuracy and enhance cosmetic applications16. In parallel, research on melanocytic lesion segmentation has leveraged techniques such as hierarchical K-means clustering17.

Recently, with the growing popularity of deep learning-based techniques18studies on skin lesion classification have been conducted using convolutional neural networks such as VGG Net, DenseNet, ResNet, EfficientNet, and XceptionNet19–23. Although these advancements have significantly improved lesion detection, research specifically focusing on freckles remains limited. Existing skin lesion detection techniques are suitable for segmenting large and well-defined lesions such as melanomas and tumors, but they struggle to accurately detect finer details like freckles and pigmentation24. Furthermore, most studies focus on lesion detection using magnified images, whereas research on detecting freckles across the entire face is relatively scarce. Deep learning-based approaches also require large amounts of annotated training data and involve high computational costs, making them less accessible for practical applications in freckle segmentation.

To address these challenges, this study proposes an automated method for full-face freckle detection and segmentation. By integrating probabilistic modeling and feature-based image processing techniques, the proposed approach aims to improve segmentation accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. This method enhances the visibility of freckles through an energy map-based contrast enhancement process and employs refined skin segmentation to remove non-skin regions, ensuring more precise freckle detection. The full process of the proposed method is demonstrated in Supplementary Figure S1, Video 1. The proposed method demonstrates clear advantages over color-based segmentation, as evidenced by improvements in key performance metrics including Recall, IoU, and the Dice coefficient. This indicates that our approach significantly improves the accuracy and robustness of freckle segmentation, paving the way for its potential application in both clinical dermatology and cosmetic analysis.

Performance evaluation confusion matrix analysis

To further evaluate the classification performance presented in Fig. 2a and d, we analyzed the confusion matrices and derived key performance metrics, including Accuracy, Recall, and Precision, to quantitatively assess the model’s classification ability. Accuracy represents the proportion of correctly classified pixels across the entire dataset, providing an overall measure of model performance. Recall measures the proportion of actual freckles that were correctly identified, while precision indicates the proportion of predicted freckles that were truly freckles. These metrics are crucial in determining how well the model distinguishes between positive and negative cases, highlighting both its strengths and areas for improvement. The results indicate that Accuracy remains consistently high, ranging from 0.9665 to 0.9863, suggesting a strong overall prediction capability. However, recall values range from 0.3981 to 0.5592, indicating that the model tends to miss a considerable portion of actual positive cases. Additionally, Precision values range from 0.2597 to 0.5862, reflecting a relatively low proportion of correctly predicted positive cases among all positive predictions. These findings suggest that while the model maintains high accuracy, there is a need to optimize the balance between recall and precision. A low recall indicates that the model may overlook actual positive cases, whereas a low precision suggests a higher likelihood of false positive predictions. Potential improvements may include adjusting decision thresholds, enhancing feature selection techniques, or integrating ensemble learning approaches.

Discussions

This study proposes an effective freckle detection method by integrating the GMM and the Viola-Jones algorithm for facial region segmentation while utilizing an energy map derived from the blue channel and saturation values. The experimental results demonstrated improvements in Recall, IoU, and Dice coefficient compared to conventional color-based segmentation methods, indicating that the proposed approach effectively captured a larger proportion of freckles. However, precision remained relatively unchanged, suggesting an increased number of false positives. This is likely due to the method’s emphasis on maximizing freckle candidate detection, which, while beneficial for ensuring high sensitivity, also leads to the inclusion of non-freckle regions that share similar characteristics. Moreover, certain freckles were difficult to segment due to minimal differences in color and texture from the surrounding skin, highlighting a fundamental limitation of purely color-based segmentation techniques.

To address these challenges, future research should focus on refining the segmentation process by integrating additional post-processing techniques and feature extraction methods. Techniques such as morphological filtering, texture-based feature enhancement could improve the specificity of freckle detection and reduce false positives. Additionally, the current method’s performance may vary under different lighting conditions and skin tone variations, necessitating further studies to enhance its robustness across diverse populations. Investigating adaptive thresholding strategies or employing illumination normalization techniques could mitigate variations caused by inconsistent lighting. Beyond these technical refinements, this study faces a fundamental limitation: it relied on a publicly available, non-clinical dataset. Future validation with large-scale clinical datasets and collaboration with dermatologists will be essential to confirm its real-world efficacy. Following clinical validation, this tool could be used in clinical practice to enhance diagnostic objectivity through quantitative skin analysis and to streamline patient monitoring via systematic tracking of treatment response. Such validation will also substantiate its role as a diagnostic aid and guide algorithm refinements for deployment in both clinical and cosmetic settings.

Methods

This study proposes a method for automatically detecting and segmenting freckles across the entire face. To achieve this, we combine a GMM with the Viola–Jones algorithm to segment the facial skin region, followed by freckle detection using an energy-map–based analysis. We constructed a dataset of frontal, full-face color photographs sourced from Freepik (https://www.freepik.com) and Pexels (https://www.pexels.com). The dataset comprises 8 images from distinct individuals with clearly visible facial freckles across diverse skin tones and lighting conditions. All images are JPG files in the RGB color format. The resolutions range from a minimum of 1920 × 2880 to a maximum of 7113 × 4852. Inclusion criteria were a complete frontal face within the frame (forehead, both eyes, nose, both cheeks, mouth, and chin) and the presence of identifiable freckles. Exclusion criteria were AI-generated images, severe occlusions, excessive makeup/filters, duplicates, or low quality images. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, GMM is used to perform clustering based on the color of the input image, while the Viola-Jones algorithm is applied to detect the facial region. Subsequently, skin labels are extracted within the detected facial region, and a post-processing step is applied to refine the skin region. Skin segmentation serves as a preprocessing step for freckle detection, restricting the ROI to the skin, thereby improving the accuracy of freckle detection. Freckle detection involves generating an energy map by combining the blue channel and the saturation channel, followed by applying CLAHE and Blackhat operation to enhance freckle contrast. Subsequently, threshold-based binarization and morphological post-processing are applied to filter candidate freckle regions. The proposed method demonstrated improved performance in terms of Recall, IoU, and Dice coefficient compared to conventional color-based segmentation techniques, contributing to enhanced accuracy in freckle detection.

Viola-Jones algorithm

The Viola-Jones algorithm utilizes feature extraction based on Haar-like features, classifier training through the AdaBoost algorithm, and a cascade structure for computational optimization26. The AdaBoost algorithm constructs a strong classifier by combining multiple weak classifiers, where each weak classifier is trained on a single Haar-like feature. During training, the weights of the weak classifiers are adjusted to enhance overall classification performance. The cascade structure arranges strong classifiers in a sequential manner, employing a staged approach that enhances face detection speed while reducing unnecessary computations on non-face regions. In this study, a pre-trained Haar Cascade Classifier was used for frontal face detection in the face detection stage. The detected face regions were utilized to effectively define the Region of Interest (ROI), which was subsequently applied in the skin segmentation process.

In the Label Extraction stage, a post-processing procedure is performed to extract only the facial skin region based on the background-removed mask image. As illustrated in Fig. 6, this process consists of three sequential steps. First, the opening operation is applied to the mask image. This morphological transformation consists of erosion and dilation, where erosion reduces small noise while dilation restores the Main structures of the objects. This step effectively removes small unwanted regions while preserving the overall shape of the face. In the second step, connected component labeling is performed. This process identifies groups of connected pixels as distinct regions and assigns a unique label to each region. After labeling, the area of each region is calculated, and regions smaller than 500 pixels are removed. By removing regions that were not removed during the opening operation, this step allows for a more precise selection of the facial skin region. In the third step, the width is reduced to 20% of the original size, and the Height is expanded 120% downward, based on the center of the original bounding box. Only the labels within this region are retained, generating the final mask image that includes only the facial skin region.

To generate the energy map, the segmented skin image is converted into both the RGB and HSV color spaces. Thresholding and post-processing for freckle segmentation

As shown in Fig. 8, CLAHE is first applied to the energy map to maximize the energy difference between the skin and freckles. CLAHE divides the image into small tiles and performs histogram equalization within each tile27. This method enhances contrast locally for each tile while preventing excessive contrast enhancement using a predefined clip limit. In this study, the tile size was set as the quotient of the larger value between the image Height and width divided by 100, and the clip limit was set to 2.

Thresholding is then applied to binarize the image, and morphological properties are used to remove non-freckle regions, resulting in the final freckle mask.

Through the binarization process, skin and background pixels are removed, representing freckle candidate regions. Even after the binarization process, noise or non-freckle objects may still be present. To filter these out, Connected Component Labeling is applied to compute the area and perimeter of each region. The area is defined as the number of pixels in the region, while the perimeter is calculated as the number of pixels along the region’s boundary. Additionally, circularity and concavity are computed to analyze the shape characteristics of the regions. These two metrics are calculated as follows.11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}Circularity=\frac{4\pi\:\times\:Area}{Perimeter},\:\:Concavity=\frac{Convex\:Hull\:Area-Area}{Area}\end{array}$$\end{document}

Circularity approaches 1 as the region becomes more circular, while concavity represents the difference in area between the convex hull and the actual region. Based on the calculated geometric metrics, regions with an area smaller than 1/100 of the image Height in pixels are considered noise and filtered out. Additionally, regions with a circularity value below 0.2 are regarded as having complex shapes and are removed, while regions with a concavity exceeding 0.5 are considered irregular compared to their convex hull and are also removed. As a result, the filtered binary mask represents only the regions that can be considered freckles on the face.

Evaluation of metrics

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, accuracy, recall, precision, IoU, and the Dice coefficient (Dice) were used. Accuracy represents the proportion of correctly predicted samples out of the entire dataset, indicating how well the model performs in overall prediction. Recall measures the proportion of actual positive samples that are correctly identified as positive, while precision represents the proportion of predicted positive samples that are truly positive. Each metric is calculated as follows.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}Accuracy=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}\:,\:\:Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\:\:Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}\end{array}$$\end{document}

True Positive (TP) refers to cases where actual positive samples are correctly predicted as positive, while True Negative (TN) refers to cases where actual negative samples are correctly predicted as negative. False Positive (FP) refers to cases where actual negative samples are incorrectly predicted as positive, and False Negative (FN) refers to cases where actual positive samples are incorrectly predicted as negative. IoU is a metric that measures the ratio of the intersection between the predicted and actual regions. Dice is a harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, emphasizing the intersection to better evaluate the segmentation performance of the model.
