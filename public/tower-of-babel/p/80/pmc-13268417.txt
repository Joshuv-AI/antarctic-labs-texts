Expression of p63, CD10, and Ki-67 in giant cell tumor of bone: A diagnostic immunohistochemical study
Authors: Heba M. K. Youssef, Abdulkarim Hasan, Abd El Rahman M. Sharfeldeen, Abdulhadi Samman, Dahlia S. Mirdad, Mohammed S. Abdelwahed, Hussain Noorwali, Eman Hassan Ibrahim, Mostafa Mahmoud Youssef Mohamed, Ahmed Rabie, Mahmoud Samir, Hussein Eldamery, Assem Amer, Ashraf E. M. Ebaid, Marwa M. El-Mosely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) is a locally aggressive tumor driven by neoplastic mononuclear stromal cells. Stromal immunophenotyping may support diagnosis in giant cell-rich mimics and provide insight into tumor biology. Archival formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue samples from 31 GCTB cases diagnosed between 2015 and 2025 were studied and reviewed by consultant pathologists on-site and remotely to confirm the diagnosis and assess stromal atypia and mitoses. Immunohistochemistry for p63, cluster of differentiation 10 (CD10), and Ki-67 was performed and interpreted in stromal cells only; p63 and CD10 expression were categorized as low or high, and Ki-67 labeling index (LI) was grouped using a 10% cutoff. Available clinical variables included age, sex, anatomical site, tumor size, and recurrent-case status. Recurrent-case status was based on documentation at presentation or referral, not on prospectively confirmed recurrence. The mean age was 42.5 ± 15.4 years; 16/31 cases (51.6%) were female. Recurrent-case status was recorded in 14/31 cases (45.2%). High p63 and CD10 expression were observed in 20/31 cases (64.5%) and 22/31 cases (71.0%), respectively. In univariate analysis, CD10-high expression was associated with recurrent-case status (P = .021), while p63-high expression showed a borderline association (P = .057). Ki-67 LI was not associated with recurrent-case status (P = .389). p63 expression correlated significantly with CD10 expression (P = .003) and with higher Ki-67 LI. Stromal p63 and CD10 were commonly expressed in GCTB. CD10-high expression was associated with recurrent-case status in univariate analysis, but this exploratory finding should not be interpreted as recurrence prediction.

1. Introduction

Giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) is classified by the World Health Organization as an intermediate, locally aggressive primary bone tumor.[1,2] It most often affects young adults and typically arises in the epiphyseal-metaphyseal region of long bones, particularly around the knee. Despite its historically “benign” designation, GCTB may be locally destructive, producing extensive osteolysis, pain, swelling, deformity, joint dysfunction, and pathologic fractures.[2,3] Achieving local control is challenging because treatment must balance oncologic clearance with functional preservation. Intralesional curettage, often combined with local adjuvants and cementation, is widely used but is associated with a non-negligible risk of local recurrence, and the risk may increase with factors such as extraosseous or soft-tissue extension.[4,5] Accordingly, there remains interest in pathology-based parameters that may help characterize tumor biology alongside surgical and radiologic considerations.

Biologically, GCTB is composed of 2 principal cell populations: a mononuclear stromal cell component that represents the neoplastic driver, and abundant osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells that are reactive and recruited through osteoclastogenic signaling. This stromal–osteoclast axis explains the characteristic osteolytic behavior and focuses attention on stromal phenotype and proliferative activity as potential correlates of tumor behavior. In recent years, diagnostic pathology has been strengthened by the recognition of recurrent histone H3.3 alterations in GCTB and the availability of mutation-associated immunohistochemistry (IHC) in challenging cases.[2] Nevertheless, in routine diagnostic practice and studies using archival material, diagnosis often relies on morphology supported by widely available immunohistochemical markers, making additional data on stromal immunophenotype relevant for diagnostic interpretation and biological characterization.

Among proposed immunomarkers, p63 is one of the most consistently reported in GCTB. Nuclear p63 expression in mononuclear stromal cells has been demonstrated in multiple cohorts and is often used as an adjunct to support the diagnosis in the appropriate morphologic setting and to help separate GCTB from selected giant cell-rich mimics.[6–8] The Ki-67 labeling index (LI) provides a practical estimate of proliferative activity within the stromal population. Prior studies have reported inconsistent associations between Ki-67 and recurrence in GCTB, likely reflecting differences in scoring thresholds and outcome definitions, underscoring the need for studies using explicitly defined approaches.[9] CD10 (cluster of differentiation 10; also known as neutral endopeptidase) has been reported in the neoplastic mononuclear component of GCTB, suggesting possible value as an adjunct stromal marker and as an exploratory correlate of stromal biology.[10]

The primary aim of this study was to characterize stromal p63, CD10, and Ki-67 expression in a retrospective GCTB cohort and to explore their associations with available clinicopathologic variables.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design, setting, and case retrieval

This was a retrospective cross-sectional archive-based immunohistochemical study performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue from cases diagnosed as GCTB in the departmental pathology archives between 2015 and 2025. Cases were retrieved from a tertiary referral hospital that commonly receives complicated cases, including those referred after prior management in peripheral centers. All cases were assigned to anonymized study codes prior to data extraction and analysis.

2.2. Case selection, variables, and histologic review

A total of 31 histologically confirmed GCTB cases with adequate tissue for IHC were included. Cases with exhausted tissue, poor fixation, or insufficient material for reliable immunostaining interpretation were excluded. Cases lacking the minimum demographic data required for analysis were also excluded.

Available clinical variables recorded for analysis were age, sex, anatomical site, tumor size when reliably documented, and recurrent-case status. Available radiologic information was assessed when documented in the medical records or referral data, including tumor site, tumor size, and reported radiologic features of GCTB such as cortical breach, expansile osteolytic change, and soft-tissue extension. However, complete standardized radiologic staging and central imaging review were not available for all cases. Treatment-related variables, including local adjuvant use, cementation, phenol, cryotherapy, denosumab exposure, soft-tissue extension, and follow-up duration, were not consistently available.

Hematoxylin and eosin sections were reviewed by 3 consultant histopathologists, primarily on-site and with remote review when needed, to confirm the diagnosis of GCTB (Fig. 1), ensure that selected blocks contained viable representative tumor, and assess histologic features, including stromal atypia and mitotic activity. 2.3. Definition of recurrent-case status

Recurrent-case status was defined according to whether the submitted case, as received at this center, was recorded as recurrent at presentation or referral. This variable does not represent prospectively documented recurrence after uniform primary treatment and follow-up. Accordingly, analyses involving recurrent-case status were treated as exploratory comparisons between recurrent presentation and nonrecurrent presentation submitted cases, not as recurrence prediction analyses.

2.4. Immunohistochemistry

Representative formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded blocks were selected, and 3 to 4 micrometer sections were prepared on coated slides. IHC was performed for p63, Ki-67, and CD10 using routine laboratory protocols (deparaffinization, antigen retrieval, primary antibody incubation, secondary detection, 3,3′-diaminobenzidine chromogen, and hematoxylin counterstain). Appropriate positive controls were used for each marker, and negative controls were prepared by omitting the primary antibody.

Interpretation was restricted to the mononuclear stromal cell component. Necrotic/hemorrhagic areas were avoided, and osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells were excluded from scoring and counts.

2.5. p63 (nuclear staining; stromal cells)

p63 was assessed as the percentage of stromal cell nuclei showing unequivocal staining. Cases were categorized using a predefined percentage-based system: negative (<1%), low (1%–50%), and high (>50%) stromal nuclear positivity. For statistical comparisons requiring dichotomization, high (>50%) was compared against the combined negative/low (≤50%) group.

2.6. CD10 (membranous ± cytoplasmic; stromal cells)

CD10 was assessed in stromal cells based on the percentage showing membranous (with or without cytoplasmic) staining. Cases were categorized as negative (<10%), low (10%–50%), and high (>50%). For analyses requiring dichotomization, high (>50%) was compared against the combined negative/low (≤50%) group.

2.7. Ki-67 (LI; stromal cells)

Ki-67 LI was determined by identifying hot-spot areas at low magnification and counting stromal nuclei at high power. The LI was recorded as the percentage of Ki-67-positive stromal nuclei. Ki-67 was analyzed as a continuous variable (%) and also as a binary group using a 10% threshold (≤10% vs >10%) as defined in the dataset.

2.8. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize clinicopathologic and immunohistochemical variables. Categorical associations were assessed using the Fisher exact test because of small cell counts. Ki-67 LI was evaluated as a continuous variable using the Mann–Whitney U test. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < .05. Given the small sample size and absence of treatment and radiologic data, multivariable analysis was not performed.

2.9. Ethics and confidentiality

The study was conducted on archived tissue and existing records after approval by Al-Azhar University, Assiut, Egypt (Approval No.: Research/AZ.AST./PAT005/7/246/2025). There was no patient contact and no change in clinical management. All data were deidentified before analysis and handled according to institutional research governance requirements.

3. Results

The most common anatomical sites were the fibula, humerus, and tibia (5 cases each, 16.1%), followed by the femur and radius (4 cases each, 12.9%). Less frequent sites included the calcaneus (2 cases, 6.5%) and the iliac bone, metacarpal, ribs, sacrum, toe, and ulna (1 case each, 3.2%). Tumor size was available in 21 cases and ranged from 3 to 15 cm, with a mean of 8.5 ± 2.9 cm and a median of 9 cm. Tumor size was missing or not reliably available in 10 cases.

Recurrent-case status was recorded in 14/31 cases (45.2%), while 17 cases (54.8%) were not recorded as recurrent at presentation. Stromal atypia was present in 4/31 cases (12.9%), and mitotic activity was recorded in 9/31 cases (29.0%). High p63 expression was observed in 20/31 cases (64.5%), and high CD10 expression was observed in 22/31 cases (71.0%). The Ki-67 LI ranged from 8% to 20%, with a mean of 12.0 ± 3.6% and a median of 10%; Ki-67 >10% was present in 14/31 cases (45.2%).

4. Discussion

This discussion focuses on the principal findings of the present cohort: frequent stromal expression of p63 and CD10, a significant association between CD10-high expression and recurrent-case status, and the absence of a significant association between the Ki-67 LI and recurrent-case status. Because recurrence in GCTB is multifactorial and strongly influenced by operative, anatomic, radiologic, and treatment-related factors, pathology-based markers should be interpreted as adjunctive and exploratory rather than independently predictive.[11–13]

Within this context, pathology-focused immunohistochemical studies remain valuable for supporting diagnosis among giant cell-rich mimics, especially in limited or challenging specimens, and for exploring whether stromal immunophenotypes reflect aspects of tumor biology.[14] Diagnostic practice has also been strengthened by molecular insights, including recurrent histone H3.3 alterations in GCTB and the availability of mutation-associated histone immunostains, notably H3G34W, which can improve diagnostic confidence in challenging biopsy and cytology material.[15,16] Nevertheless, many routine and archival settings continue to rely on morphology supported by widely available immunohistochemical markers, making practical stromal panels with reproducible interpretation clinically relevant.

As noted, GCTB biology is driven by the mononuclear stromal compartment, providing the rationale for restricting immunohistochemical interpretation to stromal cells in the present study.

In the present cohort, stromal p63 expression was frequent, with high p63 expression observed in 64.5% of cases. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating nuclear p63 positivity in GCTB stromal cells and supporting its diagnostic value as an adjunct marker when interpreted in the appropriate morphologic context.[6–8] Although p63 is not entirely specific across all giant cell-rich lesions, published evidence supports its usefulness in separating GCTB from selected mimics when used carefully as part of an integrated diagnostic approach.[6–8,14] In our cohort, p63-high expression showed a borderline relationship with recurrent-case status but did not reach statistical significance. More importantly, p63 expression correlated significantly with CD10 expression and with higher Ki-67 proliferative activity, suggesting that p63-high tumors may harbor a more proliferative or biologically active stromal compartment. However, given the small cohort size and the absence of detailed treatment and follow-up data, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that p63 predicts recurrence.

CD10 expression was also common, with high CD10 expression identified in 71.0% of cases. This agrees with previous work documenting CD10 expression in the neoplastic mononuclear component of GCTB and suggesting its potential value as an adjunct stromal marker in giant cell-rich bone lesions.[10] In the present study, CD10-high expression was significantly associated with recurrent-case status; 13 of 14 recurrent-presentation cases were CD10-high. This finding is noteworthy, but it requires cautious interpretation. Recurrent-case status in this study refers to whether the submitted case was recorded as recurrent at presentation or referral, not to prospectively documented recurrence after uniform primary treatment and follow-up. Therefore, the observed association reflects an exploratory comparison between recurrent presentation and nonrecurrent presentation submitted cases, rather than a prognostic analysis. In addition, 9 of 22 CD10-high cases were not recurrent-presentation cases, indicating that CD10-high expression alone has limited positive predictive value in this dataset. More broadly, CD10 has been linked to stromal biology and tissue remodeling in several tumor systems, providing a biologically plausible basis for its investigation as a stromal marker in GCTB.[17] Nonetheless, because GCTB recurrence is strongly shaped by operative, anatomic, and treatment-related factors, the association between CD10 and recurrent-case status should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires validation in larger, clinically annotated cohorts.[11–13]

Ki-67 LI in our cohort ranged from 8% to 20%, with a median of 10%. Ki-67 was not significantly different between recurrent presentation and nonrecurrent-presentation cases, supporting the view that Ki-67 alone is not a robust marker of recurrent-case status in this dataset. Published studies evaluating proliferative indices in GCTB have reported variable associations with recurrence and aggressive behavior, likely reflecting differences in cohort composition, scoring methods, thresholds, treatment history, and outcome definitions.[9,18] In our study, Ki-67 LI was significantly higher in cases with stromal atypia, mitotic activity, and p63-high expression. These findings are biologically plausible because atypia and mitotic activity may reflect increased stromal cellular activity; however, they should not be interpreted as independent predictors of recurrence. Other biomarkers, such as p53, have also been explored in GCTB with mixed prognostic implications, reinforcing that no single immunomarker has emerged as a definitive predictor of local behavior in isolation.[19]

The tertiary referral setting is important for interpreting the present findings. The cases were retrieved from a tertiary hospital that commonly receives complicated cases, including recurrent-presentation cases referred after prior management in peripheral centers. Therefore, the proportion of recurrent-presentation cases in this cohort may not reflect the recurrence rate expected in an unselected primary GCTB population. This context is particularly important because recurrent-case status was based on available documentation at presentation or referral and was not derived from standardized prospective follow-up after uniform treatment. As a result, recurrence-related analyses in this study should be viewed as descriptive and exploratory rather than predictive.

Treatment-related factors could also influence both morphology and immunohistochemical interpretation. Denosumab and other antiresorptive therapies can produce marked histologic alterations in GCTB, including depletion of osteoclast-like giant cells, stromal changes, and new bone formation, and may create morphologic overlap with other entities or with posttreatment changes.[20–22] Because treatment history, including denosumab exposure, was not consistently available in the present cohort, therapy-related effects could not be systematically assessed. Similarly, data regarding surgical approach, local adjuvant use, cementation, phenol, cryotherapy, soft-tissue extension, and follow-up duration were unavailable or incomplete. These missing variables limit the interpretation of any association with recurrent-case status and preclude the assessment of independent prognostic value.

Within these constraints, our findings support the diagnostic and exploratory value of stromal immunophenotyping in GCTB. p63 and CD10 were frequently expressed in stromal cells, p63 correlated with CD10 and higher Ki-67 proliferative activity, and CD10-high expression was enriched among recurrent-presentation cases. However, because recurrent-case status in this study does not represent prospective recurrence outcomes, and because major surgical, radiologic, and treatment-related confounders were unavailable, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Larger studies with standardized treatment data, radiologic staging, soft-tissue extension assessment, denosumab status, and longitudinal follow-up are required to determine whether these stromal markers have reproducible clinical or prognostic significance.

4.1. Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective archive-based design, small cohort size, and tertiary referral-center setting, which may enrich the cohort for complicated or recurrent-presentation cases. Recurrent-case status reflected documentation at presentation or referral and was not based on prospective follow-up after uniform primary treatment; recurrence-related analyses should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than predictive. Important clinical, surgical, radiologic, and treatment-related variables were unavailable or incompletely documented, preventing multivariable modeling and limiting the assessment of independent associations. Tumor size was analyzed only when clearly available. Immunohistochemical scoring was semiquantitative and may be affected by fixation variability, intratumoral heterogeneity, and observer-related thresholds. Formal interobserver agreement testing for immunohistochemical scoring was not conducted, and the absence of a non-GCTB comparator group limits the assessment of diagnostic sensitivity or specificity.

5. Conclusion

In this pathology-centered cohort of GCTB, stromal p63 and CD10 expression were frequently observed. CD10-high expression was significantly associated with recurrent-case status, but this association should be interpreted as exploratory because recurrent-case status reflected presentation/referral documentation rather than prospectively confirmed recurrence after standardized treatment and follow-up. Ki-67 was not significantly associated with recurrent-case status, although a higher Ki-67 LI was related to stromal atypia, mitotic activity, and p63-high expression. These findings support the diagnostic utility of stromal p63 and CD10 in GCTB and suggest that their co-expression may reflect a more proliferative or biologically active stromal phenotype, warranting further investigation. Further studies with larger cohorts, detailed surgical and radiologic data, treatment history, and longitudinal follow-up are needed to clarify the clinical significance of these markers.
