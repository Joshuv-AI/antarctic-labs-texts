Patterns of relapse and survival outcomes in pediatric ALL from a single center cohort under the CCCG ALL 2015 protocol
Authors: Jianming Fang, Hongyan Liu, Qi Liu, Yingli Zhang, Guoqin Hai, Han Gong, Xin Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo characterize relapse patterns, identify relapse-related risk factors, and evaluate prognostic determinants of post-relapse outcomes in pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) patients treated with the Chinese Children’s Cancer Group (CCCG)-ALL-2015 protocol.MethodsThis retrospective study included 380 children diagnosed with ALL between 2016 and 2019. We analyzed relapse timing (very early, early, late), relapse sites (bone marrow (BM) vs. extramedullary), early treatment response (morphology and minimal residual disease (MRD)), and post-relapse outcomes. Survival was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods.ResultsRelapse rate was 11.1% (42/380), predominantly isolated BM (69.0%) and very early timing (47.6%). Relapse varied significantly by risk group (low-risk: 5.9%; intermediate-risk: 10.4%; high-risk: 22.4%; P < 0.001), immunophenotype (B-ALL: 9.8% vs. T-ALL: 23.5%; P = 0.015), Day 19 morphology (M1:9.9% vs. M3:20.0%; P < 0.001), Day 46 morphology (M1:10.5% vs. M3:21.4%; P = 0.025), and Day 46 MRD status (positive:21.4% vs. negative:8.7%; P = 0.002). Fusion gene positivity was significantly associated with earlier relapse timing (P < 0.05). Post-relapse survival differed by relapse timing (very early: 7.2 months; early: 23.2 months; late: 37.8 months; P < 0.001) and initial risk group (low-risk: 38.2 months; high-risk: 18.1 months; P = 0.006).ConclusionsNon-remission on Days 19/46, Day 46 MRD positivity, and high-risk status predict ALL relapse. High-risk patients experience earlier relapses and poorer survival. Fusion gene positivity is associated with earlier relapse timing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05358-6.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a malignant disorder arising from the uncontrolled proliferation of immature B-lymphoid or T-lymphoid precursor cells within the bone marrow. It is the most prevalent childhood cancer, with an estimated annual incidence of 3 to 5 cases per 100,000 children globally [1, 2].

Significant progress has been achieved in the diagnosis and management of pediatric ALL in recent decades. This advancement stems from the widespread adoption of comprehensive diagnostic approaches integrating morphology, immunology, cytogenetics, and molecular biology (MICM classification [3]). These methods enable precise risk stratification and facilitate the implementation of tailored, risk-adapted therapeutic strategies [4, 5]. Deeper insights into leukemogenesis and the development of immunotherapy or targeted therapy have further contributed to improved clinical outcomes. Contemporary treatment protocols now achieve 5-year overall survival (OS) rates surpassing 80% [6].

Despite these substantial improvements, disease relapse remains a critical obstacle to cure. Approximately 10% to 20% of children achieving initial remission subsequently experience relapse in the bone marrow (BM), central nervous system (CNS), testes, or other extramedullary sites [7, 8]. Relapse is a leading cause of treatment failure and leukemia-related mortality. Survival outcomes following relapse, particularly early relapse, are significantly inferior to those observed in newly diagnosed disease [9]. Identifying factors predictive of relapse timing, pattern, and subsequent prognosis is therefore essential for developing preemptive interventions and optimizing salvage therapies.

The CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol, derived from the Berlin-Frankfurt-Münster (BFM) framework and adapted for the Chinese pediatric population, represents a standard frontline treatment regimen in China [10]. While effective for the majority, a subset of patients still experiences relapse [10]. A detailed characterization of relapse events under this specific protocol, including the site of relapse (isolated BM, isolated extramedullary, or combined), the timing of relapse (very early, early, or late), and their relationship to presenting biological features (immunophenotype, genetic abnormalities) and early treatment response markers, is crucial. Such analysis can inform risk re-stratification and guide decisions regarding therapeutic intensification or novel approaches.

The primary objectives were to characterize relapse patterns, identify factors associated with relapse risk and timing, evaluate post-relapse outcomes including second complete remission (CR2) rates and survival. In addition, we explored clinical factors associated with post-relapse survival in this cohort. Understanding the characteristics and outcomes of relapse under the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol may help clarify clinical factors associated with relapse risk and post-relapse prognosis, thereby supporting more informed clinical decision-making for pediatric ALL patients.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study initially screened 450 consecutive pediatric patients diagnosed with ALL at Kunming Children’s Hospital between January 2016 and December 2019. Inclusion criteria required: (1) age ≤ 18 years at diagnosis; (2) Diagnosis of ALL was confirmed according to standard morphology, immunophenotyping, cytogenetic, and molecular criteria; (3) Children with leukemia who received treatment under the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol; and (4) The patient was followed up until January 2024. Exclusion criteria were applied as follows: Patients with relapsed ALL at initial presentation (n = 5); Secondary malignancies or treatment-related AML (n = 3); Death during initial therapy (n = 11); Discontinue treatment before the remission assessment during the initial treatment (n = 9); Incomplete risk stratification or treatment response data (n = 35); Transfer to other institutions before protocol completion (n = 7). Patients who died during initial therapy or discontinued treatment before remission assessment were considered not at risk for post-remission relapse and were therefore excluded from the relapse cohort. These events were regarded as competing events for relapse in the overall treatment course.

After exclusions, 380 patients met all eligibility criteria and constituted the final study cohort for relapse analysis (Fig. 1). Among these 380 children who achieved CR1 following treatment with the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol, 42 patients subsequently developed BM or extramedullary relapse and were included in the relapse cohort for further analysis. The cohort comprised 27 males (64.3%) and 15 females (35.7%), with a median age at relapse of 5.7 years.

Inclusion and exclusion flowchart

Minimal residual disease (MRD) was assessed using multiparameter flow cytometry (sensitivity 10⁻⁴) or quantitative PCR for specific fusion genes (sensitivity 10⁻⁴), standardized across the cohort according to CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol guidelines [11, 12]. Bone marrow samples were collected at the end of induction (day 33) and end of consolidation (week 12). MRD ≥ 10⁻⁴ was considered positive, indicating the presence of residual leukemic cells, while MRD < 10⁻⁴ was considered negative. MRD results were used to monitor treatment response and guide risk stratification, but relapse was defined strictly by clinical and hematologic evidence of leukemia reappearance.

Study procedures

Risk stratification

Patients were stratified into low-risk (LR), intermediate-risk (IR), and high-risk (HR) groups according to the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol (supplementary Table 1), which is based on the BFM strategy. The treatment intensity varied by risk group, primarily in the dose and duration of consolidation and maintenance therapy. Stratification criteria included age at diagnosis, prednisone response (assessed on day 8), initial white blood cell (WBC) count, cytogenetic abnormalities, and the presence of specific fusion genes [10].

Treatment protocol

According to the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol (supplementary Tables 2 and 3), patients received multi-agent chemotherapy, including dexamethasone, prednisone, vincristine, and daunorubicin. Bone marrow MRD testing was conducted to evaluate therapeutic response. After induction therapy (lasting approximately 4 weeks), consolidation therapy was carried out for 8–12 weeks, followed by continuation/maintenance therapy for 24–30 months, involving drugs such as methotrexate and mercaptopurine, as well as intrathecal chemotherapy. Ph + ALL patients in this cohort received tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in addition to conventional chemotherapy.

Assessment of early treatment response

Treatment response was evaluated using standardized criteria. Complete remission (CR) was defined as the absence of clinical signs or symptoms of leukemia, peripheral blood counts recovering to hemoglobin ≥ 100 g/L and absolute neutrophil count > 1.5 × 10⁹/L, with no circulating blasts, and a bone marrow aspirate showing < 5% blasts (M1 morphology). Partial remission (PR) required 5% to 25% blasts in the bone marrow (M2 morphology), while non-remission (NR) was defined as > 25% blasts (M3) and/or failure to meet peripheral blood count recovery criteria. Prednisone response was assessed on day 8 of therapy [13]; poor prednisone response (PPR) was defined as ≥ 1 × 109/L blasts in the peripheral blood, and good prednisone response (PGR) as < 1 × 109/L blasts [14].

Definition of relapse

Relapse is defined as the reappearance of any of the following conditions in children initially diagnosed with ALL after achieving induction remission: ≥25% blasts (primitive and immature lymphocytes) in bone marrow smears, CNS leukemia, testicular leukemia, or leukemic cells identified by pathological biopsy of other organs/tissues; it is further classified into isolated BM relapse, isolated extramedullary relapse, and combined relapse, where isolated BM relapse refers to > 25% blasts (primitive and immature lymphocytes) in the BM with no evidence of CNS, testicular, or other extramedullary infiltration, isolated extramedullary relapse means relapse involving the CNS, testes, and/or other extramedullary sites without meeting the criteria for BM relapse, and combined relapse is defined as ≥ 5% total blasts (primitive and immature lymphocytes) in bone marrow smears accompanied by at least one type of extramedullary relapse. According to the CCCG-ALL relapse protocol, relapse is defined as the reappearance of leukemic cells in the bone marrow, peripheral blood, central nervous system, or testes after achieving remission. Relapse was categorized based on the time from initial diagnosis: very early relapse (< 18 months), early relapse (18 to 36 months), and late relapse (≥ 36 months).

Management and evaluation of relapse

Patients experiencing BM or extramedullary relapse were treated according to the CCCG-ALL relapse protocol (detailed in Supplementary Table 4). The primary therapeutic goal was to achieve a CR2, defined as < 5% blasts in a normocellular or hypercellular BM aspirate with no evidence of extramedullary disease, following salvage chemotherapy. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) was considered for patients with high-risk (HR) features, including early relapse, combined bone marrow and extramedullary relapse, or poor response to salvage chemotherapy. All patients with isolated CNS relapse received cranial irradiation, with a median dose of 18 Gy (range: 18–24 Gy) as per protocol.

Patient outcomes and follow-up

Outcomes for relapsed patients included: event-free survival (EFS), overall survival (OS), death, treatment abandonment, and loss to follow-up. EFS was defined as the time from the date of relapse diagnosis to secondary relapse, death, or treatment abandonment. OS was calculated from the date of relapse diagnosis to death from any cause or the last follow-up. Both endpoints were used to evaluate post-relapse outcomes, excluding the period from initial diagnosis to first relapse. The patient was followed up until January 2024. All 39 patients who received salvage therapy underwent second-line treatment following relapse.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were < 5. Continuous data were expressed as medians with ranges. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, with differences between groups assessed by the log-rank test. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided α level of 0.05.

Results

Relapse patterns by risk stratification

Among 380 ALL patients, the cumulative incidence of relapse differed by initial risk stratification: 5.9% (95% CI 3.0–10.7%) in LR, 10.4% (95% CI 5.8–17.4%) in IR, and 22.4% (95% CI 14.5–33.2%) in HR groups (P < 0.001; Table 1). Risk category also predicted relapse timing (P = 0.002; Table 2), with HR patients showing higher cumulative incidence than IR patients, and both HR/IR groups exhibiting increased very early/early relapses compared to LR patients.

Impact of genetic abnormalities on relapse

Relapse rates differed among specific fusion gene subtypes (Table 3). Favorable fusion gene ETV6::RUNX1 showed a relapse rate of 3.2% (1/31; 95% CI 0.1–16.7%). Among B-ALL patients without tested fusion genes (fusion gene-negative), the relapse rate was 7.0% (18/257; 95% CI 4.2–10.9%). Unfavorable fusion genes were associated with higher relapse rates: TCF3::PBX1, 60.0% (3/5; 95% CI 14.7–94.7%); BCR::ABL1, 44.4% (4/9; 95% CI 13.7–78.8%); KMT2A::AFF1, 22.2% (2/9; 95% CI 2.8–60.0%). Among these unfavorable subtypes, most relapses occurred very early (< 18 months from diagnosis). Overall, the presence of specific fusion genes was significantly associated with relapse timing (P = 0.008; Table 3).

Early treatment response and relapse risk

PPR on day 8 predicted higher relapse risk (23.3% vs. 10.0% in responders; P = 0.025). Morphological remission status demonstrated progressive relapse risk: at day 19, relapse rates were 9.9% (95% CI 6.9–13.7%) for M1, 15.4% (95% CI 4.4–34.9%) for M2, and 20.0% (95% CI 7.7–38.6%) for M3 (P < 0.001); similarly, day 46 rates were 10.5% (95% CI 7.5–14.2%) for M1, 16.7% (95% CI 2.1–48.4%) for M2, and 21.4% (95% CI 4.7–50.8%) for M3 (P = 0.025). MRD positivity on day 46 conferred a 2.5-fold increased relapse risk (21.4% vs. 8.7% in MRD-negative patients; P = 0.002; Table 4).

Relapse patterns by site

Among 42 relapsed patients, isolated BM relapse predominated (69.0%, 29/42; 95% CI 52.9–82.4%), with 44.8% of these BM relapses occurring very early (< 18 months). Isolated extramedullary relapse accounted for 23.8% (10/42; 95% CI 12.1–39.5%), primarily involving the CNS (19.0%, 8/42; 95% CI 8.6–34.1%). Most CNS relapses occurred during the very early and early relapse periods, whereas combined BM/extramedullary relapse was rare (7.2%, 3/42; 95% CI 1.5–19.9%) (Table 5). When analyzed by initial risk group, isolated BM relapse was observed in 20.0% of LR, 53.8% of IR, and 63.2% of HR patients who relapsed. Isolated CNS or testicular relapse occurred more frequently in intermediate- and HR patients compared with LR patients. Combined BM/extramedullary relapse was only observed in intermediate- and HR groups.

Second complete remission (CR2) outcomes

Following relapse diagnosis, 2 patients abandoned treatment and 1 was lost to follow-up, leaving 39 patients who received salvage therapy. Of 39 patients receiving relapse therapy, 41.0% (16/39; 95% CI 25.6–57.9%) achieved CR2. Site-specific CR2 rates were: 38.5% for isolated BM relapse (10/26; 95% CI 20.2–59.4%), 50.0% for isolated extramedullary relapse (5/10; 95% CI 18.7–81.3%), and 33.3% for combined relapse (1/3; 95% CI 0.8–90.6%). Isolated extramedullary relapse showed a higher CR2 rate than isolated BM relapse (Table 6).

Impact of relapse timing on survival

The median follow-up period for these patients was 28.4 months (95% CI 23.1–33.6 months). For 39 treated relapse patients, 28.2% (11/39; 95% CI 15.0–44.9%) remained event-free, while 51.3% (20/39; 95% CI 34.8–67.6%) died. Median EFS for the treated relapse cohort was 12.5 months (95% CI 8.3–17.6 months), and median OS was 28.4 months (95% CI 18.2–40.1 months) (Table 7). Survival differed significantly based on the timing of relapse, with very early relapse having a median OS of 7.2 months compared to 37.8 months for late relapse (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2). Similarly, initial risk group was a strong predictor of post-relapse outcome, with LR patients surviving significantly longer than HR patients (median OS: 38.2 vs. 18.1 months; log-rank P = 0.006; Fig. 3). Median EFS for the treated relapse cohort was 12.5 months (range: 1.1–60.4 months). The median OS for the entire cohort was 34.7 months (Fig. 4).

Discussion

ALL is the most common type of leukemia in children, accounting for 75%-80% of pediatric acute leukemia cases [15]. Over recent decades, the cure rates and survival outcomes of pediatric ALL have improved significantly; however, relapse in the BM or extramedullary sites remains a major clinical challenge, affecting 10%-20% of patients [16, 17]. This retrospective cohort study analyzed 42 pediatric ALL patients who experienced BM or extramedullary relapse after receiving initial treatment with the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol at Kunming Children’s Hospital between 2016 and 2019, aiming to identify factors associated with relapse and their prognostic implications.

Relapse is a key cause of treatment failure in ALL. In this cohort, the overall relapse rate of patients treated with the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol was 11.1%, which is consistent with the relapse rates reported by other centers in China using the same protocol [18]. As expected, LR patients showed the lowest relapse rate (5.9%), consistent with their more favorable biological characteristics. The relapse rates were 10.4% and 22.4% in the IR and HR groups, respectively. These findings likely reflect the more aggressive disease characteristics in higher-risk groups, which have been associated with an increased likelihood of relapse in previous studies. Compared with patients in the LR/IR groups, HR patients had a higher relapse rate, lower CR2 rate, shorter survival time after relapse, and poorer overall prognosis. This pattern is consistent with the known prognostic impact of baseline risk stratification in pediatric ALL.

Additionally, relapse events were observed in patients with BCR::ABL or TCF3::PBX1 gene fusions in this cohort, which is consistent with previous literature reports [19]. However, the absolute difference in relapse rates between fusion gene-positive and fusion gene-negative patients was small (13.5% vs. 10.3%), which may limit clinical relevance. The small number of cases within each fusion gene subtype (n = 5–9) further limits the reliability of subtype-specific conclusions and may exaggerate apparent relapse risk. Observations regarding the absolute number of relapses in BCR::ABL1-positive patients should be interpreted cautiously, as this reflects cohort composition rather than intrinsic risk. Two children with KMT2A gene rearrangements were identified, a genetic alteration that has been reported to be associated with unfavorable outcomes in pediatric ALL [20, 21]. Previous experimental studies have suggested that KMT2A fusion proteins can not only transform hematopoietic stem cells into leukemic stem cells but also reprogram hematopoietic progenitor cells—including common myeloid progenitors and granulocyte-macrophage progenitors—into leukemic stem cells, thereby leading to the development of leukemia [22, 23]. Advances in risk-adapted therapy and the development of targeted treatments may provide additional therapeutic options for these patients [24, 25].

Early treatment response is a core factor influencing risk stratification, treatment regimen selection, and prognostic evaluation in children with ALL. This study found that PPR, MRD positivity on day 46 of chemotherapy, and NR in the BM on day 19 or day 46 were associated with an increased risk of relapse in children with ALL. Mayne et al. have clearly demonstrated that children with ALL who show a sensitive response on day 8 of induction chemotherapy have a better prognosis [26]; consistent with this finding, our study also showed that the relapse rate of children with a sensitive prednisone response was significantly lower than that of children with an insensitive response, which is largely in line with conclusions reported in existing literature [26]. In addition, MRD has critical prognostic value for childhood ALL. A lot of study indicated that even if children with ALL achieve good clinical remission on D19 or D46 of induction chemotherapy, their relapse risk will still increase significantly if MRD testing is positive [27–30]. However, the reported 2.5-fold increased relapse risk based on proportions does not account for time-to-event, and hazard ratio modeling would provide a more appropriate estimate of risk over follow-up. Similarly, in our study, the relapse risk of patients with positive MRD on D46 was 2.5-fold higher than that of MRD-negative patients, which is consistent with the trends observed in other studies. Furthermore, it has been clinically confirmed that early treatment response, as reflected by MRD levels at the end of induction therapy, is one of the most important indicators for evaluating the prognosis of patients with ALL [31]. This view has also been recognized in clinical practice—the CCCG-ALL 2015 protocol developed by the Chinese Children’s Cancer Group has officially incorporated MRD testing into the core system for risk stratification assessment of children with ALL.

The site of relapse has a significant impact on outcomes. Isolated BM relapse is the most common relapse pattern, followed by isolated CNS relapse and testicular relapse; combined BM/extramedullary relapse is relatively rare. In the present cohort, isolated BM relapse accounted for the majority of relapse events, which is consistent with previously reported relapse patterns in pediatric ALL. The distribution of relapse sites observed in this study is comparable to that reported in other clinical studies [11, 12]. In addition, there are significant differences in relapse rates among different immunophenotypes: the relapse rate of T-cell ALL (T-ALL) is higher than that of B-cell ALL (B-ALL), which is consistent with the results of other studies [32]. The T-cell immunophenotype remains an adverse prognostic factor in childhood ALL, and patients with this immunophenotype still face an increased risk of induction failure and early relapse [33, 34]. However, conclusions regarding CNS relapse patterns, combined BM/extramedullary relapse, and isolated extramedullary relapse outcomes are limited by very small absolute numbers (n = 8, n = 3, n = 10, respectively), and timing or CR2 rate patterns should be interpreted with caution.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design. The relatively small cohort size of 42 relapse events limits the statistical power for subgroup analyses and multivariate modeling. Therefore, larger studies with sufficient event numbers are needed to perform robust multivariable modeling to confirm independent prognostic factors. Data on specific molecular aberrations beyond common fusion genes were not uniformly available for all patients, potentially omitting important prognostic markers. The follow-up duration, while extending to January 2024, may still be insufficient to fully capture long-term outcomes, particularly for late relapsers. Socioeconomic factors influencing treatment adherence and abandonment, though noted, were not quantitatively analyzed. Multiple hypothesis testing was performed across sections without correction, which may increase the risk of type I error. Differences in relapse treatment approaches were not accounted for in the analyses, which may substantially influence CR2 and survival outcomes. Finally, the applicability of findings to other treatment protocols or diverse populations requires validation through multi-center prospective studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, children treated under the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol who present with T-ALL or show poor early treatment response were observed to have a higher risk of relapse, particularly very early or early relapse. Once relapse occurs, especially in the BM, achieving CR2 remains challenging, and prognosis is generally poor. Children with features associated with higher relapse risk may benefit from close clinical monitoring and consideration of risk-adapted management. Socioeconomic factors were noted to influence outcomes, with 7 patients abandoning treatment, suggesting that support mechanisms and adherence may be relevant considerations in clinical management.
