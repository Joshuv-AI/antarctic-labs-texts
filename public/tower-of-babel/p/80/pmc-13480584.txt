Artificial Intelligence‐Based Approach for Determining the Risk of Temporomandibular Disorders
Authors: Damla Torul, Mustafa Hakan Bozkurt, Mehmet Melih Ömezli, Muhammed Furkan Yılmaz, Kübra Yıldız
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aims to predict TMD using ML approaches based on clinical and sociodemographic variables to aid in the early detection and risk assessment.MethodsThe study was on patients with and without TMD who met the inclusion criteria in a retrospective–prospective manner. Clinical and demographic variables of 340 patients were collected during the study. Data were split into 80% training and 20% test sets. Five different machine learning models (DANets, KAN, KNN, MLP, SVM) were used. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, weighted precision, recall, and F1‐score. A model interpretability analysis was performed using SHapley Additive ExPlanations (SHAP).ResultsFor result classification, the DANets model achieved the highest performance (accuracy = 0.8529). The DANets model achieved the strongest performance in diagnostic classification by far (accuracy = 0.8382, F1 = 0.8212). SHAP‐based analyses revealed that demographic factors (especially age and gender) and certain clinical symptoms (e.g., joint pain, clicking, and limited mouth opening) had the highest contribution to the models' decision‐making processes.ConclusionAI‐based models—particularly deep learning algorithms—may provide substantial support to clinicians in the diagnosis and risk assessment of TMD. These tools may reduce diagnostic uncertainty, especially for inexperienced practitioners, facilitate early diagnosis and referral processes, and decrease the risk of TMD progression.

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are the second most common musculoskeletal disorder after back pain and affect the temporomandibular joints (TMJs), masticatory muscles, and related structures [1]. The main clinical symptoms include pain, tenderness, clicking, popping, and trismus in the TMJ region and/or masticatory muscles, abnormalities in mandibular movements, restrictions in range of motion of the jaw, joint locking, headache, and ear symptoms that significantly affect the quality of life of patients [1, 2, 3].

TMD is most commonly seen in individuals between the ages of 20 and 40 and is also more common in women than in men [2, 4, 5]. The prevalence of TMD reported 31% among the general population [6]. The aetiology of TMDs is multifactorial, and the pathogenesis has not yet been fully elucidated, but behavioural, biological, and psychosocial, demographic factors, medical history, lifestyle habits, genetic predisposition, and various other biological and environmental factors may contribute to the onset or progression of TMD [1, 2]. Comprehending these variables facilitates the creation of all‐encompassing models for early diagnosis and detection of TMDs [4].

While artificial intelligence (AI) can be defined as the process of transferring human intelligence to computers, machine learning (ML), a subfield of AI, involves making inferences by learning from data [4]. ML refers to a set of methods that recognise data patterns and use them to predict future data or make decisions. Deep learning (DL), a subtype of machine learning particularly adept at large datasets, uses complex, multilayered algorithms modelled after the human brain. These algorithms mimic the structure of the human cognitive system in the formation of artificial neural networks (ANNs) [7]. It has been reported that the use of AI and ML approaches in dentistry can help detect and diagnose TMDs more practically and accurately [1, 2, 3, 8].

TMDs require a complex diagnostic process, and diagnosis often relies on the physician's experience. The time‐consuming and costly nature of these processes necessitates the use of decision support systems to assist the diagnostic process and overcome this problem [4]. AI has been successfully applied in the diagnosis of TMD, particularly using imaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), and ultrasonography [9, 10, 11]. Furthermore, in recent years, AI has been able to diagnose TMD [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20] and predict TMD risk using information obtained from clinical data and surveys [1, 2, 5, 11]. Among the limited studies that explore risk prediction, Yıldız et al. [1] and Cui et al. [2] evaluate clinical data, sociodemographic variables, and psychological status; Lee et al. [11] included clinical data, sleep‐related variables, and Lee et al. [5] used biological and psychosocial factors and working conditions.

TMD reduces patients' quality of life and results in high treatment costs in societies [1, 8]. By preventing chronic pain and impairment, early diagnosis and treatment can greatly improve patients' prognosis and quality of life [1, 7]. Considering all these challenges, innovative technologies such as highly accurate AI can help combat common misdiagnoses and insufficient knowledge about TMD in clinics. Thus, TMD can be diagnosed more practically and can be detected at an early stage by inexperienced dentists, thus referring patients to specialists [1, 7].

To date, there is a lack of studies that simultaneously integrate clinical and sociodemographic variables to both diagnose TMD subtypes and predict future risk in asymptomatic or at‐risk individuals using ML approaches. This represents an important gap in the literature, particularly considering the need for early identification and preventive strategies in TMD. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by developing ML models that not only this study aims to detect TMD presence but also predict the future risk of TMD based on combined clinical and sociodemographic data. TMD presence but also predict the future risk of TMD based on combined clinical and sociodemographic data. The primary aim of this study was to predict the future risk of TMD in individuals using ML models developed from clinical and sociodemographic data. Accurate prediction of TMD risk in asymptomatic but susceptible individuals may facilitate early diagnosis, enable preventive strategies, and support timely clinical interventions and appropriate referrals. The secondary aim of this study was to identify TMD presence in diagnosed patients using ML approaches. Given that TMDs are often identified at relatively advanced stages, the ability to predict future risk may help address a critical gap in early detection and prevention, ultimately supporting more effective clinical decision‐making.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Ordu University (No:2023/322) and conducted following the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Materials

The study was conducted in a retrospective‐prospective manner on patients with and without TMD who met the inclusion criteria. Specifically, the retrospective component of the study consisted of previously recorded clinical and sociodemographic data obtained from patients' archival records. The prospective component involved the collection of clinical and sociodemographic data from patients in a forward‐looking manner during the study period. The study population comprised patients who were admitted to Ordu University, Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery between January 2013 and October 2023, with/without TMD complaints.

Demographic Variables

Information on age, gender, marital status, education, occupation, income, region, and the presence of systemic diseases and medication use was collected from all patients. The presence of alcohol consumption, tobacco, and smoking was noted.

Clinical Variables

To ensure consistency and reliability, all TMD diagnoses were based on standardised clinical examinations performed by experienced clinicians with expertise in TMD. Clinical records were reviewed, and efforts were made to maintain diagnostic consistency across both retrospective and prospective components of the study.

Etiologic Factors/History

Noted as tooth extraction, filling, root canal treatment, oral surgery, trauma, stress, other, or not available.

Bruxism

Recorded as present/absent based on clinical records. The patient's history, clinical examination, and tooth wear were taken into consideration while evaluating bruxism [21].

Oral Habits

Based on the patient's reports, behaviours like tongue push and finger or lip sucking are noted as either present or absent.

TMJ Sounds

Using a stethoscope, clicking, popping, and crepitus were assessed and noted as either present or absent.

Pain in TMJ

Pain during palpation or mandibular movement in the TMJ region is noted as either present or absent.

Pain in the Muscles

When the masticatory muscles are palpated, pain is noted as either present or absent.

Earache

Based on the patient's report, ear pain was recorded as present or absent.

Headache

Based on the patient's report, headache was recorded as present or absent.

Deviation/Deflection

Deviation or deflection during mouth opening was noted as either present or absent based on the clinical observation.

The Maximum Mouth Opening (MMO)

The MMO was measured as the distance between the r'ght mandibular central incisor and the right maxillary central incisor incisal edges.

Tinnitus

Based on patient report, recorded as present or absent.

Malocclusion

Angle's classification (Class I, Class II, and Class III) was used to assess sagittal dental malocclusion. If the overbite was greater than 20%, it was considered a deep bite; if one or more teeth did not make contact with antagonists, it was considered an open bite. Transverse dental malocclusion was evaluated using a unilateral or bilateral posterior crossbite based on the vestibular cusps of the lower premolars and molars that laterally overbite the upper ones. Any sagittal, transversal, or vertical occlusion problems were assessed and noted as present or absent based on clinical examination [22].

TMD

Recorded as either present or absent based on the presence of at least one sign or symptom (sounds, discomfort, deflection/deviation, and restriction in mouth opening).

Artificial Intelligence

Dataset

This study used a dataset containing 340 patient records, each with 28 comprehensive features, derived from clinical evaluations of TMJ disorders. The dataset was specifically structured to evaluate different clinical outcomes: The Target Result dataset focuses on general patient outcome (TMD/healthy), while the Target Diagnosis dataset was used for detailed diagnostic classification for risk factors and clinical assessment.

Each dataset covers a wide range of patient information, including demographic variables such as gender, age, education level, income, marital status, and occupation, as well as lifestyle factors such as smoking habits and alcohol consumption. The dataset also includes detailed medical histories and comprehensive clinical symptomatology profiles, including systemic health conditions, current medication use, bruxism, and oral behavioural habits. These symptoms include headache patterns, earache symptoms, hearing loss, presence of tinnitus, malocclusion characteristics, joint clicking, crepitus, and popping, mouth opening limitations, jaw deviation and deflection, and joint and muscle pain assessments.

Data Preprocessing and Preparation

This study used a MacOS (Unix) machine with an i5 processor. Efficient memory management practises were utilised for batch processing and system resource optimisation, as appropriate for this hardware. A standard preprocessing pipeline was implemented to prepare the datasets for machine learning. First, missing values were filled. Median imputation was used for the 28 numerical features in each patient record. For the median‐based categorical features, mode imputation was used. This method attempted to preserve class representation by filling in missing entries with the most frequent value in each column.

After imputation, feature coding and scaling were performed. All categorical features, including demographic and clinical symptoms, were converted to numerical representations using LabelEncoder. The same process was applied to two separate target variables (Target Result and Target Diagnosis) to generate consistent integer‐based class labels for model training and evaluation. Numerical features were standardised using StandardScaler. This process transformed the data to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, ensuring that larger‐scale features do not disproportionately impact model training. The preprocessed data was split into 80% training and 20% test sets. This resulted in 272 samples for training and 68 for testing. A stratified splitting strategy was used to ensure that the class distribution of each target variable was proportionally represented in both the training and test sets.

Machine Learning Models

Experiments were conducted on five different machine learning models to provide a comprehensive evaluation of different learning paradigms and computational approaches.

Multi‐Layer Perceptron (MLP)

A feedforward neural network consisting of an input layer, two hidden layers (100 and 50 neurons, respectively), and an output layer was implemented. The hidden layers used a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function. The training was performed using a learning rate of 0.001, an L2 regularisation penalty (α = 0.0001), and an Adam optimizer.

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

In this study, an support vector machine (SVM) model using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel was implemented. The regularisation parameter C was set to 1.0, and the gamma parameter was set to ‘scale’. A One‐versus‐Rest (OvR) decision function was used for multi‐class prediction tasks.

K‐Nearest Neighbours (KNN)

The number of nearest neighbours was chosen as k = 5. All neighbours were given equal weight, and distance was measured using the standard Euclidean metric.

Knowledge Augmented Network (KAN)

This study implemented a custom‐developed hybrid classifier designed to integrate global, class‐level prototype summaries derived from the training data with local, data‐driven evidence. The model calculates a final score by combining two components with equal weights (α = 0.5, β = 0.5). The ‘Knowledge component’ represents a prototype‐based classification by calculating a score based on the exponent of the negative Euclidean distance from a given data point to the centroid of each class. The ‘Data component’ performs a standard KNN voting (k = 5).

Deep Abstract Network (DANet)

A PyTorch‐based deep neural network consisting of an input layer, two hidden layers (with ReLU activation) of 128 and 64 neurons, and a final output layer was implemented. A 20% dropout was applied for regularisation after each hidden layer. Residual (shortcut) connections were introduced to improve the gradient flow. The network was trained for 50 epochs with a batch size of 32 using the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.001) and the Cross‐Entropy Loss function.

Statistical Analysis

Model performance was evaluated using four standard classification metrics: accuracy, weighted precision, weighted recall, and weighted F1‐score. The weighted metrics were chosen to account for potential class imbalance in the target variables. The experimental protocol involved training each of the five models independently on each of the three target datasets. To ensure transparency and clinical relevance, a model interpretability analysis was performed using SHapley Additive ExPlanations (SHAP). For Scikit‐learn‐based models (MLP, SVM, KNN, KAN), KernelExplainer was used with a background dataset of 100 samples to estimate SHAP values. For the PyTorch‐based DANet, DeepExplainer, which requires less processing, was used with 50 background samples. Feature importance analysis for each model and target using SHAP values, along with confusion matrices.

Results

While the MLP, KNN and KAN models achieved similar accuracy values, KAN was found to be superior in terms of F1‐score. The DANets model achieved the strongest performance in diagnosis by far (accuracy = 0.8382, F1 = 0.8212). Traditional methods performed relatively poorly on this objective. Discussion

In this study, various AI‐based ML approaches were evaluated to predict the risk and diagnosis of TMD using clinical and sociodemographic data, and it was demonstrated that the DL–based DANets model provided more successful results compared with other methods. Considering the multifactorial and complex nature of TMD, these findings may offer significant contributions to risk prediction, diagnostic processes, and clinical decision‐making mechanisms. The clinical significance of the model's performance lies in its potential to support early identification of individuals at risk and to facilitate timely clinical decision‐making. Even moderate predictive performance may be considered valuable in a screening context, particularly when the primary aim is to rule out low‐risk cases and prioritise patients requiring further diagnostic evaluation. In this regard, the model may contribute to reducing diagnostic delay and improving patient triage in routine clinical practise. Additionally, its use as a decision‐support tool may be particularly beneficial for non‐expert clinicians, helping to standardise initial assessments and reduce variability in clinical judgement.

SHAP‐based analyses were used to determine the variables that are effective in the decision‐making processes of the models in this study. The results were consistent with previous studies reporting that TMD is associated with clinical findings such as bruxism, joint sounds, muscle and joint pain, and limitation of mouth opening [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. The fact that these variables played a decisive role in the diagnosis and joint‐problem classifications in the SHAP analysis strengthens the concordance between clinical knowledge and AI models. Similarly, demographic variables—particularly age and gender—were found to contribute strongly to the overall risk classification, which aligns with the epidemiological distribution of TMD reported in the literature [6, 28, 29, 30]. In the study by Cui et al. [2], ML methods were also used to predict TMD risk, and SHAP analysis identified age, gender, bruxism, joint sounds, pain, and measures of mouth opening as the most influential predictors. Lee et al. [5] reported by using the random forest that the top 20 risk parameters of TMDs as body mass index, age, household income, sleep quality, obesity, working condition, subjective health, education, occupation, smoking status, residence type, region, sex, marital status, and allergic rhinitis. Yıldız et al. [1] reported using the Bagging algorithm that the 5 most important variables for predicting TMD were pain intensity, MMO, lateral excursion and pressure pain threshold (PPT) values of masseter and temporalis anterior muscles, respectively. The findings of these studies were also consistent with the findings of the present study.

When ML models were compared in our study, the DL–based DANets model achieved superior performance across most target variables compared with other methods. While MLP, SVM, and KNN produced imbalanced sensitivity or precision results in certain classes, the DANets model maintained class distribution more effectively. Previous studies have shown that DL methods provide strong performance in the diagnosis and classification of TMD using imaging or clinical data [7, 11, 19, 31, 32, 33]. Regarding risk prediction, Lee et al. [11] compared a DL model (DNN) with ML methods such as logistic regression and random forest to predict chronic TMD risk using clinical, behavioural, and MRI data, and similar to our findings, reported the superior performance of the deep model. This superiority may be attributed to deep learning's ability to better capture complex relationships within multidimensional clinical data. Yıldız et al. [1] evaluated measurements via over 20 ML algorithms, and the Bagging algorithm (accuracy = 0.8966, F1‐score = 0.9032) was the best performing model regarding the performance criteria. Lee et al. [5] evaluated six models and reported that the greatest mean accuracy was observed in logistic regression, random forest, support vector machines, and artificial neural networks, respectively. In multifactorial conditions like TMD, where class boundaries may be ambiguous, such performance advantages hold significant clinical importance.

Direct comparison of the present findings with previous studies should be interpreted with caution, as differences in AI models, feature selection methods, dataset characteristics, and parameter optimisation strategies may significantly influence model performance. Variations in study design, population characteristics, and outcome definitions further limit the comparability across studies. Therefore, discrepancies in reported performance metrics should not necessarily be interpreted as superiority or inferiority of models, but rather as reflections of methodological heterogeneity.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly the limited number of individuals in the test set, may affect the robustness and generalizability of the results. Second, the single‐centre design may limit the external validity of the findings, as the study population may not fully represent broader and more diverse populations. In addition, due to the inclusion of retrospectively collected data, standardised diagnostic criteria such as diagnostic criteria for temporomandibular disorders (DC/TMD) could not be uniformly applied across all participants, which may introduce variability in diagnostic classification. Furthermore, although efforts were made to ensure consistency through examinations performed by experienced clinicians, some degree of observer‐dependent variability cannot be completely excluded. Finally, despite the use of appropriate validation strategies, the absence of external validation with independent datasets remains a limitation. Therefore, future multi‐centre studies with larger sample sizes, standardised diagnostic protocols, and external validation are warranted to confirm and generalise the findings.

Conclusions

The proposed preliminary model is designed to function as a decision‐support tool for risk prediction and diagnosis. It may be particularly useful for non‐expert clinicians by allowing straightforward data input and providing risk‐based outputs to support clinical decision‐making. In this context, the model is not intended to replace clinical judgement but to assist in the early identification of patients who may require further evaluation or referral. Furthermore, as a preliminary framework, the model has the potential to be continuously refined and improved through the integration of additional data over time, which may enhance its predictive accuracy and overall clinical applicability.

In conclusion, AI‐based models—particularly DL algorithms—may provide substantial support to clinicians in the diagnosis and risk assessment of TMD. These tools may reduce diagnostic uncertainty, especially for inexperienced practitioners, facilitate early diagnosis and referral processes, and decrease the risk of TMD progression. With future studies incorporating larger datasets and multimodal data sources, deep learning approaches have the potential to become a routine component of TMD management.
