Temporal biobehavioral pathways from perceived stress to symptom burden in patients with cancer: A three-wave longitudinal cross-lagged panel network analysis to inform supportive care
Authors: Ruoyi Xu, Huiyu Li, Hongwen Ma, Xiang Gao, Yaqin Zhou, Yanfei Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer-related stress and symptom burden constitute a complex, interrelated process across psychological, biological, and functional domains. Existing studies often examine isolated associations, limiting understanding of their dynamic interactions over time. This study aimed to construct a cross-lagged panel network to examine the longitudinal temporal structure among perceived stress, emotional distress, inflammatory biomarkers, physical functioning, and symptoms.MethodsA three-wave cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) analysis was conducted in oncology departments of two tertiary hospitals in Nanjing and Tianjin, China. A total of 276 patients completed baseline assessments; 258 and 241 completed follow-ups at 4–8 and 8–12 weeks post-treatment, respectively. Measures included perceived stress, emotional distress, biomarkers (C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, cortisol), functioning, and symptoms. Temporal associations were estimated using ridge-regularized CLPN models.ResultsTemporal networks revealed a consistent hierarchical organization of prospective cross-domain associations, with perceived stress occupying an early temporal position, and functioning and symptom-related variables occurring later in the temporal sequence. Bidirectional feedback was observed. Centrality analyses highlighted perceived stress and fatigue as prominent nodes.ConclusionsCancer symptom progression appears to follow a temporally organized hierarchical structure, with perceived stress serving as an early predictor. Fatigue functions as a key bridge across domains. These findings highlight the potential importance of early stress management and targeted fatigue interventions and may help inform phase-specific supportive care within multidisciplinary oncology practice.Keywords: Cancer-related symptom burden, Perceived stress, Cross-lagged panel network analysis, Fatigue

Background

Cancer diagnosis and its treatments impose a substantial and persistent symptom burden that extends beyond tumor-directed outcomes to patients’ daily functioning and quality of life (Mols et al., 2025). A substantial proportion of patients experience clinically meaningful psychological distress—most notably elevated perceived stress—alongside common physical symptoms such as fatigue, sleep disturbance, pain, and cognitive difficulties, which frequently co-occur and reinforce one another (Baussard et al., 2024; Reis et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023). Perceived stress, defined as an individual’s appraisal of situational demands as exceeding available coping resources, has increasingly been recognized as a prospective predictor of symptom burden in patients with cancer (Jakovljevic et al., 2021). Contemporary biobehavioral oncology models propose that cancer-related perceived stress is not only a subjective experience but also a biological challenge that engages multiple regulatory systems (Reis et al., 2025). Longitudinal studies have reported prospective associations between psychological distress and inflammatory biomarkers in patients with cancer (Amirkhanzadeh Barandouzi et al., 2023; Renna et al., 2020). Complementing this, cross-sectional evidence suggests that inflammation-related fatigue and cognitive impairment may in turn be associated with reduced emotional well-being and higher perceived stress (Iaquinta et al., 2022; Jurgens & Hayes, 2023), pointing to potential bidirectional and self-reinforcing processes. Oncology clinicians and multidisciplinary care teams therefore face the challenge not only of managing this complex co-occurring symptom burden across all phases of cancer treatment, but also of understanding the temporal sequence through which perceived stress connects to symptoms, in order to identify optimal windows for intervention and when along the treatment timeline different strategies should be deployed. Such temporal understanding is central to moving beyond reactive symptom control toward evidence-based, upstream clinical protocols.

Despite growing evidence linking psychological distress, biological markers, physical functioning, and symptoms, important knowledge gaps remain. Existing longitudinal studies have largely examined isolated pairwise relationships—such as perceived stress and inflammation or inflammation and fatigue—rather than simultaneously investigating these domains within a unified longitudinal framework (Hoogland et al., 2023; Kwekkeboom et al., 2018). This fragmentation limits the ability to characterize how changes within one domain are prospectively associated with changes in other domains and obscures the complex, potentially bidirectional pathways through which symptom processes unfold over time. The clinical implications are substantial: current symptom management guidelines largely treat symptoms as independent targets, yet if these symptoms share common prospective predictors or reciprocally reinforce one another, siloed single-symptom interventions may be inefficient and may miss critical intervention windows. Therefore, there is a need for an approach capable of simultaneously capturing the dynamic cross-domain relationships among stress, biological processes, and symptoms over time.

Network analysis provides such a framework by conceptualizing observed variables as mutually interacting nodes and quantifying which nodes are most central or serve as bridges between domains (Borsboom et al., 2021). Extending network methods longitudinally, cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) approaches estimate directed, prospective associations while simultaneously accounting for autoregressive persistence and concurrent relationships among multiple variables (Wysocki et al., 2025). Unlike traditional structural equation modeling, which requires a priori specification of directional paths, CLPN allows data-driven discovery of unexpected connections—a critical advantage when the specific temporal sequence of stress–biology–symptom pathways remains empirically unresolved. Network analysis has been applied to map symptom relationships in cancer populations (Henneghan et al., 2021), yet these studies have been largely cross-sectional or restricted to narrow variable domains. Applying CLPN in this context may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of how perceived stress, emotional distress, biomarkers, physical functioning, and symptoms are prospectively associated over time, identify variables that occupy central or bridging positions within the longitudinal network, and generate hypotheses regarding potentially important targets for future supportive care interventions. Furthermore, because the neuroendocrine and inflammatory milieu can shift substantially as patients transition between treatment phases, examining whether the temporal architecture of these pathways remains stable across successive intervals is equally essential for determining whether intervention strategies should be adapted at different points along the cancer continuum (Funkhouser et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2025; Wysocki et al., 2025).

Accordingly, guided by integrative biobehavioral models in oncology (Reis et al., 2025) and inflammation-based accounts of sickness behavior (Santos & Pyter, 2018), we applied a longitudinal cross-lagged panel network framework to examine temporal interactions among perceived stress, mood states, inflammation-related biomarkers (CRP, IL-6), an HPA-axis marker (cortisol), physical functioning, and symptoms in a three-wave cancer cohort. We addressed three research questions: what is the temporal architecture of prospective relationships across psychological, biological, and clinical domains (RQ1); which nodes exhibited relatively high expected influence and bridge expected influence within the temporal network (RQ2); and whether these network characteristics differed across treatment phases (RQ3). Based on existing biobehavioral models, we expected to observe a layered temporal organization consistent with emotional and inflammatory processes potentially occupying intermediate bridging positions. The specific nodes and pathways were nonetheless explored in a data-driven manner. The findings of this study are expected to provide insights into the patterns of interaction among psychological, biological, and functional variables in patients with cancer and help identify potential targets for future intervention strategies.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Participants were recruited via purposive sampling from the oncology departments of two comprehensive tertiary hospitals in Nanjing (Jiangsu Province) and Tianjin, China. Three measurement waves were designed to capture temporal changes in perceived stress, psychological states, biological markers, and functional and symptom outcomes across three clinically distinct phases of cancer treatment: the pre-treatment period (T1), the active systemic treatment phase (T2), and the early post-treatment period (T3). This phased design was adopted because the neuroendocrine and inflammatory milieu can shift substantially as patients move across these transitions, enabling direct examination of whether the temporal architecture of the stress–biology–symptom pathways remains stable or is reconfigured across the cancer continuum.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) histopathologically or cytologically confirmed malignant tumor diagnosis; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) expected survival of at least one year as assessed by the attending physician; (4) intact consciousness with adequate reading comprehension and verbal communication; and (5) voluntary participation with written informed consent. Participants were excluded if they had: (1) comorbid severe psychiatric disorder or cognitive impairment; (2) comorbid severe physical conditions precluding study participation; or (3) prior receipt of psychological crisis intervention or major emergency treatment.

Baseline data (T1) were collected after diagnostic confirmation and prior to initiation of systemic treatment. The second wave (T2) was conducted approximately 4–8 weeks after treatment initiation, and the third wave (T3) approximately 8–12 weeks after completion of the primary treatment course.

Data were collected by trained research staff following standardized procedures. After study briefing and written informed consent, participants completed self-report questionnaires and provided blood samples at each wave. Questionnaires were administered in a quiet ward or outpatient area; for participants unable to complete them independently due to illness, responses were recorded by trained staff. Completed questionnaires were checked for item completeness, and all data were double-entered and cross-verified in Microsoft Excel by two independent researchers.

Variables

Variables were selected to represent psychological, biological, functional, and symptom domains relevant to the study objectives and are described below.

Participant characteristics

Participants’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were collected using a self-developed structured questionnaire, including sex, age, marital status, place of residence, educational level, cancer type, TNM stage, and family history of cancer.

Perceived stress

Perceived stress was assessed using the 14-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-14) (Cohen et al., 1983). The scale comprises a 7-item negative subscale and a 7-item positive subscale, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = “never” to 4 = “very often”). Total scores are computed by reverse-scoring positive items and summing across all 14 items, yielding a range of 0–56, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. The Chinese version has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in previous validation studies(She et al., 2021). In the present study, internal consistency was excellent across the three assessment waves (Cronbach’s α = 0.981–0.986; see Supplementary material Table S1).

Emotional distress

Emotional distress was assessed using the Profile of Mood States Short Form (POMS-SF). The scale comprises 30 items across six subscales—tension–anxiety, depression–dejection, anger–hostility, vigor–activity, fatigue–inertia, and confusion–bewilderment—each treated as a single node in the network analysis. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 4 = “extremely”), with higher scores on negative subscales indicating greater distress and higher vigor scores reflecting better well-being. The Chinese version of the scale has been validated (Chen et al., 2002). In the present study, Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.928 to 0.941 for the total POMS-SF and from 0.828 to 0.875 across the six subscales over the three measurement waves (see Supplementary material Table S1).

Biomarkers

Biomarkers were selected to index the intermediate biological processes linking perceived stress and emotional distress to downstream changes in functional status and symptom burden (Reis et al., 2025). Three markers were included: CRP and IL-6 as pro-inflammatory indicators with established positive associations with psychosocial stress (Blaes et al., 2023; Jehn et al., 2012), and cortisol as an index of HPA axis activity whose diurnal rhythm can be flattened under sustained stress exposure (Chang et al., 2022).

Blood samples were collected at each wave (T1–T3). Venous blood was drawn between 08:00 and 10:00 after an overnight fast to control for diurnal variation in cortisol (Juliana et al., 2025; Mohd Azmi et al., 2021). Serum was obtained by centrifugation and either analyzed immediately or stored at −80 °C until assay. All assays followed the standardized procedures of the hospital’s clinical laboratory and were conducted under identical conditions across time points to ensure longitudinal comparability.

Functional status and symptom burden

Functional status and symptom burden were assessed using selected subscales of the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire–Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) (Fayers et al., 2002). Subscales were selected to reflect core domains most relevant to daily functioning and most commonly examined in psychosocial oncology research. Functional domains included Physical Functioning (PF; 5 items), Cognitive Functioning (CF; 2 items), and Social Functioning (SF; 2 items); symptom domains comprised Fatigue (FA; 3 items), Pain (PA; 2 items), and Insomnia (SL; 1 item). Items were rated on a 4-point scale (1 = “not at all” to 4 = “very much”) and linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale per the EORTC scoring manual, with higher functional scores indicating better health status and higher symptom scores reflecting greater severity. The EORTC QLQ-C30 has been extensively validated across diverse cancer populations (Gundy et al., 2012). In the present study, EORTC functioning and symptom subscales generally showed acceptable to good reliability, with comparatively lower values for shorter subscales (e.g., CF and SF at specific waves), consistent with their limited item counts. Because SL consists of a single item, Cronbach’s alpha is not applicable (see Supplementary material Table S1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.2.

Data preprocessing

Missing data arising from study attrition and intermittent non-response were assumed to be missing at random (MAR) and handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) (Azur et al., 2011), implemented via the mice package (Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). Twenty imputed datasets were generated with 20 iterations per dataset (maxit = 20); continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, binary variables using logistic regression, and unordered categorical variables using polytomous regression.

To reduce right skewness and the influence of extreme values, biomarkers were log-transformed using log(x + c), where c was set to one-half of the smallest observed positive value for each biomarker across all participants and time points (Vetter, 2017). This constant was held fixed across waves to ensure longitudinal comparability (FENG et al., 2014), yielding values of 1.500 mg/L for CRP, 0.836 pg/mL for IL-6, and 7.516 nmol/L for cortisol. Log-transformed values were used in all subsequent analyses.

Descriptive analysis

Participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics at baseline, and variable scores across all three waves, were summarized using descriptive statistics: means and standard deviations for approximately normally distributed continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges for biomarkers, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Cronbach’s α was computed for each scale at each time point (Connelly, 2011). All descriptive statistics and reliability coefficients were estimated within each imputed dataset and pooled across imputations using Rubin’s rules, which account for both within- and between-imputation variability (Austin et al., 2025; Sterne et al., 2009). Preliminary associations among study variables were examined using Spearman correlation analyses (Yang & Lee, 2025). Descriptive tables and figures were prepared using the openxlsx, officer, and flextable packages in R.

Cross-Lagged panel networks

To address all three research questions, the temporal dynamics among study variables were examined using CLPN models estimated within a regularized multivariate regression framework, implemented using the glmnet package (Friedman et al., 2010). Notably, no universally accepted method currently exists for formal sample size or statistical power determination in CLPN models; the stability of the estimated networks was therefore evaluated through ridge regularization, nonparametric bootstrapping, case-dropping procedures, and sensitivity analyses.

Directed edges were estimated separately for each measurement interval (T1→T2 and T2→T3) using ridge-penalized regression. Ridge regularization was preferred over sparsity-inducing alternatives (e.g., LASSO) because it continuously shrinks all parameters without forcing any to zero, thereby reducing estimation variance and improving reproducibility—a critical advantage under multicollinearity and when retaining potentially meaningful temporal associations across a densely connected multi-domain network (Sztepanacz & Houle, 2024). The regularization parameter (λ) was selected via 10-fold cross-validation with the one standard error rule (Abram et al., 2016; Chen & Yang, 2021). In the CLPN framework, each variable at time t was simultaneously entered as a predictor of all variables at t + 1, including itself. Thus, each directed edge represents the unique prospective association from one node to another after accounting for all other network nodes measured at the previous wave. External baseline sociodemographic and clinical covariates were not included in the final CLPN estimation. Autoregressive edges index the temporal stability of individual variables; cross-lagged edges capture directed predictive associations between distinct variables over time (Wysocki et al., 2025).

Networks were visualized using the qgraph package (Epskamp et al., 2012). A fixed “butterfly-shaped” layout was applied consistently across both temporal networks to facilitate visual comparison. To avoid visual clutter, adaptive thresholding was applied to cross-lagged edges for display purposes only (minimum absolute weight ≈ 0.03); all estimated edges, including those below this threshold, were retained in all quantitative analyses. Global network structure was summarized using network density and mean absolute edge weight (Flores-Cohaila et al., 2025; Nunes et al., 2025).

To distinguish overall strongest associations from cross-domain mechanistic pathways, two complementary edge sets were reported. Top edges denote the strongest connections across the full network for a given interval, ranked by absolute edge weight, encompassing both within-domain and cross-domain pairs. Top bridge edges were identified by first restricting candidates to predefined cross-domain connections and then selecting those with the largest absolute weights; this approach more directly captures cross-domain coupling and facilitates mechanistic interpretation (Jones et al., 2021).

Node centrality was characterized using in-expected influence (in-EI), reflecting total incoming edge weight, and out-expected influence (out-EI), reflecting total outgoing edge weight. A high out-EI indicates that a node has strong predictive associations with other nodes, whereas a high in-EI indicates that a node is strongly predicted by other nodes (Hao et al., 2025). In addition, bridge in-EI and bridge out-EI were calculated to identify key nodes linking different theoretical domains (Jones et al., 2021).

Network stability and accuracy

Edge weight accuracy and the uncertainty of autoregressive effects were evaluated using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1000 iterations, and 95% confidence intervals reported (Epskamp et al., 2018). The robustness of centrality estimates was assessed via case-dropping subset bootstrap procedures (B = 1000), summarized by correlation stability (CS) coefficients. CS coefficients ≥ 0.25 indicate acceptable stability; values ≥ 0.50 are considered preferable and indicative of good robustness (Epskamp et al., 2018).

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate whether network findings were sensitive to the biomarker preprocessing strategy, the primary log-transformation (log(x + c)) was compared against a rank-based inverse normal transformation, with all other model specifications, estimation procedures, and regularization parameters held constant (Lee et al., 2025). Network consistency between the two strategies was quantified separately for T1→T2 and T2→T3 using: (a) Pearson correlations between corresponding cross-lagged edge weights; (b) overlap and Jaccard similarity of the strongest K edges (K = 15); and (c) sign concordance of overlapping edges, interpreted using conventional effect size guidelines for psychological network analysis (Epskamp et al., 2018; Forbes et al., 2021; Hevey, 2018).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Ethics No 2025–233). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence for their clinical care, and remained free to decline any study component. All personal information was anonymized and stored in password-protected databases with access restricted to authorized research personnel.

Results

Participants

Of the 295 patients initially approached for enrollment, 276 (93.6%) completed the baseline assessment (T1). Subsequently, 258 (93.5% of T1 completers) and 241 (87.3% of T1 completers) participants completed the follow-up assessments at T2 and T3, respectively. These three assessment waves corresponded, respectively, to the pre-treatment period, the active systemic treatment phase, and the early post-treatment period, as specified in the study design. Among the 35 participants (12.7%) who discontinued after T1, reasons for attrition included death (n = 8, 22.9%), deteriorating health status precluding further participation (n = 15, 42.9%), voluntary withdrawal of consent (n = 9, 25.7%), and loss to follow-up (n = 3, 8.6%). Network edges

Across both temporal CLPNs, all nodes demonstrated positive self-lagged effects, indicating substantial temporal stability across assessment waves. The median self-lagged coefficient was 0.438 (range: 0.261–0.642) for the T1→T2 network and 0.456 (range: 0.345–0.559) for the T2→T3 network (Supplementary material Table S2). In the T1→T2 network, the strongest self-lagged effect was observed for perceived stress (β = 0.642), whereas in the T2→T3 network, pain (β = 0.559) showed the largest autoregressive coefficients.

Supplementary material Tables S3 and S4 list the top 15 edges and bridge edges with the largest weights in the two networks, respectively. In the T1→T2 network, perceived stress showed several prominent prospective associations with emotional distress variables, including Confusion (β = 0.268), Fatigue (β = 0.254), Anger (β = 0.178), and Tension (β = 0.169). Within the emotional domain, several strong intra-domain associations were also identified, most notably Depression → Confusion (β = −0.167) and Vigor → Confusion (β = 0.158). Several prominent bridge edges linked emotional distress variables with biological markers, including Fatigue → Cortisol (β = 0.295), Fatigue → CRP (β = 0.235), and Fatigue → IL-6 (β = 0.160). Within the trunk of the network, the strongest edges linking the inflammatory biomarkers and functioning domains were negative. In addition, strong bridge edges were observed between the physical functioning and biomarker domains (SF → Cortisol, β = −0.167). The symptom nodes in the tail of the network also exhibited strong cross-lagged associations with the physical functioning domain. Notably, a prospective association from IL-6 to confusion (β = 0.142) was identified, indicating that temporal associations were observed in both directions across some domains rather than being confined to the study’s conceptual ordering.

In the T2→T3 network, perceived stress remained prospectively associated with multiple emotional distress variables. Emotional distress remained linked to the biological domain, with the edges from Tension to CRP (β = 0.140) and Cortisol (β = 0.129) showing markedly larger weights compared with the T1→T2 network. Within the trunk, biomarkers remained negatively predictive of functioning. Symptom burden in the tail continued to be predicted by functional impairment. Compared with the T1→T2 network, biomarkers appeared more prominently among the higher-ranked predictive edges for symptoms, exemplified by IL-6 → FA (β = 0.140). A feedback pathway from the symptom domain to the emotional domain was also observed (FA → Confusion, β = 0.146). Overall, the two temporal networks showed similar cross-domain organizational patterns, while differences were observed in the relative strength of several associations across the two intervals. Specifically, stronger associations involving fatigue and biomarkers were observed in the T1→T2 network, whereas associations involving tension and biomarkers were more prominent in the T2→T3 network, and biomarker-to-symptom connections became more prominent in the later interval.

Centrality and bridge centrality

Centrality analyses indicated that in the T1→T2 network, PSS (0.852), Tension (0.787), and Fatigue (0.704) exhibited relatively high overall out-EI, whereas Confusion showed the highest in-EI (0.757) and bridge in-EI (0.859). Nodes with high bridge out-EI were PSS (0.852), Fatigue (0.745), and Tension (0.574). These findings suggest that at the pre-treatment-to-active-treatment transition, perceived stress and fatigue emerged as the primary prospective predictors, whereas confusion was primarily influenced by predictive associations from other nodes.

In the T2→T3 network, nodes with high overall out-EI included Fatigue (1.095), Tension (0.744), and PSS (0.601), while nodes with high in-EI were Fatigue (0.600), Confusion (0.430), and Tension (0.429). High bridge out-EI was observed for Fatigue (0.784), PSS (0.601), and Tension (0.594), whereas high bridge in-EI was observed for Fatigue (0.410), Tension (0.363), and Confusion (0.346). Network accuracy and stability

Bootstrap analyses of edge weights indicated reliable edge estimation in both networks (see Supplementary material Figure S3). Centrality stability analyses showed that in the T1→T2 network, the CS coefficients were 0.6 for in-EI and bridge in-EI, and 0.7 for out-EI and bridge out-EI. In the T2→T3 network, the CS coefficients were 0.6 for overall EI, 0.6 for bridge in-EI, and 0.7 for bridge out-EI (see Supplementary material Figures S4 and S5). All CS coefficients exceeded 0.50, indicating that the relative ordering of both overall EI and bridge EI remained stable under substantial case-dropping in both networks.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis indicated a high degree of consistency between the primary log-transformed and the rank inverse normal transformed biomarker networks across both time windows.

Edge-weight correlations were high across the two time intervals, with a correlation coefficient of 0.975 for the T1→T2 network and 0.995 for the T2→T3 network. The sets of strongest edges also showed substantial overlap. Among the top 15 edges, marked overlap was observed, with an overlap proportion of 0.800 and a Jaccard coefficient of 0.667 for the T1→T2 network, and an overlap proportion of 0.933 and a Jaccard coefficient of 0.875 for the T2→T3 network. Moreover, all overlapping edges exhibited complete directional consistency across both intervals (sign concordance = 1.000), indicating that the directional interpretation of key cross-domain pathways was robust to the choice of biomarker preprocessing strategy.

Discussion

In this three-wave study of patients with cancer, we applied CLPN models to disentangle the temporal and directional associations among perceived stress, multidimensional emotional distress, biomarkers, and functional and symptom indicators. The study spanned the pre-treatment, active treatment, and early post-treatment phases. By integrating psychological and biological variables within a unified longitudinal network framework, we observed a butterfly-shaped network topology and a consistent pattern of temporal associations linking stress, emotional distress, inflammatory biomarkers, functional status, and symptoms. Together, these findings suggest that the temporally ordered pattern of prospective associations linking stress and symptoms in cancer is not only directional but also temporally dynamic, with distinct nodes assuming shifting structural roles as patients progress through the treatment continuum.

Specifically, across both intervals, the strongest bridge edges originating from perceived stress were directed toward emotional nodes. This pattern was most pronounced at the pre-treatment-to-active-treatment transition (T1→T2), where perceived stress exhibited the highest bridge out-EI, suggesting that it may have functioned as a temporal antecedent to subsequent cross-domain associations. This pattern is consistent with stress and coping theory, which conceptualizes stress appraisal as an antecedent of emotional disturbance (Folkman, 2013). Notably, stress showed particularly strong effects on fatigue and tension, both of which later assumed central bridging roles in the network, linking psychological distress to inflammatory and functional indicators. As elaborated in the centrality analyses below, compared with the T1→T2 interval, the bridge out-EI of perceived stress was lower during the T2→T3 interval, suggesting that its upstream influence may be most consequential in the period surrounding initial diagnosis and treatment entry, when the stress of confronting an uncertain medical trajectory is most acute.

Previous research has demonstrated a mutually reinforcing relationship between psychological distress and biomarkers (Acevedo-Ibarra et al., 2024; Gan et al., 2025). Our study further delineates the predictive pathways between these domains in a population of patients with cancer. The CLPNs indicated that cross-lagged edges predominantly extended from emotional distress nodes to subsequent biomarkers. Critically, the specific emotional node dominating the affect–biomarker interface shifted across treatment phases. In the T1→T2 network, both fatigue and depression predicted subsequent increases in cortisol, and fatigue served as the primary affect–biomarker bridge, positively predicting CRP and IL-6. In the T2→T3 network, however, tension displaced fatigue as the principal emotional pathway linking psychological distress to inflammatory markers, with tension → CRP and tension → cortisol pathways displaying markedly larger weights than in the earlier interval. This phase-specific shift from a pattern characterized primarily by fatigue-related associations to one characterized primarily by tension-related associations represents a novel observation in the temporal relationships between emotional distress and inflammatory biomarkers. One possible interpretation is that different dimensions of emotional distress may show distinct temporal associations with inflammatory biomarkers across the treatment trajectory. During active treatment, fatigue may be more closely associated with subsequent inflammatory biomarker changes, whereas during the transition to post-treatment, tension related to concerns about disease recurrence and adjustment to reduced treatment support may become more prominently associated with inflammatory biomarkers. In contrast, relatively few pathways were observed from biomarkers to emotional distress. This suggests that emotional distress may temporally precede inflammation, rather than simply being predicted by it—a finding that challenges the traditional view of inflammation as purely upstream of psychological symptoms and has important implications for early psychological intervention.

Across both networks, biomarkers exhibited negative predictive associations with functioning. This finding is consistent with previous evidence indicating that inflammation is associated with impairments in energy, cognitive performance, and social participation among patients with cancer (Duivon et al., 2024; Lipshitz et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024). In the T1→T2 network, IL-6 negatively predicted SF and PF. In the T2→T3 interval, the negative predictive effect of IL-6 on CF became more pronounced, and the cumulative inflammatory burden appeared to show progressively stronger prospective associations with functional capacity. This progressive amplification of the biomarker-to-functioning pathway is consistent with the biological trajectory of treatment, in which sustained inflammatory activation during active therapy may be progressively associated with reduced functional reserves, such that patients may show increasing susceptibility to inflammation-related functional impairment as treatment advances. Functional impairment, particularly reduced physical and cognitive functioning, emerged as a key node linking biological alterations to symptoms such as fatigue and insomnia. Moreover, compared with the T1→T2 network, predictive pathways from biomarkers to functioning and from functioning to symptoms ranked higher among the top bridge edges in the T2→T3 network and accounted for a larger proportion. This pattern suggests that prospective associations involving biomarkers and functioning became more prominent at later stages, with these domains appearing as temporal antecedents to symptom burden within the observed temporal network. Accordingly, the focus of clinical interventions may need to be adjusted.

Although the overall network structure was broadly consistent with the hypothesized temporal ordering of prospective associations—from perceived stress through emotional distress and inflammatory biomarkers to physical functioning and symptoms, the presence of feedback pathways highlights the dynamic complexity of cancer-related symptoms. For example, in the T1→T2 network, IL-6 predicted subsequent confusion, a pathway consistent with evidence that inflammatory cytokines can modulate neurotransmitter metabolism and alter neural activity in brain regions implicated in emotion regulation, thereby disrupting affective states (Han & Ham, 2021). In the T2→T3 network, fatigue predicted subsequent confusion, suggesting that as treatment progresses, symptom processes may increasingly become associated with emotional disruption alongside inflammatory mechanisms. This phase-specific shift in the predominant pattern of feedback associations—from biomarker-to-emotion during T1→T2 to symptom-to-emotion during T2→T3—was accompanied by broader changes in the temporal organization of prospective associations across treatment phases and further highlights the dynamic nature of the longitudinal network. In addition, physical conditions such as pain and fatigue may be cognitively appraised as threatening or distressing, directly amplifying negative emotional experiences (Kim et al., 2023). The identification of these bidirectional pathways underscores the necessity of holistic intervention strategies that simultaneously address psychological, biological, and symptom-related domains, rather than treating them in isolation.

Centrality and bridge centrality indices identified key nodes within the network, and directly addressed RQ2 and RQ3 by characterizing both the identity of cross-domain connectors and the temporal evolution of their structural roles across the treatment trajectory. Consistent with the temporal network pattern, perceived stress exhibited the highest out-EI and bridge out-EI, but relatively low in-EI and bridge in-EI, in the T1→T2 network. This profile suggests that perceived stress was more strongly associated with subsequent changes in other domains than with being predicted by preceding nodes, indicating that it may function as an early temporal predictor within the longitudinal network, aligning with the notion that perceived stress may temporally precede psychological distress in patients (Liu et al., 2025). Notably, fatigue showed a progressive increase in its centrality ranking over time. In the T2→T3 network, fatigue exhibited the highest in-/out-EI as well as the highest bridge in- and out-EI. This pattern suggests that fatigue may occupy a structurally central position, simultaneously transmitting and accumulating longitudinal influences across domains. Fatigue is a common symptom across various cancer treatments and often intensifies during the course of therapy. It has been linked to immune and endocrine processes and substantially compromises patients’ quality of life, underscoring the need for prioritized clinical attention (García-González et al., 2025). Beyond the prominent temporal association between perceived stress and fatigue, tension also showed a notable change in its network position across the two intervals: whereas it ranked as the third highest bridge out-EI node in T1→T2, it emerged simultaneously as a high bridge out-EI and high bridge in-EI node in T2→T3. This pattern suggests that, during the post-treatment period, tension became more extensively involved in longitudinal associations spanning symptom and emotional domains, both as a predictor of subsequent changes and as a node associated with preceding changes in other variables. One possible explanation is that patients transitioning out of active treatment may experience persistent tension related to fear of recurrence, uncertainty surrounding follow-up surveillance, and reduced contact with healthcare providers. These psychosocial challenges may contribute to the broader pattern of temporal associations involving tension observed in the present network. Additionally, functional indicators exhibited negative in-/out-EI in both temporal networks, indicating that they primarily act as convergence points for negative influences while also exerting feedback effects on nodes, thereby forming a negative feedback loop (Ding et al., 2024). Collectively, the phase-specific shifts in centrality profiles—from PSS-dominated in T1→T2 to fatigue-dominated in T2→T3, with tension assuming an increasingly integrative role—suggest that the relative positions of key nodes within the longitudinal network varied across treatment phases, constituting an answer to RQ3 and providing useful guidance for the timing of targeted clinical interventions.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study provide several novel insights for clinical practice and translational intervention. First, the temporally stratified structure suggested that early perceived stress was prospectively associated with subsequent changes across multiple emotional and symptom domains, indicating that stress screening and psychosocial support may be particularly important during the pre-treatment and early treatment phases. Therefore, integrating stress screening and stress management approaches (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness-based stress reduction) into routine oncology care may provide broader benefits for patients (Dehnavi & Ghodsi, 2025).

Second, the consistently high centrality and bridge centrality of fatigue across treatment phases suggest that fatigue may warrant closer monitoring throughout the treatment continuum. Given its broad temporal associations with both symptom and emotional domains, comprehensive fatigue assessment and evidence-based fatigue management—such as tailored exercise programs and energy conservation and activity management (ECAM)—may be particularly valuable as part of routine supportive cancer care (Barsevick et al., 2004; Li et al., 2024). Furthermore, tension ranked among the nodes with the highest out-EI and in-EI during the T2→T3 interval, suggesting that it was extensively involved in temporal associations with multiple symptom and emotional domains during the post-treatment transition. Therefore, the clinical management of anxiety-related tension warrants heightened attention as patients approach treatment completion. Psycho-oncological interventions targeting fear of recurrence and end-of-treatment transition anxiety—the likely psychological substrates of elevated tension at this phase—may be particularly important for mitigating the inflammation-related pathway associated with tension in the T2→T3 network.

As the disease trajectory progresses, the feedback pathways from functioning to symptoms and from symptoms to emotional distress appear to strengthen, suggesting that once functional impairment and symptom burden increase, they may in turn amplify emotional distress. In clinical practice, integrating symptom management with functional rehabilitation within multidisciplinary oncology teams may help attenuate the intensity of these feedback loops (Ebbestad et al., 2023). In addition, the predictive influence of inflammatory indicators strengthened at later time points, highlighting the potential value of continued assessment of key inflammatory biomarkers, such as IL-6, CRP, and cortisol, alongside routine clinical and psychosocial follow-up. These findings collectively underscore the value of integrating psychosocial assessment, biological monitoring, and functional rehabilitation within a unified, phase-sensitive clinical management framework for cancer patients.

Strengths and limitations

This study employed a three-wave longitudinal design, with assessment waves deliberately aligned with the pre-treatment, active systemic treatment, and early post-treatment phases, enabling not only the examination of temporal and directional associations within each treatment interval but also the comparison of longitudinal network structures across clinically meaningful stages of the cancer treatment trajectory. By applying a cross-lagged panel network model, we were able to integrate stress, emotional, biological, functional, and symptom-related variables within a unified dynamic framework, enabling the identification of temporal predictive associations both within and across domains that are difficult to characterize using traditional regression approaches and extending prior research. The integration of objective biomarkers with patient-reported outcomes enhanced the multidimensional relevance of the findings and provided a more comprehensive depiction of symptom dynamics in cancer.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, CLPN edge weights reflect prospective conditional associations after accounting for other network nodes measured at the previous wave, rather than causal effects or strictly defined mediation pathways (Borsboom et al., 2021; Sorjonen et al., 2025; Wysocki et al., 2025). In addition, although centrality and bridge centrality indices provide information on the relative structural position of nodes within the longitudinal network, their clinical interpretation, causal relevance, and replicability remain subjects of ongoing methodological debate (Ryan & Hamaker, 2022). Accordingly, the highly central and bridging nodes identified here should be regarded as candidate points of reference for future intervention research rather than as established or definitively effective intervention targets. Second, CRP, IL-6, and cortisol are non-specific biological markers that may be influenced by multiple clinical and physiological factors, such as infections, medications, obesity, metabolic disorders, comorbidities, and cancer treatments. Because these factors were not comprehensively measured or controlled, residual confounding may have influenced the observed longitudinal associations. Additionally, biomarker assessments were limited to CRP, IL-6, and cortisol; inclusion of other relevant markers (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) could provide a more complete picture of inflammatory processes. Third, perceived stress was assessed exclusively using the PSS-14. Although this instrument is well validated for measuring general perceived stress, it may not fully capture cancer-specific stressors, such as fear of recurrence, treatment-related uncertainty, or existential concerns. Incorporating cancer-specific stress measures in future studies may provide a more comprehensive assessment of stress experiences. Fourth, the assessment schedule was aligned with typical treatment cycles, allowing comparisons across treatment phases; however, each phase was represented by a single assessment, precluding the examination of within-phase temporal dynamics. More intensive longitudinal designs, including shorter assessment intervals or ecological momentary assessment, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of dynamic symptom processes. Finally, the sample comprised patients with cancer recruited from a limited number of clinical institutions, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings across diverse healthcare settings. Related to this issue, participants were recruited using a broad malignant tumor diagnosis criterion, resulting in heterogeneity in cancer type and TNM stage. Given the available sample size and the increasing complexity of the longitudinal network model, the final CLPN models did not include external clinical covariates such as cancer type, TNM stage, or treatment modality. Consequently, residual clinical heterogeneity may have contributed to between-patient variability in inflammatory biomarkers, symptom burden, and psychological adjustment, while also influencing the magnitude and pattern of the estimated temporal associations among network nodes. The observed pathways should therefore be interpreted as cross-domain temporal associations within a heterogeneous oncology sample rather than cancer-specific, stage-specific, or treatment-specific mechanisms. Future research should recruit larger and more clinically diverse samples across multiple healthcare settings, examine whether temporal associations differ according to cancer type, TNM stage, treatment modality, and treatment intensity, incorporate cancer-specific measures of stress alongside a broader panel of inflammatory biomarkers, more comprehensively assess biological and treatment-related confounders, and employ denser longitudinal sampling to capture symptom dynamics with greater temporal resolution.

Conclusion

This study employed a three-wave longitudinal design anchored to the pre-treatment, active systemic treatment, and early post-treatment phases, and applied a cross-lagged panel network model to integrate perceived stress, emotional distress, inflammatory biomarkers, functioning, and symptoms among patients with cancer within a unified longitudinal network framework. Addressing the three research questions, the findings demonstrated: (RQ1) a consistent hierarchical temporal structure of prospective associations was observed, with perceived stress, emotional and biological variables, and functional and symptom-related variables occupying relatively earlier, intermediate, and later positions within the longitudinal network; (RQ2) perceived stress, fatigue, and tension consistently exhibited high outgoing and bridge expected influence, suggesting that these nodes may warrant particular attention during clinical monitoring and supportive care. Confusion consistently exhibited high in-EI, suggesting that it may serve as a clinically informative indicator of the cumulative influence of multiple psychological, biological, and symptom-related processes; and (RQ3) the temporal pattern of cross-domain associations was not static across treatment phases. Perceived stress exhibited the most prominent predictive role during the early phase, whereas fatigue became the more prominent predictor during the later phase. These findings deepen our understanding of the temporal dynamics underlying symptom progression and highlight the dynamic, hierarchical nature of cancer-related symptom development. Moreover, the prominent bridging roles of perceived stress during the early treatment phase, fatigue throughout the treatment trajectory, and tension during the active-to-post-treatment transition underscore the importance of phase-sensitive psychosocial management and targeted intervention throughout the disease trajectory, thereby informing the timing and prioritization of multidisciplinary supportive care.
