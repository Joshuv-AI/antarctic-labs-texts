Mass balance study of [14C]-ABBV-552, a synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A positive modulator, in healthy male participants following single oral dose administration
Authors: Nick Miles, Brian McNamee, Sharif I. Soleman, Cindy Zadikoff, Joey Boiser, Loic Laplanche, Richard Burton, Mohamad Shebley, Ramesh R. Boinpally
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeSynaptic density is strongly correlated with cognitive function in many neurological disorders. All synapses contain synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2 A (SV2A), making it a potential therapeutic target for neurodecline. ABBV-552, a high-affinity positive SV2A modulator, increases neurotransmitter release, improving synaptic efficiency. This Phase 1, open-label, mass balance study examined ABBV-552 safety, pharmacokinetics, metabolism, and elimination.MethodsEight healthy males were orally administered 15 mg [14C]-ABBV-552. Safety was assessed via treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE), vital sign, electrocardiogram, and laboratory value monitoring. Serial blood, urine, and fecal samples were collected pre-dose, Days 1–14, and daily until discharge. Sample radioactivity was measured with HPLC and metabolites characterized with mass spectrometry.ResultsEight participants (30–49 years old) received 15 mg [14C]-ABBV-552 (~ 100 µCi) and were included in analyses (3 prematurely withdrew [personal reasons]). All TEAEs were Grade 1 in severity; euphoria (25.0%), dizziness (25.0%), and muscular weakness (12.5%) were deemed possibly-related to ABBV-552. No meaningful changes in laboratory values, vital signs, or ECGs were observed. Mean radioactive recovery was 78.8% (urine: 70.9%, feces: 7.83%), with < 1% parent drug in excreta. Parent and one metabolite (M2, mono-oxidized parent) accounted for 84% and 13%, respectively, of circulating drug-related material. M5, M9, and M33 were most prominent in urine (~ 13%, ~ 17%, ~ 11% of dose, respectively); M2/M36 (coeluted; 1.4% of dose) in feces.ConclusionsOrally-administered ABBV-552 was well absorbed and primarily recovered in urine as metabolites. M2 was the only major circulating metabolite. Therefore, ABBV-552 is primarily hepatically metabolized and metabolites are renally eliminated. These findings support further investigation of ABBV-552 in neurodegenerative disorders.Clinical trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06278766)Date of registration: 26 February 2024Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00228-026-04078-8.

Introduction

Synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2 A (SV2A), a transmembrane protein of pre-synaptic vesicles, is present in all synaptic terminals and plays a key role in regulating neurotransmitter release and synaptic function [1]. SV2A density is measurable with positive emission tomography (PET) scanning and has become a biomarker for synaptic density. Because reductions in synaptic density have been strongly correlated with cognitive function in a variety of neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders [2], SV2A has become a therapeutic target of interest for several conditions, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD).

ABBV-552 (also known as SDI-118) is an orally available small molecule and is a high affinity positive modulator of SV2A. The molecular structure of ABBV-552 and location of the 14C radiolabel are provided in Fig. S1. ABBV-552 has been shown to enhance synaptic efficiency by increasing presynaptic neurotransmitter release. Pre-clinical studies with SV2A modulators have shown promising cognitive function improvement in some neurological disorders [3], with ABBV-552 having acceptable toxicology/pharmacology [4], high radioactive recovery (≥ 94%; unpublished data) in rats and dogs, and cognition enhancing effect in rodents with cognitive deficit [4]. Thus, ABBV-552 held the potential for a pro-cognitive effect in patients with AD through direct synaptic function modulation. However, the Phase 2b dose-ranging, proof-of-concept trial did not meet its primary efficacy endpoint (change from baseline in the 14-item Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale) [5]. The pharmacokinetics (PK), tolerability, and safety of ABBV-552 were previously evaluated in a single-ascending dose study (up to 80 mg) in healthy participants (NCT05486195), showing an acceptable safety and tolerability profile (N = 32). The most common adverse events (AEs) included dizziness (28%), hypersomnia (16%), and somnolence (6%) [4]. All drug-related AEs were mild in severity, no serious AEs occurred, and no participant discontinued due to AE [4]. No new safety signals were identified with repeated dosing ≤ 15 mg/day of ABBV-552 [5]. The follow-up multiple ascending dose study showed similar findings (unpublished data). ABBV-552 PK data showed rapid absorption after oral dosing (maximum plasma concentration 1.5 h post-dose), a long elimination half-life (34–50 h for 0.3–80 mg single dose), and dose-proportional PK [4]. These findings supported daily administration of ABBV-552 [4]. ABBV-552 had dose-dependent modulation of SV2A, very good penetration into the brain, and a predicted SV2A occupancy of 76% at a dose of 10 mg/day (based on steady-state trough concentration) [4].

Human mass balance studies are critical to the drug development process, providing vital information on absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) of both the parent drug and its metabolites. These studies also provide key information for further compound development, guiding the need for studies in special populations (e.g., renal and hepatic impairment) and advising on the safe enrollment of future clinical trials [6–8]. The current mass balance study was performed in healthy, male participants who were administered a single oral dose of radiolabeled ABBV-552 ([14C]-ABBV-552). We present data on ABBV-552 exposures, describe the identified metabolites and their exposures, detail elimination pathways, and summarize safety outcomes.

Methodology

The Independent Ethics Committee or Institutional Review Board at the study site approved the study protocol, informed consent forms, and recruitment materials before patient enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the International Conference for Harmonisation guidelines, applicable regulations, and the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before screening.

Patients and study design

Regulatory guidance suggests that at least 6 evaluable participants be included in the overall analysis [8]. To account for potential dropout, 8 healthy adult male participants were enrolled in this Phase 1, single radiolabeled dose, open-label, single-center, mass balance study (NCT06278766). All participants were 18–55 years of age, non-smokers, and had a body mass index (BMI) of 18–32 kg/m2. Key exclusion criteria included known use of inhibitors or inducers of cytochrome P450 3 A (CYP3A), as well as participation in a radiolabeled study within 4 months if exposures were known by the investigator, or 6 months if exposures were not known by the investigator. On Study Day 1, each participant received a single oral dose of 15 mg [14C]-ABBV-552 (~ 100 µCi) after an overnight fast (≥ 10 h); fasting continued for 4 h after dosing. Regulatory guidance [8] recommends that the investigational drug dose used in human mass balance studies be the intended clinical dose (if known), or in the anticipated therapeutic dose range. The 15 mg dose selected for the current study matched the maximum dose administered in a Phase 2b dose-finding trial [5]. Serial blood, plasma, urine, and fecal samples for determination of the mass balance of [14C]-ABBV‑552, were collected for a minimum of 336 h (14 days) after dosing and up to a maximum of 648 h (27 days) after dosing. Plasma and whole blood samples were collected for drug/metabolite concentration measurement, radioactivity measurement, and metabolite identification at predefined time points before and after dosing (Tables S1 and S2). Safety was monitored throughout the study with adverse event (AE) reporting, vital sign measurements, orthostatic vital sign measurements, electrocardiogram (ECG), and clinical laboratory testing (hematology, chemistry, urinalysis). Participants were confined to the study site beginning the day before dosing (Day -1) through Day 14 (15 days total; Fig. 1). If the following discharge criteria were not met, participants continued confinement (maximum of 12 additional days) and sample collection: > 90% of total radioactivity recovered or < 1% of radioactive dose recovered in two consecutive 24-hour combined urine and fecal collection periods. Participants received a standard high-fiber diet for all meals during confinement.

Plasma and urine ABBV-552 concentration measurements

Blood and urine samples were collected for ABBV-552 concentration analysis into lithium heparin-containing collection tubes and containers, respectively, at prespecified timepoints and intervals, respectively (Tables S1 and S2). Blood samples were collected pre-dose; 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 15, 24, 36, and 48 h post-dose, and every 24 h during confinement. Within 90 min of collection, blood samples were processed in a refrigerated centrifuge (~ 2000 g for 10 min, ~ 4 °C), plasma was transferred to labeled storage tubes, and samples stored at colder than -70 °C. Urine samples were collected pre-dose (12-hours prior) and 0–6, 6–12, 12–24, and 24–48 h post-dose, and subsequent 24 h intervals during confinement. Urine samples were stored at approximately -20 °C until being shipped for analysis to AbbVie’s Drug Metabolism and Disposition (DMD) group of the Quantitative, Translational & ADME Sciences (QTAS) Department (North Chicago, IL). ABBV-552 concentrations were measured using validated methods for liquid-liquid extraction followed by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometric (LC-MS/MS) detection (API 5500, SCIEX, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA). Peak areas of ABBV-552 and internal standards were obtained using Analyst™ software (version 1.7; SCIEX). All samples within a run used the same integration parameters. Integration data underwent regression and quantitation and a calibration curve was derived from the peak area ratio of ABBV-552 to the standard concentration (8 concentration levels ranging 0.200–500 ng/mL for plasma and 0.200–200 ng/mL for urine). A weighting of 1/x2 was used for curve fitting where x is the concentration of a given standard. ABBV-552 concentrations were then back-calculated using the calibration curve regression equation. Results were compared to the theoretical concentrations for each standard and quality control (QC) solution to determine measurement accuracy; comparison to QC results were used to verify study sample measurement accuracy and precision. All reported results were generated within calibration curve quantitation limits and the lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) for ABBV-552 was 0.200 ng/mL for both plasma and urine matrices. Any values below LLOQ were reported as zero.

Blood, plasma, urine, and fecal radioactivity measurements

Blood samples for whole blood and plasma total radioactivity measurement and metabolite identification were collected into K2EDTA-containing collection tubes at pre-specified timepoints (Tables S1 and S2) and processed and stored as described above. Urine and fecal samples were collected into polyethylene containers and plastic bags, respectively. Urine samples were collected as described above. Fecal samples were collected pre-dose (upon check-in before dosing) and quantitatively at 24-hour intervals after dosing through 480 h or until discharge criteria had been met (90% of administered radioactivity recovered or < 1% of the radioactive dose recovered in two consecutive 24-hour urinary and fecal collection periods). Samples of plasma, feces, and urine after identification were kept frozen below -70 °C and sent to AbbVie (Drug Metabolism and Distribution, QTAS) for metabolite profiling and Labcorp (Madison, WI, USA) for radioanalysis.

Each sample was homogenized or mixed before radioanalysis (unless the entire sample was to be used). Blood, plasma, and urine samples were vortex-mixed and divided into ~ 0.2 g aliquots. Feces samples were combined by participant at 24-hour intervals and the total weight recorded. A weighed amount of 1:1 (v: v) acetonitrile:reverse osmosis water was added, and the sample was homogenized and divided into ~ 0.2 g aliquots. Blood and feces samples were combusted in a sample oxidizer (Model 307, PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), and the resulting 14CO2 was trapped in Carbo-Sorb™ (Revvity, Waltham, MA, USA) and mixed with PermaFluor™ (Revvity). Oxidation efficiency was evaluated on each day of sample combustion by analyzing a commercial radiolabeled standard both directly in scintillation cocktail and by oxidation. A combustion recovery of 95–105% was required.

All samples were analyzed for radioactivity using liquid scintillation counters (LSC; Model 4910TR, PerkinElmer [AbbVie QTAS, Labcorp] or 600 SLe, Hidex, Turku, Finland [AbbVie QTAS]) for at least 5 min or 0.6% 2-sigma, an instrument-programmed statistical significance cutoff. Ultima Gold XR scintillation (Labcorp]) or Insta-Gel Plus™ (AbbVie QTAS; both PerkinElmer) cocktail was used. All samples were analyzed in duplicate (sample size permitting), with results reported as 14C dpm/g sample. If results from sample replicates differed by > 10% from the mean value and sample aliquots had radioactivity > 200 dpm, the sample was reanalyzed after sample remixing and rehomogenation (sample size permitting). Scintillation counting data (reported in cpm) were automatically corrected for counting efficiency using the external standardization technique and an instrument-stored quench curve generated from a series of sealed, quenched standards. The LLOQ for blood, plasma, urine, and feces was 9.54, 7.16, 7.05, and 43.9 ng eq/g, respectively. Samples quantified below the LLOQ were reported as zero. For all samples, total radioactivity was determined using LSC techniques (Model 4910TR, Packard Company) and corrected for quenching with automatic external standardization. All counting batches started with an appropriate background sample for background subtraction. Additionally, dosing vials were analyzed for residual radioactivity. A weighed amount of 1:1 (v:v) acetonitrile:reverse osmosis water was added to each empty vile and a ~ 0.5 g aliquot was analyzed by LSC. Any recovered radioactivity was subtracted from the total administered dose for each participant.

Pharmacokinetic parameters

ABBV-552 and [14C]-ABBV-552 radioactivity PK parameters were estimated using noncompartmental methods. Maximum concentration (Cmax) and time to Cmax (Tmax) were determined directly from plasma or whole blood concentration-time data. The apparent terminal phase elimination rate constant (λz) was obtained as the least squares linear regression slope of the logarithms of the plasma or whole blood concentration vs. time data collected during the terminal phase. The range of concentration-time data points to include in λz calculations was determined by optimizing the adjusted R2 value, examining all points between the first three non-zero concentrations and up to (but not including) Tmax. Visual inspection was then used to remove/add data points to the calculation where appropriate. In these cases, the regression was rerun and the range reoptimized. The ABBV-552 terminal phase elimination half-life (t1/2) was calculated as ln(2)/λz.

Exposures were determined using area under the plasma or whole blood concentration-time curve (AUC). AUC from dosing (time zero) to time infinity (AUCinf) was calculated as:

where, AUCt is AUC from time zero to last measurable concentration (calculated using linear trapezoidal rule) and AUCext is extrapolated exposure (calculated as last measurable concentration/λz). The apparent oral clearance (CL/F) and apparent volume of distribution (Vz/F) were calculated administered dose/AUCinf and (CL/F)/λz, respectively.

Urine PK parameters were also examined. The fraction of unchanged drug excreted in urine (Fe) was calculated as the amount of drug recovered in the urine divided by the total administered dose. Renal clearance (CLr) was calculated as the amount of drug recovered in urine divided by AUCinf.

Extractions were performed on individual participant and all-participant AUC0-24 h pooled samples, as well as on all-participant pooled plasma samples at each sample collection time point (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96 h). All-participant pooled samples were created by transferring 350 µL from each individual/timepoint into a 5 mL Eppendorf tube. An aliquot was removed from each AUC (150 µL) and timepoint pooled (100 µL ) sample and counted on an LSC for extraction recovery calculations. Extractions were performed by adding 2-fold the initial plasma volume of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile to each tube to precipitate proteins. Samples were then vortexed for ~ 60 s and centrifuged at 20,800 g for ~ 5 min. Plasma supernatant was transferred to separate glass tubes. Using the same procedure, the pellet was washed (with 1-fold the initial volume) two additional times, with pellet sonication (15 min) during the first wash. Supernatants were combined and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at ~ 40 °C. Each sample was reconstituted on the day of analysis in 200 µL 0.1% formic acid:DMSO:acetonitrile (7:1.5:1.5 v/v/v), vortexed for 60 s, and centrifuged for 5 min at 20,800 g. An aliquot of the supernatant for each time point was used for extraction recovery determination and plasma radioprofiling.

Urine samples were pooled across time points and across participants to generate six collection interval samples (0–12, 12–24, 24–48, 48–96, 96–144, 144–240 h). For each collection interval, samples were pooled across participants proportionally by volume so that each participant was equally represented (equal percentage) in the sample. Late timepoint samples (48–96, 96–144, 144–240 h) were concentrated under nitrogen to ~ 50% the original volume prior to injection. Each sample was vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at approximately 20,800 g for 2 min prior to analysis.

Fecal samples were pooled across participants and timepoints to generate three time interval samples (0–24, 24–96, 96–144 h). For each collection interval, samples were pooled across participants proportionally by weight so that each participant was equally represented (equal percentage) in the sample. The pooled 24–96-hour sample was generated from 24–48, 48-72 and 72–96-hour timepoints. Each 96-144 h timepoint was generated from 96-120 and 120-144 h timepoints. Each sample was extracted (~ 2 g for all sample intervals) with 8 mL of methanol (MeOH) and mixed with a fixed speed tube rotator (Model TR-03 A; Crystal Technology and Industries, Addison, TX, USA) for ~ 60 min and centrifuged (~ 3220 g for 5 min; 5810R, Eppendorf SE, Hamburg, Germany). The supernatant was then transferred to a 50 mL Falcon™ conical centrifuge tube and the extraction repeated two times on the remaining pellet and supernatant combined. The total volume was adjusted to 30 mL with water and passed through 600 mg Agilent Captiva EMR-Lipid cartridges (P/N 5190–1004). The eluent was then collected and combined in a 50 mL Falcon™ conical centrifuge tube. Eluent was transferred to a silanized glass tube in 5 mL aliquots, evaporated to dryness under nitrogen (~ 40 °C), and reconstituted on the day of analysis in 200 µL 0.1% formic acid:DMSO:acetonitrile (7:1.5:1.5, v/v/v). Following extraction, the remaining pellet was dried to completion and incubated overnight with 10 mL 1 mg/mL proteinase K (Promega™, P/N V302B) in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 5 mM CaCl2 at 37 °C in an incubator (Incubator 3130, Forma Scientific [now part of Thermo Fisher Scientific], Waltham, WA). The incubation mixture was centrifuged (~ 3220 g for 5 min) and 5 mL of the supernatant was oxidized using a Hidex 600 OX oxidizer for radioanalysis. The remaining pellet was also oxidized to determine unextracted drug related material. The reconstituted samples were centrifuged and the supernatants used for extraction recovery determination and radioprofiling. The extraction recoveries for 0–24, 24–96 and 96–144 h samples were 107%, 54%, and 59%, respectively. The recoveries of the 24–96 h and 96–144 h samples were increased to 86% and 89%, respectively, after incubation of the pellet with Proteinase K and addition of radioactivity from oxidized pellet, indicating at least ~ 30% of the material was protein-bound.

HPLC radioprofiling and column recovery

Plasma, urine and fecal extract radioprofiles were generated by HPLC fraction collection followed by radioactivity counting. HPLC column effluent was collected into 96-well Scintiplates at 15 s intervals. The plates were dried under centrifugal vacuum and counted for radioactivity on a Microbeta scintillation counter (5 min per well; Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Electronic data from the scintillation counter were plotted and integrated (Laura software, LabLogic, Sheffield, UK). Limit of quantitation (LOQ) was set at five times the background counts per minute (CPM) value (relative to the middle of the noise) and limit of detection (LOD) was set at three times the background CPM value (relative to the middle of the noise). Each integrated peak was expressed as present region of interest (%ROI), with the sum of all peaks 100% ROI. HPLC system condition details are provided in Table S3.

HPLC column effluent from representative injections of plasma, urine, and feces extracts were collected using the same HPLC method as for study samples. A sample aliquot was mixed with scintillation fluid and counted by LSC. An additional aliquot of each sample was injected in the absence of the column, collected, and counted by LSC. For feces and plasma samples, effluent from the on-column injection was concentrated under vacuum to improve radioactivity signal. The mean recoveries of radioactivity for plasma, urine and feces were: 106%, 99%, and 107%, respectively. Column recovery corrections were not utilized to calculate the reported percent radioactivity or percent of dose values.

Metabolite identification

Characterization and structural information for ABBV-552 and its metabolites were determined via accurate mass/high resolution mass spectrometry (Orbitrap Exploris 480, ThermoFisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) of plasma extract, urine, and feces extract. The spectrometer was equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source operated in positive ion mode. Accurate mass LC/MS and LC/MSn data were generated for parent drug and metabolite identification. MS data were processed using Thermo Xcalibur 4.1 (ThermoScientific, San Jose, CA, USA) and the absolute value of analyte m/z errors relative to calculated m/z values were typically < 5 ppm. ABBV-552 and metabolite structural information was determined using representative retention times, observed m/z values, calculated m/z values, and mass error (Table S4).

Data analysis

No formal sample size calculations were done for this Phase 1 study. Per FDA guidance [8], approximately 6 evaluable participants would provide adequate data to meet study objectives. Statistical analyses were limited to descriptive statistics, such as means and standard deviations. Treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs; coded using MedDRA preferred terms) were examined and defined as any AE that began or worsened after ABBV-552 dosing. ABBV-552 plasma concentrations and radioactivity measurements were summarized for each sampling time. For blood and plasma samples, PK parameters were summarized based on plasma concentrations and on radioactivity measurements. The radioactivity percent recovered and cumulative percentage of administered radioactive dose recovered were done for urine and feces, and for the sum of urine and feces.

Results

Study population

A total of 8 healthy male adults were enrolled in the study (Table 1), all of whom received a single oral dose of 15 mg [14C]-ABBV-552 (~ 100 µCi). Five participants completed the study; the remaining 3 withdrew consent (participant choice, not AE-related) after study drug dosing, one each on Day 9, 14, and 19. All available data were included in safety and PK analyses.

Safety

No safety concerns were identified in this small study population of healthy adult males. Seven of 8 (87.5%) participants reported ≥ 1 TEAE, all of which were non-serious, and Grade 1 in severity (Table S5). TEAEs occurring in more than one participant included bowel movement irregularity, euphoria, and dizziness (2 participants each). Of TEAEs reported, euphoria, dizziness, and muscular weakness were deemed possibly related by the investigator and resolved without any action taken with study drug. These findings were consistent with prior ABBV-552 clinical studies [4, 5]. No serious TEAEs or deaths were reported. No clinically meaningful trends were identified in vital signs, laboratory parameters, or ECG results.

Pharmacokinetics

Key PK parameters were successfully obtained for a single 15 mg dose of ABBV-552 and are summarized in Table 2. Mean plasma concentration (mean ± SD) vs. time profiles after study drug administration are shown in Fig. 2. The median (range) Tmax in plasma was 1.0 (0.5–1.0) hour with a harmonic mean t1/2 of 41.2 h. As reported in previous publications, ABBV-552 appears to have a biphasic elimination profile [4]. PK parameters were also obtained with radioactivity measurements, with estimates of t1/2 in whole blood and plasma of 51.4 and 53.5 h, respectively (Table 2). The average Cmax and AUCinf blood:plasma ratio for total radioactivity was 0.71 and 0.76, respectively, suggesting no significant binding to red blood cells. Total radioactivity vs. time profiles are shown in Fig. 2B. The proportion of total radioactivity accounted for by parent ABBV-552 in plasma was 80% and 56%, as measured by Cmax and AUCinf, respectively.

On average, 78.8% of the total administered radioactivity was recovered in urine and feces through the last collection interval (Fig. 3). Individual total radioactive recovery ranged from 72.7% to 83.7%. Of the total administered radioactive dose, approximately 70.9% and 7.83% were recovered in the urine and feces, respectively. Clearance of ABBV-552 was primarily via metabolism with < 1% of the dose recovered as unchanged parent drug in excreta. These results suggest that ABBV-552 is well absorbed, predominantly metabolized, and negligibly eliminated into excreta as unchanged drug.

Metabolites of ABBV-552

Circulating metabolites

A listing of [14C]-ABBV-552 and all identified metabolites with the corresponding structures and proposed biotransformation pathways are depicted in Fig. 4. Radioprofiling of AUC0-24 h pooled plasma samples from healthy male participants revealed the presence of ABBV-552 and two circulating metabolites (M2, M5; Fig. 5A). Parent drug and metabolite M2 were the most abundant circulating drug-related components, accounting for ~ 84% and ~ 13% of the relative total radioactivity, respectively, at 24 h (Fig. 4A). M2 resulted from mono-oxidation, with its exact structure determined via NMR and confirmed via synthetic standard. M5, identified to be a mono-oxidative glucuronide, was detected in 2 of 6 (33.3%) participants but levels were below the LLOQ. Therefore, M2 was the only major circulating metabolite (> 10% of systemic exposure [11]). The relative percent radioactivity of ABBV-552 and the corresponding quantitative exposures for parent drug and metabolites for individual timepoints is provided in Table 3 (plasma samples pooled across participants). The radioprofile at 72 h suggested rapid clearing of metabolites (Fig. 5B).

Excreted metabolites

Mean total recovery of excreted radioactivity was 78.8%, with 70.9% in the urine and 7.8% in the feces. Therefore, ABBV-552 was well absorbed and renal excretion was the primary route of clearance following oral administration. No quantifiable parent drug was observed in the urine, indicating that ABBV-552 was primarily cleared through metabolism. The predominate drug related component identified in urine (0–240 h) was M5 (mono-oxidative glucuronide conjugate), which represented a mean of 31% of the radioactive dose across participants (Table S6). Six additional metabolites were identified in urine as follows: M9 (oxidative glucuronide, ~ 17% of radioactive dose), M33 (oxidative defluorination and glucuronidation, ~ 11%), M2 (oxidation, 5%), M12 (N-dealkylation/oxidation, 1%), M34 (defluorination/cysteine conjugation and oxidation, 4%), and M35 (oxidative defluorination/sulfation, 1%). Representative radioprofiles for 0–12 h and 144–240 h urine samples are shown in Fig. 5C, D.

Radioprofiling of feces (0–144 h) showed parent drug accounted for ~ 0.5% of the administered dose. M2 (oxidation) and M36 (reductive dichlorination) were also identified, representing a combined 1.4% of the administered dose (M2 and M36 co-eluted; Fig. 5E, F). An additional late-eluting component, which remains unknown in structure, represented ~ 1.1% of the administered dose. Based on its late elution, this unknown component is predicted to be protein bound. Lastly, another unknown metabolite (not shown on radioprofile), eluting with the solvent front, accounted for < 1% of the dose and could not be identified.

Discussion

Human mass balance studies provide critical information on the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of a drug and its metabolites, advising on future clinical trial enrollment populations, drug-drug interaction potential, and the need for specific studies in patients with renal and/or hepatic impairment [6–8]. The current mass balance study was performed in healthy, male participants who were administered a single oral dose of 15 mg [14C]-ABBV-552 (~ 100 µCi). ABBV-552 was generally well tolerated by this small number of healthy adult males with all AEs reported as mild (Grade 1) in severity, and no safety concerns were identified.

Following administration of a single oral dose of 15 mg (100 µCi) [14C]-ABBV-552, median Tmax in plasma was 1 h with a harmonic mean t1/2 of 41.2 h, which was consistent with the PK of ABBV-552 observed in the previously published Phase 1 study [4]. For total radioactivity, harmonic mean t1/2 was 51.4 and 53.5 h in whole blood and plasma, respectively. ABBV-552 did not significantly bind to red blood cells, as suggested by the blood-to-plasma total radioactivity ratio which averaged 0.71 for Cmax and 0.76 for AUCinf. ABBV-552 accounted for 80% and 56% of total radioactivity in plasma as measured by Cmax and AUCinf, respectively. The only major circulating metabolite identified (> 10% of systemic exposure [11]) was M2, which resulted from mono-oxidation of the parent drug and accounted for ~ 13% of total systemic exposure (based on AUC0−24 h). M2 has a 50-fold lower affinity from SV2A and is not expected to significantly bind to SV2A (unpublished data). Further, M2 was relatively short-lived in the current study population, with exposures adequately covered in preclinical toxicology studies in dog and rat (≥ 4 fold compared to humans administered 15 mg ABBV-552; unpublished data). Therefore, there was no evidence of risk for M2-related toxicity and no further toxicology studies were warranted. M5, a mono-oxidative glucuronide of ABBV-552, was the only other circulating metabolite identified but accounted for a very small percentage of exposure (concentrations < LLOQ). Additionally, because M5 is a glucuronide, it is unlikely to be pharmacologically active [12]. These results are comparable to preliminary results from the 80 mg cohort in the single ascending dose study, which found that M2 accounted for approximately 13% of ABBV-552 in pooled plasma (unpublished data), which is in agreement with the 12.8% in the current study. In addition, M5 was observed to be the predominant metabolite in pooled urine (unpublished data) which was also observed in this mass balance study. Renal elimination of parent ABBV-552 in the current study was comparable to results in multiple previous Phase 1 studies where parent ABBV-552 accounted for < 1% of the dose administered (unpublished data).

Clearance of ABBV-552 was primarily via hepatic metabolism with < 1% of the dose recovered in excreta as unchanged parent drug. A total of 78.8% of the radioactive dose was recovered in urine and feces through the last collection interval, almost entirely in the form of metabolites, which suggests that ABBV-552 is well absorbed after oral administration. Based on in vitro data, ABBV-552 metabolism is primarily via CYP3A4, with a minor contribution of CYP2C19. Based on the CYP mediated metabolism of ABBV-552, future CYP3A4 inhibitor and inducer drug-drug interaction studies, as well as a hepatic impairment study, are warranted. Of the total administered radioactive dose, an average of ~ 71% was recovered in urine and ~ 8% was recovered in feces (~ 79% in total; range: 72.7–83.7%). Regulatory guidance states that the optimal recovery for human mass balance studies is > 90%, with suggested confinement release criteria when both total administered radioactivity recovered is > 90%, and excreted radioactivity (urine plus feces) is < 1% over two 24-hour periods [8]. Despite extended confinement beyond the mandatory period, all participants had achieved < 1% of administered radioactive dose excreted but did not have > 90% recovery by 480 h (maximum confinement in the current study). Even though only ~ 79% of the overall radioactive dose had been recovered, a plateau began at ~ 336 h post-dose. Because all participants had < 1% recovery over multiple consecutive collection intervals prior to stopping sample collection, inadequate confinement duration is unlikely to explain the < 90% recovery. It is also possible that some radioactivity was lost in samples but the use of well-established sample collection and analysis methods minimized the likelihood of this. Of note, recovery of < 90% is not uncommon. A retrospective review of 171 human mass balance studies, showed a mean (± SD) radioactivity recovery rate of 89 ± 11%, with 42% of studies reporting < 90% recovery and 15% of studies reporting < 80% recovery [13]. Potential causes of low recovery, as listed in the review, include non-covalent tissue sequestration, covalent sequestration, phospholipid affinity, specific protein binding in tissues, melanin binding, and prolonged fecal excretion [13]. Given that fecal excretion of ABBV-552 was relatively low and had plateaued prior to end of collection, prolonged fecal excretion is unlikely to have contributed to the observed recovery. Additionally, binding of ABBV-552 to melanin can be ruled out based on findings in the rodent quantitative whole-body autoradiography (QWBA) study which showed that ABBV-552 did not preferentially associate with melanin containing tissues (unpublished data). Additional causes proposed by Roffey et al. [13], including covalent tissue sequestration, non-covalent tissue sequestration, or phospholipid affinity, could potentially explain the long half-life of ABBV-552 and cannot be ruled out with current data. Additionally, the rodent QWBA study showed highest ABBV-552 exposures in organs of elimination, suggesting that significant tissue sequestration is unlikely. Even with a mean radioactivity recovery ~ 10% below guidance recommendations, the overall clearance pathways of ABBV-552 were well defined, with no human-specific metabolites observed compared to preclinical species. Therefore, this study achieved the primary objectives of a human mass balance analysis and findings did not raise concerns regarding potential safety issues surrounding unrecovered drug-related material.

A total of eight metabolites were identified in excreta, and M5 (oxidation/‌glucuronidation) was the only major metabolite identified (~ 31% of recovered dose in urine). M2 (oxidation), the primary circulating metabolite, was identified as a minor metabolite in excreta and was adequately covered, as established by margins from prior preclinical toxicology studies (unpublished data). Based on urine and feces metabolite recovery, ~ 52%, ~ 58%, and ~ 11% of administered ABBV-552 underwent oxidation, glucuronidation, and oxidative defluorination, respectively. Other identified metabolic processes/pathways included N-dealkylation, cysteine conjugation, and sulfation, all of which accounted for ≤ 5% ABBV-552 metabolism. Overall, these findings were consistent with those of a preclinical ADME study in rats (unpublished data).

Strengths and limitations

This study had several strengths including the study design which adequately accounted for the long half-life of ABBV-552. All participants had met the criteria of < 1% of radioactivity excreted over two consecutive collection intervals, indicating that study length was not a contributing factor to the < 90% recovery at study completion. Additionally, enrollment of two additional participants to account for potential dropout allowed for adequate characterization of ABBV-552 clearance pathways and metabolite identification despite study participant attrition. This study had one main limitation. Two metabolites of unknown biotransformation or structure were identified. Both were exclusively identified in the feces and only accounted for a small percentage of recovered radioactivity (combined 1.4%).

Conclusion

The current study demonstrated that ABBV-552 is well absorbed after oral administration, with the majority of drug-related material recovered as metabolites in urine (< 1% of recovered drug-related material was the parent compound). M2 was the primary circulating metabolite; while M5, M9, and M33 were the primary urinary-recovered metabolites. Therefore, ABBV-552 is primarily metabolized in the liver, with the majority of metabolites renally eliminated. The safety and mass balance findings presented here support further investigation of ABBV-552 for cognitive function improvement in patients with neurodegenerative disorders.
