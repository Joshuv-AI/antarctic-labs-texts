Causal relationship between uric acid and stroke: a two-sample mendelian randomization study
Authors: Fangxiao Hu, Jie Yang, Susu Wu, Chuanhua Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Many previous observational studies have disputed whether there is a link between uric acid and stroke. And the causal relationship between uric acid and stroke is unclear. To determine whether there is a causal relationship between uric acid and stroke by using mendelian randomization (MR). Uric acid dataset was obtained from Anna Kottgen et al, with a sample size of 110,347 people, including 2450,548 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Stroke pooled data from Malik R et al, publicly available in MEGASTROKE genome-wide association study, included meta-analysis data from 40,585 stroke patients and 406,111 control patients, totaling 8211,693 SNPs. The summary data of genome-wide association study of uric acid and stroke were collected from publicly available online databases. Inverse variance weighting was used to determine the causal relationship between uric acid and stroke. MR-Egger and weighted median model were used for supplementary analysis. Results were then analyzed for heterogeneity, pleiotropy, and sensitivity to ensure no statistical pleiotropy and to reduce bias. A total of 27 SNPs were included in this study after the disequilibrium instrumental variables were excluded. Check the PhenoScanner database for SNPs associated with confounders. In the end, a total of 8 SNPs were excluded. Two SNPs were excluded because the correction direction was the same. Since the F statistic is >10, rs10761587 and rs1825043 are excluded. Finally, 15 SNPs were selected as uric acid instrumental variables. Inverse variance weighting-fixed effect model suggested that there was no causal relationship between uric acid and stroke (odds ratio = 1.004, 95% confidence interval = 0.940, 1.072). MR-Egger and weighted median model also showed the same result. In addition, the results of this study were robust without heterogeneity and pleiotropy. This MR study suggests no support of a causal relationship between uric acid and stroke.

1. Introduction

Stroke is an acute cerebrovascular disease, which is a group of diseases caused by the sudden rupture of blood vessels in the brain or the blockage of blood vessels that prevents blood from flowing into the brain.[1] It has a high mortality and disability rate, and is the main cause of death and disability worldwide.[2] Global Burden of Disease data released in 2019 shows that 1 in 4 people will have a stroke in their lifetime.[2] In 2019, an estimated 12.2 million new cases of stroke occurred globally (ranging from 11–13.6 million), with approximately 101 million individuals affected by stroke, and a total of 1.43 billion disability-adjusted life years attributed to stroke.[1] The global burden of stroke is increasing, with an increasing trend in low income and low-middle income countries.[1] Hypertension, atrial fibrillation, smoking, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and so on are all risk factors for stroke.[1]

Uric acid is the final metabolite of purine nucleotides in the body and food, which is mainly produced in the liver and excreted by the kidneys and intestines.[3] Uric acid, a human antioxidant, plays a crucial role in regulating blood pressure and combating aging.[4] Elevated serum uric acid levels serve as a significant biochemical indicator for hyperuricemia.[5] This condition is closely associated with disorders in lipid metabolism, hypertension, insulin resistance, and coronary heart disease.[4] The global incidence and prevalence of hyperuricemia are on the rise, with the US, Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES2007–2016) estimating that approximately 20% of Americans suffer from hyperuricemia 20.2% among men and 20% among women-leading to increased healthcare costs and contributing to higher rates of co-morbidity and mortality.[6]

In recent years, researchers have paid more and more attention to the correlation between uric acid and stroke, but the role of uric acid in the occurrence and development of stroke is still controversial. Some studies have found a significant correlation between uric acid and stroke,[7–9] while other studies have failed to prove a significant correlation between uric acid and stroke.[10–12] However, observational studies of uric acid and stroke are susceptible to confounding factors, misclassification, and reverse causality.[13] This study considered abnormal uric acid as a common and modifiable factor, clarified the causal relationship between uric acid and stroke, or provided certain strategies for the prevention and treatment of stroke.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a commonly used statistical method that uses single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to evaluate the causal relationship between exposure factors and disease outcomes.[14,15] Because genetic alleles are randomly assigned during pregnancy, genetic variation is relatively less affected by measurement errors or biases, so the results obtained by using MR to study causal relationship are generally credible.[16]

There are existing studies on MR with similar themes, such as Zhong J et al and Kim J et al related studies.[11,17] The outcome of the former study is the 3-month functional outcome of ischemic stroke, while the outcome of the latter study is ischemic stroke, which is a type of stroke. Therefore, in order to further study, this study used two-sample MR, based on the open large sample genome-wide association study (GWAS) database to explore the causal relationship between uric acid and stroke.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

The study design follows Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using MR: The STROBE-MR Statement[15]: ① SNP is strongly correlated with exposure; ② SNP is not associated with confounding factors; ③ SNPS can only affect outcome through exposure and not through other pathways (shown in Fig. 1).

The principle of two-sample MR study.

2.2. Data source and SNP filtering

In order to mitigate bias resulting from overlapping exposure and outcome samples, we sourced exposure and outcome data from separate databases. Both uric acid and stroke GWAS are from public websites, all data are available online and do not require ethical approval or informed consent. The genetic background of the study population was limited to European ancestry to reduce bias in results due to race-related confounding factors.

The uric acid summary data came from the study of Anna Köttgen et al.[18] The meta-analysis of uric acid levels combined GWAS from 48 studies (e.g., AGES Reykjavik Study, Amish, ARIC), totaling 110,347 people. In all the studies, the average uric acid concentrations ranged from 3.9 to 6.1 mg/dL (median 5.2 mg/dL). A total of about 2450,548 SNPs were used for GWAS, and the summary data was publicly available at Global Urate Genetics Consortium GWAS. Stroke pooled data were obtained from the study of Malik R et al. The study analyzed approximately 8 million SNPs and markers of minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01 in a cohort of 67,162 stroke cases and 454,450 controls to investigate their associations with stroke. Relevant stroke data were obtained from a comprehensive GWAS meta-analysis comprising 29 GWAS (e.g., NINDS-SIGN, Barcelona, DECODE) involving exclusively European individuals, including meta-analysis data of 40,585 stroke patients and 406,111 control patients, with a total of 8211,693 SNPs, which were publicly available in MEGASTROKE GWAS.[19] More detailed sample characteristics of the above 2 studies and related research associations can be found in the original article.

In order to satisfy the MR Analysis hypothesis and obtain SNPs closely related to uric acid, the following screening was performed: ① The strongly correlated initial data were obtained with P < 5 × 10−8 as the screening condition; ② r2 = 0.001, kb = 10,000 to remove linkage disequilibrium; ③ each SNP was tested using PhenoScanner to exclude the influence of confounding factors; ④ in order to ensure the strong correlation between the selected SNP and uric acid and avoid bias in weak IVs, the study defined F > 10 as free of weak instrumental bias,[20] and the F statistic represents the strength of the relationship between SNP and uric acid. Use the following formula F = R2 × (N − 1)/(1 − R2). Where N is the sample size in the exposure database and R2 is the proportion of variation explained by SNPs in the exposure database. R2 was calculated as follows R2 = 2 × (1 − MAF) × MAF×(β/SD)2, where MAF is the minor allele frequency, β is the allele effect value, and SD is the standard error of β. SNPs with F < 10 are deleted; ⑤ the “harmonise_data” function in the Two-Sample MR Package was used to coordinate the direction of exposure and outcome alleles, and the palindromic SNP was deleted. Finally, the remaining SNPs were collected.

2.3. MR Analysis

Inverse variance weighting (IVW) was used as the main analytical method in this study. In addition, the MR-Egger and weighted median model were used for supplementary analysis. IVW uses the Wald ratio method to estimate the causal effect of each included tool SNP and then performs a weighted summary analysis. In the absence of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy, it has a strong causal detection ability.[21] MR-Egger assumes that all IVs are invalid and provides consistent causal estimation.[22] Weighted median model analysis provides consistent estimates when at least half of the tools are valid, which can reduce the bias in the estimation of causal effects.[22] If the results of causal relationship inference are consistent with the above 3 different MR Analysis methods, we consider the results to be reliable.

2.4. Heterogeneity analysis

In this study, Cochran’Q was used to assess the heterogeneity among SNPs If P > .05 of Cochran’Q, there was no significant heterogeneity, at the same time, the fixed effect model is used for IVW, otherwise the random effect model is used.[14]

2.5. Pleiotropy analysis

This study used the MR-Egger intercept as an indicator to evaluate directional pleiotropy, and if P > .05, there is no significant pleiotropy.[23]

2.6. Sensitivity analysis

In this study, “leave-one-out” and MR-PRESSO were used to analyze the sensitivity of the results. “leave-one-out” removes each SNP 1 by 1 and observes the comprehensive effect of the remaining SNPs, so as to judge the degree of influence of a single SNP on causal relationship.[24] MR-PRESSO was used to identify a single abnormal SNP.[25] Abnormal SNPs found in the 2 methods should be removed, and MR Analysis should be performed again after the removal.

2.7. Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis methods were performed using TwoSampleMR software package in R 4.2.1 software. P < .05 on both sides was considered statistically significant. This MR Analysis used odds ratio (OR) as the effect value and 95% confidence interval (CI) as confidence interval.

3. Results

3.1. IVs filtering result

After removing the IVs with linkage imbalance, a total of 27 SNPs were included in this study. SNPs associated with confounders were examined through the PhenoScanner database. Finally, a total of 8 SNPs related to weight, hypertension, diabetes, triglycerides, alcohol consumption, and platelet count were excluded. Two SNPs were excluded due to correction in the same direction. rs10761587 and rs1825043 were excluded because the F statistic was >10. The remaining 15 SNPs were selected as IVs for uric acid.

3.2. MR Analysis result

The IVW-fixed effect method showed no evidence to support a causal relationship between uric acid and stroke (OR = 1.004, 95% CI = 0.940, 1.072, Table 1, Figs. 2 and 3). MR-Egger analysis showed no causal relationship between uric acid and stroke (OR = 1.002, 95% CI = 0.889, 1.130, shown in Table 1, Figs. 2 and 3). The weighted median method also showed the same result (OR = 0.997, 95% CI = 0.924, 1.077, shown in Table 1, Figs. 2 and 3). The results of MR Analysis obtained by the above 3 methods are consistent. Therefore, the results of MR Analysis proved that there was no causal relationship between uric acid and stroke.

4. Discussion

This study used a public database and two-sample MR method to analyze the causal relationship between uric acid and stroke, and the results did not support the causal relationship between uric acid and stroke.

Some clinical studies have reported a link between uric acid and stroke. In a cross-sectional study conducted in India between October 2018 and September 2020, Lokkanahalli SS et al investigated uric acid levels in 66 patients with acute stroke and observed that acute ischemic stroke was linked to hyperuricemia, without significant gender discrepancies.[26] Qiao Tianci team conducted a meta-analysis of 19 prospective cohort articles, including 37,386 men and 31,163 women, which ultimately suggested that the overall stroke risk increased significantly with increasing uric acid concentration.[9] Shan Zheng et al conducted a 7-year prospective cohort study in Northwest China, utilizing a Cox proportional risk model to estimate the risk ratio of stroke incidence associated with uric acid levels. Their findings revealed that baseline uric acid levels and relative changes in uric acid levels were positively correlated with stroke incidence in both men and women.[27] There are some differences between these reported estimates and the results of this study. From a mechanistic point of view, uric acid levels stimulate the renin–angiotensin system and inhibit the release of endothelial nitric oxide, which can lead to preglomerular arteriolar sclerosis and increase blood pressure.[28] In addition, oxygen free radicals generated in the process of uric acid metabolism will damage vascular endothelium and easily lead to cerebral thrombosis.[29] The level of uric acid in the blood will affect the level of antioxidants in the plasma, and the 2 are negatively correlated.[5] Therefore, the scavenging ability of free radicals is weakened at high uric acid level, while the role of platelets is amplified in the presence of uric acid, which easily leads to coagulation cascade reaction. Eventually new blood clots form leading to stroke.[30] Studies have pointed out that elevated uric acid promotes the progression of atherosclerosis by increasing the production of free radicals and promoting LDL cholesterol oxidation and lipid peroxidation, which can also lead to atherosclerotic stroke.[31,32] Therefore, the incidence of stroke caused by uric acid may be caused by the production of other risk factors. Although some confounders in observational studies are adjusted, unmeasured risk factors cannot be completely excluded. So, even if an observational study reports a strong association, it does not prove a direct causal association. In addition, there are a number of clinical studies that have shown different results. Wang Anxin et al study showed that high uric acid is not associated with the risk of total stroke.[33] Another study showed that elevated uric acid levels were significantly associated with the acute phase of ischemic stroke, and this relationship was only found in women.[34] In conclusion, it is not clear whether the differences between this study and other clinical studies are due to environmental factors, immune response differences, or genetic factors affecting different disease subtypes, and the correlation between uric acid and stroke needs further exploration.

4.1. Strengths

While the results should be interpreted with caution, this study has several strengths: ① large amounts of aggregated level genetic data, which can minimize potential confounders and reverse causation in observational studies; ② the study was limited to European populations, reducing the biased chair introduced by demographics; ③ in this study, heterogeneity and pleiotropy analysis were performed to ensure the consistency of causal estimation and robustness of results.

4.2. Limitations

This study has some limitations: ① in order to verify the results, further GWAS for ethnicities other than European races are needed. ② no potential nonlinear relationships or stratification effects due to health status, age, or gender could be examined.

5. Conclusion

This MR Study does not provide evidence of a causal relationship between uric acid and stroke. In comparison with randomized controlled trials, while MR Analysis offers cost-saving benefits by avoiding external influences like environmental factors and experimental conditions, its scope is restricted to elucidating linear relationships without accommodating nonlinear analyses. Future research should employ a comprehensive range of epidemiological methods to uncover more intricate genetic laws and mechanisms. Additionally, due to limitations in databases and software tools, this study focuses on a small sample from the European population resulting in inadequate statistical power. Subsequent studies should utilize larger samples from diverse regions and ethnicities for further validation.
