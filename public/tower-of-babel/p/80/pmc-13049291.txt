Criterion Validation for Step Count and Distance Covered During the 6-Minute Walk Test Using a Low-Cost, Commercially Available Activity Monitor
Authors: Abraham Samuel Babu, Achsah A. Abraham, Becky M. Mathew, Sara Manohar Malpe, Akhila Satyamurthy
Published: 2026

Although it is important to think about the accuracy of devices in measuring step counts, research grade equipment (ie, accelerometers) and high-end wearables (eg, Fitbit and Garmin) are often costly and are not easily accessible or feasible to use in large clinical trials, population studies, and clinical practice. Herein comes the low-cost wearables that are now available in the market and a common fitness trend.1 The Redmi Band-6 (Xiaomi) is a low-cost device, having limited studies on its validation for both earlier and current models.2 This highlights a strong need because markets are easily flooded with new devices, which should be reevaluated as they enter the market.3 Therefore, the aim of this study was to perform a device-specific criterion validation of a commercially available, low-cost wearable for estimating step count and distance during the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) in healthy young adults.

Patients and Methods

This was a prospective observational study on healthy young adults aged 18 years or older, of either gender, with no self-reported comorbidities. They were excluded if they had any neuromuscular diseases limiting ambulation, used walking aids, or had any psychological impairments. The sample size of 48 was determined using the reference tables provided by Zou,4 for ρ=0.80 and ω=0.10 at 80% assurance. The study received approval from the Kasturba Medical College and Kasturba Hospital Institutional Ethics Committee—2 (Student Research) (IEC2/692/2023) and was registered prospectively in Clinical Trial Registry India (CTRI/2024/02/062484) on February 9, 2024. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Participants who met the inclusion criteria wore the device on the dominant hand, as previously reported,2,5 and performed the 6MWT according to standard guidelines in a well-lit, 30-m corridor, free from obstruction.6 The test was video recorded as previously described.5 In brief, the entire test was video recorded to ensure the number of steps walked could be counted and calculated. Three raters (A.A.A., B.M.M., and S.M.M.) independently reviewed the recordings and counted the steps walked during each 6MWT (intraclass correlation coefficient: 0.78; 95% CI, 0.68-0.86). These were then entered into a data sheet and were sent to a fourth investigator (A.S.B.) for calculation of the average step counts. This investigator was unaware of the steps walked as measured by the device. Distance covered was obtained from the test and device. Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) was estimated as described by Fuller et al.2 Paired analysis was performed for both distance covered (in meters), and steps walked. Considering repeated testing for distance covered and step count, correction for α inflation was done, and statistical significance was considered when P<.025. Statistical analysis was performed using JAMOVI version 2.4.11 (The Jamovi Project 2025).

Discussion

The findings indicate that although the device did not accurately estimate the distance walked, it showed good agreement with direct observation for step counts. The inaccurate measurement in distance covered is similar to a previous study using the Fitbit Charge 2.5 This accuracy in step counts and MAPE reported are similar to those in controlled settings and for high-end wearables.2 Despite minimal mean bias, the limits of agreement indicate that individual step counts may differ by up to approximately 150 steps, thereby limiting individual-level decision making. Distances, on the contrary, when used as an outcome measure using the Redmi Band-6, must be used cautiously considering the wide limits of acceptability.

The strength of this study is the use of video-based direct observation as a reference standard for estimating step counts. However, algorithmic bias of the device, inability to estimate per-step distance through sensors, and limited use of the 6MWT in this population are some limitations. Furthermore, these findings represent a technical validation of device accuracy under controlled conditions and do not support end point qualification, regulatory use, or deployment in patient populations without further validation in disease-specific and real-world settings.

Conclusion

This study adds to the growing evidence supporting the use of low-cost activity monitors for measuring step count among young healthy adults. Distance estimates from the device, however, have wide limits of agreement and are not recommended for use in research or practice.
