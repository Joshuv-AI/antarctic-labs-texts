Strong association between psychiatric disorders co-occurrence and dementia: a Bayesian approach on a 14-year clinical data warehouse
Authors: Edouard Baudouin, Emmanuelle Duron, Marie Verdoux, Matthieu Gasnier, Yann Pelloux, Hugo Bottemanne, Emmanuelle Corruble, Romain Colle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychiatric disorders alone are associated with an increased risk of developing dementia. However, the relationship between co-occurring psychiatric disorders and dementia odds remains unclear. This study aimed to assess the odds of dementia (all types) among individuals with several psychiatric disorders and identify relevant co-occurrence patterns.MethodsData were extracted from the clinical data warehouse of the psychiatry department of Bicêtre Hospital, France, between 29 August 2009 and 29 October 2023. Patients aged 45 years and older diagnosed with at least one psychiatric disorder—depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, psychotic disorders, substance use disorders, personality disorders or bipolar disorders—were included. Subgroups were created to evaluate specific patterns of psychiatric co-occurrence associated with dementia. In this case-control study, Bayesian models, including hierarchical models and logistic regression adjusted for age, sex and cardiovascular risk factors, were used to estimate posterior probabilities and ORs for dementia.ResultsAmong 3688 subjects, the mean (SD) age at inclusion was 68.7 (12.1) in the dementia group (653 (17.7%) subjects) and 58.2 (10.5) in the non-dementia group (3035 (82.3%) individuals). Compared with those with one psychiatric disorder (2608 (70.7%) patients), the adjusted OR (95% credible interval) for dementia increased from 2.3 (1.7–3) with two comorbidities (789 (21.4%) patients) to 11.1 (5.4–22.2) with four comorbidities (65 (1.8%) subjects). Patients with co-occurrence of mood and anxiety disorders had a mean posterior probability from 48% (34.1–62.2) up to 89.6% (76.8–98.6) of dementia.ConclusionsDementia odds significantly increase with the number of psychiatric comorbidities, with mood and anxiety disorder co-occurrence showing the highest posterior probabilities. Targeted screening strategies should be developed for these patients, with a special focus on patients developing more than one psychiatric disorder.Keywords: PSYCHIATRY, Depression & mood disorders, Adult psychiatry

Introduction

Dementia has emerged as a significant global health concern, with an estimated 10 million new cases annually, as reported by the WHO.1 Although current treatments are being evaluated for specific neurodegenerative diseases, they address only a fraction of dementia-related pathologies, highlighting the need for preventive strategies targeting both psychiatric and cardiovascular risk factors, given their shared contribution to dementia.2

The clinical manifestations of dementia include cognitive alterations, such as memory loss and/or executive dysfunction, leading to loss of autonomy.3 However, dementia can also manifest as psychiatric symptoms such as behavioural or affective symptoms.4 5 Conversely, cognitive alterations can also be observed in psychiatric disorders.6

A nationwide cohort study in Sweden found that patients with dementia had a higher risk of developing psychiatric disorders such as depressive disorders (3.4 vs 9.6 per 1000 person-years), anxiety disorders (2.2 vs 4.6 per 1000 person-years), substance use disorders (4 vs 2 per 1000 person-years) or psychotic disorders (1.8 vs 0.4 per 1000 person-year).7 This link is bidirectional since a higher risk of dementia is observed in patients with psychiatric disorders, as shown by the results of a meta-analysis that assessed the association between depressive disorders and dementia (OR 1.91 (95% Confidence Interval (CI) 1.72 to 2.12)) or anxiety disorders (OR 2.23 (95% CI 1.46 to 3.41)).8 Similarly, another meta-analysis found an OR of 2.19 (95% CI 1.44 to 3.31) between psychotic disorders and dementia.9 Finally, a large Australian cohort found that HRs of developing dementia when suffering from bipolar disorders were 3.46 (95% CI 2.41 to 4.95) when the age of onset was <50 years and 2.99 (95% CI 2.17 to 4.12) when the age of onset was >60 years.10

Although co-occurrence of psychiatric disorders is frequent, the association with dementia remains unclear in this population. Identifying specific patterns of psychiatric comorbidities associated with increased dementia risk is relevant in order to tailor specific screening and preventive actions.

The objective of this study was, therefore, to assess the odds of dementia (all types) when suffering from several psychiatric disorders and identify relevant patterns of psychiatric disorder comorbidities.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study was conducted using the ‘Assistance Publique-Hôpitaux de Paris’ (AP-HP) Clinical Data Warehouse, which contains electronic health records of inpatients and outpatients of Bicêtre Hospital. Clinical data (age, sex and pathologies according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10)) were extracted between 29 August 2009 and 29 October 2023 (follow-up period). All pathologies coded in this record are made by senior physicians. Informed consent for approved studies was ensured through a transparency portal established by the AP-HP clinical data warehouse, in accordance with European regulations from the National Commission for Information Technology and Civil Liberties (CNIL) on data protection and authorisation (n°1980120). This study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Participants, data collection and variable manipulations

Study population

Patients suffering from psychiatric disorders diagnosed by senior psychiatrists from the psychiatric department of Bicêtre Hospital, according to the ICD-10, were included. Baseline was defined as the date of the first psychiatric diagnosis recorded between 29 August 2009 and 29 July 2023. Patients were followed until their last recorded hospital contact or the end of the observation period (29 October 2023). In order to ensure sufficient follow-up time and account for the significant increase in dementia incidence with age, patients aged 45 years and older were included, considering that dementia incidence rises notably after the age of 55.11 No other exclusion criteria were applied.

Psychiatric disorders

Six types of psychiatric disorders were included, such as depressive disorders (F32–F33), anxiety disorders (F40–F43), psychotic disorders (F20–F29), substance use disorders (F10–F19), personality disorders (F60 and F61) and bipolar disorders (F30–F31), as they are the most common psychiatric disorders.12 Any of these codes present at any time during the follow-up were considered as a single diagnosis within their respective category (eg, all F32 codes were grouped into depressive disorders and counted as one). In cases where both depressive disorder codes and bipolar disorder codes were present, only bipolar disorder was retained, as depressive episodes were considered as bipolar disorder onset. Diagnoses were established between 29 August 2009 and 29 July 2023.

The total number of psychiatric disorders for each patient was the main explicative variable. An ordinal variable was constructed with four categories, ranging from having only one psychiatric comorbidity to having four or more. Second, subgroups were created according to the various patterns of associations between psychiatric comorbidities.

Dementia diagnoses

Dementia diagnoses of the patients included were established between 29 August 2009 and 29 October 2023 exclusively in all 39 university hospitals of the AP-HP by physicians. All forms of dementia and cognitive impairments, as defined by the ICD-10, were included in the study: Alzheimer’s disease, including early and late onset as well as atypical or mixed forms (F00–F03); organic amnesic syndrome not induced by alcohol or other psychoactive substances (F04); mild cognitive disorders resulting from brain damage or dysfunction (F067); other degenerative diseases of the nervous system, including frontotemporal and Lewy body dementia (G30–G31); and symptoms and signs involving cognitive functions and awareness not yet categorised as a specific dementia type (R41).

These diagnoses could have been established before, during or after psychiatric diagnoses.

Co-variables

Age used for adjustment in multivariate models was age at dementia onset if applicable, age at the end of the follow-up period otherwise. The assigned sex at birth was recorded at baseline according to the social security number. A variable indicating the presence of cardiovascular risk factors was calculated based on the number of comorbidities diagnosed during follow-up. This allowed for an assessment of the cumulative burden of risk factors, including tobacco consumption, arterial hypertension, obesity, dyslipidaemia and diabetes.13 Cardiovascular factors were included because of their well-established and modifiable association with dementia.14

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are provided for continuous and qualitative variables, respectively, in the form of mean, SD and percentage.

To evaluate the association between psychiatric disorders co-occurrence and dementia probability, we employed Bayesian models, including logistic regression and hierarchical beta-binomial regression, to account for the complexity of subgroup analyses. Given the study’s objective to divide the population into small-sized subgroups, we opted for a Bayesian analysis over a frequentist approach. This choice allows us to overcome asymptotic limitations inherent to smaller sample sizes and benefit from regularisation to provide more stable and robust estimates.15

First, a logistic regression model was employed to analyse the relationship between dementia and the total number of psychiatric comorbidities. The mean OR (95% credible interval (CrI)) was calculated after adjustment for age, sex and number of cardiovascular risk factors. Given the assumption of a non-linear association between the total number of psychiatric disorders and the dependent variable, the total number of psychiatric disorders was treated as an ordinal variable. To challenge our hypothesis (the accumulation of psychiatric disorders co-occurrence is at higher odds of dementia), we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an independent somatic condition as the outcome: renal failure (chronic and acute). Although renal failure is a severe condition and may be associated with shorter follow-up, it was chosen because, like dementia, it is captured through hospital diagnoses and has been associated with psychiatric disorders.16 In case of negative results, this would support the specificity of our findings and reduce the likelihood that they are merely due to a general vulnerability to somatic illness or to a higher probability of diagnostic coding in patients with multiple psychiatric disorders. Furthermore, to account for the rule-out potential diagnosis, we also performed a sensitivity analysis that included subjects with at least two times the same diagnosis codes. Next, we also performed a sensitivity analysis, which included only subjects with more than 5 years of follow-up between the last psychiatric disorder code and the first dementia code in order to provide insight into the direction of the relation.

Subsequently, a Bayesian hierarchical model with a binomial distribution based on a beta regression was used to estimate the posterior probability of dementia in relation to the patterns of psychiatric co-occurrence. A hierarchical model was used to account for the nested structure of the data, with individuals grouped on patterns of co-occurring psychiatric disorders. This approach pooled information across subgroups, stabilising estimates for smaller subgroups while maintaining subgroup-specific variability.

All the analyses were conducted using a Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) method with the Just Another Gibbs Sampler (JAGS) on R V.4.2.2. The full details, including the priors’ distributions, are provided in online supplemental methods 1; convergence was assessed visually with trace plots and using the Gelman-Rubin statistic (online supplemental tables 1 and 2 and online supplemental figures 1 and 2). Details of the sensitivity analyses involving a priori are provided in online supplemental methods 3 and online supplemental figures 3 and 4.

Results

A total of 7973 subjects were present in the data warehouse, of whom 3688 were included in the study (4285 were excluded because of age <45 years at inclusion). The mean age (SD) of the overall cohort at the end of follow-up was 67.1 (11.2) years, 70.3 (12.1) in the dementia group and 66.4 (10.9) in the non-dementia group; the mean delay between the first psychiatric disorder diagnosis and dementia diagnosis was 1.6 (3) years (range from −7.4 to 13.8 years; online supplemental figure 8). Descriptive statistics are provided in table 1. From the six psychiatric disorders, 46 patterns were identified: 2608/3688 (70.7%) subjects suffered from one psychiatric disorder, 789/3688 (21.4%) had two psychiatric disorders, 226/3688 (6.1%) had three psychiatric disorders and 65/3688 (1.8%) had four psychiatric disorders or more. All patterns and their respective sizes are presented in figure 1.

Number of psychiatric disorders co-occurrence and odds of dementia

After adjustment for age, sex and the number of cardiovascular risk factors, OR for suffering from dementia increased with the number of psychiatric disorders. Compared with patients suffering from one disorder, subjects suffering from two psychiatric disorders had an OR (95% CrI) of 2.3 (1.7–3), 4.6 (3–7.1) for three disorders and 11.1 (5.4–22.2) for patients with four or more comorbidities compared with having only one (figure 2).

The specificity of this association was confirmed in the sensitivity analysis (online supplemental figure 5), in which ORs did not increase as the number of psychiatric comorbidities increased for the control outcome (renal failure). This result was also confirmed with the sensitivity analysis restricted to subjects with at least twice the same diagnostic code (online supplemental figure 6). Finally, the sensitivity analysis, which included subjects with at least 5 years of follow-up between the last psychiatric disorder code and the first dementia code, found the same trend (online supplemental figure 7).

Patterns of co-occurring psychiatric disorders and posterior probability of dementia

Out of the 46 patterns identified, co-occurrences of a mood disorder and an anxiety disorder were associated with a mean posterior probability of dementia of 48.3% (34.1–62.2) up to 89.6% (76.8%–98.6%). Discussion

This study found that dementia odds strongly increased with the number of psychiatric comorbidities. Furthermore, we identified that patterns including an association of anxiety disorder and mood disorders are particularly associated with a high posterior probability of dementia.

Psychiatric disorders and dementia odds

To our knowledge, this is the first study to establish an association between the number of psychiatric disorders and dementia. Our findings reveal a progressively stronger effect, with the likelihood of developing dementia increasing significantly as the number of psychiatric disorders rises. This effect appears specific to dementia since it was not observed with an independent pathology (renal failure) tested in the sensitivity analysis. Moreover, the sensitivity analysis, including only subjects with at least 5 years of follow-up, suggests that psychiatric disorders may be early markers of dementia.

This result may be partially explained by the vascular theory of dementia, which posits that part of dementia is the result of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD). CSVD is considered a major risk factor for dementia as it causes neuroinflammation and adds up to neurodegeneration.17 A recent 14-year cohort study found on 503 participants that CSVD progression predicted incident all-cause dementia (HR 1.76 per 1-SD increase, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.63).18 Additionally, CSVD has been demonstrated in numerous psychiatric disorders, including depressive disorders, anxiety disorders and bipolar disorders.19 20 It is further exacerbated by chronic stress and systemic inflammation, both of which are prevalent in these conditions.21

Finally, accumulation of psychotropic medications, resulting from the accumulation of psychiatric disorders, may also contribute to the observed association. Although medication data were not available, the association of benzodiazepines, antidepressants with anticholinergic properties and antipsychotics has been associated with dementia in a previous systematic review.22 This is consistent with the ‘dose-response’ pattern observed in which the odds of dementia increased with the number of psychiatric disorders. However, whether the accumulation of psychiatric disorders should be interpreted as a causal factor or as an early marker of dementia remains to be determined.

Psychiatric disorders’ association pattern and dementia posterior probability

Although previous meta-analyses and large-scale studies have found a stronger association between depressive disorders and dementia than with anxiety disorders,7 9 even in older adults,8 none have specifically examined the co-occurrence of multiple psychiatric disorders.

In this population, we observed that specific co-occurrence of psychiatric disorders, particularly those involving mood disorders in association with anxiety disorders, had an increased posterior probability of dementia.

Indeed, anxiety disorders have been frequently associated with both depressive disorders23 and bipolar disorders.24 However, in studies assessing the association between psychiatric disorders and dementia, anxiety disorders and symptoms may have been underevaluated when mood disorders were also present.25 This could have led to an overestimation of the individual impact of mood disorders while underestimating the contribution of anxiety disorders. Our findings suggest that the elevated dementia odds traditionally attributed to mood disorders may, in fact, be the result of the co-occurrence of psychiatric disorders. This is in line with recent findings showing that neuropsychiatric disorders (including bipolar disorders, depressive disorders and anxiety disorders) may share common pathophysiology with neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease26 when considering brain–blood barrier integrity. Moreover, frontotemporal dementia (in which anxiety disorders are frequent27) and bipolar disorders may share some common molecular mechanisms.28 Furthermore, if psychiatric disorders may be considered as a sort of continuum29 with shared pathological pathways, it may not be surprising that their association leads to greater odds of dementia, in the image of the accumulation of cardiovascular risk factors in regard to stroke or heart attack. Finally, some studies suggest that psychiatric symptoms might reflect prodromal dementia.30 This is supported by the short median delay observed between psychiatric disorder diagnosis and dementia diagnosis in this cohort, strengthening the hypothesis of a shared vulnerability.

Overall, our results suggest that several psychiatric comorbidity patterns are strongly associated with dementia diagnosis, supporting the hypothesis that these patterns may serve as early clinical markers of dementia. Although the direction of this association remains to be clarified, the strength of the association justifies further investigation into shared pathological pathways.30

Strengths and limitations

The interpretation of diagnostic timing should, however, be made with caution. Dementia diagnoses may have been delayed, and, without neuropathological confirmation, misclassification may have occurred. Additionally, while ICD-10 codes provide a standardised framework for diagnosis, there may be variability in their application, especially when diagnosing dementia. However, this study did not evaluate potential differences in dementia odds across specific subtypes, such as Alzheimer’s disease versus vascular dementia. Moreover, because psychiatric disorders are part of the differential diagnosis of dementia, cognitive symptoms related to mood or anxiety disorders may have led to misdiagnosis, potentially introducing diagnostic bias. This, however, does not impact the main finding of this study (the more psychiatric disorders, the higher the odds of being diagnosed with dementia). Nevertheless, our findings are based on a large cohort from a single psychiatric department, which ensures diagnostic consistency but limits generalisability to broader populations. The use of a clinical data warehouse allowed the inclusion of dementia codes recorded in all 39 university hospitals of the AP-HP. The absence of a control group without psychiatric disorders does not allow comparison of dementia probability in non-psychiatric populations. Since patients were recruited from a single psychiatric department, they were not representative of all patients with psychiatric disorders. Several covariates of interest were not available to date in our warehouse database: socioeconomic status, family history, psychiatric disorder duration, treatment and brain imaging. While this limits the generalisability of our findings, it may also affect internal validity, as residual confounding cannot be excluded and may have influenced the strength of the observed associations. Finally, in order to ensure sufficient statistical power, psychiatric disorders were grouped into broad diagnostic categories (eg, F20–F29 for psychotic disorders), which reduces diagnostic granularity.

Future research should validate these findings in multicentric or population-based cohorts, integrating more diverse patient profiles and assessing more specific dementia and psychiatric diagnoses subtypes.

Perspectives

The findings of this study highlight the strong association between the co-occurrence of psychiatric disorders and an increased posterior probability of developing dementia, particularly for patterns with anxiety and mood disorders.

Recent advancements in dementia diagnoses, such as the development of biomarkers in blood and cerebrospinal fluid, as well as the use of positron emission tomography, encourage earlier and more accurate detection of dementia. Integrating these tools into clinical practice for high-risk individuals, especially those with specific psychiatric comorbidities identified in this study, could significantly enhance their care management, given the recent advancements in dementia treatment.

Moreover, progress in medical imaging technologies, such as MRI, coupled with machine learning algorithms for segmentation, offers a novel approach to detect CSVD at its earliest stages. Early identification of CSVD could facilitate targeted interventions, such as intensified control of cardiovascular risk factors, potentially mitigating the progression towards dementia.

In addition, future studies on dementia odds should consider psychiatry co-occurrence, especially when considering mood and anxiety disorders. Further studies with longer follow-up and larger samples should explore the temporal sequence within psychiatric diagnoses and the later risk of dementia.

Overall, individuals presenting with psychiatric disorders co-occurrence could benefit from cognitive assessments tailored to their risk profiles using advanced biomarkers (biological and imaging), allowing them to access better prevention and care management, as psychiatric disorders may serve as early markers of dementia.

Conclusion

Dementia odds significantly increase with the number of psychiatric comorbidities, with mood and anxiety disorder co-occurrence showing the highest posterior probabilities. Targeted screening strategies should be developed for these patients, with a special focus on patients developing more than one psychiatric disorder.
