Functional poly (acrylamide-co-acrylic acid) super hydrogels integrated with silver-decorated recycled nano tungsten oxide: a robust, leach-free for advanced wastewater decontamination
Authors: H. M. Zidan, Nazir Mustapha, Sally A. Eladly, Dina Ezzat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study reports the development of an efficient and structurally robust functional hybrid hydrogel composed of a Poly(acrylamide-co-acrylic acid) matrix, reinforced with silver-decorated recycled nano-tungsten oxide (Ag-WO3) composites. To ensure structural longevity and eliminate secondary pollution risks, the recycled nanoparticles derived from industrial scrap were modified via an eco-friendly photo-reduction method and covalently tethered to the polymer backbone using a vinyltrimethoxysilane coupling agent. FT-IR analysis confirmed successful interfacial chemical grafting, which acted as a nano-skeleton reinforcement. Consequently, a dramatic increase in mechanical robustness was achieved, with surface hardness rising from 43 Shore A for the pristine hydrogel to 80 Shore A for the dense hybrid network. Crucially, the hybrid system exhibited exceptional chemo-structural stability across a broad pH spectrum (3–11), maintaining a gel content exceeding 90% with nearly zero detectable leaching or turbidity. Functionally, the Ag-WO3 integrated hydrogel serves as a potent dual-action platform for wastewater purification, achieving 86.5% photocatalytic degradation of Methylene Blue dye under UV irradiation within 60 min, alongside an outstanding 99.98% antibacterial eradication rate against Escherichia coli. By transforming industrial metallurgical scrap into a high-value environmental catalyst, this research offers a sustainable, non-leaching, and scalable platform for advanced wastewater decontamination, directly contributing to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 6: Clean Water and Sanitation, and SDG 12: Responsible Consumption and Production).

Introduction

The escalating discharge of domestic, agricultural, and industrial effluents into aquatic ecosystems has become a critical global bottleneck. These wastewaters harbor a complex cocktail of heavy metals, synthetic dyes, and pathogens that pose irreversible threats to environmental stability and public health [1, 2]. While traditional remediation techniques are available, their high operational costs and the risk of generating secondary pollution have catalyzed the search for more sustainable, high-efficiency decontamination technologies. Among the various strategies, polymer hydrogels have emerged as premier candidates for wastewater purification. Their three-dimensional cross-linked networks provide a versatile scaffold with tunable functionality and high swelling capacities, enabling the effective capture of diverse contaminants [3–5]. The utilization of biopolymer-derived scaffolds has been extensively documented due to their functional groups and inherent biocompatibility. In this context, Akter et al. [6] and Ziao Zhang et al. [7] reviewed the application of cellulose-based hydrogels, noting their capacity to act as adsorbents for heavy metals and synthetic dyes. Their analysis focused on the synthesis and adsorption mechanisms that contribute to the removal of these contaminants from aqueous solutions. Similarly, Chelu et al. [8] evaluated chitosan-based hydrogels and reported their ability to interact with diverse pollutants, including pharmaceuticals and microorganisms. The study highlighted that integrating nanomaterials into Chitosan matrices can modify their performance, suggesting these composites as potential candidates for large-scale water purification.

To overcome the limitations of pure polymeric matrices, such as low mechanical strength and limited catalytic activity, the integration of metal oxide nanoparticles into water treatment frameworks has been widely explored for their high surface area, redox activity, and magnetic properties, which facilitate the degradation of organic pollutants [9, 10]. When incorporated into polymeric matrices such as hydrogels, these nanoparticles have been reported to improve adsorption selectivity and catalytic performance, while also simplifying the recovery of the adsorbent after treatment.

Expanding on this hybrid approach, Mikhailidi et al. [6] reviewed the development of cellulose-based metallogels, in which the inclusion of metal nanoparticles (such as Ag, Au, and TiO₂) confers diverse functionalities, including antibacterial and catalytic properties. Their analysis suggests that combining metallic components with sustainable polymer scaffolds enables the design of materials suitable for environmental remediation and sensing applications.

Furthermore, Aseeri et al. [11] investigated a scandium-integrated metal-organic framework (MOF) within a Chitosan/pectin hydrogel matrix. Their structural characterization confirmed the formation of a porous, nanocrystalline network capable of interacting with organic dyes. The study demonstrated that such hybrid systems can exhibit dual functionality, combining the adsorptive removal of phenol red and Congo red with antibacterial activity against wastewater-derived bacteria.

Among semiconductor photocatalysts, Tungsten Oxide (WO₃) has been identified as an effective material for wastewater treatment due to its visible-light responsiveness, high valence-band oxidation potential, and relatively narrow band gap. These characteristics enable WO₃-based nanomaterials to facilitate the mineralization of organic dyes and various aqueous contaminants [12, 13]. For instance, Gokila Viswanathan et al. [14] investigated the effects of lanthanum (La) doping on the crystal structure and charge-carrier dynamics of WO₃ nanoparticles. Their study reported that a 5 wt% La-doped WO₃ configuration narrows the optical band gap, resulting in an 83.85% degradation rate of Malachite Green dye under visible light.

In addition to inorganic fillers, the selection of the polymeric matrix is crucial for environmental applications. The synthesis of Poly(acrylic acid-co-acrylamide) [P(AA-co-AM)] provides a synergistic balance between the high hydrophilic nature of acrylic acid and the structural stability of acrylamide. The carboxylic (–COOH) and amide (–NH₂) functional groups along the copolymer chains act as active sites for both electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding with various pollutants [15]. Moreover, this specific copolymeric architecture is known for its superior mechanical resilience and its ability to respond to external stimuli, particularly pH variations, which is essential for maintaining structural integrity during multi-cycle wastewater treatment [16]. Khan et al. [17] characterized the behavior of pH-sensitive acrylamide/acrylic acid hydrogels, focusing on their swelling kinetics and gel fraction. Their findings indicated that these cross-linked networks exhibit minimal swelling in acidic media while showing pronounced expansion at pH 7.4. This stimuli-responsive behavior suggests that acrylic-based copolymers can provide a stable yet adaptable environment for functional loads across different aqueous conditions.

To fully understand the current progress in integrating customized nano-catalysts into various biopolymers and functionalized matrices for targeted water purification, several recent breakthroughs should be highlighted. Ali et al. [18] developed a CoAl-LDH CoOx heterostructure using a one-pot hydrothermal method for treating textile wastewater, reaching 98% methyl orange discoloration within 100 min of UV exposure. Raza et al. [19]. Fabricated a recoverable Ti3C2Tx@TACs -SA biopolymer composite by encapsulating triatomic-doped MXene in sodium alginate, achieving 85.3% degradation of disinfection by-products within 60 min. Claudine et al. [20] developed an in situ-polymerized Ti2C MXene functionalized with glycidyl methacrylate and integrated with conducting PEDOT: PSS thin films, achieving 94.9% Rhodamine B removal, evaluated at a microscale volume (10 mL). Yang et al. [21] reported a hydrothermally synthesized TiNbC/MnCO3@MOF-S biopolymer hydrogel for removing wastewater disinfection byproducts, demonstrating high adsorption thresholds (648 mg/g for iodoacetonitrile) over 15 regeneration cycles. Zhang et al. [22] devised a novel SA@TiO2-L/H hydrogel composite by crosslinking titanium dioxide and luminous powder into sodium alginate, achieving 99.7% contaminant degradation in 60 min, though it experienced structural decay (dropping to ~ 75%) after only 5 cycles.

The Research Gap: Despite significant advancements in photocatalytic frameworks and high-end hydrogel composites, a formidable challenge remains: achieving a balance between high performance and environmental sustainability. Most current systems, including the advanced MXene, MOF, and customized biopolymer networks mentioned above, rely heavily on expensive, pristine, and high-purity commercial precursors, which directly run counter to the principles of the Circular Economy and limit large-scale industrial feasibility. Furthermore, the widespread adoption of hybrid hydrogels is severely hindered by nanoparticle leaching, complex multi-step chemical reduction synthesis, and progressive structural fatigue manifested as a drop in cyclic efficiency or low mechanical durability, which not only restricts industrial scalability but also poses a risk of secondary environmental contamination.

This study adopts a multifaceted strategic approach that addresses these profound limitations through Waste Valorization, thereby directly contributing to the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 6: Clean Water and Sanitation, SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities, and SDG 12: Responsible Consumption and Production).

The novelty of this work lies in the successful transformation of industrial scrap tungsten heavy alloy into high-purity, reactive WO3 powder, thereby effectively closing the tungsten lifecycle loop. Moreover, instead of relying on complex hydrothermal steps or toxic chemical reducing agents for metallic implementation, we utilize a green, chemical-free, and UV-assisted silver (Ag) photodecoration pathway to create a synergistic Ag/WO3 junction. Employing vinyltrimethoxysilane as a molecular bridge further ensures the covalent grafting of nanoparticles. This chemical “locking” mechanism offers a pioneering solution to the leaching problem, ensuring structural integrity that has remained elusive with conventional physical-blending methods.

This research aims to develop a robust, sustainable, and leach-free Ag-WO3/P(AA-co-AM) hybrid platform for advanced water purification. We aim to demonstrate that recycled industrial waste can be engineered into a high-value catalyst capable of near-total eradication of Escherichia coli (99.98%) and efficient photocatalytic degradation of organic dyes (86.5% within only 60 min) across a broad pH spectrum. Crucially, the resulting cross-linked synthetic copolymer network secures a monumental leap in surface hardness (from 43 to 80 Shore A), effectively eliminating the structural degradation and mechanical aging constraints common to traditional hydrogel platforms. Ultimately, this work seeks to provide a scalable model for bridging the gap between industrial waste management and high-performance environmental restoration.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of recycled tungsten oxide nanoparticles (nano-WO3)

The recycled Tungsten oxide nanoparticles (nano-WO3) used in this study were synthesized via a sustainable, ultrasonically assisted, acidic leaching process that repurposes industrial heavy tungsten alloy scrap. This recycling method was performed at the Central Metallurgical Research and Development Institute (CMRDI). To ensure high reactivity and surface area, the leaching process was effectively combined with ultrasonic irradiation, enabling efficient transformation of the metallic alloy into its oxide form. The chemical purity and morphological features of the resulting nano-WO3 powder were carefully validated. Figure 2 shows that Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis confirmed a high elemental purity of 98.9%. At the same time, Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) observed a well-defined crystalline structure with a particle size range of 80 to 200 nm. Notably, the thorough optimization of recycling parameters, along with detailed phase characterization of the produced oxide, has been extensively documented in our previously published work (H.M. Zidan et al.) [23, 24].

Coating of recycled tungsten nanoparticles with silver by photoreduction process

To enhance the photocatalytic and antimicrobial efficiency under UV irradiation, the reclaimed nano-WO₃ particles were decorated with silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs) via a controlled photo-reduction process. In a typical procedure:

First, 5.0 g of the recycled nano-WO₃ powder was thoroughly dispersed in a precisely prepared aqueous solution of silver nitrate (AgNO3, > 99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich). The concentration was adjusted to yield a theoretical silver loading of 10 wt%.

The resulting suspension was kept under continuous magnetic stirring in the dark for 30 min to ensure homogenous adsorption of Ag+ ions onto the oxide surface facets [25].

Subsequently, the photo-reduction was initiated by exposing the stirred suspension to a high-intensity UV-A/UVB irradiation source (lambda = 365 nm). The light source was securely positioned at a fixed vertical distance of 12 cm above the solution surface.

Preparation of acrylamide-co-acrylic acid hydrogel grafted with nano Ag/WO3

Materials

All chemical reagents used in this study were of analytical grade, used without further purification, and obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Company (Milwaukee, WI). Distilled water was used throughout this study. Acrylamide (AM, electrophoresis grade ≥ 99%), acrylic acid (AA, > 99%), N,Nʹ-methylenebisacrylamide (BIS, 99%), used as a crosslinker, as well as the redox initiator system, ammonium persulfate (APS), and N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyl ethylenediamine (TEMED), Vinyltrimethoxysilane (VTMOS, bp = 123 °C), 5 mol NaOH prepared from sodium hydroxide (NaOH pellets, > 99%).

Fabrication of hydrogel

The synthesis of the nanocomposite hydrogel was performed via a synchronized, multi-step in situ free-radical polymerization. The process was carefully divided into three sequential stages to ensure maximum integration of the functionalized nanoparticles within the polymer matrix.

The first stage, which involved synthesizing the nanoAg-WO3 nanocomposite hydrogels, began by silanizing the recycled tungsten oxide nanoparticles to ensure strong interfacial bonding within the polymer matrix. Vinyl Trimethoxy Silane (VTMS) served as the coupling agent at a concentration of 1–3% relative to the total polymer weight. To assess the effect of nanoparticle loading, four formulations were prepared: a control hydrogel without nanoparticles (HG0) and three nanocomposites (HG1, HG2, and HG3), each containing 0.4 wt%, 0.8 wt%, and 1.2 wt% of nanoAg-WO3 nanoparticles, respectively.

The chemical anchoring of inorganic fillers onto the organic network follows a coordinated two-step process. First, the methoxy groups (–OCH3) of the VTMS molecule undergo hydrolysis in an acidic aqueous medium, using half the amount of acrylic acid used to adjust the reaction pH, producing reactive silanol groups (–Si–OH) and releasing methanol as a byproduct. As shown in the proposed mechanism in Fig. 3, these silanol groups then participate in a condensation reaction with hydroxyl-rich surfaces of WO3 nanoparticles or interact with the silver-decocted sites. This process creates stable Si–O–W and Si–O–Ag covalent bridges, effectively functionalizing the nanoparticle surface with polymerizable vinyl groups. During the subsequent redox polymerization of Acrylic Acid (AA) and Acrylamide (AAm), these vinyl-functionalized nanoparticles act as multifunctional cross-linking points, forming a cohesive, covalently bonded hybrid network [27].

Figure 4 illustrates the mechanism of the second stage of the synthesis, which involves the partial neutralization of the Acrylic Acid (AA) monomer to optimize its reactivity and compatibility within the polymer network. In this procedure, half of the total amount of Acrylic Acid was titrated with a 5 M Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) solution under constant stirring in an ice bath to dissipate the heat of the exothermic reaction. This neutralization step is critical because it converts a portion of the carboxylic acid groups (–COOH) into sodium acrylate salts (–COONa), as shown in the chemical equation. The primary objective of this partial saponification is to enhance the swelling capacity and hydrophilicity of the resulting hydrogel. Furthermore, this neutralization stabilizes the polymerization environment, allowing the initiator system to operate efficiently without interference from high acidity.

The final stage of the synthesis involved the free-radical redox polymerization to form the integrated nanoAg-WO3/P(AA-co-AAm) hybrid network. The monomeric solution was prepared with a total polymer concentration of 25%, maintaining a specific mass ratio of 60% Acrylamide (AAm) to 40% Acrylic Acid (AA). To this mixture, N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA/BIS) was added at 2% as a cross-linking agent to establish the primary structural framework. The reaction was initiated with Ammonium Persulfate (APS) at 1.2%, and 100 µL of TEMED was added as a catalyst to accelerate free-radical formation at room temperature. The entire process was carried out in 20 mL of deionized water [28].

The chemical mechanism of nanocomposite hydrogel formation is illustrated in Fig. 5. Under a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere to prevent oxygen inhibition, the APS/TEMED system initiates copolymerization of acrylate and acrylamide monomers. As the chains propagate, the BIS molecules create transverse linkages between the growing linear chains, forming a three-dimensional porous matrix. Simultaneously, the silanized Ag/WO3 nanoparticles, which were previously functionalized with vinyl groups, are chemically incorporated into this network. These nanoparticles act as supplementary multi-functional cross-linkers, bridging the polymer segments through stable covalent bonds. This dual-cross-linked architecture provides the hydrogel with its superior mechanical stability and enhanced swelling performance, as required for efficient wastewater treatment applications [29].

Photocatalytic degradation efficiency

The photocatalytic performance of the P(AA-co-AAm)/Ag-nanoWO₃ nanocomposite hydrogels was assessed by observing the degradation of Methylene Blue (MB, > 99.8% purity, powder) dye under UV light. A stock solution of MB was prepared at a standard concentration of 10 ppm (0.01 g/L). For each test, a fixed amount of hydrogel was immersed in the dye solution. The reaction was carried out using a high-intensity UV light source (BLACKLIGHT BL350 & BL368 FEP Toughcoat™, which emits stable UVA and UVB radiation). To track the degradation progress, samples were taken from the solution at specific times (15, 30, and 60 min). Leaching and chemical stability tests (ICP-OES)

To evaluate the structural integrity and chemical stability of the fabricated Ag-WO3/Poly (AAm-co-AA) hybrid hydrogel, metal leaching extracted during the photocatalytic operations was quantitatively monitored. Prolonged leaching tests were conducted by submerging the optimum hybrid hydrogel samples in aqueous media under continuous agitation for a designated timeframe across different pH conditions (pH 3, 7, and 11). After completion of the catalytic cycles, aliquots of the treated water samples were collected and filtered through a 0.22-mm syringe membrane filter to remove any suspended micro fragments. The concentrations of potentially leached tungsten (W) and silver (Ag) ions in the filtrate were precisely quantified using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES Optima 2000 DV). The instrument was calibrated using certified standard multi-element solutions, and the lower limit of detection (LOD) was established to ensure sub-part-per-billion (ppb) analytical accuracy.

In vitro antibacterial activity of P(AA-co-AM)/Ag-WO3 nanocomposites

The in vitro photocatalytic antibacterial properties of the synthesized poly (acrylic acid-co-acrylamide) (PAA-co-AM) hydrogels, loaded with different weight percentages (0%, 1%, 2%, and 3%) of silver/tungsten oxide (Ag/WO₃) nanoparticles, were quantitatively evaluated using the standard viable count drop plate method [32]. The Gram-negative bacterial strain Escherichia coli (E. coli) was intentionally selected as the model pathogen for this test because of its widespread presence as a primary indicator of waterborne fecal contamination, aligning with the goal of developing materials for effective water treatment [33]. Before the experiments, the E. coli strain was cultured in Nutrient Broth (NB) and incubated overnight at 37 °C, then diluted in sterile physiological saline (0. 0.9% NaCl) to produce a standardized starting inoculum of about 6. 5 × 10⁷ colony- forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL). For the antibacterial tests, equal amounts (0.1 g) of the pristine P(AA-co-AM) hydrogel and the Ag-WO₃-loaded nanocomposite hydrogels were placed into individual flasks, each containing 50 mL of the prepared bacterial suspension. A negative control flask containing the bacterial suspension without any hydrogel was also prepared to establish baseline viability. To activate the photocatalytic function of the WO₃ semiconductor, all test flasks, including the control, were exposed to direct ultraviolet (UV) irradiation for exactly 2 min. Immediately afterward, the flasks were transferred to a shaker incubator and maintained at 37 °C with continuous agitation for 1 h to ensure optimal contact between the hydrogel surfaces and the bacteria [34]. Next, 1-mL samples were carefully taken from each treatment flask and from the negative control. These samples underwent tenfold serial dilutions in sterile saline to reach readable concentrations. A 20- µL volume from the appropriate dilution was then evenly spread onto designated sections of Mueller- Hinton Agar (MHA) plates. After incubating for 24 h at 37 °C, the surviving bacterial colonies were counted manually. The viable bacterial counts, expressed as CFU/mL, from the different treatments were directly compared with the untreated control counts to accurately determine the percentage of bacterial inhibition and evaluate treatment effectiveness.

All antibacterial experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test for multiple comparisons between treatment groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Different letters (a, b, c, d, e) indicate statistically significant differences between groups. All analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.12) with the SciPy statistical library.

Results and discussion

Characterization of Ag-decorated WO3 nanoparticles

The successful deposition of silver nanoparticles onto the recycled WO3 surface was confirmed through FE-SEM and EDX analysis.

Figure 6 shows the FE-SEM micrographs of the decorated powder, which reveal a distinct change in the surface texture of the WO3 particles. The recycled tungsten oxide exhibits its characteristic plate-like or polyhedral morphology, while the silver nanoparticles appear as highly dispersed, bright, spherical spots anchored to the WO3 facets. This hierarchical structure is crucial for preventing the recombination of photo-generated electron-hole pairs during the photocatalytic process [35]. The elemental purity of the decorated catalyst was confirmed by the EDX spectrum. As shown in Area 1 in Fig. 7, the main peaks correspond to Tungsten (W) and Oxygen (O), representing the host oxide matrix. Notably, the appearance of the Silver (Ag) peak confirms the successful reduction of silver ions (Ag+) to metallic silver (Ag0) through the UV-assisted process. The quantitative analysis shows a significant presence of Tungsten (around 64.23 wt%) and Oxygen (around 34.81 wt%), with the silver content effectively integrated into the hybrid system. The absence of impurity peaks further supports the success of the recycling and decoration protocols used in this study.

The density and hardness of hydrogel samples

The experimental data show a consistent rise in both density and surface hardness with the increasing loading of Ag/nanoWO3 nanoparticles. Specifically, the bulk density increased from 1.6223 g/cm3 for the pristine hydrogel (HG0) to 1.7010 g/cm3 for the (HG3) nanocomposite. This increase is directly attributable to the high theoretical density of Tungsten Oxide (approximately 7.16 g/cm3), which significantly enhances the overall mass-to-volume ratio of the polymer matrix upon successful incorporation [24].

Furthermore, mechanical robustness, measured by Shore A hardness, increased significantly, reaching 80 units for HG3. This reinforcement results not only from the physical presence of the nanoparticles but also from the role of the Vinyl Trimethoxy Silane (VTMS) coupling agent. The silane molecules served as effective molecular bridges, forming strong covalent bonds between the inorganic WO3 surface and the organic P(AA-co-AAm) chains. This chemical bonding restricts the movement of the polymer backbone, creating a more compact and rigid network capable of better withstanding surface indentation [36].

This pronounced increase in rigidity is highly beneficial for wastewater treatment applications. Unlike conventional hydrogels designed for high swelling capacity, photocatalytic and adsorbent hydrogels require greater structural resilience to withstand dynamic hydraulic shear and continuous stirring during water decontamination. The nano-fillers effectively restrict excessive relaxation of the polymer chains, shifting the material from a highly compliant state to a robust, high-endurance matrix that retains its geometry and performance over multiple treatment cycles.

The spectrum of the recycled tungsten oxide shows a broad band at 3393 cm−1, attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of adsorbed water molecules. A clear peak at 1618.6 cm−1 represents the bending vibration of water, while the strong band at 595.9 cm-1 and the shoulder at 939.2 cm−1 are assigned to the W–O–W bridging and W=O stretching modes, respectively [37].

In the hydrogel spectra, the broad absorption band between 3293 and 3315 cm−1 represents the overlapping of N–H stretching from the acrylamide (AAm) units and O–H stretching from the acrylic acid (AA) units. The peaks observed at approximately 2925 cm−1 correspond to the C–H stretching of the aliphatic polymer backbone. Successful polymerization is indicated by the intense carbonyl (C=O) stretching vibration at 1623 cm−1 (for HG0) and 1675 cm−1 (for HG3), which are characteristic of the amide and carboxyl groups [38]. The shift and changes in intensity of these peaks across the samples suggest strong molecular interactions within the hybrid network.

Notably, in the nanocomposite samples (HG1–HG3), the appearance of bands near 1170 cm−1 and 1405 cm−1 can be attributed to Si–O–Si and Si–C vibrations from the Vinyl Trimethoxysilane (VTMS) coupling agent. Furthermore, the consistent shift in the metal-oxide fingerprint region (below 600 cm−1) confirms the successful grafting of the Ag-nanoWO3 particles onto the polymer chains via the silane bridges [39].

Swelling kinetics and statistical optimization (ANOVA)

The swelling capacity of the hybrid hydrogels was examined through a series of kinetic experiments, and the results were statistically analyzed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Design-Expert software. The 3D response surface plots in Fig. 11 show a complex interaction between immersion time, pH, and nanoparticle loading. The water uptake mechanism mainly depends on the osmotic pressure generated by Na+ ions, arising from the neutralization of Acrylic Acid with NaOH, and on electrostatic repulsion between the deprotonated carboxylate (–COO–) groups [40]. As immersion time increases, the P(AA-co-AAm) network expands to fit water molecules, forming hydrogen bonds with amide and hydroxyl groups. Statistically, the model’s reliability was confirmed by a p-value less than 0.05, indicating that the effects of pH and time on swelling kinetics are statistically significant and not due to chance. This low p-value indicates a confidence level above 95%, especially given the peak swelling of 4326% in the original HG0 sample.

However, a noticeable decrease in the magnitude of swelling was observed as the concentration of nanoAg-WO3 nanoparticles increased from HG1 to HG3. This trend is directly attributed to the increased cross-linking density facilitated by the silanized nanoparticles, which act as rigid chemical bridges within the polymer matrix. The ANOVA results further support this, showing that the interaction between filler loading and network expansion is a significant factor in controlling the hydrogel’s dimensional stability. This structural rigidity is visually corroborated by the FE-SEM micrographs, which show a transition from the smooth surface of HG0 to the highly “wrinkled” and dense morphology of HG3. While these hierarchical wrinkles provide more surface area for photocatalysis, the underlying dense mesh, reflected in the high hardness value of 80 Shore A, physically restricts the expansion of the polymer chains, leading to controlled, lower swelling capacity in the nanocomposite samples.

This high efficiency is primarily attributed to the synergistic effect between the redox initiator system (APS/TEMED) and the monomer concentration. The addition of 100 µL of TEMED served as a powerful catalyst, significantly accelerating free radical generation and ensuring near-complete conversion of the 25% polymer precursors into a permanent, insoluble 3D network.

A detailed analysis of the data reveals that sample HG1 achieved a maximum gel content of 96.6%±4 at pH 3. Chemically, this is explained by the acidic environment, which reduces the electrostatic repulsion between acrylic acid chains, allowing them to come closer together and thereby enhancing both physical and chemical cross-linking density. These knots create a highly compact architecture that prevents the dissolution of polymer segments during the washing cycles, a conclusion further supported by FE-SEM micrographs that displayed a dense, hierarchical, wrinkled morphology.

The consistently high gel content in the nanoparticle-loaded samples (HG1–HG3) relative to the pristine HG0 clearly confirms successful silanization of the samples with VTMS. Without silanization, the inorganic oxide particles would likely leach from the organic matrix, leading to weight loss and reduced gel content. These rapid kinetics demonstrate the high efficiency of the hybrid system, in which a substantial portion of the dye is removed in just one hour, with no detectable leaching of the nanoAg-WO3 particles into the aqueous medium, confirming the superior structural stability afforded by silanization and the high gel content (94%).

Figure 16 shows that the mechanism of dye sequestration and degradation involves a dual-action process. For the blank hydrogel (HG0), the removal is primarily driven by electrostatic adsorption. The negatively charged carboxylate groups (–COO−) on the P(AA-co-AAm) chains (resulting from the NaOH neutralization) act as “traps” for the cationic MB molecules [44, 45]. However, in the nanoAg-WO3-loaded samples, this adsorption is supplemented by a powerful photocatalytic degradation mechanism triggered by UV light. The embedded WO3 semiconductor absorbs UV radiation, promoting electrons (e−) from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), thereby creating holes (h+). The presence of Silver (Ag) nanoparticles is crucial here; they act as “electron sinks” due to their localized surface plasmon resonance and Schottky barrier effect, which prevents the rapid recombination of e−/h+ pairs. The trapped electrons react with dissolved oxygen to form superoxide radicals (O2−), while the holes react with water molecules to produce highly reactive hydroxyl radicals OH: [46]

This synergistic effect, combining the high surface area and wrinkly morphology of HG3 (as seen in SEM) with the enhanced charge separation of the Ag/nanoWO3 heterostructure, explains the accelerated degradation kinetics. In vitro antibacterial activity of P(AA-co-AM)/Ag/WO3 nanocomposites

Figures 17 and 18 show that the in vitro photocatalytic antibacterial efficacy of P(AA-co-AM) hydrogels loaded with varying concentrations of Ag/WO3 nanoparticles (HG0, HG1, HG2, and HG3) was evaluated against E. coli under UV irradiation, revealing a highly significant, dose-dependent bactericidal effect (p < 0.05). While the untreated control exhibited a high viable count of 6.5 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, the blank P(AA-co-AM) hydrogel (HG0) showed no inherent bactericidal activity. The observed reduction in bacterial count to 9.5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL (an apparent 85.38% removal) was a physical phenomenon, primarily attributable to the adsorption and entrapment of bacterial cells within the highly swollen polymer matrix rather than to actual bacterial inactivation. Conversely, the incorporation of photocatalytic Ag/WO3 nanoparticles provided essential bactericidal activity, thereby drastically accelerating true bacterial eradication; hydrogels HG1 and HG2 significantly reduced the viable bacterial load to 6.5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL (99.00% inhibition) and 7.5 × 10⁴ CFU/mL (99.88% inhibition), respectively. The maximum antibacterial activity was achieved with HG3, which contains the highest wt% of nanocomposite.

resulting in near-complete eradication of E. coli (1.5 × 10⁴ CFU/mL) and an outstanding maximal inhibition efficiency of 99.98%.

The remarkable dose-dependent eradication of E. coli by the synthesized nanocomposites underscores a highly efficient, synergistic interplay between the polymeric matrix and the photocatalytic nanofillers. As the results indicated, the pristine P(AA-co-AM) network lacks inherent bactericidal toxicity; its moderate apparent reduction in bacterial counts is primarily driven by the physical entrapment and adsorption of cells within the highly hydrophilic, swollen polymeric scaffold rather than by chemical inactivation [47]. Upon UV irradiation, the incorporated WO3 semiconductor undergoes charge separation, while the decorated Ag nanoparticles act as crucial electron sinks, effectively delaying the recombination of electron-hole pairs through the formation of a Schottky barrier [48]. This sustained charge separation facilitates the continuous generation of lethal reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as superoxide radicals (O2⋅) and hydroxyl radicals (OH⋅) [49]. These ROS, combined with localized surface plasmon resonance and the sustained release of Ag+ ions, synergistically attack trapped pathogens, compromising membrane integrity, inducing severe lipid peroxidation, and ultimately leading to leakage of intracellular components and irreversible cell death [50]. Ultimately, the robust three-dimensional P(AA-co-AM) network serves a dual functional role: it actively draws the targeted waterborne pathogens into close proximity with the catalytic active sites, and it effectively prevents the agglomeration of the Ag/WO3 nanofillers, ensuring a maximized surface area for optimal photocatalytic water disinfection [31].
