A Phase 1 Study to Assess the Effect of Fruquintinib on the Pharmacokinetics of P‐gp and BCRP Substrates in Healthy Subjects
Authors: Martha Gonzalez, Zhao Yang, William R. Schelman, Xiaofei Zhou, Neeraj Gupta, Caly Chien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFruquintinib, a highly selective, oral inhibitor of all three vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (−1, −2, and −3), is approved for previously treated metastatic colorectal cancer. This phase 1, two‐part, two‐period, fixed‐sequence study (NCT05368805) evaluated the potential inhibitory effect of fruquintinib (5 mg) on the pharmacokinetics (PK) of P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) substrates. In Part A, 20 healthy subjects received a single oral dose of dabigatran etexilate (P‐gp substrate) 150 mg on Day 1 and Day 5, with fruquintinib co‐administered on Day 5. In Part B, 12 healthy subjects received a single oral dose of rosuvastatin (BCRP substrate) 10 mg on Day 1 and Day 5, with fruquintinib co‐administered on Day 5. Plasma samples were collected to evaluate the PK of dabigatran and rosuvastatin over 96 h, and to measure fruquintinib and its major metabolite M11 concentrations over 192 h. Co‐administration of dabigatran etexilate with fruquintinib resulted in a ~10% decrease in the systemic exposure of dabigatran; geometric mean ratios (GMRs) and 90% CIs were 0.90 (0.66–1.23) for C max and 0.91 (0.68–1.23) for AUC0–inf. Co‐administration of rosuvastatin with fruquintinib resulted in a decrease in the systemic exposure of rosuvastatin by approximately 16% for C max and 19% for AUC0–inf; GMRs and 90% CIs were 0.84 (0.65–1.10) for C max and 0.81 (0.65–1.02) for AUC0–inf. Fruquintinib had no clinically significant effect on the PK of P‐gp or BCRP substrates, supporting that dose adjustments for substrates of P‐gp or BCRP are unnecessary when co‐administered with fruquintinib.

Introduction

Angiogenesis is a critical step in the inception and development of malignant tumors. Tumor cells growing rapidly in a state of hypoxia can secrete a variety of pro‐angiogenic growth factors that stimulate endothelial cell proliferation, leading to the formation of excessive new blood vessels around the tumor that supply both nutrients and oxygen. These rapidly formed vasculatures are often coarsely packed and ‘leaky’ resulting in an exudation of tumor cells into circulation, initiating tumor metastasis [1, 2]. One of the major growth factors related to tumor angiogenesis is vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which is secreted by tumors and binds to VEGF receptors (VEGFR) on the surface of endothelial cells to activate the VEGFR signaling pathway, leading to endothelial cell proliferation. Blocking the VEGF/VEGFR signaling pathway inhibits tumor angiogenesis and cuts off the nutrients and oxygen supply to the tumor [3].

Fruquintinib is a highly selective, oral, small‐molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor of all three VEGFRs (−1, −2, and −3) [4, 5]. Based on the results of the FRESCO (NCT02314819) study, fruquintinib was approved in China at a dose of 5 mg once daily (QD) for 3 weeks on/1 week off in 28‐day cycles for the treatment of patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) who had failed two prior lines of systemic therapy [6, 7]. Based on the results of the FRESCO and FRESCO‐2 (NCT04322539) studies, fruquintinib was approved in the United States for the treatment of adult patients with mCRC who have been previously treated with fluoropyrimidine‐, oxaliplatin‐, and irinotecan‐based chemotherapy, an anti‐VEGF therapy, and, if RAS wild‐type and medically appropriate, an anti‐epidermal growth factor receptor therapy [6, 8, 9]. Fruquintinib is also approved in several other countries/regions, including, but not limited to, Japan, the European Union, the United Kingdom, Canada, Switzerland, and Australia [10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

The pharmacokinetics (PK) of fruquintinib in phase 1 clinical studies were characterized in healthy volunteers [15, 16] and in patients with cancer [17]. Briefly, fruquintinib is rapidly and completely absorbed after oral administration, median time to maximum plasma concentration (T max) is approximately 2 h, and plasma protein binding is estimated to be 95%. Fruquintinib demonstrated linear PK over the dose range of 1–6 mg, and the elimination half‐life (t 1/2) was reported to be approximately 42 h [9]. A previous phase 1 study found that following administration of 5 mg of radiolabeled fruquintinib, approximately 60% of the dose was recovered in urine (0.50% unchanged) and 30% was recovered in feces (5.34% unchanged), with the remaining radioactivity excreted as metabolites. M11 was identified as the major circulating metabolite, accounting for approximately 17% of total radioactivity in plasma [18].

In vitro results indicated that fruquintinib is metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP) and non‐CYP enzymes. CYP3A4 is the main enzyme among the CYP isoforms involved in the metabolism of fruquintinib, with minor contributions from CYP2C8, CYP2C9 and CYP2C19. A previous clinical drug–drug interaction (DDI) study showed no change in fruquintinib exposure when co‐administered with itraconazole, a CYP3A inhibitor [15]. This suggests that fruquintinib is not a sensitive CYP3A substrate and its clearance is not solely dependent on CYP3A. Another study assessing the effects of food and rabeprazole, a proton pump inhibitor, on the PK of fruquintinib in healthy subjects, reported that consumption of a high‐fat meal, or concomitant administration of rabeprazole, did not significantly alter fruquintinib exposure [19]. A population PK analysis, which assessed sources of interpatient variability on the PK of fruquintinib and M11 in more than 500 subjects, found that several factors including race, ethnicity, age, country, and body weight had no meaningful impact on fruquintinib PK [20].

While fruquintinib is not a substrate for transporters such as P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP)1B1, and OATP1B3 [9], it has the potential to inhibit both P‐gp and BCRP in a dose‐dependent manner, with inhibitory concentration at 50% (IC50) values of 4.60 μM and 1.29 μM, respectively. Metabolite M11 also inhibits BCRP (IC50 = 1.03 μM) but shows minimal inhibition of P‐gp (IC50 > 20 μM). P‐gp and BCRP play key roles in the absorption and disposition of concomitant medications commonly used by patients with mCRC, including dabigatran etexilate and digoxin (P‐gp substrates) [21, 22], and rosuvastatin (a BCRP substrate) [23].

To assess the clinical relevance of these in vitro findings and guide the usage of concomitant medications in patients, a phase 1 study to assess the effect of fruquintinib on the PK of dabigatran etexilate and rosuvastatin was conducted in healthy subjects.

Methods

Study Design

This was a single center, open label, two‐part, two‐period, fixed sequence, phase 1 study in healthy subjects (NCT05368805). The study consisted of a screening phase, a treatment phase (Part A and B), and an end‐of‐study phase. All subjects were admitted to the study center on Day −1 and discharged after collection of the PK sample on Day 13 or at early withdrawal. In both Parts A and B, Day 1 was considered the start of treatment period 1, and Day 5 was considered the start of treatment period 2 (Figure 1).

Study design. All treatments were given under fasted conditions. After dosing, PK samples for dabigatran and for rosuvastatin were collected over 96 h; PK samples for fruquintinib were collected over 192 h after dosing of fruquintinib. *, dosing day; BCRP, breast cancer resistance protein; P‐gp, P‐glycoprotein; PK, pharmacokinetic.

All treatments were given under fasted conditions. In Part A, a single oral dose of dabigatran etexilate 150 mg was given alone on Day 1 and co‐administered with fruquintinib 5 mg on Day 5 (on Day 5, both fruquintinib and dabigatran etexilate were administered concurrently). In Part B, a single oral dose of rosuvastatin 10 mg was given alone on Day 1 and co‐administered with fruquintinib 5 mg on Day 5 (on Day 5, both fruquintinib and rosuvastatin were administered concurrently). After dosing, PK samples for dabigatran (t 1/2 approximately 12–17 h) and for rosuvastatin (t 1/2 approximately 19 h) were collected over 96 h. PK samples for fruquintinib were collected over 192 h after dosing of fruquintinib, as the t 1/2 for fruquintinib following a single dose in healthy subjects was previously found to be approximately 34 h [15].

Subjects

Healthy male and female subjects between the ages of 18 and 55 years with a body mass index of 18–29 kg/m2 were enrolled at PPD Development, LP (Austin, Texas). Females were of nonchildbearing potential, and males agreed to use highly effective contraception. Subjects were excluded from the study if they had evidence of clinically significant cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, hepatic, renal, respiratory, endocrine, hematological, neurological, or psychiatric diseases. Other key exclusion criteria included a known history of any gastrointestinal surgery or any condition possibly affecting drug absorption; a history of smoking or use of nicotine‐containing substances within the previous 2 months, or a positive cotinine test at screening or check‐in for any one of the treatment periods; a history of drug or alcohol misuse within 6 months prior to screening, or a positive urine drug test at screening or check‐in for any one of the treatment periods; a diagnosis of acquired immune deficiency syndrome or a positive test for human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B virus, or hepatitis C virus; participation in a clinical study of another drug before screening, when the time since the last use of the other study drug was less than five times the t 1/2 or 4 weeks, whichever was longer, or current enrollment in another clinical study; use of any medications within 14 days prior to study Day 1; or consumption of grapefruit, starfruit, Seville oranges, herbal preparations/medications including, but not limited to, kava, ephedra (ma huang), and Ginkgo biloba , dehydroepiandrosterone, yohimbe, saw palmetto, and ginseng within 7 days prior to study Day 1.

The protocol (2021‐013‐00US3) and consent form were approved by an institutional review board (Salus Independent Review Board, Austin, Texas) on February 16, 2022, before study initiation, and all subjects provided written, informed consent prior to any study procedures. The study was performed in accordance with the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonization Guideline for Good Clinical Practice, and other applicable local laws and regulations.

Bioanalysis

In Part A, blood samples for determination of total dabigatran (non‐conjugated plus conjugated dabigatran after complete alkaline cleavage of dabigatran glucuronides) plasma concentrations were collected for each subject at pre‐dose and at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h after both doses of dabigatran etexilate (period 1 and 2), and an additional collection at 96 h postdose in period 2. In Part B, blood samples for determination of rosuvastatin plasma concentrations were collected at pre‐dose and at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, and 84 h after both rosuvastatin doses (period 1 and period 2), and an additional collection at 96 h postdose in period 2. In both Part A and B, blood samples for determination of fruquintinib and metabolite M11 plasma concentrations were collected in period 2 at the same time as the dabigatran or rosuvastatin collections as well as at 120, 144, 168, and 192 h after fruquintinib dosing. Plasma samples were analyzed by LabCorp Bioanalytical (Indianapolis, Indiana) using a validated, specific, and sensitive liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry assay method. The analytical range (lower to upper limit of quantitation) for dabigatran, rosuvastatin, and fruquintinib/M11 was 2.00 to 500 ng/mL, 0.020 to 20.0 ng/mL, and 1.00 to 750 ng/mL, respectively. For dabigatran, inter‐run variability was ≤ 6.2%. Quality control (QC) samples at three concentrations (6.00, 50.0, 375 ng/mL) were assayed along with study samples, and the bias QC sample concentrations deviated by ±1.6%. For rosuvastatin, inter‐run variability was ≤ 7.7%. QC samples at four concentrations (0.060, 0.775, 10.0, 15.0 ng/mL) were assayed along with study samples, and the bias QC sample concentrations deviated by ±3.3%. For fruquintinib and M11, inter‐run variability was ≤ 6.6% and ≤ 9.6%, respectively. QC samples at four concentrations (3, 30, 300, and 600 ng/mL) were assayed along with study samples, and the bias QC sample concentrations deviated by ±2.0% and ±3.7% from the nominal concentrations for fruquintinib and M11, respectively.

Pharmacokinetic and Statistical Assessments

PK parameters were determined by noncompartmental analysis using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.3.5 (Certara, L.P. Princeton, New Jersey) and included maximum plasma concentration (C max), T max, area under the plasma concentration–time curve from 0 to time of the last measurable concentration (AUC0–t ), AUC from time 0 to infinity (AUC0–inf), and t 1/2. Apparent oral clearance and volume were also estimated for dabigatran, rosuvastatin, and fruquintinib, as well as the metabolite‐to‐parent ratios of M11 to fruquintinib for C max (MPR C max), AUC0–t (MPR AUC0–t ), and AUC0–inf (MPR AUC0–inf).

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 or higher (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina). The effect of fruquintinib on the ln‐transformed C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–inf of dabigatran and rosuvastatin was evaluated using a linear mixed‐effect model with treatment as fixed effects and subject as a random effect. The ratios of geometric least square means of combination treatment (dabigatran etexilate with fruquintinib or rosuvastatin with fruquintinib) versus treatment alone (dabigatran etexilate alone or rosuvastatin alone) and associated two‐sided 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were presented.

Safety Assessments

Safety was assessed by evaluation of adverse events (AEs), serious AEs, AEs of special interest, physical examinations, vital signs, single 12‐lead electrocardiograms and cardiac monitoring, and clinical laboratory data. AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities Version 24.1 and the severity of AEs was graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0.

Effect of Fruquintinib on a P‐gp Substrate

The mean concentration‐time profiles of dabigatran after a single dose of dabigatran etexilate 150 mg, given alone or in combination with fruquintinib 5 mg, were similar, with peak concentrations at a median T max of 2 h in both treatments (Figure 2A). Dabigatran plasma concentrations were quantifiable up to 36 h after administration alone or with fruquintinib; thereafter, most plasma concentrations were below the limit of quantitation (> 60%). The mean dabigatran t 1/2 was slightly longer when administered with fruquintinib versus when administered alone (9.44 h vs. 8.14 h, Table 2). Systemic exposure parameters of dabigatran C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–inf decreased by approximately 10% when administered with fruquintinib compared to when administered alone. The geometric mean ratio (GMR) for C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–inf was 0.90, 0.90, and 0.91, respectively (Table 2). The 90% CIs contained unity for all parameters: C max (0.66–1.23), AUC0–t (0.66–1.24), and AUC0–inf (0.68–1.23). The differences in PK parameters were not considered clinically meaningful.

Effect of Fruquintinib on a BCRP Substrate

The mean concentration‐time profiles of rosuvastatin when administered alone or in combination with fruquintinib were similar (Figure 2B). Peak rosuvastatin concentrations were achieved slightly later when co‐administered with fruquintinib versus when administered alone, with a T max of 4.50 h vs. 3.01 h. Mean rosuvastatin t 1/2 was similar when rosuvastatin was taken alone compared to when taken with fruquintinib (Table 2). Rosuvastatin plasma concentrations were quantifiable up to 72 h after administration alone or with fruquintinib; thereafter, most plasma concentrations were below the limit of quantitation (> 60%). Statistical comparison indicated that systemic exposure to rosuvastatin decreased by approximately 14%–19% when administered with fruquintinib compared to when administered alone. The GMR for C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–inf was 0.84, 0.86, and 0.81, respectively. The 90% CIs contained unity for all parameters: C max (0.65–1.10), AUC0–t (0.67–1.09), and AUC0–inf (0.65–1.02). These differences in PK parameters were not considered clinically meaningful.

Safety and Tolerability

A total of six AEs were reported in this study, all of which were grade 1 (mild) and fully recovered (Table S1). In Part A treatment period 2 (dabigatran etexilate with fruquintinib), one of the 20 subjects (5%) had an AE (skin papule). In Part B, a total of five AEs were reported in three of the 12 subjects (25.0%). Specifically, in Part B treatment period 1 (rosuvastatin alone), one subject had a skin macule, headache, and nausea; none were considered study drug‐related; another subject in period 1 had a headache, which was considered possibly related to the study drug. In Part B treatment period 2 (rosuvastatin with fruquintinib) one subject reported flatulence, which was considered possibly related to the study drugs.

There were no clinically significant changes in chemistry, hematology, urinalysis, and coagulation laboratory test results over time during the study. There were no clinically significant changes in postdose vital signs, postdose electrocardiogram results, or postdose physical examination findings compared to baseline that were attributable to fruquintinib.

Discussion

Historically, most chemotherapy treatments were administered intravenously [24]. With the development of oral cancer medications, identifying factors that may modify drug absorption is crucial to ensure patients receive doses that achieve systemic drug exposure within the expected therapeutic window. Fruquintinib, an orally administered inhibitor of all three VEGFRs −1, −2, and −3, is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) for treating patients with previously treated mCRC at a regimen of 5 mg QD for 3 weeks, followed by 1 week off in each 28‐day cycle [9]. In vitro data and prediction algorithms identified a potential DDI risk with sensitive substrates of P‐gp and BCRP at the clinical dose (Takeda data on file). Using the theoretical intestinal luminal concentration (I gut) of fruquintinib calculated as the dose/250 mL, the I gut/IC50 ratios for fruquintinib‐mediated inhibition of intestinal efflux transporters were 11 for P‐gp and 39 for BCRP. For metabolite M11, the estimated C max/IC50 ratio was 0.19 for BCRP. These values suggest a low margin beyond the threshold of ≥ 10 for the parent drug (or ≥ 0.1 for metabolite) [25]. Currently, no standardized procedure exists for quantitatively predicting exposure changes of sensitive substrates for DDIs involving P‐gp and BCRP. Regulatory recommended thresholds are intentionally conservative to avoid false‐negative predictions. However, literature suggests that the likelihood of clinically significant interactions (> 30% change in AUC) is low when the I gut/IC50 ratio remains below 45 [26, 27, 28].

The current phase 1 study was conducted to evaluate the effect of fruquintinib on P‐gp and BCRP substrates; fruquintinib has the potential to inhibit P‐gp and BCRP‐mediated drug disposition, based on in vitro findings. Dabigatran etexilate and rosuvastatin were selected as index substrates for P‐gp and BCRP, respectively. Index substrates are defined as having an AUC increase of at least 1.5‐fold when co‐administered with an inhibitor. According to unpublished study results, dabigatran AUC was increased approximately 120% in the presence of the P‐gp inhibitor itraconazole when dabigatran etexilate was given at a therapeutic dose [29], while the BCRP inhibitor regorafenib was shown to increase rosuvastatin AUC by 3.8‐fold [30]. These exposure increases support the use of dabigatran etexilate and rosuvastatin as sensitive substrates for assessing the potential inhibitory effects of fruquintinib on P‐gp and BCRP, respectively.

After co‐administration with fruquintinib, the mean concentration‐time profiles of dabigatran when administered alone or in combination with fruquintinib looked similar. Statistical comparison indicated that exposure parameters of dabigatran C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–inf decreased by approximately 10% when administered with fruquintinib compared to when administered alone. The GMR for C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–inf was 0.90, 0.90, and 0.91, respectively. The T max was not affected by co‐administration with fruquintinib; however, the mean dabigatran t 1/2 was numerically longer when administered with fruquintinib (9.44 h vs. 8.14 h). None of these small differences in PK parameters were viewed as clinically meaningful.

The mean concentration‐time profiles of rosuvastatin when administered alone or in combination with fruquintinib appeared similar. Statistical comparison indicated that exposure parameters of rosuvastatin C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–inf decreased by approximately 14%–19% when administered with fruquintinib as compared to when administered alone. The GMR for C max, AUC0–t , and AUC0–inf was 0.84, 0.86, and 0.81, respectively. Rosuvastatin T max was numerically delayed when administered with fruquintinib (4.50 h vs. 3.01 h). Mean rosuvastatin t 1/2 was similar when rosuvastatin was taken alone versus with fruquintinib. None of these differences in PK parameters were viewed as clinically meaningful.

PK parameters for fruquintinib in both Part A and Part B were consistent with historical data following single‐dose administration in healthy subjects. In a recently reported study [15], the mean C max and AUC0–inf for fruquintinib after a 5 mg single dose were 133 ng/mL and 5960 h*ng/mL, respectively. These values align closely with results from the present study: Part A showed a mean C max of 139 ng/mL and AUC0–inf of 5309 h*ng/mL, while Part B reported a mean C max of 132 ng/mL and AUC0–inf of 5612 h*ng/mL. Similarly, PK results for M11 were comparable between studies, with a mean C max of 15.2 ng/mL and AUC0–inf of 2000 h*ng/mL observed in the previous study [15].

Following multiple‐dose administration, the observed accumulation ratios were approximately 4 for fruquintinib [17]. The current study evaluated the effect of fruquintinib on P‐gp and BCRP after single‐dose administration, differing from the conventional belief that substrate drugs should be administered once the inhibitor drug has reached steady‐state plasma levels to maximize the potential for systemic interaction. In fact, single‐dose administration of P‐gp or BCRP inhibitors at therapeutic doses has been shown to effectively characterize their inhibitory effects on intestinal absorption. For example, while itraconazole exposure accumulates by 343% after multiple doses, its effect on the P‐gp substrate fexofenadine is comparable after a single dose. Fexofenadine AUC0–inf increased by 178% after a single 200 mg dose and by 169% after 6 days of 200 mg itraconazole QD [31]. Similarly, the inhibitory effect of pirtobrutinib on the BCRP substrate rosuvastatin was similar after single and multiple doses (rosuvastatin AUC0–inf increased from 88.2 ng*h/mL at baseline to 189 ng*h/mL after a single pirtobrutinib dose and 207 ng*h/mL after multiple doses) despite a 1.6‐fold accumulation of pirtobrutinib at steady state (NCT05176314) [32]. These findings suggest that the maximum inhibitory effects of P‐gp and BCRP in the gastrointestinal tract can be achieved after a single dose of the inhibitor drug, while the impact of systemic inhibition due to parent and/or metabolite accumulation appears negligible.

From a physiological point of view, I gut of an inhibitor drug is expected to remain consistent following single or multiple daily doses and is always higher than its systemic concentrations. Thus, the effects observed after a single dose can be used to predict the worst‐case scenario, as P‐gp and BCRP are efflux transporters most extensively expressed in the gastrointestinal tract. To further verify this, a physiologically based PK (PBPK) model was developed to evaluate P‐gp and BCRP inhibition after repeat dosing of 5 mg fruquintinib to steady‐state. PBPK analysis predicted fruquintinib would increase the AUC0–inf of digoxin (a P‐gp substrate) by approximately 6% and rosuvastatin by approximately 19% (Takeda data on file). These findings support the current clinical results showing that a single dose of fruquintinib did not cause clinically meaningful changes in the systemic exposure of P‐gp and BCRP substrates. The PBPK simulation results indicated that the accumulation of the parent compound (for P‐gp and BCRP) and M11 (for BCRP only) from multiple doses of fruquintinib would have minimal impact on PK of dabigatran and rosuvastatin, as evidenced by the minimal difference in the ratios of AUC in the test groups to the control groups between single‐dose and multiple‐dose simulations. In vitro data indicated P‐gp inhibition with an IC50 of 4.6 μM, suggesting an estimated I gut of 50.8 μM (based on dose/250 mL volume assumption and fruquintinib's molecular weight of 393.39 g/mol), for an I gut/IC50 ratio of approximately 11—slightly exceeding the FDA/EMA threshold of 10 that warrants clinical evaluation. The current study showed no clinically meaningful changes in dabigatran exposure when co‐administered with fruquintinib. This discrepancy aligns with broader limitations of the static I gut/IC50 approach. The ratio of ~11 is only modestly above 10, but retrospective analyses and PBPK correlations suggest a higher cutoff may be needed to predict a clinically relevant interaction (> 25% increase in victim AUC) for intestinal P‐gp inhibition [25]. The low magnitude (only 1.1‐fold above cutoff) implies insufficient inhibitory potency in the dynamic gut environment to substantially alter dabigatran absorption, consistent with the observed < 10% exposure change. While in vitro assays indicated P‐gp and BCRP inhibition, the effective concentration of fruquintinib in the gut may be lower due to rapid absorption or low solubility, reducing free I gut below the IC50 threshold during key transit windows. The static ratio assumes prolonged, uniform contact; however, dynamic transit diluted the inhibition, especially for a modestly potent inhibitor like fruquintinib. Fruquintinib demonstrates pH‐dependent solubility (solubility at pH 6.0–6.5 lower than its solubility at pH 1–2; data on file). In vitro IC50 assays may overestimate potency under variable luminal conditions. In general, the I gut/IC50 ratio is a simple, conservative screening tool to evaluate transporter‐mediated DDI risk for investigational agents, and to inform clinical study design and safer co‐administration strategies. For precise assessment, this should be integrated with clinical data and model‐based approaches.

Together, the results contribute to the growing evidence that a drug with an I gut/IC50 ratio greater than 10 for P‐gp or BCRP inhibition in vitro, does not necessarily predict a clinically significant interaction with P‐gp or BCRP substrates, and underscores the need to optimize the threshold for requiring clinical transporter‐mediated DDI studies.

In conclusion, this study evaluated the effect of fruquintinib on the pharmacokinetics of dabigatran etexilate (a P‐gp substrate) and rosuvastatin (a BCRP substrate) in healthy volunteers. The results showed no clinically significant impact of fruquintinib on the exposure of either substrate, supporting that no dose adjustments for medications that are P‐gp or BCRP substrates are necessary when co‐administered with fruquintinib.
