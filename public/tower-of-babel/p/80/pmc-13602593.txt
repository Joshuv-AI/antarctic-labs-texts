Angiocrine factors in tumor microenvironment: bidirectional crosstalk, mechanistic insights, and therapeutic strategies
Authors: Gaili Ji, Yaping Wang, Xiaoxue Li, Zuchen Yang, Luyao Kang, Yutian Chen, Caixia Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor recurrence, metastasis, and treatment resistance represent core challenges in clinical oncology closely associated with the complex and dynamic tumor microenvironment (TME). Endothelial cells (ECs), key components of the TME, have functions that extend far beyond the traditional concept of tumor angiogenesis. In recent years, extensive research has revealed that ECs perform endocrine and paracrine functions by secreting angiocrine factors. These factors have been shown to contribute to tumor progression through multiple mechanisms, including remodeling of the tumor vascular niche, tumor immune suppression, metabolic reprogramming, and chemotherapy resistance. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the functional characteristics and regulatory mechanisms of angiocrine factors is crucial for identifying effective therapeutic targets in cancer.Main bodyThis review integrates research advances on angiocrine factors associated with tumor progression, categorizing them into several key functional domains on the basis of their biological characteristics. We summarize the core signaling pathways regulated by angiocrine factors, including the Notch and Wnt pathways, and their crosstalk networks. We further elucidate the regulatory role of angiocrine factors in endothelial metabolism. Additionally, we explore the multifaceted functions of angiocrine factors in tumor dormancy, metastasis (tumor metastasis mediated by endothelial necroptosis), stemness maintenance, and immune suppression. Notably, this review highlights the complex crosstalk between ECs and tumor cells (TCs), as well as the roles of Notch and Wnt signaling pathways in vascular formation and endothelial metabolic. Finally, we discuss the current clinical limitations of anti-angiogenic therapies and combination treatment strategies, while highlighting the therapeutic potential and challenges of a “one-target, multiple-effects” approach.ConclusionAngiocrine factors are central regulatory mediators that coordinate tumor progression. The complexity and redundancy of the angiocrine factor networks is not only the root of limitations in anti-vascular therapies but also present opportunities for “one-target, multiple-effects” strategies. Rapid advances in single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and lineage tracing technologies now provide powerful tools to decipher the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of vascular secretory factors and to track the clonal evolution and functional transitions of endothelial cells and their progenitor cells. These advances offer the potential to precisely identify key therapeutic targets, facilitating the development of novel anti-angiogenic strategies.Keywords: Tumor microenvironment, Angiocrine factors, Endothelial cell, Vascular niche

Introduction

Recurrence, metastasis, and chemoresistance are important causes of death in cancer patients. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is composed of tumor cells (TCs), stromal cells, and the extracellular matrix. Stromal cells, mainly including fibroblasts, adipocytes, immune cells, endothelial cells (ECs), and pericytes, account for 50% of late-stage tumors [1]. ECs, as important components of the TME, not only play a role in angiogenesis but also have vascular secretory functions [2, 3]. This contributes to the complexity of targeting the vasculature and is an important reason for the failure of many vascular inhibitors in clinical trials [3–5]. Tumor endothelial cells (TECs) affect the malignant properties of tumors by secreting angiocrine factors that act on cancer cells themselves or stromal cells within the TME. Additionally, TECs engage in crosstalk with TCs and other stromal components, precisely regulating immune responses, metabolic reprogramming, and intercellular communication within the TME [6, 7]. For example, ligands expressed by ECs, such as delta-like canonical Notch ligand 4 (Dll4), Jagged 1 (Jag1), and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), interact with their receptors on TCs, forming a bidirectional activation loop that enhances tumor stemness and chemoresistance (Fig. 1) [6]. Elucidating these mechanisms not only underscores the central role of angiocrine signaling in malignant progression but also offers novel theoretical perspectives and interventional strategies for targeted cancer therapy.

Existing reviews have summarized the regulatory roles of angiocrine factors in tumor progression, mainly focusing on associated malignant properties such as tumor stemness and drug resistance. Meanwhile, they pay less attention to crosstalk among signaling pathways and bidirectional interactions between ECs and TCs. In this review, we highlight the crosstalk between endothelial Notch and Wnt signaling pathways in angiogenesis and endothelial metabolism, and elucidate how angiocrine mediated endothelial‑tumor (EC-TC) crosstalk shapes tumor malignant phenotypes via multi‑layered regulatory networks. When developing new therapeutic strategies, it is crucial to consider these factors in designing anti-angiogenic therapies that are multi-targeted and pleiotropic.

Diversity and functional classes of angiocrine factors

The vascular system has evolved beyond a passive conduit for oxygen and nutrient transport to become an active secretory organ. Vascular ECs synthesize and release a diverse array of bioactive molecules, including cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules, collectively termed angiocrine factors, which regulate essential pathophysiological processes, including tissue regeneration and repair, fibrosis, inflammatory responses, and tumor progression [2, 8]. On the basis of their functional properties within the TME, angiocrine factors can be classified as follows:

Pro-angiogenic factors

Pro-angiogenic angiocrine factors mainly include members of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and epidermal growth factor (EGF) families, which directly regulate vascular growth and remodeling by binding to receptors on ECs [9–13]. They can also act indirectly. For example, EGF acts through the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway, enhancing VEGF secretion and promoting angiogenesis [14–16].

Immunomodulatory factors

Immunomodulatory angiocrine factors can be categorized into three groups: (i) inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-8 [17–19]; (ii) chemokines, such as stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF-1, also known as CXCL12), CCL2, and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) [12, 20, 21]; and (iii) immune checkpoint molecules and other regulatory factors, for example, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and integrin-associated protein (IAP, also known as CD47) [22, 23]. These factors enable ECs to effectively regulate immune cell recruitment, polarization, and the innate immune response, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion and supporting tumor progression.

Cell adhesion molecules and extracellular matrix remodeling factors

Angiocrine factors in this group can be classified into the following categories: (i) cell adhesion molecules, such as vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), and endothelial selectin (E-selectin), which promote leukocyte recruitment and transendothelial migration of TCs [24–28]; (ii) cadherin proteins, such as vascular endothelial-cadherin(VE-cadherin), which maintain endothelial barrier integrity and regulate vascular permeability [29]; (iii) extracellular matrix modulators, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), specifically MMP-2 and MMP-9, and thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), which regulate basement membrane degradation and matrix remodeling [30, 31].

Metabolic regulators

The key regulators of endothelial metabolism are 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2, 6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) and specific monocarboxylate transporter (MCT) isoforms. PFKFB3 drives a high glycolytic flux to support angiogenesis [32, 33]. Additionally, ECs release tumor-derived metabolites such as lactate and ketone bodies, which contribute to microenvironment acidification, inhibit immune cell function, and provide alternative energy substrates for nutrient deficient tumors. These effects collectively promote tumor survival and progression.

Extracellular vesicles

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived by ECs are a class of angiocrine factors. These vesicles carry bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, and noncoding RNAs, participate in cell-to-cell communication, and play a dual regulatory role in promoting or inhibiting tumorigenesis and development due to differences in their contents. On the one hand, endothelial-derived EVs carrying microRNAs such as miR-126, miR-30a-5p and miR-503 can significantly suppress malignant proliferation of TCs [34–36]. On the other hand, ECs can also release EVs enriched with pro-tumorigenic molecules like Ang2 and S100A16, thereby accelerating tumor malignancy [37, 38]. Furthermore, angiocrine signaling mediated by EVs involves bidirectional crosstalk [39, 40]. For example, EVs derived from SKOV3 cells and MDA-MB-231 cells can induce AKT phosphorylation in ECs and activate downstream signaling pathways; activated AKT signaling subsequently promotes further release of EVs from ECs, ultimately cooperating to drive tumor progression and chemoresistance [39].

Beyond these functions, angiocrine factors also include key signaling molecules involved in pathways such as Notch, Wnt, and Hedgehog [41–45]. Among these pathways, the Notch and Wnt signaling pathways serve as core effectors of angiocrine factors, playing multiple regulatory roles ranging from vascular development to tumor-stroma crosstalk. The complex roles of the Notch and Wnt pathways are discussed in detail in the following chapter.

Core angiocrine signaling pathways in EC-TC crosstalk

Notch signaling pathway

Notch signaling is an evolutionarily conserved pathway that plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis [46–50]. It typically consists of four Notch receptors (Notch1-4) and five ligands (Jag1, Jag2, Dll1, Dll3 and Dll4). When a transmembrane ligand on one cell binds to a Notch receptor on adjacent cells, γ-secretase mediates the proteolytic cleavage and release of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD). The NICD then translocates into the nucleus, where it interacts with the transcription factor CSL and activates the transcription of target genes to regulate cell fate [51–53]. Within the TME, the EC‑specific Notch ligands Jag1 and Dll4 coordinate with tumor angiogenesis through an antagonistic mechanism [54]. Additionally, they shape a specialized vascular niche that is supportive of cancer progression by mediating EC-TC crosstalk.

Antagonistic roles of Dll4 and Jag1 in angiogenesis

Dll4 and Jag1 exhibit distinct expression patterns in blood vessels. Dll4 is an EC-specific Notch ligand located mainly in arterioles and capillaries, and Jag1 is expressed broadly across the vasculature [50, 52, 55, 56]. Tip cell-enriched Dll4 mediates “lateral inhibition” by binding to Notch1 on adjacent stalk cells, leading to the suppression of VEGFR2 expression, which is thus induced to retain a stalk cell phenotype [55, 57]. Jag1 competes with Dll4 for Notch binding [58, 59]. Stalk cell-derived Jag1 promotes “lateral activation” through interaction with Notch1 on tip cells, thereby promoting the tip cell phenotype [60]. The balance between this Dll4-Jag1 competition is essential for physiological angiogenesis. When Dll4 and Jag1 become dysregulated, their phenotypics diverge dramatically: excessive Dll4 exerts an inhibitory effect on angiogenesis, while Jag1 activity triggers unregulated, excessive blood vessel branching. Paradoxically, in some tumor models, the inhibition of Dll4 expression reduces tumor growth [56, 61]. This may be due to the new tumor vessels are functionally abnormal and poorly perfused [62]. For example, in vivo treatment of mouse breast tumors with the Dll4/Notch inhibitor MMGZ01 promotes functional vessel development and suppresses mammary tumor growth [63]. These studies reveal the complex and multifaceted role of the Notch signaling pathway in angiogenesis and tumor progression.

Endothelial notch-mediated EC-TC crosstalk

Beyond its angiogenic function, angiocrine factors can also regulate tumor progression by mediating bidirectional crosstalk between ECs and TCs [6, 7]. For example, in B-cell lymphoma, tumor-derived FGF4 upregulates Jag1 expression in adjacent ECs, which in turn induces Notch2-Hey1 activation in TCs and enhances the aggressive lymphoma phenotype [6]. Similarly, EC-derived JAG1 also leads to the upregulation of Notch1 and zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 1 (Zeb1) in breast cancer stem cells. Subsequently, Zeb1 increases endothelial VEGFA and JAG1 production in a paracrine manner. Depletion of Zeb1 disrupts this positive feedback loop in the tumor perivascular niche, ultimately reducing the aggressiveness of breast cancer [64]. Notably, Notch signaling in ECs also promotes tumor metastasis by regulating cell adhesion and the inflammatory tumor microenvironment. For example, sustained activation of the NICD in TECs induces the expression of VCAM-1, TNF-α and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) [65]. These molecules facilitate neutrophil infiltration, TC adhesion, and metastasis. Treatment with Notch1 or VCAM-1 blocking antibodies inhibits liver and lung metastases in mouse tumors driven by Notch signaling [47, 66]. Therefore, when designing anti-tumor strategies targeting the Notch signaling pathway, it is essential to precisely regulate Notch signaling to normalize tumor blood vessels while avoiding inadvertent activation of its tumor‑promoting function.

Wnt signaling pathway

Canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway

The canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a key driver of tumor-associated angiogenesis. In colorectal cancer (CRC), VEGF expression in ECs positively correlates with cytoplasmic β-catenin accumulation in TCs [67]. It suggests that EC-derived VEGF activates endothelial Wnt signaling. This activation is likely mediated through VEGFR2, which can phosphorylate and stabilize β-catenin, thereby enhancing its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity [68, 69]. RNA interference or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting VEGFR-1 attenuate Wnt/β-catenin signaling and suppress tumor growth [68, 70]. In addition to transcriptional regulation, β-catenin stabilizes vascular integrity by binding to cadherins at cell-cell adhesion junctions (VE-cadherin and N-cadherin) in ECs [71, 72]. For example, the ETS-related gene (ERG) expressed in ECs, enhances the binding of β-catenin to VE-cadherin, thereby reinforcing vascular stability and tumor angiogenesis. ERG deficiency impairs Wnt signaling, a defect rescued by lithium chloride through activation of Wnt signaling and β-catenin stabilization [72]. Notably, enhanced endothelial Wnt/β-catenin signaling inhibits tumor angiogenesis and induces vascular normalization in glioblastoma (GBM), suggesting significant heterogeneity in its function.

Non-canonical Wnt pathways

In addition, the non-canonical Wnt pathway, which is independent of β-catenin, also critically regulates tumor angiogenesis. Evi (also known as Wntless) is an evolutionarily conserved seven-pass transmembrane protein. It plays a crucial role in the process of Wnt protein secretion [73]. Studies have demonstrated that EC-specific Evi-KO mouse models (Evi-ECKO) exhibit significantly increased upregulated expression of the non-canonical ligands Wnt5a and Wnt5b, with no effect on the expression of classical target genes. Evi-ECKO mice exhibited reduced retinal and tumor angiogenesis after birth [74]. Additionally, in GBM, the non-canonical Wnt pathway driven by Wnt5a has also been shown to contribute to tumor progression [75]. For instance, secreted frizzled-related protein 2 (SFRP2) expressed on ECs binds to Fzd receptors on tumor EC surfaces, activating downstream non-canonical Wnt/Ca2+ signaling and promoting angiogenesis. The targeting of SFRP2 counteracts its pro-tumor angiogenic effects [76]. These results suggest that the Wnt signaling pathway regulated by the vascular niche also controls angiogenesis through non-canonical pathways. However, its specific contribution to tumor angiogenesis is not as well defined as that of classical pathways.

Endothelial Wnt-mediated EC-TC crosstalk

Endothelial-derived Wnt ligands (such as Wnt2a and Wnt7b) can activate the β-catenin pathway in TCs via a paracrine mechanism, promoting the tumor proliferation and cancer stem cells (CSC) maintenance. Linke et al. reported that ECs-derived secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (SFRP1) supports the maintenance of cancer stem cells (CSCs) by activating Wnt signaling in TCs [77]. Conversely, Wnt ligands secreted by TCs can also reciprocally regulate ECs. Taking Hodgkin lymphoma as an example, the activation of canonical Wnt signaling promotes the chemotaxis, adhesion, and invasion of lymphoma cells (LCs) toward ECs. Additionally, VEGF-A secreted by LCs, which is transcriptionally regulated by LEF-1/β-catenin, further induces ECs migration and angiogenesis [78]. Notably, Wnt5b simultaneously engages the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway and the non-canonical planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway, leading to upregulation of Snail and Slug, induction of partial endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), and subsequent enhancement of lymphangiogenesis and lymph node metastasis. Pharmacological inhibition using IWR-1-endo (targeting the canonical pathway) and Rac1 inhibitors (targeting the PCP pathway) abolishes these Wnt5b-induced effects. However, research on the EC-TC crosstalk mediated by endothelial Wnt signaling remains relatively insufficient at both the molecular mechanism and functional levels.

The crosstalk of Notch/Wnt signaling pathway

The Notch and Wnt signaling pathways exhibit crosstalk in ECs and cooperatively coordinate angiogenesis. Phng et al. identified Notch-regulated ankyrin repeat protein (Nrarp), which interacts with Lef1 to limit excessive Notch pathway activation and concurrently promotes Wnt/β-catenin signaling [79]. BATgal-reporter experiments confirmed that Wnt signaling activity was indeed enriched in stalk cells. In vitro combined with Wnt3a and Dll4 stimulation further enhanced Wnt reporter activity, which was canceled in the absence of Nrarp. In addition, studies by Corada et al. and Pegaire et al. further confirmed this cross-regulation from the perspective of Wnt receptors [80, 81]. Research found that activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in ECs upregulates Dll4 and Jagged1 expression, thereby regulating angiogenesis. Loss of the core component dishevelled‑1/3 (Dvl1/3) of the Wnt signaling pathway significantly reduces the expression of canonical Wnt target genes and Notch ligands.

Moreover, the Wnt and Notch signaling pathways also mediate crosstalk between ECs and TCs. In human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) organoids, inhibition of Wnt and Notch signaling in ECs suppresses the maintenance of CD24+CD44+ PDAC organoids [82]. To characterize the molecular mechanisms underlying this crosstalk, Alve et al. established a co-culture system between primary human lymphatic ECs (LECs) and a human melanoma cell line [83]. The study have noted that co-culture with melanoma cells induced transcriptional changes in LECs, leading to multiple functional alterations. Specifically, Dll4 derived from LECs activate Notch3 in melanoma cells, which subsequently upregulates Wnt5b transcription. Tumor-derived Wnt5b then acts as a paracrine signal feedback to LECs, promoting lymphatic metastasis [83]. Furthermore, Nasser et al. have pointed out that dual targeting of the Notch and Wnt/β-catenin pathways can produce synergistic effects, improving therapeutic outcomes for patients with triple-negative breast cancer [84]. These findings collectively indicate that the Notch-Wnt signaling network functions both within and between endothelial and TCs, serving as a critical hub connecting vascular biology with tumor progression. However, the complex, bidirectional communication mechanisms among different cell types within the TME remain to be fully elucidated. A deeper understanding of the precise crosstalk will provide a theoretical basis for anti-tumor strategies that simultaneously target both Notch and Wnt signaling.

Metabolic reprogramming mediated by angiocrine factors

The cooperation and antagonism between Notch and Wnt signaling determine the fate of tip and stalk cells during angiogenesis. Recent studies have revealed metabolic heterogeneity between these two cell types: tip cells rely on glycolysis to support migration, whereas stalk cells depend on fatty acids to sustain proliferation. This suggests that Notch and Wnt signaling may differentially regulate metabolic remodeling in ECs. Therefore, this chapter will explore how Notch and Wnt signaling regulate EC metabolism and the impact of EC metabolism on tumor progression.

Metabolic reprogramming in TEC

Tumor ECs support angiogenesis through metabolic reprogramming. There is evidence that ECs regulate their metabolism when they switch from a quiescent state to angiogenic activation, and that metabolism regulates genetic signals that control vascular sprouting (Fig. 2). De Bock and colleagues found that glycolysis generates up to 85% of the total ATP content in ECs, indicating that glycolysis is the primary energy source for ECs [85–87]. Moreover, PFKFB3 is the most highly expressed glycolytic enzyme in ECs, and its upregulation increases glycolytic flux. PFKFB3-driven glycolysis inhibits the pro-stalk activity of Notch, and stimulates vascular sprouting [86]. In contrast, PFKFB3 deficiency impairs the formation of tip cells upon Notch blockade and reduces vascular sprouting.

Under normoxia (left), VEGF signaling activates the Notch pathway, which suppresses VEGFR1 expression and promotes VEGFR2 expression, thereby supporting a proliferative vascular phenotype. In contrast, under hypoxia (right), elevated VEGF signaling upregulates glycolysis driven by PFKFB3 in tip cells to maintain their migratory phenotype. Meanwhile, Notch signaling downregulates VEGFR2 expression, limiting tip cell formation

Cancer is increasingly recognized as a metabolic disease. TME is extremely hypoxic, and this hypoxia upregulates genes associated with glycolysis, including glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK1), and PFKFB3 in tumor ECs, leading to a significantly increased glycolytic rate compared to normal ECs [88–91]. In mouse models, endothelial-specific deletion of PFKFB3 leads to reduced vascular perfusion and slower tumor growth. Taylor et al. revealed that TCs trigger white adipose tissue depletion and cancer cachexia through excessive activation of endothelial Notch1 signaling and enhanced retinoic acid synthesis in distant white adipose tissue (WAT), and this mechanism can be targeted clinically to relieve adipose tissue loss, further validating the impact of endothelial metabolism on tumor progression [92]. Notch signaling not only regulates metabolic heterogeneity within ECs but also directly influences the malignant phenotype of TCs through metabolic reprogramming, forming a key component of the EC-TC metabolic dialogue. For example, in gastric cancer cells, He et al. observed that activation of the Notch pathway promotes the synthesis of key glycolytic enzymes such as LDHA, PFKB3, and PKM2. In breast cancer, it was further confirmed that Notch3 activates mTORC1 through an atypical pathway, stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α, upregulates glycolytic enzymes, suppresses mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and promotes the shift toward the Warburg effect.

Unlike the Notch signaling pathway, few studies have addressed the direct regulatory role of Wnt signaling in endothelial metabolism. Nevertheless, the crosstalk between Notch and Wnt signaling pathways provides crucial insights into their collaborative roles in endothelial metabolism. For instance, Nrarp-mediated Notch and Wnt interactions may coordinate metabolic adaptation in ECs during tip-stalk cell transitions; the balance between Notch and Wnt activities may jointly determine the shift from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation. Shao et al. reported that Wnt signaling modulates both mitochondrial function and glycolytic reprogramming in endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), providing additional evidence that Wnt signaling affects angiogenesis via metabolic reprogramming.

Furthermore, TECs also regulate the metabolic reprogramming of ECs themselves as well as that of adjacent stromal and immune cells through various other pathways. For example, TECs upregulate VEGF expression via a cyclooxygenase-2 (COX2)-dependent mechanism, which in turn increases PFKFB3 expression, promoting glycolysis and tumor growth [93]. ECs can also reprogram metabolic epigenetic regulation of macrophage polarization and suppress inflammatory damage by secreting the angiocrine factor Rspondin3 [94]. Although the understanding of endothelial metabolic reprogramming and its molecular crosstalk has significantly advanced in recent years, research in this field remains limited. The causal relationship between endothelial metabolism and tumor progression is still unclear, and the metabolic heterogeneity across different vascular beds and tumor stages has not been fully elucidated. Moreover, how ECs coordinate intercellular metabolic interaction networks via angiocrine factors remains to be clarified.

Lactate: a key oncometabolite and signaling molecule

The high glycolytic properties of tumors lead to a large accumulation of lactic acid. Lactate binds to the receptor tyrosine kinases Axl, Tie2, and VEGFR2 to activate the PI3K-AKT pathway and promote angiogenesis [95]. It can also promote the activation of prolyl hydroxylase domain protein 2 (PHD2) and ROS-dependent NF-κB and IL8 in ECs through the MCT1, thereby driving tumor angiogenesis [96]. Knockdown of PFKFB3 reduces phosphorylated AKT and intracellular lactate levels, impairing ECs proliferation and migration. These defects can be reversed by exogenous lactate supplementation [97]. Lactate accumulation also impacts immune responses [98, 99]. For instance, lactate induces M2-like macrophage polarization through MCT1 in ECs, promotes VEGF secretion, and generates a positive feedback loop to further stimulate angiogenesis [33].

Therapeutic targeting of endothelial metabolism

Given the importance of endothelial metabolism in pathological vascular formation, targeting endothelial metabolism for pathological vascular therapy has become a new approach. Among them, the PFKFB3 inhibitor 3-(3-pyridinyl)-1-(4-pyridinyl)-2- propen-1-one (3PO) is the most representative drug in this field. However, the in vivo effects of 3PO are significantly dose-dependent. For example, high doses of 3PO treatment suppressed ECs proliferation and promoted ECs death, leading to vascular disruption. While low doses of 3PO did not affect tumor growth. Instead, they reprogrammed the tumor vasculature into a more normal architecture, improving oxygen perfusion and delivery of chemotherapy drugs [92, 100]. 3PO also inhibits vascular sprouting and pathological vascular formation by inhibiting glycolysis [97]. Furthermore, the mechanism of action of 3PO remains controversial, as subsequent studies have demonstrated that its anti-angiogenic effects are not mediated by direct inhibition of PFKFB3. Veseli et al. revealed that these effects cannot be attributed to the binding of 3PO to its target protein PFKFB3, but may instead result from the accumulated lactic acid inside the cells, leading to a decrease in the intracellular pH and an inhibition of enzymatic reactions of the glycolytic pathway. Therefore, future research should focus on developing more selective PFKFB3 inhibitors and thoroughly elucidating the precise mechanisms of this target to overcome the limitations imposed by existing compounds.

Orchestration of malignant phenotypes by angiocrine factors

Above, we describe the metabolic reprogramming characteristics of TECs and their role in the regulation of endothelial metabolism by the Notch/Wnt signaling pathway. Notably, endothelial cells not only influence vascular function through intrinsic metabolic alterations but also directly coordinate various malignant behaviors of TCs—such as the transition from dormancy to activation, invasion and metastasis, maintenance of stemness, and immune evasion by secreting angiocrine factors (e.g., Dll4, Jagged1, and lactate). Moreover, TCs can reciprocally regulate ECs functions, forming a bidirectional crosstalk network.

Control of tumor dormancy and reactivation

The vascular niche dictates the fate of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs), primarily through bidirectional endothelial‑tumor crosstalk [101, 102]. As reported by Indraccolo et al., such crosstalk drives dormant TCs to escape quiescence and triggers tumor outgrowth. Mechanistically, EC‑expressed Dll4 activates Notch3 signaling in TCs to facilitate this process. RNA‑interference‑mediated Notch3 silencing inhibits the TCs proliferation and apoptosis in vitro and vivo [104]. Consistently, additional studies have further validated phenomenon. In spontaneous mouse metastasis models, endothelial derived TSP-1 colocalizes with dormant TCs on pulmonary ECs, where it stabilizes the lung microvascular structure by inhibiting EC motility and proliferation, thereby sustaining prolonged dormancy in breast cancer [31]. Conversely, TCs expressing Kangai-1 protein (KAI1,also known as CD82) can directly interact with Duffy antigen receptor for chemokines (DARC, also known as gp-Fy) on the surface of vascular ECs, suppressing the metastatic activity of prostate cancer cells [103].These findings validate the essential role of EC-TC cell crosstalk in promoting tumor dormancy escape and subsequent outgrowth.

Recent studies suggest that the activation and dormancy regulation of DTCs also depends on their own epigenetic properties. Jakab and colleagues found that endothelium-derived Wnt factors drive dormancy or extravasation associated proliferation of DTCs. Although one might expect changes in Wnt expression, it was found to be unaltered in ECs, and mTC responsiveness to Wnt was established at the methylation level, with low methylation enabling high Wnt activity leading to metastatic latency, while highly methylated mTCs exhibited low activity and proliferated intravascularly [102]. That is, dormancy is determined by the autonomous epigenetic state of TCs, which makes DTCs responsive to ECs derived Wnt signaling [104, 105]. In summary, the dormancy and reactivation of DTCs result from the combined influence of internal and external factors. How the dynamic balance between intrinsic epigenetic characteristics and signals from the tumor microenvironment is coordinated remains a core scientific issue that urgently needs to be resolved.

Promotion of tumor metastasis

Beyond regulating dormancy and reactivation, angiocrine factors are also critically involved in driving the subsequent steps of tumor metastasis [8, 12, 106]. These factors mediate metastatic progression through four principal mechanisms: (1) inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) to enhance tumor invasiveness; (2) remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM); (3) establishing distant pre-metastatic niches; and (4) facilitating TC transendothelial migration. Necroptosis is a programmed cell death mechanism that critically promotes tumor metastasis. Therefore, we focus on whether angiocrine factors influence tumor metastasis by regulating necroptosis, and the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Strilic et al. observed that in vitro TC-EC co-culture systems, ECs preferentially undergo necroptosis rather than apoptosis. Further in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that endothelial necroptosis is mediated by amyloid precursor protein (APP) expressed on TCs and death receptor 6 (DR6) expressed on ECs (Fig. 3). Endothelial specific knockout of DR6 and RIPK3 inhibits EC-induced necroptosis and consequently suppresses tumor extravasation and metastasis. Conversely, endothelial-specific deletion of caspase-8 or TGF-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1), which serves as a negative regulator of necroptosis, promotes extravasation and metastasis [107, 108]. In both human and mouse ECs, TAK1-deficient ECs exhibit elevated RIPK3 expression and demonstrate enhanced susceptibility to necroptosis both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, in hepatocellular carcinoma, upregulated heparanase (HPSE) increases the expression of syndecan-1 (SDC-1) and TNF-α in ECs, inducing necroptosis hepatic microvascular ECs(MVEC), accompanied by extensive MVEC necrosis and a high incidence of intrahepatic metastasis [108]. Further studies demonstrated that TNFα-induced necroptosis is largely dependent on the proteolytic shedding of the TNFR1 which is mediated by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 17 (ADAM17), this process can be triggered by the proteolytic activity of ADAM17 and γ-secretase on TNFR1. Genetic ablation of ADAM17 in ECs or inhibition of its catalytic activity is sufficient to prevent ECs necroptosis, thereby reducing TCs extravasation and metastasis [109].

TC induced EC necroptosis promotes metastasis. Canonical TNF-α-TNFR1 signaling forms Complex I, then Complex II. Active caspase-8 in Complex II induces apoptosis. When caspase-8 is inhibited, RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL assemble into the necrosome, triggering necroptosis. In tumors, EC necroptosis is induced by tumor-derived APP binding to EC DR6 or by HPSE tumors promoting EC release of TNF-α and SDC-1, thereby facilitating tumor metastasis

Notably, endothelial necroptosis not only promotes metastasis through crosstalk with TCs but also engages in bidirectional communication with the immune system, inducing an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, ultimately facilitating tumor immune evasion and chemoresistance [21, 110]. Our recent research revealed that poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) dependent endothelial necroptosis, helps tumors evade immunosurveillance and chemotherapy and promotes progression in ovarian and breast cancers by disrupting anti-tumor interactions between CXCL10⁺ macrophages and CXCR3⁺ CD8⁺ T cells [21]. Therefore, elucidating the complex crosstalk between endothelial necroptosis and tumor progression and immune evasion will provide a theoretical basis for the development of novel anti-tumor targets.

Maintenance of cancer stemness

CSCs are critical contributors to tumor recurrence and cancer patient mortality [111–114]. Notably, CSCs are often enriched in the perivascular niche, where they engage in close interactions with ECs to maintain their tumorigenicity and drug resistance. This section will focuses on the molecular mechanisms underlying their crosstalk and functional significance in tumor progression.

CSCs share genetic information with indolent TCs (non-stem cells) but exhibit a distinct CSC phenotype under the influence of intrinsic and extrinsic factors, such as ECs [115, 116]. Research has shown that specific ECs provide angiocrine factors trigger the propagation and chemoresistance of CSCs. To eavesdrop on the crosstalk between ECs and TCs, Cao et al. developed a serum-free system to co-culture human HUVECs and TCs. They observed that TCs exhibited greater resistance to doxorubicin when co-cultured with ECs than when cultured alone. Further mechanistic studies revealed that insulin-like growth factor-binding protein-7 (IGFBP7) expressed by TECs inhibits insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF1) and downregulates IGF1 receptor (IGF1R) expression, thereby promoting the expansion and engraftment of TCs. However, chemotherapy triggers TECs to suppress IGFBP7 expression, which in turn stimulates the TCs expression of IGF1R and FGF4, initiating an FGFR1-ETS2 paracrine feedforward loop that sharply reduces TECs expression of IGFBP7 and converts naive TCs into chemotherapy-resistant CSCs, promoting their engraftment and progression [7]. By blocking IGFBP7 expression and enhancing IGF1 expression, transforming indolent tumors into chemically resistant and aggressive TSCs. Simultaneously, FGF4 expressed by TCs also upregulates the Jag1 on TECs, and the upregulation of Jag1 activates the Notch pathway in lymphoma cells and CRCs, endowing the tumor with invasive, migratory, and chemotherapy-resistant features [6, 117]. In addition, TECs are involved in regulating the CSC phenotype of GBM, gastric cancer, and skin cancer by secreting bFGF, FGF2, SDF1, and VEGF, thus promoting their progression [118–120]. Therefore, disrupting CSC-EC crosstalk may be an effective strategy to overcome aggressiveness and chemoresistance.

Establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment

Angiocrine factors contribute to shaping the tumor immune microenvironment, (Fig. 4). Extensive in vitro co-culture studies have demonstrated bidirectional interactions between ECs and immune cells [19, 121, 122]. Krejbich et al. indicate that NSCLC-derived TECs modulate immune responses by promoting the polarization of immunosuppressive T cells (Th22, Treg) and M2-like macrophages. Macrophages are spatially close to ECs, and angiocrine signals establish a niche conducive to an immunosuppressive TME. For instance, the transcriptional regulator recombination signal binding protein for immunoglobulin kappa J region (RBPJ) promotes an immunosuppressive environment by mediating the upregulation of SDF1 secretion and enhancing homing cell adhesion molecule (HCAM, also known as CD44) expression on macrophages [123]. In GBM, ECs induce the polarization of macrophages by releasing IL-6. EC-specific knockout of IL-6 inhibits selective activation of macrophages and improves survival [18].

EC-immune crosstalk drives immunosuppression in the TME. ECs secrete angiocrine factors including cytokines (e.g., IL6, IL8, and IL10), chemokines (e.g., SDF1 and CXCL16), growth factors (e.g., VEGFA, PDGF-β, TGF-β), and adhesion molecules (e.g., VCAM1), that directly regulate T cell differentiation and infiltration, as well as macrophage polarization. ECs also indirectly shape T cell responses by modulating macrophage phenotypes. Notably, PARP1 induced EC necroptosis contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment

Furthermore, T cell infiltration is essential for anti-tumor immunity and elimination. Tumors use the complex biological processes of angiogenesis and immune evasion to establish a selective immune barrier, which restricts T cell infiltration, and blocks T cell trafficking and adhesion through angiocrine factors such as VEGF-A and VCAM-1 [124–126]. For example, the death mediator Fas ligand (FasL, also known as CD95L), which is specifically expressed in ECs, can be upregulated through the synergistic effect of VEGF-A, IL-10, and PGE2. This preferentially killing CD8+ effector T cells rather than regulatory T cells (Treg)+ cells, thereby establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment that promotes tolerance in tumors. In addition, the lacking Fas-FasL signaling or treated with anti-FasL antibodies in mice, the number of intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltrates increased and tumor growth was suppressed [125].

Beyond macrophages and T cells, EC-derived CXCL1/2/8 recruit tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), which produce MMP9 and VEGF-A to form a pro-angiogenic feedback loop. Additionally, EC-secreted TGF-β and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) impair antigen presentation by dendritic cells. Meanwhile, tumor-infiltrating immune cells further induce TECs anergy by secreting pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF, MMPs, PDGF and TGF-β), thereby preventing further immune cell infiltration into the TME [127–129]. These studies highlight the complexity of EC-immune interactions and confirm that angiocrine factors are key regulators of tumor immunity. Therefore, deciphering the signaling crosstalk between TECs and tumor immune cells will provide insights into therapeutic targets and strategies.

Targeting angiocrine functions in cancer

Drug resistance

The clinical benefits of anti-angiogenic agents in advanced cancers tend to be temporary [130, 131]. Tumors eventually undergo recurrence and metastasis due to both tumor-intrinsic and acquired resistance. Intrinsic resistance is driven by inherent genetic and epigenetic characteristics of tumors, such as inherent insensitivity to VEGF blockade and constitutive activation of pro-proliferative signaling pathways. In contrast, acquired resistance develops dynamically under sustained anti‑angiogenic stress, manifested as the upregulation of pro-angiogenic factors including HGF, FGF2, and the platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF-D), as well as vascular phenotype adaptation mechanisms [132, 133]. Emerging evidence reveals that the remodel TME, such as an immunosuppressive microenvironment, leads to the resistance. In the following sections, we discuss the mechanisms of tumor-intrinsic, vascular adaption and TME-mediated protection in detail.

Tumor intrinsic resistance

Intrinsic resistance refers to the low sensitivity of the tumor to anti-angiogenic therapy prior to therapeutic exposure [134, 135]. In anti-angiogenic therapy, the pre-activation of intrinsic hypoxia signaling pathways within tumors (e.g., HIF-1α) promotes tumor reliance on non-VEGF-mediated angiogenic pathways (e.g., FGF, PDGF, and ANG-2). Meanwhile, the constitutive activation of proliferative and survival signaling pathways, such as the PI3K-AKT, Wnt/β-catenin and MAPK pathway, drives intrinsic drug resistance. Upstream mutations KRAS, BRAF and PIK3CA or deletion of negative regulators PTEN lead to pathway over activation, allowing TCs to autonomously generate growth signals [136, 137]. Meanwhile, these cells exhibit high baseline expression of multidrug resistance-associated transporters such as ABCB1 and ABCG2, which actively efflux drugs from the cell, reducing effective drug concentrations and directly diminishing pharmacological efficacy [138]. Additionally, their intrinsic DNA damage repair system enables rapid repair of drug-induced genetic lesions, allowing cells to evade chemotherapy-induced damage [139, 140]. During EMT, TCs lose their epithelial characteristics and acquire mesenchymal properties [141, 142]. Notably, EMT-derived mesenchymal cells often express pericytic markers, enabling them to bind to and stabilize blood vessels. Together, these mechanisms confer resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy in TCs.

Vascular adaption

Vasculogenic mimicry (VM) and vessel co-option (VC) are two effective mechanisms mediating resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy. VM refers to the formation of vessel-like structures by TCs independently of ECs involvement, thereby providing an adequate blood supply and nutrients to support tumor growth. VM has been reported in multiple malignancies, including melanoma, GBM, osteosarcoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, and gastric cancer [143–148]. However, some investigators have questioned the existence of non-endothelial-derived vessels, arguing that the lack of pericyte coverage and endothelial lining may preclude their capacity to transport blood. To address these concerns, Hannon et al. performed intravenous injection of fluorescently labeled lectin in VM-positive 4T1 mammary tumor-bearing animals. Their findings demonstrated that these structures are capable of transporting blood and connecting to the host vasculature [149]. VM promotes tumor progression through multiple mechanisms. In hepatocellular carcinoma, the hypoxic microenvironment induces LOXL2 upregulation, which in turn promotes VM formation [146]. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, EphA2-regulated EMT drives VM [150]. Furthermore, non-coding RNAs also play critical roles in VM formation across various malignancies [145, 148].

VC is a non‑angiogenic process in which tumors acquire blood supply by hijacking pre‑existing vessels from the surrounding non‑malignant tissue. Histopathologically, the most distinctive feature of VC is the preservation of the orderly vascular architecture of the normal host tissue within the tumor, whereas typical tumor vasculature is characterized by chaotic and disorganized patterns. VC occurs in multiple cancer types, including hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer, especially lung cancer [4, 151–153]. Clinical evidence indicates that VC is a major cause of failure in anti-angiogenic therapy. In breast cancer models, while sunitinib and pazopanib effectively inhibited primary tumor growth and reduce vascular density, no changes in vascular density or improved survival were observed in lung metastasis models. Furthermore, a case report of a renal cell carcinoma (RCC) patient who progressed during anti-angiogenic therapy showed that resistance was associated with VC in pulmonary metastases. The molecular mechanisms underlying VC in drug resistance involve multiple steps. In various cancers, TCs promote co-option of pre-existing blood vessels by expressing adhesion molecules including β1‑integrin (ITGB1), α6‑integrin (ITGA6) and L1CAM, as well as altering their EMT phenotype [154, 155]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, the Arp2/3 complex, which mediates TCs motility, is essential for VC during liver metastasis [151]; combined inhibition of angiogenesis and VC proves more effective than inhibiting angiogenesis alone [7]. Additionally, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play critical roles in VC. For example, lncRNA SYTL5-OT4 promotes VC and mediates resistance by inhibiting autophagic degradation of alanine serine cysteine transporter 2 (ASCT2) [156].

Because VM and VC do not rely on VEGF-driven classical angiogenesis from the outset, they exhibit inherent insensitivity to VEGF-targeted agents (intrinsic resistance). Moreover, under therapeutic pressure, these non-angiogenic mechanisms can be further reinforced or induced as alternative survival pathways (acquired resistance). These mechanisms are closely associated with treatment outcomes and chemoresistance in many cancer patients, yet the underlying molecular drivers remain poorly understood.

Microenvironment-mediated resistance

The complex interaction of angiocrine factors with the TME plays a key role in anti-angiogenic therapy resistance. Emerging evidence reveals that angiocrine factors remodel the TME, leading to the development of drug resistance through multiple mechanisms, such as enhancing the malignant phenotype of tumors (Sect. Orchestration of Malignant Phenotypes by Angiocrine Factors) and metabolic remodeling (Sect. Metabolic Reprogramming Mediated by Angiocrine Factors). Furthermore, endothelial and tumor derived EVs contribute to malignant proliferation and drug resistance by carrying pro-angiogenic miRNAs, lncRNAs, and proteins (Sect. Extracellular Vesicles). Meanwhile, stromal cells secrete and regulate compensatory factors, thereby modulating vascular remodeling and function. For example, TAMs secrete VEGF, bFGF, EGF, TGF-β, and HGF, thereby activating alternative angiogenic pathways. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) similarly promote angiogenesis by secreting FGF2, HGF, and CXCR4. For example, CXCR4⁺ CAFs are recruited to the tumor site via the CXCR4-CXCL12 axis and release FGF2, enhancing microvascular proliferation and promoting resistance to bevacizumab. Consequently, angiocrine factors form a complex resistance network that poses a significant challenge to the anti-angiogenic therapy strategy.

Therapeutic strategies

Combination strategies

Based on these resistance mechanisms, anti-angiogenic treatment strategies are evolving from a single pathway to multidimensional interventions. Numerous preclinical and clinical studies have confirmed the significant advantages of combining anti-angiogenic drugs with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in treating various malignant tumors, including HCC, ovarian cancer and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [157–161]. Anti-angiogenic therapy enhances ICI perfusion and efficacy in tumor tissues via vascular normalization, and studies have shown that combination therapy exerts synergistic effects by reprogramming the TME into an immunosupportive niche. After combined treatment with anti-PD-L1 and VEGFR2 inhibitors, the levels of IFN-γ+ CD8+ and IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cell increased in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors and mammary carcinomas [162, 163]. Combined VEGFR and CTLA-4 blockade enhances the antigen-presenting function of intratumoral DCs and reduces the recruitment of immunosuppressive MDSCs [164, 165]. A global, multicenter phase III clinical trial (IMbrave150) has demonstrated that, in the first-line treatment of unresectable or advanced HCC, atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) combined with bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) significantly prolongs overall survival (OS) compared with sorafenib, with a hazard ratio (HR) that is both statistically and clinically significant [166, 167].

Clinical limitations

Although the combination of anti-angiogenic agents with immune checkpoint inhibitors or other targeted therapies holds great promise, significant limitations remain, including additive or synergistic toxicities, a lack of validated predictive biomarkers to guide patient selection and drug resistance [11, 168, 169]. Adding anti-angiogenic agents to immunotherapy substantially increases the risk of adverse events. A meta-analysis of 1,735 patients showed that the overall rate of treatment related adverse events (TRAEs) was significantly higher in the combination therapy group than in the mono-therapy group. The most notable TRAEs included rash, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and diarrhea [170]. These data indicate that while combination therapy shows potential, effective toxicity management poses a greater challenge for clinical implementation.

Second, not all patients benefit from this combined strategy, and identifying suitable patient populations remains a key bottleneck for clinical translation. For example, in NSCLC with KRAS mutations, immunotherapy combined with anti-angiogenic therapy has enabled some patients to achieve a progression-free survival of up to 27 months [171]. Unfortunately, in a randomized phase II clinical trial with recurrent GBM, the combination of pembrolizumab and bevacizumab failed to improve outcomes compared to pembrolizumab alone [172]. In four patients who benefited from combination immunotherapy, baseline tumors showed lower CD68⁺ and CD163⁺ cells infiltration and lower PD-L1 CPS than post-progression lesions [173]. Furthermore, the hypoxic microenvironment induced by anti-vascular inhibitors activates the upregulation of alternative angiogenic pathways and the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, which often negates the benefits of initial therapy [173–175]. Taken together, these findings underscore the need to consider the dynamic evolution of baseline immune infiltration patterns and resistance mechanisms across different tumors/individuals to guide personalized treatment strategies.

Multidimensional therapy strategies

The complexity and redundancy of the angiocrine factor network underlie the limitations of current anti-angiogenic and combination therapies. Importantly, these factors exhibit a “one-cause, multiple-effects” characteristic, which provides a theoretical rationale for developing a “one-target, multi-effect” therapeutic strategy. For instance, the use of PFKFB3 inhibitors 3 PO not only suppresses pathological angiogenesis but also increases CD3⁺ T cell infiltration, demonstrating the “multi-effect” therapeutic potential, such as 3PO. Similarly, targeting R-spondin 3 enables synchronous regulation of Wnt signaling, vascular stability, and immune cell recruitment. Moreover, in view of the redundancy and cross-compensation characteristics of angiocrine factor networks, multi-target therapy has emerged. Related studies on hematologic malignancy further support this principle: response to CD70-directed CAR-T cell therapy in AML was strongly dependent on antigen density, and epigenetic priming with decitabine or chidamide enhanced target expression and therapeutic efficacy, underscoring the importance of biomarker-guided patient selection and rational combination strategies [176–178]. Conclusion and prospects

Despite significant advances in this field, the expression of angiocrine factors exhibits significant heterogeneity across different stages of tumor development, spatial regions within tumors, and distinct cellular subtypes [2, 12, 179–181]. The expression of angiocrine factors exhibits significant heterogeneity across different stages of tumor development, spatial regions within tumors, and distinct cellular subtypes [182–184]. For example, in CRC, tissue expression and serum levels of VEGF and bFGF vary by tumor subtype and are associated with different prognoses. High tissue expression and serum levels of VEGF are associated with improved prognosis in the metabolic subtype but with poorer prognosis in the immune subtype [185]. Similarly, VEGF expression is heterogeneous in different tumors and responds differently to anti-vascular therapy [186]. This implies that the same therapeutic target may produce entirely different pharmacological effects depending on tumor type or subtype, placing higher demands on personalized and precise drug design.

Furthermore, the crosstalk between angiocrine signaling and stromal components in the TME is not fully understood. Although several studies have demonstrated the interactions between ECs and immune cells as well as TCs, the relationships between ECs and other stromal cells, such as pericytes, CAFs, and adipocytes are less well defined. The interaction between them also dynamically evolves with tumor progression and treatment pressure, which constitutes an important basis for treatment resistance. These interactions dynamically evolve during tumor progression and under therapeutic pressure, forming a critical basis for treatment resistance and limited efficacy. However, traditional research methods struggle to unravel the complexity and spatial dynamics of this crosstalk, hindering the identification of key therapeutic targets.

In recent years, spatial transcriptomics, single-cell multiomics, and lineage tracing technologies have made unprecedented progress. Using these techniques, recent studies have revealed significant heterogeneity in the spatial structure and function of tumor ECs. Tip cells, stalk cells, and phalanx cells play different roles in vascular germination, lumen formation, and vascular maintenance, and TECs in different tumor regions exhibit unique metabolic profiles: Tip cells primarily rely on glycolysis to drive vascular sprouting, stalk cells support proliferation and lumen formation through oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, whereas phalanx cells depend on Notch signaling [187, 188]. Li et al. resolved typical tip TECs as known migratory tips and a hypothetical basement membrane remodeling breach phenotype, and tip/breach TECs were most sensitive to vascular endothelial growth factor blockade [189]. However, the molecular mechanism of by which TEC heterogeneity precisely regulates the spatiotemporal release of angiocrine factors still needs to be explored in depth. Kang et al. combined microengineering angiogenesis models with mathematical modeling and reported that TECs differentiate into tip cells and stalk cells under VEGF induction, forming a regular spatial distribution pattern. However, under high VEGF stimulation, the differentiation state of some front-end cells showed temporal instability and could dynamically switch between different phenotypes [190]. Together, these evidences suggest that the “spatiotemporal code” of angiocrine factors is determined by the molecular identity, metabolic state and tissue background of TECs. Therefore, accurate interpretation of the heterogeneity of vascular ECs and their angiocrine factors, including their spatial distribution characteristics, lineage differentiation trajectories, and functional adaptations in the context of TME, is a key prerequisite for the development of novel targeted drugs.

These approaches ultimately elucidate the key regulatory targets and mechanisms of angiocrine factors. Targeting these targets has the potential to overcome the limitations of current anti-angiogenic therapies and improve treatment outcomes for cancer patients. Furthermore, given the considerable heterogeneity and spatiotemporal specificity of TECs, establishing biomarker-guided stratification systems for different cancer types and individual patients, identifying effective targets, and developing personalized, multidimensional therapeutic strategies are crucial for improving clinical efficacy.
