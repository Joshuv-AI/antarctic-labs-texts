Resurgence of multistate cholera in Sudan amidst ongoing conflict
Authors: Ayman Azhary, Nooh Mohamed Hajhamed, Rana Mohamed, Eman Taha Osman Ali, Mohammed Elfatih Hamida, Claude Mambo Muvunyi, Emmanuel Edwar Siddig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Cholera, a waterborne illness caused by Vibrio cholerae, remains a significant public health threat in developing countries due to poor access to drinking water, sanitation, hygiene, armed conflict, and climate change. Globally, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates there are approximately 4 million cases and 143,000 deaths annually [1]. Since April 15, 2023, Sudan has faced a severe humanitarian crisis due to armed conflict, displacing over 10 million people and leaving over half of the population food-insecure [2]. Such a humanitarian emergency is associated with poor water quality and inadequate sanitation, facilitating the re-emergence and rapid transmission of infectious diseases such as cholera and measles [3].

Recently, two separate studies investigated the cholera outbreak in Al Qadarif state and Kosti city in Sudan. While these focused studies in Al Qadarif state and Kosti city have identified key local drivers of transmission, including the critical role of water supply disruptions, overcrowded displaced populations, limited access to health care, and low vaccination coverage in driving cholera outbreaks [4,5], their scope is inherently limited. Consequently, there remains a paucity of comprehensive data describing the national-scale dynamics, spatial distribution, and temporal trends of cholera amid ongoing conflict. Understanding these patterns is essential for designing targeted interventions, optimizing resource allocation, and ultimately reducing cholera morbidity and mortality in this fragile landscape.

Therefore, this study aims to provide critical insights into the multistate cholera outbreaks in Sudan from August 2023 to June 2025, providing critical insights into disease patterns amid conflict, assessing vaccination coverage, and informing future public health strategies.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed cholera surveillance data collected at the national level in Sudan from August 25, 2023, to June 4, 2025. The data were sourced from the National Surveillance Interactive Dashboard maintained by the General Directorate of Health Emergencies and Epidemics Control, Federal Ministry of Health, Sudan [6]. The dataset included reports of suspected and confirmed cholera cases (absolute numbers), fatalities, vaccination coverage, and demographic details, including data on internally displaced persons (IDPs) and refugee populations. The key variables extracted included the number of suspected and laboratory-confirmed cases, number of fatalities, vaccination coverage rates per state, and case fatality rates (CFRs). CFR was calculated as the percentage of deaths among confirmed cases. Additionally, CFRs among IDPs and refugee populations were specifically analyzed to assess differential vulnerabilities. Crude and state-specific CFRs were derived by dividing the number of fatalities by the total cases reported in each category and multiplying by 100. Temporal trends and spatial distributions of cases and CFRs were examined to identify outbreak patterns across the different states.

A suspected cholera case was defined as any individual presenting with acute watery diarrhea, with or without dehydration, in a context and timeframe consistent with ongoing cholera transmission, or in accordance with WHO guidelines. Confirmed cases were laboratory-confirmed via stool culture or rapid diagnostic tests identifying Vibrio cholerae.

The involvement of populations in cholera transmission was characterized within different demographic groups, including IDPs—individuals displaced within their own country due to conflict or disaster—and refugees, defined as individuals who have crossed international borders seeking safety. These vulnerable groups are often at higher risk due to overcrowding, limited access to clean water, and inadequate sanitation facilities.

Data analysis was conducted using R software, version 4.2.2. Descriptive statistics, graphical representations, and trend analyses were performed to elucidate outbreak dynamics over the study period.

All the data used was anonymized secondary data provided by the Surveillance Directorate dashboard, ensuring confidentiality and adherence to ethical standards. As this study did not involve primary data collection or direct patient interaction, ethical approval was obtained in accordance with guidelines for retrospective secondary data analysis.

Results

This study highlights the resurgence of multistate cholera in Sudan from 25 August 2023 to 14 June 2025 (Figure 1a). Between August and December 2023, 9581 cases and 254 fatalities were reported across 10 states, with the Red Sea state recording the highest number of cases (3379, with 101 deaths and a CFR of 3%). In 2024, there was an alarming increase in cases and fatalities (50,832 cases and 1380 deaths, with a CFR of 2.7%). Al Jazirah state reported the highest number of cases (11,582), followed by Al Qadarif state (10,339), while the Nile state reported the highest number of deaths (315 deaths and a CFR of 4.2%). In the subsequent period from January to June 2025, reported cases decreased to 23,488, approximately half the number reported in all of 2024, with 517 fatalities (CFR of 1.7%). Khartoum state reported the highest number of cases and fatalities (14,084 cases and 243 deaths). Overall, Khartoum, White Nile, and the North Kordofan states experienced an increasing trend between August 2023 and June 2025, while the Red Sea, Al Jazirah, Al Qadarif, and Kassala states showed a decreasing trend (Table 1, Figure 1b).

Discussion

Sudan is experiencing the re-emergence of multiple vaccine-preventable diseases amidst ongoing armed conflict, including measles, rabies, and prominently cholera [4,7,8]. The resurgence of cholera in Sudan during the period from August 2023 to June 2025 highlights the profound public health impact of ongoing conflict and related disruptions to water, sanitation, and healthcare services. The elevated number of cases in 2024, totaling over 50,000, underscores a significant escalation compared to previous years, reflecting worsening environmental and socio-political conditions conducive to cholera transmission. The geographical distribution of cases reveals both spatial heterogeneity and temporal variation. Regions such as Al Jazirah and Al Qadarif bore the brunt of the outbreaks, with these areas experiencing persistent high transmission rates. These patterns are likely driven by water supply disruptions, inadequate sanitation, and large-scale displacement of populations, which are typical in conflict settings. The high CFR in Nile State (4.2%) and other regions signifies challenges in timely case detection and access to quality medical care, compounded by security constraints that hinder health service delivery.

An intriguing observation from the data is the divergent trends among states. While Khartoum, White Nile, and North Kordofan demonstrated increasing case trends due to intense recent conflict with consequent disruption of water supply and a lag in intervention deployment, states such as Red Sea, Al Jazirah, Al Qadarif, and Kassala showed declining cases, possibly attributed to intensified control measures, vaccination campaigns, or a natural decline in susceptible individuals following high transmission in earlier phases. However, whether these decreases are sustainable remains uncertain, especially in volatile contexts where sanitation infrastructure can rapidly deteriorate.

While an increase in vaccination coverage was observed, particularly in 2025, significant gaps persist. These gaps are most acute among geographically and socially marginalized populations, such as IDPs and refugees, who face systemic barriers to healthcare access, sanitation, and consistent vaccination, which contribute to persistent transmission and elevated CFRs. The stability of the overall CFR at approximately 2.7%—though high by international standards—suggests that basic case management protocols were maintained in some areas despite the turmoil, although disparities between regions may exist.

This study underscores the importance of adaptive, context-specific interventions, including rapid deployment of cholera vaccines, enhancement of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, and bolstered disease surveillance systems. Addressing underlying social determinants—conflict resolution, displacement management, and health system resilience—is essential to achieve long-term control. Furthermore, collaboration among national and international stakeholders is vital to mitigate the impact of future outbreaks in conflict-afflicted zones.

While this retrospective analysis provides valuable insights, limitations such as potential underreporting, especially from conflict-inaccessible areas, may lead to an underestimation of cases and deaths. Furthermore, inconsistencies in data completeness across states and over time could affect the accuracy of the observed trends. Finally, presenting incidence rates would provide a more accurate assessment of the disease burden and facilitate better comparison between regions. Due to limitations in the available data, we initially reported absolute case numbers. Future research should focus on detailed risk factor analysis, vaccination coverage assessment at micro-geographies, and intervention efficacy evaluation to inform more robust disease control strategies. Overall, the persistent cholera threat in Sudan exemplifies the urgent need for integrated public health responses aligned with broader peace and stability initiatives.

Conclusion

The ongoing cholera resurgence in Sudan amidst complex conflict highlights the urgent need for comprehensive, context-specific public health strategies. The evolving epidemiological patterns reflect the profound influence of conflict-related disruptions, displacements, and infrastructural deterioration on disease transmission dynamics. To effectively control and prevent future outbreaks, it is imperative to sustain and scale up vaccination efforts, reinforce water and sanitation infrastructure, and improve health system resilience, especially in the most vulnerable and conflict-affected areas. Achieving these goals requires coordinated efforts among national authorities, international partners, and local communities, coupled with conflict mitigation and peace-building to address the root causes of health vulnerabilities. Such an integrated approach is essential for reducing cholera morbidity and mortality, and building a resilient health system capable of withstanding future emergencies.
