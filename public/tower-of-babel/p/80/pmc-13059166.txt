Subclassification of atypical endometrial cells (Not Otherwise Specified vs favor neoplasia) on Pap tests: age-dependent risk of endometrial neoplasia
Authors: Xin Zhou, Yi Zhou, Suming Huang, Yanqiu Wang, Qiuqin Zou, Xuxi Yang, Wanrun Lin, Feng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtypical endometrial cells (AEMc) detected in cervical cytology are uncommon yet significantly associated with an elevated risk of precancerous and malignant lesions. Nevertheless, limited data are available on the histopathological correlation of AEMc cases. This study aims to evaluate the immediate histological outcomes in women diagnosed with AEMc and to assess how HPV status and patient age influence oncogenic potential.MethodA retrospective analysis was conducted on 163 women diagnosed with AEMc at the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital between January 2019 and December 2023. Each patient underwent liquid-based cytology (LBC) and high-risk HPV (hrHPV) testing for 14 genotypes, followed by histological evaluation within six months after the cytological assessment.ResultAmong the 163 AEMc patients, 86.5% (141/163) were classified as AEMc-not otherwise specified (NOS) and 13.5% (22/163) as AEMc-favor neoplasia (FN). Histological analysis indicated that 2.5% (4/163) had precancerous lesions and 12.9% (21/163) had cancer. Immediate pathological severity was significantly higher in AEMc-FN than in AEMc-NOS (p = 0.015), with endometrial carcinoma more prevalent in AEMc-FN (27.3% vs. 9.9%, p = 0.037; odds ratio 3.30, 95% CI: 1.11–9.79). The diagnostic accuracy of AEMc-FN for endometrial carcinoma was 81.6% (95% CI: 74.8%–87.3%). hrHPV status did not reliably predict cancer risk stratification in AEMc, however, age was significantly associated with disease severity. Compared with younger patients, those aged > 65 years showed a markedly higher prevalence of high-grade endometrial glandular abnormalities (AEH +) and adenocarcinoma (AC) (p = 0.001 and p = 0.000401, respectively). With an age cutoff of 45 years, older AEMc patients had an increased prevalence of AEH + and AC, a trend also evident in the AEMc-NOS subgroup.ConclusionWomen aged ≥ 45 years, especially those classified as AEMc-NOS, exhibit a substantially higher risk of high-grade endometrial glandular lesions, underscoring the need for more vigilant follow-up. Given the significantly higher prevalence of endometrial carcinoma in AEMc-FN patients compared to AEMc-NOS patients, it may be advisable for cytopathologists to annotate the presence or absence of “favor neoplasia” when diagnosing AEMc. Refining the diagnostic criteria for AEMc-FN may facilitate earlier detection of endometrial cancer.Keywords: Atypical endometrial cells, High-risk HPV, Age, Cervical cytology, Risk assessment

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma is one of the most common malignancies tumor of the female reproductive tract and predominantly affects postmenopausal women [1]. Nevertheless, 2%–14% of cases occur in women of reproductive age, with an incidence of up to 7.8% among those younger than 40 years [2, 3]. Meanwhile, large-scale screening for cervical cancer has substantially reduced its incidence [4]. In contrast, screening strategies for endometrial carcinoma remain limited, and research progress has been relatively slow. Only a few regions (e.g., Japan) have adopted screening methods similar to those used in cervical cancer screening [5]. The utility of endometrial cytology has been constrained by interpretative challenges, such as blood contamination, cell overlap, poor preservation, and clustered cell arrangements. These limitations have prevented the establishment of a widely recognized international reporting system or standardized format for endometrial cytology, impeding its broader clinical application.

In the 2014 Bethesda System (TBS) for cervical cytology, glandular cell abnormalities are classified according to their malignant potential: atypical glandular cells not otherwise specified (AGC-NOS) and atypical glandular cells favor neoplasia (AGC-FN) [6]. AGC-NOS is further subclassified by presumed cell origin (endocervical, endometrial, or glandular). Conversely, AGC-FN includes “endocervical cells favor neoplastic” and “glandular cells favor neoplastic,” but lacks endometrial cells favor neoplastic category due to concerns about diagnostic reproducibility; the TBS does, however, allow additional descriptive comments when warranted. Alternative classification systems have been proposed to improve diagnostic precision [7–9], with some reporting better accuracy in clinical management [10, 11]. For instance, Karakitsos et al. devised a Bethesda-like reporting system for endometrial cytology, subdividing atypical endometrial cells (AEC) into AEC-U (undetermined significance), AEC-L (low malignant potential), and AEC-H (high malignant potential) [11]. Similarly, Yanoh et al. introduced a descriptive system for atypical endometrial cells (ATEC), further classifying them into ATEC-US (undetermined significance) and ATEC-A (the possibility of atypical hyperplasia or higher-grade lesions) [8]. Building upon the aforementioned classification frameworks, the present study further refined the cytological interpretation of AEMc in our own specimens by introducing the diagnostic category “Atypical Endometrial Cells, favor neoplastic” (AEMc-FN). This subcategory was proposed to represent endometrial cytological abnormalities of greater severity than those seen in AEMc-NOS, with the aim of more accurately identifying high-grade glandular lesions of endometrial origin. Such stratification is intended to enhance the diagnostic utility of cytology and provide more clinically relevant guidance for patient management.

Since AEMc are detected in fewer than 1% of cervical cytology samples yet carry a strong correlation with high-grade lesions, clinical management is challenging given the limited histological follow-up data from LBC specimens. Identifying high-risk subgroups among AEMc patients is critical for improved follow-up strategies and early intervention. This study systematically evaluates the immediate risk of precancerous and malignant lesions in AEMc patients based on hrHPV testing and patient age, aiming to clarify the roles of HPV status and age stratification in guiding the management of AEMc.

Materials and methods

Samples collection

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of the International Peace Maternity & Child Health Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, (GKLW-A-2024-088-01). A retrospective analysis was conducted on 1,060 patients diagnosed with glandular cell abnormalities who underwent cervical cytology screening between January 2019 and December 2023 at the hospital. As demonstrated in Fig. 1, a total of 163 patients met the inclusion criteria: a diagnosis of AEMc according to the ASCCP guidelines, completion of hrHPV testing, and availability of comprehensive histological findings and age data.

Flowchart depicting the selection criteria for study participants. Between January 2019 and December 2023, a total of 1,060 women were identified with glandular cell abnormalities on Pap smear screening in our pathology database. Among them, 284 cases were diagnosed with AEMc, while 776 cases with other types of glandular abnormalities were excluded. Of the 284 AEMc cases, 121 women lacking histopathological follow-up and/or high-risk HPV (hr-HPV) test results were further excluded. The final cohort included 163 women with both histopathological and hr-HPV data, comprising 141 cases of AEMc-NOS and 22 cases of AEMc-FN. Liquid-based cytology and hrHPV mRNA testing

All cervical cytology examinations were performed using the SurePath liquid-based Pap test (BD TriPath, Burlington, NC) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytology slides were automatically prepared using the BD PrepStain slide processor (Becton, Dickinson and Company). The residual liquid-based cytology specimens were subsequently processed for AHPV testing (Hologic, Inc., San Diego, CA), also following the manufacturer’s guidelines.All cytological interpretations were independently reviewed in a double-blind manner by experienced cytopathologists, who rendered diagnoses of atypical endometrial cells based on the following established diagnostic criteria. The diagnosis of AEMc-NOS was determined based on the following morphological criteria: (1) mild nuclear enlargement with slightly increased chromatin density and small nucleoli; (2) uneven chromatin distribution with scant cytoplasm, occasionally containing vacuoles with neutrophils; and (3) irregular cell borders. In contrast, AEMc-FN was defined by: (1) tightly packed three-dimensional clusters or large sheets with irregular borders; (2) loss of polarity and a markedly increased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio; (3) a higher frequency of atypical nuclei with irregular nuclear contours and coarse chromatin distribution; and (4) prominent nucleoli. Representative cytological features of AEMc-NOS and AEMc-FN, along with their corresponding histological follow-up outcomes, are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Simultaneously, hrHPV testing was performed for 14 hrHPV genotypes (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68). HPV-positive samples were categorized into four groups: HPV-16, HPV-18, HPV-16/18 co-infection, and other 12 hrHPV types.

Follow-up histopathological diagnoses

Histological specimens were obtained via colposcopy-guided endocervical curettage, cervical biopsy, endometrial curettage, loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP), cold knife conization, or hysterectomy. All histopathological evaluations were independently reviewed in a double-blind manner by two pathologists (Z.X. and Z.F.) from the hospital’s pathology department.

All included patients underwent histopathological evaluation within six months following their Pap test and hrHPV testing. Based on malignant potential, histological diagnoses were classified into four categories: (1) Negative lesions, including no pathological abnormalities, benign/reactive changes, and self-limiting or treatable conditions such as low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), endometrial hyperplasia, and endometrial polyps; (2) High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL +), defined as HSIL or squamous cell carcinoma (SCC); (3) High-grade cervical glandular abnormalities (AIS +), encompassing adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) and invasive endocervical adenocarcinoma (ECA); (4) High-grade endometrial glandular abnormalities (AEH +), referring to atypical endometrial hyperplasia (AEH) and endometrial carcinoma (EMC). The diagnosis of HSIL was confirmed using immunohistochemical staining for p16 and Ki-67. In cases with multiple histological samples, the highest-grade lesion was recorded as the final diagnosis. Any discrepancies in pathological interpretation were resolved through consensus by a panel of experienced pathologists. For analytical purposes, premalignant lesions were defined as HSIL, AIS, and AEH.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were examined using Pearson’s chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Continuous variables with non-normal distributions were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Univariable logistic regression was first performed to screen potential risk factors (p < 0.05). Variables meeting this threshold were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors while adjusting for confounding factors. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to evaluate the discriminatory performance of the final model. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0, IBM) and MedCalc (version 20.0). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of AEMc patients

A total of 163 patients with cytologically diagnosed AEMc and available immediate histological outcomes were included. Of these, 86.5% (141/163) were classified as AEMc-NOS, and 13.5% (22/163) were classified as AEMc-FN. As summarized in Table 1, the mean age of the entire cohort was 47.91 ± 10.38 years. Although AEMc-FN patients were older on average than AEMc-NOS patients, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.180). The overall hrHPV infection rate was 11.7%, with no significant difference between the AEMc-FN and AEMc-NOS groups.

Among all AEMc patients, the majority (84%) ultimately had benign follow-up histology 3.1% (5/163) were ultimately diagnosed with premalignant lesions, and 12.9% (21/163) had malignancy. Notably, AEMc-FN patients exhibited a higher degree of histological severity on immediate biopsy compared with AEMc-NOS patients (p = 0.015) (Table 1). Specifically, as shown in Fig. 3, among AEMc-NOS patients, 4 cases had precancerous lesions (1 HSIL and 3 AIS), and 14 had endometrial cancer (14 EMC). Logistic regression analysis

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified age and atypical endometrial cells as variables significantly associated with endometrial carcinoma (P < 0.05). As shown in Table 6, age had a significant effect on the occurrence of endometrial carcinoma, with each additional year increasing the odds by 12% (P < 0.0001). The presence of atypical endometrial cells was also positively associated with endometrial carcinoma; specifically, individuals with AEMc-FN had a 4.233-fold higher likelihood of developing endometrial carcinoma compared to those with AEMc-NOS (P = 0.0073) (Table 6).

Discussion

Cervical cytology remains a cornerstone of screening for cervical cancer. Although the detection rate of atypical glandular cells (AGC) is generally low (< 1%) [12], the 2014 TBS nonetheless advises specifying the likely cellular origin (endocervical or endometrial) [6]. Notably, atypical glandular cells detected in cervical smears are not always derived from the endocervical or endometrial epithelium. Case reports have described AGC findings in cervical cytology associated with ovarian, vaginal, colorectal, and even breast carcinomas [13, 14]. These observations suggest that, in addition to focusing on cervical glandular lesions, attention should also be given to non-cervical glandular pathologies presenting with AGC. Given the anatomical continuity among the endometrium, cervix, and vagina, cervical cytology can detect exfoliated cells from upper genital tract tumors, including endometrial carcinoma. In some instances, cervical cytology in patients with endometrial cancer reveals glandular abnormalities such as endometrial adenocarcinoma cells, AGC, or endometrial cells in women aged 45 years or older. A systematic review showed that approximately 45% of women with endometrial carcinoma presented with abnormal findings on cervical cytology [13], underscoring how exfoliated malignant endometrial cells may appear in Pap specimens. This highlights the importance of studying AEMc detected on Pap tests to advance early detection of premalignant and malignant endometrial lesions. Unlike most studies that address AGC in general, the present study focuses specifically on women with AEMc diagnoses.

Our data on AEMc follow-up outcomes are aligned with prior findings from both LBC and conventional Pap smear investigations [15–18]. The mean age of AEMc patients in this cohort (47.91 years) is similar to the 49.7 years reported by Chen et al. [19]. Additionally, the hrHPV infection rate among AEMc patients in our study was 11.7%, comparable to the 16.7% reported by Kim et al. [20]. Though AEMc comprised a small fraction of all cytological diagnoses and the majority of patients ultimately exhibited benign histology, a non-negligible subset nonetheless harbored clinically significant endometrial lesions. This highlights that AEMc, despite its overall low incidence and predominance of benign outcomes, cannot be considered a low-risk finding and warrants vigilant clinical follow-up. Several clinically relevant points arise from our findings.

First, the prevalence of high-grade endometrial glandular lesions in patients aged ≥ 45 years was significantly higher compared with younger patients. Notably, this finding has not been broadly documented in the cytology literature. Previous studies have primarily recommended heightened surveillance for endometrial pathology in AGC-EM patients aged ≥ 50 years [17, 21]. However, our results suggest that the threshold for concern should be lowered to 45 years. Consequently, clinicians should maintain greater vigilance when encountering AEMc on Pap tests in women aged 45 or older. An endometrial biopsy or curettage is warranted to exclude premalignant or malignant conditions.

Second, endometrial carcinoma was markedly more common in patients classified as AEMc-FN than in those with AEMc-NOS, pointing to a higher malignant potential in the former subgroup. Notably, a study investigating the clinicopathological significance of AGC also adopted a subclassification similar to the present study, distinguishing between AEM and AEM-FN. Their findings demonstrated the prevalence of endometrial adenocarcinoma was markedly elevated in the AEM-FN group (78.6%) compared to the AEM group (17.6%), further supporting the notion that AEMc-FN is strongly associated with malignant pathology [20]. We propose that the characteristic cytologic alterations observed in AEMc-FN represent cytomorphologic features indicative of underlying endometrioid adenocarcinoma rather than incidental atypia. These changes closely mirror the canonical histopathological hallmarks of endometrial carcinoma. Accordingly, the cytopathologic pattern of AEMc-FN may serve as a morphological surrogate for early neoplastic transformation within the endometrium, providing valuable diagnostic clues even in the absence of histologic confirmation. Retrospective studies have reported wide variability in the sensitivity of cervical liquid-based cytology for detecting endometrial carcinoma (ECa), with estimates ranging from 31.9% to 89.6% [22–24]. Higher rates of cytologic abnormalities have been observed in non-endometrioid and high-grade ECa, potentially due to their greater propensity for cellular exfoliation [22, 25]. While the diagnostic criteria for AEMc-FN used in our study exhibited high specificity and moderate overall accuracy, sensitivity remained suboptimal. This may partly reflect limited experience and diagnostic familiarity with AEMc-FN among screening cytologists, underscoring the need for more standardized interpretive criteria. Recent studies have begun to explore morphometric analysis of endometrial cell nuclei in liquid-based cervical cytology specimens to differentiate various categories of endometrial cells, suggesting that quantitative nuclear features may further enhance diagnostic precision [26]. Further investigations with larger, well-characterized cohorts are warranted to refine the cytopathological and clinical definitions of AEMc-FN and to establish more standardized diagnostic guidelines that can better inform clinical management and risk stratification.

Finally, based on univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, both age and AEMc subtype were independently associated with the diagnostic accuracy for endometrial carcinoma. A predictive logistic regression model incorporating age and AEMc subtype was subsequently developed, demonstrating robust discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.873), superior to models using either factor alone. This model provides a clinically practical approach for individualized risk assessment in women presenting with AEMc cytology. Furthermore,although all AEMc patients in this study underwent hrHPV testing, categorizing cancer risk based on hrHPV infection status alone was not possible. This aligns with Zhao et al., who found that hrHPV testing after an AGC result offered no clinical utility in detecting endometrial or ovarian carcinoma in women ≥ 50 years [27]. Although hrHPV co-testing can improve the detection of high-grade cervical lesions in AGC-endocervical (AGC-EC) or AGC patients [28], it provides no diagnostic value for endometrial atypical hyperplasia or adenocarcinoma, further supporting that endometrial carcinogenesis proceeds through HPV-independent mechanisms.

Endometrial cancer represents a significant public health concern for women worldwide and the development of novel screening approaches remains crucial. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have provided new opportunities for improving screening technologies. Notably, Li et al. developed a deep convolutional neural network (CNN)-based AI diagnostic system capable of automatically identifying and diagnosing endometrial cytopathological images, achieving an accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity exceeding 90.0%, thus demonstrating substantial clinical potential [29]. In addition, DNA methylation-based detection has garnered increasing attention in endometrial cancer screening, as aberrant DNA methylation is closely associated with cancer progression. Specific DNA methylation patterns have been proposed as potential biomarkers for early endometrial carcinoma. Hypermethylation of CDO1, ZNF454, and CELF4 in cervical exfoliated cells has been reported to correlate with disease occurrence [30, 31]. In contrast, Zhao et al. observed the opposite, likely due to their cohort being limited to premenopausal women with abnormal uterine bleeding [32]. Overall, evidence indicates that combined analysis of multiple methylated genes substantially improves detection sensitivity and specificity. Notably, the integration of LBC with methylation analysis not only enhances sample quality but also enables the complementary use of molecular testing and morphological assessment, thereby improving overall screening efficiency. Moreover, the incorporation of DNA methylation profiles with clinical parameters, such as age and body mass index, has shown promise in optimizing risk stratification for endometrial cancer [33]. Nonetheless, the clinical application of these emerging technologies requires further optimization and validation to ensure their diagnostic accuracy and reliability.

Our study has several limitations. First, it is based on a retrospective analysis from a single center, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. Second, due to the relatively small number of cases, the sample size of each subgroup was limited, which may affect the stability and statistical power of the results. Third, the variables included in this study were relatively limited. Previous studies, such as the one mentioned by Ozdemir et al. [34]. regarding the HLAP score, as well as nutritional and inflammation-related indicators [35], have been suggested to hold potential value for the preoperative or early diagnosis of endometrial carcinoma. Therefore, future large-scale, multicenter prospective studies incorporating additional clinical and molecular variables are warranted to further validate and expand upon our findings.

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the clinicopathological implications of AEMc and demonstrates that Pap test plays a role in the initial detection of endometrial carcinoma. We further examined the feasibility of subclassifying AEMc within cervical cytology, revealing distinct patterns across subtypes. Despite these differences, the overall risk of endometrial carcinoma remained elevated across all AEMc categories. Accordingly, women aged ≥ 45 years with AEMc—regardless of subtype—should undergo comprehensive endometrial assessment to facilitate timely detection and appropriate clinical management. Moreover, our logistic regression model integrating age and AEMc subtype achieved superior predictive performance compared with models based on either variable alone, underscoring its potential utility as a clinically practical risk-stratification tool.
