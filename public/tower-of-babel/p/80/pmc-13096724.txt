Risk of 3‐Year Cytological Progression by Baseline HPV Status Among Women With Normal Cytology: A Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Chong‐Bi Huang, Guang‐Dong Liao, Shao‐Kai Zhang, Xi Zeng, Zhi‐Fang Li, Dan‐Dan Zhao, Fan Zhao, Meng‐Yin Ao, Le‐Ni Kang, You‐Lin Qiao, Wen Chen, Yin Liu, Lian Xu, Ming‐Wei Yuan, Shao‐Long Xue, Shu‐Yuan Liu, Ming‐Rong Xi, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTailored cervical cancer screening strategies are essential, particularly in resource‐limited settings. This study aimed to assess the genotype‐specific impact of high‐risk human papillomavirus (HR‐HPV) infections on cervical cytological progression over a 3‐year interval among Chinese women, thereby providing evidence for more precise and individualized screening approaches. A multicenter cohort was established in 2017 across three Chinese provinces. Participants underwent baseline HPV genotyping and cytological examination, with genotyping for five high‐risk types (HPV16, 18, 33, 52, and 58), and follow‐up evaluations conducted from 2018 to 2020. Cytological progression was defined as a transition from normal cytology (negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy, NILM) at baseline to low‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) or worse at the final follow‐up. A total of 7240 women were included to evaluate the cytological progression. The overall progression rate was 0.7%, with a notably higher rate among HR‐HPV‐positive women (2.1%) compared to HR‐HPV‐negative women (0.5%). Specifically, HPV16, HPV52, and HPV58 were significantly associated with an increased risk of progression, with adjusted odds ratios (aORs) of 6.26 (95% CI: 2.62–14.95), 6.68 (95% CI: 3.30–13.53), and 4.24 (95% CI: 1.51–11.94), respectively. Moreover, women with persistent infections with HPV16, HPV52, and HPV58 had approximately 8‐fold, 6‐fold, and 5‐fold higher risks of progression, respectively, compared with women without infections. Stratified management based on high‐risk genotypes—particularly HPV16, HPV52, and HPV58—may help prioritize colposcopy and more intensive follow‐up for women at elevated risk, which could contribute to cervical cancer prevention efforts. Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common malignant tumor and the fourth leading cause of cancer‐related death among women globally [1]. In China, it ranks fifth in incidence and accounts for 22.7% of global cases and 20.0% of global mortality [2], with both trends continuing to rise [3]. Persistent infection with high‐risk human papillomavirus (HR‐HPV) genotypes is the primary driver of cervical carcinogenesis [4]. Consistent with World Health Organization guidelines [5], HPV testing has been recommended as the primary method for cervical cancer screening, although cytology and co‐testing remain widely used in some regions [6]. Cytology continues to play an important role in detecting morphological abnormalities in cervical epithelial cells [7], and longitudinal cytological assessment supports monitoring of lesion progression.

Cross‐sectional studies have shown higher HR‐HPV prevalence with increasing cytological severity [8, 9], but they provide limited evidence on genotype‐specific risks of cytological progression. Reported progression intervals also vary substantially. One longitudinal study estimated an average progression time of 3.6–5.5 years [10]; however, it did not evaluate progression among women with normal baseline cytology—negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM)—nor did it assess genotype‐specific risks. More robust evidence is needed to clarify how individual HR‐HPV genotypes influence cytological progression.

To address these gaps, we conducted a multicenter, population‐based cohort study in China to (i) characterize HPV genotype distribution across baseline cytological categories and (ii) evaluate 3‐year cytological progression among women with baseline NILM, particularly progression to low‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) or worse, stratified by baseline and follow‐up HPV infection status. These findings aim to refine genotype‐specific risk estimates and inform more targeted HR‐HPV screening and management strategies for cervical cancer prevention in Chinese women.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a population‐based cohort study conducted in 2017 to assess cervical cancer screening outcomes across three regions in China: Qianwei County (Sichuan Province); Changzhi County (Shanxi Province); and Xiazhi Town (Henan Province). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) women aged 21–64 years; (ii) an intact cervix; (iii) physically and mentally capable of undergoing the cervical cancer screening protocol, including colposcopy and biopsy if indicated; (iv) provision of written informed consent and full legal capacity to participate. The exclusion criteria included: (i) a history of cervical cancer diagnosis or treatment; (ii) prior cervical surgery or pelvic radiation therapy; (iii) history of hysterectomy; (iv) current or suspected pregnancy, or within 8 weeks postpartum; (v) sexual intercourse or use of vaginal topical drugs (such as lotions and contraceptives) within 48 h prior to screening; (vi) inability to provide informed consent or to complete the screening procedures due to physical or mental limitations.

At baseline, a total of 10,481 women underwent both HR‐HPV testing and cytological examination as part of the cervical cancer screening program. This cohort included 3129 women from Sichuan, 3327 from Shanxi, and 4025 from Henan. Women who tested positive for any HR‐HPV genotype and/or had cytological results of atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC‐US) or worse were followed annually between 2018 and 2020. In 2020, all participants, regardless of their initial screening results, were invited for follow‐up assessment. At each visit, participants received both HPV genotyping and cytological examination. Those presenting with abnormal findings were referred for colposcopy and, if necessary, biopsy (Figure 1). HPV testing and ThinPrep cytological examinations were performed in the clinical laboratories of West China Second Hospital of Sichuan University, Henan Cancer Hospital (Zhengzhou University Affiliated Cancer Hospital), and Heping Hospital Affiliated to Changzhi Medical College.

In this study, we focused on 9443 women who exhibited negative cytology at baseline, defined as negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM). Of these, 7240 participants completed cytological follow‐up in 2020 and were included in the final evaluation of cytological progression, and the numbers of women from the three regions were 2000, 2332, and 2900, respectively.

This study received ethical approvals from the Clinical Trial Ethics Review Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No: Q2017003); the Medical Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial Cancer Hospital (Approval No: 2017009); and the Medical Ethics Committee of Changzhi Medical College (Approval No: 2017002). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at each study visit.

HPV Testing

HPV testing was conducted using the TELLGEN HPV 5+9 assay, which employs fluorescence‐based polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technology for qualitative detection of 14 high‐risk HPV (HR‐HPV) genotypes. This assay provides specific genotyping for HPV16, HPV18, HPV33, HPV52, and HPV58. The remaining nine types—HPV31, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV 56, HPV 59, HPV66, and HPV68—were detected as a pooled group and classified as “types not determined” in this study.

HPV Infections Were Categorized as Follows

Single infection: Infection with only one of the five genotyped HPV types; Multiple infection: Co‐infection involving two or more of the genotyped types, or a combination of at least one genotyped type and one or more untyped types; Unclassified infection: Presence of one or more untyped HPV types, without distinguishing between single or multiple infections.

Cytological Examination

Cervical cytology was performed using ThinPrep cytology testing (TCT) and interpreted according to the Bethesda System (TBS) guidelines [11]. Under TBS, normal cytological findings are classified as negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM); cytological abnormalities are categorized as follows: Atypical squamous cells of unknown significance (ASC‐US), Atypical squamous cells—cannot exclude high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC‐H), Atypical glandular cells (AGC), Low‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), High‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL), Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), Adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), and Adenocarcinoma (ADC).

Statistical Analysis

All data were pre‐processed by coding variables into binary or multi‐categorical formats. No data transformation, normalization, or outlier removal was performed, and missing values were not imputed. Age, as a continuous variable, was summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas all other variables were categorical and presented as proportions. The sample size (n) for each statistical analysis is reported in the corresponding tables and figure legends.

Cytological progression was defined as a transition from NILM at baseline to LSIL, HSIL, SCC, AIS, or ADC (LSIL or worse) at the 3‐year follow‐up. HPV prevalence was analyzed in relation to baseline cytological diagnosis and categorized by infection type (single, multiple, or unclassified) as well as by specific HPV genotypes. Progression rates were stratified by baseline and follow‐up HPV status. Statistical comparisons were conducted using the Chi‐square (χ 2) test. Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with age included as a continuous covariate. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and an α level of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No post hoc tests or alpha adjustments were applied. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline HPV Prevalence and Genotype Distribution by Cytological Diagnosis

At baseline, a total of 10,481 women aged 21–64 years (mean age: 46.51 ± 9.17 years) were enrolled in the cervical cancer screening cohort. The overall prevalence of HR‐HPV infection was 16.1%. Among the type‐specific HPV: HPV52 (4.5%), HPV16 (3.3%), and HPV58 (2.9%) were the most common, while HPV33 (1.1%) and HPV18 (1.0%) were less frequently detected. Single infections accounted for 7.2% and multiple infections for 3.7%. The majority of participants (9443/10,481) had normal cytology (NILM).

Clear trends were observed across cytological categories. HR‐HPV prevalence showed a statistically significant increasing trend with cytological severity (χ 2 = 107.537, p < 0.001), ranging from 14.4% among women with NILM to 38.3% among those with HSIL. Genotype patterns also varied by cytology: HPV52 was the most prevalent genotype among women with NILM and ASC‐US, followed by HPV16, whereas HPV16 became the predominant genotype among women with ASC‐H, AGC, LSIL and HSIL, followed by HPV52 or HPV58 (Table 1).

Cytological Outcomes at 3‐Year Follow‐Up Among Women With Baseline NILM

Among the 9443 women with normal cytology (NILM) at baseline, 28 individuals were diagnosed with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or worse (CIN2+) during interim assessments and were referred for clinical management; they were therefore excluded from follow‐up analysis. Of the remaining 9415 women, 7240 (76.9%) completed cytological follow‐up after 3 years. Most participants remained cytologically normal with abnormal cytological findings detected in only 3.8% of women. Specifically, 0.6% were diagnosed with LSIL and 0.1% with HSIL (Table S1).

Three‐Year Progression Risk Stratified by Baseline HPV Status

The overall 3‐year cytological progression rate—from NILM to LSIL or worse—was 0.7%. Women who were HR‐HPV‐positive at baseline exhibited a significantly higher progression rate (2.1%) than HPV‐negative women (0.5%) (p < 0.001, aOR = 4.72, 95% CI: 2.68–8.33). Progression rates also varied by infection type: 3.9% for multiple infections, 2.5% for single infections, and 0.8% for unclassified infections, although differences between multiple or unclassified infections and single infections were not statistically significant (p = 0.280 and 0.077, respectively).

Genotype‐specific patterns were evident. Absolute progression rates were highest for HPV16 (3.6%), HPV52 (3.6%), HPV58 (2.6%), HPV33 (1.7%), and lowest for HPV18 (0.0%). Compared with genotype‐negative women, significantly increased progression risks were observed for HPV16 (p < 0.001, aOR = 6.26, 95% CI: 2.62–14.95), HPV52 (p < 0.001, aOR = 6.68, 95% CI: 3.30–13.53) and HPV58 (p = 0.006, aOR = 4.24, 95% CI: 1.51–11.94) (Table 2).

Three‐Year Progression Risk Based on Baseline and Follow‐Up HPV Status

HPV status across the two time points showed a strong association with progression risk. Women who were HR‐HPV‐positive only at follow‐up had a higher progression rate (1.3%) than those who were consistently HPV‐negative (0.4%, aOR = 3.92, 95% CI: 1.77–8.68). In contrast, women who were HPV‐positive only at baseline had a progression rate of 0.9%, which did not differ significantly from persistently HPV‐negative women (p = 0.123).

The highest progression risk was observed among women who were HR‐HPV‐positive at both visits, with a progression rate of 2.7% and a markedly increased risk compared to persistently HPV‐negative women (aOR = 8.06; 95% CI: 4.23–15.37). Persistent infections with HPV16, HPV52, HPV58 were particularly associated with elevated risks (aOR: 10.16, 95% CI: 3.90–26.45; aOR: 8.39, 95% CI: 3.85–18.28; aOR: 7.39, 95% CI: 2.59–21.02, respectively) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study was the first to evaluate the impact of type‐specific HR‐HPV infections on cervical cytological progression from NILM to LSIL or worse in a prospective, population‐based cohort of Chinese women with baseline NILM cytology. Over the 3‐year follow‐up period, women who were HPV‐positive at baseline had four times the risk of progression compared to HPV‐negative women. Among the five genotypes analyzed, HPV16, HPV52, and HPV58 were significantly associated with increased progression risk, while HPV18 and HPV33 showed minimal effect. Notably, multiple infections did not confer higher risk than single infections. These findings highlight the potential value of genotype‐specific risk stratification in cervical cancer screening programs.

The overall HPV prevalence in this cohort was 16.1%, slightly higher than the 12.3% reported in a previous nationwide study in China [12]. HPV52 emerged as the most prevalent genotype, consistent with findings from studies conducted in Shanghai [9], Hunan [13], Guangdong [14] and Zhejiang [15], Beijing [16]. In contrast, regions such as Weifang [17] and Xinjiang [18] reported HPV16 as the dominant type, reflecting regional variability in genotype distribution—likely influenced by differences in sexual behavior, health education and access to healthcare services [19]. Consistent with prior research [9, 12], our study confirmed that HPV prevalence increased with cytological severity, with HPV16 and HPV52 predominating in LSIL and HSIL cases. These results provide supportive evidence for the development of regionally tailored cervical cancer screening strategies that consider local genotype distribution, though further validation is needed. The HPV positivity rates observed in LSIL (25.3%) and HSIL (38.3%) in our cohort were relatively low but comparable to those reported in a recent nationwide, population‐based study in China [12]. In contrast, several hospital‐based studies have reported substantially higher HPV positivity in LSIL and HSIL [9, 20], largely due to differences in study populations.

Consistent with previous studies [21, 22], we found that multiple infections did not significantly elevate progression risk compared to single‐type infections. While a cross‐sectional study previously suggested that multiple infections may confer an increased risk for cervical cancer and HSIL [20], our findings suggest that oncogenic risk is primarily driven by the most pathogenic genotype present. These findings suggest that the number of concurrent infections does not provide additional risk information beyond the most pathogenic genotype present, indicating that risk stratification may prioritize genotype‐specific risk rather than the multiplicity of infections.

In genotype‐specific analysis, HPV 16 and HPV52 had the highest 3‐year progression rates (both 3.6%), followed by HPV58, HPV33, and HPV18. These results align with findings from a longitudinal study in Chinese populations identifying HPV16 and HPV52 as major contributions to CIN2+ [23]. Although HPV33 was not significantly associated with progression in our NILM cohort, previous research has demonstrated a strong association between HPV33 and HSIL [24], suggesting that its role may be more pronounced among women with existing cytological abnormalities. In China, current screening guidelines primarily emphasize HPV16 and HPV18 for colposcopy referral, while other high‐risk genotypes are often managed collectively. Given that HPV52 and HPV58 are among the most prevalent genotypes in the Chinese population and were associated with progression risks comparable to HPV16 within the five genotypes analyzed in this study, our findings provide supportive evidence for the consideration of genotype‐specific risk stratification that extends beyond HPV16 and HPV18 in future updates to regional or national screening frameworks. In women with normal cytology, implementing precision management strategies such as genotype‐specific referrals and shortened screening intervals may improve cervical cancer prevention outcomes. However, longer follow‐up and prospective validation are needed before these findings can be translated into clinical practice.

Persistent HR‐HPV infection was a strong predictor of cytological progression [25]. Women who tested HPV‐positive at both baseline and the 2020 follow‐up had the highest progression rate, which aligns with prior evidence that persistent HPV infection is a robust predictor of CIN2+ development [25]. Notably, women who were HPV‐positive only at follow‐up showed a higher progression rate than those who were HPV‐positive only at baseline (1.3% vs. 0.9%), indicating that newly acquired infections may be associated with an increased risk of cytological progression, and 3 years may be an appropriate interval for repeated HPV testing among initially HPV‐negative women. Moreover, persistent infection with HPV16, HPV52, and HPV58 was associated with 9‐fold, 7‐fold, and 6‐fold higher risks of progression, highlighting the potential value of longitudinal HPV testing—particularly for these types—in risk‐based cervical cancer screening frameworks, pending further validation. In the context of emerging risk‐stratified approaches, it is noteworthy that Tian et al. [26] reported the potential value of HPV integration testing as a triage tool for HPV‐positive women. Our findings complement this integration‐based strategy by demonstrating that genotype‐specific risks—especially for HPV16, HPV52, and HPV58—also provide meaningful stratification in a general screening population, which may enhance the identification of women at elevated risk.

This study possesses several strengths, including its large sample size and multicenter cohort design, which enhance the reliability and generalizability of the findings. In addition, robust statistical analysis adjusted for age further supports the validity of our results. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, while a 3‐year follow‐up provides meaningful insight into short‐term progression, it may not be sufficient to fully capture long‐term outcomes, as a previous study reported an average progression interval of 50–62 months [10]. Second, lesion progression was assessed through cytology, whereas histopathology remains the diagnostic gold standard. Third, our analysis was limited to five HR‐HPV genotypes (HPV16, 18, 33, 52, and 58). While these include the most common high‐risk types in China, the contribution of other carcinogenic HPV types to cytological progression may be underrepresented. Fourth, behavioral confounding factors—such as smoking, sexual activity, and contraceptive use—were not controlled for in the analysis. These factors may influence both HPV acquisition and disease progression; their absence may have introduced residual confounding. Future studies incorporating detailed behavioral data would help clarify the independent effect of HPV genotype on cytological progression. Fifth, to minimize potential misclassification bias, cases categorized as “Unclassified Infection” were retained as a separate group rather than being merged into the single or multiple infection categories. This approach ensured more accurate interpretation of infection patterns without distorting the distribution of classified infections. Finally, a 23% loss to follow‐up may have introduced selection bias, as women lost to follow‐up had a higher baseline HPV positivity rate (as shown in Table S2). Given that HPV‐positive women are at higher risk of cytological progression, this differential attrition likely led to an underestimation of the true progression rates in this cohort.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence of the strong association between HR‐HPV infection and cervical cytological progression in women with normal baseline cytology. Women with baseline HPV16, HPV52, or HPV58, as well as those with persistent infection of these genotypes, exhibited substantially higher progression risks, whereas multiple infections did not confer additional risk beyond single infections. These findings suggest that genotype‐based risk stratification—where HPV52 and HPV58 may warrant management approaches similar to HPV16, including repeat HPV testing—may help prioritize colposcopy and more intensive follow‐up for women at elevated risk. However, prospective validation with longer follow‐up and histopathological endpoints is needed before clinical implementation. Additionally, the limitations discussed above should be considered when interpreting these findings.
