Urinary copper is linked to regional cortical volume reductions in adolescents with major depressive disorder
Authors: Xiaocong Jiang, Wenjing Wang, Jingbo Zhang, Hongyi Chen, Ningyi Wan, Ting Wang, Du Lei, Dan Zhu, Xuemei Li, Xinyu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is highly prevalent among adolescents, but its neurobiological mechanisms remain unclear. Neuroimaging studies have shown cortical abnormalities in MDD, and emerging evidence suggests that trace elements may impact brain structure. This study aimed to investigate the association between urinary element concentrations and cortical alterations in adolescent MDD. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) was conducted on 190 adolescents with MDD and 123 healthy controls (HCs), measuring cortical volume, cortical thickness, and urinary metal concentrations. Partial correlation analyses examined the associations between differential elements and cortical features, while mediation analyses tested whether cortical changes mediated the effects of elements on symptom severity. Furthermore, nine supervised machine learning models were trained using differential cortical and element features, and model performance was evaluated using cross-validation and stacking ensembles. The results revealed significant reductions in cortical volume in the left lateral orbitofrontal cortex (LLOF), left medial orbitofrontal cortex (LMOF), left rostral middle frontal gyrus (LRMFG), and left frontal pole (LFP), as well as reduced cortical thickness in the right pars triangularis (RPTRI), right pars orbitalis (RPORB), and right medial orbitofrontal cortex (RMOF) in adolescents with MDD compared with HCs. Urinary copper (Cu) levels were negatively correlated with LFP volume and positively associated with both depressive and anxiety symptom severity. In machine learning classification, the stacking ensemble demonstrated the best overall performance, indicating the discriminative value of combined cortical and urinary element features in distinguishing adolescents with MDD from HCs. This study suggests that trace elements, particularly Cu, are linked to structural brain alterations and symptom severity in adolescent MDD. The integration of neuroimaging and element data enhances predictive modeling and offers new insights into the pathophysiology of MDD.Subject terms: Depression, Neuroscience

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of illness and disability among adolescents [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately one in seven adolescents is affected by a mental disorder, with MDD accounting for a significant proportion of cases. The prevalence of MDD increases with age, affecting 1.4% of individuals aged 10–14 and 3.5% of those aged 15–19 [2]. Beyond emotional distress, MDD impairs educational, occupational, and social functioning [3], and constitutes a major risk factor for suicide—the third leading cause of death among individuals aged 15–29 [4]. In China, mental disorders are the leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost, with adolescent MDD being a major contributor [2, 5, 6]. Without timely intervention, MDD can lead to long-term physical and mental health problems, severely impacting quality of life into adulthood [7]. Despite its substantial burden, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying adolescent MDD remain poorly understood, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Copper (Cu) has increasingly been implicated as a potential contributor to depressive disorders [8, 9]. As an essential trace element, Cu serves as a cofactor or structural component of numerous metalloenzymes and proteins, participating in iron metabolism, mitochondrial energy production, and antioxidant defenses. Disruption of Cu homeostasis may promote oxidative stress, perturb neurotransmitter systems, and impair synaptic plasticity [10, 11]. Several studies have reported elevated blood Cu levels in patients with MDD [12–14], with decreases observed following pharmacological treatment, suggesting that Cu dysregulation may represent a candidate biomarker or pathway relevant to MDD [15–17]. Although no study has directly quantified regional brain Cu levels in patients with MDD, Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase has been reported to be increased in the prefrontal cortex of individuals with MDD, indirectly suggesting a state of altered Cu-related biology in the brain [18]. Importantly, Cu-related research in MDD has largely focused on circulating markers, while evidence linking urinary Cu to MDD remains limited. Nevertheless, one study in older adults reported a positive association between urinary Cu and depressive symptoms, with higher urinary Cu levels linked to greater MDD risk [19]. Given its non-invasiveness, high acceptability, and ability to reflect recent excretory burden, urinary Cu may be particularly suitable for pediatric and adolescent populations. However, the contribution of Cu dysregulation to adolescent MDD and the relationship between urinary Cu and neuroimaging-defined structural phenotypes in specific brain regions remain insufficiently explored.

Adolescence represents a critical period of brain development characterized by extensive cortical remodeling that supports the maturation of emotion regulation, cognitive control, and social behavior [20–22]. Disruption of cortical development during this sensitive window has been strongly linked to the emergence of psychiatric disorders, including MDD [20, 23–25]. Structural neuroimaging studies have consistently reported cortical abnormalities in adolescent MDD, particularly in regions implicated in affective and cognitive regulation, such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) [26], orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) [27], anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [28], striatum [29], and hippocampus [30]. Converging evidence also suggests that Cu-related neurotoxicity may affect circuits central to emotion and cognitive regulation, including the DLPFC and ACC [18, 31–33]. These observations raise the possibility that Cu exposure or Cu dysregulation may contribute to the cortical alterations observed in adolescent MDD. However, existing neuroimaging findings show substantial heterogeneity, likely attributable to differences in sample size, diagnostic criteria, and methodological approaches [34]. Moreover, environmental contributors to these cortical changes remain underexplored. Together, these gaps highlight the need to clarify the neurobiological pathways that may link environmental exposures to brain structural alterations in adolescent MDD.

Adolescent MDD is increasingly conceptualized as the result of complex interactions among genetic, psychological, social, and environmental factors [35, 36]. Among environmental exposures, neurotoxic elements—including lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), Cu, and others—have been proposed as potential contributors to MDD-related pathophysiology [11, 15, 37–39]. Given the vulnerability of the developing brain, early-life exposure to neurotoxic elements may exacerbate neurodevelopmental perturbations and increase the risk of MDD. Yet, few studies have systematically examined whether urinary Cu exposure is associated with cortical structural alterations in adolescent MDD, representing a critical gap in the current literature.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to investigate the association between urinary element concentrations and cortical structure in adolescent MDD. Based on previous studies reporting that elevated levels of certain elements are linked to neurotoxicity and cortical alterations [8, 9, 40–44], and that structural brain changes in regions involved in emotional and cognitive regulation are commonly observed in MDD [27, 45], we hypothesized that: (1) higher urinary concentrations of specific elements would be associated with reduced cortical volumes in adolescent MDD; and (2) cortical volume reductions would mediate the relationship between urinary element concentrations and depressive symptom severity. A better understanding of these associations may inform early intervention strategies and contribute to the development of targeted prevention approaches, ultimately helping to mitigate the burden of MDD during this critical developmental period.

Methods

Participants

Adolescent MDD were recruited from the Department of Psychiatry, the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, China. Age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) were recruited through public advertisement. All participants underwent a structured interview, and diagnoses were confirmed independently by two board-certified psychiatrists. MDD was diagnosed according to the criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5) [46].

Inclusion criteria for patients were (1) aged 13 - 18 years; (2) a diagnosis of MDD based on DSM-5 criteria; and (3) a 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) score ≥ 7 [47]. Exclusion criteria for patients were: (1) presence of any major medical or neurological disorders; (2) current or past psychiatric disorders other than MDD (comorbid anxiety disorders were permitted if MDD was the primary diagnosis and primary reason for seeking treatment); (3) history of substance or medication abuse; (4) contraindications to MRI; (5) pregnancy or lactation; and (6) left-handedness.

HCs were required to have: (1) no history of severe medical, neurological or psychiatric disorders; (2) a HAMD score <7; (3) no history of substance abuse; (4) no contraindications to MRI; (5) right-handedness; and (6) no current pregnancy or lactation.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians prior to enrollment. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Ethical Approval No: 2020–864).

Clinical assessment

General demographic and clinical information, including sex, age, years of education, body mass index (BMI), and medication history were collected from all participants. Depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) and the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) [48].

Urinary element data acquisition and analysis

Urinary elements were available for 97 patients with MDD and 63 HCs. Participants with both urinary data and MRI data constituted the overlap subset (MDD, N = 97; HCs, N = 63), which was used for urine-MRI association and sensitivity analyses. Urine samples were collected using contamination-free polypropylene tubes and immediately stored at −80 °C until analysis. Prior to testing, samples were thawed at room temperature and diluted with 1% (v/v) nitric acid. Elemental concentrations were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; NexION 300X, PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA). Sample preparation, digestion, and measurement procedures were performed according to previously published protocols [49]. Quality control was ensured using Seronorm™ Trace Elements Urine L-2 (SERO AS, Billingstad, Norway). Urinary element concentrations were normalized to creatinine, measured using the sarcosine oxidase method (Beckman Coulter DXC 800). For values below the limit of detection (LOD), concentrations were imputed as LOD/√2 [50]. Elements with detection rates below 70% were excluded from analysis. To reduce right-skewness and improve normality, creatinine-adjusted metal concentrations were natural log-transformed [ln(x)] prior to statistical analyses.

MRI acquisition

All participants underwent structural MRI scanning using a 3 T Siemens Magnetom Skyra scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Participants were instructed to minimize head motion, keep their eyes closed, and remain awake during the scan. T1-weighted images were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.56 ms, inversion time (TI) = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, matrix size = 256 × 256, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm², slice thickness = 1 mm, 192 slices per slab, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³. All images were visually inspected by experienced neuroradiologists to confirm the absence of significant motion artifacts and gross anatomical abnormalities.

Image preprocessing

T1-weighted structural MRI data were preprocessed using the FreeSurfer software package (Version 7.4, https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). The standard recon-all pipeline was employed, which performs a series of processing steps including motion correction, intensity normalization, affine registration to Talairach space, skull stripping, segmentation of subcortical white matter structures, tessellation of the gray–white matter boundary, topological defect correction, and surface deformation to generate cortical surface models [51]. Cortical regions were parcellated into 68 areas based on the Desikan–Killiany atlas [52], and subcortical regions were segmented using FreeSurfer’s default automated labeling based on the aseg atlas [53]. For each region of interest, cortical volume and cortical thickness measures were extracted bilaterally. Cortical thickness was computed as the distance between the gray–white matter boundary and the pial surface at each vertex across the cortex, providing a sensitive marker of cortical architecture. Cortical volume was calculated as the product of cortical thickness and surface area at each vertex, integrated across the cortical surface within each parcellated region. Image quality was assessed by calculating the Euler number for each T1-weighted scan, and participants with low-quality images were excluded from further analyses.

Statistical analysis

Group differences in demographic and clinical characteristics, and urinary element concentrations were examined using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables [54, 55]. Group differences in cortical volumes and cortical thickness were evaluated using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), with adjustments for age, sex, and total intracranial volume (TIV). Prior to analysis, urinary element concentrations were normalized to urinary creatinine levels to account for differences in urine dilution. To explore the neurobiological relevance of altered element levels, partial correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations between urinary element concentrations and cortical volumes, cortical thickness, and both HAMD and HAMA scores, while controlling for age, sex, and TIV. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method [56]. Mediation analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro (version 4.1) in SPSS (version 25.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). In these models, urinary element concentrations were specified as independent variables, cortical volume as the mediator, and HAMD scores as the dependent variable, with age and sex included as covariates [57]. Statistical significance for mediation effects was evaluated using a bootstrap procedure with 5000 resamples, an indirect effect was considered significant if the 95% confidence interval did not include zero. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at P < 0.05 [58].

Machine learning

We developed a supervised machine learning framework to differentiate between MDD and HCs using predefined features. Nine widely used classifiers were evaluated: Random Forest, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT), Decision Tree, XGBoost, k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). Each classifier was assessed using a standardized pipeline that included z-score normalization (StandardScaler), Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) for handling class imbalance, and classifier fitting. SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training folds during cross-validation to prevent data leakage. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted via grid search within a 5-fold stratified cross-validation framework, where model fitting and parameter adjustment occurred only on the training set, with evaluation performed on the held-out validation fold. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, and specificity. Classifiers achieving an average accuracy above 0.60 were retained for ensemble learning. A stacking ensemble strategy was employed to integrate the eligible classifiers. For each ensemble configuration, two classifiers were designated as base learners and one as the meta-learner. An exhaustive search loop was implemented to train all possible combinations, and the optimal ensemble model was identified by systematically comparing classification performance across configurations. We employed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to quantify the contribution of each feature by calculating its SHAP value, thereby elucidating the role of individual features in the optimal stacked model for the classification task.

A total of 190 adolescent MDD and 123 HCs were included in the final analysis. There were no significant differences between the two groups in age, BMI, or years of education (all P > 0.05). However, compared to HCs, adolescent MDD exhibited significantly higher scores on both the HAMD and HAMA (P < 0.001). A significant difference in sex distribution was also observed between the groups (P = 0.012). Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Cortical volume and thickness

After adjusting for age, sex, and TIV, adolescent MDD exhibited significant reductions in both cortical volume and cortical thickness relative to the HCs across several prefrontal regions. Specifically, volume reductions were observed in the left lateral orbitofrontal cortex (LLOF, part of the orbitofrontal cortex, OFC; Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.016), the left medial orbitofrontal cortex (LMOF, part of the OFC; Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.001), the left rostral middle frontal gyrus (LRMFG, part of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, DLPFC; Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.025), and the left frontal pole (LFP, part of the anterior prefrontal cortex, aPFC; Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.003). In terms of cortical thickness, significant reductions were found in the right pars triangularis (RPTRI, part of the inferior frontal gyrus, IFG; P = 0.017), the right pars orbitalis (RPORB, part of the IFG; Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.010), and the right medial orbitofrontal cortex (RMOF, part of the OFC; Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.018) (Fig. 2, Supplementary table S1). These regions are critically involved in emotional regulation, inhibitory control, and language processing. Together, these findings suggest region-specific structural alterations in both cortical volume and cortical thickness associated with adolescent MDD.

Urinary element concentrations

A total of 32 urinary elements were initially tested, and after excluding those below the LOD (Supplementary table S2), 25 urinary elements were included in the final analysis: manganese (Mn), cobalt (Co), Cu, Zn, arsenic (As), rubidium (Rb), strontium (Sr), molybdenum (Mo), Cd, cesium (Cs), Pb, lithium (Li), boron (B), magnesium (Mg), titanium (Ti), vanadium (V), chromium (Cr), iron (Fe), selenium (Se), bromine (Br), tin (Sn), antimony (Sb), tellurium (Te), iodine (I), and barium (Ba). Group comparisons revealed significant differences in urinary element concentrations between adolescent MDD and HCs. Specifically, levels of Cu (P < 0.001) and Se (P = 0.017) were significantly higher in the MDD group, whereas levels of Mn (P < 0.001), Sn (P < 0.001), Sb (P < 0.001), and Pb (P = 0.002) were significantly lower compared to the HCs (Fig. 3, Supplementary table S3). These findings suggest potential alterations in trace element homeostasis associated with adolescent MDD.

Associations between urinary element concentrations and cortical structure

To further investigate the neurobiological relevance of altered urinary element levels, partial correlation analyses were conducted between urinary element concentrations and both cortical volumes and cortical thickness in regions that showed significant group differences, controlling for age, sex, and TIV. Our analysis revealed that the LFP volume was significantly negatively correlated with both HAMD scores (r = −0.145, P = 0.047) and HAMA scores (r = −0.151, P = 0.039), indicating a relationship between decreased volume in this region and increased depressive and anxiety symptom severity. Additionally, the RPORB thickness was significantly negatively correlated with HAMD scores (r = −0.204, P = 0.005), further supporting the connection between prefrontal cortical volume reductions and symptom severity.

In terms of cortical volume and thickness, Cu levels were significantly negatively associated with LFP volume (r = −0.216, P = 0.037), suggesting that elevated copper levels may contribute to structural alterations in this region. In addition, Sb levels were negatively correlated with both the LFP (r = −0.232, P = 0.025) and RMOF thickness (r = −0.221, P = 0.032), while Sn levels were negatively associated with the RMOF thickness (r = −0.215, P = 0.038). These findings suggest that changes in the levels of certain elements may be associated with structural alterations in specific cortical regions involved in emotional regulation and cognitive functions.

To further assess the clinical relevance of these elements, we performed additional correlation analyses between urinary element concentrations and symptom severity. Mn levels were significantly positively correlated with HAMD scores (r = 0.205, P = 0.048), suggesting a potential role of Mn in the severity of depressive symptoms. Cu levels were also significantly positively correlated with HAMA scores (r = 0.217, P = 0.036), while Sb levels were significantly positively correlated with HAMA scores (r = 0.205, P = 0.047) (Fig. 4).

Mediation analysis

The mediation analysis revealed that Cu levels had a significant negative effect on LFP volume (path a: β = −0.145, P = 0.040), and LFP volume significantly negatively influenced both HAMD scores (path b: β = −0.225, P = 0.008) and HAMA scores (path b: β = −0.226, P = 0.007). The indirect effect of Cu on depressive symptoms through LFP volume was statistically significant, as evidenced by the 95% bootstrap confidence interval [0.004, 0.086], which did not include zero. Similarly, the indirect effect of Cu on anxiety symptoms through LFP volume was also significant, with the 95% bootstrap confidence interval [0.006, 0.081] excluding zero. These findings suggest that elevated urinary Cu concentrations are associated with a decrease in LFP volume, which in turn is linked to higher depression and anxiety symptom severity (Fig. 5).

Machine learning classification

To evaluate the discriminative potential of combined features, nine supervised machine learning classifiers were trained using six differential urinary element concentrations (Mn, Cu, Se, Sn, Sb, Pb), four cortical volume measures (LLOF, LMOF, LRMFG, LFP), and three cortical thickness measures (RPTRI, RPORB, RMOF). Among individual models, random forest achieved the best performance (accuracy = 0.844; AUC = 0.937). Notably, a stacking ensemble model—combining AdaBoost and SVM as base learners with logistic regression as the meta-learner—achieved the highest overall performance (accuracy = 0.894; AUC = 0.931; sensitivity = 0.928; specificity = 0.842). The SHAP analysis revealed that Cu was the most influential feature, with higher shap values consistently contributing positively to the model output. Other elements, including Mn, Sb, and Sn, showed moderate contributions, while brain structural features such as LMOF volume and RMOF_thickness exerted relatively smaller effects. Overall, these findings highlight the dominant role of Cu in driving the classification performance (Fig. 5, Supplementary table S4). These results suggest that integrating urinary element concentrations with structural MRI features substantially enhances the ability to distinguish adolescent MDD from HCs.

Discussion

We observed significant reductions in cortical volume in brain regions associated with emotional processing, cognitive control, and decision-making in adolescent MDD. These findings are consistent with previous structural neuroimaging studies of MDD in adults [59, 60], which have reported alterations in prefrontal regions implicated in emotional regulation and executive functioning.

Specifically, our study revealed a notable decrease in cortical volume in LFP in adolescent MDD patients compared to HCs. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting smaller medial frontal poles in MDD patients, with regional volume negatively correlated with both symptom severity and illness duration [61]. Moreover, a recent voxel-based meta-analysis reported reduced cortical volume in prefrontal regions in MDD, consistent with our finding of reduced frontal pole volume [62]. A Mendelian randomization study further supports this association, suggesting that genetically proxied MDD is associated with reduced frontal pole volume, and that IL-6 signaling may partially mediate this relationship, although the mechanism remains unclear [63].

Beyond the LFP, we also identified alterations involving the OFC and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC), regions central to valuation, reward-based learning, social behavior, and emotion regulation—domains that are commonly disrupted in MDD [64, 65]. These findings further emphasize the critical role of the OFC and VMPFC in the regulation of mood and behavior, processes that are often impaired in individuals with MDD. Furthermore, significant volume reductions were found in the DLPFC, consistent with prior structural MRI studies and meta-analytic evidence indicating smaller DLPFC volumes in MDD patients compared to HCs [45, 66]. Structural abnormalities in prefrontal regions such as the LFP, DLPFC, and OFC may underlie the emotional dysregulation, impaired inhibitory control, and decision-making deficits commonly observed in adolescent MDD [27, 67]. These converging findings support a neurodevelopmental model of adolescent MDD, wherein region-specific disruptions in prefrontal cortical maturation may underlie the affective and cognitive disturbances characteristic of MDD.

Interestingly, Cu concentrations were associated with volume changes in LFP, suggesting a potential role of Cu metabolism in MDD pathogenesis. Cu is essential for multiple enzymatic reactions and neurotransmitter-related processes; however, excess Cu can promote oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory signaling [68, 69]. Mechanistically, elevated Cu may participate in redox cycling and increase reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby contributing to lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and synaptic protein damage—processes that can compromise neuronal integrity [70, 71]. Such effects may be especially detrimental to highly metabolically active prefrontal regions that are heavily engaged in emotion regulation and cognitive control. Importantly, our mediation analyses further suggest that urinary Cu may influence depressive symptom severity indirectly through its association with LFP structural variation. While urinary Cu does not directly measure brain Cu content, these convergent associations across biochemistry, neuroanatomy, and clinical symptoms support the relevance of Cu dysregulation as a candidate pathway contributing to prefrontal vulnerability in adolescent MDD, and highlight urinary Cu as a potentially informative, noninvasive marker for risk stratification that warrants longitudinal validation.

Moreover, although urinary Sb levels were lower in adolescent MDD compared to HCs, higher Sb concentrations within the MDD group were associated with reduced cortical volume and thickness in regions such as the LFP and RMOF. This apparent discrepancy may reflect a non-linear or threshold effect, where even relatively low levels of Sb—within the MDD group—could exert neurotoxic effects on vulnerable brain regions. Similar patterns have been reported in environmental neuroscience, where subclinical exposures show differential effects depending on individual susceptibility or underlying neuropathology. The neurotoxic potential of elements like Sb has been linked to impaired synaptic plasticity and neuroinflammation, which may further contribute to emotional dysregulation and cognitive deficits observed in MDD [40, 72, 73]. While the neurobiological role of Sb remains poorly understood, accumulating evidence suggests that chronic or low-level Sb exposure can impair neural, suggesting complementary information between trace element profiles and structural brain markers [74, 75]. These findings highlight the potential of combining multimodal data to capture the complex neurobiological underpinnings of adolescent MDD. Notably, the high sensitivity of the final model underscores its potential utility in early screening contexts. However, given the exploratory nature of this framework, caution is warranted; independent validation in external cohorts is essential before clinical application [76]. Future research may benefit from incorporating longitudinal designs, functional imaging, or potentially genomic data to further refine predictive models and support precision psychiatry efforts [77].

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and prevents characterization of developmental trajectories linking element dysregulation, cortical maturation, and symptom progression. Second, urinary element concentrations, although safe and noninvasive, likely reflect systemic status and exposure more than direct brain element burden; thus, mechanistic conclusions about brain element accumulation cannot be made from urinary measures alone. Third, to address concerns that group differences might be driven by sex imbalance—and acknowledging that urinary elements can differ by sex—we conducted sex-stratified analyses of urinary elements. The overall pattern of key findings remained broadly consistent, but some differences were more evident in females whereas results in males were less stable, likely reflecting the smaller male subsample and reduced statistical power after stratification. These subgroup results are therefore presented to enhance transparency and robustness, rather than to imply a definitive sex-specific mechanism.

Conclusion

This study identifies significant structural brain changes in adolescent MDD that are associated with altered urinary copper levels. Mediation analysis suggests that Cu may modulate depressive and anxiety symptoms through its impact on frontal pole volume. These findings provide valuable insights into the relationship between element dysregulation and adolescent MDD and open new avenues for exploring therapeutic strategies targeting Cu metabolism to potentially mitigate both neuroanatomical and clinical manifestations. Further longitudinal studies are needed to establish causality and evaluate whether Cu-related pathways may represent viable therapeutic targets.
