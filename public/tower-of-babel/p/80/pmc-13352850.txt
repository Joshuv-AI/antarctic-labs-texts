Enhancing perceived clinical competence of nursing students in the AI era: the role of AI acceptance and self-directed learning
Authors: Boshra Karem Mohamed El-Sayed, Ayman Ateq Alamri, Maha Gamal Ramadan Asal, Hanaa Hamad Mohamed Akl, Ahmed Abdelwahab Ibrahim El-Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the context of AI-driven transformation in healthcare education, preparing nursing students to effectively engage with generative artificial intelligence tools has become increasingly important. While self-directed learning (SDL) has been consistently associated with clinical competence, the role of AI acceptance in this relationship remains underexplored.ObjectiveTo examine the mediating role of AI acceptance in the relationship between self-directed learning ability and perceived clinical competence among nursing students.MethodsA cross-sectional, correlational design was employed. Data were collected from 550 nursing students at Alexandria University, Egypt, using validated self-report instruments measuring self-directed learning ability, AI acceptance, and perceived clinical competence. Structural equation modeling was conducted to test the hypothesized relationships and examine the mediating effects.ResultsSelf-directed learning ability was significantly associated with clinical competence (β = 0.452, p < 0.001), and AI acceptance was positively associated with perceived clinical competence (β = 0.489, p < 0.001). AI acceptance partially mediated the relationship between self-directed learning and perceived clinical competence (indirect effect: β = 0.206, p < 0.001). The model accounted for 51.0% of the variance in clinical competence.ConclusionThe findings indicate that both self-directed learning and AI acceptance are associated with perceived clinical competence, with AI acceptance acting as a mediating factor. These results highlight the relevance of integrating learner-centered approaches with supportive AI-enabled learning environments in nursing education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09453-8.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming healthcare systems and reshaping the educational preparation of future healthcare professionals [1]. In nursing education, generative AI tools such as ChatGPT are increasingly integrated into teaching and learning processes, providing opportunities for real-time feedback, enhanced knowledge access, and simulation of clinical scenarios [2, 3]. These technologies support interactive and personalized learning experiences that extend beyond traditional pedagogical approaches [4]. As AI becomes embedded in both academic and clinical contexts, the ability of nursing students to effectively engage with such tools is essential for preparing them to function competently in technologically advanced healthcare environments [5].

Clinical competence remains a core outcome of nursing education and a critical determinant of safe and effective patient care [6]. It encompasses a multidimensional integration of knowledge, technical skills, critical thinking, and professional judgment applied in real-world clinical settings [7, 8]. Despite its importance, many nursing students experience challenges in translating theoretical knowledge into competent clinical practice, particularly within increasingly complex and technology-driven healthcare systems [9, 10]. Consequently, contemporary nursing education emphasizes learner-centered approaches that promote adaptability, critical thinking, and independent learning as essential components of clinical competence development [10].

Self-directed learning (SDL) has been widely recognized as a key mechanism for developing such competencies. SDL refers to the learner’s ability to take initiative in identifying learning needs, setting goals, selecting strategies, and evaluating outcomes [11]. In nursing education, SDL is associated with enhanced critical thinking, autonomy, and clinical performance, enabling students to actively engage in their learning and apply knowledge effectively in clinical contexts [12, 13]. However, the effectiveness of SDL may depend on the learning environment and the tools available to support autonomous learning, particularly in digital and AI-enhanced educational settings [14].

In this context, students’ acceptance of AI technologies emerges as a critical factor influencing the effectiveness of learning [15]. AI acceptance refers to individuals’ perceptions of a technology’s usefulness and ease of use, which shape their intention to use and actual engagement with the technology [16]. Evidence suggests that nursing students who demonstrate higher acceptance of AI tools are more likely to engage with them effectively, leading to improved learning outcomes and clinical competence [17].

Additionally, positive attitudes toward AI have been associated with increased motivation and self-directed learning behaviors, whereas concerns regarding reliability and ethical implications may hinder engagement [18, 19]. Despite these insights, limited research has examined how self-directed learning and AI acceptance interact to influence clinical competence within an integrated framework. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the mediating role of AI acceptance in the relationship between self-directed learning ability and clinical competence among nursing students, providing a more comprehensive understanding of learning in AI-enhanced educational environments.

Background and conceptualization of the study

Clinical competence is a central outcome in nursing education and reflects the integration of knowledge, technical skills, critical thinking, and professional judgment within clinical contexts [7, 8]. Contemporary perspectives suggest that competence development is shaped not only by curriculum content but also by how learners engage with educational processes and available learning resources [10]. As healthcare and educational environments become increasingly technology-mediated, there is growing interest in understanding how individual learning characteristics and technology-related factors are associated with variations in clinical competence [20, 21].

Self-directed learning (SDL) represents a key learner-related construct within this context [14]. Rooted in adult learning theory, SDL refers to the extent to which individuals take initiative in identifying learning needs, selecting strategies, and evaluating outcomes [11, 22]. In nursing education, SDL has been associated with higher levels of engagement, critical thinking, and clinical reasoning, which are essential components of competence development [12, 13]. From a constructivist perspective, SDL reflects an active learning process in which students engage with knowledge and apply it within relevant contexts, potentially contributing to their perceived clinical competence [23].

At the same time, the increasing integration of AI tools, such as ChatGPT, introduces new dimensions to the learning environment [15]. These technologies provide opportunities for interactive learning, immediate feedback, and access to diverse information sources, which may support knowledge application and reflective learning [2, 4]. However, prior research indicates that the presence of technology alone does not ensure meaningful engagement [24]. Instead, students’ perceptions of and attitudes toward such tools appear to play an important role in shaping their use within educational contexts [5, 25].

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) offers a widely used framework for examining these perceptions. According to TAM, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are associated with individuals’ behavioral intentions and patterns of technology use [16, 26]. Within educational settings, these constructs are often operationalized as indicators of technology acceptance, reflecting the extent to which learners are inclined to engage with digital tools as part of their learning processes [27]. Empirical findings suggest that higher levels of AI acceptance among nursing students are associated with greater engagement and more favorable learning outcomes, including aspects related to clinical competence [17]. Conversely, concerns regarding reliability, ethical considerations, or overreliance may be linked to lower acceptance and reduced engagement [19].

Integrating these perspectives, AI acceptance may be considered as a relevant explanatory construct in the relationship between self-directed learning and clinical competence. Students with higher levels of SDL may be more inclined to explore and utilize diverse learning resources, including AI-based tools [28]. However, the extent to which such tools are incorporated into their learning processes may depend on their level of acceptance, as reflected in their perceptions of usefulness and ease of use [27]. In this sense, AI acceptance may represent a potential pathway through which self-directed learning is associated with learning experiences that are relevant to clinical competence [29]. This interpretation is consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that engagement with AI-supported learning environments is associated with enhanced application of knowledge and learning outcomes [30].

Based on this conceptual orientation, the present study adopts a model in which self-directed learning is examined as an independent variable, clinical competence as an outcome, and AI acceptance as a mediating variable. The model reflects a sequence of associations in which self-directed learning is related to AI acceptance, and AI acceptance, in turn, is associated with clinical competence.

Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1: Self-directed learning ability is positively associated with clinical competence among nursing students.H2: AI acceptance is positively associated with clinical competence among nursing students.H3: AI acceptance mediates the relationship between self-directed learning ability and clinical competence.

Objectives of the study

This study has three main objectives. First, it aims to assess the levels of self-directed learning ability, AI acceptance, and clinical competence among nursing students. Second, it examines the associations between self-directed learning ability and AI acceptance with clinical competence. Third, it investigates the mediating role of AI acceptance in the relationship between self-directed learning ability and clinical competence.

Methods

Research design and setting

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional research design and was conducted at the College of Nursing, Alexandria University, Egypt. The College of Nursing is an academic unit within a large public university that enrolls students from diverse geographic and socio-demographic backgrounds across the country. As the primary setting for undergraduate nursing education, it offers a structured academic and clinical learning environment, making it an appropriate context for examining factors influencing nursing students’ learning behaviors and clinical competence.

Participants and sampling

The target population for this study consisted of nursing students enrolled at the College of Nursing, Alexandria University, during the 2024–2025 academic year. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) for linear regression analysis. Based on a statistical power of 0.85, a small effect size (f² = 0.02), and a significance level (α) of 0.05, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 440 participants. To enhance the reliability of the findings and account for potential non-response or attrition, the sample size was increased to 550 students.

A stratified simple random sampling method with proportional allocation was employed to ensure representative sampling across all academic levels. The sample was proportionally distributed based on the number of students enrolled in each academic year. For each stratum, a complete list of student identification numbers was obtained, and the required number of participants was calculated accordingly. Random selection within each stratum was conducted using the lottery technique, whereby student IDs were randomly drawn until the predetermined sample size for each academic level was achieved. Study instruments

Data were collected using three standardized scales corresponding to the study variables, supplemented by five additional items designed to capture participants’ socio-demographic and learning-related characteristics. These included age, gender, marital status, academic level, Cumulative GPA and prior participation in training related to how to use AI tools -supported in learning and nursing education.

The Technology Acceptance Model Evaluation for ChatGPT questionnaire, developed by Sallam et al. (2023) [31], was utilized to assess participants’ perceptions and acceptance of generative AI tools. This validated instrument is grounded in the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and specifically tailored for healthcare students. It comprises 36 items that measure key constructs, including perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, risk perceptions, and behavioral intentions toward ChatGPT. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree [1] to strongly agree [5]. A higher score on the scale reflects a more favorable perception of ChatGPT, indicating greater acceptance, perceived usefulness, ease of use, and stronger behavioral intention to utilize the tool in academic or clinical settings. Reverse-coded items were appropriately considered in the scoring process. The tool has demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87 [31], indicating excellent reliability.

The Self-Rating Scale of Self-Directed Learning (SRSSDL_ITA) was employed to assess the self-directed learning (SDL) abilities of undergraduate nursing students. Originally developed by Williamson (2007) [32] and later validated by Cadorin et al. (2017) [12], the SRSSDL_ITA consists of 40 items distributed across eight dimensions: Awareness (7 items), Attitudes [8], Motivation [6], Learning Strategies [5], Learning Methods [4], Learning Activities [4], Interpersonal Skills [4], and Constructing Knowledge [2]. Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always), resulting in a total score between 40 and 200. Higher scores indicate stronger self-directed learning abilities, reflecting greater autonomy, motivation, and engagement in the learning process. The instrument has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93, indicating excellent internal consistency and reliability [12].

The Clinical Competence Questionnaire (CCQ), developed by Liou and Cheng (2014) [33], is a validated instrument designed to assess nursing students’ self-perceived clinical competence. The CCQ comprises 47 items categorized into two main subscales: professional nursing behavior and skill competence. The skill competence subscale is further divided into three domains: general performance, core nursing skills, and advanced nursing skills. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (do not have a clue) to 5 (known in theory, competent in practice without supervision), with total scores ranging from 47 to 235. Higher scores reflect greater levels of perceived clinical competence. The instrument has demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.98, indicating strong reliability for use in both academic and clinical education settings [33].

Adaptation, validity, and reliability of the study instrument

To ensure linguistic accuracy and cultural relevance of the study instruments, a rigorous back-translation procedure was implemented in accordance with the methodology outlined by Beaton et al. (2000) [34]. The original tools were translated into Arabic and subsequently back-translated into English by independent bilingual experts to identify and resolve discrepancies, thereby minimizing threats to construct validity. Content validity was further evaluated by an expert panel comprising five faculty members from the College of Nursing, Alexandria University, two from the Department of Nursing Administration and three from the Department of Nursing Education who reviewed the instruments for clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness.

A pilot study was conducted with a sample of 55 nursing students to assess the clarity of the questionnaire items and to estimate the average time needed for completion. Participant feedback confirmed that the items were clear and comprehensible. These students were excluded from the final study sample to avoid potential bias.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS for SPSS (Version 0.28) to evaluate the construct validity of the translated instruments. Model fit was assessed according to established criteria, with acceptable fit indicated by a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08, a Chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df) < 5, and values for the Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Incremental Fit Index (IFI) exceeding 0.90 [35]. For the Technology Acceptance Model Evaluation for ChatGPT questionnaire, CFA demonstrated a strong model fit, with a Chi-square value of 231.026, χ²/df = 1.851, and excellent fit indices (GFI = 0.936, IFI = 0.914, CFI = 0.923), all surpassing the recommended thresholds. The RMSEA was 0.064, indicating a good approximation of model fit to the data.

The Self-Rating Scale of Self-Directed Learning (SRSSDL_ITA) also exhibited a good model fit, with a Chi-square value of 269.031 and a χ²/df ratio of 1.273. Fit indices (GFI = 0.914, IFI = 0.931, CFI = 0.944) and an RMSEA of 0.054 further support the adequacy of the model. Similarly, the Clinical Competence Questionnaire (CCQ) demonstrated acceptable model fit, with a Chi-square value of 215 and a χ²/df ratio of 4.12. The fit indices (GFI = 0.911, IFI = 0.933, CFI = 0.952) were all within the recommended range, and the RMSEA of 0.0738 indicated a close fit between the hypothesized model and the observed data.

The reliability of the study instruments was assessed by evaluating their internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The obtained alpha values were 0.88 for the Technology Acceptance Model Evaluation for ChatGPT questionnaire, 0.90 for the Self-Rating Scale of Self-Directed Learning (SRSSDL_ITA), and 0.95 for the Clinical Competence Questionnaire (CCQ). These values reflect a high level of internal consistency across all instruments, indicating that each tool demonstrated excellent reliability for use in this study.

Ethical considerations and data collection

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Nursing at Alexandria University, Egypt (SN: AU-20-8-365, IRB 00013620) on 15 June 2025. The study was conducted according to the requirements of the Helsinki Declaration. The confidentiality of the data was maintained. The anonymity of the study subjects was maintained. Subjects participated in the study voluntarily and had the right to withdraw at any time from the study without any consequences.

Data were collected using an anonymous, self-administered paper-based questionnaire distributed directly to participants at the study setting. Eligible students were approached in their academic environment, and the purpose of the study was explained prior to participation. Written informed consent was obtained before the questionnaires were distributed.

Participants completed the questionnaires individually and returned them in sealed envelopes to ensure anonymity. Each participant was allotted approximately 15 to 20 min to complete the questionnaire. No personally identifiable information was collected. Completed questionnaires were stored securely in locked cabinets accessible only to the research team. Data were later coded and entered into a password-protected database for analysis, and all analyses were conducted using anonymized data.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 and the PROCESS Macro version 4.2. Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize the study variables: categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was employed to explore the associations among continuous variables.

To test the hypothesized mediation model, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was carried out using AMOS version 23. The analysis examined the mediating role of AI acceptance in the relationship between self-directed learning ability (independent variable) and perceived clinical competence (dependent variable) among nursing students. Several covariates gender, age, marital status, academic level, cumulative GPA, and prior participation in workshops or training related to AI-supported learning in nursing education were statistically controlled to account for their potential confounding effects.

A significance level of 0.05 was adopted for all statistical analyses. To assess the statistical significance of the indirect (mediation) effect, a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated. The mediation effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include zero, indicating a non-zero indirect pathway within the model.

Mediating role of AI acceptance

As shown in Table 5 and Fig. 1, the model path results revealed a statistically significant direct effect of self-directed learning on clinical competence perception (B = 0.095, p = 0.002), thereby supporting H1. This suggests that students who engage more actively in self-directed learning are more likely to develop stronger clinical competence. Also, the results indicated a robust and statistically significant effect of AI acceptance on clinical competence perception (B = 0.632, p < 0.001), providing strong support for H2. This underscores the relevance of AI-related digital skills in enhancing perceived clinical performance among nursing students. Moreover, the mediation analysis confirmed a significant indirect effect (B = 0.101, 95% CI [0.025, 0.186], p < 0.001), supporting H3. Notably, the indirect pathway through AI acceptance accounted for 52.4% of the total effect, while the direct effect represented 47.6%, indicating a partial mediation model.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between self-directed learning ability, AI acceptance, and perceived clinical competence among nursing students, with particular attention to the mediating role of AI acceptance. The findings indicate that self-directed learning was positively associated with perceived clinical competence, AI acceptance was also positively associated with perceived clinical competence, and AI acceptance partially mediated the relationship between self-directed learning and clinical competence. Collectively, these results highlight the potential interplay between learner autonomy and technology-related perceptions in shaping clinical learning outcomes within AI-enhanced educational environments.

The observed association between self-directed learning and clinical competence perception is consistent with the theoretical assumptions of self-directed learning theory [32], which emphasize the role of learner autonomy in promoting deeper engagement and knowledge application. Prior studies have similarly reported that students who actively manage their learning processes tend to demonstrate higher levels of clinical reasoning and decision-making ability [11, 13]. The present findings align with this body of evidence, suggesting that self-directed learning may be linked to how effectively students engage with and apply knowledge in clinical contexts.

At the same time, this relationship may be influenced by contextual and environmental factors. For instance, Xu et al. (2024) highlighted variability in self-directed learning levels among health profession students, indicating that access to resources and institutional support may shape how SDL is translated into practice [14]. Likewise, Liu et al. (2025) suggested that the relationship between SDL and competence may vary depending on factors such as mentorship and organizational culture [36]. These considerations suggest that while SDL is an important learner-related factor, its association with clinical competence may depend on the broader educational context.

The findings also indicate a significant association between AI acceptance and perceived clinical competence, supporting H2. Students who reported higher levels of acceptance—reflected in more favorable perceptions of usefulness and ease of use—also reported higher levels of clinical competence. This observation is consistent with the Technology Acceptance Model [16], which proposes that positive perceptions of technology are associated with greater engagement and use. In educational contexts, such engagement may provide additional opportunities for learning, practice, and feedback, which may be relevant to competence development.

These findings are consistent with previous research examining AI use in nursing education. For example, Arkan et al. (2025) reported that ChatGPT-based educational interventions were associated with improvements in problem-solving skills and attitudes toward AI [37]. Similarly, González-García et al. (2025) found that students who used ChatGPT demonstrated higher levels of critical thinking and clinical documentation skills [38], while Higashitsuji et al. (2025) observed improvements in case-based learning efficiency [4].

Although these studies do not directly measure acceptance, they suggest that engagement with AI tools may be associated with learning outcomes that are relevant to clinical competence. Notably, such engagement may often occur outside formal curricula, as students increasingly utilize AI tools informally for tasks such as clarifying theoretical concepts, summarizing academic content, generating study materials, and supporting case-based understanding [2, 30, 38]. This pattern of self-initiated use may help explain the observed levels of AI acceptance despite the limited exposure to formal training, as familiarity and perceived usefulness can develop through repeated informal interaction with these tools.

At the same time, the literature also highlights potential limitations of AI integration. Sarıkahya et al. (2025) cautioned that overreliance on AI tools may contribute to superficial learning if students do not critically evaluate AI-generated content [39]. Similarly, Bouriami et al. (2025) raised concerns regarding the potential for reduced independent thinking [19]. These perspectives suggest that the relationship between AI use and learning outcomes is complex and may depend on how students engage with these tools. Within this context, AI acceptance may be understood as a factor associated with students’ likelihood of engaging with AI in ways that support or limit learning, rather than as an indicator of competence itself.

An important consideration in interpreting the present findings relates to the nature of the clinical competence measure. The Clinical Competence Questionnaire is a self-assessment tool and therefore reflects students’ perceived competence rather than objectively observed clinical performance. As such, the associations identified in this study should be interpreted with caution, as higher reported competence may, in part, reflect increased confidence rather than actual clinical proficiency. This distinction is particularly relevant in the context of AI use, where access to rapid information and feedback may enhance students’ sense of preparedness without necessarily corresponding to validated performance in real clinical settings.

In this regard, the concept of critical AI literacy becomes particularly relevant. Effective engagement with AI requires not only the ability to use technological tools but also the capacity to critically evaluate the accuracy, limitations, and ethical implications of AI-generated outputs [40]. Without such critical engagement, there is a risk that reliance on AI may reinforce surface-level understanding or overconfidence [30]. Accordingly, AI acceptance should be interpreted as a factor associated with engagement in AI-supported learning environments, rather than as a direct indicator of clinical competence.

Importantly, recent developments in AI-assisted educational practices provide additional context for these findings. Emerging evidence suggests that AI is increasingly used not only as a learning support tool but also within the assessment of clinical skills. For example, Erkayıran (2025) reported that AI-assisted evaluation of therapeutic communication skills produced outcomes comparable to traditional standardized patient-based assessments, while offering advantages in consistency and scalability [41]. Such developments indicate that AI is becoming embedded within core components of clinical education and evaluation. In this context, students with higher AI acceptance may be more inclined to engage with AI-supported learning and assessment approaches, which may provide additional opportunities for feedback, reflection, and skill refinement. This perspective offers a more concrete link between AI-related engagement and clinically relevant learning processes.

The mediating role of AI acceptance represents a key finding of this study. The results suggest that the association between self-directed learning and perceived clinical competence may be partially explained through AI acceptance. Students with higher levels of self-directed learning may be more inclined to explore and utilize diverse learning resources, including AI tools; however, their engagement with these tools may depend on their perceptions of usefulness and ease of use. In this sense, AI acceptance may represent a pathway through which self-directed learning is associated with learning experiences that are relevant to perceived clinical competence. This interpretation is consistent with findings from Han et al. (2025), who reported that chatbot-based learning was associated with enhanced self-directed learning and engagement [42], as well as Li et al. (2025), who identified a mediating role of engagement and SDL in the relationship between AI use and learning outcomes [30].

An important consideration in interpreting the present findings is the potential discrepancy between perceived and actual clinical competence, particularly in the context of AI-supported learning. The use of self-reported measures may introduce cognitive biases, including overestimation of ability and self-enhancement tendencies. This concern can be further understood through the lens of the Dunning–Kruger effect, which suggests that individuals with limited knowledge or experience may overestimate their competence [43, 44]. In AI-enhanced learning environments, this bias may be amplified by the rapid accessibility, coherence, and perceived authority of AI-generated information, which can create an illusion of understanding without corresponding depth of knowledge or clinical reasoning [15].

Given that a substantial proportion of participants in this study (77.5%) reported no formal training in AI use, it is plausible that some students may lack the critical AI literacy required to effectively evaluate the accuracy and limitations of AI-generated content. Consequently, the observed positive associations between AI acceptance and clinical competence should be interpreted with caution, as they may partially reflect increased confidence rather than objectively demonstrated competence. This highlights the importance of integrating critical AI literacy and metacognitive skill development within nursing education to ensure that engagement with AI tools supports accurate self-assessment and genuine competence development rather than reinforcing cognitive bias.

These findings have implications for nursing education. While fostering self-directed learning remains important, the results suggest that students’ acceptance of AI technologies may also be relevant to how they engage with emerging digital learning environments. As noted by Ma et al. (2025), the integration of AI into nursing education requires not only access to technology but also the development of skills and competencies related to its critical and responsible use [5]. From this perspective, educational strategies may benefit from addressing both learner autonomy and the development of critical AI literacy.

Finally, the findings align with broader discussions regarding the evolving nature of nursing education in the context of AI. As highlighted by Topaz et al. (2025) and Luo et al. (2025), AI is increasingly shaping how knowledge is accessed, applied, and evaluated in healthcare education [2, 3]. The present study contributes to this evolving discourse by suggesting that the relationship between learner characteristics and clinical competence may be associated with how students perceive and engage with AI technologies within their learning environments.

Implications of the study

The findings of this study have implications for nursing education, practice, policy, and leadership. In nursing education, the observed associations between self-directed learning and clinical competence support the use of pedagogical approaches that promote learner autonomy, such as problem-based learning, reflective practice, and student-centered instruction. At the same time, the findings suggest that students’ acceptance of AI technologies may be relevant to how they engage with emerging digital learning environments. Accordingly, educational programs may benefit from integrating structured guidance on the responsible, critical, and contextually appropriate use of AI tools, alongside efforts to strengthen self-directed learning. This integration can be operationalized within clinical rotations by allowing students to use AI tools in preparing preliminary care plans, while requiring them to explicitly justify how AI-generated recommendations were interpreted, verified against clinical guidelines, and adapted to patient-specific conditions. Preceptors can evaluate students by focusing on two key criteria: their ability to justify AI-generated care plans using clinical evidence and patient-specific data, and their ability to critically appraise and appropriately modify AI outputs to ensure safe and context-appropriate care.

From an educational leadership perspective, the mediating role of AI acceptance highlights the importance of digital readiness within contemporary nursing education. Leaders may consider supporting faculty development initiatives aimed at integrating AI into teaching and evaluation practices, as well as ensuring that curricula reflect evolving technological contexts. In addition, facilitating access to appropriate digital tools and supporting informed use of AI in learning environments may contribute to more effective educational engagement.

At the policy level, the findings suggest that AI-related competencies and perceptions may warrant consideration within nursing education standards. Curriculum developers and accreditation bodies may benefit from incorporating guidance on AI literacy, including ethical considerations such as data privacy, bias, and responsible use of AI in academic and clinical contexts. Such efforts may support more consistent and informed engagement with AI technologies among nursing students.

In clinical practice settings, the findings indicate that students’ engagement with AI technologies may be associated with their learning experiences and perceived competence. As AI tools become more prevalent in healthcare, educators and clinical preceptors may play a role in supporting students and new graduates in the appropriate and reflective use of these technologies. Importantly, preceptors should evaluate not only the final clinical outputs but also the student’s clinical reasoning process when AI is used. For example, students can be asked to explain how AI-informed suggestions were critically appraised, identify potential inaccuracies or biases, and justify any modifications or deviations from AI-generated recommendations based on patient needs. Additionally, incorporating process-oriented assessment criteria—such as critical evaluation of AI outputs, integration with evidence-based practice, and transparency in AI use—can provide a more accurate assessment of clinical judgment. This may include fostering critical evaluation skills and ensuring that AI use complements, rather than replaces, professional judgment.

Overall, the study suggests that preparing nursing students for AI-enhanced healthcare environments may involve attention to both learner autonomy and AI acceptance. Integrating these elements within educational and practice contexts may support students’ engagement with evolving technologies while maintaining a focus on safe and effective clinical practice.

Limitations and future directions

This study has limitations that warrant consideration. While it examines the mediating role of AI acceptance, it does not explore the depth, quality, or context of AI usage, which may influence outcomes. In particular, the study did not capture how frequently students used AI tools, the specific academic or clinical tasks for which they were used, or the extent to which such use was guided or unguided, which may limit the interpretation of AI-related engagement. Self-reported measures may also introduce bias, potentially inflating perceived competence. Specifically, the use of the Clinical Competence Questionnaire reflects perceived rather than objectively assessed clinical competence, and therefore may be influenced by students’ confidence or self-perception rather than actual clinical performance. This distinction is especially relevant in the context of AI use, where access to rapid information and feedback may enhance perceived preparedness without necessarily corresponding to validated clinical skill.

Moreover, the majority of participants (77.5%) had no formal AI training, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with higher levels of AI literacy. In such contexts, the strength and nature of AI acceptance and its mediating role may differ. In addition, the study did not assess students’ critical AI literacy, which may influence their ability to evaluate and appropriately apply AI-generated information in clinical decision-making. The absence of such evaluative skills may increase the risk of overreliance on AI tools, potentially leading to superficial understanding or unwarranted confidence in clinical judgments. Furthermore, contextual and institutional factors such as faculty guidance, curriculum integration, and availability of AI-related infrastructure were not examined. These factors may play a significant role in shaping students’ development of AI-related competencies and their perceptions of AI usefulness and acceptance.

Future research should investigate longitudinal effects, explore diverse educational settings, and incorporate observational or performance-based measures to better understand the real-world relationship between AI-related engagement and clinical competence. Further studies are also needed to examine patterns of AI use (e.g., frequency, purpose, and context) and to integrate assessments of critical AI literacy to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how AI influences both perceived and actual clinical performance.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that self-directed learning and AI acceptance are both associated with perceived clinical competence among nursing students, with AI acceptance acting as a mediating factor in this relationship. These results highlight the role of learner autonomy alongside engagement with AI technologies in shaping clinical learning outcomes. Together, they underscore the relevance of integrating self-directed learning approaches with supportive AI-enabled learning environments to enhance nursing education in the context of evolving digital healthcare systems.
