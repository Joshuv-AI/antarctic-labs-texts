Evidence for the evolution of male and female cardinals’ plumage colouration being affected by both natural and sexual selection
Authors: Natália S Porzio, Paulo G Mota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe evolution of sexually selected traits, such as colouration, has been the object of considerable research. Among birds, plumage colouration is highly variable and includes some of the most exuberant signals. While sexual selection is generally considered the primary driver of the most conspicuous forms of these signals, several ecological, morphological, and social factors have also been shown to influence their evolution. Thus, a complex interaction of factors can drive the evolution of colouration in different groups of birds, which needs to be considered for a broader perspective. Cardinals constitute a monophyletic group of birds with an extensive palette of colour variation, including some species with quite exuberant colouration, covering a wide geographical and environmental range, making them an exciting group to study the possible main drivers of colour evolution. We considered factors related to natural and sexual selection to test their role in explaining cardinals’ plumage colour evolution. We also considered males and females separately, as they are likely subject to different selective pressures. We found that the evolution of male and female colour diversity and brightness was positively associated with the intensity of sexual selection. In females, it was also associated with colour saturation. Female colour evolution was also influenced by natural selection, including habitat cover (negatively related to colour diversity, saturation, and brilliance) and net primary productivity (negatively related to brilliance). Both sexes shared similar types of colouration sources, but diverged in colour expression. For carotenoid colouration, yellow is predominant in females, while males display reds more often, which agrees with the indicator hypotheses for sexually selected signals. These findings provide new insights into the evolution of cardinal colours and support the importance of both sexual and natural selection in shaping colour evolution. Background

Understanding the evolution of sexual ornaments has been central to the theory of sexual selection [1] and for our understanding of communication in the context of sexual signalling. Plumage colouration is one of the most conspicuous and often exuberant traits of birds, and the differences between males and females have led to their being associated with sexual selection [2–5]. However, it has also become evident that male and female colouration play several other roles that were very likely influenced and constrained their evolution [2, 6], requiring us to consider a diversity of environmental factors that can have significant importance in plumage colour evolution. Colouration can be used for crypsis, conspicuousness, group identification, or individual characteristics, so that a number of different drivers may affect its evolution, including ecological factors, morphological attributes, social organisation, and phylogenetic relationships [2, 7–10]. Some of the identified functions of plumage colouration include mate attraction, species recognition, thermoregulation, camouflage, and social interactions [7, 11–14]. Colour signalling can be affected by environmental characteristics such as vegetation density, precipitation and, in general, environmental productivity. Colouration is a complex trait that cannot be measured under a single dimension, with several needing to be considered [15]. There is variation in the type of colour, or hue, its saturation and brightness and, considering an animal with different body parts coloured differently, that heterogeneity.

Considering colour heterogeneity within individuals, or colouration complexity, colour elaboration in birds is higher in the tropics, which tend to be moister and more densely vegetated [9]. This is partly due to sexual selection, which is the main factor explaining the greater colouration in the tropics. In contrast, precipitation, net primary productivity and forest dependency also explain some of the variation, so that in warmer, wetter forest environments, contrasting colours are strongly favoured in birds [2, 9, 16]. Migratory behaviour was also related to colour complexity, as migratory species tend to have shorter breeding seasons, which would increase the intensity of sexual selection [17, 18], namely over male colour signalling [19, 20]. Body size may also affect plumage colour variation. Some studies report a positive association between body size and plumage elaboration [2, 21, 22], which might be related to predation risk, as larger species are under less risk. In other studies, it was the smaller species that were the more colourful, which is attributed to the decreased cost of expressing colours in smaller bodies or to size-related differences in visual communication, where smaller species must work harder to become conspicuous [9, 23]. These results, pointing in opposite directions, may likely be related to methodological differences in measuring colouration and in the taxonomic scope of the species considered [9], but do certainly require more attention for a better understanding of the effect of body size on colourfulness.

When considering brightness, which affects the visibility of animals, available results also do not agree on how environmental openness, light intensity, and precipitation affect the evolution and current distribution of colour brightness in birds. In some studies, duller colouration appeared associated with closed areas [24, 25], while in others brighter species were actually associated with more closed environments [9, 26]. The rationale for cryptic colours being associated with darker environments was based on higher predation risk in darker areas, while for the opposing association was that conspicuousness was selected for more efficient signalling in conditions where visibility is reduced [2, 27]. Duller colouration was also associated with habitats with higher precipitation levels [28], which tend to be areas with higher vegetation density. This aligns with Gloger’s rule, which predicts darker colours in drier and wetter environments [29]. These opposing findings may be due to differences in taxonomic scope, i.e., focusing on a smaller group versus encompassing many groups that differ in several life-history characteristics or the metrics of colouration used [9], or even the inclusion or exclusion of sexual selection in the analyses. Resolving them will require considering the different dimensions in which colouration can vary and having more data from groups comprising a wide range of conditions, but sharing life histories to control for some possible confounding factors.

The evolution of colour diversification, that is, variation in hue (e.g. yellow to orange or red), and in chroma (e.g. colour saturation/intensity) in bird plumage is not linear and involves increases and decreases in colouration within evolutionary lineages [3, 30–33], mostly related to the advantages and disadvantages of crypsis and conspicuousness. Sexual selection appears as a significant factor for greater elaboration in plumage colouration [2, 3, 34], particularly in the case of the evolution of carotenoid-based colouration [4]. Also, changes in colouration may often be done to selection in females rather than in males [34], so when we consider sex differences in colouration, it must be regarded as that increases and decreases in colouration may be as much the result of sexual selection acting on males as natural selection acting on females [6, 35]. In birds, the diverse range of plumage colours is caused by the deposition of pigments (mostly melanins and carotenoids) or due to interference patterns of light in structural colours, which can also combine with pigments [7, 8]. Carotenoid colouration has been particularly implicated in sexual signalling due to the possibility of originating honest signals of individual quality, given the associated roles carotenoids play in immune function, vitamin A synthesis, and acting as antioxidants [36–40]. The key issue was how a colouration could be an honest signal of condition or quality. The trade-off hypothesis for carotenoid honest signalling [41, 42] did not obtain support from data [38]. An alternative index hypothesis was proposed, suggesting that carotenoid colouration can serve as an index signal of metabolic and cellular function [38, 43, 44]. However, if carotenoids were also subject to chemical transformations within individuals, rendering the index unfakable [36, 37, 43]. Part of the carotenoid-based colours result from the direct deposition of untransformed carotenoids, as it happens with most of the yellow colouration [36]. Only red and some yellow (e.g. canary xanthophylls) carotenoids, which undergo a conversion process before being deposited in feathers or other tissues [23, 36, 45], are appropriate candidates for index signals of condition, as it was confirmed [36]. Consequently, based on the index signalling hypothesis, red colouration is expected to be much more frequent in males than in females if colouration is being sexually selected [32].

The Cardinals (Cardinalidae) are a monophyletic group of passerine birds, broadly distributed from North to South America and inhabiting a wide range of environments, from tropical to temperate [46, 47]. They are also highly variable in the degree of sexual dichromatism, in migratory behaviour, and vary considerably in diet. Most species are monogamous with biparental care [46]. The diversity of characteristics (e.g. body size, habitat type, migratory behaviours) among cardinal species makes this group an exceptional model for testing hypotheses related to colour evolution in birds and assessing the importance of environmental factors and sexual selection. Recent research investigated cardinals’ colour evolution in terms of ecology, with findings that males with more elaborated plumages inhabit open to semi-open areas and that migratory behaviour was negatively associated with females’ plumage colouration [48]. However, the study did not consider sexual selection, which is one of the expected primary drivers of colour evolution in birds, thereby leaving out a key driver of colour evolution [2, 4, 32].

We used cardinals, a diversely coloured group of birds, to test the relative importance of sexual and natural selection in the evolution of plumage colouration. We aimed to determine (1) if sexual selection could explain colour evolution in cardinals; (2) if natural selection, through ecological and morphological factors, could instead or complementarily explain that evolution; (3) if there was a colour source sex bias and if it could be explained by index signalling, for which we expect to find a greater prevalence of red carotenoids among male patches.

Methods

Colour measurements

Plumage colour measurements were obtained from bird skins at the American Museum of Natural History and Carnegie Museum with reflectance spectrophotometry. Five individuals of each sex were chosen to represent each species. This number could not be reached for females of five species, as all available exemplars were used (see Supporting Information: Dataset 3 for the number of specimens per species and sex). Colouration was measured in six body regions: belly, breast, throat, crown, back, and rump (see Supporting Information: Dataset 4). Three readings were taken in each area, with the probe raised and slightly moved between readings. Reflectance values were extracted using a spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics USB4000), connected to a deuterium-halogen light source (DT-Mini-2-GS, Ocean Optics), through a Y-shaped probe (Ocean Optics, Dunedin, FL), which was mounted in a holder to keep it at 3.5 mm from the feathers (38- mm2 measuring area), following procedures as in Leitão, Ferreira [49]. A total of 220 male and 211 female skins were measured from 44 species, according to the species classification presented in the phylogeny published by Barker, Burns [47].

Colour measurements were first processed using the pavo package 2.9.0 [50] in R [51], with each spectrum restricted to the bird-visible wavelength range (300–700 nm). Then, we averaged the three readings per patch and performed curve smoothing (span = 0.2, prospect function). Colour variables were calculated using the TETRACOLORSPACE [8] program in Matlab software based on UVS birds’ visual models (cardinals are UVS birds [52]) [53, 54]. We processed the spectra for the six plumage patches measured and obtained three parameters that describe the plumage colouration of each bird: colour volume, achieved chroma, and brilliance. Colour volume measures the colour diversity of the bird by estimating the minimum convex polygon that could be drawn in the tetrahedron containing all the colour points measured among all six patches [8]. Achieved chroma is a measure of saturation, represented by the length of the vector from the achromatic origin to the data point, but adjusted to the polygon shape of the tetrahedron being estimated as a ratio [8]. Higher values of achieved chroma represent a more intense colour (e.g. red) than lower scores (e.g. black/grey). Brilliance is calculated from the reflectance spectra as the mean reflectance, obtained by summing the photons reflected across the entire spectrum (300 nm to 700 nm) and dividing by the number of measured points. This measure represents how bright each patch can be, with lower scores associated with darker colours and higher scores representing more luminous ones. We calculated sexual dichromatism, which is considered a proxy for sexual selection [2], by measuring the average colour span of each sex and calculating the difference between them. Average colour span measures the Euclidean distance between each pair of colours in a colour space, which can be plotted as a three-dimensional triangle with the angles represented by red, green, blue, and UV, or it can be illustrated in a 2D sphere (Fig. 1). This variable calculates the average distance among those points (here 12 in total, resulting in 6 pairs), which results in a scale where fewer plumage differences between males and females correspond to lower scores. In contrast, higher scores correspond to greater dichromatism. We are aware that the use of sexual dichromatism is not ideal, particularly when testing the evolutionary diversification of colouration in both sexes. But, alternative measures related to sexual selection are not necessarily better [9] due to the rarity of data available for many of the species considered. Still, we could confirm that sexual dichromatism, as we measured it, was not correlated with any of the colour metrics we tested.

We also evaluated pigment types and carotenoid colours among patches per sex, characterising them based on spectral curves [55, 56], hue measurement (θ), and RGB scores (for carotenoid colour categorisation) based on our spectra measurements (pavo package). We considered five categories of colour sources: melanin, carotenoids, which are responsible for yellow, orange, and red colours; melanin with carotenoids; white (absence of pigmentation); and structural colour. Based on this classification, we categorised each sex and whether it possessed (present/absent) any of the six analysed patches coloured in red, orange or yellow for all 44 species included in the phylogeny. Differences among these three colours can be primarily identified in the spectral curves, as they typically have distinct peaks (yellow at around 570 nm, orange at 600 nm, and red at 650 nm) [7]. Besides that, hue measurements and RGB scores were used in parallel to confirm the colours. Reds and yellows are highly discrepant, as orange typically appears in species with red and yellow patches that are connected (e.g., Piranga rubriceps has a yellow belly, orange breast and throat, and a red crown).

Ecological and morphological data

We considered six factors as possible predictors of colour evolution between species and sexes. These were: body mass per sex that we took from published sources [46, 57, 58], sexual dichromatism as a proxy for sexual selection (difference of average span between sexes, as described above), habitat cover (open, semi-open, and closed), migratory behaviour, divided into (1) sedentary species, (2) short, and (3) medium-long distant migrants [4, 46]. We also included a score of net primary productivity [59] related to the available resources in a species’ environment. We obtained data for these variables for 38 cardinal species.

Phylogenetic comparative analyses

We performed multiple phylogenetic generalised least squares (PGLS) using the caper package [60] in R [51] to determine which factors shaped the evolution of plumage colouration in cardinals, allowing us to control for the effects of phylogenetic relationships between species. This analysis adjusts the lambda value in each regression to control for the phylogenetic signal. Moreover, to account for phylogenetic uncertainty, we used multiple trees (1000) in our PGLS models, obtained from Birdtree-Hackett Stage 2 [61], which included 38 cardinal species out of 44 present in Barker et al. phylogeny [47]. This difference is attributed to the absence of sequenced data for some species at Birdtree at the time of analysis, as well as the lack of morphological/ecological data for a few species. Multiple PGLS models were performed to test for the effect of natural and sexual selection-related predictors on the variation of colouration between species, separately for each sex. For this, colouration measures were averaged per species and sex. Every model included habitat cover, net primary productivity, migratory status, body mass, and sexual dichromatism as predictors, and one of the three colour traits as the response variable, with one model per dependent variable. Colour volume was log-transformed to achieve normal distribution. We tested for multicollinearity (VIF - variation inflation factor, performance package [62] between the predictors, which was found to be low (correlation index: VIF < 3.4). Individual phylogenetic signal (Pagel’s lambda) was estimated for the average plumage parameters (colour volume, achieved chroma, and brilliance) for which lambda was weight-averaged across the 1000 phylogenetic trees.

To determine whether the body percentage of carotenoids in both sexes was correlated, we performed a simple PGLS, in which the average carotenoid percentage of males was the predictor and the average carotenoid percentage of females was the response. We also tested for the type of colour expression distribution between the sexes. For the index signal hypothesis, red colouration should be more present in males than in females as they are more likely index signals of individual quality [38], whereas, for the trade-off hypothesis, it is irrelevant which type of carotenoids are expressed. We then run Bayesian phylogenetic logistic mixed models implemented in the package brms [63] to analyse the effect of sex, as a predictor, on the occurrence of yellow (present/absent) and red (present/absent) carotenoid-based colours separately. Dependent variables were assigned a Bernoulli distribution with a logit link function, as each species either had or did not have red/yellow plumage colouration. We included phylogeny through a phylogenetic covariance matrix (calculated using the vcv command of the ape package [64]) as a random factor to account for the lack of independence among related species. We defined flat priors for sex and a Student_t (3, 0, 20) for the covariance matrix. For each model, we obtained posterior distributions of all parameters by running four chains in parallel for 2,000 iterations, discarding the first 500 iterations as burn-in. The models converged, with Rhat = 1.0 in both cases. Results were presented as likelihoods.

Results

Effects of natural and sexual selection on male and female colouration

The evolution of colouration in cardinals reveals significant differences between the sexes (Table 1; Fig. 2). In males, sexual selection has a significant impact on colour evolution, as it is positively associated with colour volume (p < 0.0001), a measure of colour diversity, and plumage brilliance (p = 0.001). There was also a negative but marginally non-significant association between males’ colour volume and body mass (p = 0.058), with larger males being less colored than in smaller species. Male cardinals are more colourful and bright in species where sexual selection is more intense. Achieved chroma did not present significant associations with the tested predictors.

Habitat cover primarily affected female colouration evolution, as this predictor was associated with the three colour parameters (Table 1; Fig. 2). As in males, females’ colour volume and brilliance were positively associated with sexual dichromatism (respectively: p < 0.0001 and p < 0.015), which relates to sexual selection. Habitat cover was negatively associated with female colour volume (p = 0.002), average chroma (p = 0.005), and brilliance (p = 0.018). Thus, females with more diverse colouration, characterised by highly saturated colours and brighter plumages, tend to live in more open habitats. Brilliance was also negatively associated with net primary productivity (p = 0.034), indicating that brighter females are more likely to be found in less productive habitats.

Individual analyses of the phylogenetic signal of each trait showed that achieved chroma and brilliance had a moderate phylogenetic signal in both sexes (Table 2). For colour volume, there was an almost null phylogenetic signal for males and a moderate one for females, indicating that the evolution of this trait is highly variable in males.

Sexual selection and pigments

There were some perceived differences in the proportion of colour sources between the sexes (Fig. 2). Carotenoids were the main pigment type present in males (39.47%) (Supporting information), while melanin was the most common in females (60.09%). Also, only males presented structural colours. A simple PGLS revealed that the average percentages of carotenoids in males and females were positively associated (βst = 0.747, p < 0.0001). However, the distribution of carotenoid pigments suggests that males tend to express red more often, while females tend to express yellow more often (Fig. 3). We tested this possibility by measuring the presence of red and yellow colouration in the feathers of the two sexes for the entire 44 species. We found that males had a much higher likelihood of expressing red (Sex: posterior Mean = 4.49, lower 95%CI = 2.49, upper 95%CI = 6.93), while there was little difference between the sexes in the presence of yellow colouration, although with a slightly higher likelihood for being expressed by females (Sex: Posterior Mean = -1.57, lower 95%CI = -3.18, upper 95%CI = -0.14). This result is expected by the index signal hypothesis concerning carotenoid colouration. Carotenoid-based red colouration can be found in all measured body regions in males but only in the crown and throat in females.

Discussion

Our study on the evolution of colouration in cardinals revealed that it was shaped by several selective factors related to natural and sexual selection. Our results indicate that the colouration of cardinals is predicted by sexual selection in both sexes, and natural selection variables affect females only. The results also reveal that there are a greater number of factors explaining the evolution of female colouration than those of males, even though females are much less diverse in colouration than males.

The colour diversity of males was affected by sexual selection and body mass (marginally non-significant). Male cardinals subject to stronger sexual selection (as measured by sexual dichromatism) have more colourful and brighter plumages, as indicated by colour volume and brilliance. Additionally, smaller males tended to have more chromatic (saturated) plumage colouration. These results align with previous findings that show that body size [9] may constrain colour complexity. Many studies have shown the association between colour diversity and body size [2, 21, 34]. However, this is not immediately apparent due to mixed results. Considering the significant proportion of carotenoid colouration in cardinal males, this tendency agrees with previous findings in Australian passerines [23]. This may result from larger species ingesting proportionally less food, including carotenoids, which will become less available [65].

It is well established that sexual selection leads to the evolution of male-biased secondary sexual characters due to the advantages males obtain from being preferred by females [7, 66, 67]. Sexual dichromatism results from this differential selection between the sexes. We used sexual dichromatism as a measure of the intensity of sexual selection, as it has a very strong basis [4]. We found that colour diversity in males was strongly and almost exclusively predicted by sexual dichromatism, indicating that the evolution of more complex and varied colours in male plumage in cardinals evolved under sexual selection. This agrees with other findings that place sexual selection as the primary predictor of the evolution of colour ornamentation in birds [2, 68]. Male colour saturation and brightness were not associated with the natural selection factors considered; however, brilliance was positively associated with sexual selection, indicating that males are brighter in species with more intense sexual selection.

Both natural and sexual selection factors explain the evolution of female colouration. Colour diversity was predicted by sexual dichromatism and habitat cover. Females from species presenting greater sexual dichromatism also have higher colour diversity. This result does not support a common assumption that females evolve less elaborate plumages when sexual selection selects high visibility in males, consequently increasing the sexual dichromatism between the sexes [2, 35, 69]. Our results can be well explained by the existence of a genetic correlation between the sexes, so that the selection of colouration in males has a carryover effect on females [70]. When conspicuousness is selectively disadvantageous for females, evolution against it should occur, requiring a break in the genetic correlation between trait expression and males to cancel trait expression, which the comparatively decreased colourfulness in females indicates is common. Although blocking the expression of colouration in feathers is possible and not complicated developmentally [71], females would still need to express other colours, a modification that is certainly evolutionarily more complicated [72]. The expression of carotenoid colouration is correlated between the sexes in cardinals. Another possibility is that female colouration evolved independently through social selection so that the presence of conspicuous colours results from a positive selection related to a role in social interactions [73, 74]. These types of signals can evolve in females in cases where sexual selection also acts on females [75] or when colouration traits are indicators of age [76], signal condition [75, 77], or are used in social dominance and competition [78]. Evidence indicates that ornaments can be as informative in females as in males [79]. Investigating the role of signals in females should be considered independently, rather than assuming they are present through genetic correlation with selection on males [80]. Considering that, it would be interesting to conduct an ancestral-state reconstruction of plumage colour complexity for each sex in this group, to understand when these changes began, and to determine if there is an association between carotenoid colour expression and sex.

Female colour volume was also negatively associated with habitat cover, so species inhabiting closed habitats have less diverse plumage colouration. Cryptic and less elaborate colours, such as dull yellows and brown shades, are less detectable in darker environments, thus reducing predation risk [32]. This could explain the association between colour diversity and the habitat structure of cardinal females.

Female achieved chroma was negatively related to habitat cover, meaning that females of species inhabiting more closed habitats tended to have less saturated colours, which may be associated with the evolution of camouflage in environments where chromatic colours are more detectable. Again, this result is not in agreement with the findings of Cooney, He [9], where female colouration was found to increase with precipitation and net productivity, in a very large assemblage of bird species. The colouration of female cardinals evolved towards reduced conspicuousness in more closed habitats. This result also highlights the importance of considering the evolution of very plastic traits, such as colouration, in specific groups, since the general trends revealed in wider studies will certainly include exceptions worth understanding.

Females inhabiting more densely vegetated habitats or with higher net primary productivity were less bright. Our results align with Gloger’s rule, which predicts that darker colours are more common in tropical regions with higher productivity and denser vegetation [24, 29].

The phylogenetic signal in the chroma and brilliance of both sexes was moderate. In contrast, the phylogenetic signal in colour volume was moderate to low in females and practically non-existent in males, indicating a high plasticity in the evolution of colour elaboration in males and not so much in females. These moderate to non-existent phylogenetic signals are rare, with other studies having found considerably higher values for specific groups [10, 81, 82]. Thus, in Cardinals, male colouration seems to evolve in variable directions, independent of phylogenetic constraints, and primarily due to sexual selection [4, 83].

These results highlight the importance of considering the evolution of male and female colouration, as phylogenetic relationships can significantly impact both sexes [79].

Sex and carotenoids as a sexual signal

Colouration in birds serves several different roles, from mating and social interactions to environmental protection through crypsis [32]. Carotenoids have been identified as the primary source of colouration implicated in sexual signalling [7, 84, 85], as they can serve as indicator signals of immune conditions [86] or general metabolic function [23, 36]. We identified the main sources of colouration in male and female cardinals, which include birds with very exuberant colours. Males presented a much wider range of colours than females, according to colour volume (Fig. 1). Carotenoids are more often used as the primary source of colours in males, particularly in regions relevant to face-to-face social interactions (crown, throat, and breast) [4, 79, 87]. This supports the use of carotenoid colouration in sexual signalling in cardinals, with males investing more in carotenoid colouration. According to the index signalling hypothesis, not all carotenoids carry the same signalling values, considering the metabolic costs in their transformation when not directly deposited in feathers [36]. When considering the use of yellow, which mainly results from the deposition of untransformed carotenoids in this group of birds, and red, which is the result of a metabolic transformation of the ingested carotenoids [36], we found that males are much more often red than females. At the same time, the latter are more often yellow. Red colours are a derived condition, as they almost always evolved from previously present yellow colouration in the evolutionary line [32]. Our results agree with the index signalling hypothesis but not with the trade-off hypothesis, as only the former predicts a sex difference in the type of carotenoid expression as the one we found, with males expressing more red colours, which work better as indicators of condition and metabolic ability to transform ingested carotenoids [36, 43, 88], associating reds to greater male quality [4, 23].

Conclusion

Our study reveals that cardinals’ colouration evolved under both sexual and natural selection, with male and female colouration indicating that they have been affected by similar factors, although not all in the same way. Colour diversity was influenced by sexual selection in both sexes, and it evolved mainly independently of phylogenetic history in males. While the evolution of male colouration has been extensively studied, much less is known about the evolution of female colouration. Here, we found that female colouration was affected by both environmental factors and sexual selection, with strong selection for less conspicuous colouration in rich and densely vegetated environments, typically tropical regions. Our results highlight the importance of studying the evolution of female colouration as a subject. We also found that the evolution of the type of pigment used in colour expression differs between the sexes in that red carotenoids, which are more costly to express, are more often present in males. This supports the idea that metabolic costs associated with colour expression can render carotenoid colouration an index signal in the evolution of sexual signalling [38]. Taken together, our results indicate that different selective forces determined the evolution of colour in males and females of cardinals, with sexual selection acting similarly in both sexes.
