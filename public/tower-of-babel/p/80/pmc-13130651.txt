A harmless-to-harmful switchable and spatiotemporally activated nano-CRISPR hierarchically amplifies ferroptosis in melanoma
Authors: Chunqing Ou, Xianzhou Huang, Dongxue Huang, Rui Luo, Ruhan Chen, Xinchao Li, Xinyue Wu, Qinjie Wu, Changyang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFerroptosis therapy faces challenges due to low lipid peroxide (LPO) levels. Herein, we develop a harmless-to-harmful switchable and spatiotemporally activated nano-CRISPR system (termed ARCHER) that sequentially amplifies ferroptosis sensitivity, iron ion levels, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) to amplify ferroptosis therapy efficiency. ARCHER targets cancer cells and releases CRISPR-Cas9 and Ce6Fe(III)Cl in response to hyaluronidase and tumor acidity. CRISPR-Cas9 sustains effective suppression of LPO-reducing protein GPX4. By persistently enhancing LPO accumulation through GPX4 downregulation, ARCHER primes cancer cells for ferroptosis and sensitizes them to subsequent interventions. Upon laser irradiation under acidic conditions, Ce6Fe(III)Cl undergoes spatiotemporal activation, transforming from inert form into cytotoxic Fe(III) ions and Ce6-generated ROS. Liberated Fe(III) and ROS synergistically amplify lipid peroxidation, driving LPO accumulation to trigger ferroptosis storm in sensitized cancer cells. In vivo studies demonstrated that ARCHER achieves 60% (3/5) tumor ablation with minimal off-target effects, validating its high therapeutic efficacy in ferroptosis-driven cancer treatment.Keywords: harmless-harmful switchable, spatiotemporally activated, ferroptosis, CRISPR/Cas9, cancer therapy

Introduction

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death form, has sparked great interest in treatment of cancer, which is caused by lipid membrane rupture due to the overwhelming accumulation of lipid peroxide (LPO).1,2,3,4 Ferroptosis heavily relies on the intracellular iron ion level and the reactive oxygen species (ROS) level.5,6 The iron-mediated Fenton reaction converts endogenous H2O2 to highly toxic ROS, and ROS then oxidize lipids to generate LPO to rupture the lipid membrane, resulting in ferroptosis.7,8,9,10 Thus, iron ion supplementation and ROS generation to increase LPO production have been developed to induce cancer ferroptosis.11,12,13,14 However, the accumulation of LPO is rigorously regulated by exceptionally low levels of homeostatic systems within cells, resulting in decreased ferroptosis efficiency.15,16,17,18,19 In particular, in plastic malignancies like melanoma, the therapeutic efficacy of ferroptotic therapy is limited because different subtypes are variably vulnerable to LPO-induced cytotoxicity, and the melanocytic and transitory subtype was less vulnerable to LPO.20,21,22,23 Hence, the interruption of homeostatic systems is a potential approach to increase ferroptosis efficiency.

Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a crucial molecule in the homeostatic systems of ferroptosis.24,25,26 GPX4 is a professional antidote for LPO to protect cells from ferroptosis,27,28,29,30 which selectively arrests the excessive accumulation of LPO by reducing toxic LPO to nontoxic lipoalcohols with the help of glutathione (GSH).31,32,33,34 To interrupt GPX4 for sensitizing ferroptosis, small-molecule drugs and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) have been widely applied in research, which has encouraged progress, whereas the regulation of these strategies is temporary and has a risk of drug resistance.35,36,37,38,39,40 In contrast to small-molecule drugs and siRNAs, the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) system inhibits the target protein at the genome level, which has the advantage of permanent and heritable suppression of the target protein.41,42,43 Thus, by utilizing the CRISPR/Cas9 system to target the Gpx4 locus (CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4), GPX4 in tumor cells can be permanently disrupted. CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4, which mediates stable ferroptosis sensitivity passes down to the daughter cancer cells to further enhance response to other ferroptosis therapeutics.

To address these challenges, we developed a harmless-harmful switchable and spatiotemporally activated nano-CRISPR (termed ARCHER) for melanoma treatment, which was designed to increase ferroptosis sensitivity, iron overload, and ROS generation in a controlled manner (Figure 1). ARCHER co-delivers CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 (to disrupt ferroptosis homeostasis) and Fe(III)-chelated chlorin e6 (Ce6Fe(III)Cl) (a dual-functional ROS/iron generator), which remains inert until it is activated by laser irradiation in the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment. Guided by a cancer-targeting peptide (Cys-R8-c[RGDfK]), hyaluronic acid (HA), and Ce6Fe(III)Cl, ARCHER selectively accumulates in tumor tissues and is internalized by cancer cells.44,45 Following lysosomal escape mediated by hyaluronidase in acidic lysosomes, CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 translocates to the nucleus and permanently edits the GPX4 gene, resulting in sustained GPX4 downregulation. GPX4-deficient cancer cells and their progeny exhibit hypersensitization to ferroptosis, a precondition for amplifying subsequent therapeutic responses. Under spatially and temporally controlled conditions (i.e., laser irradiation in an acidic microenvironment post-GPX4 suppression), the inert Ce6Fe(III)Cl complex is activated to release Fe3+ and generate ROS. Fe3+ depletes intracellular GSH via redox cycling and propagates Fenton reactions, while ROS synergistically accelerate LPO accumulation, culminating a ferroptotic cascade. ARCHER achieved 60% tumor ablation in preclinical models, demonstrating significantly enhanced ferroptosis efficacy. Importantly, in normal tissues lacking laser activation and acidic conditions, Ce6Fe(III)Cl scavenges superoxide radicals (O2−⋅) to suppress off-target ferroptosis, thereby ensuring tumor-specific cytotoxicity.46

Results

Synthesis and characterization of synthetic polymers

The synthesis and characterization of fluorinated polyethyleneimine 1.8 kDa (PF33) were previously reported.47 Briefly, polyethyleneimine 1.8 kDa (PEI 1.8K) was fluorinated via reaction with heptafluorobutyric anhydride in anhydrous methanol under a nitrogen atmosphere. PF33 was confirmed by 19F-NMR [D2O, 400 MHz: δ −81.07 (t, J = 8.7 Hz, 3F), −118.52 (q, J = 8.6 Hz, 2F), −127.61 (2F)] and FT-IR spectroscopy (C-F stretching at 1,225 cm−1) (Figures S1A and S1B).

While ROS and Fe2+ are critical mediators of ferroptosis, exogenous Fe2+ and Ce6 have limitations in terms of safety, solubility, stability, tumor-targeting capability, and effective accumulation at tumor sites. To address these challenges, we designed RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl, a multifunctional polymer leveraging tumor-specific biomarkers (e.g., hyaluronidase overexpression and acidic pH) to increase ROS/Fe2+ accumulation in cancer cells. The synthesis of Ce6Fe(III)Cl was achieved by chelating Ce6 with Fe3+ under reducing conditions (FeCl2 and L-ascorbic acid in acetone at 37°C). Successful chelation was confirmed by 1H-NMR (Figure S1C), ESI-MS (Figure S1D), and XPS (Figure 1B), with the XPS spectra verifying the Fe3+ oxidation state. Next, hyaluronic acid (HA) was functionalized with adipic dihydrazide (ADH) via carboxyl group activation to form HA-ADH (Figure S1E). Subsequent conjugation of Ce6Fe(III)Cl to HA-ADH yielded HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl, which was further modified with Mal-PEG2000-NHS and the RGD-R8 peptide through Michael addition (Figures 1C and S1E–S1G). Structural validation of the intermediates and the final polymer was performed via 1H-NMR and FTIR (Figures 2D and S2A–S2F). UV-vis spectra (Figure S2G) revealed identical absorption peaks for Ce6Fe(III)Cl and the final polymer, confirming the retention of Ce6’s photophysical properties. The Ce6Fe(III)Cl grafting efficiency in the polymer was quantified as 6.5%.

According to the results of the MTT assay (Figures S2H and S2I), PF33 showed a far lower level of toxicity than did PEI 25K and equal to that of PEI 1.8K, and RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl did not exhibit significant cytotoxicity without laser administration. The preparation and characterization of ARCHER

ARCHER was fabricated via a two-step self-assembly process (Figure 2F), designed to disassemble in response to hyaluronidase (HAase) in the tumor microenvironment (TME). The core complex (PF33/pX330) was first assembled through electrostatic interactions between cationic PF33 and plasmid DNA (pX330). Subsequent surface functionalization with HA-based components enabled a second assembly step to form ARCHER.

To evaluate TME responsiveness, ARCHER was incubated with HAase (15 mg/mL, 4 h). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed a size increase from 108 ± 3 nm (core) to 183 ± 3 nm (ARCHER), followed by HAase-triggered disassembly to 119 ± 6 nm (Figures 2G and 2H), closely matching the core size. Zeta potential measurements showed reversible charge inversion: from +20.6 ± 1.9 mV (core) to −15.3 ± 1.8 mV (ARCHER) and back to +14.9 ± 3.6 mV post-HAase treatment (Figure 2I). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) corroborated these size and charge trends (Figure 2J). Gel retardation assays confirmed the ability of PF33 to fully condense pX330 at a 0.5:1 mass ratio (Figure 2K). These results demonstrate the homogeneous nanoparticle formation, TME-responsive disassembly, and potential for controlled plasmid release of ARCHER. Moreover, considering that laser activation of Ce6(III)Cl leads to plasmid degradation, the intensities of the bands corresponding to the plasmids subjected to different processes were quantified, and the results revealed that, compared with free Ce6(III)Cl with HA/PF33/pX330, ARCHER could protect the plasmid from photodegradation (p < 0.001) (Figure S3A).

ARCHER showed high cellular uptake and transfection efficiency

The pX330 was labeled with YOYO-1 to track the plasmid for cellular uptake and distribution. The uptake efficiency of ARCHER was greater than that of HA/PF33/pX330 and RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/pX330 (Figures 3A and 3B). Similarly, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of ARCHER was significantly higher than that of HA/PF33/pX330 and RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/pX330 (Figure 3C). ARCHER exhibited better cell internalization for the subsequent release of gene cargo. This may result from the penetration of R8, RGD coupling to integrin αvβ3 receptor, and the coupling of Fe3+ to transferrin.

After plasmid DNA (pDNA) is internalized into cells via multiple-receptor-mediated endocytosis, the escape of pDNA from endosome/lysosome is a major obstacle to achieving high transfection efficiency. According to laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) images, more PF33/pX330 or ARCHER were entrapped within the lysosome during the first 0.5 h than PEI 25K/pX330. PEI 25K/pX330 was kept detained in the lysosome, and then a small portion was released until 4 h. PF33/pX330 and ARCHER escaped into the cytoplasm at 1 h, and a few entered the nucleus at 4 h (Figures 3D, S3B, and S3C). Compared with PEI 25K/pX330 and PF33/pX330, ARCHER displayed superior lysosomal escape capabilities.

Gene transfection efficiency plays a key role in gene therapy. pX459 (encoding green fluorescence protein [GFP]) was used to form ARCHER instead of pX330, to investigate the transfection performance in B16-F10 cells, and PEI 25K/pX459 and PEI 1.8K/pX459 were used as controls. The fluorescence signals of GFP in B16-F10 cells were shown in Figure 3E. PF33/pX459, HA/PF33/pX459, RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/pX459, and ARCHER exhibited higher transfection efficiency than did PEI 25K/pX459 and PEI 1.8K/pX459. In addition, ARCHER (75.6% ± 2.1%) was higher than HA/PF33/pX459 (69.2% ± 2.0%) and RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/pX459 (69.8% ± 4.1%) (Figures 3G and 3F). The MFI of GFP displayed similar results (Figure 3H). On the basis of the above results, excellent cellular internalization, superior lysosome escape capability, and potent transfection efficiency proved that ARCHER is a desirable CRISPR/Cas9 delivery system for knocking out a target protein in cancer cells.

Construction and optimization of CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4

Four pairs of targeted Gpx4 locus sgRNAs were designed, synthesized, and inserted into linear pX330 (Figures 3I and S3D). The obtained plasmids were named pCas-1, pCas-2, pCas-3, and pCas-4, respectively, and then the gene editing efficiency of CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 (Figure S3E) was determined via Sanger sequencing analysis after transfection by Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The pCas-4 containing sgRNA4 (GTCCAGGTCCACGGCGTCGC) showed the most manifest mutation peaks in the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products and was named CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 to be used as a therapeutic plasmid.

ARCHER has high gene editing efficiency

The gene editing efficiency of CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 was determined via Sanger sequencing analysis after transfection with ARCHER. As shown in Figures 4A and 4B, CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 showed manifest mutation peaks in the PCR products, and the insertion/deletion (indel) mutation was 33.3% determined by T7 endonuclease I (T7EI) digestion analysis. These results suggested that ARCHER had high gene editing efficiency for Gpx4 and had capacity to sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis.

ARCHER shows high anti-tumor efficiency in vitro

To assess the anti-tumor efficacy of ARCHER on ferroptosis-sensitive cell phenotypes, MTT and LIVE/DEAD assays were then performed with or without a 660 nm laser under 100 mW/cm2 for 1 min. An MTT assay was used to quantify the anti-tumor efficiency (Figure S4A). The cell viability of cancer cells treated with ARCHER was 28.4% lower than that of G2 (p = 0.0017), demonstrating that GPX4 suppression led to cytotoxicity. Cell viability of G7 (23.2% ± 1.2%) dropped by 56.1% (p < 0.0001) vs. G3 (79.3 ± 12.9%), indicating that cancer cells were more sensitive to subsequent interventions when GPX4 was suppressed. The cell viability of ARCHER + laser-treated cells was substantially lower (p = 0.0010) than that of ARCHER-treated cancer cells, indicating that ARCHER switched from harmless to harmful, leading to more effective therapy. Cell viability of the cancer cells treated with ARCHER + laser was 16.2% lower than that of G5 (39.5% ± 5.6%), demonstrating that active ARCHER released Fe3+ and improved therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, ARCHER also displayed cytotoxicity in CT26 cells (Figure S4B) and Panc02 cells (Figure S4C) with laser irradiation (p < 0.001). Furthermore, ARCHER did not exhibit obvious cytotoxicity in NIH3T3cells (a representative normal cell line; Figure S4D). NIH3T3cells displayed a poor uptake efficiency for ARCHER (14.4% ± 1.2%; Figure S4E), which leaded to GPX4 expression remaining nearly unaltered in NIH3T3cells (Figure S4F). With the brightest red fluorescence, the ARCHER + laser showed the strongest anti-tumor effect (Figures 4C and S5A). These findings demonstrated that laser irradiation switched harmless Ce6Fe(III)Cl to harmful Fe3+ and ROS, which further facilitated to kill ferroptosis-sensitive cancer cells.

Active ARCHER enhances ferroptosis efficiency by suppressing GPX4 and inducing ROS generation and iron release

To further investigate the harmless-to-harmful switchable ability of ARCHER, the cellular ROS of B16-F10 cells treated with different formulations were observed by fluorescence microscopy. The fluorescence of the DCF in ARCHER + laser-treated cells was significantly brighter than that in the RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 + laser-treated cells (Figure 4D), elucidating that ARCHER + laser led to more ROS generation, which suggested that harmless ARCHER could switch to harmful ROS in tumor cells with a laser.

To further confirm the ferroptosis efficiency of ARCHER in B16-F10 cells, typical ferroptosis biomarkers, mitochondria morphology, and lipid peroxidation (LPO) were measured. B16-F10 cells treated with ARCHER with laser displayed the brightest JC-1 green fluorescence and the weakest red fluorescence (Figure S5B). Compared with those in the other groups, the number of FITC-positive B16-F10 cells in the ARCHER with laser group was slightly greater, excluding G5 (Figure 4E), and the number of PE-positive of B16-F10 cells in ARCHER with laser group remained virtually unchanged (Figure S5C) by FCM. As shown in Figure 4F, the mitochondrial membrane potential of B16-F10 cells treated with ARCHER with laser was lower than that of B16-F10 cells in G1 (p < 0.001). As shown in the TEM images (Figure 4G), the mitochondria of B16-F10 cells were observably pyknotic, and the mitochondrial ridge of B16-F10 cells disappeared in the ARCHER + laser group, suggesting that active ARCHER induced cancer cells to undergo ferroptosis to exhibit great anti-cancer efficiency. These results revealed that laser-activated harmful ARCHER induced mitochondrial dysfunction to enhance B16-F10 cell ferroptosis.

LPO in B16-F10 cells that were administrated with different treatments was measured by LSCM and FCM. In the ARCHER with laser irradiation group, B16-F10 cells showed the brightest fluorescence of LPO compared with all the other groups (Figures 4F and S5D). Regardless of the presence or absence of Fe3+ and/or laser irradiation, LPO was clearly and comparably observed in cells with GPX4 deletion (G4, G5, G6, and G7), as shown in Figure 4H, delineating that GPX4 suppression led to ferroptosis. LPO was most prominently observed in cells treated with ARCHER + laser (29.5%), whereas LPO in G3 cells was 1.5% (p < 0.0001). The MFI of LPO also enhanced from 1.4 × 104 to 2.4 × 104 (ARCHER + laser vs. G3) (p < 0.0001). These results illustrated that active ARCHER induced cancer cells to undergo ferroptosis, while cells in G3 did not undergo ferroptosis, demonstrating that GPX4 suppression was the first step in initiating ferroptosis. The LPO-positive ratio in the ACHER + laser group was 3.1-fold higher than that in the ARCHER group, which elucidated that active ACHER augmented ferroptosis in ferroptosis-sensitive cancer cells by Fe(III)-ROS storm. The LPO-positive ratio in ARCHER + laser group was 2.0-fold higher than that in the G5 group, which demonstrated that the release of Fe3+ by active ARCHER facilitated the induction of ferroptosis by ROS. The concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) in B16-F10 cells treated with harmful ARCHER also increased, indicating that the cells were undergoing ferroptosis (Figure S5E).

The western blot result showed that the expression of GPX4 was most strongly suppressed after B16-F10 cells were treated with ARCHER + laser (Figure S5F). ARCHER + laser treatment not only actively inhibited the expression of GPX4 through CRISPR/Cas9 but also indirectly inhibited the expression of GPX4 through Fe3+ and ROS. Importantly, laser irradiation induces immediate iron release and ROS generation, and these effects are maintained for at least 6 h under conditions of GPX4 suppression (Figures S5G–S5I).

These results elucidated that the high transfection efficiency of ARCHER led to the sustained effective suppression of GPX4 to prime cancer cells and sensitize them to subsequent interventions. Additionally, the laser switched the harmless Ce6Fe(III) of ARCHER to harmful Fe3+ and ROS in an acidic environment to disrupt the homeostasis of oxidation and the labile iron pool. Finally, ARCHER + laser elicited a ferroptosis storm.

ARCHER targets and accumulates in tumor tissues

To investigate the biodistribution and accumulation of ARCHER, in vivo tracking was performed. After intravenous (i.v.) injection, the fluorescence distribution in the cancer-bearing mouse model was observed at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h. The majority of ARCHER was accumulated in the tumor tissue and reached a maximum accumulation at 12 h after i.v. injection (5.5 × 108 p/s) (Figures 5A and 5B), which suggested that a laser should be administered after 12 h of i.v. injection. ARCHER can efficiently target and accumulate in tumor tissue through HA coupling to CD44, RGD coupling to integrin αvβ3 receptor, and Fe3+ coupling to transferrin, and R8 enhances permeability.

ARCHER has powerful anti-tumor effects in vivo by inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells

We further appraised the anti-cancer efficiency of ARCHER in a subcutaneous xenograft cancer mouse model of B16-F10 cells. When the cancer size approached ∼100 mm3, the therapeutic regimens were executed as shown in Figure 5C. For groups with laser irradiation, the irradiation was administrated at cancer sites 12 h post-injection for 10 min (660 nm, 50 mW/cm2). The therapies were administered every 3 days for a total of three times. As shown in the cancer volume curve (Figures 5D and 5E), the cancer growth of G6, G7, ARCHER, and ARCHER +laser groups was much slower than that of the other control groups (G1, G2, and G3), suggesting that the growth of tumors was inhibited only when CRIPSRCas9-GPX4 was efficiently transported and worked. Compared with that in the G5, cancer growth in the ARCHER + laser group was slower, and the cancer volume in the ARCHER + laser group was obviously shrunk on day 13 (p = 0.0058) and day 16 (p = 0.0013), which illustrated that GPX4 knockout was the initiation step for ferroptosis. Compared with that of the G6 (with a tumor volume of 486.2 ± 111.5 mm3 on day 16), the growth of the ARCHER group (with a tumor volume of 285.3 ± 88.5 mm3 on day 16) was much slower, indicating that the enhanced accumulation of Fe3+ by coupling with transferrin could enhance GPX4 suppression to improve ferroptosis sensitivity in cancer tissues. As shown in Figures 5D–5F, compared with that in the ARCHER group, the cancer growth was slower on day 16 (p = 0.028), and the cancer volume was obviously shrunk and even ablated in the ARCHER + laser group when the treatments were terminated, which suggested that involving of Fe in ARCHER could significantly boost the generation of Fe3+ and ROS to elicit ferroptosis storms in ferroptosis-sensitive cancer cells. Consistently, the ARCHER + laser group presented the lowest cancer weight (Figure 5G).

Additionally, the overall survival rate of the mice that received ARCHER + laser treatment was 2/7, whereas that of the other groups was 0/7. The median survival period of the mice in ARCHER + laser group was prolonged to 42 days, whereas those in the other groups were 16 days (G1), 19 days (G2, G3, G4, and G5), 28 days (G6), 32 days (G7), and 31 days (ARCHER group).

These results demonstrated that ARCHER induced cascading amplification of ferroptosis via sustained GPX4 suppression and a laser-activated Fe3+/ROS generator. ARCHER was targeted and accumulated by HA, the RGD-R8 peptide, and sustainedly downregulated GPX4. GPX4-deficient cancer cells and their progeny initiate ferroptosis and are sensitized to ferroptosis. In an acidic microenvironment post-GPX4 suppression, a laser was administrated, and an inert Ce6Fe(III)Cl complex was activated to release Fe3+ and generate ROS, synergistically accelerating LPO accumulation, culminating in a ferroptosis cascade.

To further reveal the changes in cancer tissue in each group, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunohistochemical staining (IHC) of GPX4, Ki-67 staining, and terminal deoxynucleotidyl-transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) analysis were carried out. As illustrated in Figure 6, compared with all the other treatment groups, the ARCHER + laser group showed the sparsest nucleus, the lowest of GPX4 expression, and the highest proliferation inhibition (Figures 6A, S6A, and S6B). Meanwhile, the ARCHER + laser group exhibited the highest level of apoptosis (Figures 6B and S6C), compared to all the other treatment groups. These results were in accordance with the results of the subcutaneous treatment described above, proving that ARCHER induced ferroptosis in cancer tissues and inhibited cancer cell proliferation to slow down the progression of cancer.

Moreover, we also investigated anti-cancer efficiency of ARCHER in a subcutaneous xenograft cancer mouse model with CT26 cells and Panc02 cells. For CT26-cell-bearing BALB/c mice, when the cancer size approached 100 mm3, the therapeutic regimen was executed as in Figure S7A. The cancer volume curves were shown in Figures S7B and S7C. Cancer growth was severely inhibited in the treatment of ARCHER + laser group (Figure S7D), and the cancer weight was also the minimum (Figure S7E). As shown in Figures S7F–S7H, H&E staining and IHC staining of GPX4 and Ki-67 in CT26 tumor tissues showed similar results to those of B16-F10 tumor tissues. For the Panc02-cell-bearing C57BL/6 mice, as shown in Figure S8, the cancer volume curve in the treatment of ARCHER + laser group was the slowest, cancer growth was severely inhibited, and the cancer weight was also the minimum. These results demonstrated that the laser-activated harmful ARCHER can also be applied in the treatment of other cancers.

Potential toxicity evaluation

Hematological and histological analyses were conducted for the toxicity evaluation. Compared with those in the saline-treated group, blood chemistry did not differ after treatment (Figure S9A). In addition, neither body weight nor organ tissues showed apparent abnormalities after such laser irradiation therapy (Figures S9B and S9C).

Additionally, the results of the complete blood count and blood biochemistry of the mice administered a high dose of ARCHER (3× therapeutic dose) were within the reference range (Figures S10A and S10B).48 The important organ tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) also did not exhibit apparent abnormalities (Figure S10C). The gene encoding GPX4 and three potential off-target sites were not edited, revealing that ARCHER showed no off-target effects on the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys (Figure S11). These results suggested that the ARCHER had good biosafety.

Discussion

In this study, we developed ARCHER, which is a TEM-responsive and spatiotemporally activated nano-CRISPR system, to specifically target cancer cells. ARCHER synergistically delivers the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated GPX4 knockout megaplasmid and laser-triggered harmless-to-harmful switchable Fe3+ and ROS generator to amplify ferroptosis in only cancer cells. ARCHER combines hierarchical targeting, spatiotemporally controlled release, permanently sensitive to ferroptosis, and a responsive redox switch to overcome major limitations of conventional ferroptosis inducers, such as insufficient tumor selectivity, off-target toxicity, and transient Fe2+/ROS activity.

Mechanistically, RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl acts as the multifunctional outer layer of ARCHER, which enables tumor tissue accumulation through HA/CD44, RGD/integrin αvβ3, and Fe3+/transferrin by receptor-ligand interactions; promotes cells penetration via RGD and R8; and undergoes HAase-triggered disassembly within the TME.13,45,49,50,51,52 After the outer layer falls off, the fluorinated polyethyleneimine PF33 exhibits a stronger plasmid encapsulation capacity and lysosome escape performance, resulting in high CRISPR-Cas9 intracellular delivery efficiency. This might be due to “the fluorine effect” of fluorine atoms enhancing the lipid solubility of nanoparticles and the interaction between nanoparticles and cell membranes.53,54 Finally, Gpx4 gene disruption (33.3% indel frequency) effectively enhances the susceptibility of tumor cells to ferroptosis.

Upon 660 nm laser irradiation, Ce6Fe(III)Cl is photodegraded and produces ROS-bioavailable Fe(II/III), resulting in an increase in LPO (29.5% vs. controls, p < 0.0001) and GSH consumption. Compared with small-molecule ferroptosis inducers such as erastin or RSL3, spatiotemporally activated ARCHER overcomes the strict redox balance and off-target effects through sustained GPX4 depletion followed by localized Fe(III)-ROS production.55,56 Finally, this cascade strategy induces mitochondrial pyknosis and LPO accumulation, resulting in tumor ablation in vivo. The reduced tumor volume and prolonged survival (median 42 days vs. 16–32 days in controls, p = 0.0013) indicate high ferroptosis therapy efficiency.

Importantly, the rational design and architecture of ARCHER achieve precise spatiotemporal control of therapeutic activation. As shown in Figures 4C–4K, S4, and S5, under conditions without GPX4 knockout (G3: RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl/PF33/CRISPR-Null + laser), although significant ROS/LPO accumulation and mitochondrial membrane potential changes were observed, no substantial cell death occurred. These findings indicate that mild Fe3+ release and ROS generation alone are insufficient to induce ferroptosis. In contrast, when GPX4 was suppressed (G7: ARCHER + laser), laser irradiation triggered robust Fe3+ release and ROS production, leading to markedly elevated lipid peroxidation, loss of the mitochondrial membrane potential, and significant cell death (p < 0.001 vs. G6). Notably, the ROS and LPO levels in G7 were significantly higher than those in G3 (p < 0.001), underscoring that GPX4 suppression prevents the detoxification of lipid hydroperoxides. Specifically, in NIH3T3 cells, we observed that ARCHER exhibited minimal toxicity. This may be attributed to the fact that NIH3T3cells exhibit an ARCHER uptake efficiency of only 14%, which is insufficient to achieve GPX4 knockout. Thus, the synergy operates through two sequential steps: first, GPX4 deficiency disrupts GSH-dependent antioxidant defense, permitting initial lipid peroxide accumulation; second, laser irradiation induces lethal LPO escalation via Fe3+ release and an ROS burst. These comparative experimental results clearly demonstrate the cooperative mechanism by which GPX4 suppression and ROS/Fe(III) release jointly drive ferroptotic cell death.

In vivo fluorescence imaging demonstrated that ARCHER preferentially accumulated in tumor tissues (5.5 × 108 p/s at 12 h), confirming the targeting capabilities of the hierarchical ligand system. As shown by the lack of observable variations in hematological or histological characterization, TEM-responsive disassembly and photodegradation of ARCHER not only increases therapeutic efficiency but also reduces systemic toxicity (Figures S9–S11).

Limitations of the study

Despite these promising findings, several limitations remain. First, the performance of ARCHER may differ across tumor types, because of the heterogeneity in HAase expression, pH gradients, and receptor profiles in different tumor tissues.57,58 Therefore, the pH/ROS-dual or other multiple-factor responsiveness may be considered in the design of the outer layer to be more adaptable to the treatment of pan-cancer. Second, the limited penetration depth of visible-range laser light limited applicability of ARCHER to superficial tumors. Considering the depth penetration, near-infrared (NIR) responsive systems, X-ray-activated components, sonodynamic-switchable chelates, or upconversion nanoparticles may increase the variety of tumors that can be treated.59,60 Third, although short-term safety seems to be beneficial, the long-term effects of residual Fe3+ and persistent GPX4-deficient cells require systematic toxicological assessment.29,61 Fourth, while ferroptosis is known to elicit immunogenic cell death, the immunomodulatory consequences of ARCHER have not yet been investigated.62 Although we found T cells took up ARCHER (Figure S12), their uptake efficiency is relatively low and insufficient to cause damage to the T cells. Furthermore, intratumoral ROS generation by ARCHER, coupled with the GPX4-knockout-induced ferroptosis, may primarily disrupt tumor cells and potentially release immunogenic signals, rather than systemically suppressing infiltrating T cells. The limited T cell uptake data support this localized effect model.

In conclusion, ARCHER provides a paradigm in ferroptosis-based precision cancer therapy, which combines gene editing with microenvironment-responsive nanomedicine via a highly programmable platform. This “two-step defense disarmament” design enables spatiotemporal control over ferroptosis activation while minimizing off-target effects. This platform also provides a blueprint for cancer therapy to other ferroptosis-regulatory targets (e.g., Acsl4 and Slc7a11) or alternative microenvironmental stimuli (e.g., glutathione depletion, hypoxia, and matrix metalloproteinases).

Method details

Synthesis of RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl

0.1 mmol of Chlorin e6 (Ce6) and 1 mmol of L (+)-ascorbic acid were co-dissolved in acetone. Next, 0.15 mmol of Fe(II)Cl2·4H2O was added to 100 mL of acetone solution, and the mixture was stirred at a temperature of 60°C. After 4 h, the reaction solution was cooled down to room temperature, and 100 mL of dichloromethane was added. The Ce6Fe(III)Cl was obtained by extraction and drying of the organic layer (yield: 64.1%). The mass spectrum is m/z = 650 ([M - CI] +).

The carboxyl groups of HA (0.5 mmol) were activated by EDCI (0.9 mmol) and NHS (0.75 mmol) in 0.05 mol/L of 4-Morpholineethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer for 2 h, and then 2.5 mmol of adipic dihydrazide (ADH) was added to MES buffer for stirring for 24 h at the dark environment. The free ADH was removed by dialysis, and HA-ADH was obtained by freeze-drying. Next, 0.1 mmol of Ce6Fe(III)Cl, 0.18 mmol of EDCI, and 0.15 mmol of NHS were stirred in 2 mL of DMSO for 4 h, and then the mixture was added to 10 mg/mL HA-ADH MES buffer (25 mL) and stirred for another 24 h in the dark. After undergoing dialysis and freeze drying, 196.1 mg of HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl and 99.8 mg of Mal-PEG2000-NHS were dissolved and stirred in 19.6 mL MES buffer for 24 h. Then 48.8 mg of the RGD-R8 peptide was added to the mixture, and this solution was further stirred for another 48 h. RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl was obtained through dialysis and freeze-drying. RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl was characterized by UV-Vis, 1H-NMR, and IR.

Preparation of RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl/PF33/pX330 nanoparticles

PF33 and pX330 plasmids were blended by vortexing at the optimal mass ratio (w/w = 5:1) to self-assemble PF33/pX330 nanoparticles. The core nanoparticles were further coated with RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl/PF33/pX330 at the optimal mass ratio (w/w/w = 55:5:1, which meant that in nanoparticle, 1 μg of plasmids were corresponding with 3.6 μg of Ce6Fe(III)Cl) to prepare the RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl/PF33/pX330 nanoparticles.

The hydrodynamic diameter and ζ potential of PF33/pCas and RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl/PF33/pX330 nanoparticles (5 μg plasmid/mL) were measured with/without 4 mg/mL HAas via dynamic light scattering (DLS) and micro-electrophoresis (LDV) through a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP. The morphological characteristics of these nanoparticles were imaged by transmission electron microscopy. Finally, a series of PF33/pX330 nanoparticles (w/w = 0.25:1, 0.5:1, 2:1, 4:1, 8:1) and RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl/PF33/pX330 nanoparticles with/without HAase were seeded onto a 1% (w/v) agarose gel for electrophoresis and imaging. 1 μg of nude pX330, a physical mixture of Ce6(III)Cl/HA/PF33/pX330 (containing 1 μg of pX330), and ARCHER (containing 1 μg) were irradiated with a 660 nm laser (100 mW/cm2, 1 min), and then an equal volume of 20 mg/mL heparin solution was added to replace the pX330. After 1 h of incubation, the samples underwent gel electrophoresis analysis and analyzed by ImageJ software.

Cellular internalization and distribution study

For cellular internalization, B16-F10 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well for overnight incubation. pX330 labeled with YOYO-1 (at 1:10000 in DMEM medium attenuation with incubation with pX330 for 1 h at 37°C) was utilized to prepare different nanoparticles. Then, the original culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different labeled nanoparticles at a plasmid concentration of 1 μg/mL. After 2 h of incubation, the cells were collected, resuspended in 500 μL of PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry (FCM, Agilent, NovoCyte, USA).

For analysis of the cellular distribution, B16-F10 cells were seeded into 35 mm glass bottom dishes (Φ = 15 mm) at a density of 2 × 105 cells/dish. After overnight incubation, YOYO-1 labeled different nanoparticles were individually added to dishes at different time points (0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h). After co-incubation, the cells were stained with LysoTracker Deep Red (at 1:10000 in DMEM medium attenuation for 30 min at 37°C) for lysosome labeling, fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde, and further stained by DAPI (at 1μg/mL) for 10 min at room temperature. The labeled cells were imaged by laser confocal scanning microscopy (LSCM, ZESS, LSM880, Germany).

Transfection in vitro

B16-F10 cells were planted into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight. pX458, containing a green fluorescent protein encoding sequence, was replaced with pX330 to fabricate different nanoparticles (PEI 1.8K/pX458, PEI 25K/pX458, PF33/pX458, HA/PF33/pX458, RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/pX458, or RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl/PF33/pX458). These nanoparticles were respectively added to cells at a plasmid dosage of 1 μg/well for 24 h of transfection. The expression of GFP was qualitatively observed via inverted fluorescence microscopy. The transfection efficiency of the nanoparticles and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of GFP in B16-F10 cells were quantified by FCM.

Construction of CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4

The CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 plasmid (CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4) was constructed by inserting a sgRNA targeting Gpx4 into the pX330 vector. Four pairs of oligos were picked up by the online tool of CRISPR KO Design (http://crispr.mit.edu), annealed, and ligase into the linear pX330 digested by the BbsⅠ restriction endonuclease. The series of CRISPR/Cas-GPX4 was transfected into B16-F10 cells for 48 h, and then the genome was extracted via the FastPure Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Mini Kit. The Gpx4 target genomic sequence was amplified by the primers with the following sequences: Prime 1F (sgRNA Gpx4 1–3)-GGATGACTTTGACACGCTTTC, Prime 1R (sgRNA Gpx4 1–3)-CCTATCAGGCTCCGCTTTC; Prime 2F (sgRNA Gpx4 4)-TGGGATCGGTTAGCTAGAGA, Prime 2R (sgRNA Gpx4 4)-GAATATGCTACCAGGGAAGAGG. Purified PCR products were analyzed by Sanger sequencing.

Cytotoxicity of synthetic polymers in vitro

B16-F10 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells/well and cultured overnight. PEI 1.8K, PEI 25K, or PF33 was added to the wells at a series of different mass concentrations (0, 0.39, 0.78, 1.56, 3.13, 6.25, 12.50, and 25.00 μg/mL) and further cultured for 48 h at 37°C. Then, MTT was added to the cellular medium for 2 h at 37°C, the medium was discarded, and DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan. The absorbance of the formazan was detected via a microplate reader at 570 nm. The cell viability was calculated according to the following Equation 1. The cytotoxicity of RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl was measured using a similar method to measure at different mass concentrations (0, 0.39, 0.78, 1.56, 3.13, 6.25, 12.50, and 25.00, 50.00, 100.00 μg/mL). The cytotoxicity of ARCHER also followed a similar method to measure with/without a 660 nm laser at a power of 100 mW/cm2 for 1 min.

LIVE/DEAD assay

B16-F10 cells, CT26, Panc02, or NIH3T3cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight. RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/CRISPR-Null, RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4, or ARCHER was added to the cells in the dark and co-cultured for 12 h, and then a 660 nm laser was or wasn’t administered at 100 mW/cm2 for 1 min. After 12 h, the cells were analyzed by MTT assays, and B16-F10 cells were stained with calcein-AM/PI following the instruction manual. The fluorescence was observed by an inverted fluorescence microscope.

The morphology of cells and examination of ROS, JC-1, iron ion, Fe2+, LPO and MDA

B16-F10 cells were planted in 12-well plates for overnight incubation and transfected with different nanoparticles. After 24 h of transfection, cells were stained with DCFH-DA at a final concentration of 10 μM and further incubated for 20 min. Then, cells were irradiated with a 660 nm laser at 100 mW/cm2 for 1 min. The fluorescence of DCF was also observed by an inverted fluorescence microscope. For JC-1, ARCHER was added and co-cultured with cells in each well of a 6-well plate. After 12 h, the laser was/wasn’t administered, and the cells were further cultured for another 12 h. Then, the cells were collected, stained with JC-1 as instruction manual, and observed by an inverted fluorescence microscope or analyzed by FCM. To determine the cellular morphology of the cells, ARCHER was added and co-cultured with cells in each well of a 6-well plate. After 12 h, the laser was/wasn’t administered, and the cells were further cultured for another 12 h. The cells were collected and fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde. The morphology of the mitochondria and the integrity of the cell membrane were observed and imaged via Bio-TME in ultrathin sections of different samples. For examination of lipid peroxidation (LPO), before the administration of the laser, the cells were successively stained with 2 μM of Hoechst 33324 and 5 μM of C11-BODIPY 581/591 for 30 min each to apply live-cell imaging by LSCM. The quantitative analysis of LPO used FCM. To determine the concentrations of iron ions, Fe2+, and malondialdehyde (MDA), ARCHER was added and co-cultured with cells in each well of a 6-well plate. After 12 h, the laser was/wasn’t administered, and the cells were further cultured for another 12 h. Then, the cells were collected, performed as instruction manual, and detected by a microplate reader.

Western blot analysis

B16-F10 cells were seeded on 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37°C. Then, B16-F10 cells were treated with different nanocomplexes for 12 h. The cells were (weren’t) irradiated with a laser at 100 mW for 1 min. After another 12 h, the cells were collected and lysed to extract total protein. The target proteins were analyzed by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The anti-GPX4 antibody and anti-β-tubulin were attenuated at a 1:5000 dilution.

Biodistribution and accumulation

After the establishment of the A375 subcutaneous xenograft model, CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 was labeled by TOTO-3 (excitation/emission: 640/660 nm), and ARCHER-TOTO-3 or HA/PF33/CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 -TOTO-3 was injected through the tail vein at a plasmid dosage of 10 μg/mouse. Images were taken at a given point in time (2 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h) by an IVIS Lumina imaging system (Caliper, USA).

Anti-cancer efficiency in vivo

When the xenograft cancer model was established, the mice were randomly assigned to one of nine groups (n = 5) and given intravenous injections of PBS, CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4, CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 + laser, RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl, RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6Fe(III)Cl + laser, RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4, RGD-R8-PEG-HA-ADH-Ce6/PF33/CRISPR/Cas9-GPX4 + laser, ARCHER or ARCHER + laser every two days to treat the xenograft cancer model at a plasmid dosage of 10 μg/mouse (36 μg Ce6(III)Cl/mouse), respectively. After 12 h of nanoparticle administration, the irradiated laser groups were administrated with 50 mW/cm2 for 10 min. The treatment began when the cancer volume of the xenograft cancer model was 80 mm3. The treatment ended when the first mouse in the PBS-treated group died. Blood was collected from the orbit of each mouse and all the mice were sacrificed. The tumors were isolated, weighed, and photographed. The harvested cancer and major organs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for further hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, IHC staining of Ki-67 (1:200) andGPX4 (1:200), and TUNEL analyses.

Safety evaluation in vivo

Healthy mice received PBS and 30 of μg plasmids/mouse ARCHER only once. After 30 days, the mice were sacrificed, and their blood, serum, and important organs were collected. A complete blood count was performed. Serum biochemistry, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), albumin (ALB), amylase (AMY), total bilirubin (BILT), total protein (TP), creatine kinase myocardial band (CKMB), triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) creatinine (CREA), urea, and uric acid (UA) levels, was detected. The important organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) were stained with H&E to observe whether there were pathological changes and extracted DNA, PCR was performed for Gpx4 and 3 potential off-target sites, and conducted next-generation sequencing (PEI150). The primer sequences for Gpx4 and its three potential off-target sites are as follows: Gpx4 prime F-ACCTTGAGACTCCTCCACCTA, Gpx4 prime R-GTACTTATCCAGGCAGACCAT; Uros prime F-TTTAGTCACAAGTGGTGGCAGAGG, Uros prime R-AAGATCCGCCGATCCAGACA; Uros prime F-GAAGATCCGCCGATCCAGAC, Uros prime R-ACAAGTGGTGGCAGAGGTGG; Setd3 prime F-GACCGTGGGTCTGGAAAGGAC, Setd3 rime R-ATCTCAGCATTGAAAAATGAAGG.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The difference among groups was analyzed by one-way ANOVA of variance, two-way repeated measures ANOVA, Student’s test for two groups, and Log rank test for survival curve. The statistical details are shown in figure legends. Significant differences were defined by ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ns, not significant.
