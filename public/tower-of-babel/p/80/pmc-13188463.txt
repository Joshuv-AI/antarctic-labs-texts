Mechanistic insights and ameliorative strategies: advances in DEHP-induced metabolic toxicity and intervention by natural bioactive compounds and probiotics
Authors: Huanyu Cui, Yansong Hu, Ye Tang, Tingzhuang Yi, Shengkui Tan, Zhengbao Zhang, Xiaonian Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) is a ubiquitous plasticizer and environmental contaminant linked to severe metabolic disorders. This review concisely elucidates the multi-organ mechanisms of DEHP-induced toxicity, highlighting its disruption of lipid, glucose, and protein homeostasis. Specifically, we delineate the crucial crosstalk between oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and inflammatory pathways that drive DEHP pathobiology. Furthermore, we systematically evaluate emerging ameliorative strategies, focusing on natural bioactive compounds and probiotics. We highlight how these interventions mitigate DEHP toxicity by targeting specific molecular hubs (e.g., PPARs, Nrf2) and restoring gut microbiota balance. By integrating mechanistic insights with natural interventions, this review provides a targeted framework for developing therapeutic countermeasures against phthalate-induced metabolic disruption.

Introduction

Phthalates, a class of industrial plasticizers, are ubiquitous in plastics, medical devices, and personal care products. Among them, di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) is one of the most prevalent. Its non-covalent binding to plastics allows for easy leaching, facilitating widespread human exposure through consumer products [1]. The extensive use of plastics in food packaging makes oral ingestion a primary exposure route [2], with documented daily intakes varying across populations. For instance, a median of 33.9 µg/kg in the general population of Taiwan Province, China [3], and an average of 2.7 µg/kg/day from infant foods in Denmark [4]. DEHP and its analogs are recognized endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) capable of perturbing metabolic homeostasis and contributing to diverse adverse health outcomes. Given these widespread and multifaceted toxicities, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified DEHP as a Group 2B carcinogen, indicating it is possibly carcinogenic to humans [5]. This classification underscores the urgent need to fully comprehend its mechanistic pathways and to develop effective intervention strategies to mitigate its adverse health impacts.

Following ingestion, the gastrointestinal tract serves as the first line of interaction, where DEHP’s toxicokinetic and its complex interplay with the gut microbiota commence, establishing the initial link in its systemic toxicity [6]. As a typical EDC, the biological effects of DEHP are intrinsically tied to its metabolic fate. Upon entering the gut, DEHP undergoes partial hydrolysis by intestinal epithelial cells and microbial esterases to form its key bioactive metabolite, mono-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (MEHP).

A major limitation of previous literature is the tendency to examine these metabolic disruptions in isolated organ systems. To address this gap, the unique contribution of this review lies in its holistic approach: we systematically integrate the mechanisms of multi-organ crosstalk and highlight the pivotal, yet often overlooked, role of the gut microbiota in DEHP-induced toxicity. Furthermore, while current research identifies various toxic pathways, comprehensive summaries of effective countermeasures remain limited. Therefore, this review prominently features the protective mechanisms of specific natural bioactive compounds (e.g., lycopene, curcumin) and probiotics (e.g., specific Lactobacillus strains) against DEHP toxicity. We elaborate on their therapeutic roles along the text of this review and synthesize these findings to provide a targeted framework for mitigating phthalate-induced metabolic disorders.

Critically, the gut is not merely a passive site for absorption and metabolism, its own structural and functional integrity represents a direct and early target of DEHP toxicity. Exposure to DEHP can compromise the intestinal barrier, provoke inflammation, and induce microbial dysbiosis [7–10]. This local assault on intestinal homeostasis is a fundamental component of DEHP’s toxic profile. Moreover, by disrupting the vital physiological crosstalk between the gut and distant organs like the liver, this intestinal damage may evolve into a central mechanism driving DEHP-induced systemic metabolic disorders.

DEHP and gut microbiota dysbiosis

DEHP exposure directly assaults the gut’s physical barrier, disrupting the tight junction complexes that are crucial for epithelial integrity. Studies in zebrafish and intestinal cell models have shown that DEHP significantly downregulates the expression of genes encoding tight and adherens junction proteins, pinpointing this disruption as a core mechanism of intestinal injury [7, 8]. Concordantly, direct histopathological damage—characterized by shortened intestinal villi, thinning of the muscular layer, and reduced goblet cell numbers—has been documented in both rodent and zebrafish models, impairments that logically undermine digestive, absorptive, and barrier functions [9, 10].

Beyond the physical barrier, DEHP profoundly reshapes the gut ecosystem. It significantly alters the composition, structure, and diversity of the intestinal microbiota, a state termed dysbiosis that is a pivotal event in its systemic toxicity. Ex vivo experiments with human fecal microbiota and neonatal cohort studies consistently report reduced microbial diversity following DEHP exposure [11, 12]. A common observation across multiple studies is an altered Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio (for instance, while specific biomarker expression or lipid accumulation is often increased in sensitive species like Sprague-Dawley rats, it exhibits decreased or divergent responses in human in vitro models, such as human fetal testis explants or HepG2 human hepatoma cell lines, highlighting significant species-specific differences in DEHP sensitivity) and a general expansion of opportunistic pathogens such as Proteobacteria [13]. This taxonomic shift is paralleled by functional changes in the microbial metabolome, most notably a depletion of beneficial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, which play essential roles in maintaining intestinal health, modulating inflammation, and regulating host energy metabolism [14].

From local dysbiosis to systemic dysfunction

The combination of gut microbiota dysbiosis and concomitant barrier breach forms the launchpad for DEHP’s systemic effects. The altered microbial community and its metabolites can activate intestinal helper T cell pathways (Th1, Th2, Th17), igniting both local and systemic inflammatory cascades. In neonates, DEHP-induced disruption of the gut microbiota alters the early development of the immune system. This aberrant microbiota-immunity interplay can manifest as a blunted immunological response to routine vaccines, often characterized by reduced specific antibody titers or an imbalance in Th1/Th2 cell ratios, ultimately compromising vaccine efficacy [10, 11]. Furthermore, dysbiosis reprograms host bile acid metabolism. The resultant changes in the bile acid pool act as potent agonists for the intestinal farnesoid X receptor (FXR). Activation of this pathway suppresses hepatic bile acid synthesis via fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF15) and fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19) signaling, ultimately disrupting cholesterol homeostasis [15]. The systemic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress emanating from this gut-centric turmoil are now recognized as core mechanisms that exacerbate insulin resistance, promote hepatic lipid accumulation (NAFLD), and contribute to weight gain.

DEHP and PPAR signaling: a central mechanism

Importantly, interference with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling constitutes a central axis of DEHP/MEHP-mediated metabolic disruption [16]. The PPAR nuclear receptor family (PPARα, PPARβ/δ, PPARγ) functions as ligand-activated transcription factors governing key metabolic processes. DEHP and its metabolites act as potent agonists for PPARα and PPARγ. By co-opting these critical regulators of glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory responses, DEHP/MEHP instigates a wide spectrum of metabolic toxicity [17, 18].

In pivotal metabolic organs like the liver, DEHP exposure triggers an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and an upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors [19]. It concomitantly activates a network of stress and signaling pathways—including phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38), and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/p53—that collectively drive lipid metabolic disorders and the progression of associated diseases [20, 21].

Regarding glucose metabolism, DEHP/MEHP promotes insulin resistance largely through PPARγ activation. It also suppresses the expression of key glucose transporters: hepatic glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2) and peripheral glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4), and impairs pancreatic β-cell function [22, 23], systemic disturbances that contribute to glucose intolerance and type 2 diabetes mellitus.

The reach of DEHP’s toxicity extends to the dysfunction of cellular organelles. It can impair lysosomal clearance by inhibiting the galectin-3 (GAL3)/tripartite motif-containing protein 16 (TRIM16) axis, leading to neuronal apoptosis [24]. By interfering with Trim38-mediated degradation of p53, it disrupts placental development [25]. Furthermore, it induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, resulting in the accumulation of misfolded proteins [26].

This review systematically synthesizes the toxic effects of DEHP and its primary active metabolite, MEHP, with a focus on their disruption of glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism. By integrating evidence across multiple interrelated signaling pathways, we construct a coherent narrative of their metabolic interference. We aim to provide a robust theoretical foundation to inform future research directions, ultimately supporting the safer use of plasticizers and enabling effective exposure prevention and therapeutic strategies regarding DEHP present in food packaging, medical devices, and consumer products.

Effects on lipid metabolism homeostasis

Epidemiological evidence linking DEHP to lipid disorders

Human studies reveal that DEHP exposure is consistently associated with dyslipidemia across various life stages and population groups, highlighting its role as a metabolic disruptor.

Prospective studies in children and adolescents indicate lasting metabolic risks. Although urinary phthalate metabolite levels are typically higher in early childhood (ages 3–5) than in school age (ages 7–9), multiple DEHP metabolites show consistent associations with an increased risk of hypertriglyceridemia and insulin resistance across both developmental periods [27]. This suggests sustained metabolic susceptibility during growth. Research confirms that the adolescent metabolic system is particularly vulnerable. DEHP may disrupt lipid homeostasis by interfering with the expression of core regulatory genes such as sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1), liver X receptor alpha (LXR-α), and low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) [28]. Furthermore, a Japanese cohort study underscores the pivotal role of DEHP metabolites during early childhood exposure in shaping later metabolic health [29]. Notably, while phthalate mixtures showed no overall significant link to early adiposity rebound, the cumulative burden of DEHP metabolites was significantly associated with longitudinal increases in body mass index (BMI) in girls and represented the strongest contributor to trajectories of excess weight gain.

In the general adult population, exposure to environmentally relevant doses of DEHP acts as an independent risk factor for dyslipidemia. Data from the U.S. general adult population demonstrate that DEHP metabolite levels are inversely correlated with high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and positively correlated with total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG). Advanced mixture analysis using weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression confirms the dominant, negative contribution of DEHP metabolites to adverse blood lipid profiles [30].

Individuals with pre-existing metabolic conditions exhibit heightened susceptibility and potentially altered DEHP toxicokinetics. In adolescents with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), serum levels of DEHP/MEHP positively correlate with insulin resistance and triglyceride levels, independent of obesity status [31]. Low-dose DEHP exposure is linked to obesity, a connection often mediated by chronic inflammation in adipose tissue [32]. Evidence further suggests that environmental-level DEHP exposure may contribute to the early onset of lipid dysregulation in obese individuals during critical midlife windows [33]. These findings illustrate distinct patterns of metabolic interference: obese individuals primarily manifest lipid disorders (elevated TG), patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) display pronounced glucose metabolism abnormalities, while apparently healthy controls may show a paradoxical mix of lower blood lipids alongside increased insulin resistance. The frequent observation of elevated liver enzymes in exposed populations further supports the hepatotoxic potential of DEHP and its association with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and insulin resistance [34]. Furthermore, pre-existing metabolic disorders, such as fatty liver disease, can significantly impair the hepatic metabolism and excretion of DEHP. Specifically, these conditions downregulate key phase I and phase II metabolic enzymes, including cytochrome P450 (CYP450) and UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (UGT), thereby delaying DEHP clearance, prolonging its internal retention, and potentially exacerbating its hepatotoxic effects [35].

In summary, epidemiological evidence underscores that the impact of DEHP on lipid metabolism is both stage-dependent and population-specific. Children, adolescents (especially girls), and individuals with underlying conditions like obesity, PCOS, or fatty liver disease are at greater risk. This heightened susceptibility in disease states may stem from altered metabolic pathways, prolonged DEHP half-life, and an increased internal burden of the active metabolite MEHP.

Mechanistic pathways disrupting lipid homeostasis

DEHP/MEHP disrupts lipid metabolism through a sophisticated network of interlinked signaling pathways, moving beyond simple receptor activation.

The central role of PPAR signaling

The peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) serve as primary molecular targets. PPARα, highly expressed in hepatocytes, is the master regulator of fatty acid β-oxidation. Conversely, PPARγ, predominant in adipose tissue, drives adipogenic differentiation and lipogenesis. MEHP potently activates PPARγ, promoting the differentiation of preadipocytes and, in concert with parent compounds, disrupting mature adipocyte function. This leads to aberrant lipid storage, metabolic imbalance, and a pro-inflammatory, insulin-resistant state that mirrors the effects of a high-fat diet or the PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone [18, 36]. The reversibility of these effects by a PPARγ antagonist confirms the receptor’s central role [18]. Animal studies reveal that MEHP can also synergistically activate PPARα and PPARγ in liver macrophages, reprogramming their phenotype and crippling their regulatory role in lipid homeostasis [36].

DEHP’s interference is multi-layered. Beyond classic ligand activation, it can epigenetically enhance PPARγ transcriptional output via promoter demethylation [32]. MEHP directly binds to the PPARγ ligand-binding domain at low concentrations, initiating the adipogenic program marked by upregulation of key genes like FABP-4 [37]. The disturbance extends into a broader transcriptional network. PPARs intricately cross-regulate with sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP1c), which governs de novo lipogenesis, and liver X receptors (LXRs), which control cholesterol metabolism [38]. PPARγ promotes adipogenesis via SREBP1c and cooperates with LXRs to regulate lipid storage. Furthermore, bile acid-activated FXR mediates part of its lipid metabolic effects through the PPARγ pathway. DEHP disrupts this delicate balance by derailing the LXR/PPARγ feedback loop, causing abnormal activation of the PPARγ-SREBP1c-LXR axis. Concurrently, it impairs the normal negative regulation that PPARs exert on the pro-inflammatory nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway. The resulting breakdown in the PPARs/NF-κB balance culminates in lipid metabolism disorders and chronic low-grade inflammation [19].

Interference with the JAK-STAT pathway

Emerging evidence highlights the Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway as another key nexus. DEHP exposure suppresses JAK-STAT activity in liver and adipose tissue, inhibiting lipolysis and promoting total cholesterol accumulation [39]. Notably, DEHP exerts tissue-specific regulation: it activates the JAK2/STAT5 pathway in adipose tissue to promote adipogenic gene expression, while inhibiting the same pathway in the liver to enhance lipid synthesis. A high-fat diet synergizes with DEHP, exacerbating these metabolic disturbances [40]. Specifically, MEHP inhibits hepatic JAK2/STAT5 signaling, activating downstream fatty acid synthesis enzymes, boosting hepatocyte lipid storage capacity, and driving intrahepatic lipid accumulation characteristic of NAFLD [41]. MEHP can also activate the TYK2/STAT3 axis in fibroblasts, upregulating PPARγ and inhibiting the fatty acid oxidation enzyme ACOX1, thereby promoting lipid droplet formation [42]. Moreover, MEHP-induced oxidative stress suppresses STAT5A expression, which in turn promotes the expression of fibrosis-related molecules (CTGF, MMP2, MMP9, TIMP2), revealing a mechanism by which DEHP exposure can progress from steatosis to liver fibrosis [43].

Perturbation of cAMP/PKA and ERK1/2 signaling

The cyclic AMP (cAMP)/protein kinase A (PKA) cascade, a vital second messenger system, is another target. DEHP can enhance lipid metabolism in placental tissue by inhibiting cAMP/PKA activity [44]. A detailed mechanistic axis has been elucidated in Leydig cells: MEHP induces mitochondrial oxidative stress, which inhibits the cAMP/PKA pathway. This reduces the transcriptional activity of the key steroidogenic factor 1 (SF-1), leading to downregulation of the steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR) and enzymes for testosterone synthesis, linking oxidative damage directly to hormonal disruption [45]. DEHP also synergistically inhibits both cAMP and ERK1/2 (extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2) signaling, blocking steroid hormone synthesis in gonadotropin-stimulated cells [46].

Activation of the Notch-inflammation axis

The highly conserved Notch signaling pathway has recently been implicated as a delicate regulator of lipid metabolism. DEHP/MEHP induces hepatic lipid disorders through a Notch-inflammation axis. Activation of Notch signaling drives the release of inflammatory factors and mediates lipid accumulation, a process reversible by Notch inhibitors or anti-inflammatory treatments [47, 48]. In adipocyte models, MEHP activates the Notch-1/Jagged-2 pathway, significantly promoting intracellular triglyceride accumulation and establishing Notch activation as a core mechanism in MEHP-induced lipid metabolic abnormality [49].

In conclusion, DEHP disrupts lipid homeostasis through a complex, multi-pathway interplay. These pathways do not operate in isolation but engage in cross-talk, creating an amplified disruptive signal that extends far beyond the initial activation of nuclear receptors. The gut microbiota as an amplifier of DEHP-induced lipid toxicity

Traditionally, DEHP’s lipotoxic effects were attributed to direct nuclear receptor activation. However, a paradigm shift has occurred with the recognition of the gut microbiota as a critical amplifier and mediator via the “gut-liver” and “gut-fat” axes. DEHP exposure significantly reduces both the alpha diversity (within-community richness) and beta diversity (between-community variation) of the gut microbiota, indicating a shift towards a less complex and stable ecosystem [50]. This dysbiosis is functionally coupled with host metabolic perturbations, including abnormal adipogenesis, impaired energy expenditure, and disturbances in glucose metabolism [51]. A key consequence is the depletion of beneficial SCFAs like acetate, butyrate, and valerate [52]. SCFAs are not merely an energy source for colonocytes; they systemically regulate energy metabolism and inflammation. Crucially, studies confirm that SCFAs can directly inhibit DEHP-induced lipogenesis and PPARγ phosphorylation, demonstrating their protective role against DEHP lipotoxicity [53].

The gut-liver dialogue is profoundly affected through bile acid metabolism. DEHP-induced dysbiosis alters the composition and proportion of host bile acids [54, 55]. These modified bile acids become potent agonists for the intestinal FXR. Activated intestinal FXR upregulates FGF15, which, upon reaching the liver, represses the expression of cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1), the rate-limiting enzyme for bile acid synthesis. The resultant blockade in converting cholesterol to bile acids leads to cholesterol metabolism imbalance [15].

Furthermore, gut dysbiosis and its metabolic products activate systemic immune-inflammatory pathways, such as the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)/NF-κB cascade [56]. This triggers the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)), establishing a chronic low-grade inflammatory state [57]. This systemic inflammation is a well-known driver of both insulin resistance and lipid metabolism disorders.

Therefore, the core mechanism involves DEHP-induced gut dysbiosis destabilizing the homeostasis of the gut-liver and gut-fat axes. By reshaping the microbial community and its metabolic output (e.g., reducing SCFAs, altering bile acids), DEHP indirectly interferes with host metabolic signaling hubs (e.g., FXR, PPARγ). This gut-centric disruption, coupled with direct cellular effects, creates a synergistic push towards systemic lipid metabolism disorders and obesity.

In summary, the core of DEHP’s lipotoxicity lies in its dual perturbation of PPAR signaling. It disrupts the balance between lipid catabolism and anabolism: short-term exposure can inhibit PPARα-driven fatty acid β-oxidation, while chronic exposure may dysregulate this pathway, contributing to metabolic imbalance. Concurrently, DEHP activates PPARγ, upregulating its downstream effector SREBP-1c to drive the expression of lipogenic genes (e.g., fatty acid synthase (FAS), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC)), thereby promoting hepatic steatosis and NAFLD [19, 32, 58]. Critically, this direct cellular interference is powerfully amplified via the gut-liver and gut-fat axes. By inducing gut dysbiosis, depleting protective short-chain fatty acids (e.g., butyrate), and disrupting bile acid metabolism/FXR/FGF15 signaling, DEHP exacerbates systemic lipid disorders and chronic low-grade inflammation [15, 52, 53].

Influence on carbohydrate metabolism homeostasis

Epidemiological evidence linking DEHP to glucose metabolism disorders

Human epidemiological studies provide compelling evidence that DEHP exposure disrupts glucose homeostasis across the lifespan, with particular windows of vulnerability.

The prenatal period represents a critical window for metabolic programming. Long-term cohort data reveal that in utero exposure can have lasting consequences. In males followed from age 3 to 27, prenatal exposure to moderate levels of MEHP was associated with higher fasting insulin in adulthood, while exposure to higher total DEHP metabolites correlated with elevated adult fasting plasma glucose [59]. The vulnerability extends to the mother herself; among a mixture of 37 endocrine disruptors in second-trimester women, DEHP metabolites were identified as the “leading contributor” to elevated maternal fasting glucose, insulin, and insulin resistance (HOMA2-IR), independent of BMI [60]. Systematic reviews corroborate a “positive and very consistent association” between postnatal phthalate exposure and markers of diabetes, with DEHP/MEHP being among the most robustly documented [61].

Childhood and adolescence, pivotal stages for growth and metabolic set-point establishment, are also sensitive. Longitudinal studies measuring the same children at ages 3–5 and again at 7–9 found that urinary levels of DEHP metabolites (MEHP, MEOHP, MiNP) were consistently and significantly associated with a higher homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) at both time points [27]. This suggests DEHP exposure during development can instigate early, persistent markers of insulin resistance. In adolescents with PCOS, a condition marked by metabolic dysregulation, circulating DEHP/MEHP levels—while not differing from healthy controls at baseline—were strongly associated with more severe insulin resistance and higher triglycerides within the PCOS group itself, an association that persisted after adjusting for BMI [31]. This points to a potential role for DEHP in exacerbating metabolic complications in susceptible individuals.

In the general adult population, a robust and consistent positive association exists between DEHP exposure and impaired glucose metabolism. Studies link DEHP metabolites to higher HOMA-IR, fasting insulin, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and compensatory β-cell function (HOMA-β) [62, 63]. Given that real-world exposure involves complex chemical mixtures, advanced statistical models (e.g., WQS regression, Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression) have been employed to identify key drivers. These analyses consistently pinpoint DEHP and its metabolites as the “primary contributors” or “dominant chemicals” responsible for the increased risk of insulin resistance within mixtures containing phenols, parabens, and other phthalates [63–65]. Case-control studies further confirm a significant combined effect of phthalate mixtures on T2DM risk, with MEHP often identified as the most dominant contributor [66]. The highest level of evidence, meta-analysis, confirms that DEHP exposure is a significant risk factor for diabetes, approximately doubling the risk [67].

Individuals with specific conditions or physiological states exhibit heightened sensitivity. Pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) have significantly higher urinary MEHP levels than healthy pregnant women, showing a dose-response relationship [65]. Mechanistic support comes from studies showing not only elevated MEHP in GDM serum but also demonstrating in human liver organoids that MEHP remodels gene expression to promote glycolysis, inhibit the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and induce insulin resistance and lactate accumulation [68]. Similarly, while DEHP may not initiate PCOS, it appears to act as a significant environmental exacerbator of its metabolic complications by aggravating underlying insulin resistance, explaining the worse outcomes in PCOS patients at comparable exposure levels [31].

Collectively, the evidence indicates that DEHP’s impact on glucose metabolism follows a dose-response pattern and is influenced by life stage, pre-existing metabolic status, and nutritional context.

Mechanistic pathways underlying glucose metabolism disruption

DEHP/MEHP undermines glucose homeostasis through a multi-pronged attack on insulin signaling, cellular stress responses, and pancreatic β-cell health. Figure 3 outlines the multi-pronged mechanisms by which DEHP/MEHP impairs glucose homeostasis.

Direct sabotage of insulin signaling

The most direct route to insulin resistance is the impairment of the insulin signaling cascade itself. In key insulin-sensitive tissues, liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose, DEHP and MEHP disrupt critical nodes. They inhibit the phosphorylation and/or expression of the insulin receptor (IR), insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1), PI3K, and Akt [69, 70]. This dysregulation directly impedes the translocation of the glucose transporter GLUT4 to the cell membrane, reducing cellular glucose uptake in muscle and fat, and contributing to hyperglycemia. This mechanism has been validated across diverse tissues, including liver, skeletal muscle, and heart [71, 72]. DEHP exposure can also worsen pre-existing insulin signaling defects, suggesting synergistic toxicity in metabolically compromised states [69, 70].

The PI3K/Akt pathway is of paramount importance. Evidence from natural compounds like alpinetin, which improves insulin sensitivity by activating PI3K/Akt and promoting GLUT4 translocation, inversely implies DEHP’s inhibitory role [9]. Direct confirmation comes from studies showing DEHP/MEHP inhibit PI3K/Akt in skeletal muscle cells, leading to the dephosphorylation and activation of the transcription factor forkhead box O1 (FoxO1). Activated FoxO1 translocates to the nucleus to upregulate gluconeogenic genes, driving hepatic glucose production and further exacerbating hyperglycemia [73, 74]. Comprehensive molecular profiling reveals that DEHP broadly alters the expression of numerous insulin signaling components (IRS-1, PI3K, Akt, GLUT4, GSK-3α/β, mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)) and related proteins (RAS, ERK1/2, MEK1/2). Critically, it promotes the serine phosphorylation of IRS-1, a classic marker of insulin resistance that uncouples the receptor from its downstream effectors, crippling the entire signaling pathway from receptor activation to glucose uptake [20].

Induction of oxidative stress, inflammation, and β-cell dysfunction

Beyond direct signaling interference, DEHP/MEHP instigates a state of cellular stress that further impairs glucose metabolism.

Nuclear receptor-mediated disruption

Acting through PPARs, DEHP significantly perturbs PPARγ expression, amplifying both oxidative and inflammatory responses [75]. In models of developmental exposure, DEHP inhibits PPARγ and leptin receptor (LepA) in zebrafish embryos, amplifying oxidative stress and inflammation in a hyperglycemic context and ultimately inducing insulin resistance.

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and β-cell failure

MEHP induces ER stress in pancreatic β-cells, activating the JNK/GADD153/NF-κB pathway and interfering with PPARα/γ expression, which disrupts the homeostasis of insulin synthesis and secretion [66]. It also downregulates key proteins essential for β-cell function, including the glucose transporter GLUT1, glucokinase (GCK), and core transcription factors PDX1 and MafA, directly impairing insulin secretion, particularly under low-glucose conditions [76].

Epigenetic and redox imbalance

DEHP suppresses the expression of microRNA-17 (miR-17) by interfering with antioxidant pathways, such as nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), promoter methylation, and long non-coding RNA regulation. This suppression disrupts the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)-Nrf2 redox system, activates oxidative stress, and upregulates thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP). Subsequent oxidative stress induces miR-200a, which then targets the 3’-UTRs of the IR and IRS1 genes, blocking insulin signaling and glucose uptake that a cascade culminating in insulin resistance [77].

β-cell apoptosis via autophagy and pyroptosis

MEHP triggers a substantial rise in ROS in β-cells, activating abnormal autophagy. This hyperactivated autophagy leads to lysosomal membrane permeabilization, releasing cathepsin D into the cytosol. Cathepsin D then inhibits the pro-survival PI3K/Akt/Bcl-2 axis, induces mitochondrial dysfunction, and activates executioner caspases, resulting in autophagy-dependent apoptosis [78]. The antioxidant N-acetylcysteine can mitigate this process by reducing ROS and autophagy [79]. At higher concentrations, MEHP can also activate the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, promoting lysosomal cathepsin B release and triggering a pyroptotic cell death pathway involving caspase-1 (Caspase-1) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β). Even at lower, more environmentally relevant concentrations, MEHP upregulates autophagy, leading to β-cell damage [80].

Thyroid hormone mediation

An intriguing systemic link involves thyroid function. Mediation analysis indicates that decreased levels of free thyroxine (FT4) mediate approximately 24–35% of the association between MEHP exposure and increased HOMA-IR, suggesting that phthalate-induced suppression of thyroid function is a significant contributor to worsened insulin resistance [81].

These concerted actions systematically promote the pathogenesis of T2DM.

Effects on protein metabolic homeostasis

Beyond carbohydrates and lipids, DEHP and its major metabolite MEHP orchestrate a profound and multifaceted assault on protein metabolic homeostasis. This disruption impairs the fundamental cellular processes governing protein synthesis, folding, post-translational modifications (PTMs), and degradation, thereby compromising cellular integrity and function across multiple organ systems.

Disruption of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS)

The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), a primary pathway for controlled protein degradation, is a key target of DEHP/MEHP interference, perturbed through multiple context-specific mechanisms. First, DEHP can promote the degradation of specific regulatory proteins. For instance, it activates histone deacetylase 4 (HDAC4) to deacetylate the transcription factor specificity protein 3 (Sp3), leading to its polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, a mechanism contributing to neurotoxicity [82]. In another neurotoxic pathway, DEHP downregulates miR-93, which relieves suppression on TNFα-induced protein 1 (TNFAIP1). The elevated TNFAIP1 protein then binds to and promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of kinase casein kinase 2 beta (CK2β), inhibiting the downstream pro-survival Akt/CREB pathway and resulting in neurocognitive dysfunction [83]. Conversely, MEHP can also stabilize proteins by inhibiting their degradation. It blocks the E3 ubiquitin ligase tripartite motif-containing protein 38 (TRIM38), thereby preventing the ubiquitination and degradation of the tumor suppressor p53. The resultant accumulation of p53 leads to cell cycle arrest and has been shown to hinder placental development [25]. Furthermore, DEHP exposure can alter broader UPS dynamics. In hormone-sensitive contexts, it activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a process linked to altered estrogen receptor expression and stability via the ubiquitination system, contributing to tamoxifen resistance in breast cancer models [84]. Additionally, DEHP broadly alters the expression profile of deubiquitinating enzymes (e.g., ubiquitin-specific peptidase 12 (USP12), COP9 signalosome complex subunit 5 (COPS5)) in ovarian cells, suggesting a global perturbation of UPS balance as a mechanism underlying ovarian toxicity [85].

Impairment of the autophagy-lysosomal pathway

The UPS and the autophagy-lysosome pathway are the two primary intracellular degradation systems that maintain protein homeostasis. DEHP disrupts both systems, leading to abnormal protein accumulation and consequently exacerbating cellular metabolic toxicity. A key mechanism involves the significant impairment of the autophagy-lysosomal pathway. DEHP exposure downregulates the expression of transcription factor EB (TFEB), the master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. This downregulation subsequently inhibits the GAL3/TRIM16 axis, a critical protein complex that targets damaged lysosomes for selective autophagy (i.e., lysophagy), thereby compromising the cell’s ability to clear both protein aggregates and dysfunctional organelles. Consequently, defective lysosomes accumulate, overall autophagic flux is blocked, and cells particularly neurons become susceptible to apoptosis, revealing a novel mechanism of DEHP-induced neurotoxicity [24].

Induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress

A major consequence of DEHP/MEHP-induced metabolic and oxidative stress is the disruption of ER homeostasis, leading to the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins. This triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR), an adaptive signaling network that, if unresolved, commits the cell to apoptosis.

The core instigators are mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS overproduction [86, 87]. ROS and other stressors cause an accumulation of misfolded proteins, which bind to and sequester the chaperone glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78). This releases and activates three key UPR sensor proteins. First, protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK) phosphorylates eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) to attenuate global protein synthesis while selectively permitting the translation of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4). Second, inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α) splices X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) mRNA to promote the expression of genes involved in ER-associated degradation (ERAD). Third, activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) is cleaved to produce an active transcription factor that upregulates ER chaperone genes, including GRP78 itself, in a compensatory attempt to enhance folding capacity [88, 89].

When this adaptive response fails to alleviate stress, the UPR switches to a pro-apoptotic program. The PERK-ATF4 and IRE1α-TRAF2 pathways converge to upregulate the transcription factor C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP). CHOP promotes apoptosis by downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) and upregulating the pro-apoptotic protein Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax). Concurrently, persistently activated IRE1α promotes cell death through JNK phosphorylation [90].

Importantly, ER stress engages in tight cross-talk with inflammatory pathways. Activated IRE1α can, via TRAF2, activate NF-κB to drive pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Moreover, ER stress signals like ROS and calcium efflux are potent activators of the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to Caspase-1 activation, maturation of IL-1β, and a form of inflammatory cell death called pyroptosis [91, 92].

Induction of aberrant post-translational modifications

DEHP/MEHP also corrupts the regulatory language of proteins by inducing aberrant post-translational modifications. The oxidative stress they generate can cause direct, often irreversible, protein damage such as carbonylation, which alters protein structure and function [93, 94]. Moreover, by altering the activities of key kinases and phosphatases (e.g., AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), MAPKs), DEHP/MEHP disrupts the dynamic balance of protein phosphorylation. Given that phosphorylation is a central switch regulating metabolic enzymes, insulin signaling components (e.g., IRS-1), and myriad other signaling molecules, this dysregulation further cripples essential cellular signaling networks [54].

In summary, DEHP/MEHP disrupts protein homeostasis at virtually every regulatory level: it corrupts synthesis and folding (via ER stress), alters protein fate and function through aberrant PTMs, and cripples the two major degradation systems (the UPS and autophagy). This comprehensive interference in proteostasis constitutes a fundamental mechanism contributing to the cellular dysfunction and apoptosis observed in DEHP’s diverse target tissues.

An integrated network perspective: cross-talk among metabolic pathways in DEHP toxicity

The systemic metabolic disturbances induced by DEHP and MEHP do not arise from isolated molecular events, but from the dynamic interplay of multiple signaling pathways across organs. Moving beyond a compartmentalized view, this section synthesizes prior evidence to construct an integrated model of DEHP toxicity—a multi-layered network where upstream initiators, midstream effectors, and downstream pathologies are interconnected and mutually reinforcing. In this pathological network, ROS acts as a critical “network hub”. Specifically, ROS generated from DEHP-induced mitochondrial dysfunction directly attacks the ER membrane and oxidizes ER chaperones, serving as a potent inducer of ER stress. Simultaneously, elevated ROS levels trigger the degradation of IκB, leading to the nuclear translocation and activation of NF-κB, which subsequently drives the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Conversely, persistent ER stress and inflammation can further stimulate mitochondrial ROS overproduction, creating a vicious cycle.

The core initiators: nuclear receptor activation and cellular stress

The toxicity of DEHP/MEHP is fundamentally rooted in their simultaneous interaction with key metabolic nuclear receptors and induction of cellular stress. Acting as dual agonists, they activate PPARγ and dysregulate PPARα from the outset, creating a foundational imbalance: PPARγ activation drives adipogenesis and impairs insulin sensitivity, while PPARα dysregulation compromises hepatic fatty acid β-oxidation [17, 18, 58]. Concurrently, DEHP/MEHP induces mitochondrial dysfunction and a surge in ROS. This oxidative burst acts as a critical signaling event, activating stress-sensitive pathways like NF-κB, which drives pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α and IL-6) [19]. Thus, a self-perpetuating cycle is established where primary metabolic disruption (via PPARs) and secondary cellular stress (via ROS/NF-κB) amplify each other, setting the stage for systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction [21].

Convergence on transcriptional hubs: cross-talk among core metabolic regulators

These upstream signals converge on a network of transcriptional regulators that coordinately reprogram carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism.

In lipid metabolism, PPARγ activation upregulates SREBP-1c, the master regulator of de novo lipogenesis, increasing expression of enzymes like FAS and ACC to promote hepatic triglyceride synthesis [32, 38]. This process is often coordinated with LXRs, forming a PPARγ--SREBP-1c–LXR axis that orchestrates lipid and cholesterol metabolism [19]. The AhR, activated by DEHP, further intersects with inflammatory pathways and alters UPS function, linking xenobiotic response to proteostasis disruption.

In glucose metabolism, DEHP/MEHP impair the insulin signaling cascade at multiple nodes, diminishing GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake [69, 70]. In parallel, inhibited Akt leads to activation of FoxO1, which upregulates gluconeogenic enzymes (phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase)), thereby increasing hepatic glucose output [95, 96]. FoxO1 also participates in autophagy regulation, illustrating its dual role in carbohydrate metabolism and cellular clearance. Concurrently, oxidative stress activates the redox-sensitive transcription factor Nrf2, which bolsters antioxidant defenses but can be suppressed by DEHP via epigenetic mechanisms, further tilting the balance toward oxidative damage.

In protein metabolism and cellular stress adaptation, DEHP/MEHP induce ER stress, triggering the UPR and elevating ATF4 and CHOP, which promote apoptosis when stress is unresolved [86, 90]. The autophagy-lysosomal master regulator TFEB is downregulated by DEHP, impairing lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic flux, and contributing to the accumulation of damaged organelles and proteins [24]. Meanwhile, NF-κB serves as a central inflammatory nexus, activated by ROS, ER stress, or gut-derived signals, and in turn promotes insulin resistance by enhancing IRS-1 serine phosphorylation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production.

The gut-organ axis: a critical amplifier and integrator

A paradigm shift in understanding DEHP toxicity is the recognition of the gut-liver and gut-fat axes as central amplifiers. DEHP-induced gut dysbiosis and intestinal barrier damage initiate a cascade of systemic effects [11, 13, 50]. First, the reduction of protective SCFAs like butyrate removes a key regulator of inflammation and energy metabolism [53]. Second, dysbiosis alters the gut bile acid profile, activating intestinal FXR signaling, which suppresses hepatic bile acid synthesis via the FGF15/19 pathway, impairing cholesterol metabolism [15, 54]. Third, a “leaky gut” and dysbiosis activate systemic immune pathways (e.g., TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB), leading to chronic low-grade inflammation that exacerbates insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis [56, 57]. Therefore, the gut microbiota actively modulates and amplifies DEHP’s metabolic toxicity, explaining how local intestinal exposure translates to disorders in distant organs.

Convergence into a pathogenic network

The distinct mechanisms detailed in previous sections are highly interconnected, forming a cohesive pathogenic network:

PPARγ activation sits at a crossroads, intersecting lipid storage, insulin resistance, and inflammatory modulation. Oxidative stress acts as a central connector, with ROS generated from mitochondrial dysfunction linking nearly all aspects of toxicity—triggering ER stress, activating inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and NLRP3, and directly damaging cellular components. Inflammation functions as both cause and consequence: initiated by gut dysbiosis and tissue stress, inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α) further inhibit insulin signaling and promote gluconeogenesis, forming a self-perpetuating cycle. Ultimately, organelle dysfunction serves as a unifying endpoint, where ER stress, impaired autophagy, and mitochondrial damage converge to disrupt core cellular homeostasis, leading to apoptosis in critical cell types including pancreatic β-cells and neurons [24, 25, 78].

This network perspective explains why DEHP exposure is epidemiologically linked to a constellation of co-emerging conditions—obesity, NAFLD, and T2DM—and underscores that its toxicity is characterized by synergistic, cascade-amplifying effects across metabolic pathways.

In conclusion, DEHP, through MEHP, orchestrates systemic “metabolic interference” by hijacking and interconnecting fundamental regulatory networks. Its toxicity emerges from the confluence of direct nuclear receptor activation, pervasive organelle stress, and the amplifying role of the gut ecosystem. Appreciating this interconnectedness is crucial for developing effective safety guidelines, predictive biomarkers, and interventions to mitigate the public health impact of this pervasive environmental contaminant.

Summary of natural compounds and interventions mitigating DEHP toxicity

Protection by natural bioactive compounds

Rather than acting merely as broad-spectrum antioxidants, natural bioactive compounds exhibit highly targeted molecular efficacies across various organs. Lycopene has emerged as a prominent candidate with pleiotropic protective effects against DEHP toxicity. Extensive studies demonstrate that lycopene mitigates multi-organ injury by modulating specific signaling hubs. Specifically, it alleviates hepatic oxidative stress and mitochondrial quality control disorders via the AHR-Nrf2 and SIRT1/PINK1/mitophagy axes [93, 97], prevents renal inflammation through the cGAS-STING pathway [98], and mitigates testicular and cardiac injuries by regulating the unfolded protein response (UPR) and nuclear xenobiotic receptors [99, 100]. Furthermore, it ameliorates neurotoxicity by modulating the gut-brain axis [101].

Beyond lycopene, a spectrum of other phytochemicals provides distinct, tissue-specific defense mechanisms. In the reproductive system, sulforaphane inhibits testicular ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation via the NRF-2/SLC7A11/GPX-4 axis [102]. Lycium barbarum polysaccharide (LBP) maintains cellular iron homeostasis via NRF2 to prevent male reproductive toxicity [103]. Melatonin alleviates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress by downregulating CHOP and GRP-78, thereby improving sperm quality [104]. And morin hydrate and shatavarin-IV protect the female reproductive system (uterus and ovaries) by modulating estrogen receptor expression and apoptotic pathways (Bcl-2/Caspase-3) [105, 106]. In metabolic and hepatic tissues, luteolin uniquely prevents liver injury by promoting the degradation of the Uroc1 protein, thereby accelerating DEHP detoxification and excretion [107]. Pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) protects pancreatic islet β cells from autophagy-dependent apoptosis [79], while serum β-carotene modifies the association between phthalate mixtures and insulin resistance [62]. In the nervous system, Moringa oleifera and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) effectively suppress neurodegeneration by attenuating ER stress markers (e.g., GRP78, ATF4) and upregulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in neuronal cells [108, 109].

Mitigation via specific probiotic strains and formulations

Parallel to phytochemical interventions, modulation of the gut microbiota using specific probiotic strains or defined formulations has proven to be a highly effective strategy for systemic detoxification. As highlighted in recent comprehensive reviews, multi-strain probiotic formulations (predominantly involving Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species) can reverse phthalate-induced gut dysbiosis and avert systemic toxicity [110]. DEHP exposure severely disrupts intestinal barrier integrity; however, interventions with precisely identified bacterial strains offer remarkable restorative advantages. For instance, specific lactic acid bacteria (LAB) strains, such as Lactobacillus plantarum CCFM1019 and Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis CCFM1018, not only physically bind and promote the fecal excretion of DEHP and its metabolite MEHP, but also restore intestinal microbiota balance, subsequently protecting downstream organs such as the liver and testes from secondary oxidative stress [111]. Furthermore, a specific strain of Clostridium butyricum (CGMCC 1.5205) has been shown to effectively alleviate DEHP-induced learning and memory impairments by restoring the gut-brain axis, reducing neuroinflammation, and recovering 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) levels, alongside enriching the beneficial Bifidobacterium genus [12]. These findings underscore the critical importance of identifying precise probiotic strains and formulations to mitigate the systemic burden of phthalate exposure.

Conclusions

DEHP, a quintessential environmental endocrine disruptor, systemically disrupts metabolic homeostasis involving carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins. This “metabolic interference” is primarily mediated by its active metabolite, MEHP, through a complex inter-organ signaling network. DEHP’s toxicity is not only dose-dependent and organ-specific but is also defined by profound synergistic interactions and cascading amplification effects across biological systems, cementing its role as a broad-spectrum metabolic disruptor.

In summary, DEHP, via MEHP, hijacks and interconnects fundamental regulatory networks governing carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism, leading to systemic metabolic interference. Its toxicity arises from the convergence of direct nuclear receptor activation, widespread organelle stress, and an amplifying role played by the gut ecosystem. Understanding this interconnected web is crucial for formulating effective safety guidelines, identifying predictive biomarkers, and developing interventions to mitigate the public health impact of this pervasive contaminant.

Encouragingly, a growing body of evidence suggests that various natural bioactive compounds and probiotics hold potential for mitigating DEHP-induced toxicity, pointing to possible therapeutic or preventive avenues. We summarize the natural compounds and interventions mitigating DEHP toxicity in Table 1. However, the translation of these promising preclinical discoveries into practical clinical applications, such as dietary supplements or pharmacological drugs, faces substantial translational challenges.

A notable challenge in the field is the inconsistent and often interchangeable use of the terms “low-dose” and “environmentally relevant dose” across studies, which complicates cross-study comparisons and risk assessment. Environmentally relevant doses are typically derived from human biomonitoring data that reflect real-world exposure levels. For instance, the median estimated daily intake of DEHP in the general Taiwanese population was reported as 33.9 µg/kg [3], while a study on infant foods in Denmark reported an average intake of 2.7 µg/kg/day [4], providing population-specific benchmarks. In contrast, “low-dose” exposure in toxicological studies often refers to levels below the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) but may still elicit biological effects, particularly during critical developmental windows or in susceptible populations. For example, epidemiological studies have shown that DEHP metabolite levels within the general population range are significantly associated with adverse lipid profiles [30] and insulin resistance [62].

Furthermore, experimental studies have demonstrated that long-term low-dose DEHP exposure can induce obesity and gut microbiota dysbiosis in mice [53], and that even at low concentrations, MEHP can initiate lipid accumulation by inhibiting fatty acid β-oxidation [58]. Prenatal exposure to environmentally relevant doses has also been linked to developmental programming of hypertension in offspring [60]. A major research challenge lies in the lack of standardized definitions and the difficulty in modeling long-term, cumulative effects of mixed exposures that reflect real-world scenarios. Future studies should prioritize dose-response designs that account for species differences, life-stage sensitivity, and mixture effects to better define thresholds for metabolic disruption.

Future research must rigorously address the pharmacokinetics and low bioavailability often associated with these natural agents. Furthermore, establishing the optimal effective dosage and thoroughly evaluating their long-term safety profiles in humans remain major obstacles. The complexity of human validation cannot be overlooked; transitioning from animal models to human populations requires well-designed, large-scale randomized clinical trials to account for inter-individual variability, including genetic diversity, baseline gut microbiome compositions, and mixed environmental co-exposures. Looking ahead, a concerted and multifaceted research agenda is essential to fully decipher and counter the public health threat posed by DEHP.

Future studies must transcend isolated pathway analyses. Priorities should include elucidating tissue- and population-specific susceptibilities, with a particular focus on the consequences of realistic, long-term, low-dose exposure on metabolic networks and the gut microbiome. Integrating multi-omics approaches (e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics) with advanced physiological models (such as organoids and multi-tissue platforms) will be important to systematically mapping the interconnected cascade effects and emergent properties underlying DEHP’s metabolic interference.

From a public health perspective, it is imperative to strengthen regulations limiting DEHP migration from ubiquitous sources like food packaging and medical devices, while accelerating the development and adoption of safer, environmentally benign alternative plasticizers. Concurrently, through interdisciplinary collaboration across academia, industry, and regulatory bodies, research must advance towards the identification of robust exposure biomarkers, the development of early health risk warning systems, and the establishment of science-based, precise intervention strategies. These efforts are fundamental to translating mechanistic insights into actionable measures for protecting public health.
