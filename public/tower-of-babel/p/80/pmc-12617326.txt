Noncoronary Sinus Pivot Implantation (NCPI) Strategy for Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement With Self‐Expanding Valve in Pure Native Aortic Regurgitation: A Multicenter, Registry Study From the CCA Database‐NTCVR
Authors: Feng Xia, XinJing Chen, ZiLong Weng, Xin Pan, JinJun Liu, Hao Zhou, LianPin Wu, YanSong Guo, LongYan Zhang, WenZhi Pan, DaXin Zhou, JunBo Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTranscatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) for pure native aortic regurgitation (PNAR) remains challenging. Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become a standard treatment for aortic stenosis (AS) [1, 2], but its use in pure native aortic regurgitation (PNAR) remains challenging due to anatomical differences [3, 4]. Unlike AS, patients with PNAR typically lack sufficient calcification for valve anchoring, and the regurgitation can cause valve instability or migration during implantation [5]. Despite advancements in valve technology, no transfemoral TAVR devices are specifically approved for PNAR, leading to higher procedural complications compared to patients with AS [5, 6, 7].

To address these challenges, Pan et al. introduced the noncoronary sinus pivot implantation (NCPI) strategy, a novel approach for self‐expanding valves in PNAR [8]. This strategy anchors the transcatheter heart valve above the base of noncoronary sinus, preventing valve displacement while allowing the valve into the outflow tract in left and right coronary sinuses side. Previously published studies using the cylindrical VitaFlow valve (MicroPort, Shanghai, China) demonstrated that NCPI significantly improved device success and reduced complications such as valve‐in‐valve (VIV) implantation and permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI) [8, 9]. However, the original NCPI studies were limited by their single‐center, small‐sample designs and exclusive use of the cylindrical VitaFlow valve [10]; the findings may therefore be device‐specific. It is important to note that NCPI is a strategy that depends on predictable anchoring mechanics rather than specific device branding. Therefore, to evaluate the broader feasibility of NCPI across different transcatheter heart valve designs, we initiated the multicenter study using the TaurusElite valve (Peijia Medical, Suzhou, China). To evaluate the broader feasibility of NCPI across different transcatheter heart valve designs, we initiated a multicenter study using the TaurusElite valve (Peijia Medical, Suzhou, China), a retrievable, self‐expanding system featuring a cone‐shaped inflow end that provides high radial force.

Since April 2024, building upon the establishment of China's national valve intervention training platform, the NCPI strategy has been formally promoted across major valve centers as the preferred transfemoral TAVR approach for PNAR, particularly with the TaurusElite valve. This study aims to evaluate the real‐world efficacy of the TaurusElite valve in patients with PNAR and to assess the impact of the NCPI strategy on procedural outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

The National Transcatheter Valve Therapeutics Registry (NTCVR), initiated by the Chinese Cardiovascular Association (CCA), is the most extensive database encompassing TAVR procedures conducted in China. In this national registry‐based study, we analyzed data from a multicenter cohort involving seven tertiary hospitals across China, namely Zhongshan Hospital affiliated with Fudan University, Wuhan Asia General Hospital, Fujian Provincial Hospital affiliated with Fuzhou University, Shanghai Chest Hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University, the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College, the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. In total, data from seven centers were included, and all patient information was retrieved from the NTCVR database [11].

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Moderate‐to‐severe or severe PNAR with indications for aortic valve replacement surgery; (2) surgical contraindications or intermediate‐to‐high surgical risk in patients aged ≥ 65 years, as determined by the heart team; (3) suitable aortic root and access anatomy; (4) procedure performed at experienced centers or under the supervision of proficient interventionist; (5) expected survival of more than 1 year after the procedure; and (6) undergoing trans‐femoral TAVR using TaurusElite retrievable self‐expanding valve (Peijia Medical, Suzhou, China) [12]. Primary exclusion criteria included: patients with failed bioprosthetic valves, the effective orifice area of aortic valve < 2.0 cm2, a peak aortic valve pressure gradient > 20 mmHg as measured by echocardiography, an annular perimeter over 88 mm, or an ascending aorta diameter greater than 50 mm based on computed tomography angiography.

Between April 2022 and October 2024, 121 patients who underwent transfemoral TAVR for PNAR using the TaurusElite self‐expanding valve were included in the study (Figure 1). Since April 2024, the NCPI strategy has been promoted across valve centers in China as the primary approach for transfemoral TAVR in patients with PNAR. Patients were categorized into early (before April 2024) and late (after April 2024) groups according to procedure date. All intraoperative TAVR Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA) images from participating centers were centrally collected and sent to the core laboratory (Wuhan Asia Heart Hospital) for analysis. These images were reviewed by a standardized team consisting of one professional technician and two TAVR experts to assess whether the patients achieved NCPI at the pre‐deployment working phase, when the valve reached its recapturable limit position, the delivery system was coaxial, and the valve stent exhibited a slightly concave shape on the noncoronary side, before final device release. Based on this assessment, patients were further categorized into NCPI strategy and non‐NCPI strategy subgroups. Baseline characteristics, procedural details, and complications according to Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 (VARC‐3) criteria [13] post‐TAVR were compared between the groups. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional ethics committee of each center.

Endpoints and Follow‐Up

The primary outcome was device success defined according to VARC‐3 criteria as achieving technical success, freedom from mortality, and major device‐related complications, as well as intended valve performance with an acceptable hemodynamic outcome within 30 days [13]. Major adverse cardiac events (MACE) data collected within 30 days included all‐cause death, transfer to surgery, PPI, VIV implantation, cerebrovascular events, and coronary obstruction. Follow‐up visits at 30 days included assessments of New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, electrocardiogram, and echocardiography.

TAVR Procedures and NCPI Strategy

Multislice computed tomography angiography (MSCT) using Anythink Structural Heart & Vascular software (Beijing Crealife Technology Co. Ltd) was utilized to perform a detailed assessment of aortic annular dimensions, including aortic root anatomy, as well as the diameter, calcification, and patency of the femoral access routes, to guide the selection of TAVR approach and valve size for each patient. TAVR procedures were performed in a modified catheterization laboratory or hybrid operating room under general anesthesia, via the transfemoral approach and guided by fluoroscopy and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). The TaurusElite retrievable self‐expanding valve was (Figure 2) implanted in the procedures with initial positioning ensuring the horizontal alignment of the sinus bottoms and placement of the noncoronary cusp (NCC) at the lowest point. On the X‐ray view, the noncoronary sinus appeared on the right side of the screen, and the left coronary sinus on the left. Implantation depth was measured on both sides based on these views. Valve size was chosen based on a 15%−30% oversizing ratio.

The design and dimensions of the TaurusElite transcatheter aortic valve. The TaurusElite valve system is a self‐expanding valve system consisting of the aortic valve, delivery system, and deployment system. The valve leaflets are made from bovine pericardial tissue, and the stent frame is designed with enhanced radial support. The inflow end is equipped with inner and outer double skirts, which effectively prevent or reduce paravalvular leakage. This study used the TaurusElite valves in sizes 23, 26, 29, and 31. The procedure steps of the NCPI strategy have been previously described in detail [8, 9]. The NCPI is considered successful if two conditions are satisfied: the noncoronary sinus side valve implantation depth ranges from −2 and 2 mm, and the released valve stent on the noncoronary side exhibits a slightly concave shape on the noncoronary tangent X‐ray view. To achieve NCPI, the valve was initially positioned and released 0−2 mm above the base of the noncoronary sinus bottom but remained below the upper edge of the annulus (the lower end of the stent was still inside the annulus). This positioning, along with the delivery system's attachment to the noncoronary sinus, prevented downward valve movement during release. However, the initial valve position should not be too high to avoid stent shift into the noncoronary sinus.

After reaching the retrievable limit, valve release was paused to evaluate NCPI status, requiring minimal delivery system tension. While NCPI ensured accurate noncoronary side positioning, the opposite side (left and right coronary cusps) might slightly extend into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) due to stent‐aortic axis mismatch. However, depth was kept ≤ 12 mm to avoid post‐release downward migration. TEE confirmed proper valve alignment within the native noncoronary leaflet before final deployment. The NCPI status assessment adhered to the PASS principle (Figure 3), encompassing Position, Axial, Shape, and Sizing. If the NCPI status was achieved, the valve was finally released, otherwise the valve was retrieved and repositioned. If the NCPI strategy was unsuccessful after five attempts, the procedure was switched to the conventional approach, positioning the stent beneath all sinuses.

The details of the PASS principle (in the case of the TaurusElite valve). Position: The noncoronary sinus side implantation depth of the valve should be between −2 and 2 mm. Axial: The delivery system should have no tension, and the valve should be relatively coaxial to the aorta, in which the implant depth of the valve on the left coronary sinus side should be < 12 mm. Shape: The inferior end of the valve stent on the noncoronary sinus side should be in a slightly concave shape. Sizing: The oversizing rate of the valve should be 15%–30%; Green: Good valve compression with NCC retraction and lesser curvature compression, low risk of self‐axial displacement; Yellow: Moderate valve compression with NCC retraction but lesser curvature expansion, risk of self‐axial displacement depends on NCC anchoring and depth; Red: Poor valve compression with NCC expansion and lesser curvature expansion, high risk of self‐axial displacement. Oversizing rate: ([Prosthetic aortic valve perimeter ÷ native aortic valve annulus perimeter] − 1) × 100. NCC, noncoronary cusp. [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or as median (IQR). Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were compared with t‐tests or the Wilcoxon rank–sum test for nonnormally distributed data. An analysis of covariance model was used to compare mean changes in continuous variables from baseline to follow‐up between groups. A sensitivity analysis with multiple imputation was performed to account for missing data. A bilateral p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance for all estimations. All analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (Stata Corp LLC; College Station, TX, USA).

The mean age was 72.7 ± 8.0 years, and the mean Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) risk score was 4.6 ± 3.8% and 82 (67.7%) in NYHA class III−IV. There were 45 patients in the early group and 76 patients in the late group. No differences in age, sex, STS risk score, left ventricle size, laboratory parameters, and cardiac function occurred between the groups. The device success rate tended to be higher in the late group than in the early group (94.7% vs. 84.4%, p = 0.057). The rates of PPI (2.6% vs. 17.8%, p = 0.024), intraprocedural new‐onset high‐degree atrioventricular block (HAVB) (2.6% vs. 15.5%, p = 0.046), and new‐onset left bundle branch block (LBBB) (12.1% vs. 28.6%, p = 0.033) were significantly lower in the late group than in the early group. The late group had a significantly higher successful NCPI rate than the early group (90.8% vs. 26.7%, p < 0.001). In the late group, seven patients failed to achieve successful NCPI. One patient, with an annular perimeter of 87 mm and an area of 578.6 mm², had an aortic angulation (AA) of 78°. Despite multiple NCPI attempts, anchoring failed, requiring surgical conversion. Another patient, with an AA of 60°, experienced immediate valve displacement after release, necessitating VIV implantation. A third patient, with an annular perimeter of 88 mm, showed moderate‐to‐severe paravalvular leakage on post‐deployment angiography, prompting VIV implantation. The remaining four patients had excessive NCC implantation depth (6−8 mm). In the early group, anchoring failed in two patients: one with an annular perimeter of 87 mm and an area of 554.7 mm², with an AA of 54°, and another with an excessively large AA (73°), requiring surgical conversion.

No patients were lost to follow‐up. At 30‐day follow‐up, only one patient died, which occurred in the early group due to delayed coronary obstruction and was assessed as not achieved NCPI criteria. Compared to baseline, a significant reduction in the proportion of patients in NYHA class III–IV was observed at the 30‐day follow‐up.

The rates of VIV (0% vs. 16.2%, p < 0.001) and PPI (3.7% vs. 18.9%, p = 0.006) were significantly lower in the NCPI strategy group than in the non‐NCPI strategy group. In the NCPI strategy group, one patient underwent conversion to open surgery due to intraoperative TEE suggesting aortic dissection after valve deployment. The incidence of new‐onset LBBB and intraprocedural new‐onset HAVB was higher in the non‐NCPI strategy group compared to the NCPI group. No annular rupture, life‐threatening bleeding, or valve embolization was observed, and no other perioperative complications occurred between the subgroups.

Predictors of Device Success

Multivariate analysis indicated that successful NCPI was an independent predictor of device success (odds ratio [OR]: 6.08, 95% CI: 3.27–28.68, p = 0.025). Other factors, including operation timing (early vs. late), aortic annulus circumference, LVOT circumference, and oversize rate, were not independently associated with device success.

Discussion

The main findings of this multicenter study were that the transfemoral TaurusElite retrievable self‐expanding system demonstrated favorable performance in treating patients with PNAR and that using the TaurusElite valve with the NCPI strategy achieved higher device success rates and significantly reduced complications compared to conventional methods. The study shows that the NCPI strategy is feasible in the real‐world setting of a multi‐center cohort, with consistent effectiveness and favorable clinical outcomes across different self‐expanding valve types.

TAVR for PNAR presents unique challenges that are not as prevalent in AS [3, 14, 15]. NCPI aims to address the issue of downward valve shift after release by anchoring the prosthetic valve above the noncoronary sinus and securing the stent's lower edge in the LVOT on both the left and right coronary sinus sides, thereby preventing upward movement. Currently, there are no commercially available transcatheter heart valve devices specifically designed for transfemoral TAVR in PNAR. Although studies have demonstrated that performing transfemoral TAVR for PNAR using valves in an “off‐label” manner is feasible, it can only be applied to anatomically suitable patients, with a procedural success rate of approximately 70%−80%. Despite a significant increase in success rates with second‐generation valves, which offer retrievability, these rates rarely exceed 90% [14, 16, 17]. Our study used the TaurusElite retrievable self‐expanding valve in combination with the NCPI strategy, achieving a device success rate of 98.7%, which is consistent with the results of previous single‐center, small‐sample studies using VitaFlow self‐expanding valves [9]. This further validates the effectiveness of the NCPI strategy in overcoming the challenges of TAVR in PNAR.

The device success rate tended to be higher in the late group compared to the early group (94.7% vs. 84.4%, p = 0.057). The late group also had a lower rate of new‐onset early group PPI (2.6% vs. 17.8%, p = 0.003) and new‐onset LBBB (11.8% vs. 33.3%) as compared with the early group. In the late group, 90.8% patients using the NCPI strategy while 26.7% of the patients in early group used NCPI strategy. The device success was mainly attributed to the use of NCPI strategy as showed by multiple analysis. Notably, device failure in the late group was primarily associated with anatomical constraints, particularly large annular dimensions and extreme AA. Specifically, one case with a severely angulated aorta (AA = 78°) experienced anchoring failure despite multiple NCPI attempts, requiring surgical conversion. Another case (AA = 60°) suffered immediate valve displacement post‐release, necessitating VIV implantation, while a third patient with a large annular perimeter (88 mm) developed significant paravalvular leakage, also requiring VIV. These findings underscore the impact of AA and annular size on NCPI outcomes, consistent with previous studies suggesting that excessive AA (> 60°) may predispose to deep implantation and valve migration, whereas smaller AA may lead to inadequate anchoring [9]. While NCPI has improved procedural success, patient selection remains critical, particularly in those with extreme anatomical variations. Nonetheless, we successfully performed an NCPI of a 29 mm TaurusElite valve in a patient with extreme root dilatation (sinus of Valsalva 50.2 mm) and no calcification, which was published in JACC: Case Reports [18].

One of the key findings of this multicenter study is the consistent success and safety of the NCPI strategy, which was shown to significantly improve device success rates and reduce complications compared to conventional methods. The success of this NCPI strategy was achieved in 90.9% of patients in the late group. This study provides robust evidence supporting the real‐world feasibility of the NCPI strategy for TAVR in patients with PNAR using a self‐expanding valve across multiple centers, across different clinical settings, involving various operators, reflecting its practical applicability in real‐world scenarios. The evidence from this study further suggests that with proper training and adherence to procedural guidelines, the NCPI strategy can be standardized and effectively implemented in diverse hospital settings.

The NCPI strategy is not limited to a specific self‐expanding valve design. Although previous studies have only demonstrated the effectiveness of the NCPI strategy with the VitaFlow valve [8, 9], this study shows that the NCPI strategy is equally effective with other self‐expanding valves, such as TaurusElite. The valve, developed by Peijia Medical, incorporates a waist‐shaped design that provides a stable anchoring mechanism for the valve, allowing for precise deployment and minimizing the risk of valve displacement [19]. Despite differences in valve design—TaurusElite features a cone‐shaped frame, whereas VitaFlow has a cylindrical design—the NCPI strategy achieved comparable clinical outcomes across all valve types. This finding highlights the flexibility of the NCPI strategy. It suggests that NCPI could become a standard implantation strategy for a variety of self‐expanding valves used in TAVR, expanding its application to a wider range of patients with varying anatomical features.

One of the pivotal technical advantages of the NCPI strategy lies in its ability to significantly reduce the incidence of VIV implantation (0% vs. 16.2%, p < 0.018) and PPI (3.7% vs. 18.9%, p = 0.006), while also lowering the rate of new‐onset LBBB compared to the non‐NCPI strategy group (17.7% vs. 43.2%, p < 0.001). In the NCPI strategy group, the PPI rate is only 3.7% and the device success rate is 98.7%. This suggests that in anatomically suitable patients (with an annulus size < 83 mm), the use of current self‐expanding valves with the NCPI strategy produces results that are not inferior to those of valves specifically designed for aortic regurgitation and even demonstrates a lower PPI rate, such as with the Trilogy valve (as high as 24%) [20].

This reduction in conduction disturbances may be attributed to the NCPI strategy's emphasis on precise valve positioning above the noncoronary sinus, which minimizes mechanical trauma to the LBBB. The left bundle branch is anatomically situated near the base of the interleaflet triangle between the noncoronary and right coronary aortic leaflets. Its proximity to the aortic annulus renders it vulnerable to compression or injury from the stent frame during valve deployment, often resulting in edema, hematoma, or ischemia [21, 22, 23]. These mechanisms are well‐documented contributors to LBBB, which may progress to high‐grade or complete atrioventricular block over time. The relationship between new‐onset LBBB and PPI after TAVR remains debated, especially when stable post‐procedural conduction parameters are maintained [24, 25].

Conclusion

The transfemoral TaurusElite retrievable self‐expanding system demonstrated favorable performance in treating patients with PNAR, and its use with the NCPI strategy demonstrated real‐world feasibility and broad applicability, achieving high device success rates and significantly reducing complications compared to conventional methods.

Limitations

Some limitations of this trial should be noted. First, this study is not a randomized controlled trial, and it is a retrospective study with its inherent limitations. However, the study design included a core laboratory for assessing the NCPI effect and grouping, which helped ensure the comparability of multicenter data. Additionally, there are certain differences in the procedural techniques of different centers, and the success rate of the procedure may vary among different centers. However, in this study, the core laboratory was used to calibrate the intraoperative images, and the analysis of the impact of NCPI on procedural results was relatively objective.

Consent

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of each center. The ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent for this retrospective analysis due to the use of anonymized and deidentified data; in addition, no interventions were done to the study population.
