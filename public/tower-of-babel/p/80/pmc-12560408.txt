Impact of defect size and bone or tendon exposure on outcomes of artificial dermis repair for finger defects
Authors: Xiuxiu Zhang, Zhonghan Wu, Yining Wang, Jisen Zhang, Jialiu Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine the outcomes of artificial dermis in repairing finger pulp defects of varying sizes, with a focus on how defect size and exposure of bone or tendon influence sensory recovery, scarring, and other clinical results.MethodsThis prospective multicenter study assessed the efficacy of Lando® artificial dermis in 30 patients (36 fingers) treated between June 2022 and October 2023. Outcomes included sensory recovery (via Semmes-Weinstein Monofilament [SWM] and two-point discrimination [2-PD]), scarring (Vancouver Scar Scale [VSS)]), pain, and cold intolerance. Linear regressions were used to analyze associations between defect characteristics and recovery.ResultsOver a mean follow-up of 15.7 months, 14 patients reported residual numbness, with mean 2-PD of 5.9 mm and SWM of 3.87. All patients developed scars; 20 fingers showed tissue thinning, 14 had nail deformities, and 1 developed a flexion contracture. Larger defects were linked to worse 2-PD and higher VSS scores, and exposure of bone or tendon was associated with poorer sensory recovery.ConclusionArtificial dermis offers reliable coverage for finger pulp defects but shows clear limitations in larger defects and those with bone or tendon exposure, where sensory recovery and aesthetic outcomes are compromised. These findings underscore the need for cautious patient selection and further comparative studies.

Introduction

Finger injuries often lead to extensive soft tissue defects that may expose bones, tendons, and joints, necessitating soft tissue coverage. The primary objective is to restore and preserve finger function, including length, mobility, sensation, and stability, while minimizing complications [1, 2]. Notably, palmar skin differs from dorsal skin in its greater thickness, richer innervation, and limited elasticity [3]. In cases of finger pulp defects, sensory recovery is prioritized over the restoration of appearance and length. Traditional approaches, such as pedicled and free flap repairs, as well as self-regeneration using occlusive dressings, remain widely employed and effective [4–7].

Over the past 15 years, dermal substitutes have been increasingly employed in burn management and reconstructive surgery [3]. Previous studies have shown that artificial dermis can effectively promote healing of finger defects [8–10]. However, these studies primarily focused on smaller finger pulp defects, with limited detailed reporting on two -point discrimination (2-PD) outcomes [11]. Moreover, our prior research indicated that although artificial dermis provides adequate coverage for larger finger pulp (>1.5 cm), it does not fully restore normal sensation and inevitably leads to scar formation [12].

Previous studies have explored the use of artificial dermis for finger pulp defects; however, inconsistencies in defect size and limited outcome reporting have contributed to variable results. To address this gap, we conducted a prospective analysis to evaluate clinical recovery across a range of defect sizes (1.0–3.8 cm). Patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months, with assessments including sensory recovery, cold sensitivity, pain, scar formation, finger pulp thinning, and nail deformities. Additionally, this study analyzes the correlation between clinical outcomes and the characteristics of finger soft tissue defects to identify defect types that are less suitable for artificial dermis repair. We hypothesize that artificial dermis treatment yields poorer clinical outcomes in larger defects or in those with exposed bone.

Patients and methods

We conducted a multicenter, prospective study analyzing data from 40 patients who underwent one-stage reconstruction using Lando® artificial dermis (Lando Biomedical Materials Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) between June 2022 and October 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to surgery, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (No. YX2022-129(F1)).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) soft tissue defects of the pulp of fingers 2–5; (2) age between 18 and 70 years; and (3) informed consent obtained from both the patient and their family.

The exclusion criteria included: (1) hand soft tissue injuries complicated by infection; (2) flexor tendon tears or finger fractures; (3) comorbidities such as diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, or renal insufficiency; and (4) dorsal finger injuries or injuries extending beyond the fingers.

Surgical procedure and postoperative care

As in our previous study [12], surgery was performed within 6–8 h of injury under either local or nerve block anesthesia. Partial bone removal was performed for exposed phalanges to ensure adequate template coverage. The dermal template was soaked in saline, shaped to fit the defect, and secured with 4 − 0 Prolene sutures (Ethicon US, LLC, Somerville, NJ, USA) to enhance adhesion. Surgical repairs were performed by three surgeons classified as Level 3 or 4 according to Tang and Giddins’ grading system for expertise in managing finger soft tissue defects [13, 14]. Postoperative care was standardized, with emphasis on infection prevention through regular dressing changes and timely suture removal between three and six weeks.

Outcome assessment

All procedures were performed by the first and last authors, who, following comprehensive training, also conducted all subsequent follow-up assessments.

Sensation

Sensory function at the repaired site was assessed using the Semmes-Weinstein Monofilament (SWM) test (North Coast Medical, Inc., Gilroy, CA, USA) [15] and static 2-PD [16], centered on the original wound. The 2-PD test was carefully administered with the Disk-Criminator (North Coast Medical, Inc., Gilroy, CA, USA) to ensure precise alignment and consistent contact with the distal phalanx.

Scar assessment

Scarring was first assessed qualitatively for presence and severity, followed by quantitative evaluation using the Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) [17], which scores color, thickness, vascularity, and pliability. Each parameter was rated according to standardized criteria to provide a comprehensive scar profile.

Pain and cold tolerance assessment

Pain was quantified using the visual analog scale (VAS), and cold intolerance was assessed using a self-administered questionnaire [18]. Both measures categorize severity to facilitate comparison and monitoring of patient outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as mean (standard deviation) or n (%). Linear regression analyses were performed to examine the associations between defect size, bone or tendon exposure, and clinical outcomes. Three separate linear regression models were constructed to evaluate the relationships of defect size and bone or tendon exposure with 2-PD, SWM, and VSS, respectively. All models were adjusted for age and sex.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted for participants with bone or tendon exposure to evaluate the effects of defect characteristics on 2-PD, SWM, and VSS, adjusting for age and sex. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (http://www.R-project.org). A two-sided p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographic

In this prospective study, 40 patients were initially enrolled. However, 10 patients were lost to follow-up due to changes in contact information. Consequently, data from 30 patients (36 fingers) were analyzed at the conclusion of the study. This cohort included 18 males and 12 females, with a mean age of 47.2 years (range, 24–68 years) and a mean a mean follow-up duration of 15.7 months (range, 12–31 months).

All injuries were caused by machinery crush accidents. Thirteen patients sustained injuries to their dominant hands, and 12 exhibited bone or tendon exposure. Specifically, the injuries involved 16 index fingers, 9 middle fingers, 8 ring fingers, and 3 little fingers. The mean wound length was 2.13 cm (range, 1.0–3.8 cm), with an average width of 1.28 cm (range, 0.8–1.8 cm). Twenty-six wounds were located on the fingertip pulp, two on the lateral aspect, and eight involved both the pulp and lateral regions, with 16 wounds extending to or beyond the transverse lines of the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint. Patient characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Sutures were removed on average at 4.5 weeks postoperatively (range, 3–6 weeks), with no signs of infection observed. All patients ultimately returned to their original work duties.

Scar conditions

Fourteen patients exhibited varying degrees of nail deformities resulting from injuries involving the nail bed (Fig. 1, Case 27). All patients demonstrated some degree of scar formation, with soft tissue defects on 20 fingers showing varying levels of thinning, leading to reduced thickness compared with the unaffected side (Fig. 2, Case 16). In one case (Fig. 3, Case 1), scar formation caused a flexion contracture of the DIP joint, corresponding to a VSS score of 5 (range, 2–11), as detailed in Table 2.

Pain and cold tolerance

Two patients reported experiencing stabbing pain at the fingertips, with a mean VAS score of 0.3. Additionally, eleven patients (involving 13 fingers) reported skin hypersensitivity, and ten patients exhibited mild cold intolerance, as indicated by CISS scores ranging from 10 to 34 (Table 2).

Association analyses

As shown in Table 3, defect size (standardized β = 1.22; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.06–2.38; p = 0.04) and the presence of bone or tendon exposure (standardized β = -1.76; 95% CI, -3.45 to -0.01; p = 0.0485) were associated with a higher risk of poor 2-PD outcomes, but showed no significant association with SWM. Furthermore, defect size was positively correlated with VSS scores (standardized β = 1.48; 95% CI, 0.31–2.65; p = 0.015). Subgroup analyses revealed that defect size was significantly associated with 2-PD (standardized β = 1.07; 95% CI, 0.33–1.81; p = 0.007) in cases without bone/tendon exposure, and with SWM (standardized β = 0.61; 95% CI, 0.26–0.96; p = 0.003) in cases with bone or tendon exposure.

Discussion

This prospective study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and optimal indications for using artificial dermis in the treatment of finger soft tissue defects. At the final follow-up, the mean 2-PD was 5.9 mm, and the mean SWM score was 3.87. All patients exhibited scar formation, with 24 fingers showing soft tissue thinning and 14 exhibiting nail deformities. Regression analyses indicated that larger defect sizes and the presence of bone or tendon exposure were significantly associated with poorer 2-PD outcomes. Additionally, defect size correlated positively with VSS scores. Subgroup analyses further demonstrated a strong association between defect size and 2-PD outcomes, particularly in cases without bone or tendon exposure. These findings provide important insights into the limitations and optimal indications for artificial dermis use, thereby informing and refining treatment strategies for finger soft tissue defects.

This study identified a significant association between the presence of bone or tendon exposure and poorer outcomes in 2-PD. Artificial dermis has been effectively used to treat digital and hand injuries with soft tissue defects involving exposed bone or tendon [19, 20]. Our previous research demonstrated that sensory recovery is more favorable in fingers treated with dermal templates, when bone or tendon is not exposed, compared with cases where such exposures are present [12]. This finding is consistent with expectations, as subcutaneous tissue defects present substantial challenges for nerve fiber regeneration.

Our regression analyses revealed a significant correlation between larger defect sizes and poorer sensory outcomes, including impaired 2-PD and higher VSS scores. Previous studies have shown that using a reverse homodigital dorsoradial flap without neurorrhaphy for soft tissue defects of the thumb results in considerable variation in flap size and static 2-PD scores, supporting the relationship between defect size and sensory recovery [21]. Optimal nerve recovery depends on precise alignment of nerve ends, adequate blood supply, and sufficient soft tissue coverage, with defects exceeding 2.5 cm typically requiring nerve grafting [22]. Soft tissue defect size is inherently associated with nerve injury, which affects sensory recovery outcomes. Additionally, the initial defect size and time to healing are key determinants of scar severity [23], with larger and deeper wounds undergoing prolonged maturation processes [24]. Collectively, these findings indicate that larger soft tissue defects are associated with more severe sensory deficits (worsened 2-PD) and higher VSS scores.

In our study, 24 fingers with soft tissue thinning and 14 fingers with nail bed injuries exhibited varying degrees of nail deformities. Previous research has shown that artificial dermis, used for reconstructing nail bed defects generally produces reliable repair outcomes and satisfactory aesthetic results [25, 26]. However, hook nail deformity remains a common complication, with reported incidence rates ranging from 7% to 50% in adults [27, 28]. Lim et al. reported that nail beaking in reconstructed fingertips may worsen over time due to chronic flap contraction and soft tissue soft tissue remodeling, even when initial outcomes appear satisfactory [29]. Other studies often document soft tissue thinning without specifying aesthetic outcomes, suggesting that artificial dermis may have limited effectiveness in fully restoring normal finger volume [3, 30]. In our cohort, nail deformities and finger pulp thinning were the primary aesthetic concerns reported by patients. These findings underscore the importance of ongoing monitoring and management of nail deformities post-reconstruction, and highlight the need to refine surgical techniques and postoperative care to optimize long-term outcomes in patients with nail bed injuries.

This prospective study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size (30 patients, 36 fingers) limits the statistical power to detect subtle associations, particularly in subgroup analyses. Larger, multicenter trials with extended follow-up are needed to validate these findings and refine the indications for artificial dermis in finger reconstruction. Second, all participants were manual laborers, who may exhibit reduced finger sensitivity compared to the general population, with variations in manual work potentially influencing perception. Notably, 2-PD values can vary considerably among individuals from different occupational backgrounds, suggesting that prolonged manual labor may decrease finger sensitivity and increase pain tolerance. Third, final measurements and evaluations were performed independently by only two investigators, which could have affected the consistency of outcome assessments. Finally, a major limitation of this study is the absence of a control group, such as patients treated with local or free flaps, limiting the ability to compare the relative efficacy of artificial dermis with established reconstructive techniques. Future studies should include comparative cohorts to strengthen conclusions regarding optimal surgical strategies.

This study highlights the variable efficacy of artificial dermis in treating finger soft tissue defects, particularly in cases involving larger defects or the presence of bone or tendon exposure, factors that significantly compromise sensory recovery. This is reflected in impaired 2-PD and elevated VSS scores. While artificial dermis is effective in many cases, its performance declines notably when addressing complex injuries involving critical anatomical structures. These findings clarify the specific limitations and appropriate indications for artificial dermis use, providing valuable insights to guide the refinement of treatment strategies and improve outcomes for patients with complex finger injuries.
