Modifying VEGF-A mRNA by combinatorial optimization to enhance therapeutic efficacy for myocardial infarction
Authors: Wei Wang, Zhenping Zhan, Lan Chen, Zhonghua Wang, Lei Wang, Shizheng Liu, Zirong Lin, Zhaoyi Yang, Kanglin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A) is a highly specific endothelial cell mitogen that critically regulates angiogenesis and vascular network formation. While VEGF-A mRNA administration enables dose-dependent protein expression and therapeutic angiogenesis without genomic integration, unmodified mRNA suffers from rapid degradation and inherent immunogenicity, which substantially limits its therapeutic potential. In this study, we designed and synthesized a combinatorially optimized modified VEGF-A mRNA construct, designated Km10566, engineered to enhance translational efficiency and reduce immunogenicity through strategic incorporation of modified nucleosides. In vitro transcription (IVT) assays demonstrated that Km10566 yields substantially increased VEGF-A protein expression compared to unmodified counterparts. Following intracardiac administration in a rat model of myocardial infarction, a single dose of Km10566 formulated in citrate saline buffer significantly improved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and reduced myocardial fibrosis at 21 days post-treatment. Pharmacodynamic analysis confirmed sustained VEGF-A expression and enhanced vascularization in the ischemic myocardium. These findings establish Km10566 as a promising therapeutic candidate for cardiac repair and support its further evaluation in preclinical and clinical development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46680-x.Keywords: In vitro transcription, Synthetic mRNA, mRNA-based therapeutics, VEGF-A, mRNA, Myocardial infarctionSubject terms: Biotechnology, Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics have emerged as a transformative platform for regenerative medicine, offering distinct advantages over DNA- and protein-based approaches including transient expression, absence of genomic integration, rapid manufacturing, and favorable safety profiles1. The successful clinical deployment of mRNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated interest in expanding this technology beyond infectious diseases to cardiovascular applications2,3.

Vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A) is a master regulator of angiogenesis, stimulating endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and survival while promoting vascular permeability and progenitor cell differentiation4. Among multiple isoforms, VEGF-A165 is the most abundant and potent, exhibiting optimal bioavailability and receptor-binding characteristics4,5. Following myocardial infarction (MI), VEGF-A expression increases transiently at the border zone within hours, initiating an angiogenic response that is subsequently suppressed, contributing to inadequate vascular repair and adverse remodeling. This temporal expression pattern suggests that exogenous VEGF-A supplementation in a controlled, pulse-like manner could enhance neovascularization and improve cardiac outcomes.

Early clinical efforts using recombinant VEGF-A protein or gene transfer failed to demonstrate consistent efficacy, likely due to short protein half-life, uncontrolled long-term expression from viral vectors, and safety concerns including inappropriate angiogenesis and vascular permeability4,6,7. mRNA-based delivery addresses these limitations by enabling transient, dose-dependent protein expression without genomic integration. However, unmodified mRNA triggers innate immune responses through Toll-like receptor activation and exhibits susceptibility to degradation, compromising therapeutic efficacy8,9.

Substantial advances in mRNA engineering have overcome these barriers. Incorporation of modified nucleosides, particularly N1-methylpseudouridine (N1-Ψ), markedly reduces immunogenicity by abrogating recognition by pattern recognition receptors while enhancing translational efficiency9–11. Optimization of untranslated regions (UTRs), coding sequences, cap structures, and poly(A) tails further improves mRNA stability and protein output12–16. These combinatorial optimization strategies have enabled the clinical translation of VEGF-A mRNA therapeutics. AZD8601, a modified VEGF-A mRNA developed by Moderna and AstraZeneca, completed Phase II clinical trials in patients with heart failure undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting, demonstrating safety and tolerability with exploratory signals of improved ventricular function17. Preclinical studies in small and large animal MI models confirmed that intracardiac administration of optimized VEGF-A mRNA increases vascular density, reduces fibrosis, and improves systolic function7,18,19. Mechanistically, VEGF-A mRNA promotes cardiac repair by mobilizing epicardial progenitor cells and directing their differentiation toward endothelial lineages19,20.

Despite these advances, opportunities remain to further enhance VEGF-A mRNA performance through systematic optimization of structural elements. In this study, we designed and synthesized a combinatorially optimized VEGF-A mRNA construct, designated Km10566, engineered to maximize translational efficiency and stability. Following intracardiac administration in a rat model of MI, we evaluated its capacity to improve left ventricular function and reduce myocardial fibrosis. Comparative analysis with unmodified and circular mRNA constructs assessed the relative contributions of specific modifications to therapeutic efficacy. Our findings establish Km10566 as a promising candidate for further preclinical and clinical development in ischemic heart disease.

Results

Superior translational efficiency capabilities obtained from the optimized mRNA

To confirm the structure of VEGF-A mRNA with a enhanced translation efficiency and prolonged half-life, we systematically designed and screened extensive component combinations (Fig. 1A). The mRNA was synthesized via in vitro transcription (IVT) and subsequently transfected into HEK293T cells (see Materials and Methods for details). We employed a stepwise approach by fixing all components except one, then sequentially testing 30 optimized codon sequences (Fig. 1B), 20 combinations of untranslated regions (UTRs) (Fig. 1C), and four types of poly(A) tails (Fig. 1D), while holding other components constant. The AZD8601 sequence served as a reference control17. Detailed sequence information is provided in Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 1B shows in CDS sheet, Fig. 1C in UTR sheet, and Fig. 1D in the poly(A) & Modification sheet. AZD8601 served as a control in these experiments, and its protein expression levels were used as a benchmark for comparison. The elements selected were CDS105, Cyn5U6, Cyn3U5, and A120 (Fig. 1B–D).

Furthermore, chemical modifications of RNA bases have been shown to alter biophysical properties, influencing RNA secondary structure, folding, or interactions with specific RNA-binding proteins9. Prior studies demonstrated that synthetic mRNA incorporating modified nucleotides exhibits improved stability and reduced immunogenicity. In this study, we synthesized mRNA using five previously reported efficacious modified bases: N6-Methyl-ATP (m6A), 5-Methoxy-UTP (5moU), Pseudouridine (Ψ), 5-Methyl-CTP (m5C), and N1-Methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ)9,10. The translational efficiency of each modification was assessed using the same method described above. Results indicated that m1Ψ modification achieved the highest protein expression level (Fig. 1D). This optimal combination, designated Km10566, exhibited approximately two-fold increase in protein expression compared to AZD8601 in HEK293T cells (Fig. 1E).

Km10566 rapidly and sustainably improves cardiac function in myocardial infarction

We evaluated the effects of optimized mRNA on cardiac function in vivo in a rat model of MI, induced by ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery. Following the intracardiac injection of a single dose luciferase mRNA in citrate saline buffer into the peri-infant region, we observed successful transfection and protein expression. In vivo imaging showed that the mRNA was normally expressed in the rats’ hearts by Day 3 (Fig. 2B). The LVEF, left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), heart weight, and heart-to-body weight ratio of the rat model were compared between the treatment and control groups at 1,2-, and 3-weeks post-injection (Fig. 2A).

To further elucidate the efficacy and safety of Km10566, its effects were compared with AZD8601. Km10566 significantly improved both LVEF and LVFS at 14 days p < 0.0001), with beneficial effects persisting up to 3 weeks. No significant differences were observed in heart weight (Fig. 2E) or heart-to-body weight (Fig. 2F) ratio in either group. Notably, Km10566 showed the most pronunced improvement in cardiac function, outperforming AZD8601 significantly (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2C,D).

These results suggest that the design and assembly of synthetic mRNA—without altering the protein sequence—can achieve comparable therapeutic effects to those of mRNA drugs. Although no significant changes in heart weight or ratio were observed, the marked improvements in LVEF and LVFS indicate a beneficial effect on cardiac function following myocardial infartion (MI). This efficacy may be mediated through non-structural mechanisms, such as modulating cellular metabolism or reducing inflammation, which are not reflected by macroscopic indicators.

Based on prior studies on circular mRNA, we circularized the optimized sequence to generate C6V, which contains the CDS105 element, using type I intron splicing14,21. We plan to compare its effect with those of the linear mRNA. As shown in Fig. 2, C6V did not differ significantly from AZD8601 in terms of heart weight and heart weight-to-body weight ratio. While C6V exhibited a significant impact on key cardiac function indicators—LVEF and LVFS (p < 0.05)—it was less effective than Km10566.

Km10566 modulates expression of cardiac repair-associated genes

Myocardial infarction triggers a complex transcriptional response involving angiogenic and remodeling genes, notably Wilms’ tumor 1 (Wt1), troponin T type 2 (Tnnt2), and kinase insert domain receptor (KDR)20. We therefore examined how different mRNA constructs influenced these gene expression patterns in ischemic myocardium over the three-week treatment period.Notably, our sampling time points (days 7, 14, and 21) captured the resolution phase of the repair response; the expected early upregulation of Wt1 and KDR within the first 3–7 days post-MI19 has been well documented in previous reports and was not the focus of this study.

The kinetic profiles revealed striking differences between treatments. In animals receiving Km10566, Wt1 expression showed progressive and sustained downregulation from day 7 through day 21 (Fig. 3A). While Wt1 is classically viewed as a marker of epicardial activation during the acute injury response19,20, its persistent elevation beyond the repair phase can paradoxically signal ongoing tissue distress rather than productive repair. The gradual normalization observed with Km10566 thus appears to reflect successful resolution of ischemia and orderly closure of the wound healing program. By contrast, AZD8601 elicited only a transient dip at day 14, suggesting its influence on Wt1-driven processes was short-lived. C6V-treated animals tracked with vehicle controls throughout, indicating minimal engagement of this pathway.

KDR expression told a similar story (Fig. 3C). Km10566 drove a sharp and sustained decline (day 7, p < 0.0001; day 14, p < 0.01), consistent with efficient revascularization removing the hypoxic drive for continued VEGF receptor expression. This interpretation aligns with the well-characterized negative feedback that tunes KDR signaling to prevent excessive angiogenesis4. AZD8601 again produced only a transient reduction that faded by day 21, pointing to incomplete perfusion recovery. For Tnnt2, expression remained largely stable across groups until day 21, when both active treatments showed modest but significant changes (p < 0.05)—possibly reflecting late-stage maturation of the cardiomyocyte contractile apparatus following improved vascular support19.

C6V occupied an intermediate position: KDR downregulation persisted until day 14 before returning to baseline, while Wt1 and Tnnt2 never deviated from control levels. Across all three genes, Km10566 consistently yielded the most robust and durable effects, underscoring how its optimized design not only boosts VEGF-A output but also promotes timely resolution of injury-induced transcriptional programs—a signature of genuine functional recovery19.

These results reinforce the rapid and enduring therapeutic effects of Km10566 in the treatment of myocardial infarction.

Discussion

In this study, we developed an optimized VEGF-A mRNA construct, designated Km10566, which demonstrates enhanced translation efficiency and stability while minimizing unwanted immunogenicity. Through systematic engineering of the 5′- and 3′-UTRs, codon optimization of the coding sequence (CDS), and incorporation of a stabilizing poly(A) tail, we obtained a candidate capable of sustaining high levels of VEGF-A expression in vitro. Subsequent modification with N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ) significantly reduced innate immune activation, consistent with prior reports highlighting nucleoside modifications as a strategy to evade Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated recognition9,10.

The functional superiority of Km10566 was validated in a rat model of myocardial infarction, where it significantly improved left ventricular ejection fraction and reduced collagen deposition compared to both AZD8601 and the reference C6V construct. These findings suggest that our integrated optimization approach not only enhances protein expression but also translates into meaningful physiological improvements in a therapeutically relevant setting. Notably, the m1Ψ-modified Km10566 did not elicit significant inflammatory responses in treated animals, supporting its favorable safety profile.

Our results align with recent advances in nucleoside-modified mRNA therapeutics. Several studies have demonstrated that combinatorial optimization of mRNA sequences—including UTR selection, codon usage, and nucleoside modifications—can substantially enhance therapeutic efficacy in cardiovascular applications2,19. The selection of specific UTR elements derived from highly expressed endogenous genes has been shown to prolong mRNA half-life and increase protein yield12,13, consistent with our observation of sustained VEGF-A expression from Km10566. Furthermore, modified VEGF-A mRNA has been successfully employed to promote angiogenesis, reduce inflammation, and improve cardiac function in both small and large animal models of myocardial ischemia17,19.

The m1Ψ modification incorporated in Km10566 warrants particular discussion. Beyond its well-established role in reducing immunogenicity by abrogating recognition by pattern recognition receptors9,10, recent studies have revealed that m1Ψ also modulates translation dynamics through multiple mechanisms, including alleviation of immune-mediated translational restriction and direct effects on the translational machinery. These combined effects explain the superior translational efficiency we observed with m1Ψ-modified Km10566 compared to constructs incorporating other modified nucleosides.

Compared to existing VEGF-A therapies, Km10566 offers several advantages. AZD8601, a modified VEGF-A mRNA developed through collaboration between Moderna and AstraZeneca, has shown promise in early clinical trials for patients with heart failure undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting, demonstrating safety and tolerability with exploratory signals of improved ventricular function17. However, our direct comparison indicates that Km10566 achieves superior translational efficiency, likely due to our more comprehensive optimization strategy encompassing UTR selection, codon usage, and poly(A) tail design. Similarly, viral vector-based VEGF delivery systems, though effective in preclinical models, raise concerns regarding immunogenicity, insertional mutagenesis, and uncontrolled long-term expression that may promote inappropriate angiogenesis6,7. In contrast, Km10566 leverages non-viral, chemically defined mRNA technology, allowing for controlled, transient expression without genomic integration—a crucial consideration for regenerative applications18.

The pharmacodynamic analysis revealed that Km10566 produced sustained and significant downregulation of Wt1 and KDR genes from day 7 through day 21, whereas AZD8601 induced only transient effects. Wt1 plays a critical role in epicardial progenitor cell activation and endothelial differentiation following cardiac injury20,19, and is also re-expressed in cardiac endothelial cells after injury, where it may contribute directly to vascular regeneration. KDR (VEGFR-2) mediates the primary angiogenic signals of VEGF-A4. The prolonged modulation of these genes by Km10566 likely reflects enhanced mRNA stability and sustained protein production, leading to more durable effects on coronary revascularization and cardiac repair. These findings align with previous reports demonstrating that modified VEGF-A mRNA can promote vascular regeneration by driving the differentiation of epicardial progenitor cells into endothelial cells20,19.

The reduced collagen volume fraction observed with Km10566 treatment is particularly noteworthy, as myocardial fibrosis is a key determinant of adverse remodeling and functional decline following infarction. Recent studies have demonstrated that modified mRNA therapy can reduce inflammatory cell infiltration and inhibit fibrosis-associated signaling pathways19. Our results extend these findings by showing that optimized VEGF-A mRNA alone can achieve substantial antifibrotic effects, likely through improved vascularization and reduced ischemic injury.

Several limitations should be noted. First, while the m1Ψ modification clearly enhanced translational efficiency in our constructs, recent reports suggest that its effects may be context-dependent, with the magnitude of enhancement influenced by codon composition and the presence of uridines at wobble positions10,15. Further optimization of codon usage specifically tailored to m1Ψ-containing mRNAs could potentially yield even greater improvements. Second, although rat models provide valuable insights into safety and efficacy, species differences in mRNA sensing and VEGF signaling necessitate caution when extrapolating results to humans. Third, the translatability of our findings to large animal models and ultimately to clinical settings requires further validation, particularly regarding optimal dosing regimens and long-term safety17. Fourth, while we observed no significant changes in heart weight or heart-to-body weight ratio, the marked improvements in LVEF and LVFS indicate that therapeutic efficacy may be mediated through non-structural mechanisms, such as modulating cellular metabolism or reducing inflammation, which merit further investigation. Fifth, we did not directly measure infarct size. This decision was based on the rationale that in the permanent ligation model, area-based infarct size measurement in the chronic setting can be confounded by wall thinning, which compresses the measured values and may not sensitively reflect the functional improvements driven by VEGF-A-mediated neovascularization. Additionally, our sampling focused on the border zone rather than the infarct core, as this region is the primary site of therapeutic response; consequently, the histological images reflect border zone morphology rather than transmural scarring.

Future work will focus on advancing Km10566 into GMP-compliant manufacturing and IND-enabling studies. Concurrently, we are exploring combination strategies with biomaterial-based delivery systems, such as biodegradable hydrogels or lipid nanoparticles, to enhance tissue retention and achieve controlled release14,15. Recent advances in machine learning-driven design of UTR sequences for enhanced mRNA stability15,16 suggest that further improvements may be achievable through computational optimization. Additionally, the potential application of Km10566 in other VEGF-responsive conditions, such as chronic limb ischemia and diabetic wounds, represents an exciting avenue for ongoing research. Combination approaches with other angiogenic factors or cell therapies may also yield synergistic benefits for cardiac repair19.

In conclusion, we have developed a novel VEGF-A mRNA construct with optimized efficacy and safety profiles through systematic combinatorial optimization. Km10566 demonstrates superior translational efficiency, sustained modulation of cardiac repair genes, and significant functional improvement in a rat model of myocardial infarction. These findings establish Km10566 as a promising candidate for further development as a regenerative therapeutic for ischemic heart disease.

Methods

mRNA construct design and synthesis

To identify a VEGF-A mRNA candidate with enhanced translational efficiency and stability, the VEGF-A mRNA construct (Km10566) was designed based on the human VEGF-A165 amino acid sequence (UniProt P15692-4). The complete mRNA architecture included a 5′-cap structure (7-methyl GTP), a 5′ untranslated region (5′-UTR), the coding region (CDS), a FLAG-tag peptide sequence, a 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR), and a poly(A) tail. The DNA template encompassing these elements was synthesized de novo (GenScript Biotech) with a T7 promoter sequence positioned upstream of the 5′ UTR to enable in vitro transcription1,2.

DNA template preparation

To generate linearized DNA templates for in vitro transcription, the plasmid DNA containing the T7 promoter-VEGF-A construct was linearized by PCR amplification prior to in vitro transcription. PCR reactions (50 µL total volume) were assembled containing: 6.6 µL of 2 × Reaction Buffer, 1 µL of dNTP Mix (10 mM), 2 µL of upstream primer (10 µM), 2 µL of downstream primer (10 µM), 1 µL of DNA Polymerase, 3 µL of plasmid DNA template, and 16 µL of nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows:

PCR products were purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter). Beads were added to the reaction mixture at a 1:1 volume ratio, incubated for 10 min at room temperature, and placed on a magnetic stand. After discarding the supernatant, beads were washed twice with 80% ethanol, air-dried for 5 min, and eluted in nuclease-free water. Purified DNA concentration was determined by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop One, Thermo Fisher), and integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

In vitro transcription and mRNA purification

To produce modified mRNA for in vitro and in vivo studies, modified mRNA was synthesized by in vitro transcription (IVT) using a co-transcriptional capping approach. The 20 µL IVT reaction contained: 1 µL 10× Reaction Buffer, 1 µL each of ATP, CTP, GTP (25 mM each), 1 µL N1-methylpseudouridine-5′-triphosphate (25 mM, Trilink Biotechnologies) as a complete substitute for UTP9,10, 0.8 µL Cap1 AG analog (12 mM, Trilink Biotechnologies), 1 µg linear DNA template, 2 µL T7 RNA Polymerase (50 U/µL), and nuclease-free water to 20 µL. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Template DNA was then digested by adding 1 µL DNase I (2 U/µL) and incubating at 37 °C for 15 min.

mRNA was purified by silica membrane-based purification. The reaction was diluted with 30 µL nuclease-free water, followed by addition of 100 µL RNA Binding Buffer and 150 µL anhydrous ethanol. The mixture was applied to an RNA purification column and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 1 min. The column was washed twice with 500 µL Wash Buffer, and RNA was eluted in 50 µL nuclease-free water after 5 min incubation. mRNA concentration was quantified spectrophotometrically, and integrity was assessed by denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis11.

Cell culture and transfection

To screen mRNA constructs for translational efficiency prior to in vivo testing, HEK293T cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Freshly resuscitated HEK293T cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator and passaged to the second generation for use. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 7 × 104 cells per well, with 500 µL of complete medium (10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin) added to each well and cultured continuously for 24 h.

For transfection, a Lipo-mRNA mixture was prepared as follows: first, 5 µL of RNAiMAX was mixed with 50 µL of Opti-MEM and allowed to stand for 5 min. Second, 1 µg of mRNA (0.5 µg/µL) was mixed with 50 µL of Opti-MEM. Finally, the RNAiMAX solution was added to the mRNA mixture, thoroughly mixed by pipetting, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min19,11.

Before transfection, the medium in each well was replaced with 400 µL of fresh complete medium. Then, 105 µL of the prepared Lipo-mRNA mixture was added to each well for transfection. After 48 h, cytosol and medium supernatant samples were collected separately.

For medium supernatant, samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred and stored appropriately. For cytosol collection, cells were rinsed once with cold PBS, 25 µL of cell lysate (containing protease inhibitors) was added, and lysis was performed on ice for 5 min. The lysate was collected by pipetting and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was stored at − 80 °C.

Western blot analysis

To quantitatively compare VEGF-A protein expression from different mRNA constructs, protein samples were thawed on ice and mixed with 5 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10% SDS, 30% glycerol, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.02% bromophenol blue). Samples were heated at 99 °C for 5 min and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min. Equal volumes (10 µL) were loaded onto 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and electrophoresed at 120 V for 90 min. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Thermo Fisher) using the iBlot 2 semi-dry transfer system (Program P0, 7 min).

Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk in PBST (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti-FLAG (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #14793) and mouse anti-GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig). Membranes were washed five times with PBST (10 min each) and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG, 1:5000, Jackson ImmunoResearch; goat anti-mouse IgG, 1:5000, Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Following five additional PBST washes, chemiluminescent signals were detected using ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher) on a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Myocardial infarction model

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of Km10566 in a clinically relevant setting, all animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai RAT&MOUSE Biotech, Ltd. (approval number IACUC-2024-012) and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, National Academies Press). Male SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g) were housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the institutional animal ethics committee. Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (5% induction, 3% maintenance), intubated, and mechanically ventilated. Following left thoracotomy, the left anterior descending artery (LAD) was permanently ligated with a 5 − 0 suture. Sham-operated animals underwent the same procedure without LAD ligation. Animals were randomly assigned to sham, vehicle, or treatment groups (n = 5 per group). At 1, 2, and 3 weeks post-surgery, cardiac function was assessed, and heart tissues were collected for histological and gene expression analyses.

Postoperative analgesia was not routinely administered to avoid potential interference with cardiovascular function and inflammatory responses4,6; however, animals were closely monitored for signs of pain or distress (hunched posture, piloerection, reduced activity, vocalization, weight loss) every 6 h for the first 48 h and daily thereafter. No animals in any group required analgesic intervention. Strict aseptic technique was maintained throughout all surgical procedures to minimize infection risk, and no signs of wound infection were observed in any animal.

Intracardiac mRNA administration

To achieve targeted myocardial delivery and confirm successful transfection, for mRNA delivery studies, additional rats (n = 3) received three intracardiac injections of 150 µg luciferase-mRNA (20 µL per injection). In vivo bioluminescence imaging was performed on day 3 post-injection. Successful delivery was defined as detectable expression in at least two of three animals19. For drug treatment, animals received a single intracardiac injection of 150 µg of Km10566 dissolved in citrate buffer (20 µL total volume) immediately following LAD ligation. Vehicle controls received an equivalent volume of citrate buffer only17.

Echocardiographic assessment of cardiac function

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed at baseline (pre-surgery) and at 1, 2, and 3 weeks post-MI under light isoflurane anesthesia (1.5%) using a Vevo 3100 imaging system (Fujifilm VisualSonics) equipped with a 21 MHz linear array transducer. Two-dimensional parasternal long-axis and short-axis views were acquired, and M-mode recordings at the mid-papillary level were used to measure left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDd) and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESd). Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated using the Teichholz formula. All measurements were averaged from three consecutive cardiac cycles by an experienced operator blinded to treatment allocation19.

Tissue collection and processing

At 1, 2, and 3 weeks post-surgery, rats were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and euthanized by exsanguination followed by cervical dislocation. Hearts were rapidly excised, rinsed in ice-cold PBS, and the atria and right ventricle were removed. The left ventricle was dissected into ischemic zone (identifiable by pale appearance) and remote zone (posterior wall). Within the ischemic zone, the necrotic core (central pale region) and peri-infarct/border zone (transitional area between the pale ischemic core and visibly normal myocardium) were distinguished based on visual identification guided by LAD ligation landmarks14,21. The border zone represents the interface where surviving cardiomyocytes interdigitate with fibrotic tissue and is the primary site of active neovascularization and therapeutic response4,19; therefore, this region was consistently sampled for downstream analyses. Tissue samples from the border zone were divided: portions were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C and processed for paraffin embedding for histological analysis, and the remaining tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for RNA extraction.

Gene expression analysis

To assess the molecular effects of treatment on cardiac repair pathways, Myocardial tissue samples were collected from different experimental groups for RNA extraction and subsequent quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis. Gene expression was detected using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Novozymes). The RT-qPCR reaction mixture was prepared as follows: 6.6 µL of ddH2O, 10 µL of 2 × SYBR qPCR Master Mix, 0.2 µL of upstream primer (10 µM), 0.2 µL of downstream primer (10 µM), and 3 µL of cDNA template.

The reaction was performed under the following cycling conditions: (1) pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; (2) 40 amplification cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. At the end of the reaction, the relative expression level of the target gene was calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. Three technical replicates were performed for both the target and reference genes, and the Ct values among replicates were required to vary by no more than 0.5 to ensure reproducibility20.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Heart tissue samples were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4–8 μm. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene (twice for 10 min) and rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, 70%; 5 min each) followed by PBS washes (3 × 5 min). Hematoxylin staining was performed for 10 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water and differentiation in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol. Sections were blued with weak alkaline solution, washed, and counterstained with eosin for 3 min. After dehydration through graded ethanol (80% for 5 s, 95% for 2 min, 100% for 2 min) and clearing in xylene (2 × 4 min), sections were mounted with neutral balsam and imaged under a light microscope.

Masson’s trichrome staining

Deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were stained with preheated acidic fuchsin solution (adjusted timing as needed) to achieve red coloration. After rinsing with acidic ethanol, sections were incubated in hematoxylin solution to yield yellow/green tissue tones. Sections were then dehydrated through graded ethanol (70%, 95%, 100%), cleared in xylene or Inouye’s solvent, and mounted with transparent medium. Stained sections were examined microscopically to assess collagen deposition and tissue morphology.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software). Normality was assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were made using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Multiple group comparisons were performed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Repeated measures ANOVA with Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used for longitudinal echocardiographic data. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sample sizes were determined based on power analysis (80% power, α = 0.05) using effect sizes estimated from preliminary studies and previous publications19,16.
