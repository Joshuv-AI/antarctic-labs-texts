Positive Indian Ocean Dipole intensifies marine heatwaves in the tropical southeast Atlantic coastal region
Authors: Heng Liu, Lei Zhang, Michael J. McPhaden, Weiqing Han, Haiyu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) along the tropical southeast Atlantic (TSEA) coast have devastating impacts on marine ecosystems and productivity. Through observational analysis and numerical model experiments, we demonstrate that positive Indian Ocean Dipole (pIOD) can intensify TSEA coastal MHWs during austral summer through two distinct processes. First, pIOD induces westerly wind anomalies over the equatorial Atlantic, causing thermocline deepening along the TSEA coast through oceanic Kelvin waves, favoring development of MHWs. Second, pIOD enhances rainfall over central Africa, increasing Congo River discharge and resulting in a shallower mixed layer in the TSEA coastal region, also promoting MHW formation. We further develop an empirical model using pIOD as the predictor for TSEA MHWs, demonstrating high predictive skill with a 3-month lead time. In a warming climate, the remote influence of pIOD on TSEA MHWs may be further exacerbated, underscoring critical need to incorporate this factor into marine hazard forecasting for the region.

INTRODUCTION

The tropical southeast Atlantic (TSEA) coastal region (0° to 15°S, 2°-wide coastal band) is characterized by an upper ocean upwelling system that supports exceptionally high biological productivity and sustains rich fishery resources (1–6). High chlorophyll concentrations are observed along the TSEA coast (Fig. 1A). These vital marine ecosystems support the livelihoods of millions of coastal inhabitants and constitute a crucial economic pillar for neighboring nations (3, 7–9). Consequently, variations in coastal upwelling and associated oceanic conditions exert a direct influence on the region’s productivity and its coastal communities (6).

Marine heatwaves (MHWs) are prolonged high-temperature extremes occurring in the ocean, profoundly affecting the marine ecosystem (10–15). In the TSEA region, these thermal anomalies pose serious threats through suppressing coastal productivity, depleting critical fish stocks, and jeopardizing both natural marine ecosystems and aquaculture industries (16–18). In addition, through air-sea interactions, warm sea surface temperature (SST) associated with MHWs can enhance local rainfall in the coastal areas while causing unusually dry conditions over adjacent land regions, thereby exacerbating flooding risks in coastal Africa and drought conditions in inland areas (19–22). Therefore, understanding the mechanisms driving TSEA coastal MHWs is also crucial for predicting climate extreme events and mitigating their impacts (23–25).

MHWs in the TSEA coastal region exhibit a distinct bimodal frequency structure, with peak occurrences during austral summer and winter seasons (Fig. 1B). Interannual variability of MHWs is also particularly pronounced in these two seasons. Notably, during austral summer, baseline SSTs are higher (Fig. 1B) (26), which could exacerbate the ecological and climatic impacts of MHWs. Given the potentially greater ecological and socioeconomic consequences, we primarily focus on MHWs during austral summer in this study, which represents a critical period for understanding and predicting MHW-related impacts in the TSEA region. Chlorophyll concentrations and primary production in the TSEA coastal region also display a bimodal seasonal cycle, with peaks in both summer and winter (5). While mean chlorophyll concentrations are higher in winter, their variability is greater in summer (fig. S1), which may be partially associated with MHW activity.

Recent studies have revealed that positive Indian Ocean Dipole (pIOD) events, the dominate mode of interannual climate variability in tropical Indian Ocean that can operate independently of El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), can influence the tropical Atlantic Ocean. For instance, Zhang and Han (27) demonstrated that pIOD events that peak during austral spring can influence tropical Atlantic air-sea coupled system during austral summer through inducing equatorial westerly wind anomalies. In addition, the pIOD events can enhance rainfall over the Congo Basin, increasing Congo River discharge (28, 29). This freshwater input subsequently reduces sea surface salinity (SSS) and strengthens vertical stratification along the TSEA coast during austral summer (29).

Here, through observational analysis and numerical experiments, we demonstrate that the pIOD can significantly increase the likelihood of MHW occurrences in the TSEA coastal region during austral summer, posing serious threats to the local marine ecosystem. Building on this finding, we develop a prediction model that uses the pIOD as a reliable single predictor for TSEA MHWs, offering promising potential for early warning applications. Given the profound ecological and climatic impacts of MHWs, our results provide useful insights for prediction efforts and inform the development of proactive mitigation strategies to reduce their detrimental effects.

RESULTS

pIOD enhances MHWs along TSEA coast

During austral summer, MHWs along the TSEA coast exhibit pronounced interannual variability (Fig. 1C). Notably, MHW activity—quantified as domain-averaged MHW days across the TSEA coastal region—shows a significant increase following pIOD events (Fig. 1C). This relationship is further supported by composite analysis of SST anomalies (SSTAs) during September to November (SON), the season before MHW occurrences, which demonstrates a pIOD pattern in the tropical Indian Ocean (Fig. 1D). In addition, regression of seasonal MHW days on the dipole mode index (DMI) shows significantly enhanced MHW activity in the TSEA, particularly in the coastal region (Fig. 1E). A comparison between non-pIOD and pIOD years also reveals a notable contrast, with pIOD years exhibiting an average of 6.9 MHW days per month—approximately 6.6 times of those during non-pIOD years (Fig. 1F). These findings demonstrate the critical role of pIOD in strengthening MHWs along the TSEA coastal region. Conversely, negative IOD events tend to suppress MHW activity in the TSEA region (fig. S2).

To explore the underlying physical mechanisms for the remote influence of pIOD on TSEA MHWs, we conducted regression analyses of SSTA and 850-hPa wind anomalies on the SON-mean DMI. Results show that during the pIOD peak in November, significant cyclonic wind anomalies prevail over the southeast Atlantic (Fig. 2A). In the equatorial region, the associated westerly wind anomalies induce anomalous Ekman convergence, leading to deepened thermocline and higher sea level (Fig. 2D). Concurrently, in the off-equatorial region, the emergence of warmer SSTs appears to be driven by cyclonic wind anomalies that oppose the prevailing easterlies (fig. S3).

In the following months, pronounced SST warming appear in the TSEA, particularly in the coastal region (Fig. 2, B and C). This persistent monthly SST warming creates favorable conditions for the formation and intensification of MHWs. Notably, the coastal warming coincides with the development of positive sea level anomalies, despite the weak alongshore wind anomalies in the region (Fig. 2, E and F). These coastal sea level anomalies originate from the equatorial region where high sea level signals enhance toward the east due to eastward propagating equatorial Kelvin waves (Fig. 2, D and E), which subsequently propagate poleward along the TSEA coast as coastal Kelvin waves (Fig. 2, E and F, and fig. S4). A linear ocean model driven by westerly wind anomalies over the eastern equatorial Atlantic (blue box in Fig. 2A) reproduces the prominent high sea level anomalies along the TSEA coast associated with the poleward-propagating coastal Kelvin waves (see Materials and Methods and fig. S5). The oceanic Rossby waves radiated westward from the coastal region also lead to positive sea level anomalies in the South Atlantic in the linear ocean model.

The higher sea level is associated with deepened thermocline, which suppresses coastal upwelling and increases SST, strengthening MHWs. These oceanic responses in the TSEA coastal region exhibit strong temporal coherence during austral summer and autumn seasons following most pIOD events (fig. S6). Furthermore, a quantitative diagnosis of mixed layer heat budget reveals that vertical processes associated with the thermocline deepening account for more than 50% of the total positive contributions to the SST warming (Fig. 2, G and I). However, the vertical term also includes contributions from other processes, such as lateral mixing, nonlinear effects, and unresolved subgrid-scale dynamics. Nevertheless, we obtained similar results using another oceanic dataset (fig. S7). These findings highlight the crucial role of ocean dynamics–driven by the equatorial westerlies induced by pIOD forcing—in enhancing SST warming and MHWs along the TSEA coast.

The role of mixed layer shoaling

In contrast to the substantial warming induced by ocean dynamical processes, the net surface heat flux term (Qnetρcph)′ tends to cause cooling in the TSEA coastal region during pIOD years (Fig. 2, G and H), where h represents the mixed layer depth (MLD) (see Eq. 3 in Materials and Methods). However, further decomposition of this term into two components—the Qnet′ term, primarily driven by net surface heat flux anomalies, and the h′ term, which is associated with changes in MLD (see Eq. 10 in Materials and Methods)—reveals that while the Qnet′ term contributes negatively to the warming tendency, h′ term promotes strong warming, accounting for 27% of the total positive contributions to the SST warming and becoming the second largest contribution to the observed warming (Fig. 2, H and I).

The warming effect associated with MLD changes results from the mixed layer shoaling due to enhanced ocean stratification (Fig. 3A), facilitating heat trapping within the shallower surface layer and suppressing vertical mixing (30–33). Note that the h′ term is linked to the mean-state MLD, with regions characterized by a shallower mean MLD—such as the TSEA coastal region—exhibiting higher sensitivity to MLD anomalies (see Eq. 10 in Materials and Methods and fig. S8). Furthermore, we find that changes in MLD in the TSEA coastal region are primarily driven by SSS anomalies, whereas the SST warming plays a negligible role (Fig. 3, A to C, and fig. S8). In particular, ~75% of MHW events coincide with the SSS-induced negative MLD anomalies, while no significant MLD variations are observed during non-MHW periods (Fig. 3D). This contrast suggests that SSS variability plays an important role in modulating MHW intensity along the TSEA coast through its influence on MLD.

The shoaling of the mixed layer driven by SSS anomalies is closely linked to hydrological changes, particularly variations in rainfall patterns and river discharge (figs. S9 and S10) (34–36). In line with a recent finding (35), pIOD events lead to a prominent increase in rainfall over central Africa and the TSEA region (fig. S9), facilitated by equatorial westerly wind anomalies that enhance moisture supply (fig. S10). This intensifies freshwater input into the Congo River, resulting in enhanced river discharge and a subsequent decrease in SSS. Notably, both Congo River discharge and SSS freshening, which induce pronounced MLD changes following pIOD events, coincide with the intensification of MHWs along the TSEA coastal region (Fig. 3E). In addition, positive rainfall anomalies along the TSEA coast may also directly contribute to the SSS reduction.

A barrier layer is present in the TSEA coastal region, which typically suppresses vertical mixing and thus favors surface warming. Following pIOD events, the mixed layer shoals while the isothermal layer deepens, leading to an increase in barrier layer thickness (BLT) (from 11.1 to 12.1 m). Although this change is statistically insignificant, the presence of a potentially deepened barrier layer may also contribute to the observed sea surface warming and the intensified MHW activity to some extent.

These findings reveal a dual mechanism by which the pIOD modulates MHWs along the TSEA coast: (i) through oceanic processes that deepen the thermocline, thereby suppressing the coastal upwelling effect, and (ii) through remote terrestrial effect that influence rainfall patterns, subsequently enhancing surface stratification in the coastal ocean. Together, these two pathways allow the pIOD to exert a dominant influence on the MHW variability in the TSEA coastal region. These physical processes are summarized in Fig. 4.

Schematic diagram summarizing the remote influence of the pIOD on MHWs along the TSEA coast.

Oceanic pathway: Westerly wind anomalies over the equatorial Atlantic, remotely induced by the pIOD, generate oceanic waves that deepen the thermocline along the TSEA coastal region, favoring sea surface warming and enhancing MHW activity. Terrestrial pathway: Increased precipitation over central Africa, driven by the pIOD, leads to enhanced Congo River discharge. This freshwater input contributes to shoaling of the mixed layer in the TSEA coastal region, which further supports surface warming.

Establishing a prediction model

The findings above demonstrate the crucial role that the pIOD plays in influencing MHWs along the TSEA coastal region. To provide further evidence for this remote impact, we conducted Indian Ocean pacemaker experiments using a state-of-the-art coupled climate model (see Materials and Methods). In these experiments, observed SSTAs were prescribed exclusively within the Indian Ocean, while atmosphere and ocean were fully coupled elsewhere. Consequently, the ensemble-mean results isolated the impact of the Indian Ocean on regional and global climate system (27, 37).

Our pacemaker experiment results demonstrate that pIOD events significantly enhance MHWs in the TSEA, consistent with observations (Fig. 5A and fig. S11B). Furthermore, a comparison between positive and negative IOD events reveals a notable asymmetry in MHW occurrence along the TSEA coast: While negative IOD events are associated with muted MHW activity, pIOD events prominently promote MHWs in the region (Fig. 5B). These model simulations provide robust numerical evidence supporting the crucial role of pIOD in amplifying MHWs in TSEA, corroborating our observational findings.

Considering the strong remote influence of pIOD on TSEA MHWs in the following season, it is appealing to develop a predictive framework based on such a relationship. However, the climate model underestimates MHWs in the coastal region (Fig. 5A), potentially due to model biases in representing coastal dynamics and/or simulating river discharge. These model biases likely constrain its predictive capability for pIOD-driven MHW variability.

Hence, we instead developed an empirical prediction model for TSEA coastal MHWs during austral summer, using DMI during austral spring as the predictor (see Eq. 1 in Materials and Methods). The simple model demonstrates notable prediction skill, successfully capturing MHW variability from 1982 to 2022 (r = 0.63, P < 0.01) (Fig. 5C). Previous studies have also shown that SSTAs in the western tropical Indian Ocean exert notable influences on tropical Atlantic climate variability (38). However, using SSTAs of the western pole of the pIOD as the predictor results in a lower prediction skill (r = 0.52). This result indicates that rainfall anomalies over the western tropical Indian Ocean—the key driver of the remote influence on the TSEA—are more closely linked to changes in the zonal SST gradient across the tropical Indian Ocean during austral spring, which modulates zonal wind anomalies.

As the dominant mode of interannual climate variability, ENSO may also influence MHWs in the TSEA coastal region. Observational analysis reveals prominent influence of ENSO on TSEA MHWs (fig. S12A). However, this remote effect is largely attributable to the concurrent pIOD events; when the influence of pIOD is removed, the ENSO-related signal over the TSEA region becomes negligible (fig. S12, B and C). This suggests that the pIOD’s influence on TSEA coastal MHWs operates largely independently of the Pacific climate variability. Consistent with this finding, incorporating ENSO as an additional predictor yields only marginal improvement in prediction skill (r = 0.66) (fig. S11A), which is also consistent with the lack of correlation between ENSO and Congo basin rainfall (29). These results highlight the robustness of the pIOD-MHW relationship and its potential utility for operational forecasting.

DISCUSSION

MHWs in the TSEA coastal region during austral summer have profound impacts on the local marine ecosystem. A thorough understanding of mechanisms driving MHWs in this region is essential for improving forecasts and mitigating associated marine hazards. In this study, we show that pIOD events intensify MHWs along the TSEA coast with a lead time of one season. This finding highlights a key source of predictability for TSEA MHWs. Notably, our empirical model using the pIOD as the sole predictor exhibits strong predictive skill.

Although the pIOD plays a dominant role in intensifying TSEA MHWs, other processes may also play a role, potentially contributing to the observed diversity in MHW responses even during pIOD years (fig. S6). For instance, despite the occurrence of an extreme pIOD event in 2019 (39), MHWs in the TSEA region were not particularly strong (fig. S6). Further analysis reveals that a pronounced cooling effect associated with anomalous net surface heat flux offset the warming induced by thermocline deepening (fig. S13), suppressing MHW activity in the region. This highlights that processes either independent of the pIOD or modulating its remote influence may play an important role in certain years.

While our primary focus is on the impact of pIOD on the TSEA region during the austral summer, this remote influence can also contribute to Benguela Niño in the following austral fall season. For example, the strongest Benguela Niño in the past 40 years occurred between February and April 1995, with average SSTA reaching 2°C; its amplitude was boosted by the strong pIOD event in late 1994 (36). However, factors intrinsic to the Atlantic Ocean are critical for the development of Benguela Niño, so that the influence of the IOD is generally less pronounced than it is for TSEA MHWs in the austral summer.

Our climate model experiments also highlight the influence of the pIOD in amplifying MHWs in the TSEA. However, notable discrepancies between the model and observations emerge, particularly in the coastal region, where the model tends to underestimate pIOD-induced MHWs. Given the critical role of pIOD in modulating coastal MHWs in this region, a thorough investigation into the underlying causes of this model bias is essential, which could potentially contribute to enhancing our forecasting capabilities of marine hazards.

In this study, we used an 11-year sliding baseline to identify MHWs (see Materials and Methods), effectively excluding the influence of the long-term warming trend. However, the TSEA coastal region has been a “hot spot” for pronounced warming over the past few decades (Fig. 6, A and B) (40), which may exacerbate the disastrous ecological consequences of MHWs in the area. MHWs defined with a fixed baseline show a clear strengthening trend in the region (Fig. 6C) (41). Moreover, the significant warming trend along the TSEA coast is projected to persist in the future based on simulations from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project phase 6 (CMIP6) models (Fig. 6, D and E). These changes may further exaggerate the remote impact of the pIOD on MHWs in the region, serving as an alarming signal for the region (42–44).

In addition to the pronounced long-term SST warming trend, two-thirds of CMIP6 models analyzed in this study project an increase in the frequency of pIOD events under global warming (Fig. 6F), potentially heightening risks to the TSEA marine ecosystem through promoting more frequent MHWs in the future. However, the multimodel mean results show that this increase is not statistically significant, indicating uncertainties in future changes in the pIOD frequency.

Furthermore, atmospheric stability may increase under global warming (45), which could weaken the teleconnection between the Indian and Atlantic Oceans and thereby reduce the influence of pIOD events on the TSEA region. On the other hand, the higher background SSTs in the tropical Indian Ocean could enhance the rainfall response to the pIOD and thereby excite more prominent teleconnections. A dedicated study specifically assessing projected changes in pIOD-induced MHWs along the TSEA coast is warranted. Nevertheless, the emergence of a persistent MHW hot spot along the TSEA coast highlights the urgent need for improved forecasting and early warning systems for marine hazards in this vulnerable region.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Observations and climate model

The daily SST data from the Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature version 2.1 (OISST v2.1) (46) are used to identify MHWs. Monthly SST data from the Hadley Centre Sea Ice and Sea Surface Temperature (HadISST) (47), daily sea level data from Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service (CMEMS) (48), monthly 850-hPa wind and surface heat flux from European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) Reanalysis 5 (ERA5) (49) are used to examine the oceanic and atmospheric changes associated with the IOD events and MHWs. The data analysis period is from 1982 to 2023, and all anomaly fields in observations have been linearly detrended. The observed monthly chlorophyll data from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer Aqua (MODIS-Aqua) (50) are used to examine the climatological chlorophyll from 2003 to 2023. Daily in situ discharge data at Brazzaville/Kinshasa station are used to quantify the Congo River discharge from 1982 to 2023.

The monthly oceanic data, including sea level, horizontal velocity, ocean temperature, salinity, and MLD, are obtained from ECMWF Ocean Reanalysis System 5 (ORAS5) (51) to analyze the oceanic variability and conduct the ocean mixed layer heat budget. The ORAS5 dataset has a horizontal resolution of 0.25° and covers the period from 1982 to 2023. The Simple Ocean Data Assimilation 3 (SODA3) (52), ensemble member SODA3.12.2, is also used to compare the result of mixed layer heat budget, including monthly temperature, surface heat flux, MLD, and seawater velocity from 1982 to 2023.

To characterize the variability of climate modes, we calculated the climate indices based on monthly SSTAs, which have been linearly detrended. The DMI, representing the IOD, is calculated as the difference of SSTAs between the western (50°E to 70°E, 10°S to 10°N) and eastern (90°E to 110°E, 10°S to 0°) tropical Indian Ocean. The pIOD (1982, 1987, 1994, 1997, 2006, 2015, 2018, and 2019) events and negative IOD (1984, 1992, 1996, 1998, 2005, 2010, 2016, and 2022) are defined as years when the SON-mean DMI exceeds (falls below negative) 1 SD. We also use the Niño-3.4 index to represent ENSO, which is SSTAs averaged over equatorial central-eastern Pacific (170°W to 120°W, 5°S to 5°N).

To assess trends of the SST and IOD events in a warming climate, we analyzed monthly SST data from the CMIP6 (table S1), including historical simulations during the 1950–2014 and future projections from scenarios of Shared Socioeconomic Pathways with extremely high radiative forcing (SSP585) during 2015–2100. All variables from CMIP6 used in the study are interpolated to a horizontal resolution of 1°. The monthly SSTA data used to calculate the DMI are obtained by removing the monthly climatology over 1950–2100 and applying a quadratic detrending to remove the influence of anthropogenic global warming. To facilitate comparison of pIOD frequency across different periods, the 151-year SSTAs are divided into two periods: the historical period (1950–2024) and future projections (2025–2100). A bootstrap test is performed to assess the significance of changes in the pIOD frequency across CMIP6 models.

Identification of MHWs

A MHW event is identified as SST exceeding a baseline for a minimum of 5 consecutive days (53–55). The baseline is obtained as the 90th percentile of SSTs, within 11-day window during selected years (55). We use a running baseline with a 11-year sliding window. This approach effectively excludes the influence of the anthropogenic global warming trend on defining MHWs. If the time interval between two MHW events is 2 days or less, then we consider them as one MHW event. MHW days is defined as the number of days identified as MHW events during each calendar month (53). In Fig. 6, we, instead, use a fixed baseline over the entire analysis period to identify MHWs, which allows for a clearer illustration of how the long-term warming trend exacerbates regional MHW activity.

Model experiments

To further examine the remote influence of pIOD on the MHWs along TSEA coast, we analyzed Indian Ocean pacemaker experiments using the coupled climate model Community Earth System Model 1 from National Center for Atmospheric Research (63). The Indian Ocean SSTA was nudged to observed values, and sponge layer was applied at the western, southern, and eastern boundaries (fig. S14) (27). The model was forced by the same external forcing as the historical simulation period (1920–2005) and Representative Concentration Pathway 8.5 future projection period (2006–2019) from CMIP5. By slightly perturbing the initial conditions, we obtain a 10-member ensemble. The ensemble averaged results isolate the effect of the Indian Ocean SSTA.

To assess the contributions of pIOD-induced equatorial wind anomalies in the eastern Atlantic to sea level changes along the TSEA coast, we use a linear ocean model with a horizontal resolution of 0.25° (64). The model was forced by composites of NDJ-mean surface wind stress anomalies in the equatorial eastern Atlantic (15°W to 15°E, 5°S to 5°N) during IOD events. The model was integrated for 10 years, with the past 5 years used for analysis.
