A relevant feature combination assisted bi-level evolutionary multi-objective algorithm for feature selection in classification
Authors: Lining Xing, Jun Li, Hong Ma, Zhenhua Su, Jian Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMinimizing the number of selected features and reducing the error rate are the two primary objectives in feature selection problems. However, with the exponentially expanded search space caused by the increasing number of features, existing multi-objective feature selection methods often get trapped in local optima and fail to achieve a good balance between these two objectives. To address these issues, this study introduces definitions of relevant and irrelevant feature combinations to distinguish promising from unpromising feature subsets. These definitions facilitate exploration of the decision space and enable effective approximation to regions where segments of the Pareto front (PF) may reside. By analyzing the relationship between the two objectives, a bi-level environmental selection method is proposed to achieve two goals: (1) ensuring basic convergence performance in terms of error rate and (2) maintaining a sound balance between the two objectives. Subsequently, a novel multi-objective evolutionary feature selection algorithm named DRF-FM is developed based on the aforementioned findings. Extensive experiments were conducted on 22 datasets using five comparative algorithms to verify the performance of DRF-FM. The results demonstrate that DRF-FM outperforms the competitors with the most superior overall performance.Keywords: Multi-objective optimization, Feature selection, Relevant feature combination, Bi-level SelectionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Feature extraction serves as a fundamental preprocessing technique in diverse machine learning tasks (e.g., classification, regression, and clustering)1,2, facilitating the transformation of raw high-dimensional data into compact, discriminative representations that enhance model performance and interpretability3. In these contexts, the selection of optimal features critically influences model performance, interpretability, and computational efficiency. However, in recent years, the escalating complexity of real-world datasets–characterized by high dimensionality, noise, and non-linear relationships–has created an imperative for advanced optimization techniques4.

Over the past decades, feature selection methods have undergone rapid development, with a substantial number of algorithms proposed in the literature. Based on different evaluation criteria, these methods are broadly categorized into filter, wrapper, and embedded approaches5–7. Filter-based methods leverage the relationship between individual features and the target variable; embedded methods perform feature selection concurrently with classifier training; while wrapper-based approaches employ a learning model to explicitly evaluate feature subset performance8. Wrapper methods typically involve two stages: 1) generating feature subsets through stochastic or sequential search strategies; and 2) using a specific classifier as an evaluator to assess subset quality, with the subset exhibiting optimal classification performance selected as the final feature set. Although filter and embedded methods are computationally efficient, their results often compromise on performance due to inherent evaluation criterion limitations. In contrast, among wrapper methods, stochastic search has emerged as a more popular strategy compared to sequential search–latter being prone to local optima–with evolutionary algorithms (EAs) representing a key branch of this paradigm9.

Within EA frameworks, traditional feature selection methods often rely on single-objective optimization10, which typically struggles to balance conflicting objectives such as classification accuracy, feature redundancy, and computational complexity. Moreover, single-objective approaches frequently result in suboptimal solutions–prioritizing one criterion at the expense of others–due to their inherent inability to address multi-faceted trade-offs comprehensively11.

However, feature selection inherently involves multiple objectives–such as maximizing discriminative power, minimizing feature redundancy, and maintaining interpretability. Specifically, minimizing feature subset size while maximizing classification performance represents two core objectives in feature selection, which can be framed as a classic multi-objective optimization problem. To address this, multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs)12,13 have seen successful applications in feature selection tasks across diverse domains–including computer vision, medical informatics, pattern recognition, and bioinformatics–over recent years14–19. This trend can be attributed to two primary factors:

Comprehensive Optimization Capability: Multi-objective optimization excels in simultaneously optimizing multiple conflicting objectives, offering a holistic exploration of the problem space. Unlike single-objective methods–whose focus on a single metric often leads to biased solutions–MOEAs generate a diverse Pareto optimal set, enabling domain experts to select feature subsets aligned with specific application requirements.

In feature selection, an insufficient number of features may lead to an inability to accurately capture the subject’s characteristics, while excessive feature combinations (e.g., full-feature solutions) often introduce redundancy, degrading algorithmic efficiency. Thus, an optimal feature combination likely exists that balances these two extremes. Motivated by this observation, we hypothesize that certain features or feature combinations can be classified as relevant or irrelevant to the desired selection outcome. Consider a dataset with seven features:two feature subsets (“0 1 0 1 0 1 1” and “0 0 0 1 0 1 1”) yield identical error rates despite differing in the second feature. This discrepancy indicates the second feature’s irrelevance, as its presence/absence does not affect performance. In another case, subsets (“1 0 0 1 0 1 1” and “1 1 0 1 0 0 1”) with identical error rates share the first, fourth, and seventh features–highlighting this combination’s relevance, as these features consistently correlate with performance regardless of other feature variations. By labeling feature combinations as relevant or irrelevant, the evolutionary process can be guided more robustly toward the Pareto front. Importantly, error rates lie in a fixed set of values ranging from 0 to 1 (similar to discrete metrics not continuous variables), naturally generating multiple instances of identical values that facilitate this classification framework.

In this study, classification error rate and the number of selected features are defined as the two core optimization objectives. However, these objectives carry unequal priority when addressing selection trade-offs: a solution with poor error performance–even if it achieves an extremely low feature count–is generally unacceptable, while a solution with high accuracy but a relatively large feature set (e.g., the full-feature baseline) may retain evolutionary potential by offering rich search opportunities for subsequent iterations. This distinction highlights that error rate should be prioritized as the primary objective, serving as a prerequisite for feature count optimization rather than an equivalent criterion.

These findings provide novel directions for enhancing the performance of multi-objective feature selection algorithms. Herein, we introduce formal definitions of “relevant” and “irrelevant” feature combinations to systematically identify subsets with high evolutionary potential. By leveraging these definitions, the search process in the solution space can be guided to prioritize critical feature interactions while pruning low-potential subsets, thereby improving exploration efficiency. Furthermore, through an in-depth analysis of the objective trade-off, we propose a bi-level multi-objective environmental selection framework designed to tackle complex feature selection scenarios. Finally, by integrating these feature combination definitions into the proposed framework, we develop a novel bi-level (dual-level) relevant feature based feature selection MOEA–DRF-FM–specifically tailored for high-dimensional feature selection tasks.

Background and related studies

Single-objective feature selection methods

Feature selection algorithms designed for single-objective optimization center on refining a single performance metric–this could involve exclusively maximizing classification accuracy, or merging two factors (classification performance and the number of selected features) into a single, unified objective function. Unlike multi-objective approaches that generate multiple non-dominated solutions, single-objective methods focus on identifying a single optimal feature subset that best satisfies this sole optimization target. Such algorithms have been widely explored in prior research, with variations in their search strategies and evaluation criteria, as detailed below20,21.

Exhaustive search is rarely employed in single-objective feature selection methods6,22,23. This rarity stems from its prohibitive computational cost: even for datasets with a relatively small number of features (e.g., 50), exhaustive exploration of all possible feature subsets becomes computationally unfeasible in most practical scenarios. To address this inefficiency, heuristic search techniques have become the primary choice for single-objective feature selection. Representative examples include sequential forward selection (SFS)24 and sequential backward selection (SBS)25, both of which suffer from the “nesting effect”–a limitation where features once selected cannot be removed later, and features once removed cannot be reselected. The “plus l-take-away r” strategy26 attempts to mitigate this issue by combining l iterations of SFS with r iterations of SBS; while this approach theoretically avoids the nesting effect, it faces the practical challenge of determining appropriate values for l and r.

Beyond these, other heuristic-based single-objective methods include the two-layer cutting plane algorithm developed by Mao and Tsang27, which is tailored to search for optimal feature subsets, and the rough set theory-driven algorithm proposed by Min et al28.. Min et al.’s work includes both a heuristic search and a backtracking algorithm (the latter functioning as an improved exhaustive search). Their results demonstrated that the heuristic search achieved performance comparable to the backtracking algorithm but with significantly reduced computational time–making it more suitable for scenarios with limited resources (e.g., time or budget constraints). In recent years, early evolutionary computation (EC) techniques–such as genetic algorithms (GAs), genetic programming (GP), particle swarm optimization (PSO), and ant colony optimization (ACO)–have also been utilized for single-objective feature selection6.

The evaluation criteria for single-objective feature selection algorithms vary by method type. For wrapper-based single-objective approaches, the classification performance of the selected feature subset serves as the core evaluation metric22,29. Common classifiers integrated into these wrappers include decision trees (DT), support vector machines (SVMs), Naïve Bayes (NB), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), artificial neural networks (ANNs), and linear discriminant analysis (LDA).

Filter-based single-objective methods, by contrast, rely on metrics from diverse disciplines, such as information-theoretic measures, correlation metrics, distance measures, and consistency measures6. A simple form of filter method is single-feature ranking: features are ranked by a specific criterion, and only the top-ranked features are selected22. The Relief algorithm30 is a classic example of this approach, using a distance metric to assess the relevance of each individual feature and select all relevant ones. While single-feature ranking methods are computationally efficient, they fail to account for feature interactions–a shortcoming that often leads to redundant feature subsets (or local optima) when applied to complex tasks (e.g., microarray gene data analysis, where genes exhibit inherent interconnections)6,31. To address this, filter methods capable of evaluating entire feature subsets have gained traction. For instance, Wang et al32. proposed a distance measure that gauges the discrepancy between the selected feature space and the full feature space, aiming to find a subset that approximates the performance of all features. Peng et al33. developed the minimum redundancy–maximum relevance (mRMR) method based on mutual information.

Statistical and sparse learning methods also contribute to single-objective feature selection. Statistical approaches like T-tests, logistic regression, hierarchical clustering, and classification and regression trees (CART) are computationally straightforward while achieving competitive performance34, though they often rely on assumptions about the data’s probability distribution. Sparse learning methods have emerged more recently, with sparse logistic regression35 being a notable example for feature selection–especially for tasks involving millions of features.

Most single-objective feature selection algorithms share several defining characteristics6,22. First, their optimization target is singular, with no attempt to balance multiple conflicting objectives. Second, their design relies heavily on specific search strategies (e.g., heuristic or EC-based) and evaluation criteria (e.g., classification performance or feature relevance), which are tailored to the single objective being optimized. Finally, unlike multi-objective methods that output a set of trade-off solutions, single-objective algorithms typically return a single optimal feature subset (or a subset that meets predefined thresholds for the single objective), making them suitable for scenarios where a clear, unified performance target is prioritized20.

Multi-objective feature selection methods

While various works on filter and embedded feature selection algorithms are proposed in references40–43, this section focuses on reviewing wrapper-based MOEAs for feature selection, given the article’s emphasis on wrapper methods and MOEAs.

Based on environmental selection mechanisms, MOEAs can be broadly classified into four categories44–46: 1) dominance-based; 2) decomposition-based; 3) indicator-based; 4) others. For multi-objective feature selection methods, we focuses on discussing the innovative methodologies employed by scholars beyond multi-objective algorithms to address problems.

Improving the search strategy of the dominance-based MOEA is a popular direction. In Huang et al.47, NSGA-II is modified to select the global non-dominated feature subsets, and then the method FBSM which yields the fitness thresholds is proposed to choose the global solutions with the lowest ranks as the final solutions. Cheng et al.48 proposed a variable granularity search-based MOEA (VGS-MOEA) to tackle the increasing search space. In VGS-MOEA, one bit in the individual representation denotes a group of features and results in the search space reducing greatly. Xu et al.38 made improvements on the basic dominance-based EA framework in three aspects:To begin with, the reproductive process is refined to enhance offspring quality; next, a redundancy analysis approach is introduced to eliminate duplicate solutions; additionally, a diversity-driven selection strategy is employed to further refine the retained solutions.

Algorithms decomposing a multi-objective feature selection problem into a series of subproblems using a set of reference vectors or points are developed. In Nguyen et al.49, a new decomposition approach with two mechanisms (static and dynamic) based on multiple reference points under the MOEA based on decomposition (MOEA/D) framework was proposed to address the difficulties of feature selection. In MFMOEA50, a series of reference vectors was used to set a series of single-objective problems according to the position of vectors in the objective space, thus assisting the optimization of the original multi-objective problem. In Jiao et al.51, a multiform framework combined with its auxiliary single-objective feature selection tasks was proposed, where promising feature subsets from single-objective feature selection tasks can be utilized to boost the evolutionary search of the multi-objective FS task.

Niche strategies can also be used to address the lack of diversity in the obtained population in feature selection problems. In Wang et al. reference39, a set of sub-archives along with a set of subproblems are generated in the evolutionary process o ensure diversity by considering both the search space and the objective space. Finally, non-dominated individuals in all sub-archives are retained to guide the the evolutionary feature selection process. In reference52, a niching-based multio-bjective feature selection method that simultaneously minimizes the number of selected features and the classification error rate. Specifically, the article identified 1) a set of feature subsets with good convergence and distribution and 2) multiple feature subsets choosing the same number of features with almost the same lowest classification error rate to assist the selection of features.

Shrinking the decision space of the high-dimensional feature selection can be a sound way to tickle problems with a large number of features. In Luo et al. reference53, the conditional entropy of each feature is computed to mitigate the likelihood of selecting irrelevant features. A larger conditional entropy indicates a higher correlation between the feature and the category. To generate high-quality initial solutions and facilitate convergence, feature entropy is compared against a random number for each bit in every solution. Cheng et al.54 proposed a steering matrix to guide population evolution, which significantly enhances search efficiency while acquiring high-quality feature subsets. Specifically, each element within the steering matrix SM denotes the probability of selecting a feature in an individual (feature subset), with this probability derived from considering the importance of both features and individuals. Leveraging the proposed steering matrix, two key operators termed dimensionality reduction and individual repairing operators are devised to effectively guide the population evolution across generations.

Besides, there also exist studies that specifically target high-dimensionality feature selection. To address MOEAs’ limitations in high-dimensional datasets, DR-RPMODE55 adopts a two-stage strategy: it first uses freezing and activation operators for fast dimensionality reduction (removing irrelevant/redundant features), then leverages an improved multi-objective differential evolution framework to filter duplicated/redundant solutions and implement a classification performance-prioritized preference handling method. For high-dimensional feature selection, IMOABC proposes a filter–wrapper integrated method based on an improved multi-objective artificial bee colony framework56, optimizing three objectives (feature error rate, subset ratio, distance) to boost efficiency. It also introduces Fisher Score-based initialization (enhancing initial solution quality) and a dynamic adaptive strategy (accelerating convergence and strengthening global search). To tackle high-dimensional data’s discrete decision space, HMOFA develops a hierarchical learning multi-objective firefly framework57: it clusters features via multi-metric evaluation for population initialization, designs a hierarchy-guided learning model (guiding individuals toward superior/away from inferior solutions to avoid oscillation and local optima), and uses a duplicate solution modification mechanism to reduce redundancy.

There also exists some other algorithms that do not fall into the above categories. In reference58, two-surrogates assisted EA was proposed in which one coarse model and one fine model worked together to help the evolution process. In reference59, a multi-task multi-objective optimisation algorithm for feature selection was developed with the aim of capturing and sharing common knowledge for related feature selection tasks.

The core motivation of this study lies in exploring the interplay between the two objectives and leveraging information embedded within feature combinations that yield identical error rates. Specifically, recognizing the primacy of error rate, we propose a bi-level environmental selection scheme to first derive a population with minimized error rates. By systematically identifying relevant and irrelevant feature combinations, low-potential individuals are pruned from the population while new promising candidates are generated, thereby effectively guiding the population to approach regions that may contain segments of the Pareto front (PF).

The proposed algorithm

This section commences with an overview of the proposed DRF-FM framework. Subsequently, an offspring generation method is presented to efficiently traverse the decision space. A strategy for identifying relevant and irrelevant feature combinations is then developed. Building upon this strategy, a method for eliminating redundant solutions and generating promising candidates is detailed. Finally, a bi-level environmental selection approach tailored to two evolutionary stages is proposed. To vividly illutrate the procedure of the proposed algorithm, a flowchart is given in Fig. 1.

The main procedure of the proposed algorithm.

In DRF-FM, efforts focus on exploring and uncovering the high-dimensional decision space. Firstly, the offspring generation method and the bi-level environmental selection adopting a group of predefined reference vectors to partition the candidate population into two subpopulations: 1) individuals in the first subpopulation are selected and survive to the next generation using multi-objective environmental selection methods (Tchebycheff approach); 2) individuals in the second subpopulation are prioritized based on the dot product between its objective value vector and the corresponding reference vector to further explore the decision space in the vicinity of the reference vector. Secondly, in the offspring generation method, individuals for generating the offspring population are randomly selected from the two subpopulations. Finally, definitions of relevant and irrelevant feature combinations are introduced to distinguish feature combinations that capture essential problem characteristics, thereby enhancing the searching efficiency.

Another novelty of this paper is the bi-level environmental selection method. In the first-level selection, all the individuals are selected according to their error rate values to push the whole population keeping away from the regions exhibiting little valuable information. In the second-level selection, we adopt reference vectors and multi-objective environmental selection method to keep the diversity of the whole population, thus making a sound balance between the error rate and the number of selected features.

It is worth noting that the main novelty is the bi-level scheme instead of the two widely used methods. Besides, the cooperation between two selection methods is a well-established concept in the field of multi-objective optimization.

Performance Metric: Considering that there is no information about the ideal solution of the test problems, the hypervolume (HV)65 is used to evaluate the performance of the results obtained by the five algorithms. For an MOP, suppose that Q is the output population and is a preset reference point for the MOP. The HV value of Q is the volume of the region that dominates y and is dominated by points in Q. For each test instance in this section, the reference point is set (1,1)38.

Besides, the inverse generation distance (IGD)66 is utilized in this paper to evaluate the performance of the multi-objective optimization algorithms. In the calculation of IGD, a set of ideal solutions of the problems are need to be the reference points. However, unlike test problems, the feature recognition problem cannot obtain a fully accurate true PF. As a result, we selected the reference points in accordance with the method proposed in Reference50. Initially, the nondominated solution sets generated from 30 independent runs of seven distinct algorithms were collected for each dataset. These solution sets were subsequently merged into an extended nondominated front via non-dominated sorting. This extended front serves as the reference front for calculating IGD values and is assumed to approximate the true Pareto front for the corresponding dataset. Generally, a higher HV value indicates superior performance of an algorithm in terms of solution diversity and convergence. In contrast, a lower IGD value denotes better algorithmic performance, as it reflects a closer alignment between the generated solution set and the reference front (i.e., higher accuracy in approximating the true Pareto front).

Moreover, considering that the error rate is the main optimization objective of the problem, the minmal classification error rate (MCER) data of the six algorithms on the 22 datasets is also collected to verify the performance of the proposed algorithm38.

In spambase, individuals obtained by DRF-FM can dominate nearly all the other individuals. In Vehicle, PRDH, MOEA/D and MFFS can not converge to the two objectives, NSGA-II does not achieve expected diversity, and MFMOEA is outperformed by DRF-FM on the converge performance.

In conclusion, from Table 2 and Fig. 3, DRF-FM’s 12 single-dataset wins, dominant cross-algorithm performance (>60% superiority vs. all rivals), and statistically significant Friedman result establish it as the top-performing method.

Such superior error rate performance can be attributed to the bi-level environmental selection method. In the first level of the environmental selection, individuals with excellent error rates may be given priority retention to the next generation, while those with poor error performance are filtered out to improve the overall quality of the solution set. In this manner, the proposed algorithm can explore the decision space in the early stage of the process to obtains individuals with high-quality error rate performance. Hence, the basic population quality can be guaranteed and the populations ibtained in the late stage will not get individuals with terrible error rate performance.

Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we propose DRF-FM, a novel multi-objective feature selection algorithm that integrates relevant feature combination (RFC) and irrelevant feature combination (IRFC) identification mechanisms with bi-level environmental selection. The core contribution lies in its dual-strategy framework: 1) RFCs are leveraged to guide the search towards promising regions in the decision space, while IRFCs are filtered to mitigate computational waste; 2) a bi-level selection mechanism balances convergence and diversity by prioritizing error rate optimization and integrating the Tchebycheff approach. Theoretical analysis confirms that DRF-FM achieves a per-generation time complexity of , demonstrating scalability with population size, feature dimensionality, and objective count. Experiments on 22 UCI datasets validate its superiority over state-of-the-art algorithms in both low-dimensional and high-dimensional scenarios.

While there still exist some shortcomings of the proposed algorithm. First, it is difficult for duplicate individuals to be completely eliminated, or for their beneficial information to be fully extracted. Further in-depth research on the duplicate individuals is needed to obtain more information. Second, the bi-level environmental selection algorithm lacks strategies specifically designed for large-scale features. In the proposed algorithm, the novelties lie in the bi-level framework instead of the methods in the second level, and some tailored methods are still necessary for large-scale feature selection. Finally, it is challenging for the algorithm to achieve acceptable error rates across all test datasets. In some datasets, the average minimal error rates are still high for the proposed algorithm and the comparative algorithms. We need to find the reasons and propose some useful ideas

For future work, we intend to explore three directions. First, extending DRF-FM to handle streaming data scenarios by integrating online learning mechanisms to adapt to dynamic feature spaces. Second, developing a parallel computing framework to further reduce computational complexity, especially for ultra-high-dimensional datasets (e.g., beyond 10,000 features). Third, incorporating interpretability analysis to visualize how RFCs and IRFCs influence the feature selection process, which can enhance the algorithm’s applicability in critical domains like medical diagnosis. These advancements will solidify DRF-FM’s role in addressing real-world big data challenges with both efficiency and interpretability.
