OGT-mediated PIN O-GlcNAcylation drives depression-like behaviors by impairing NOS-stargazin-GluA1 signaling
Authors: Xunhu Gu, Xu Liu, Yong Xiong, Xuefen Chen, Yancai Zhang, Li-Li Zheng, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder is associated with impaired excitatory synaptic transmission, but the molecular mechanisms linking chronic stress to altered AMPA receptor trafficking remain incompletely understood. Here we show that chronic mild stress increases OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of PIN at serine 88, which stabilizes PIN and enhances its interaction with nitric oxide synthase. This suppresses nitric oxide synthase activity, reduces stargazin S-nitrosylation, weakens stargazin-GluA1 binding, and impairs GluA1-containing AMPA receptor trafficking. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of OGT restores this signaling pathway and alleviates stress-induced depression-like behaviors in mice. These findings identify the OGT-PIN-NOS-stargazin axis as a regulator of stress-induced synaptic dysfunction and suggest that targeting OGT may help restore AMPA receptor trafficking in depression-related conditions. Subject terms: Neurological disorders, Depression

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common psychiatric condition characterized by persistent low mood and anhedonia1. In 2008, the World Health Organization (WHO) ranked MDD as the third leading contributor to the global burden of disease and projected it would become the foremost cause by 20302, underscoring its growing public health challenge. Current treatments, such as cognitive behavioral therapy and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, show limited efficacy, with 30–50% of patients failing to achieve remission3, highlighting the urgent need for therapeutic strategies. A central pathological feature of MDD is dysregulated synaptic plasticity, particularly in mood-related brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and nucleus accumbens4. The AMPA-type glutamate receptor (AMPAR), a tetrameric ion channel composed of glutamate receptor (GluA)1–4 subunits, plays a pivotal role in excitatory neurotransmission and synaptic strength modulation5. Notably, impaired trafficking of GluA1-containing AMPAR disrupts synaptic transmission efficiency, contributing to dendritic spine loss and maladaptive neuroplasticity, key hallmarks of depression6. Therefore, modulating GluA1-AMPAR trafficking could restore synaptic function, offering a promising therapeutic target for antidepressant development.

Fast-acting antidepressants like ketamine and its metabolite (2R,6R)-hydroxynorketamine (HNK) enhance synaptic GluA1 expression and phosphorylation, promoting synaptic potentiation and rapid reversal of depressive-like behaviors7. The synaptic anchoring of AMPARs requires transmembrane AMPAR regulatory proteins (TARPs), particularly stargazin (TARP γ-2), which bridges GluA1-containing AMPARs to the postsynaptic scaffold protein postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95), ensuring their stabilization at the membrane8. Disruption of stargazin–GluA1 interactions, whether through stargazin mutations, aberrant post-translational modifications, or competitive binding interference, severely impairs GluA1 synaptic delivery, leading to synaptic weakening in preclinical models9. These findings position stargazin as a key mechanistic node linking AMPAR dysregulation to MDD pathophysiology. Yet, how stargazin’s function is modulated or whether its dysfunction contributes to treatment resistance in depression remains unresolved.

Recent studies highlight S-nitrosylation, a redox-dependent post-translational modification mediated by nitric oxide (NO), as a pivotal regulator of stargazin function and GluA1-AMPAR synaptic trafficking9,10. Neuronal nitric oxide synthase (NOS)-derived NO promotes S-nitrosylation of stargazin (SNO-stargazin) at critical cysteine residues, enhancing its binding to GluA1 and facilitating AMPAR insertion at synapses11,12. In depression, this pathway is impaired: chronic stress reduces NOS activity, leading to decreased SNO-stargazin, weakened stargazin–GluA1 interactions, and subsequently diminished trafficking of GluA1-AMPAR to the synaptic membrane10. However, the specific mechanisms underlying the impaired SNO-stargazin remain poorly understood.

NOS activity itself is tightly controlled by its endogenous inhibitor, the protein inhibitor of NOS (PIN), which binds to NOS and destabilizes its active dimeric form, thereby reducing NO production13. This inhibition may affect various biological processes through altered NO signaling, including MDD13. Nevertheless, whether PIN-mediated regulation of NO production affects SNO-stargazin remains unexplored.

O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) glycosylation (or O-GlcNAcylation) is a post-translational modification in which serine and threonine residues of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins are modified by O-GlcNAc14. The regulation of O-GlcNAc glycosylation involves two key enzymes: β-N-acetylglucosamine transferase (OGT) and β-N-acetylglucosaminidase (OGA)15. OGT catalyzes the addition of O-GlcNAc, while OGA hydrolyzes this modification16. Functionally, OGT acts as a sensor for metabolism and stress, enabling rapid physiological adaptations in response to external stimuli17. In the depression model, chronic social defeat stress significantly elevated OGT expression and global O-GlcNAcylation levels in the medial prefrontal cortex of stress-susceptible mice18. Importantly, astrocytic OGT deletion reduced synaptic damage and exerts antidepressant-like effects18. Our preliminary data revealed a putative interaction between OGT and PIN and predicted that PIN may undergo O-GlcNAcylation. Thus, we hypothesized that OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of PIN enhanced its inhibitory effect on NOS, leading to reduced SNO-stargazin, impaired GluA1-containing AMPAR trafficking, and ultimately contributing to stress-induced synaptic dysfunction and depression-like behaviors.

In this study, we elucidated an OGT-PIN-NOS-stargazin pathway that regulates GluA1-AMPAR trafficking under stress conditions. Specifically, we demonstrated that chronic mild stress (CMS) reduced astrocytic NOS activity, leading to decreased SNO-stargazin and impaired stargazin–GluA1 interactions, which disrupted GluA1-AMPAR trafficking. We further identified that CMS elevated OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of PIN at serine 88 (S88), increasing its stability and suppressing NOS activity, while OGT knockout prevented CMS-induced depressive behaviors. These findings suggest that OGT inhibition may act as a potential therapeutic strategy for MDD.

Results

Stargazin S-nitrosylation was decreased, and its interaction with GluA1 was reduced in CMS

To establish a CMS model, both male and female C57BL/6J mice were subjected to a 6-week CMS paradigm. To evaluate depressive-like behavior, mice were subjected to the SPT, FST, and TST. As shown in Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1A, mice in the CMS group exhibited reduced sucrose preference beginning at week 4, which became significant by week 6. In the OFT, CMS mice showed reduced total distance traveled and decreased time spent in the center zone compared with control mice (Fig. 1B). Additionally, immobility times in both the FST and TST were significantly increased at week 6 compared to control mice (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1B). CMS significantly reduced both total distance traveled and time spent in the center zone. To investigate the molecular changes associated with CMS, hippocampal tissues were analyzed. The biotin-switch assay revealed that SNO-stargazin levels were decreased in CMS mice at weeks 4 and 6 (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1C), while total stargazin levels remained unchanged. Furthermore, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis demonstrated a reduced interaction between stargazin and GluA1 in CMS mice compared to controls (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 1D). To further assess synaptic alterations, surface biotinylation assays were performed in hippocampal neurons. The results showed a significant reduction in cell surface GluA1 expression with prolonged CMS exposure (Fig. 1E). Altogether, CMS led to reduced SNO-stargazin and impaired its interaction with GluA1 in the hippocampus from both female and male mice.

NOS-mediated S-nitrosylation of stargazin was decreased, leading to reduced GluA1 interaction in the CMS model

To investigate the role of NOS in regulating SNO-stargazin and chronic stress, a series of experiments was performed with CMS and control mice. As shown in Fig. 2A, both GFAP⁺ cells and GFAP⁺NOS⁺ double-positive cells were significantly reduced in CMS mice compared to controls. Next, AAV-oe-NOS vectors were injected into CMS mice to overexpress NOS. Following NOS overexpression, the reduced sucrose preference observed in CMS mice was significantly restored (Fig. 2B). Similarly, the increased immobility times recorded in both the FST and TST, as well as the decreased total distance traveled and time spent in the center zone in the OFT, were reversed upon NOS overexpression (Fig. 2C), indicating an amelioration of CMS-induced behavioral abnormalities. Moreover, GFAP⁺NOS⁺ cells were increased in the CMS + NOS group compared to CMS alone (Fig. 2D). NOS overexpression restored SNO-stargazin (Fig. 2E). Additionally, the impaired interaction between stargazin and GluA1 in CMS mice was also rescued by NOS overexpression (Fig. 2F). NOS overexpression also reversed CMS-induced decrease in surface GluA1, while total GluA1 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 2G). Taken together, NOS deficiency in CMS contributed to decreased SNO-stargazin and impaired GluA1 interaction, both of which were reversible upon NOS overexpression.

To determine whether astrocytic NOS overexpression protects against stress-induced synaptic dysfunction, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) in layer II/III pyramidal neurons of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) from CMS and CMS + NOS mice. Representative traces showed a clear reduction in mEPSC frequency following CMS treatment, which was prevented by NOS overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant decrease in mEPSC frequency in CMS compared to controls, whereas the NOS overexpression group maintained mEPSC frequency at levels comparable to non-stressed mice (Supplementary Fig. 2B). In contrast, mEPSC amplitude was unchanged across all groups (Supplementary Fig. 2C), indicating preserved postsynaptic AMPAR function. In summary, astrocytic NOS overexpression attenuated the CMS-induced impairment of glutamatergic synaptic transmission by maintaining presynaptic input onto mPFC pyramidal neurons.

PIN bound to NOS and attenuated the S-nitrosylation of stargazin

To investigate the regulatory role of PIN on NOS activity, Co-IP was first performed in HEK293T cells. The results confirmed a physical interaction between PIN and NOS (Fig. 3A). This interaction was further validated in primary astrocytes, where Co-IP analysis also demonstrated a PIN–NOS association (Fig. 3B). Knockdown of PIN in primary astrocytes and primary neurons using shPIN significantly reduced PIN protein levels, as confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 3C). Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis revealed markedly reduced PIN expression in both GFAP⁺ astrocytes and NeuN⁺ neurons following shPIN treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). PIN depletion led to a marked increase in NOS enzymatic activity (Fig. 3D). To evaluate the role of PIN in chronic stress, AAV-shPIN was administered via ICV injection to knock down PIN expression in CMS mice. Behavioral assessments showed that PIN knockdown significantly restored sucrose preference and reduced immobility times in both the FST and TST, and increased total distance traveled and time spent in the center zone in the OFT in CMS mice, indicating an attenuation of depressive-like behaviors (Fig. 3E, F). At the molecular level, PIN knockdown reversed the CMS-induced decrease in GFAP⁺NOS⁺ cell numbers (Fig. 3G). PIN knockdown also decreased binding of PIN to NOS, which increased NOS activity and SNO-stargazin in CMS mice (Fig. 3H–J). In addition, the decreased stargazin and GluA1 interaction in CMS mice was restored upon silencing of PIN (Fig. 3K). Consistently, CMS significantly reduced surface GluA1 levels in hippocampal neurons, while PIN knockdown restored GluA1 surface expression without affecting total GluA1 levels (Fig. 3L). Collectively, PIN interacted with NOS to suppress the SNO-stargazin.

To investigate whether astrocytic PIN knockdown mitigates stress-induced synaptic dysfunction, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings of mEPSCs in layer II/III pyramidal neurons of the mPFC from CMS and CMS plus PIN knockdown mice. mEPSC frequency was significantly reduced following CMS treatment, whereas this reduction was prevented upon PIN knockdown in mice (Supplementary Fig. 3A). mEPSC frequency was significantly decreased in the CMS groups compared to controls, while PIN knockdown preserved mEPSC frequency at levels similar to non-stressed mice (Supplementary Fig. 3B). mEPSC amplitude remained unchanged across all groups (Supplementary Fig. 3C), suggesting that postsynaptic AMPAR function was not affected. These findings indicate that astrocytic PIN knockdown prevented CMS-induced impairment of glutamatergic synaptic transmission by preserving presynaptic excitatory input to mPFC neurons.

In the CMS model, the O-GlcNAcylation of PIN was elevated

To investigate the role of O-GlcNAcylation of PIN in chronic stress, succinylated wheat germ agglutinin (sWGA) pull-down and Co-IP assays were performed on hippocampal tissues collected from CMS mice and control mice. A significant increase in the O-GlcNAcylation of PIN in CMS mice was detected compared to controls (Fig. 4A, B). To evaluate the regulatory role of O-GlcNAc-related enzymes, CMS mice were treated with TMG, an O-GlcNAcase inhibitor, or OSMI-1, an OGT inhibitor. TMG treatment further increased PIN O-GlcNAcylation, while OSMI-1 partially reversed the CMS-induced enhancement (Fig. 4C, D). In summary, PIN O-GlcNAcylation was increased in the CMS model.

The O-GlcNAcylation of PIN at serine 88 was mediated by OGT

To further explore the interaction of PIN and OGT, the Co-IP assay was employed. The results confirmed a physical interaction between OGT and PIN (Fig. 5A), which was further validated in primary astrocytes, where Co-IP analysis also demonstrated a PIN–OGT association (Fig. 5B). Bioinformatic prediction using the YinOYang algorithm identified three potential O-GlcNAcylation sites on PIN, including T26, T67, and S88 (Fig. 5C). To experimentally confirm the critical modification site, HEK293T cells with PIN knockdown were transfected with Flag-tagged wild-type (WT) or mutant PIN constructs, including PIN-T26A mutant, PIN-T67A mutant, and PIN-S88A mutant. sWGA pull-down assays showed that O-GlcNAcylation was markedly reduced in the cells transfected with PIN-S88A mutant, whereas those transfected with T26A and T67A mutants retained levels comparable to the WT, identifying S88 as the key site (Fig. 5D). Additionally, PIN-knockdown cells transfected with either PIN-WT or PIN-S88A were treated with TMG. TMG increased O-GlcNAcylation in cells transfected with PIN-WT, but not in those transfected with PIN-S88A mutant, further supporting that the S88 site is required for this modification (Fig. 5E). Together, the S88 site was the primary OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation site on PIN.

O-GlcNAcylation of PIN promoted its protein stability and facilitated its interaction with NOS, thereby suppressing NOS activity

To explore the functional consequences of OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of PIN on its stability and interaction with NOS, primary astrocytes were used to generate OGT knockout (OGT-/-) cells. Compared to WT controls, OGT expression was abolished in OGT-/- cells (Fig. 6A). Following CHX treatment, PIN exhibited a markedly accelerated degradation rate in OGT-/- astrocytes in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 6B). Ub-mediated degradation was increased in OGT-/- astrocytes (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, PIN’s interaction with NOS was diminished in the absence of OGT, while NOS activity was significantly elevated (Fig. 6D, E). To validate whether S88 is the key O-GlcNAc site responsible for these effects, PIN-/- astrocytes were transfected with AAV-PIN-WT or AAV-PIN-S88A mutant. Compared to PIN-WT, transfection with AAV-PIN-S88A led to increased protein degradation in a time-dependent manner, enhanced Ub-mediated degradation, reduced binding to NOS, and higher NOS activity (Fig. 6F–I). These results demonstrate that OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of PIN at S88 enhanced PIN stability, promoted its association with NOS, and suppressed NOS activity.

O-GlcNAcylation of PIN reduced S-nitrosylation of stargazin and diminished its interaction with GluA1

SNO-stargazin has been implicated in the pathophysiology of depression due to its role in modulating AMPAR trafficking and synaptic plasticity19. Specifically, SNO-stargazin enhances its binding to AMPAR subunits, such as GluA1, thereby promoting increased surface expression of these receptors and altering synaptic transmission19. To investigate whether astrocytic PIN O-GlcNAcylation regulates stargazin S-nitrosylation and its interaction with GluA1, OGT-/- primary astrocytes were co-cultured with neurons. Biotin-switch assays revealed that astrocytic OGT deletion significantly increased neuronal stargazin S-nitrosylation, accompanied by enhanced interaction between stargazin and GluA1 (Fig. 7A, B). Similarly, in PIN-/- astrocytes, reconstitution with the PIN-S88A mutant, but not wild-type PIN, led to elevated neuronal SNO-stargazin and increased stargazin–GluA1 binding (Fig. 7C, D). In vivo, mice subjected to CMS exhibited reduced stargazin S-nitrosylation and diminished stargazin–GluA1 interaction in the hippocampus, changes that were partially reversed by administration of the OGT inhibitor OSMI-1 (Fig. 7E, F). The PIN-S88A mutant in astrocytes significantly increased GluA1 surface expression in co-cultured neurons compared to the PIN-WT group (Fig. 7G). Moreover, OSMI-1 treatment reversed the CMS-induced reduction in GluA1 surface levels, while total GluA1 levels remained unchanged in both groups (Fig. 7H). Collectively, PIN O-GlcNAcylation suppressed SNO-stargazin and its association with GluA1.

Targeting OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation alleviated depression-like behaviors

To validate the in vitro results in vivo, wild-type (OGT-WT) and OGT knockout (OGT-/-) mice were subjected to a 6-week CMS period, followed by a series of molecular and biochemical analyses. CMS significantly reduced sucrose preference, increased immobility time in both the FST and TST, and decreased total distance traveled and time spent in the center zone in the OFT in OGT-WT mice (Fig. 8A, B). However, these effects were markedly attenuated in OGT-/- mice, indicating a protective effect of OGT deletion against stress-induced behavioral deficits. Immunofluorescent (IF) analysis of hippocampal tissue revealed a significant decrease in the proportion of GFAP⁺NOS⁺ astrocytes following CMS exposure in WT mice, while this change was reversed in OGT-/- mice (Fig. 8C). At the molecular level, Co-IP assays revealed an increase in PIN O-GlcNAcylation in the CMS group, which was reduced in OGT-/- mice (Fig. 8D). Moreover, Biotin-switch analysis showed a CMS-induced reduction in SNO-stargazin, while Co-IP assays demonstrated decreased stargazin–GluA1 interaction; both effects were reversed in OGT-/- animals (Fig. 8E, F). Importantly, CMS significantly reduced surface GluA1 levels in the hippocampus of OGT-WT mice, without affecting total GluA1 levels, whereas this reduction was reversed in OGT-/- mice (Fig. 8G). Together, astrocytic OGT contributed to the development of depression-like behaviors and hippocampal synaptic dysfunction under CMS, via modulation of PIN O-GlcNAcylation and downstream effects on stargazin S-nitrosylation and AMPAR interaction.

To further confirm the functional role of astrocytic OGT under stress, we reintroduced OGT selectively into astrocytes of OGT-/- mice using a GFAP-OGT AAV vector. IF staining confirmed restoration of OGT expression in GFAP⁺ astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Behaviorally, astrocytic OGT overexpression in OGT-/- mice had no effect under basal conditions but significantly induced depressive-like behaviors following CMS, as evidenced by reduced sucrose preference and increased immobility in FST and TST (Supplementary Fig. 4B, C). At the molecular level, CMS combined with astrocytic OGT overexpression elevated PIN O-GlcNAcylation, decreased SNO-stargazin levels, and disrupted stargazin–GluA1 interactions (Supplementary Fig. 4D–F). These results further reinforce that astrocytic OGT promoted stress-induced depressive phenotypes and synaptic dysfunction via modulation of the PIN–NOS–stargazin pathway.

Discussion

MDD is a highly prevalent psychiatric condition with suboptimal treatment efficacy3. Convergent evidence implicates dysregulated trafficking of GluA1-AMPAR in MDD pathophysiology, where impaired synaptic targeting disrupts excitatory neurotransmission in mood-regulating circuits. Despite the therapeutic potential of normalizing GluA1-AMPAR trafficking6, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In this study, we demonstrated that CMS reduced NOS activity, leading to decreased SNO-stargazin and impaired stargazin–GluA1 interactions. Mechanistically, we identified PIN as a critical upstream regulator, showing that CMS increased OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of PIN at the S88 site, which enhanced PIN stability and its binding to NOS. This suppressed NOS activity, resulting in reduced SNO-stargazin and impaired GluA1-AMPAR trafficking. Finally, we showed that the knockout of OGT prevented CMS-induced depressive behaviors, confirming the pathway’s functional significance. These results reveal OGT inhibition as a potential strategy to restore stress-induced synaptic deficits in MDD.

Disrupted synaptic trafficking of GluA1-AMPAR impairs long-term potentiation in mood-regulating circuits, contributing to depression-related cognitive and behavioral symptoms20. At the molecular level, NOS-derived NO facilitates AMPAR synaptic delivery through SNO-stargazin, an essential AMPAR auxiliary protein21. This post-translational modification enhances stargazin–GluA1 binding and promotes synaptic receptor insertion10. Consistent with these findings, we showed that in the CMS mouse model, the NOS-mediated SNO-stargazin was markedly reduced, resulting in decreased interaction with GluA1 in hippocampal tissues. Importantly, we identified PIN as an upstream regulator of this pathway. PIN bound to NOS and suppressed its enzymatic activity in astrocytes, thereby decreasing SNO-stargazin and impairing subsequent interaction with GluA1. These results align with prior studies showing PIN binding destabilized the NOS homodimer required for NO synthesis13,22 while supporting a role for PIN-mediated NOS inhibition in regulating SNO-stargazin and the interaction between stargazin and GluA1.

One issue requiring further consideration is that reduced surface GluA1 did not lead to a detectable decrease in mEPSC amplitude in the present study. In general, mEPSC amplitude is interpreted as reflecting quantal size, including postsynaptic receptor number or conductance, whereas changes in mEPSC frequency are often associated with altered presynaptic release probability and/or the number of functional synapses23,24. Therefore, the selective change in mEPSC frequency suggests that the effect of reduced surface GluA1 may not simply reflect a direct reduction in basal postsynaptic AMPAR-mediated current. Several mechanisms may explain this apparent discrepancy. First, altered postsynaptic AMPAR trafficking may indirectly influence presynaptic release through trans-synaptic or retrograde signaling. For example, endocannabinoid-mediated retrograde signaling can suppress presynaptic glutamate release and reduce mEPSC frequency without necessarily changing mEPSC amplitude25. Second, other AMPAR subunits, such as GluA2- or GluA3-containing receptors, may compensate for the reduction in GluA1 and thereby preserve basal AMPAR-mediated miniature current amplitude26,27. Third, GluA1-containing AMPARs may be more important for activity-dependent synaptic strengthening than for maintaining basal miniature synaptic transmission. Thus, the functional consequence of reduced surface GluA1 may become more evident during synaptic plasticity, such as long-term potentiation (LTP), rather than under basal mEPSC recording conditions27,28. Future studies examining LTP, paired-pulse ratio, synaptic density, and presynaptic markers, such as synaptophysin, synapsin I, Bassoon, or vGlut1, will help determine whether the observed mEPSC frequency change reflects presynaptic dysfunction, altered synapse number, or impaired trans-synaptic communication.

O-GlcNAcylation is a common reversible post-translational modification catalyzed by OGT in the brain that regulates protein function in response to cellular nutrients29. A published study found that increased O-GlcNAcylation levels were closely correlated with long-term depression30. Similarly, OGT in the medial prefrontal cortex modulated CMS-induced depressive-like behavior via O-GlcNAcylation of glutamate transporter-1 in astrocytes18. While Chen et al. demonstrated OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of NOS during excitotoxicity31, the involvement of this modification in PIN-mediated depression mechanisms remained unknown. Our study suggests that OGT mediated PIN O-GlcNAcylation at the S88 site. Importantly, the OGT-modulated O-GlcNAcylation of PIN enhanced PIN protein stability and strengthened its binding to NOS, resulting in the inhibition of NOS enzymatic activity and reduced SNO-stargazin. Notably, a recent study by Dong et al. reported that astrocytic OGT plays a protective role by suppressing NF-κB-mediated inflammation, and its depletion leads to cognitive impairment and increased neuroinflammation32. These findings, although seemingly inconsistent with ours, likely reflect differences in disease models and mechanisms. Dong et al. focused on Alzheimer’s disease and inflammation-related cognitive decline, whereas our work centers on stress-induced depression-like activities and synaptic dysfunction. These divergent outcomes underscore the context-dependent roles of astrocytic OGT in the brain.

This study identifies an OGT-PIN-NOS-stargazin signaling axis regulating synaptic function and CMS-induced behaviors, demonstrating that OGT-regulated O-GlcNAcylation of PIN enhanced NOS inhibition, thereby reducing SNO-stargazin, impairing GluA1 binding, and disrupting AMPAR synaptic delivery and contributing to CMS-induced behavioral abnormalities relevant to MDD.

While these findings position OGT inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating CMS-induced behavioral and synaptic abnormalities, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we identify a clear molecular cascade linking OGT-mediated PIN O-GlcNAcylation to altered S-nitrosylation of stargazin and AMPAR trafficking, direct causal evidence connecting these molecular changes to neuronal function remains limited. The majority of mechanistic conclusions are based on biochemical and behavioral analyses, and although electrophysiological recordings and surface biotinylation assays were incorporated, additional approaches, such as live imaging of AMPAR trafficking or synaptic plasticity measurements, would further strengthen the functional link between molecular regulation and synaptic transmission. In particular, because the proposed glial O-GlcNAcylation–NOS–GluA1/stargazin mechanism affects neuronal activity indirectly, it remains to be formally demonstrated whether this pathway is sufficient and necessary to drive the observed synaptic defects. Future studies using glial cell type-specific models, including microglia-specific OGT- or O-GlcNAcylation-deficient mice, will be required to further determine whether glial O-GlcNAcylation causally regulates NOS activity, SNO-stargazin, GluA1/stargazin interaction, and AMPAR surface trafficking under chronic stress conditions. Second, while the study focuses on PIN as a critical downstream effector of OGT, OGT is a pleiotropic enzyme with numerous neuronal and astrocytic substrates; thus, other downstream targets of OGT inhibition may also contribute to the observed molecular and behavioral phenotypes and were not systematically explored here. Third, pharmacological and genetic manipulation of OGT may exert off-target or broader metabolic effects, given the central role of O-GlcNAcylation in cellular energy sensing and metabolism, which could indirectly influence both synaptic function and behavior. Finally, although astrocyte-centered mechanisms were emphasized, the broader network-level interactions between astrocytes and neurons, as well as potential region-specific effects, warrant further investigation. Addressing these limitations in future studies will be essential for fully defining the therapeutic potential and safety of targeting OGT in MDD.

Methods

Cell isolation and culture

Primary astrocytes were isolated from the cerebral cortices of neonatal (P0–P2) C57BL/6J mice33. Neonatal mice were decapitated, and the brains were rapidly removed and placed in ice-cold 1× Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) under sterile conditions. The olfactory bulbs were removed, and the meninges were meticulously dissected from the cortical surface using fine forceps under a stereomicroscope. Meninges-free cortices were then minced into small fragments and enzymatically digested in 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, Gibco, USA, Cat# 25200056) at 37 °C for 10 min. The tissue was subsequently dissociated mechanically by gentle trituration, and the resulting cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. Cells were centrifuged and resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Cat# 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat# 10099141) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Cat# 15140122), then plated in poly-D-lysine-coated T-75 flasks. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. After 10–12 days in vitro, flasks were shaken overnight at 260 rpm. The adherent astrocyte monolayer was washed with a pre-warmed medium and used for subsequent experiments.

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from embryonic day 15–17 (E15–E17) C57BL/6J mouse embryos34. Cortices were dissected in ice-cold HBSS, and meninges were carefully removed under a stereomicroscope. Tissues were minced and digested with 2 mg/mL papain in high-glucose DMEM at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by a brief treatment with 2.5 mg/mL DNase I in HBSS for 30 s. Digestion was terminated with 10% FBS in DMEM. The tissue was gently triturated, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, and centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min. Cells were resuspended in a Neurobasal™ medium supplemented with 2% B-27, 0.5 mM GlutaMAX, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, then seeded at a density of 50,000 cells/cm² on 0.1 mg/mL poly-D-lysine-coated plates. After a 4-h attachment period, the medium was replaced, and cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator with half-medium changes every 3 days.

HEK293T cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

For astrocyte-neuron co-culture experiments, confluent astrocyte cultures were treated with 10 μg/mL cytarabine (Ara-C; Cayman Chemical, Cat# 16069) for 24 h to inhibit astrocyte proliferation. Subsequently, primary neurons were seeded directly onto the astrocyte monolayer. Co-cultures were maintained in Neurobasal™ Medium supplemented as described above with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Cat# 15140122) for 10–14 days prior to analyses.

Vector construction and cell transfection

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) specifically targeting PIN (shPIN) and a non-targeting control shRNA (shNC) were designed and synthesized by Genesee Biotech. These constructs were cloned into GV102 vectors by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). For overexpression studies, full-length coding sequences of mouse NOS were inserted into adeno-associated virus (AAV; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) vectors to generate the overexpression construct (AAV-oe-NOS). For transient expression in HEK293T cells, the full-length coding sequence of NOS was amplified from cDNA and cloned into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1α-Puro vector (System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA; Cat# CD510B-1) with an N-terminal Myc tag to generate Myc-NOS. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using a commercial kit (QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit, Agilent Technologies, Cat# 200513) to generate three PIN mutants, PIN-threonine 26-to-alanine (PIN-T26A), PIN-threonine 67-to-alanine (PIN-T67A), and PIN-S88-to-alanine (PIN-S88A). The full-length coding sequence of wild-type PIN or mutant PIN-S88A sequences was inserted into AAV vectors to generate wild-type AAV-PIN or mutant AAV-S88A. The shRNA-overexpressing AAV was used to infect primary astrocyte cells. Cells were then collected for subsequent analysis.

OGT knockout in astrocytes was accomplished using lentiviral delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 components, with guide RNAs targeting exon regions of the OGT gene. Similarly, PIN knockout was achieved using CRISPR/Cas9-based lentiviral vectors targeting the PIN gene.

The full-length coding sequence of mouse PIN or mutants was amplified from cDNA templates and inserted into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1α-Puro vector (System Biosciences, Cat# CD510B-1), with an N-terminal Flag tag to generate Flag-tagged PIN (Flag-PIN). The full-length coding sequence of PIN or PIN-S88A mutants was inserted into an adeno-associated virus vector (AAV) to generate wild-type AVV-PIN or mutant AVV-S88A. For OGT overexpression, the full-length coding sequence of mouse OGT was cloned into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1α-Puro vectors with an N-terminal HA tag to generate the HA-tagged OGT expression construct. HEK293T cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, cells were transfected with plasmids encoding Flag-tagged wild-type or mutant PIN constructs and/or HA-tagged OGT using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# L3000008). Cells were collected 48 h post-transfection. The sequences of shRNAs and guide RNAs are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Establishment of in vivo CMS models

Male and female C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old, approximately 20–25 g) were procured from Hunan Slike Jingda Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China) and housed under standard laboratory conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 50–60% humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University Medical Research Ethics Committee (No. Review [2025] No. 0155). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. For each study, mice were divided into eight mice per group. Mice were subjected to a 6-week CMS protocol, which included a variety of mild, unpredictable stressors, such as cage tilting, light/dark cycle reversal, continuous illumination, wet bedding, and periods of food or water deprivation, following established protocols35. Control mice were maintained under standard housing conditions. Behavioral assessments were conducted to evaluate anhedonia and behavioral despair, with the sucrose preference test (SPT) performed weekly from week 1 to week 6, and the open field test (OFT), the forced swimming test (FST), and the tail suspension test (TST) conducted once at the end of week 2, 4, and 6. Hippocampal tissues were rapidly dissected on ice immediately following behavioral testing. Afterward, hippocampi were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until the following analyses.

Generation and use of genetically modified mouse models

OGT-WT mice and OGT knockout mice (OGT-/-) were purchased from Cyagen (Suzhou, China). For NOS overexpression, mice were divided into four groups (eight mice per group): control, CMS group, CMS plus negative control (NC), and CMS plus NOS overexpression. For CMS plus NC or NOS overexpression groups, CMS mice received bilateral intracerebroventricular (ICV) injections of AAV-oe-NOS or control vectors (AAV-NC) at a dose of 4 × 10¹⁰ vg per mouse 1 week prior to the CMS procedure36.

To achieve astrocyte-specific knockdown of PIN, mice were divided into four groups (eight mice per group): control, CMS group, and CMS plus shNC or CMS plus shPIN. AAV vectors carrying shRNA targeting mouse PIN (AAV9-shPIN) or non-targeting control shRNA (AAV9-shNC) were constructed and administered via ICV injection (4 × 10¹⁰ vg per mouse) into mice one week prior to the CMS procedure.

To assess the effect of inhibiting OGT, an additional group of CMS mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of OSMI-1 (10 mg/kg/day; MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-13466) during the entire six-week CMS period37. The vehicle group received an equivalent volume of DMSO diluted in saline.

To confirm the cellular specificity of astrocytic OGT, a rescue experiment was conducted in OGT-/- mice using an AAV vector expressing OGT under the control of the GFAP promoter (AAV-GFAP-OGT). OGT-/- mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8 per group): OGT-/-, OGT-/- + CMS, OGT-/- + GFAP-OGT, and OGT-/- + GFAP-OGT + CMS. AAV-GFAP-OGT was bilaterally injected into the hippocampus at a dose of 4 × 10¹⁰ vg per mouse, 4 weeks prior to CMS induction. Behavioral and molecular analyses were conducted after the 6-week CMS protocol.

Surface biotinylation assay

Surface expression of GluA1 was assessed in both hippocampal brain slices and co-cultured neurons using a membrane-impermeable biotinylation assay38. For hippocampal tissue, mice were euthanized, and hippocampi were rapidly dissected and incubated in ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF). For co-culture experiments, neurons cultured for 14 days were washed and chilled in cold aCSF. Both sample types were incubated with 1 mg/mL NHS-SS-Biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C for 45 min to label cell surface proteins. Unreacted biotin was quenched using a glycine buffer (192 mM glycine, 25 mM Tris, pH 8.3). Tissues and cells were then lysed in NP-40-containing lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) with protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of total protein were incubated with streptavidin-conjugated beads overnight at 4 °C. The beads were washed, and surface proteins were eluted using 2× Laemmli sample buffer, resolved by SDS-PAGE, and subjected to Western blotting using anti-GluA1 antibodies. Total GluA1 in the lysates was analyzed in parallel to assess total protein levels.

Cell treatments

Primary astrocytes (1 × 10⁶ cells/well) and HEK293T cells (1.5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates. Cycloheximide (CHX; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# C7698) was applied at a final concentration of 50 μg/mL, and cells were harvested at 15, 30, 60, 120, and 240 min to assess protein degradation dynamics. To modulate O-GlcNAcylation, thiamet-G (TMG; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# SML0244), an O-GlcNAcase inhibitor, was used at 20 μM for 24 h to enhance O-GlcNAcylation, while OSMI-1 (Selleck Chemicals, Cat# S8439), an OGT inhibitor, was applied at 25 μM for 24 h to reduce O-GlcNAcylation. All treatments were performed in serum-free DMEM, and corresponding vehicle controls were included.

Behavioral assessments

To evaluate depression-like behaviors in mice, three established behavioral tests were conducted: SPT, the TST, and the FST39. The SPT was used to assess anhedonia. Mice were habituated to two bottles containing 1% sucrose solution for 24 h, followed by a 12-h deprivation of food and water. During the test phase, mice were given free access to one bottle of 1% sucrose and one bottle of water for 12 h, and sucrose preference was calculated as the percentage of sucrose intake relative to total liquid consumption. The OFT was used to assess spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior. Each mouse was individually placed in the center of an open-field arena and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The center zone was defined as the central 20 × 20 cm area of the arena. Total distance traveled and time spent in the center zone were recorded and analyzed using an automated video-tracking system. The TST was performed by suspending mice by the tail utilizing adhesive tape attached 1 cm from the tip of the tail, fixed to a horizontal bar 50 cm above the floor. Immobility was recorded over a 6-min session, and the duration of immobility during the final 4 min was analyzed. For the FST, mice were placed individually in transparent cylinders (25 cm in height, 10 cm in diameter) filled with water (23–25 °C, 15 cm depth) for 6 min. The total duration of immobility during the last 4 min was measured. Immobility was defined as the absence of active escape behaviors. All behavioral tests were performed under standardized lighting and noise conditions, and the experimenter was blinded to group allocation.

Immunofluorescent (IF) staining

Hippocampal tissues were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer in a microwave oven. After cooling to room temperature, the nonspecific binding was blocked with goat serum for 1 h. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-NOS (Abcam, Cat# ab52917; 1:250), anti-OGT (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# SAB2702272; 1:500), and anti-GFAP (DakoCytomation, Cat# Z0334; 1:500). The next day, sections were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abcam, Cat# ab150077; 1:1000) and goat anti-mouse IgG2a heavy chain secondary antibody conjugated to APC/Cy7 (Abcam, Cat# ab130788; 1:1000). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min. Fluorescence images were acquired under identical settings for all groups using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Primary astrocytes or neurons were used to evaluate PIN expression following shPIN treatment. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-PIN (Abcam, Cat# ab51603, 1:1000), anti-NOS (Abcam, Cat# ab52917; 1:250), and anti-GFAP (DakoCytomation, Cat# Z0334; 1:500). The following day, cells were washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor®-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000) for 1 h in the dark. DAPI was used to counterstain nuclei. Imaging was performed using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope under consistent settings across all groups.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from hippocampal tissues or cultured cells utilizing radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 89900) supplemented with protease inhibitors, and samples were incubated on ice for 30 min. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane), which were measured with the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23227), were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were all purchased from Abcam and these antibodies include: anti-stargazin (Cat# ab300519, 1:1000), anti-glutamate receptor 1 (GluA1; Cat# ab32351; 1:5000), anti-β-actin (Cat# ab6276; 1:1000), anti-PIN, (Cat# ab51603, 1:1000), anti-NOS (Cat# ab199956; 1:1000), anti-O-GlcNAc (Cat# ab308178; 1:1000) and anti-ubiquitin (Ub, Cat# ab140601; 1:1000). After three washes in TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Cat# 31402; 1:2000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (Merck Millipore, Cat# WBKLS0500) and imaged.

sWGA pull-down assay

Cells or tissue lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 89900) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche, Cat# 11836170001) and phosphatase inhibitors Cocktail-I (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 20-201). After centrifugation, supernatants were denatured in glycoprotein denaturing buffer and digested with PNGase F (P0704S; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and then incubated with sWGA-agarose beads (Vector Laboratories, Cat# AL-1023) at 4 °C overnight with gentle rotation. The beads were washed thoroughly with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling them in the SDS loading buffer. Eluted samples were then subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting using specific antibodies to evaluate O-GlcNAcylation levels. Input controls were processed in parallel to assess total protein levels.

Co-IP assay

Astrocyte or hippocampal tissue samples were processed using a Co-IP assay adapted from published procedures40. Following this, the specimens were lysed using RIP lysis buffer (Merck Millipore, Cat# 20-188). The lysates were then subjected to sonication on ice in immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer, after which they were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. From the resulting supernatant, 30 μL was reserved as the Input control. The remaining 420 μL was incubated overnight at 4 °C with one of the following antibodies: anti-stargazin (Abcam, Cat# ab300519, 1:30), anti-NOS (Abcam, Cat# ab76067, 1:50), anti-PIN (Abcam, Cat# ab51603, 1:30), or a nonspecific IgG control (Abcam, Cat# ab18413, 1:1000). Subsequently, protein A agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 20333) were added and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. After four washes with IP buffer, the samples were centrifuged for 2 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The remaining pellet was resuspended in 30 μL of 2× SDS loading buffer and incubated for 10 min. The prepared samples were then analyzed via immunoblotting.

Biotin-switch assay

S-nitrosylation of proteins was assessed using a biotin-switch assay adapted from published procedures41. Briefly, hippocampal tissue or cultured astrocytes were lysed in HEN buffer supplemented with 1% SDS and protease inhibitors (Roche, Cat# 11836170001). Free thiols were first blocked by adding 20 mM methyl methanethiosulfonate (MMTS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23011) and incubating at 50 °C for 20 min with frequent vortexing. Excess MMTS was removed by acetone precipitation, and the protein pellet was resuspended in HEN buffer containing 1% SDS.

S-nitrosothiols were then selectively reduced by adding 1 mM ascorbate and labeled with 0.25 mM biotin-HPDP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 21341) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. After a second acetone precipitation, proteins were resuspended, and biotinylated proteins were pulled down using streptavidin-agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 20347) overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed extensively, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer containing 50 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). Eluted proteins were then analyzed by Western blot.

Measurement of NOS enzymatic activity

NOS activity was quantified using a commercial Nitric Oxide Synthase Activity Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# A014-2), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue or cell lysates were prepared in the assay buffer supplied with the kit, and total protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Fifty micrograms protein per sample was incubated in the reaction buffer containing L-arginine and essential NOS cofactors, including NADPH, tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄), flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), flavin mononucleotide (FMN), calmodulin, and molecular oxygen. Reactions were carried out at 37 °C for 60 min. The reaction was then terminated by the addition of a stop solution. Nitric oxide production was indirectly assessed by quantifying nitrite via the Griess reagent. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. To determine NOS-specific activity, samples were pre-incubated with selective inhibitors to differentiate NOS-derived activity from that of inducible NOS (iNOS) and endothelial NOS (eNOS). The results were normalized to total protein content.

Bioinformatic prediction of O-GlcNAcylation sites

Potential O-GlcNAcylation sites in the mouse PIN protein sequence were predicted using the YinOYang 1.2 algorithm, available from the ExPASy Bioinformatics Resource Portal (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?YinOYang-1.2).

Ex vivo electrophysiology

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and decapitated. Brains were rapidly removed and placed in ice-cold oxygenated aCSF containing (in mM): 220 sucrose, 2.5 KCl, 1.3 CaCl₂, 2.5 MgSO₄, 1 NaH₂PO₄, 26 NaHCO₃, and 10 glucose (Sigma-Aldrich). Horizontal slices (300 μm) containing the mPFC were prepared using a Leica LS1200S vibrating microtome (Leica Microsystems). Slices were first incubated at 35 °C for 30 min and then maintained at room temperature (~22–24 °C) for at least 1 h before recordings. During recordings, slices were continuously perfused (~3 mL/min) with oxygenated recording aCSF at 35 °C, containing (in mM): 126 NaCl, 26 NaHCO₃, 3.0 KCl, 1.2 NaH₂PO₄, 2.0 CaCl₂, 1.0 MgSO₄, and 10 glucose (Sigma-Aldrich), saturated with 95% O₂ and 5% CO₂. mPFC layer II/III pyramidal neurons were visualized using an upright microscope (Axio Examiner, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with a 40× water immersion objective. Patch electrodes (4–8 MΩ) were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (1.5 mm OD; Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA, USA) using a P-97 puller. The internal pipette solution contained (in mM): 125 cesium methanesulfonate, 5 CsCl, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 1 MgCl₂, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, 10 phosphocreatine, and 5 QX-314 (pH 7.4, 290 mOsm; Sigma-Aldrich). Whole-cell recordings were performed using a HEKA EPC10 amplifier (HEKA Elektronik, Lambrecht, Germany). Signals were filtered at 5 kHz and digitized at 10 kHz. Intrinsic excitability was assessed in current-clamp mode by injecting step currents (500 ms duration) ranging from 0 to 120 pA in 20 pA increments. Action potential amplitude and firing frequency were analyzed offline using Clampfit software (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). mEPSCs were recorded in voltage-clamp mode (V_hold = −70 mV) in the presence of 1 μM tetrodotoxin (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) and 20 μM bicuculline (Sigma-Aldrich) to block sodium currents and GABA_A receptor-mediated inhibitory currents, respectively. Events were detected and analyzed using Mini Analysis software (Synaptosoft Inc., Decatur, GA, USA). For each neuron, a stable 5-min segment was used to calculate mean mEPSC frequency and amplitude. Only recordings with stable series resistance and holding current were included. A total of 13–23 neurons from 4 animals per group were analyzed.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons between the two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests were used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied, as appropriate. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact p-values are provided where possible. Sample sizes for both in vitro and in vivo experiments were determined based on prior studies and preliminary data using power analysis to achieve ≥80% power at α = 0.05. Biological replicates were defined as independent cell preparations, independent tissue samples, or individual animals, as appropriate. Technical replicates were not treated as biological replicates. In vitro experiments were performed with at least three biologically independent samples or independent experiments, and in vivo experiments were performed with eight biologically independent animals. For electrophysiological recordings, 13–23 neurons from four biologically independent animals per group were analyzed, as indicated in the figure legends. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using computer-generated random numbers. All behavioral testing, image acquisition, and data quantification were performed by investigators blinded to treatment conditions. Exact n values are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Key biochemical, imaging, behavioral, and electrophysiological experiments were independently repeated with similar results.
