Developing aluminum molecular plating for neutron-induced reaction target fabrication
Authors: Scott D Essenmacher, Sean A Kuvin, Hye Young Lee, Khachatur Manukyan, Veronika Mocko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAl is an important radionuclide for research on stellar nucleosynthesis in the Galaxy and the environment of the early solar system. Computational simulations involving the production and destruction of Al in stellar nucleosynthesis and supernovae rely on accurate nuclear reaction network data. Some of the destruction channels via neutron-induced reactions for Al either have no measured data or discrepancies in the measured data. The Low Energy (n,Z) instrument at the Los Alamos National Laboratory can perform these measurements on neutron-induced charged-particle reactions; however, a thin and uniform target is required. Such a target can be manufactured via molecular plating, but this process needs to be optimized. This study was able to determine a procedure that allows for the production of an Al target on a 1 m thick Ti foil with a >70 yield. Adding a small amount of Ce in the initial plating solution and increasing the drying time resulted in a thin, uniform, and physically stable Al target.Subject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Aluminum (Al) is a naturally monoisotopic element with many short-lived radioisotopes (T 6.56 minutes), and a single long-lived radioisotope, aluminum-26 (Al), which has a half-life of 717,000 years1. From a galactic perspective, this is a relatively short half-life and indicates that it is actively being produced in the galaxy, but the half-life is still long enough that it can be used as an observable for stellar nucleosynthesis in the Galaxy and the environment of the early solar system. By combining measurements of Al found in the environment on Earth, in meteors, and in asteroids with various nuclear data, including those on the nuclear reactions involved in the production and destruction of Al, computational models can be constructed and used to provide insight into the processes of mixing in stars and supernovae along with the transport of radionuclides in interstellar medium2. These models are heavily dependent on the accuracy of the nuclear data, as inaccuracies in these data could cause the results of these models to deviate from the observed yields for the nuclei being used as observables.

One such example where inaccuracies in nuclear data could lead to disagreement between theoretical models and actual observations is predictive yields of core collapse supernovae tend to show more iron-60 with respect to Al than has actually been measured3–5. Al is produced primarily through the (p,)Mg reaction and consumed through (n,), (n,p), and (n,) reactions in Wolf-Rayet, massive stars, and the supernovae of such stars2,6,7. Data from studies in literature on measurements of the destruction channel reactions do not agree with each other. Furthermore, these measurements have only been performed in normal kinematics with up to 150 keV neutron beams and high quality data are needed for incident neutron energies up to at least 500 keV to confidently constrain reaction rates up to 1-3 GK2,7.

The Low Energy (n,Z) (LENZ) instrument was developed at the Los Alamos Neutron Science CEnter (LANSCE) to allow for neutron-induced charged particle reaction cross-sections to be measured with high accuracy. This instrument has the capability to measure such reactions with radioactive targets and is modular, which allows for direct measurements of (n,p) and (n,) for neutron energies ranging from thermal to about 14 MeV8 via a time-of-flight (TOF) method. Such work has already been completed with measurements on stable targets9–11 as well as the astrophysically important titanium-4412, potassium-4013, and nickel-5914 radionuclides. It is essential that thin, uniform, and well-characterized targets are used for these measurements to obtain high accuracy data15,16.

One of the first Al targets made for experiments to utilize a TOF method to determine the Al(n,p) and Al(n,) cross-sections was fabricated via vacuum evaporation of a carbon-aluminum-carbon sandwich onto a 76 m thick copper foil17. The thick copper backing resulted in a large background and needed to be etched off and replaced with a 25 m niobium foil, which allowed for data to be collected with reasonable uncertainties18. Improvements upon these measurements were made both at the Geel Linear Accelerator19,20 and by the neutron TOF collaboration at the European Organization for Nuclear Research21,22 with a molecular plated Al target. This target was manufactured on a 7 m thick nickel (Ni) foil at the European Commission Joint Research Center in Geel, Belgium23, but the Ni is too thick for high-precision measurements to be made with the LENZ instrument at LANSCE. Also, stable Ni is not monoisotopic and has large proton and alpha production cross sections24, both of which have led to Ni not being used as a backing for targets with the LENZ instrument. Fortunately, the target manufacturing technique utilized, molecular plating, is a well-established; albeit, not well-understood, technique for making thin and uniform alpha sources and targets with high-yields for nuclear physics experiments25.

Performing molecular plating at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) usually comes with additional challenges; especially when large quantities of relatively short-lived radionuclides, such as lutetium-17326, are involved. This is because the fabrication may need to be conducted in a hot cell with remote manipulators where space is limited and various other work, including processing for isotope production27 and other types of target manufacturing28, is performed. Although Al has a long enough half-live that a hot cell is not needed, being able to fabricate a high-quality target solely using methods that could be utilized in a hot cell is more important for future targetry needs at LANL.

Preliminary work on fabricating a new Al target (with stable Al as a surrogate material for Al) via molecular plating followed the procedure described by Ingelbrecht et al.23 and used various foil backings, including gold (Au), tantalum (Ta), Ta foil anodized following the procedure described in Lee et al.10, and titanium (Ti). Each trial resulted in plated Al films that adhered poorly to the backings as they all began peeling and flaking shortly upon the completion of the plating process. This study sought to determine how to overcome this adhesion issue in a manner that could be utilized in a hot cell and to optimize this technique for producing a thin and uniform (up to several hundred g/cm) non-radioactive Al target on a 1 m thick Ti foil. The optimized procedure has already been used to fabricate a high quality Al target for (n,Z) measurements at LANSCE. This Al target fabrication and characterization effort will be discussed in a future manuscript. The result of the work described herein was a refined target fabrication procedure that produces a thin, well-adhering, and uniform Al target with a reasonably high yield.

Results and discussion

Improving adhesion

Preliminary work with Al being the only metal ion present in the plating solution resulted in Al films that began flaking and peeling off a variety of substrates immediately after removing the solution from the cell. To improve adhesion, attempts were made to co-molecular plate Al with a small amount of cerium (Ce). Ce was selected because small quantities of Ce are known to increase the electrical conductivity of Al alloys29. With better electrical conductivity, the plated Al film could be less susceptible to electrostatic charges. In this case, including small quantities of Ce (2.6 wt. with respect to the total of Al and Ce) in the initial plating solution did seem to result in a deposited film that adhered well to Ti foil backings. If the molecular plating solution initially had less Ce (2.2 or 1.5 wt.), then the deposited film would flake and peel once the film began drying post-plating. This flaking and peeling increased as the initial Ce concentration was decreased.

It is difficult to discern why poor adhesion was observed unless a small amount of Ce is added. Many factors play a role in adhesion between the foil backing and the deposited film, including the chemical compatibility, crystal structure, and surface morphology of both layers along with environmental factors, such as temperature30,31. Nonetheless, the results from these tests suggested as long as the Ce content in the initial plating solution was 2.6 wt. with respect to the sum of Al and Ce, then the deposited film would adhere well to the Ti foil backing. For the optimization part of this work, the initial Ce composition with respect to the total of Al and Ce was set to approximately 3.3 wt..

Optimizing fabrication procedure

Current density

Molecular plating has been described as a process that allows for thin films to be deposited via electrodeposition from an organic solution32. The underlying process, as noted in numerous studies33–35, does not result in the oxidation or reduction of the dissolved metal ion species to a compound that precipitates onto the surface. It instead appears to be similar to non-aqueous electrophoretic deposition36 as both processes require the application of a large electrostatic potential gradient to drive ions, compounds, and particles to an electrode surface. Interestingly, the exact mechanisms that are behind both of these processes remain unknown25,37. For molecular plating, these mechanisms seem to be fairly complex as the plated films contain numerous species in addition to metal oxides and hydroxides38–43. Furthermore, the magnitude of the applied potential can affect the quality, and possibly the yield, of the resulting film as higher current densities can increase the gas production from the hydrogen evolution reaction at the cathode surface43.

As part of the optimization process in this study, the current density was varied while plating for 1.25 hours and the yields were compared. Table 1 shows these results, while Fig. 1 shows the resulting deposit for the trials that had good adhesion. The films produced during the other trials exhibited poor adhesion, but this appears to be reliant on how the initial solution was prepared (note: those with poor adhesion had the stock Al and Ce solutions evaporated and reconstituted in 50 L of 1 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) prior to adding the 4.950 mL of isopropanol, while the others were prepared by directly mixing the stock Al and Ce in 1 M HCl solutions with isopropanol). Overall, increasing current density led to increasing yields, but flaking was sometimes observed at the edges of the film deposited with an applied current density of 35 mA/cm during some trials. To ensure the best results, the optimum current density was chosen to be 24 mA/cm.

Drying targets

Another challenge with target fabrication was the observation of “mudcracking” on the edges of the deposited films. Vascon et al.41 demonstrated that this is the result of rapid evaporation of the molecular plating solvent after plating. Attempting to minimize the effects of this phenomenon by placing samples in an isopropanol saturated chamber overnight post-plating did significantly reduce the mudcracking on the edges of plated films (see Fig. 2). Performing scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on the cross-section of these films (see Fig. 3) shows that drying the film in the isopropanol saturated chamber reduces the porosity of the plated film, which leads to a reduction in the film thickness (see Table 2). These results suggest this step is a crucial step in the plating process, as the improved film quality and reduced thickness will minimize the uncertainty in measurements of neutron-induced reactions with the LENZ instrument15.

Manufacturing yields and target characteristics

Along with the determination of the drying process and optimal current density, the last major step to develop in the plating procedure was the plating time. To help determine this, the voltage and plating yields were monitored at various times throughout a 5 hour plating at a constant current density of 24 mA/cm. During this monitoring, the voltage continued to rise as the concentration of metal ions in the solution decreased (see Fig. S.1 in the Supplementary Information). The plating yields were calculated from measurements of the metal ion concentration in the solution before and at various times during the plating process. These yields increased logarithmically over time (see Fig. 4). By subtracting the yields from 100, the yield is then reported as the amount of the metal ion remaining in the solution. This plot would show the metal ion concentration decreases exponentially with plating time, much like what Vascon et al.42 observed when molecular plating neodymium (Nd). When attempting to plot the integrated forms of some reaction rate laws as a function of time (see Fig. S.2 in the Supplementary Information), a linear relationship could only be obtained when plotting [Al] against plating time, where [Al] is the concentration of Al in the plating solution. This suggests the overall plating reaction for Al is a third-order reaction and does not follow first-order kinetics as observed by Vascon et al.42 for Nd. It is possible that the inclusion of Ce caused first-order kinetics to be no longer observed, but it is difficult to pinpoint why this reaction proceeds via third-order kinetics because the reaction mechanisms involved in molecular plating seem to be very complex25.

Molecular plating yields for Al and Ce with respect to plating time. For a 5 hour plating time, the Al yield was 75.8(7), while the Ce yield was 69(3). Uncertainties in brackets are 1 standard deviations calculated from experiments performed in quadruplicate for 5 hour plating time, triplicate for 1.25 hour and 5 minute plating time, and duplicate for 30 minute plating time.

By analyzing the surfaces of samples acquired at different points of the plating process, more information can be gathered to potentially better understand the reaction mechanism behind molecular plating with these Al/Ce solutions. From SEM images of regions approximately 0.5 mm from the edges of the Al/Ce deposits (see Fig. 5), the cracking observed in the films appears to decrease as the plating time increased with the cracking decreasing significantly between 5 and 30 minutes of plating time and less so afterwards. It is possible that the significant mudcracking observed for the film produced after 5 minutes of plating was the result of rapid evaporation of the plating solvent through the very thin film shortly after plating and removing the plating solution from the cell. The electron dispersion spectroscopy (EDS) results (see Table 3) for each sample suggest that films produced after 30 minutes and 5 hours are similar in composition. The film produced after 5 minutes of plating appears to have a higher oxygen (O) composition, but it is very likely that most of the O was from the oxide layer on the Ti foil backing surface. The composition of Al also is much lower than what was observed in the films from the longer plating times; however, the composition of chlorine (Cl) and Ce do not seem to change relative to their compositions in the other films. Essenmacher et al.15 performed X-ray fluorescence analysis of an Al/Ce target fabricated from an initial solution produced with Ce as CeO instead of CeCl and found that Cl appears only in the deposited film area. So, detection of Cl in both the EDS and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra comes from the deposited film and not the Ti foil backing. Furthermore, the Al and Cl detected in the Al/Ce layer appeared to be qualitatively uniformly distributed in the film, which is the expected result for molecular plated films15,25. Because the composition of these films does not appear to change between 30 minutes and 5 hours of plating, it is very likely that the plating rate decreases primarily due to the process becoming limited by mass transfer of the ions toward the foil surface. A plating time of 5 hours was determined to be optimal for the target fabrication as the Al yield of 75.8(7) would provide a sufficient amount of Al for future neutron-induced reaction studies with the LENZ instrument.

By examining the final deposit after 5 hours of plating via XPS (see Fig. S.3 in the Supplementary Information for one of the full XPS spectra), the chemical makeup can be discerned. In this case, the Al to O atomic ratio suggests the deposit contains primarily aluminum hydroxide as Al(OH)44,45, but the position of the O 1s peak (about 531.2 keV) suggests the layer contains a mixture of oxides and hydroxides. Furthermore, it is difficult to discern aluminum oxide (AlO) from Al(OH) using the Al 2p peak position as there is a significant overlap between them46. Also, the deposit appears to contain amorphous material because the peaks in the acquired XPS spectra are broader than they would be when using a monochromatic source on crystalline material47. Two peaks at about 884.9 and 903 keV are for emissions from Ce 3d and Ce 3d, respectively, and are likely associated with Ce because no peak was observed around 915 keV, which would suggest species containing Ce were present48–53. This Ce appears to primarily be a combination of cerium(III) oxide (CeO) and cerium(III) hydroxide (Ce(OH)) with none to a negligible amount chlorinated Ce species as there does not appear to be a shifted Cl 2p peak around 207 keV that would correspond to a cerium oxychloride compound54. Although, this does not necessarily mean there is no cerium oxychloride compound present in the plated layer as this peak may not be observed if the quantity is relatively small55. The position of the Cl 2p peak of around 199.1 keV suggests the Cl ions are present at or near the surface of the plated layer56, but these do not seem to be bonded to either Ce or Al because of the aforementioned reason (for Ce) and the Al 1s peak being at 118.9 keV, which corresponds with AlO material while this peak would be shifted to 119.8 keV for AlCl57. These XPS results suggest the deposited layer is amorphous and comprises of primarily AlO and Al(OH) with some impurities from the plating solution (e.g. Cl ions). All of which is in agreement in general with past observations on the composition of molecular plated films38–43. The presence of stable carbon, aluminum, oxygen, chlorine, or cerium impurities at the quantities observed will not significantly affect future measurements of radioactive Al at astrophysically relevant neutron energies.

Conclusion

This study focused on determining the optimal molecular plating procedure for fabricating a thin and uniform Al target for neutron-induced charged particle reaction measurements at LANSCE. This involved determining the ideal initial plating solution composition when using isopropanol as the solvent, the optimal current density, plating time, and drying conditions for plated films. The optimized procedure involved using an initial plating solution composed of at least 2.6 wt. Ce with respect to the sum of Al and Ce, applying a 24 mA/cm current density for 5 hours, and drying the final target overnight in an isopropanol-saturated chamber immediately after the completion of the plating process. The addition of a small amount of Ce resulted in the minimization of the deposited layer flaking and peeling; thus, a stable Al layer on a 1 m thick Ti backing. Ultimately, the final optimized procedure allowed for the fabrication of a thin and uniform Al target with an Al yield of 75.8(7).

Methods

Chemicals

The following chemicals and materials, except the foils, were used as received and prepared using an analytical balance where necessary (OHAUS analytical balance). All foils, the electrochemical cell, and the counter electrode were cleaned prior to plating by rinsing the surface with deionized water and acetone, being sure to allow the foils to dry after each rinse, followed by soaking for approximately 5 minutes in approximately 5 mL of isopropanol prior to plating. ASTM Type I deionized water was obtained from LabChem. Anhydrous aluminum chloride (99.0 purity) was procured from Honeywell Fluka, while anhydrous cerium(III) chloride (99.9 purity) was acquired from Alfa Aesar. Optima grade hydrochloric acid and HPLC grade isopropanol (99.0 purity) were obtained from Fisher Scientific. HPLC grade acetone (99.0 purity) was acquired from MilliporeSigma. A Pt wire (1.0 mm diameter x 10 cm long, 99.995 purity) obtained from VWR International was used as the counter electrode for all electrodepositions. Finally, 1 and 12.5 m thick Ti foils with 99.99 and 99.6 purity, respectively, 3 m thick Au foil with 99.9 purity, and 30 m thick Ta foil with 99.9 purity were all procured from Goodfellow.

Molecular plating

Molecular plating was performed using a custom designed electrochemical cell. The version of the cell utilized for this study allowed for the foil backing to rest on top of a copper plate, which the negative lead was attached to, and was held down by the glass container for the solution being clamped down on top of a silicone gasket. The plating area for this cell covered a 6 mm diameter circle. Platings were performed for various current densities with a PS310 power supply from Keithley and the Pt wire counter electrode being suspended in the plating solution at a distance of approximately 5 mm from the plating area on the foil. The plating solution was prepared by adding 50 L of a stock 936(9) ppm Al in 1 M HCl solution and 65 L of a stock 24.7(23) ppm Ce in 1 M HCl solution to 4.885 mL of isopropanol. For determining the amount of Ce to add to the initial solution, the amount of Ce solution added was varied from 29 to 59 L and the plating time was 5 hours. Upon completion of the plating, the plating solution was removed from the cell and the cell was disassembled. After the foil had been removed from the cell, a few drops of isopropanol (200 L) were added directly on top of the deposited film and the entire foil was placed in an isopropanol saturated chamber to dry. This chamber comprised of a 125 mL Nalgene jar container placed upside-down on top of a 90 mm diameter glass petri dish, and had a vial cap with 750 L of isopropanol placed inside the Nalgene jar container. If the foil was not being placed inside this chamber, then it was allowed to dry in the ambient air for about 15 minutes.

Microscopic and spectroscopic measurements

To determine the plating yields, the final plating solution was allowed to evaporate to dryness after being removed from the electrochemical cell. The residue was reconstituted in 10 mL of 1 M HCl. This solution was then analyzed via inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) using a Perkin-Elmer Avio 500 ICP-OES instrument. The calibration was performed using Al (1000 ppm, SPEX CertiPrep, ZeptoMetrix) and Ce (1000 ppm, SPEX CertiPrep, ZeptoMetrix) ICP standards. The Al ICP standard was diluted to 5, 1, 0.5, 0.1, and 0.05 ppm with 1 M HCl, while Ce ICP standard was included in the same samples, but as 0.5, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01, and 0.005 ppm. The wavelengths used for measuring the Al concentration were 237.313 nm, 308.215 nm, 394.401 nm, and 396.153 nm, while those used for measuring Ce were 413.380 nm, 413.764 nm, and 418.660 nm.

The Al/Ce deposited layers were characterized using a variety of techniques. Optical and laser confocal microscopy were performed using a Keyence VK-X3100 laser microscope with lens of either 2.5x, 5x, or 50x magnification, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using a Helios NanoLab 600 (ThermoFisher Scientific) microscope with either a 3 kV or 5 kV beam and magnifications of 1,000x (for surface images), and 35,000x or 40,000x (for cross-sectional images), and electron dispersion spectroscopy (EDS) was perform using a silicon drift detector (energy resolution of 130 eV at 5.9 keV) as part of an electron microscope analyzer (Bruker). Prior to performing any measurements with the SEM, samples were sputtered with iridium (Ir) to lower the surface charging effects. Before performing cross-sectional measurements, samples were sputtered with Pt and then milled with a focused ion beam. When measuring the deposited layer thickness, only one spot near the center of a deposit was measured via SEM, while two regions of either 215 or 286 m in width and having lengths that span the whole 6 mm diameter of the deposit were analyzed via laser confocal microscopy. To analyze laser confocal microscopy data, measurements on Al/Ce films were acquired over wavelengths ranging from 300 to 600 nm with a J. A. Woollam variable angle spectroscopic ellipsometer (VASE). The index of refraction at 404 nm was extracted from the fit of the experimental data and used to correct the laser confocal microscopy data.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on two of the samples plated for 5 hours on a 12.5 m thick Ti foil and, due to the high Ir and Pt content, needed to have the top layer sputtered off prior to obtaining an XPS spectrum with a Physical Electronics VersaProbe II spectrometer and a monochromatic 1486.6 eV Al K X-ray source. The spectrometer was operated with a base pressure of < 1 10 Pa, calibrated in accordance with American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) procedure with all spectra being calibrated to the accepted value for the Ir 4f peak for pure Ir metal of 60.9 eV58, and utilized a low energy electron flood gun at 1.0 V and 0.2 A for charge neutralization. Spectra with visible C 1s peaks had the C 1s peak position checked after calibrating using the Ir 4f peak and the position was found to be 284.8 eV. Elemental composition was determined using survey scans at a pass energy of 187.80 eV, and high-resolution scans were performed using a pass energy of 23.5 eV for chemical valance state determination.

Uncertainty determination

Uncertainties in the ICP-OES measurements were determined by taking the standard deviation of measurements performed at different wavelengths for each element. These errors were then propagated to the uncertainties of the deposition yields. Stochastic uncertainties of EDS and XPS measurements were also propagated to the uncertainties of the averages of the measurements and sample replicates. For film thickness measurements determined via SEM, the uncertainties were standard deviations of 100 and 149 measurements for targets fabricated by having films dried rapidly and slowly, respectively. For film thickness measurements performed with laser confocal microscopy, the stochastic errors were propagated to the uncertainties of the weighted average thicknesses.
