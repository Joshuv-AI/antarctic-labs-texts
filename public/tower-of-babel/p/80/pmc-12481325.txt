High Treg and PMN-MDSC densities are a hallmark of tertiary lymphoid structures in fatal cases of cervical cancer
Authors: Linn A Syding, Klára Plačková, Lucie Pavelková, Cecilia Aquino-Perez, Saskia J Santegoets, Jan Laco, Marek Grega, Pavel Dundr, Kristýna Němejcová, Miroslav Hodek, Jan Bouček, Michal Zábrodský, Hana Vošmiková, Michael J Halaška, Lukáš Rob, Petr Čelakovský, Viktor Chrobok, Munachiso Ndukwe Iheme, Ivan Práznovec, Milan Vošmik, Stanislav Katina, David Cibula, Aleš Ryška, Sjoerd H van der Burg, Radek Špíšek, Anna Fialová
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh densities of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are associated with improved clinical outcomes in various malignancies, including human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). However, the role of TLSs in shaping antitumor immunity in HPV-induced cervical cancer (CESC) remains unclear. Therefore, we analyzed the density, composition, and prognostic impact of TLSs in patients with CESC as well as patients with HNSCC.MethodsMultiplex immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry, and spatial transcriptomics were used to analyze TLS density and composition in HNSCC and CESC tissue sections with respect to patient prognosis. The spatial approach was supplemented by flow cytometry-based analysis of the polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cell (PMN-MDSC) phenotype in freshly resected primary tumor tissues.ResultsAlthough both indications were associated with HPV infection, we confirmed a positive correlation between TLS density and improved overall survival only in patients with HNSCC. The TLS composition differed markedly between HNSCC and CESC samples, with a shift toward high regulatory T cell (Treg) and PMN-MDSC abundance in CESC samples. The highest Treg and PMN-MDSC levels were observed in patients with CESC who died of the disease. CESC-infiltrating PMN-MDSCs showed high arginase 1 expression, which correlated with diminished T-cell receptor (TCR)ζ chain expression in CESC-infiltrating T cells. Additionally, the high number of PMN-MDSCs in TLSs was associated with the absence of HPV-specific T cells in CESC.ConclusionsUnlike in HNSCC, the composition of TLSs, rather than their quantity, was associated with the overall survival of patients with CESC. High numbers of Tregs and PMN-MDSCs infiltrating immature TLSs prevail in patients with CESC who succumbed to the disease and seem to affect tumor-specific immune responses.Keywords: Cervical Cancer, Head and Neck Cancer, Myeloid-derived suppressor cell - MDSC, T regulatory cell - Treg

Background

High tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte densities have been associated with good patient response to both standard-of-care protocols and immunotherapy for various solid tumors,1,3 including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC).4,7 Although immune cell densities, proportions, and distributions vary greatly among indications and individual patients, organized tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation has been described in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of most cancer types.8

High intratumoral TLS densities generally correlate with improved clinical outcomes in different malignancies, including HNSCC.7 9 Moreover, TLS presence is a predictive biomarker of immune checkpoint blockade efficacy in patients with melanoma, soft tissue sarcoma, and renal cell carcinoma.1,3 However, little is known about the composition and prognostic impact of TLSs on cervical cancer (CESC). Although both CESC and HNSCC may be caused by the same etiologic agent, human papillomavirus (HPV), their immune landscape is distinct.10 11

Depending on their cellular composition and organization level, TLSs are observed in different maturation and activity states.12 13 Early TLSs are characterized by poorly organized dense T and B lymphocyte aggregates. Primary B cell follicles and T cell zones are distinguished in immature TLSs. Mature TLSs comprise a secondary lymphoid follicle with a germinal center and a well-defined T cell zone. Follicular dendritic cells (fDCs) expressing CD21 and CD23, PNAd+ endothelial venules, and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells are typical markers for mature TLSs. Together with conventional lymphocytes and dendritic cells (DCs), TLSs may include macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), regulatory B cells, and T follicular regulatory cells (Tfr), all of which affect TLS functionality.814,16

In this study, we confirmed the association between high TLS densities and improved overall survival (OS) in patients with HPV-associated HNSCC, but not with CESC. The TLS composition differed markedly between HNSCC and CESC samples, with an abundance of Tregs and polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) in TLSs from CESC tissues. High densities of regulatory cells infiltrating the TLSs were mainly observed in patients who succumbed to the disease, indicating diminished TLS functionality, but not numbers, in patients with high-risk CESC. Moreover, CESC-infiltrating PMN-MDSC showed high expression of arginase 1 (Arg1), which correlated with diminished T cell receptor (TCR)ζ expression on CESC-infiltrating T cells. Interestingly, high TLS density was associated with the presence of HPV-specific T cells in the TME of patients with HNSCC, but not with CESC. However, no fully mature TLSs were present in patients with CESCs lacking detectable HPV-specific T cells in the TME. Moreover, immature TLSs in these patients were infiltrated with high numbers of PMN-MDSCs, indicating an important impact of PMN-MDSCs on the functional capacity and/or maturation of TLSs.

Methods

Patients and samples

Cohort 1

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) primary HPV+ HNSCC specimens were obtained from 62 patients who underwent radical surgery at University Hospital Hradec Králové, Czech Republic, between 2001 and 2014. All samples were characterized as p16+ squamous cell carcinomas of the oropharynx.

Cohort 2

FFPE primary HPV+ CESC specimens were obtained from 71 patients who underwent radical surgery at the University Hospital Hradec Králové, Czech Republic, between 2010 and 2020 (Cohort 2a) and from 44 patients who underwent radical surgery at the General University Hospital in Prague, Czech Republic, between 2013 and 2020 (Cohort 2b). All samples were characterized as squamous cell carcinomas of the uterine exocervix. Cohorts 2a and 2b were used to calculate overall and progression-free survival. Cohort 2a was used for the in-depth TLS analysis. A comparison of Cohorts 1 and 2 is summarized in online supplemental table S1. Data on long-term clinical outcomes were retrospectively obtained from the municipal registers.

Cohort 3

Fresh primary HPV+ HNSCC specimens, characterized as p16+ squamous cell carcinomas of the tonsil, were obtained from eight patients immediately after therapeutic surgery at the University Hospital Motol in Prague, Czech Republic, between 2023 and 2024. Fresh primary CESC specimens were obtained as punch biopsies from 11 patients at the University Hospital Kralovske Vinohrady in Prague, Czech Republic, between 2023 and 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients before their inclusion in the study. Cohort 3 was used for the flow cytometric analysis.

Cohort 4

Fresh primary HNSCC tissues and matching FFPE tumor sections, characterized as p16+ squamous cell carcinomas of the oropharynx, were obtained from 20 patients who underwent therapeutic surgery at the University Hospital Motol in Prague, Czech Republic, between August 2015 and May 2016. HPV-specific T cells were previously detected in this cohort.17

Cohort 5

FFPE primary HPV+ CESC specimens were obtained from 24 patients who participated in the CIRCLE study, a large observational study investigating cellular immunity against anogenital lesions at the Department of Gynecology of the Leiden University Medical Center.18 19

None of the enrolled patients in this study had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. An experienced pathologist reviewed and classified the pathological staging of HNSCC and CESC according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer and the 2018 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging classification, respectively. All procedures performed in this study adhered to the ethical standards of the Institutional and National Research Committees and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

HPV detection

Immunohistochemical analysis in HNSCC samples

Antibodies against p16INK4a (Purified Mouse Anti-Human p16, Clone G175-405, BD Pharmingen, dilution 1:100) and the CINtec Histology Kit (Roche) were used. The staining intensity and proportion of stained cells were evaluated. Samples positive for p16 expression showed more than 70% positive cells and revealed nuclear and/or cytoplasmic staining.

PCR

HPV DNA was extracted from paraffin-embedded tissues using a MagCore Genomic DNA FFPE One-Step Kit (RBC Bioscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. HPV DNA was detected and genotyped by qualitative real-time PCR using the AmoyDx Human Papillomavirus Genotyping Detection Kit (Amoy Diagnostics). The test was designed for the specific amplification of the L1 gene in HPV DNA to detect and genotype 19 high-risk and 2 low-risk HPV strains (HPV 6 and 11). The sensitivity of the test is 100 HPV DNA copies per reaction. An internal control was used in the assay to test the sample quality and the presence of inhibiting factors.

Immunohistochemistry

FFPE sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with BLOXALL (Vector Laboratories) and unspecific binding was blocked by 2.5% horse serum (Vector Laboratories). The sections were stained with primary antibodies against CD20 (1:400, MO755, Agilent), CD23 (1:50, 16 702 Abcam) PNAd (1:100, 120801, BioLegend), and Ki67 (1:100, Ab16667, Abcam), followed by the manifestation of enzymatic activity and staining with hematoxylin or Nuclear Fast Red (Vector Laboratories). Images were acquired using a PhenoImager scanner (Akoya).

Immunofluorescence

Deparaffinized 2 µm FFPE sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and subsequently stained with Treg FixVUE Panel (Ultivue), where Tregs were defined as CD4+FOXP3+ and conventional T helper cells as CD4+FOXP3−. A custom 8-plex panel (Ultivue) containing antibodies against CD4, CD8, PANCK, PD-1, PD-L1, CD68, GZMB, and CD20 was used to detect additional immune cells. Cytotoxic T cells were defined as CD8+GZMB+ and macrophages were defined as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)+ or PD-L1− CD68+. PANCK was used to differentiate between the tumor stroma and epithelium. Finally, PMN-MDSCs were visualized using antibodies against LOX1 (1:150; ab126538; Abcam) and CD15 (1:200; 555400; BD Biosciences). Images were captured using a PhenoImager scanner (Akoya).

Immune cell quantification and scoring of tertiary lymphoid structures

The stained slides were stripped and re-stained with CD20 and CD23 to identify the TLSs. Following image acquisition, the slides were analyzed using HALO software (Indica Labs). Aggregates were defined as clusters of more than 20 CD20+ B lymphocytes. Structured B-cell follicles without a germinal center, with no or weak CD23 positivity on B cells only, were considered primary follicles (PF+ TLSs). Mature follicles with germinal centers (GC+ TLSs) were characterized as structures with visible germinal centers that exhibited high CD23 positivity in DC-like cells (fDCs). The area covering the B-cell follicle and an additional 100 µm surrounding was used to quantify PF+ and GC+ TLSs. The presence of T cells in the TLSs was confirmed by immunofluorescence (IF) scans of the tissues (figure 3B).

Tumor tissue processing

Fresh tissues were processed into single-cell suspensions via enzymatic and mechanical dissociation. Samples were cut into fine pieces and digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase D (Roche) and 0.05 mg/mL DNase I (Roche) in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37°C under a gentle rocking motion. The samples were further processed by passing through a 100 µm nylon cell strainer (BD Biosciences) and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Lonza). Single-cell suspensions were immediately stained for flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry

Freshly isolated single-cell suspensions were blocked with Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend), and stained in PBS+brilliant stain buffer (BD Horizon) using a panel of fluorescently labeled primary antibodies and a viability marker (online supplemental table S2). For intracellular detection of Arg1 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), cells were fixed and permeabilized with a Fixation/Permeabilization Buffer Set (eBioscience). The cells were analyzed using a Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry data were evaluated using the FlowJo software (TreeStar).

Suppression assay

Neutrophils were isolated from whole blood using the EasySep Direct Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). Isolated neutrophils were labeled with anti-Lox1-PE mAb (BD Biosciences), and Lox1+ PMN-MDSCs were separated using the EasySep PE Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL Technologies). Concurrently, T cells were isolated from the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) of the same patient as PMN-MDSCs using the EasySep Human T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). T cells were labeled with the CellTrace Violet Cell Proliferation Kit (Invitrogen), and 3×105 T cells/well were seeded with different ratios of PMN-MDSCs in a 96-well plate coated with 3 µg/mL anti-CD3, followed by the addition of 1 µg/mL of soluble anti-CD28 (both Miltenyi Biotech). After 4 days of culture, supernatants were collected and stored at −20°C, and cells were labeled with anti-CD3 antibody. T-cell proliferation was analyzed using a Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were evaluated using the Proliferation Modeling tool in FlowJo software (TreeStar).

Interferon-γ detection

Cell culture supernatants were harvested after 4 days of T cells and PMN-MDSCs co-cultivation and stored at −20°C until used. Interferon (IFN)-γ was detected in the cell culture supernatants diluted 1:3 to 1:9 using a human IFN-γ ELISA kit (Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Detection of HPV-specific T cells

The detection of HPV16 E6/E7-specific T cells in HNSCC was performed as previously described.17 In brief, freshly prepared tumor-derived single-cell suspensions were seeded at a concentration of 3×105 cells/mL into a 24-well plate, and T cells were expanded for 2 weeks in the presence of interleukin (IL)-2 (Proleukin, Prometheus Laboratories). Monocytes from autologous PBMCs were isolated using the Human CD14 Positive Selection Kit (STEMCELL Technologies), loaded with HPV16 E6/E7 peptide pools (5 µg/mL) (JPT), and added to expanded T cells at a ratio of 1:10. After 6 hours of incubation in the presence of Brefeldin A (BioLegend), the cells were stained with antibodies for the intracellular detection of IFN-γ.

The detection of HPV16/18 E6/E7-specific T cells in CESC was performed as previously described.18 In brief, tumor-derived T cells were expanded for 2–3 weeks in the presence of IL-15 and IL-7 (PeproTech). T cells were stimulated with autologous monocytes or irradiated autologous EBV-transformed B cell lines (B-LCL) pulsed with HPV16 or HPV18 E6/E7 peptides (5 µg/mL) in a 3-day proliferation assay. Additionally, B-LCL were pulsed with 5 µg/mL of HPV16 or HPV18 E6/E7 peptide pools and added to expanded T cells at a ratio of 1:5. After overnight incubation in the presence of Brefeldin A (BD Pharmingen), the cells were stained with antibodies for the detection of CD154, CD137, IFN-γ, and IL-2.

Xenium in situ

For the Xenium analysis, 5 µm FFPE sections of two CESC and two HNSCC samples were placed on Xenium slides. Following deparaffinization and permeabilization, messenger RNA was targeted using a pre-designed Human Immuno-Oncology panel (380 genes) and an add-on custom panel (37 genes, listed in online supplemental table S3) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, probe hybridization was performed at 50°C overnight. The slides were then washed to remove the unhybridized probes. The hybridized probes were ligated at 37°C for 2 hours. After ligation, amplification was performed at 30°C for 2 hours. The slides were washed and incubated with Xenium Block and Stain Buffer for 1 hour. After blocking, the slides were incubated overnight with Xenium Multi-Tissue Stain Mix, followed by staining enhancement and autofluorescence quenching. Nuclei were stained using Xenium Nuclei Staining Buffer, and the slides were subsequently analyzed using a Xenium Analyzer. After analysis, serial ethanol washes were performed, and slides were stored in 30% glycerol at −20°C. The slides were re-stained with hematoxylin.

Xenium in situ data analysis

Raw image processing, segmentation, and raw count matrix generation were performed using onboard pipelines on a Xenium machine. The resulting matrices were read into a SpatialFeatureExperiment object (V.1.8.4) and processed using the Voyager package (V.1.8.1). Applied cell filtering criteria were >10% negative probe presence, <20 captured transcripts, and a minimum of five neighboring cells within 20 µm. Additional filtering was performed on the low nucleus-to-cell area distribution to remove out-of-focus cells. After filtration, the cells were log-normalized using the size factor of the cell area. AUCell (V.1.28.0) was used to identify TLS by the enrichment of a gene signature (CD3E, CXCL13, MS4A1, FCER2, and CR2). AUCell enrichment was subsequently performed on non-TLS cells using the top 20 marker genes for gated cancer cells to obtain a tumor-specific enrichment score. Stroma was defined as tissue located proximal to the identified tumor regions within the non-TLS compartment. Quality control and annotation of tissue niches are shown in online supplemental figure S3.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and statistical analyses were performed using R (V.4.4.2 R Core Team 2025). The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the TLS densities and flow cytometry data between the indications. For other analyses, the data were winsorized using Tukey’s IQR for each indication (HNSCC and CESC) or indication and status (Remission and Exitus). Owing to the skewed distribution and small sample size of the Exitus group, bootstrap two-sample Wald statistics were used. For survival analyses, patients were divided according to median TLS density and the presence/absence of GC+ TLSs. Survival analysis of right-censored data was performed for OS for each indication and relapse-free survival (RFS) for CESC, using a two-sample log-rank χ2 test. The estimates of Kaplan-Meier survival curves were visualized as step functions, together with 95% CIs for survival curves. Dependent samples in the suppression assay were analyzed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs test. To compare the data obtained using the Xenium platform, an unpaired two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test, reporting Bonferroni-Hochberg-adjusted p values, was used. Results were considered statistically significant at p<0.05. The differences between the indications are summarized in online supplemental table S4.

Results

Tertiary lymphoid structures are associated with improved OS in patients with HNSCC but not CESC

To assess the validity of TLS presence/density as a prognostic biomarker in HPV-associated carcinomas, we analyzed the number of aggregates, PF+ TLSs, and GC+ TLSs in HNSCC and CESC samples (figure 1A). The sum of all the TLSs, regardless of their maturation status, was defined as the bulk TLSs. We observed significantly higher levels of bulk TLSs (19.2±2.4 vs 28.4±2.1 per mm2; p=0.015) and aggregates (17.1±2.2 vs 24.6±1.8 per mm2; p=0.024) in CESC samples than in HNSCC samples (figure 1B). GC+ TLSs were present in 48.8% and 34.2% HNSCC and CESC tissues, respectively.

Although high bulk TLS densities and GC+ TLS presence in the TME were associated with improved OS (p=0.023 and 0.015, respectively) in patients with HNSCC (figure 1C,D), TLSs had no significant impact on the OS of patients with CESC (figure 1E,F). The presence of GC+ TLSs slightly improved RFS in patients with CESC (figure 1H); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.081).

CD8+GZMB+ T-cell levels are significantly higher in HNSCC samples than in CESC tissues

As cytotoxic T cells are considered key players in the antitumor immune response, we analyzed the cytotoxic granzyme B-expressing CD8+ T cell density and distribution in the tumor epithelium, tumor stroma, aggregates, PF+ TLSs, and GC+ TLSs of HNSCC and CESC samples. While HNSCC samples showed very high levels of CD8+GZMB+ T cells in all compartments analyzed, particularly in aggregates, only minimal levels of CD8+GZMB+ T cells were found in CESC tissues (figure 2B; online supplemental table S4). The number of CD8+GZMB+ T cells alone did not differ between patients in remission and those who died of each tumor (figure 2C, online supplemental table S5). Interestingly, when combined with the presence of GC+ TLSs, high (above median) levels of CD8+GZMB+ T cells were associated with excellent survival (p=0.020, compared with patients with low levels of CD8+GZMB+ T cells and absent GC+ TLSs) and significantly improved RFS (p=0.043, compared with patients with low levels of CD8+GZMB+ T cells and absent GC+ TLSs) in patients with CESC (figure 2F).

Levels of PD-L1+ macrophages are comparable in TLSs from HNSCC and CESC samples

To understand why TLSs do not truly contribute to OS/RFS in patients with CESC, we analyzed the potentially suppressive immune cells in the tumor epithelium, tumor stroma, aggregates, PF+ TLSs, and GC+ TLSs. Healthy tonsil sections were used as reference tissues, representing a secondary lymphoid organ with primary and secondary lymphoid follicles. First, we analyzed the density of macrophages with an emphasis on PD-L1+CD68+ macrophages in the TLSs of HNSCC and CESC samples (figure 2A). HNSCC samples showed significantly higher levels of PD-L1+CD68+ macrophages in the stroma; however, no significant differences were observed between the TLSs from patients with HNSCC and CESC (figure 2D, online supplemental table S4). The densities of PD-L1+CD68+ macrophages were markedly higher in TLSs than in healthy tonsils (figure 2D). No statistically significant differences were observed between patients in remission and those who died of each tumor (figure 2E, online supplemental table S5).

TLSs in CESC tissues contain high levels of Treg cells

Then we assessed the absolute number and proportion of CD4+FoxP3+ Tregs (figure 3A), which were significantly higher in aggregates and PF+ TLSs in CESC tissues than in HNSCC tissues (figure 3C,D; online supplemental table S4). Additionally, the proportion of Tregs among CD4+ T cells was significantly higher in CESC-derived PF+ TLSs than in primary follicles analyzed in healthy tonsils (p=0.025; figure 3C). Treg densities in TLSs were the highest in patients who succumbed to CESC (figure 3F,G; online supplemental table S5). Tregs in CESC-derived aggregates and PF+ TLSs showed higher Ki-67 levels than conventional CD4+FoxP3− T cells (figure 3E); however, these differences were not statistically significant. According to the density of Tregs, patients were divided into low-level (below the upper quartile) and high-level (the upper quartile) groups. The survival rate of patients with CESC with low Treg levels in PF+ TLSs was 85.7% compared with 40% in patients with high Treg levels in PF+ TLSs (p=0.098). The survival rate of patients with CESC with low Treg levels in aggregates was 91% compared with 37.5% in patients with high Treg levels in aggregates (p=0.013). All patients with HNSCC were ranked in the low-Treg group.

PMN-MDSCs prevail in TLSs from CESC samples

In addition, we analyzed the Lox1+CD15+ PMN-MDSC proportions in CESC and HNSCC tissues (figure 4A) and compared their levels with the PMN-MDSC densities in the primary and secondary follicles of healthy tonsils. Although the phenotyping of human MDSCs has discrepancies owing to their heterogeneity, suppressive PMN-MDSCs in cancer tissues were shown to express CD15, lectin-type oxidized LDL receptor 1 (Lox1), and high levels of Arg1.20 We observed markedly higher PMN-MDSCs levels in TLSs from CESC tissues than in those from healthy tonsil and HNSCC tissues. However, only the differences between PF+ TLS levels in CESC and HNSCC were statistically significant (p=0.251, 0.024, and 0.680 for aggregates, PF+ TLSs, and GC+ TLSs, respectively; figure 4B; online supplemental table S4). The highest PMN-MDSC levels were observed in the PF+ TLSs of patients who died of CESC (figure 4C, online supplemental table S5). Interestingly, we observed that patients with CESC and markedly higher levels of PMN-MDSCs in both aggregates and PF+ TLSs lacked GC+ TLSs in the TME (figure 4D). Additionally, a significantly higher proportion of patients with CESC and low PMN-MDSC numbers in PF+ TLSs developed GC+ structures in the TME than patients with high PMN-MDSC infiltration (upper quartile) in PF+ TLSs (60.7% vs 18.2%, p=0.016; figure 4E). The survival rate of patients with CESC and low levels of PMN-MDSCs in PF+ TLSs was 84.6%, compared with 66.6% in patients with high levels of PMN-MDSCs in PF+ TLSs. The survival rates did not depend on the level of PMN-MDSCs in aggregates.

High TLS densities are associated with the presence of HPV-specific T cells in the TME of patients with HNSCC but not CESC

To elucidate the association between TLS abundance and the presence of tumor-specific T cells in the TME, we analyzed the aggregate, PF+ TLS, and GC+ TLS densities in HNSCC and CESC samples with or without HPV-specific T cells in the tumor tissue. The presence of HPV-specific T cells was associated with higher bulk TLS density in HNSCC samples than in tissues lacking HPV-specific T cells (31.4±4.4 vs 15.7±4.1 per mm2; p=0.027; figure 6A). Additionally, HNSCC samples with HPV-specific T cells showed significantly higher PF+ TLS densities (2.6±0.4 vs 0.5±0.3 per mm2; p=0.004), and markedly higher aggregate (27.0±4.6 vs 14.8±4.1 per mm2; p=0.085), and GC+ TLS (1.9±0.6 vs 0.4±0.2 per mm2; p=0.059) densities than HNSCC samples without detectable HPV-specific T cells (figure 6A). No statistically significant differences were observed in the CESC tissues; however, no GC+ TLSs were detected in samples from patients lacking tumor-infiltrating HPV-specific T cells (figure 6B).

Discussion

TLS abundance has been associated with a good response of patients to both standard-of-care and immunotherapy protocols in multiple malignancies,1,3 including HNSCC.4,7 In this study, we showed that, in contrast to HNSCC, TLS numbers were not correlated with the outcome of patients with CESC. TLSs were abundant in CESC tissues but contained a low percentage of GZMB+CD8 T cells, high numbers of Tregs and PMN-MDSCs, and exhibited a significant T-cell exhaustion transcriptomic signature. High PMN-MDSC levels in TLSs were also associated with impaired TLS maturation and a lack of HPV-specific T cells in CESC.

Zhang et al23 showed a correlation between TLS formation in CESC tissues and improved progression-free survival of the patients. However, 50% of patients with CESC in the study were considered HPV-negative, and 30% received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which might cause some inconsistencies with our results. Xiong et al24 reported a positive prognostic value of TLSs in patients with CESC; however, the study was based on in silico analysis and tumor microarray staining with H&E only, which may cause bias in the detected TLS numbers owing to the small size of tissues and lack of specific markers. As we observed high TLS levels in both patients with CESC who succumbed to the disease and those in remission, we focused on the immune cell composition of the TLSs. Interestingly, we detected significantly fewer cytotoxic GZMB+CD8+ T cells in the CESC tumor epithelium, stroma, and TLSs than in HNSCC samples, further suggesting the potential TLS suppression in CESC.

High macrophage densities in the TLSs of patients with colorectal cancer correlate with short progression-free survival.16 In oral squamous cell carcinoma, tumor-infiltrating PD-L1+ macrophages are associated with T-cell apoptosis and immune suppression.25 However, we observed comparable levels of PD-L1+ macrophages in patients with HNSCC and CESC, which is in line with the results of other studies showing both the positive and negative clinical impacts of PD-L1+ macrophages.26

Compared with HNSCC-derived TLSs, aggregates and PF+ TLSs from CESC samples contained markedly higher Treg levels, expressed as both absolute numbers and proportions of CD4+ T cells. These results, based on multiplex immunofluorescence staining, were also supported by the results obtained using in situ transcriptomic analysis. The highest Treg density was observed in the TLSs of patients with CESC who died of the disease. Specifically, the survival rate of patients with low Treg levels in aggregates and Treg scores within the upper quartile was 91% and 37.5%, respectively. Similar to our findings, a high Treg density in TLSs has been associated with the short-term survival of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).27 Both tumor-infiltrating and TLS-infiltrating Tregs in NSCLC express high levels of TIGIT and CTLA-4 and show strong capacity to suppress autologous CD4+ T-cell proliferation.27 In accordance with these data, we also observed high expression of TIGIT and CTLA-4 in TLS-infiltrating Tregs in both HNSCC and CESC samples, suggesting a high suppressive capacity of these cells.

Tregs were also shown to accumulate in TLSs in a mouse model of lung adenocarcinoma. After systemic Treg depletion, conventional T-cell proliferation increased rapidly inside TLSs, highlighting the significant role of Tregs in TLS activity in this model.28 In breast cancer, functional Tfh cells were linked to active TLSs, which are characterized by the induction of both humoral and cytotoxic immune responses. However, the presence of regulatory FoxP3+ T cells suppresses these processes, and the Tfh to Tfr cell ratio significantly affects TLS activity.15

Our previous study demonstrated a high PMN-MDSC abundance in the CESC TME.10 MDSCs expand during cancer and are potent suppressors of T cell-mediated immune responses.29 Here, we observed markedly more PMN-MDSCs in TLSs from CESC tissues than in those from healthy tonsils and HNSCC tissues. Moreover, patients with CESC who died of the disease had significantly higher PMN-MDSC densities in PF+ TLSs than those in remission. A high proportion of Arg1+ PMN-MDSCs also negatively correlated to TCRζ expression on the tumor-infiltrating T cells, suggesting an impact of PMN-MDSCs on TCR signaling and T cell-mediated antitumor immune responses. The suppressive capacity of Lox1+ PMN-MDSC was confirmed in multiple studies. A significant downregulation of granzyme B and Ki-67 expression was observed in T cells that co-localized with Lox-1+Arg+ PMN-MDSC in HNSCC tissues.30 Lox1+CD15+ PMN-MDSC also markedly reduced T-cell proliferation in patients with HNSCC and NSCLC,20 and T-cell proliferation and IFN-γ production in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma.31 These suppressive effects were reversed by an arginase inhibitor, N-hydroxyl-nor-l-arginine, further underlining arginase as the key molecule in PMN-MDSC-mediated T-cell suppression.20 31 In our study, we confirmed the impact of PMN-MDSC isolated from the peripheral blood of CESC patients on T-cell proliferation and IFN-γ production. Isolation of live functional PMN-MDSCs from tumor tissue was not possible due to technical issues; however, PMN-MDSCs from CESC tumor samples expressed the highest levels of arginase, compared with PMN-MDSCs derived from HNSCC samples, control tonsils, and peripheral blood, suggesting a high suppressive capacity of CESC-infiltrating CD15+Lox1+ PMN-MDSCs.

Interestingly, HPV E6/E7-specific T cells were previously detected in 64–73% HPV+ HNSCC tissues,17 32 but only 43% CESC samples.19 In our study, TLS abundance significantly correlated with the presence of HPV-specific T cells only in HNSCC tissues. We found high TLS densities in samples with and without detectable HPV-specific T cells in CESC; however, no GC+ TLSs were detected in samples lacking HPV-specific T cells. Analysis of the cellular composition of TLSs revealed that patients with CESC and without detectable HPV-specific T cells showed markedly higher PMN-MDSC, but not Treg, densities in TLSs, particularly in early aggregates. Mature, GC+ TLSs are considered essential centers of humoral and cytotoxic antitumor immunity in the TME,33 34 which is consistent with the absence of GC+ TLSs in CESC tissues with the lack of detectable HPV-specific T cells. A potential negative impact of high densities of PMN-MDSC on TLS maturation is corroborated by our observation of high PMN-MDSC levels in aggregates and PF+ TLSs in CESC tissue samples that lack GC+ TLSs in the TME. Nevertheless, experimental evidence is needed to prove this association. Our study was based on the detection of E6/E7-specific T cells in the TME. However, it has been recently shown that a significant proportion of HPV-specific T cells in HNSCC TME and tumor-draining lymph nodes comprises E2-specific and E5-specific T cells.35 Therefore, further studies are needed to elucidate the association between the TLS density and composition and the presence of the whole spectrum of HPV-specific T cells. Additionally, functional validation of the impact of TLS-resident PMN-MDSC and Tregs on TLS maturation and T-cell functionality is needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying our findings.

In conclusion, our data demonstrate that although TLSs are abundant in CESC tissues, their presence does not correlate with patient OS. Immature CESC-derived TLSs were infiltrated by high numbers of Tregs and activated Arg1-producing PMN-MDSCs, particularly in patients who succumbed to the disease. The proportion of PMN-MDSCs negatively correlated with TCRζ expression on the tumor-infiltrating T cells and the lack of HPV-specific T cells in CESC. Our findings suggest that in patients with high-risk CESC, the presence of PMN-MDSCs and Tregs within immature TLSs may impair local immune responses and hinder TLS maturation into functional germinal center-containing structures. Consequently, targeting CD15+Lox1+ PMN-MDSCs could represent a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for this patient group.
