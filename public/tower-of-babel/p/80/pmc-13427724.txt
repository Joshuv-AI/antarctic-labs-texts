Discovery of potent kinase inhibitors with improved pharmacokinetics and safety potentials through structural optimization of dasatinib
Authors: Kan He, Lining Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDasatinib exhibits broad therapeutic potentials for several diseases; however, its clinical application is limited by safety concerns and unfavorable pharmacokinetics.MethodsStructural optimization of dasatinib yielded PDD-87. Preclinical studies evaluated kinase inhibition, antiproliferative activity in leukemia and lymphoma cell lines, antitumor effects in a xenograft model, pharmacokinetics and metabolism in vitro and in rodents, and exploratory safety in mice.ResultsHere we show that PDD-87 strongly inhibits ABL and BTK along with their key mutants, and SRC family kinases (IC₅₀ <1 nM), displaying potent antiproliferative effects against several leukemia and lymphoma cell lines. In a K-562G mouse xenograft model, PDD-87 significantly reduces tumor growth in a dose-dependent manner. Compared to dasatinib, PDD-87 exhibits lower clearance (405 vs 989 mL/hr/kg), reduced volume of distribution (0.9 vs 2.4 mL/hr/kg), higher plasma exposure (2521 vs 190 hr*pmol/mL for oral dose), and improved oral bioavailability (18.9 vs 3.9%) in rats. PDD-87 demonstrates lower metabolic clearance than dasatinib in human, mouse, rat, and dog liver microsomes. Metabolically, PDD-87 undergoes hydroxylation on the 2-chloro-6-methylphenyl ring, followed by sulfation, and glucuronidation. Significantly, PDD-87 shows markedly lower lung accumulation than dasatinib, suggesting a reduced risk of pulmonary toxicity. The lung/plasma ratios of Cmax and AUClast for PDD-87 are 2 and 4, respectively, compared to 12 and 22 for dasatinib. Additionally, PDD-87 is well-tolerated in mice at doses up to 200 mg/day for two weeks, with no overt toxicity.ConclusionsPDD-87 is a highly potent, selective kinase inhibitor with improved pharmacokinetics and safety potentials, holding promise for broader clinical applications.

Introduction

Many drugs on the market are associated with severe toxicity, which can often lead to treatment failure. Pharmacokinetic variability further complicates therapy, as fixed dosages can result in highly variable drug exposure: some patients may experience subtherapeutic levels which reduces efficacy, while others encounter higher-than-expected concentrations which increases the risk of toxicity1. In some instances, toxicity is linked to drug metabolites, which may introduce adverse effects not present with the parent compound2. Discovering structural analogs of drugs with improved pharmacokinetics and safety is an important strategy in modern drug research and development.

Several examples highlight the success of this approach. Fexofenadine, a metabolite of terfenadine, retains the desired antihistamine effect while eliminating the risk of cardiotoxicity3. Within the thiazolidinedione class of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ) activators, rosiglitazone and pioglitazone were developed to avoid the liver toxicity associated with troglitazone2,4,5. Statins, such as pravastatin, simvastatin, and lovastatin, share similar structures yet demonstrate distinct pharmacokinetic and safety profiles, with pravastatin offering better safety and fewer pharmacokinetic interactions due to its hydrophilic property6. Nilotinib was developed as a second-generation analog of imatinib with enhanced potency, reduced off-target effects, and optimized pharmacokinetics, resulting in greater efficacy and safety in patients resistant to imatinib therapy7. These examples underscore the importance of structural refinement in advancing drug safety and efficacy.

Dasatinib (Fig. 1), a potent multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, is currently approved for the treatment of Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph + ) chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Its clinical efficacy is attributed to its strong inhibition of Breakpoint Cluster Region-Abelson (BCR-ABL) kinase, including both active and inactive conformations, and its broader activity against a range of kinases implicated in various diseases8–10. This comprehensive kinase inhibition profile has made dasatinib a promising candidate for additional therapeutic applications beyond its initial indications, such as B-cell and T-cell malignancies11–14. Dasatinib has shown potential for treating solid tumors as well as autoimmune and inflammatory diseases15–19. It has also demonstrated synergistic effects in combination therapies with immunomodulators, such as PD-1 antibodies20.Fig. 1Chemical Structures of Dasatinib and PDD-87.

Dasatinib was recently discovered to have strong senolytic activity in preclinical and clinical studies21–30. It selectively eliminates senescent cells by targeting key survival pathways through its inhibition of erythropoietin-producing hepatoma receptor B kinases (EphB) and SRC family kinases (SFKs), disrupting ephrin ligand (EFN) B1 and B3-dependent survival mechanisms and the Src-p38 axis9,31–34. Several clinical trials are ongoing to evaluate the efficacy and safety of dasatinib in the treatment of osteoporosis, Alzheimer’s disease, fibrotic non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, accelerated aging in mental disorders, and other senescence-driven conditions (www.clinicaltrials.gov). These investigations suggest that dasatinib or its analogs hold potential for treating a wide range of medical conditions, including lung, kidney, and liver fibrosis, diabetes, osteoarthritis, and intervertebral disc degeneration.

Despite its efficacy, the clinical application of dasatinib is limited by significant safety and pharmacokinetic challenges35–38. Among the most concerning toxicities is pulmonary toxicity, likely related to the potential accumulation of dasatinib in lung tissue38–40. Patients on dasatinib may experience a high frequency of adverse pulmonary events, including pleural effusion, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and interstitial pneumonitis, which, in severe cases, leads to treatment failure37,41–46. Furthermore, dasatinib displays high interindividual PK variability, which complicates its therapeutic use36,38. Its high clearance rate and extensive first-pass metabolism led to low oral bioavailability and highly variable plasma exposure, resulting in inconsistent therapeutic effects and a narrow therapeutic window. Consequently, the potential of dasatinib for broader indications remains constrained due to these toxicities and large individual pharmacokinetic variations.

We hypothesize that accumulation of dasatinib in lung tissue contribute to its pulmonary toxicity; this is supported by clinical observations showing that a once-daily dosing regimen reduces lung-related toxicities compared to twice-daily dosing38,39. The low oral bioavailability of dasatinib and high clearance contribute to its highly variable plasma exposures38. Taken all these considerations, our strategy for structural optimization had focused on: (1) preserving dasatinib’s potent and selective kinase inhibition profile; (2) reducing metabolic clearance and enhancing oral bioavailability to minimize pharmacokinetic variability; (3) optimizing tissue distribution to limit accumulation in non-target organs, particularly the lung; (4) preventing the formation of the N-dealkylated metabolite, which may accumulate in peripheral tissues and contribute to toxicity; and (5) ensuring structural novelty. We optimized the chemical structure of dasatinib by modifying the hydroxyethyl piperazine moiety in order to refine its tissue distribution, particularly reducing lung accumulation, and to improve its pharmacokinetic profile. In addition, we also explored deuterium substitution at the 4-position of the 2-chloro-6-methylphenyl ring in an attempt to block one of the major metabolic pathways of dasatinib47. This effort led to the discovery of N-(2-chloro-6-methylphenyl)-2-[[2-methyl-6-((S)-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-1-yl)pyrimidin-4-yl]amino]thiazole-5-carboxamide (PDD-87, Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Chemical synthesis

All regents were purchased from commercial sources. Dasatinib was purchased from MedChemExpress USA (Monmouth Junction, NJ). Commercially available intermediate 1H (Combi-Blocks, Inc., San Diego, CA) or internally synthesized 1D was used as the intermediate for the synthesis of dasatinib analogs. In general, 1H or 1D was reacted with piperidine, 4-hydroxypiperidine, (R)-3- hydroxypyrrolidine, (S)-3- hydroxypyrrolidine to form 3H or 3D, 4H or 4D, 8H or 8D, 9H or 9D, respectively (Fig. 2). 1H or 1D was reacted with tert-butyl (R)-pyrrolidin-3-ylcarbamate and tert-butyl (S)-pyrrolidin-3-ylcarbamate to form 6a and 7a, followed by deprotection to produce 6H or 6D and 7H or 7D, respectively. 1H was reacted with pyrrolidine to form 5H. 1D was reacted with 1-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine to form 2D.Fig. 2Chemical Synthesis of Compounds.a: N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIEA), dioxane, 90–92 °C; b: DIEA, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 110 °C.

Preparation of N-(2-chloro-6-methylphenyl)-2-[[2-methyl-6-((R)-3-aminopyrrolidin-1-yl)pyrimidin-4-yl]amino]thiazole-5-carboxamide (6H)

To a mixture of the starting material 1H (150 mg, 0.38 mmol) and dioxane (8 mL) were added tert-butyl (R)-pyrrolidin-3-ylcarbamate (142 mg, 0.76 mmol, 2 eq) and DIEA (147 mg, 1.14 mmol, 3 eq) at room temperature. The mixture was then stirred at 90–91 °C under nitrogen for 12 h. LC-MS analysis showed the product peak. The mixture was cooled to room temperature and concentrated under reduced pressure. After addition of methanol (4 mL), the mixture was re-concentrated to give the intermediate 6a as a gray solid. LC-MS: 544.12 (M + H).

To the crude sample (6a) was added dichloromethane (DCM) (4 mL). The mixture was cooled to 5oC, and then a mixture of trifluoracetic acid (TFA)-DCM (1:1, 5 mL) was added dropwise. After addition, the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 3 h and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was mixed with methanol (4 mL), followed by addition of trimethylamine (TEA) (2 mL) and stirring a while. The mixture was concentrated to dryness, then suspended in 50 mL distilled water, and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The pellet was re-suspended with 50 mL distilled water, and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The pellet was further suspended with 100 mL 80% acetonitrile, and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was evaporated to dryness to afford the target 6H as an off-white solid (approximately 96 mg). Kinase inhibition assays

The experiments were carried out by Carna Biosciences, Inc. (Natick, MA). An off-chip Mobility Shift Assay (MSA) was used to measure kinase activity. For each assay, 5 µL of x4 compound solution, 5 µL of x4 substrate/ATP/metal solution, and 10 µL of x2 kinase solution in an assay buffer (20 mM HEPES, 0.01% Triton X-100, 2 mM DTT, pH 7.5) were prepared, mixed, and incubated in a polypropylene 384-well microplate at room temperature for either 1 or 5 h depending on the kinase. Following the incubation, 70 µL of Termination Buffer (QuickScout Screening Assist MSA; Carna Biosciences) was added to each well, and the mixture was applied to a LabChip system (PerkinElmer). Product and substrate peptide peaks were separated and quantified, with reaction efficiency calculated using the product ratio (P/(P + S)) derived from the peak heights of product (P) and substrate (S) peptides. The experimental conditions adhered to assay protocols from Carna Biosciences, Inc. (BMA 3 F, 1-5-5 Minatojima-Minamimachi, Chuo-ku, Kobe 650–0047, Japan; www.carnabio.com). Percent inhibition was calculated using the reaction control (complete reaction mixture) as 0% inhibition and the enzyme-negative background as 100% inhibition. IC₅₀ values were calculated using the median-effect plot method49. All experiments were performed in duplicate.

Cell inhibition assays

SU-DHL-4 (ATCC® CRL-2957™), K-562 (ATCC® CCL-243™), and SU-DHL-6 (ATCC® CRL-2959™) cell lines were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA). Cell culture medium, fetal bovine serum and other reagents were purchased from Themo Fisher. SU-DHL-4 and SU-DHL-6 cells were cultured in ATCC-formulated RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Life Technologies), while K-562 cells were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) with 10% FBS. All cell lines were maintained in T-75 flasks at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Once the cell concentration reached approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL, the cultures were diluted to a concentration of 2.5–5 × 10⁴ cells/mL in their respective media. A 200-µL aliquot of the diluted cell suspension was added to 96-well plates preloaded with 1 µL of the test compounds at various concentrations. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h under 5% CO₂ and saturated humidity. After incubation, 10 µL of PrestoBlue® Cell Viability Reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific) was added to each well and incubated for 60 min before absorbance measurements were taken at 570 nm and 600 nm using a SpectraMax Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices). Absorbance values were normalized to the 600 nm reading, and IC₅₀ values were calculated using the median-effect plot method49.

In another study conducted by Crown Bioscience (Beijing) Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), 46 leukemia and lymphoma cell lines were used as described in the Results section. Cells were cultured in appropriate medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ and saturated humidity. On Day 1, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested, counted, and adjusted to the desired concentration using the culture medium. A 90 μL aliquot of cell suspension was added to each well of a 96-well plate, including T0 controls, and plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity. On Day 2, for the T0 control group, 10 μL of culture medium was added to each well, followed by 50 μL of CellTiter-Glo reagent (Promega). Plates were shaken for 2 min to ensure complete cell lysis, equilibrated at room temperature for 10 min, and luminescence was measured using an EnVision plate reader (PerkinElmer). For the drug treatment group, 10 μL of a 10x working solution containing test compounds or a positive control was added to each well in triplicate for each concentration. The highest test concentration was 10 μM, with nine 3-fold serial dilutions. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity for 72 h. On Day 5, the drug-treated plates were equilibrated at room temperature for 30 minutes, and 50 μL of CellTiter-Glo reagent was added to each well. Plates were shaken for 2 min to ensure cell lysis, equilibrated for 10 minutes, and luminescence was measured using an EnVision reader. IC₅₀ values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 5.0 by fitting the dose-response data to a nonlinear S-curve regression.

Liver microsomal incubations for metabolic stability

Male ICR/CD-1 mouse pooled liver microsomes (MLM), male Sprague-Dawley rat liver microsomes (RLM), male Beagle dog liver microsomes (DLM) and mixed gender Cynomolgus monkey (MrLM) were purchased from BioIVT (Westbury, NY). Pooled human liver microsomes (HLM) were purchased from Corning Inc (Tewksbury, MA). β-nicotinamide adenine phosphate (NADPH), alamethicin and uridine 5’-diphosphoglucuronic acid (UDPGA) were purchased from SigmaAldrich.

Metabolic stability in liver microsomes was assessed for PDD-87 and dasatinib, which were incubated together at a concentration of 1 µM in liver microsomes (0.5 mg/mL) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer containing 10 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM NADPH, 50 µg/mL alamethicin and 2 mM UDPGA at 37 °C for 0, 5, 15, 30, and 60 min. The experiment was performed in triplicate. At each time point, the incubation reactions were quenched by adding 2x volumes of acetonitrile containing 100 nM reserpine (internal standard). The quenched samples were centrifuged at 4000 RPM for 10 min, and the supernatants were subjected to LC/MS/MS analysis.

LC/MS/MS analysis was performed on an AB Sciex 4000 Q Trap LC/MS/MS system coupled with a Shimadzu Prominence UFLCXR 20 series, including a CBM-20A controller, two LC-20ADXR solvent delivery units, a SIL-20AC HT autosampler, a CTO-20A column oven, and an SPD-20A UV detector. Samples were separated on a Phenomenex Columbus column (C18, 4 µm, 50 ×2 mm) eluted with two solvent systems: 2 mM ammonium acetate in water containing 0.1% formic acid (A) and methanol (B) at a linear gradient starting with 25% B. Electrospray ionization in positive mode was used to acquire LC/MS/MS data. The in vitro half-life (t₁/₂) and intrinsic clearance were calculated using a previously reported method50

Hepatocyte incubations for metabolism studies

Cryopreserved pooled hepatocytes from mouse (29 male, ICR/CD), rat (18 male, Sprague-Dawley), dog (3 male, Beagle), monkey (3 male, Cynomolgus), and human (LiverPool, 10 donors) were obtained from BioIVT (Baltimore, MD). Hepatocyte thawing medium was also sourced from BioIVT. William’s E medium was purchased from Life Technologies (Carlsbad, CA).

PDD-87 was incubated at a concentration of 10 µM with hepatocytes from mouse, rat, dog, monkey, and human in William’s E medium at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity for 4 hours. Incubations were performed in 25-mL tissue culture flasks containing 10 mL of the incubation mixture with approximately 5 × 10⁶ viable cells. At 0 and 4 hours after the addition of PDD-87, 2 mL aliquots of the incubation mixture from each species were collected and mixed with 6 mL methanol in 15-mL centrifuge tubes. The remaining 6 mL of each incubation mixture was stored at −20 °C for further experiments. The quenched aliquots (0 and 4 h) were vortex-mixed for 1 min and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min at room temperature. Supernatants were transferred to new tubes and dried under nitrogen flow. Residues were reconstituted in 200 µL methanol-acetonitrile (1:1) for metabolite identification.

Samples were analyzed using an LC/MS/MS system consisting of an AB Sciex 4000 Q Trap mass spectrometer coupled with the Shimadzu Prominence UFLCXR 20 series (including a CBM-20A controller, two LC-20ADXR solvent delivery units, SIL-20AC HT autosampler, CTO-20A column oven, and SPD-20A UV detector). Separation was achieved on an ACE-3 column (C18, 3 µm, 150 × 4.6 mm) using a gradient elution with two solvent systems: 2 mM ammonium acetate in water containing 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B), transitioning linearly from 10% B to 90% B over 40 minutes. The HPLC eluent was introduced into the mass spectrometer in positive electrospray ionization mode, and mass spectral data were acquired using EMS, Q1, and EPI scans. Metabolite structures were assigned based on characteristic mass fragmentations of the identified metabolites.

Pharmacokinetic Studies in Rats

The in-life portion of the study was carried out by PRODUCT SAFETY LABS (Dayton, NJ) in compliance with a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Male Sprague-Dawley rat (JVC-cannulated; approximately 275–300 g at study initiation; Charles River Laboratories) were individually housed in solid-bottom polycarbonate cages that conformed to the size recommendations outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011). Animals were acclimated to the housing facilities for at least 2 days prior to dosing. The animal room was maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle and kept clean and vermin-free. Environmental conditions were controlled to maintain a temperature of 21 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 30–70%. Envigo Teklad Global 16% Protein Rodent Diet was provided ad libitum during acclimation and throughout the study, except that animals in the oral dosing groups were fasted overnight prior to dosing, with food returned approximately 4 h after dose administration. Filtered tap water was available ad libitum via individual bottles attached to each cage. No known contaminants in the food or water were expected to interfere with the study results. Each cage was labeled with a card indicating, at minimum, the study number, group assignment, individual animal identification number, and sex.

PDD-87 and dasatinib were dissolved together at a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL for each compound in water containing 2.5% DMSO, 30% propylene glycol 300, and 10% dextrose solution (50%) for intravenous dosing. For oral dosing, the compounds were dissolved together at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL in water containing 5% DMSO, 50% propylene glycol 300, and 10% Solutol® HS 15 (Sigma-Aldrich). Rats (N = 3) were dosed intravenously at 5 mL/kg and orally at 10 mL/kg using these dosing solutions. Blood samples were collected into tubes containing EDTA as an anticoagulant at 0, 0.25 (intravenous only), 0.5, 1, 2, 4-, 8-, 10-, and 24-h post-dosing. Plasma samples were prepared by centrifugation and mixed with 3x volumes of acetonitrile containing 100 nM reserpine as an internal standard. The mixtures were centrifuged at 4000 RPM for 15 min, and the supernatants were injected for LC/MS/MS analysis as described above. Plasma concentrations of PDD-87 and dasatinib were quantified using standard curves for each compound. Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using WinNonlin, and statistical analyses were performed using two-sided t-test in Excel where applicable. P values were rounded to three decimal places, with p < 0.001 reported as p < 0.001. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

K-562G xenograft studies in mice

The study was conducted by Crown Bioscience (Beijing) Inc. (Beijing, P.R. China) in compliance with a protocol approved by the IACUC. Imatinib-resistant K-562 cells (K-562G) were developed by the Tianjin Institute of Hematology. K-562 cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were exposed to gradually increasing concentrations of imatinib over several months to generate the imatinib-resistant K-562G line. These tumor cells were maintained in vitro in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 1 μmol/L imatinib at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere with saturated humidity. Tumor cells were sub-cultured twice weekly, and exponentially growing cells were harvested and counted for tumor inoculation.

Female NOD.SCID mice (12 weeks old, body weight 20.6–29.1 g) were purchased from Shanghai Lingchang Bio-Technology Co. Ltd. (LC, Shanghai, China). The animals was housed in individual ventilated cages (up to 5 mice per cage) under the following conditions: temperature: 20 ~ 26 °C; humidity: 40–70%; light cycle:7:00 a.m.–19:00 p.m. light and 19:00 p.m.–7:00 a.m.(next day) darkness; polysulfone IVC cage: size of 325 mm × 210 mm × 180 mm; bedding material: corn cob; diet: mouse diet, Co60 irradiation sterilized dry granule food, animals had free access during the entire study period; water: reverse osmosis water, autoclaved before using, animals had free access to sterile drinking water; cage identification label: number of animals, sex, strain, receiving date, treatment, study number, group number, and the starting date of the treatment, etc.; animal identification: animals were marked by ear coding (notch); adapt housing: the animals was adapted in the facility for at least 7 days.

Each mouse was subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank with 1 × 10⁷ K-562G cells in 0.1 mL of PBS mixed 1:1 with Matrigel for tumor development. Treatments began when the mean tumor size reached approximately 161 mm³. Animals were randomly allocated into treatment groups (N = 8 per group): vehicle control, PDD-87 at 5 mg/kg, PDD-87 at 15 mg/kg, dasatinib at 5 mg/kg, and dasatinib at 15 mg/kg. Randomization was performed based on randomized block design on day 6. Animals were stratified into homogeneous blocks based on tumor volume and randomly assigned to treatment groups within each block to ensure comparable baseline tumor burden and minimize allocation bias. No additional randomization of treatment administration order or cage location was implemented beyond standard facility procedures.

PDD-87 and dasatinib were prepared at 0.5 mg/mL and 1.5 mg/mL, respectively, in water containing 5% DMSO, and 45% propylene glycol 400. The test articles were first dissolved completely in DMSO (sonicated if needed), followed by the addition of PEG400 and thorough mixing, then water was added and sonicated if necessary. The dosing solutions were stored at room temperature. Animals were dosed by oral gavage at 10 mL/kg once daily for 15 days. After tumor cell inoculation, animals were monitored daily for morbidity and mortality. Routine monitoring included assessments of tumor growth, behavior (e.g., mobility, food, and water consumption), body weight gain or loss (measured twice weekly), and other abnormalities. Mortality and clinical signs were recorded for individual animals. Tumor volumes were measured twice weekly in two dimensions using a caliper. The study was terminated on Day-15 after dosing. Humane endpoints were established a priori. Animals with tumors exceeding 3000 mm³, or with body weight loss of 20% for 72 h were euthanized prior to death or before entering a comatose state. No animals were euthanized due to other health concerns. Tumor samples were isolated and weighed at the end of the experiment.

Data analysis was conducted as follows: Tumor volume (TV) = 0.5 × a × b², where a and b are the tumor’s long and short diameters, respectively. T/C is the ratio of TV in the treatment group to that in the control group on a given day. Data were analyzed using Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) version 18.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). P values were rounded to three decimal places, with p < 0.001 reported as p < 0.001. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

1D was reacted with 1-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine to produce 2D, the deuterium derivative of dasatinib (Fig. 2). 2D did not exhibit substantial differences from dasatinib in metabolic stability in human liver microsomes (HLM), and systemic clearance (CL), volume of distribution (Vd), or oral bioavailability (F) in rats. As expected, 2D showed similar potency to dasatinib in inhibiting ABL and Bruton’s Tyrosine Kinase (BTK) activities (Table 1).

1H and 1D were reacted with piperidine to produce 3H and 3D, respectively, which showed potent inhibition of ABL and BTK activities (Fig. 2 and Table 1). Compared to dasatinib, both 3H and 3D were metabolically stable in human liver microsomes with dramatically reduced intrinsic clearance. However, 3D displayed low oral bioavailability in rats. The clearance and volume of distribution results for 3D were not presented given the concern about its solubility in blood after IV dosing. The poor bioavailability was likely due to poor solubility (<1 µg/mL) and a high first-pass effect. Consistently, the calculated LogD (distribution coefficient) and LogP (partition coefficient) for 3H and 3D were substantially higher than those for dasatinib (Table 1).

3D at 10 nM inhibited the following kinases by >90% in the initial screening assays: YES, FRK, SRC, LYNa, FMS, BMX, ABL, FYN(isoform b), FGR, HCK, FYN(isoform a), LCK, DDR2, ARG, ABL(E255K), BTK, EPHA1, BLK, the following kinases by 30 – 90%: ACK, SRM, PDGFRβ, PIK3CA/PIK3R1, PDGFRα, CSK, KIT(D816V), KIT(D816Y), BRK, PDGFRα(V561D), DDR1, KIT, KIT(V560G), and the following kinases by <30%: HER4, KIT(D816E), EGFR(L858R), KIT(V654A), PDGFRα(D842V), EGFR, EGFR(L861Q), EGFR(d746-750), JAK1, RET, RET(S891A), FGFR3, ALK, WNK3, RET(Y791F), BRAF(V600E), RAF1, ROCK1, ABL(T315I), AurA, MAP2K2, RET(M918T), KDR, FGFR2, Erk1, EGFR(T790M), RET(G691S), PDGFRα(T674I), p38α, HER2, JAK3, MAP2K1, p38β, EGFR(T790M/L858R), FLT1, BRAF, KIT(T670I), MET, skMLCK, MNK1, MST1, PKD1, JAK2, YES(T348I), FGFR1, EGFR(d746-750/T790M).

3D was further tested at 0.1 nM and 1 nM. The following kinases were inhibited by 3D at 0.1 nM by >50%: YES, LCK, ARG, FMS, FRK, SRC, LYNa, FYN(isoform a), HCK, FGR, BMX, ABL, BTK, BLK, and the following kinases by 3D at 1 nM by >50%: EPHA1, ACK, PDGFRβ, PDGFRα.

1H and 1D were reacted with 4-hydroxypiperidine to produce 4H and 4D, respectively, to address the poor solubility issue (Fig. 2). The calculated LogD and LogP for 4H and 4D were substantially lower than those for 3H and 3D (Table 1). Compared to dasatinib, both compounds retained comparable potency in inhibiting ABL and BTK activities and demonstrated similar metabolic stability in HLM (Table 1). 4D also showed apparently higher clearance, volume of distribution, and oral bioavailability in rats compared to dasatinib. The improved oral bioavailability compared to 3H and 3D is likely due to enhanced solubility. 4D at 1 nM inhibited the following kinases by >50%: SRC, YES, LCK, HCK, LYNa, FRK, FYN(isoform a), FYN(isoform b), TEC, BMX, LYNb, ABL, BTK, FGR, FMS, BLK, EPHA1, ABL(E255K), and the following kinases by <50%: PDGFRβ, PDGFRα, PIK3CA/PIK3R1, KIT, KIT(V560G), EGFR, HER2, YES(T348I), ITK, p38α, ABL(T315I), RAF1, p38β.

1H was reacted with pyrrolidine to produce 5H, which showed slightly higher intrinsic clearance in HLM than dasatinib (Fig. 2 and Table 1). Based on the learning from 3H and 3D, 5H was not profiled further due to the concern about its possible low solubility as suggested by its high LogD and LogP values.

1H and 1D were reacted with tert-butyl (R)-pyrrolidin-3-ylcarbamate to produce 6aH and 6aD, followed by deprotection to produce 6H and 6D, respectively (Fig. 2). Compared to dasatinib, 6H exhibited comparable kinase inhibition potency and was metabolically much more stable in HLM (Table 1). However, 6H was not orally bioavailable and showed high clearance and large volume of distribution in rats. 6D exhibited similar kinase inhibition and metabolic stability to 6H.

1H and 1D were reacted with tert-butyl (S)-pyrrolidin-3-ylcarbamate to produce 7aH and 7aD, followed by deprotection to produce 7H and 7D, respectively (Fig. 2). Their biological activities and pharmacokinetic profiles were comparable to their enantiomers, 6H and 6D (Table 1).

1H and 1D were reacted with (R)-3-hydroxypyrrolidine and (S)-3-hydroxypyrrolidine to produce 8H and 8D, and 9H and 9D, respectively (Fig. 2). Compared to dasatinib, 8H and 9H demonstrated similar potency in inhibiting ABL and BTK activities, were more stable in HLM, and had improved rat pharmacokinetic profiles (Table 1). There was no substantial difference between the two enantiomers. 8D and 9D exhibited similar kinase inhibition potency and pharmacokinetics profiles to 8H and 9H. N-(2-chloro-6-methylphenyl)-2-[[2-methyl-6-((S)-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-1-yl)pyrimidin-4-yl]amino]thiazole-5-carboxamide (9H, PDD-87) was ultimately selected as the preclinical development candidate for further pharmacological investigation.

Exploratory toxicity study with PDD-87

In a preliminary dose-escalation toxicity study in CB-17 SCID mice dosed by oral gavage at doses up to 200 mg/day for 2 weeks, PDD-87 demonstrated no mortality, no body weight reduction, and no overt signs of toxicity, indicating that PDD-87 was well-tolerated.

Discussion

Dasatinib, originally discovered as a potent pan-SRC kinase inhibitor, inhibits several cytosolic and receptor tyrosine kinases such as SFKs, ABL, BTK, TEC, and Ephrin receptors8,9,19. This comprehensive yet selective kinase inhibition profile is crucial for dasatinib efficacy across multiple diseases while maintaining tolerability in humans.

The high binding affinity of dasatinib to kinases, based on the crystal structure with ABL, is attributed to key interactions through the aminothiazole group in the ATP-binding site and the orthogonal 2-chloro-6-methyl phenyl ring in a primarily hydrophobic pocket near Thr31510. The piperazine moiety forms only additional van der Waals interactions in the COOH-terminal hinge region, and the hydroxyethyl group is highly mobile. These structural insights led us to hypothesize that modifying the hydroxyethyl piperazine moiety could preserve kinase inhibition while addressing the pharmacokinetic, tissue distribution, and toxicity issues of dasatinib. Dasatinib undergoes N-dealkylation on the piperazine ring to generate an active metabolite that may contribute to toxicity, which is also a major clearance pathway51. Moreover, the basicity of the piperazine likely contributes to the extensive tissue distribution of dasatinib. Thus, we focused on altering the hydroxyethyl piperazine moiety while retaining the structure of the rest of the molecule. Additionally, we explored deuterium substitution at position 4 on the 2-chloro-6-methyl phenyl ring to reduce metabolic oxidation and clearance.

This structure-guided optimization led to the discovery of several analogs that maintained the kinase inhibition potency (Table 1). Comparable to dasatinib, these analogs do not contain the piperazine moiety, thereby eliminating the possibility of N-dealkylation (Fig. 6). However, the analogs presented various challenges, including high clearance, extensive peripheral tissue distribution, and low oral bioavailability. For example, the piperidine derivatives 3H and 3D were metabolically stable in HLM with dramatically reduced intrinsic clearance. However, 3D displayed low oral bioavailability. The hydroxypiperidine derivatives (4H and 4D) demonstrated similar metabolic stability in human liver microsomes but 4D also showed apparently higher clearance, volume of distribution, and oral bioavailability in rats compared to dasatinib. The aminopyrrolidine derivative 6H was metabolically much more stable in human liver microsomes. However, 6H was not orally bioavailable and showed high clearance and large volume of distribution in rats. Finally, the hydroxypyrrolidine derivatives (8H and 8D, and 9H and 9D) demonstrated comparable potency in inhibiting ABL and BTK, but were metabolically more stable in human liver microsomes, and had lower systemic clearance, smaller volume of distribution and higher oral bioavailability in rats compared to dasatinib. There was no substantial difference between the two enantiomers. It is noted that the deuterium derivatives at the 4-position of the 2-chloro-6-methyl phenyl ring did not reduce metabolic clearance in HLM or systemic clearance in rats, even though 4-hydroxylation is a major metabolic pathway of dasatinib47. Ultimately, N-(2-chloro-6-methylphenyl)-2-[[2-methyl-6-((S)-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-1-yl)pyrimidin-4-yl]amino]thiazole-5-carboxamide (PDD-87) emerged as a promising candidate for further investigations.

PDD-87 retained potent inhibition against ABL and its primary resistant mutant E255K with IC50 < 1 nM (Fig. 3 and Table 2). Although it is not profiled for other ABL mutants, PDD-87 is supposed to show similar inhibition profile to dasatinib. Moreover, PDD-87 showed strong antiproliferative activity in CML cell lines such as KU-812, MEG-01, and K562 with IC50 < 1 nM (Fig. 3 and Table 3). In xenograft models with imatinib-resistant K562G cells, PDD-87 achieved dose-dependent tumor growth inhibition (Fig. 4 and Table 4). PDD-87 showed similar or slightly lower efficacy than dasatinib, however, it should be noted that the efficacy of PDD-87 may be underestimated in this subcutaneous model, as it preferentially distributes to blood rather than peripheral tissues (Fig. 5 and Tables 5, 6). These profiles support the potential of PDD-87 as a therapeutic for CML and ALL as approved for dasatinib.

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common type of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL), accounting for 30%–58% of cases. It is an aggressive and highly heterogeneous cancer, posing challenges for precision medicine. PDD-87 demonstrated potent inhibition of DLBCL cell proliferation, with IC50 < 10 nM in SU-DHL-4 and SU-DHL-6 cell lines—approximately 100 times more potent than ibrutinib, the first BTK inhibitor tested in late-phase clinical trials for the treatment of DLBCL (Fig. 3). Further testing in various lymphoma and leukemia cell lines revealed that PDD-87 effectively inhibited the proliferation of several DLBCL cell lines such as Pfeiffer, WSU-NHL, DOHH-2, U-2932, SU-DHL-5, OCI-LY7, HBL-1, and KARPAS-422, the Burkitt’s lymphoma cell line Daudi, the mantle cell lymphoma line JVM-2, and the B-cell ALL line SUP-B15 (Table 3). However, several DLBCL cell lines, such as OCI-LY19, OCI-LY3, SU-DHL-2, SU-DHL-8, Toledo, and WSU-DLCL2, were not sensitive to PDD-87 treatment. These results are generally consistent with the reported activity of dasatinib13,52.

BTK and SFKs, particularly LYN and FYN, play crucial roles in B-cell receptor signaling, and are frequently dysregulated in B-cell malignancies13,53,54. PDD-87 exhibited potent inhibition of BTK along with its major drug-resistant mutant C481S, as well as SFKs, with subnanomolar IC50 values (Fig. 3 and Table 2). Dual inhibition of BTK and SFKs may provide a synergistic effect in treating B-cell malignancies and help overcome resistance to first-generation BTK inhibitors as implicated in the case of nemtabrutinib which is currently in phase III clinical development54. Dasatinib, which has a similar kinase inhibition profile to PDD-87, was evaluated in a phase 1/2 clinical trial for non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, including eight DLBCL patients, however, only limited partial and complete responses were observed55. This could be due to the lack of biomarkers to identify responsive patients or insufficient dosing due to toxicity concerns13,56. Efforts have been made to identify biomarkers that predict sensitivity to dasatinib for DLBCL. For instance, PTEN loss and PI3K activation have been linked to dasatinib resistance, and low CD19 expression in DLBCL cell lines also correlates with resistance13,56. These findings support PDD-87 as a promising candidate for treating DLBCL. However, given the high heterogeneity of DLBCL, its success will depend on precise patient stratification to identify those who are sensitive to the treatment. The variability of drug plasma exposure among patients also contribute to the success rate of the treatment.

Recent discoveries have highlighted the critical roles of SFKs, (EFNB1 and EFNB3) in regulating cellular senescence by modulating key survival pathways that sustain senescent cells31,32. SRC-mediated activation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) leads to p53 downregulation, a pivotal apoptotic regulator, thereby shifting the balance toward senescence instead of cell death. Additionally, SRC activation stimulates p38 MAPK signaling, which enhances the expression of senescence-associated survival proteins while further suppressing apoptotic pathways. This coordinated suppression of p53 and activation of p38 promotes the persistence of senescent cells, contributing to the accumulation of senescence-associated pathologies. Nectin-4 has also been implicated in senescence-associated cell enlargement via the SFK/PI3K/Rac1 pathway, further reinforcing the role of SFKs in maintaining the senescent state. As a key regulator of cell viability in early senescence, SRC signaling prevents apoptosis, and its inhibition has been shown to reduce the senescent cell burden in vivo. Additionally, interfering with EFNB1 expression significantly reduces the viability and survival of senescent—but not proliferating—human abdominal subcutaneous preadipocytes32. These mechanisms allow senescent cells to evade programmed cell death and accumulate in various tissues, driving the progression of numerous age-related diseases, including atherosclerosis, osteoarthritis, cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

PDD-87 potently inhibits SFKs and EFNBs, suggesting it may exhibit senolytic effects similar to dasatinib (Table 2). Dasatinib was discovered to be senolytic through a mechanism-based bioinformatic approach, and was demonstrated to selectively induce apoptosis in senescent cells while sparing proliferating ones32. Preclinical studies have validated its efficacy in reducing senescent cell burden across various disease models; while clinical trials have further supported its therapeutic potential21,22,30,32,57,58. A Phase 1 pilot study in diabetic kidney disease showed that a short course of dasatinib and quercetin significantly reduced senescence markers, macrophage infiltration, and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors such as IL-1α and IL-6 in adipose tissue and skin within 11 days58. Similarly, clinical trials in IPF patients reported improvements in physical function and a reduction in SASP-related inflammation57. Therefore, PDD-87 holds significant promise as a senolytic agent to effectively eliminate senescent cells and mitigate age-related disease progression. Further studies are needed to assess the efficacy of PDD-87 across various disease models and to establish its therapeutic potential in clinical settings.

It is important to note that PDD-87 demonstrated significantly improved pharmacokinetics, including reduced clearance and increased oral bioavailability, resulting in enhanced plasma exposure compared to dasatinib in rats (Fig. 5 and Tables 5, 7)59. Notably, the N-dealkylated metabolite of dasatinib is not able to be formed from PDD-87 due to the replacement of the hydroxyethyl piperazine group with hydroxypiperidine (Fig. 2)47. With a smaller volume of distribution, PDD-87 exhibits a preference for central compartment distribution, which is advantageous for treating hematologic cancers59,60. The enhanced bioavailability and reduced hepatic extraction of PDD-87 could potentially minimize individual pharmacokinetic variability, contributing to more consistent clinical efficacy and safety across patients.

The most significant finding was the marked reduction of lung accumulation of PDD-87 compared to dasatinib (Fig. 5 and Table 6). The ratio of the lung-to-plasma tissue exposure was approximately 3.5 for PDD-87 compared to 22 for dasatinib. Tissue distribution has been recognized as a critical factor influencing both drug efficacy and safety60,61. Drugs with accumulations in certain organs at which the therapeutic target does not reside, are often more susceptible to these organ’s toxicity. Our discovery that dasatinib is significantly accumulated in lung may explain why dasatinib is associated with high frequency of pulmonary toxicity. This hypothesis is supported by the clinical observation that twice-daily dosing of dasatinib is associated with more pulmonary toxicity than once-daily dosing38,39. The exact mechanism for dasatinib to be accumulated in lung is not clear, but may be related to its basicity. In general, basic amine drugs intend to be accumulated in lung tissue by nonspecific bindings with cellular membrane and/or trapped in cytosol or subcellular organelles such as lysosomes, endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. If dasatinib is accumulated to high concentration level and lasts long enough in the lung, it would damage the vascular endothelial integrity by persistently inhibiting kinases like LYN and PDGFR or by release of reactive oxygen species, which would eventually cause plural effusion, pulmonary arterial hypertension and edema40,62,63. Additionally, dasatinib is metabolically cleaved away of the hydroxyethyl group to generate the active metabolite with unsubstituted piperazine which exhibits properties to be highly accumulated in lung tissue64,65. Based on this hypothesis, we replaced the hydroxyethyl piperazine moiety with hydroxy pyrrolidine to generate PDD-87 which showed marked reduction in lung tissue along with the elimination of the possibility to produce the N-dealkylated metabolite, and reduced basicity (Fig. 6 and Table 1). Therefore, PDD-87 should have a lower risk of pulmonary toxicity, potentially enhancing its overall safety profile in clinical settings. Additionally, PDD-87 was well-tolerated in mice during preliminary dose-escalation studies, with no observed toxicity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PDD-87 exhibits a more favorable safety potentials compared to dasatinib.

In summary, these findings establish PDD-87 as a potent, selective kinase inhibitor with improved pharmacokinetic profiles and safety potentials compared to dasatinib. PDD-87 emerges as a promising candidate for further development, offering a wider therapeutic window thus potential for broader clinical applications.
