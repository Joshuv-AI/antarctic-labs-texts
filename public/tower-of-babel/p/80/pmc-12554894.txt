The Autoimmune March: Bullous Lichen Planus and New‐Onset Lupus Erythematosus Occurring After COVID‐19 mRNA Vaccination in a Filipino Female With Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Patrick Antonne Gonzalez, Geraldine T Zamora‐Abrahan, Francisca C Roa, Mae N Ramirez‐Quizon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe novelty of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) vaccines implies the possibility of unexpected reactions. There is also a relative paucity of data regarding interactions between COVID‐19 immunization and preexisting autoimmune disease. That said, we present a case with a known autoimmune illness developing a second with possible association with COVID‐19 vaccination.Keywords: COVID‐19 vaccine, lichen planus, systemic lupus erythematosus, vaccine reaction

Introduction

The COVID‐19 pandemic is an unprecedented health crisis in recent memory. While the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) is not known to directly induce autoimmunity, the inflammatory cascade it promotes is responsible for the systemic damage and severe manifestations observed, occasionally exacerbating known comorbidities [1, 2]. In lieu of this, vaccine production for COVID‐19 has accelerated at an unprecedented rate. Vaccine‐associated reactions are possible in any new vaccine and are paramount in assessing their safety [1]. What follows is the case of a young adult Filipino female who developed an unusual flare‐up of her previously known lichen planus following her first COVID‐19 vaccination and later developed complicating lupus erythematosus.

Case History/Examination

A 32‐year‐old Filipino female was admitted in December 2021 due to weakness. She had rheumatoid arthritis since 2017 and biopsy‐confirmed lichen planus since 2019 with low disease activity maintained on oral methotrexate 12.5 mg once weekly and methylprednisolone 4 mg daily. She experienced nausea and self‐discontinued her methotrexate by October 2021. Her antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti‐double stranded DNA (anti‐dsDNA), and herpesvirus (HSV) serology were all negative at this time.

Four weeks before admission (November 2021), she received her first COVID‐19 vaccine (Moderna). Three days later, blisters formed around her perioral region, feet, and atop her existing lichen planus (Figure 1A). Poor appetite and subsequent weight loss of nine kilograms were also reported. She was then admitted last December 2021. Consent was taken for documentation of her course.

Evolution of the patient's lesions over time, demonstrated on the feet and oral mucosa. (A) Initial lesions of the patient prior to admission showing polygonal, pink plaques over feet and tongue. Nail dystrophy is also observed (B) Emergence of bullae and mucosal erosions over the previously described lesions during her first admission in December 2021 prior to secukinumab infusion. (C) Improvement of the same lesions 3 weeks after the first secukinumab infusion. (D) Reemergence of skin plaques and erosive lesions in the oral mucosa during her 2023 admission.

Nail dystrophy, palmoplantar erythema, bullae, and painful mucosal erosions were observed on admission (Figure 1B). Bullous lichen planus was considered. Initial workup was mostly unremarkable. Of note were the following: mild anemia, normal serum C3 and C4, negative anti‐dsDNA, negative rapid plasma reagin, hypoalbuminemia, mild hypokalemia, and hypocalcemia attributed to poor alimentation.

Methods

Skin biopsy performed on admission revealed acanthotic epidermis with saw‐toothed rete ridges and bandlike, lymphocytic papillary dermal infiltrates indicative of lichen planus (Figure 2). The patient's original skin biopsy from 2019 was near‐identical, with patchy bandlike perivascular and interstitial infiltrates made of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and histiocytes. Direct immunofluorescence (DIF) was not done during this time due to financial reasons, as per the patient.

Histopathology of patient's skin (hematoxylin & eosin). (A) low power magnification (100×) of original 2019 skin biopsy compared to (B) equivalent December 2021 biopsy and 2023 biopsy (C). Note a dense bandlike infiltrate obscuring the dermo‐epidermal junction with focal areas of parakeratosis, acanthosis, vacuolar interface changes with saw‐toothed epidermal rete ridges indicated by the black arrows. The epidermis is absent in the 2023 biopsy sample. Credits to the Department of Dermatology, University of the Philippines—Philippine General Hospital.

Throughout her first admission, the patient's vital signs remained within normal limits. However, her lesions persisted and caused considerable distress despite intravenous hydrocortisone 100 mg twice daily, mycophenolate mofetil 500 mg tablet twice daily, and a dual mouthwash of povidone‐iodine and benzydamine hydrochloride.

All services agreed on secukinumab following disclosure and explanation to the patient based on limited case reports [1]. Secukinumab 300 mg was given subcutaneously on the twelfth hospital day, followed by weekly 150 mg injections over the next 3 weeks. Mild improvement was observed upon discharge on the fourteenth hospital day.

Monthly secukinumab was administered along with mycophenolate 500 mg OD with continued improvement in her general condition with regression of the bullae and erosions described (Figure 1C), enabling methylprednisolone to be tapered to 4 mg twice a week. By January 2023, citing work stressors, she was lost to follow up and discontinued her medications. Shortly after, the recurrence of her skin lesions was observed. She had lost more weight, reaching a nadir of 34 kg.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow Up)

She was admitted 5 months later following malaise and a three‐day history of vomiting previously ingested food, watery diarrhea, and worsening of her skin and mucosal lesions (Figure 1D). Parenteral nutrition was given through a central venous line due to difficulty in establishing peripheral access. Repeat autoimmune workup revealed positive ANA (1.691, cutoff value: 1.1) and anti‐dsDNA (2.346, cutoff value: 1.1). A repeat skin biopsy returned positive for Alcian blue staining for mucin in the reticular dermis (Figure 3). A diagnosis of bullous lichen planus‐lupus erythematosus overlap was made. This was considered as she was already a biopsy‐confirmed case of lichen planus with negative serology for lupus erythematosus in both 2019 and 2021. Consent for DIF was given and revealed a shaggy basement membrane zone pattern positive for IgM in the upper dermis, fibrinogen at the basement membrane zone, and C3 in the perivascular regions.

Immunofluorescence and Alcian blue staining of the patient's 2022 skin biopsy. Demonstration of shaggy basement membrane zone pattern, positive for (A) C3 (B) cytoid bodies on IgM and (C) fibrinogen deposits along the basement membrane indicated by the red arrows. Alcian blue staining (100× magnification) showing blue mucin deposits in reticular dermis indicated by the black arrow (D).

Due to the severity of her autoimmune conditions, she was given high‐dose glucocorticoids and two doses of rituximab 500 mg with initial improvement of lesions. She expired at home; however, 8 weeks after.

Discussion

McMahon et al. [3] reported 414 cases of dermatoses associated with mRNA‐based COVID‐19 vaccines. Larson et al. reported similar findings. The most common for Moderna were delayed local site reactions [4]. Conversely, for Pfizer, urticaria was most common. Most were female and in their thirties to forties, similar to our patient. No mention of bullous dermatoses was made. Kong et al. detailed a 67‐year‐old male with numerous comorbidities who developed painful violaceous plaques with secondary bullae following his second dose of Moderna [5]. A skin biopsy showed full thickness epidermal necrosis suggestive of Stevens‐Johnson syndrome. Another report by Khalid et al. described a mentally challenged 62‐year‐old male with generalized erythematous patches and blisters 2 weeks after Moderna [6]. A skin biopsy revealed only eosinophilic infiltrates. Neither reported mucosal involvement nor were histologically compatible with our patient's results.

The pathophysiology behind mRNA‐based vaccine reactions remains incompletely understood. Tregoning et al. postulated that autoantibody production from antibody‐dependent or pathologic T‐cell responses may occur, leading to a myriad of systemic pathologies [1].

Either of these mechanisms may have contributed to the patient's flare, although causality may be confounded by the patient's other autoimmune comorbidities. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin 17 (IL‐17), and CD20 have been implicated in blistering diseases; thus, secukinumab, adalimumab, and rituximab were reasonable therapeutic options.

Lichen planus is treated with corticosteroids and immunosuppressants such as calcineurin inhibitors [7, 8]. Owing to the T cell‐mediated pathophysiology of lichen planus, targeting important mediators (e.g., IL‐10, IL‐23, IL‐17, TNF‐ɑ) in patients suffering from lichen planus or its variants has provided targets for biologic therapy. Solimani et al. have rationalized IL‐17 inhibitors such as secukinumab and Th17 cell active agents like guselkumab and ustekinumab for compassionate use in lichen planus, citing improvement after administration [8]. An isolated case was also described by Boch et al. [9], who improved after having been given adalimumab. Rituximab, an anti‐CD20 antibody used for lupus and hematologic malignancies, was stated by Mital et al. [10] as another option for lichen planus in patients with conditions that may serve as triggers or confounders. The results, however, were variable and insufficient in number to produce a recommendation. Caution has been advised on the compassionate use of these agents due to their price and their implication in inducing variants of lichen planus or other lesions following administration [1, 11].

Cutaneous lupus erythematosus–lichen planus overlap is a rare and inconsistently reported entity considered when coexisting clinical and histologic features of the two otherwise distinct pathologies are evident. Jicha et al. [12] have proposed criteria and divided this entity into classic and probable categories, albeit with no clearly defined temporal relation. Their study described two African American women with features of both lichen planus and lupus erythematosus on initial presentation. Another case report by Liu et al. [13] described a 48‐year‐old Chinese female with the first known bullous lichen planus–lupus erythematosus overlap. In contrast to our patient, this case had histologic and clinical findings of both bullous lichen planus and lupus erythematosus on presentation. Said article also mentioned that bullous lichen planus must be differentiated from bullous systemic lupus erythematosus (BSLE) and lichen planus pemphigoides (LPP). Neither the patient described by Liu et al. [13] nor the patient described in this case report showed hypergranulosis, irregular acanthosis, or leukocyte fragmentation vasculitis on histopathology found in BSLE and LPP, respectively. In our patient's DIF, IgG and C3 were not present on the BMZ. Furthermore, bullous systemic lupus erythematosus is not always DIF positive.

In relation to the seeming temporal evolution and proposed trigger, a meta‐analysis made by Hinterseher et al. [14] on autoimmune skin diseases worsened by SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination suggested causality. This included over 52 SLE patients and 19 lichen planus patients whose lesions worsened or were triggered following a COVID‐19 vaccine. This study, however, treated all included dermatologic conditions as independent of one another, did not mention bullous lichen planus, and also included non‐mRNA‐based vaccines.

While still controversial, vaccines for well‐known diseases such as polio and influenza have been linked to molecular mimicry to self‐antigens and subsequent autoantibody generation in rare instances [15, 16]. As mentioned, this is a proposed mechanism behind COVID‐19 vaccines triggering or worsening preexisting autoimmune conditions. A systematic review by Chen et al. involving autoimmune phenomena following COVID‐19 vaccination, and a case series by Akkuzu et al. posited similar conclusions on the central mechanism of pathophysiology [15, 16]. While it appears that SARS‐CoV 2 vaccines induce an adequate humoral immune response in patients with preexisting autoimmune disease, there is a paucity of data on patients with preexisting autoimmune illnesses who later developed another distinct autoimmune illness following COVID‐19 vaccination [17].
