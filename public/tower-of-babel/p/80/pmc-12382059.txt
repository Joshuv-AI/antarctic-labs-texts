Clinical outcomes of neobladder or augmentation after cystectomy in locally advanced colorectal cancer involving urinary tract: case series
Authors: Chunwoo Lee, Wook Nam, Chan Wook Kim, Yong Sik Yoon, Dalsan You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn most cases of colorectal cancer (CRC) with bladder invasion, ileal conduit (urostomy) is commonly performed. However, with increasing survival rates, quality of life becomes a significant concern, making this approach less favorable. Thus, this study aims to investigate the clinical outcomes of patients with CRC who underwent neobladder or augmentation surgery.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the records of nine patients who underwent neobladder or augmentation surgery combined with surgical management of locally advanced CRC involving the urinary tract between 2011 and 2018 at Asan Medical Center.ResultNine patients with rectal or sigmoid colon cancer underwent neobladder or augmentation surgery following total or partial cystectomy. The median operative time was 341 min (range, 208–459 min), and the median urinary tract reconstruction time was 125 min (range, 66–255 min). Early complications occurred in three patients, and one patient experienced a late complication requiring surgical repair. At one year, seven patients achieved full continence without the need for clean intermittent catheterization. With a median follow-up of 56 months, five patients were disease-free, three had persistent disease, and one had died of disease.ConclusionQuality of life is crucial for CRC patients with improved survival rates. Neobladder or augmentation surgery are feasible options for CRC patients with urinary tract invasion.

Introduction

More than 1.9 million new colorectal cancer (CRC) cases and 935,000 deaths were estimated to occur worldwide in 2020, representing about one death in 10 cases [1]. Locally advanced CRC accounts for approximately 10–20% of all CRC cases [2]. In patients with CRC, invasion into the bladder and prostate is relatively rare. Generally, it is reported that the genito-urinary tract, which includes the bladder, prostate, ureters, or vagina, is infiltrated in about 3–10% of CRC cases [3, 4]. The most common location for CRC involvement of the urinary bladder is the distal sigmoid colon, followed by the rectum [3].

Patients with bladder invasion by CRC may present with symptoms, such as hematuria, increased urinary frequency, urgency, or urinary tract infection. In some cases, a fistula may develop between the bladder and colon or rectum, leading to pneumaturia (passage of air during urination) and fecaluria (passage of fecal matter in the urine). Imaging studies, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), can help identify the extent of the tumor and invasion into the bladder. Cystoscopy, a procedure that visualizes the interior of the bladder, can also be used to confirm the diagnosis. The treatment approach for CRC with bladder invasion is complex and often requires a multidisciplinary team [5].

For patients with bladder invasion from CRC, surgery typically serves as the initial treatment, involving the excision of the affected segment of the colon or rectum along with partial or complete removal of the bladder (partial or total cystectomy). Urologist and colorectal surgeon often collaborate to provide the best treatment for patients. After partial or total cystectomy, reconstructive surgery might be necessary to create a new way for the body to store and pass urine. This can include procedures, such as ileal conduit (urostomy), augmentation, and creation of a neobladder. The choice of the surgical approach depends on various factors, including the location, size, and stage of the cancer, as well as the patient's overall health. Regardless of the factors mentioned above, urologists tend to prefer a urostomy because of the simplicity of the technique and the shorter operative time. On the other hand, neobladder reconstruction is associated with a higher risk of postoperative mortality and complications [6].

Currently, it has been observed that neoplasms located in the sigmoid colon and rectum may necessitate a lower propensity for permanent colostomy [7–9]. Furthermore, contemporary advancements in medical science and refinements in surgical methodologies have facilitated surgical procedures that either employ a temporary colostomy or obviate the need for a colostomy altogether. Additionally, as the survival rate of CRC improves, patients may seek a better quality of life (QoL) [10–12]. If a patient with CRC with bladder invasion successfully preserves the anal sphincter but ends up with a urostomy instead of a bladder, the patient may not readily accept this situation. If bladder invasion is clinically suspected and cystectomy is performed but the pathologic stage does not show bladder invasion, the urologist becomes even more embarrassed. This situation makes it more difficult for the urologist to obtain the patient's consent for a urostomy, and as a result, the patient may become dissatisfied with the urologist who performed a urostomy.

Neobladder or augmentation surgery assists the patients in leading a more comfortable daily life. But currently, there is no universally agreed-upon method for genitourinary reconstructive surgery when CRC spreads to the urinary tract. Thus, this study aims to investigate the clinical outcomes of patients with CRC who underwent neobladder or augmentation surgery.

Materials and methods

The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Asan Medical Center (No. 2023–1356), and it was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of the Asan Medical Center waived the requirement for obtaining informed consent due to the retrospective design of the study. We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of nine neobladder or augmentation surgeries combined with surgical management of locally advanced CRC involving the urinary tract performed by a single urologist (DY) between 2011 and 2018. The indications for neobladder or augmentation surgery included the following: 1) direct involvement of the genito-urinary tract on radiography, 2) no evidence of distant metastasis or complete resection of metastatic lesions, 3) no history of preoperative radiation therapy, and 4) no significant comorbidities that would contraindicate surgery. The patients underwent total cystectomy followed by neobladder surgery using standard techniques. The patients received partial cystectomy followed by augmentation. Pathologic staging was performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (8th ed. 2017). The urodynamic study to evaluate the function of neobladder or augmented bladder was performed approximately 1 year after surgery, with a median (range) follow-up period of 14 (9–17) months. In addition, continence after surgery and presence or absence of medications related to urination were evaluated. The oncological follow-up schedule included chest radiography and abdomino-pelvic CT every six months for two years, and annually thereafter. The diagnosis of disease recurrence was based on the radiological findings, and it was confirmed by a biopsy if required. We evaluated the surgical procedure, reconstruction of the urinary tract, complication, surgical histologic subtype, and pathologic stage.

Surgical technique

According to the degree of invasion observed during surgery, en bloc resection was performed as much as possible by a single surgeon. The method for reconstructing damaged bladder tissue was determined by the urologist based on defect size at the time of surgery (W, U, or I type). If the ureteral length was insufficient due to ureteral invasion, a chimney was constructed and either neobladder or augmentation surgery was performed. The length of the resected small intestine ranged from approximately 25 to 55 cm, with a chimney measuring about 5–7.5 cm and a pouch segment measuring 10–40 cm. Results

A total of nine patients underwent neobladder or augmentation surgery after total or partial cystectomy for rectal or sigmoid colon cancer. Table 1 provides the clinical characteristics of these nine patients diagnosed with rectal or sigmoid colon cancer. The median (range) age of the enrolled patients was 58 (45–71) years, and they included seven men and two women. Sigmoid colon cancer was the most common, followed by recto-sigmoid cancer and rectal cancer. Bladder was the most frequently involved organ in the genito-urinary tract, followed by uterus and ovary. All of nine patients were diagnosed with the cT4b clinical stage. Out of nine patients, three patients had clinical lymph node metastases. Two patients had clinical metastatic lesions before surgery and they underwent complete metastasectomy.

Table 2 provides a comprehensive overview of treatment of CRC and urinary tract, complication, and pathological stage for each of these nine patients. Out of these nine patients, two patients underwent total cystectomy, prostate enucleation, and neobladder surgery. Seven patients underwent partial cystectomy and augmentation surgery. Four patients underwent ureterectomy due to suspected ureteral invasion. The median (range) total operative and urinary tract reconstruction time were 341 (208–459) and 125 (66–255) minutes, respectively. The median (range) estimated blood loss was 255 (20–1500) mL. Early complications were observed in three patients within 30 days after surgery. Patient No. 4 experienced a wound dehiscence requiring surgical wound closure on local anesthesia. Patient No. 5 developed a vesico-vaginal fistula 14 days after surgery, for which closure was recommended. However, she declined surgery and was managed with a Foley catheter for 5 months. Patient No. 8 was diagnosed with anastomotic site leakage 14 days after surgery and was treated conservatively with a Foley catheter for 1 month. Late complication occurring more than 30 days after surgery was observed in one patient. Patient No. 3 developed a vesico-vaginal fistula 15 months postoperatively and underwent surgical repair. Out of nine patients with suspected bladder involvement on radiography, three patients did not have bladder invasion according to the pathological stage.

Table 3 provides details on the continence status of these nine patients approximately 1 year after surgery. Seven patients achieved day and night continence (indicated as +/+), and two patients did not achieve day and night continence. In patients who had a large amount of residual urine after surgery, we gave medication and watched their progress instead of starting clean intermittent catheterization (CIC). During follow-up after medication, we considered initiating CIC if residual urine persistently remained ≥ 400 mL and signs of renal function deterioration or damage due to vesicoureteral reflux were observed, particularly when the estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) showed a gradual decline from baseline. The median (range) follow-up period was 56 (29–146) months. Discussion

Historically, CRC has been more commonly diagnosed in older adults. However, recent studies have shown an increase in the number of younger adults being diagnosed with this type of cancer. The general 5-year relative survival rate for regional stage (T4bN0 or T4bN1) CRC has improved to 35–76% [13–15]. Based on the aforementioned considerations, it is important to include a neobladder or augmentation surgery during surgical treatment of CRC patients with bladder invasion. But, urologists often perform a urostomy due to various factors. These factors include extended duration of urological and reconstructive surgeries, physician fatigue, scheduling conflicts between urology and surgery, and the overall condition of the patient. Additionally, to date, a universally accepted strategy for genito-urinary reconstructive surgery for CRC infiltrating the urinary tract has not yet been defined.

Urologists might regret later if they decide to perform a urostomy without thinking over it. Modern surgical techniques, including sphincter-preserving and minimally invasive procedures, have decreased the reliance on permanent stomas for recto-sigmoid colon cancer patients [16]. Neoadjuvant therapies, which shrink the tumors before surgery, also play a role in reducing the need for stomas [17]. In this study, patient No.9 received neoadjuvant chemotherapy to actively avoid stoma and preserve the anal sphincter. In this situation, performing urostomy is burdensome for the urologist and can cause great disappointment to the patient after surgery. Therefore, we performed augmentation surgery to address this concern. Patient preferences and improved postoperative care further drive this trend. Following CRC surgery, a patient might feel uneasy or self-conscious on seeing a urostomy without the presence of a stoma. In clinically invasive bladder-infiltrating CRC, the histological noninvasiveness of the bladder relies on factors such as preoperative imaging accuracy and the presence of inflammatory adhesions [18]. A definitive determination of invasion is made histologically, i.e., by examining the tissue under a microscope after surgical removal. According to Vuillermet et al. [19] histological analysis revealed bladder adherence in 53% of the cases and bladder invasion in the remaining 47% of cases. Winter et al. [18] reported that, despite the clinical indications of bladder infiltration, histological staging ultimately revealed bladder invasion in 54% of the cases. In their study, four of the ten patients who underwent total cystectomy showed inflammation, perforation, and abscess, but no bladder infiltration. In our research, six of nine patients showed bladder invasion on microscopic examination. If there is no bladder infiltration in the pathology report after urostomy surgery, the urologists may have difficulty in explaining and persuading the patients who have received a urostomy. As CRC survival rates improve, urologists will hear complaints from patients for a long time.

There is evidence suggesting that a better QoL can lead to better survival outcomes in patients with CRC [20, 21]. Patients with a higher QoL might be more physically and mentally resilient, better able to tolerate treatments, and more proactive in their care. Enhancing QoL has been associated with improved immune function, which may contribute to better control of cancer progression or recurrence [22–24]. Patients with a good QoL might be more likely to follow dietary and exercise recommendations, leading to better overall health. This can reduce the risk of complications during treatment [25]. A good QoL is often associated with strong social support, which can have positive effects on the mental health, reduce stress, and potentially influence disease outcomes [26]. For this reason, several studies have reported that QoL is related to the survival rate in CRC patients [10]. For some patients, especially those with advanced CRC, the focus of treatment might shift from aggressive interventions aimed at prolonging life to palliative care aimed at improving or maintaining their QoL. In urology, the urostomy is a representative surgical method that reduces the QoL [27–30]. Therefore, urologists should first actively consider neobladder or bladder preservation surgery in patients with CRC that has invaded the bladder.

There are several papers on reconstructive procedures following total cystectomy in patients with CRC that has invaded the bladder [31, 32]. Based on these papers, urologists serve an essential role in the surgical management of advanced CRC by facilitating curative resection and contributing to improved postoperative survival and QoL. In addition, it is noted that even if we inevitably perform total cystectomy, a neobladder creation is better than or similar to a urostomy in all factors, including QoL and complications [33]. We have no reason to perform a urostomy based on this fact. However, we suspect that in many cases in which a urostomy is performed, it might be due to the urologist's personal preference or judgment.

Based on the previous results, we should preferentially consider bladder conserving surgery or neobladder surgery in CRC patients with bladder invasion. However, this study has several limitations. The first limitation of this study is its retrospective design and small sample size. Although we did not directly compare different types of urinary diversion, previous studies in bladder cancer patients have demonstrated better QoL with neobladder or augmentation surgery compared to urostomy diversion [27, 34, 35]. These findings suggest that similar benefits may extend to CRC patients undergoing comparable procedures. The second limitation was that the interpretation and presentation of patient cases could have been influenced by the author's beliefs, training, or biases. This could have resulted in subjective portrayal of the case. The third limitation was that the insights from patient cases were often qualitative. Although this offers depth, it can be challenging to perform quantification and statistical analysis. Nevertheless, summaries of this study allow for more flexibility in data collection and interpretation, making it easier to adapt to unexpected findings or conditions, unlike structured experimental studies. This study has an immediate clinical relevance, offering guidance or insights to the clinicians who might encounter similar cases.

Conclusions

As the survival rate of patients with CRC involving the urinary tract improves, QoL after treatment is becoming an important concern. In these patients, if a patient undergoes a urostomy despite preservation of the anal sphincter, some patients may not readily accept this situation. Therefore, urologists need to actively participate in surgical method decisions before surgery and provide patients with information about various surgical methods.
