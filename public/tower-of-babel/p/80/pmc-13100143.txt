Modulation of Pt electron transfer via engineered ultra-thin TiO2-Al2O3 interfaces for coke-resistant methane dry reforming
Authors: Shanshan Zhao, Li Wang, Shuzhen Lyu, Ruichen Liu, Xiangwen Zhang, Rongrong Zhang, Guozhu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dry reforming of methane (DRM) is plagued by rapid catalyst deactivation, primarily due to carbon deposition exacerbated by exposed Al2O3 surfaces in conventional mixed-phase supports. Herein, we construct a well-defined Pt/TiO2-Al2O3 interface by depositing an ultra-thin anatase TiO2 overlayer onto Al2O3 via an in situ growth strategy to eliminate detrimental Al2O3 exposure. Characterization coupled with DFT calculations reveal that the Al2O3 support induces lattice contraction and electron enrichment of the ultra-thin TiO2 layer through interfacial stress and charge transfer. This concurrently activates lattice oxygen (Ti-O) and optimizes Pt charge density, endowing the catalyst with balanced CH4 activation and a heightened CH* → C* barrier. The resulting Pt/TiO2-Al2O3 catalyst achieves exceptional durability, maintaining 91% CH4 conversion at 800 °C for 100 h with negligible carbon deposition, outperforming Pt/Al2O3 and Pt/TiO2 benchmarks. This work demonstrates that engineering a continuous ultra-thin TiO2 overlayer on Al2O3 is a superior alternative to mixed-phase supports, providing a generalizable blueprint for coke-resistant catalyst design via precise interface control.

Introduction

Driven by escalating global energy demands and the urgent need to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions, the development of efficient energy conversion strategies has garnered significant attention. Among these, dry reforming of methane (DRM) emerges as a promising route. This reaction not only directly consumes two major greenhouse gases but also generates high-value-added syngas, which finds extensive applications in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis, hydrogen fuel production, and the synthesis of chemical products such as methanol and dimethyl ether1,2.

Compared with conventional methane reforming routes, DRM offers distinct environmental and process advantages. Steam reforming of methane (SRM, CH4 + H2O → CO + 3H2, ΔH298K = +206 kJ mol−1) is a mature process that produces syngas at the industrial scale, but it requires a continuous supply of high-temperature steam and substantial external heat input. Partial oxidation of methane (POX, CH4 + 0.5O2 → CO + 2H2, ΔH298K = –36 kJ mol⁻¹) can be thermally self-sustained. However, the intense local heat release can create hot spots leading to safety concerns, and it may still suffer from carbon deposition3. In contrast, DRM (CH4 + CO2 → 2CO + 2H2, ΔH298K = +247 kJ mol⁻¹) directly utilizes CO2 together with CH4 to generate syngas without addition of steam or oxygen. Its high-temperature operation also enables effective integration with renewable heat or industrial waste-heat sources. As a result, DRM offers higher carbon utilization efficiency and potential process-level energy benefits, making it an attractive route for sustainable energy conversion4.

Despite the significant chemical and environmental importance of DRM, its industrial implementation remains hindered by severe catalyst deactivation, primarily due to carbon deposition and metal sintering under high-temperature conditions (700–900 °C)5,6. Carbon accumulation not only passivates active sites but also obstructs reactant diffusion, disrupts metal-support interfaces, and undermines catalyst porosity and mechanical integrity7,8.

Ni-based catalysts are widely studied due to their high CH4 activation ability and low cost. To improve their long-term stability in DRM, strategies such as introducing promoters, alloying Ni with other metals, and constructing confinement structures have been explored9–12. However, the inherent tendency of Ni to promote carbon formation and to undergo sintering under DRM conditions remains a major limitation for achieving long-term durability. In contrast, noble metals such as Pt generally exhibit a much higher intrinsic resistance to carbon deposition, which allows for a clearer investigation of coke suppression and interfacial reaction mechanisms under harsh reforming conditions1.

Building on these insights, many efforts have focused on enhancing CO2 adsorption and activation for oxidative carbon removal. These include: (i) introducing oxygen vacancies in reducible oxide supports, which capture CO2 and generate reactive oxygen species capable of oxidizing deposited carbon13–15; (ii) increasing the surface basicity of supports to promote CO2 chemisorption and facilitate the formation of stable carbonate intermediates, thereby suppressing carbon nucleation and growth16–18; (iii) leveraging metal-support interfacial synergy, where active interfacial sites promote CO2 dissociation into oxygen-containing intermediates that assist in coke elimination19–21.

More importantly, CH4 activation behavior plays an equally critical role in determining coke formation tendencies. Uncontrolled deep dehydrogenation of CH4 to carbon species, especially via the CH* → C* pathway, is a primary source of carbon deposition. Therefore, the rational design of catalysts should aim to precisely control CH4 decomposition pathways while enabling CO2-derived oxygen species to actively participate in carbon oxidation. A key factor in this balance is the electronic structure of active metal sites, which governs CH4 adsorption and its subsequent activation. A moderately enriched electron density facilitates the initial C–H bond cleavage while suppressing excessive CH4 adsorption and deep dehydrogenation to CH* or C* species. Tailoring the support composition and interfacial architecture provides a promising pathway to optimize the surface electronic environment, achieve synergistic CH4/CO2 activation, and improve the long-term stability of DRM catalysts. To explore how electronic and structural tuning of the support influences catalytic behavior in DRM, TiO2 and Al2O3 were selected as representative oxide supports due to their distinct physicochemical characteristics. TiO2, as a classical support for studying strong metal-support interactions (SMSI), readily generates oxygen vacancies under reducing conditions, which play a crucial role in CO2 activation and the oxidation of surface carbon species during DRM22,23. Compared with other reducible oxides such as CeO2 and ZrO2, TiO2 offers a more cost-effective, tunable, and moderately reducible platform that enables controlled SMSI with Pt. This tunability enables controlled electron transfer at the metal-support interface, optimizing the Pt electronic state to balance CH4 activation and coke resistance24,25. However, TiO2 alone suffers from poor thermal stability and phase transformation at elevated temperatures, which may degrade catalytic performance. In contrast, Al2O3 is a thermally stable, non-reducible oxide that provides a relatively electron-deficient environment, promoting CH4 activation and enables high initial activity. Yet, its lack of redox capability and oxygen vacancies makes it prone to carbon accumulation and rapid deactivation under DRM conditions.

In this study, we construct an atomically precise Pt/TiO2-Al2O3 interface through in situ growth of an ultra-thin anatase TiO2 overlayer on Al2O3. This tailored architecture enables the Al2O3 support to exert long-range electronic control over Pt active sites via interfacial charge redistribution while eliminating Al2O3 exposure that is favorable for carbon deposition. Combined analyses of XRD, GIXRD, XPS, CO-FTIR, in situ DRIFTS, and DFT calculations demonstrate that the interfacial structure formed between the anatase TiO2 overlayer and Al2O3 effectively modulates the electronic state of Pt by suppressing excessive electron transfer from TiO2, thereby maintaining an optimal adsorption for reactants. Importantly, DFT results show that the Pt/TiO2-Al2O3 catalyst increases the activation barrier for the CH* → C*, a key step in deep methane dehydrogenation leading to carbon formation, and effectively mitigates coke accumulation. Engineering electron transfer in ultra-thin anatase TiO2 overlayers on Al2O3 offers a promising strategy for enhancing coke resistance and improving the long-term stability of Pt-based catalysts in the DRM.

Results

Structure of catalysts

Structures of as-prepared Pt/A, Pt/T, and Pt/T-xA catalysts were well characterized. XRD patterns of reduced Pt/A, Pt/T, and Pt/T-xA catalysts were depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1. The diffraction peaks at 31.94°, 37.60°, 39.49°, 45.79°, 60.46° and 66.76° are assigned to γ-Al2O3 (JCPDS 29-0063), The diffraction peaks at 25.28°, 37.8°, 48.05°, 53.89°, 55.06°, 62.69°, 68.76°, 70.31° and 75.03° are assigned to anatase TiO2 (JCPDS 21-1272)24. Additionally, with decreasing Ti content, the TiO2 diffraction peaks gradually diminish in intensity. When the Ti/Al ratio is 1:10, no characteristic diffraction peaks of TiO2 were observed, indicating that TiO2 is present in a highly dispersed state on the surface of Al2O3 support25. For the reduced Pt/A and Pt/T-xA catalysts, the characteristic diffraction peaks of metallic Pt at around 39.8° (Pt(111)) and 46.2° (Pt(200)) coincide with the main diffraction peaks of γ-Al2O3. In the surface-sensitive GIXRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. 2), these Pt-related features appear as weak shoulder signals, indicating the presence of small, highly dispersed Pt nanoparticles.

To further elucidate the structural relationship between TiO2 and Al2O3, GIXRD was employed to enhance surface sensitivity. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, while no TiO2 diffraction peaks were detected for Pt/T-10A and Pt/T-20A catalysts in conventional XRD patterns, GIXRD revealed characteristic anatase TiO2 diffraction peaks in the Pt/T-10A catalyst, indicating the presence of an ultra-thin TiO2 layer on the surface of the Al2O3 support. In contrast, no TiO2 signals were observed for the Pt/T-20A catalyst, likely due to the TiO2 layer being too thin or poorly crystallized. The XPS sputtering results further confirmed this phenomenon (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). Under the same sputtering conditions, the Al/Ti atomic ratio (Al2p/Ti2p) of the Pt/T-3A catalyst is almost unchanged, indicating that the TiO2 layer is thick. In contrast, the Al/Ti atomic ratios of the Pt/T-10A and Pt/T-20A catalysts is increasing.

The AC-HAADF-STEM images and the corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) elemental mappings of the reduced Pt/T-10A catalyst were further carried out. AC-HAADF-STEM analysis (Fig. 1) reveals lattice fringes corresponding to the Pt (111) and Al2O3 (311) planes, with interplanar spacings of 0.227 nm and 0.249 nm, respectively. However, no crystal planes corresponding to TiO2 were observed in the images, which is consistent with the XRD results. A slight decrease in the lattice clarity of Al2O3 was observed in some regions, which may be associated with the presence of an ultra-thin TiO2 layer on the support surface (Supplementary Fig. 4). The thickness of the TiO2 layer is in the range of 2.6–3.6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 5a). EDX elemental mapping images showed the uniform distribution of Pt, Ti, Al, and O elements. The average Pt crystallite diameter of Pt/A and Pt/T-10A catalysts was determined by HAADF-STEM images (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6), which indicates that the average particle size of the Pt/T-10A catalyst (1.01 nm) is smaller than that of the Pt/A catalyst (1.68 nm). This phenomenon may be attributed to the SMSI between Pt and TiO226, which effectively inhibits the growth and agglomeration of Pt nanoparticles, leading to a more uniform dispersion and smaller particle size of the Pt nanoparticles. The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms and pore size distribution of reduced Pt/A, Pt/T, and Pt/T-xA catalysts were shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, and their corresponding BET surface areas, pore diameters, and total pore volumes are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Among these catalysts, Pt/T exhibited the lowest BET surface area, pore diameter, and volume. In contrast, the Pt/T-xA catalysts display significantly increased surface areas, which can be attributed to the high surface area of the Al2O3 component. Surface and interface properties

To evaluate the reducibility of the as-prepared catalysts and the metal-support interaction, hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) experiment was performed. As shown in Fig. 2a, the Pt/A catalyst showed a single reduction peak centered at 138 °C, which is mainly related to the reduction of PtOx species. In contrast, the Pt/T and Pt/T-xA catalysts exhibited two reduction peaks in the range of 86–135 °C and 350–400 °C. The low-temperature reduction peak corresponds to the reduction of PtOx species to metallic Pt0. The high-temperature reduction peak is primarily associated with the SMSI between Pt and TiO223,27–29. As the Ti content increases, the low-temperature reduction peak gradually shifts to lower temperatures, indicating that the incorporation of TiO2 promotes the hydrogen spillover effect. This facilitates the migration of hydrogen species to the vicinity of PtOx, thereby accelerating its reduction30. Meanwhile, the high-temperature reduction peak temperature gradually increases, and the temperature at the center of the peak reaches 400 °C for the Pt/T catalyst, indicating that the SMSI between Pt and TiO2 is enhanced. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze the surface composition and chemical states of elements. The XPS O1s spectra of the reduced Pt/T-xA and Pt/A catalysts were fitted with three binding energies at ~534.5, 532.9, and 531.2 eV, which correspond to the oxygen atoms of surface hydroxyl groups (OH), oxygen species adjacent to oxygen vacancies (OV), and lattice oxygen of the supports (OL), respectively (Fig. 2b)32,33. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was used to examine oxygen vacancies (Supplementary Fig. 8). In the reduced Pt/T-10A catalyst, a symmetrical signal with a g-value of 2.002 was detected, which is attributed to surface Ti3+ defects and superoxide radicals (O2−) bound to the surface, trapped by oxygen vacancies34. The relative fraction of oxygen species adjacent to oxygen vacancies, expressed as OV/OA (OA = OL + OH) increases from 5.8% to 7.5% with increasing Ti content (Supplementary Table 3). Among the catalysts, Pt/T-3A and Pt/T-10A catalysts have enhanced interfacial interaction between Pt and TiO2. The XPS analysis of Ti 2p showed the presence of Ti4+ and Ti3+ in both the reduced Pt/T-10A catalyst and reduced T-10A support (Fig. 2c). Under the same reduction conditions, the Ti3+/Ti4+ ratio in the Pt/T-10A catalyst is higher than that in the T-10A support, indicating that the presence of Pt facilitates the partial reduction of TiO2. This phenomenon may be attributed to SMSI between Pt and TiO2, which promotes interfacial electron transfer and facilitates and release of lattice oxygen (O2−), thereby generating oxygen vacancies and leading to the partial reduction of Ti4+ to Ti3+ in the support. The Ti3+/Ti4+ ratio in all reduced catalysts ranges from 5.2% to 9.6%. (Fig. 2d) Notably, Pt/T-3A exhibits the highest Ti3+/Ti4+ ratio, indicating relatively SMSI, which may enhance the formation of oxygen vacancies and the reducibility of the support surface. To investigate the electronic environment surrounding Pt atoms, The XPS Pt 4d spectra reveals a systematic correlation between Ti content and the electronic structure of Pt nanoparticles (Fig. 2e). As the Ti content in the support increases, the Pt 4d5/2 binding energy exhibited a pronounced downward shift, indicative of a progressive enhancement in Pt electron density. This trend is attributed to the electron-donating effect of Ti3+ defects in TiO2, which facilitate charge transfer to Pt via SMSI.

This electron transfer from TiO2 to Pt regulated by Al2O3 is further corroborated by CO-FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 2f). CO adsorbed on the Pt/A catalyst exhibited a sharp peak at 2069 cm−1 and a shoulder peak at 2090 cm−1, both corresponding to linearly adsorbed CO on Pt atoms. With increasing Ti content, a red shift in both peaks was observed. This shift is attributed to increased electron density of Pt, extracted from TiOx species, which enhances the back-donation from its d-orbitals to the CO 2π* antibonding orbital, thereby weakening the C–O bond and lowering its vibrational frequency35. This observation aligns with the electronic structure changes revealed by XPS analysis and is further supported by TEM results, which showed smaller and more uniformly distributed Pt particles in the Pt/T-10A catalyst. Furthermore, the intensity of the CO adsorption peaks significantly decreased with increasing Ti content, suggesting a reduction in the available CO adsorption sites on the Pt surface. This phenomenon could be attributed to two possible factors: (1) the migration of TiOx species may lead to partial coverage of Pt sites, thereby reducing the number of accessible CO adsorption sites25; (2) the electron transfer from TiOx species to Pt increases the electron density of Pt, which may weaken CO adsorption36. However, the elevated electron density at Pt sites leads to a downshift of the d-band center, weakening the adsorption strength of CH4 intermediates and thereby impairing C–H bond activation efficiency37,38. The introduction of Al2O3 provides a controlled electron-withdrawing effect, modulating the electronic state of Pt and mitigating electron over-saturation, as demonstrated by DFT calculations. The surface acid properties of Pt/T-10A and Pt/A were further characterized by NH3-TPD (Supplementary Fig. 9), revealing the influence of Ti incorporation on the surface acidity. The detailed explanation is provided in Supplementary Information.

To investigate methane activation behavior and validate the electronic modulation induced by Al2O3, temperature-programmed surface reaction (CH4-TPSR) experiments were conducted on Pt/T, Pt/T-10A, and Pt/A catalysts (Fig. 3). The results showed that the initial C–H bond cleavage temperatures for Pt/T, Pt/T-10A, and Pt/A catalysts were 323 °C, 313 °C, and 301 °C, respectively. Combined with XPS and CO-FTIR results, as well as theoretical analyses of Bader charge and d-band center, these findings suggest a correlation between the electronic environment of Pt and its ability to activate CH4. Specifically, Pt/T catalyst showed the highest activation temperature, which is attributed to its electron-rich Pt state, resulting in weakened CH4 adsorption. In contrast, Pt/T-10A catalyst exhibited a moderately electron-rich Pt state, as the electron transfer from TiO2 to Pt is regulated by Al2O3, which effectively adjusts the electronic properties of Pt and lowers the activation barrier compared to Pt/T catalyst, while maintaining a more stable performance than Pt/A catalyst.Fig. 3Methane activation behavior of various catalysts.CH4-TPSR profiles of reduced a Pt/T, b Pt/T-10A, and c Pt/A catalysts.

Methane activation behavior of various catalysts.

CH4-TPSR profiles of reduced a Pt/T, b Pt/T-10A, and c Pt/A catalysts.

At temperatures below 600 °C, secondary products such as CO2 and H2O were mainly derived from the interaction between CHx intermediates and active oxygen species on the catalyst surface. In this process, surface oxygen or lattice oxygen from the support played a crucial role by oxidizing surface carbon species and promoting CO2 formation. As the temperature increased beyond 600 °C, the generation of CO2 significantly decreased, and methane primarily reacted with bulk oxygen through CHx intermediates to produce H2 and CO39,40. The Pt/A catalyst exhibited stronger signals of CO, CO2, H2, and H2O compared to the Pt/T-10A catalyst. This may be attributed to its d-band center being closer to the Fermi level, which promotes stronger adsorption and activation of methane on Pt sites. The CHx intermediates species can subsequently react with surface or lattice oxygen on Al2O3, predominantly forming CO, with partial oxidation leading to CO2. Due to the weaker oxygen donation capability of Al2O3, CO is more inclined to desorption41. Additionally, the increased H2 concentration may promote the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction, further generating CO and H2O. In contrast, oxygen vacancies in the Pt/T-10A catalyst facilitate CO2 adsorption and activation, promoting the formation of stable carbonate species and consequently reducing the gas-phase CO2 signal. Furthermore, oxygen vacancies facilitated CO2 dissociation and the oxidation of CHx intermediates. The dynamic regeneration of oxygen vacancies continuously supplies active oxygen species, maintaining catalytic activity.

Catalyst performance evaluations

To investigate the impact of interfacial electronic modulation induced by Al2O3 on catalytic performance, the DRM tests of Pt/T, Pt/T-3A, Pt/T-10A, Pt/T-20A, and Pt/A catalysts were evaluated under reaction conditions of 700 °C with a CH4/CO2/N2 feed ratio of 30/30/30 mL min−1 for 20 h. The results were presented in Fig. 4a–c. Additional performance comparisons across a broader temperature range (600–800 °C) were provided in Supplementary Fig. 10. Among them, those supports containing TiO2 exhibit significantly better stability compared to Pt/A catalyst. A volcano-shaped activity-stability relationship for Pt/T-xA catalysts was governed by the interplay of Ti-mediated electronic and structural effects (Fig. 4d). Notably, Pt/T-10A catalyst demonstrated high stability, maintaining its initial conversion levels even after 20 h of reaction. In contrast, Pt/A catalyst showed the poorest stability, with methane and carbon dioxide conversions decreasing by 47.6% and 39.2% after reaction for 20 h, respectively. Additionally, the H2/CO ratio for Pt/A catalyst dropped from 0.88 to 0.56 over the same period. Compared to Pt/T catalyst, the interfacial structure between the TiO2 overlayer and the Al2O3 support effectively modulates the electronic state of Pt by suppressing excessive electron donation from TiO2. This regulation maintains an optimal electronic environment for the adsorption of reactants and reaction intermediates, thereby balancing CH4 activation and minimizing the risk of carbon accumulation. These effects are further supported by DFT calculations. Moreover, varying TiO2 loadings may influence the ability of Al2O3 to modulate the electronic structure of Pt through the TiO2 layer. As indicated by GIXRD analysis, excessive TiO2 in the Pt/T-3A catalyst likely forms a thicker overlayer, thereby weakening the interfacial regulation of Al2O3. However, the lower TiO2 content in the Pt/T-20A catalyst is detrimental to reaction performance, likely because it cannot provide a sufficient interface. Concurrently, the presence of oxygen vacancies facilitates carbon removal. Together with the electronic modulation provided by Al2O3, these features synergistically enhanced carbon resistance and overall catalyst durability. To further evaluate the stability of the Pt/T-10A catalyst, a long-term stability test was conducted at 800 °C under a GHSV of 36000 mL h−1 gcat−1 for 100 h. As shown in Fig. 4e, the CH4 and CO2 conversions exhibited declines of approximately 4% and 1%, respectively.

Figure 4f and Supplementary Table 4 comprehensively compare the catalytic performance of the catalyst developed in this study with oxide-based catalysts reported in the literature for the DRM reaction. Pt/T-10A catalyst exhibited high CH4 and CO2 conversions within the temperature range of 700–800 °C while maintaining a H2/CO ratio close to 1. Under reaction conditions of 800 °C, Pt/T-10A catalyst achieved CH4 and CO2 conversions of 91% and 95%, respectively, with an H2/CO ratio of 0.97. These values significantly outperform those of most Ni-based catalysts (e.g., Ni/CeO2-Al2O3, Ni/Al2O3-La2O3) as well as certain noble metal catalysts (e.g., Pt/Gd-CeO2/Al2O3, Pt/CePr/Al2O3)42–46.

Additionally, Pt/T-10A catalyst exhibited a low deactivation rate constant (kd = 4.25 × 10−3 h−1)47,48, which is significantly lower than those of Ni/TiO2 (kd = 0.110 h−1) and Ni/CeO2-Al2O3 (kd = 0.055 h−1), among other oxide-based catalysts. This indicates that Pt/T-10A catalyst maintains stable performance during long-term reactions. Furthermore, at a lower reaction temperature of 650 °C, Pt/T-10A catalyst maintained an H2/CO ratio of 0.81, which suggests limited side reactions such as methane cracking or RWGS. In comparison, the Pt-Ni@CeO2 catalyst reported in the literature exhibits an H2/CO ratio as low as 0.60 under similar conditions49.

Structural and carbon evolution of spent catalysts

To comprehensively evaluate the structural evolution and carbon formation behavior after DRM, XRD, TEM, XPS, TG, and Raman characterizations were performed on the spent catalysts. All catalysts were characterized after reaction at 700 °C for 20 h. The XRD characterization of the spent Pt/T catalyst revealed diffraction peaks corresponding to the rutile phase of TiO2, indicating that rutile is the predominant crystalline phase (Fig. 5a). Phase transformation from the anatase phase to the rutile phase typically leads to a significant reduction in specific surface area, which negatively affects catalytic performance50. In contrast, for the Pt/T-xA catalysts, only the Pt/T-3A catalyst exhibited diffraction peaks corresponding to the anatase phase, and no characteristic peaks of the rutile phase were detected. Even after reacting at 800 °C for 100 h, no diffraction peaks corresponding to the rutile phase were detected for Pt/T-10A (Supplementary Fig. 11). This observation suggests that the addition of Al2O3 significantly enhances the thermal stability of TiO2, inhibiting the phase transformation at high temperatures. The suppression of this transformation may result from Al2O3 limiting grain growth and stabilizing grain boundary structures24. For the Pt/A catalyst, its XRD pattern showed a distinct diffraction peak at 2θ = 26.3°, corresponding to the graphite carbon phase formed after the reaction51,52. The graphite carbon originates from the accumulation of carbon species generated during methane cracking on the catalyst surface. Excessive graphite carbon can block active sites, significantly suppressing catalytic performance. After the reaction at 700 °C for 20 h, the metallic Pt diffraction peaks became more apparent, indicating particle growth. HAADF-STEM confirmed that Pt/T-10A still contained a large population of Pt nanoparticles below 2 nm, with only a small fraction of larger crystallites. In contrast, Pt/A exhibited more uniformly grown particles with an average size of 3.54 nm (Supplementary Fig. 12). After the stability test at 800 °C for 100 h, pronounced Pt(111) signals appeared in the XRD pattern (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating the presence of relatively larger Pt crystallites. HAADF-STEM analysis reveals that a dominant population of finely dispersed Pt nanoparticles is still retained (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 5). The Pt crystallite size estimated from the Scherrer equation is 10.34 nm, while the volume-average particle diameter (D[4,3]) derived from STEM analysis is approximately 7.21 nm. This discrepancy likely arises because the Scherrer-derived XRD size reflects the largest Pt crystallites that produce resolvable diffraction peaks owing to their limited long-range order, whereas the volume-average diameter is mainly influenced by a small fraction of large particles within a broad size distribution. To directly quantify the accessible metal surface, CO chemisorption measurements were conducted. The results show that the accessible Pt surface area decreases slightly after the stability test, from 4.07 to 3.75 m2 g−1 (Supplementary Table 6), consistent with the sustained catalytic performance under DRM conditions.

An EDS line-scan analysis was performed on the spent catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 13b). The results revealed a strong spatial overlap between Pt and Ti distributions, whereas the Pt signal was relatively weak in regions rich in Al. This observation confirms that Pt species remained predominantly anchored on the TiO2 surface rather than directly on the Al2O3 support, consistent with the AC-HAADF-STEM analysis discussed above. Such spatial consistency in Pt distribution suggests that the interfacial architecture between Pt and TiO2-Al2O3 was preserved during DRM.

Furthermore, the XPS results of Pt/T-10A catalysts before and after DRM revealed that no discernible shift in the Pt 4d binding energies could be observed, while the Ti3+/Ti4+ ratio and OV/OA fraction remained nearly constant (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results indicate that the redox state of the TiO2-Al2O3 support and the interfacial electronic environment between Pt and the support were effectively preserved throughout the reaction, despite presence of moderate particle growth, thereby rationalizing the long-term stability of Pt/T-10A under high-temperature DRM conditions.

TGA has been performed to quantify the amount of carbon formed during the 20 h reaction, as shown in Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 7. All catalysts exhibited weight loss below 100 °C, primarily attributed to the desorption of physically adsorbed water. Increasing the Ti content led to a gradual reduction in the weight loss of the catalysts, suggesting that the incorporation of TiO2 improved their resistance to carbon deposition53. The TGA results showed that the Pt/T catalyst had the least weight loss (0.33%). In contrast, the Pt/A catalyst exhibited the greatest weight loss (5.90%), indicating more pronounced carbon deposition, which could adversely affect its long-term stability.

Raman spectroscopy provides detailed structural information on carbon species with different degrees of order. As shown in Fig. 5c, the D-band at 1342 cm−1 is associated with the defects or disordered vibrations of sp2-hybridized carbon, while the G-band at 1590 cm−1 corresponds to the planar vibrations of graphitized sp2 carbon. Therefore, the intensity ratio of the D-band to the G-band (ID/IG) is commonly used as a key indicator for assessing the degree of graphitization in carbon materials13,54. Experimental results (Supplementary Table 7) indicated that the ID/IG value of the Pt/A catalyst (0.91) is significantly lower than that of Pt/T-10A (1.53), suggesting a higher degree of graphitization for the carbon species on the Pt/A catalyst.

Coke resistance mediated by activated lattice oxygen

During the methane adsorption process, in situ DRIFTS in the range of 1100–2700 cm−1 were collected to investigate potential intermediates involved in the adsorption and reaction, as shown in Fig. 6a, b. Several characteristic absorption peaks reveal the presence of various surface-adsorbed species and their interactions with the catalyst. The peak at 1304 cm−1 corresponds to gaseous CH4, while the peak at 1342 cm−1 is associated with partially dissociated CHx* intermediates (x = 1–3)55. Peaks at 1460 cm−1, 1568 cm−1, and 1643 cm−1 are assigned to monodentate carbonate (m-CO32−), bidentate carbonate (b-CO32−), and bicarbonate species, respectively56–58. Additionally, the peak at 2040 cm−1 is linked to linearly adsorbed CO on the catalyst surface, and the peak at 2335 cm−1 corresponds to CO2 adsorption59–61. Under CO2-free conditions, carbonate species may be generated from the reaction between surface hydroxyl groups of the catalyst and CH3* intermediates, where the formed bicarbonate decomposes into carbonate at high temperatures. Another possible formation mechanism involves the activation of CH3* intermediates by active lattice oxygen, leading to the generation of CO2, which subsequently interacts with surface metal cations to form stable carbonate species. Compared to the Pt/A catalyst, the relative intensity of carbonate species is higher on the Pt/T-10A catalyst, which may be closely related to the presence of oxygen vacancies and their derived active oxygen species. These oxygen vacancies and active oxygen species further facilitate the activation and reaction of CHx intermediates, resulting in the formation of more stable carbonate species. Additionally, the bidentate carbonate species formed on the Pt/T-10A catalyst exhibit a more stable coordination structure, which promotes their further reaction with coke intermediates62–64. At temperatures above 450 °C, a significant CO2 desorption peak was observed on the Pt/T-10A catalyst, whereas only a weak signal was detected on the Pt/A catalyst. This phenomenon can be attributed to the synergistic effect of oxygen vacancies and lattice oxygen in the Pt/T-10A catalyst. Specifically, oxygen vacancies facilitate the activation of methane-derived CHx* intermediates, promoting their further oxidation to CO2. The generated CO2 may also be temporarily captured by oxygen vacancies, forming stable adsorbed species. Meanwhile, the oxygen vacancies in the Pt/T-10A catalyst can dynamically regenerate lattice oxygen through interactions with surface or bulk oxygen, maintaining continuous oxidation of CHx* intermediates. In contrast, the Pt/A catalyst lacks a similar mechanism for oxygen vacancy or lattice oxygen regeneration, limiting its ability to oxidize CHx* intermediates. Once the lattice oxygen on the catalyst surface is depleted, it cannot be replenished through a dynamic process, leading to the accumulation of CHx* intermediates and subsequent coke formation. This observation aligns with the results of the CH4-TPSR experiment, where the Pt/T-10A catalyst demonstrated a stronger resistance to coke accumulation and higher oxidation efficiency compared to the Pt/A catalyst.

In situ DRIFTS were obtained during CO2 adsorption for the range of 1200–1800 cm−1, corresponding to the formation of species in strong CO2 chemisorption, as shown in Fig. 6c, d. The band at 1443 cm−1 is generally associated with monodentate carbonate species, whereas those at 1354 cm−1 and 1570 cm−1 are linked to bidentate carbonate species. The features observed at 1228 cm−1 and 1655 cm−1 are ascribed to bicarbonate species14,65. The relative intensities of the adsorption peaks corresponding to monodentate and bidentate carbonates on the Pt/T-10A catalyst were significantly higher than those on the Pt/A catalyst. On the Pt/A catalyst, no signals of bidentate carbonates were detected. The formation of bidentate carbonates originates from the synergistic interaction of CO2 molecules with two adjacent active sites on the catalyst surface, such as metal cations and oxygen vacancies. This coordination mode endows bidentate carbonates with higher binding energy and thermal stability, which helps to prolong the residence time of CO2 and facilitates the storage of active oxygen species62,66,67. In contrast, monodentate carbonates interact with CO2 through isolated active sites, exhibiting higher reactivity and promoting the rapid conversion of CO268. As the temperature increased, the Pt/A catalyst primarily exhibited the gradual depletion of bicarbonates, and simultaneous decomposition of monodentate carbonates and bicarbonates was observed above 400 °C. By comparison, the adsorption peak intensities of carbonates on Pt/T-10A remained relatively unchanged, indicating that the existence of TiO2 significantly enhances the adsorption and stability of carbonate species by increasing the density of oxygen vacancies. Oxygen vacancies not only provide additional binding sites for CO2 adsorption but also maintain the activity of surface lattice oxygen through a dynamic regeneration mechanism, thereby suppressing the premature desorption of CO2 at high temperatures. These structural and electronic features endow the Pt/T-10A catalyst with enhanced coke resistance and catalytic activity.

Besides, in the in situ DRIFTS under DRM conditions (Supplementary Fig. 15), the absorption peak at 2341 cm−1 is attributed to the adsorption of CO2, while the absorption peak at 3016 cm−1 corresponds to the adsorption of CH469. The vibrational signals of CHx* species gradually weaken as the reaction temperature increases. Methane adsorbs on Pt active sites and undergoes stepwise dissociation to form CHx* (x = 1–3) and H* reactive intermediates. Simultaneously, CO2 is adsorbed on the catalyst surface in the form of carbonates and reacts with CHx* species to produce syngas (CO and H2). The increase in temperature accelerates the dissociation of CH4 and the activation of CO2, thereby enhancing the syngas production rate.

Mechanism for overcoming the activity-stability trade-off in Pt/TiO2-Al2O3

In order to further reveal the essential reason why Pt/TiO2-Al2O3 maintains high activity and stability from the perspective of catalyst structure and properties, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to reveal the electronic regulation between Pt and TiO2 induced by Al2O3. Pt5 cluster on three supports (TiO2(001), TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) and Al2O3(100)) were built to simulate structures of Pt/T (Pt5/TiO2(001)), Pt/T-10A (Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100)) and Pt/A (Pt5/Al2O3(100)), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 16). Bader charge calculation reveals that the Pt cluster carry negative charges of –0.79 |e| and –0.16 |e| in the Pt5/TiO2(001) and Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) models70,71, respectively, whereas it exhibits a positive charge (+1.24 |e|) in the Pt5/Al2O3(100) model (Supplementary Table 8). These findings were consistent with XPS and CO-DRIFTS results. To further elucidate the structure–electronic synergy induced by Al2O3, we performed structural optimization and Bader charge analyses of the representative Pt atoms and their directly coordinated oxygen atoms (Supplementary Fig. 17). In the Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) model, the average Ti–O bond length was 1.92 Å, slightly shorter than that in the Pt5/TiO2(001) model (1.95 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 9). This contraction originates from interfacial strain, which induces lattice shrinkage of the TiO2 overlayer, thereby strengthening Ti–O bonding and enhancing the reactivity of lattice oxygen. Compared with the Pt5/TiO2(001) model, the interfacial oxygen atoms (Ti–O–Al) in the Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) system exhibited a higher electron density (–1.08 |e| vs –1.02 |e|). Meanwhile, the interfacial oxygen facilitated electron transfer from Al2O3 to Ti, alleviating the electron depletion of Ti ions (interface Ti: +1.84 |e| vs +1.93 |e| in pure TiO2). As a consequence, the electron donation from TiO2 to Pt was weakened. Correspondingly, the Pt atoms shifted from an electron-enriched state (–0.13 |e|) to a mildly electron-deficient state (+0.14 |e|).

To gain deeper insights into the influence of electronic structure on catalytic behavior, the projected density of states (pDOS) of Pt d orbitals in Pt5/TiO2(001), Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100), and Pt5/Al2O3(100) models was calculated, as shown in Fig. 7b. The d-band center of the Pt cluster of Pt5/TiO2(001) exhibits the lowest d-band center (–2.16 eV), which gradually shifts upward to –1.60 eV in Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) and further to –1.27 eV in Pt5/Al2O3(100). An upward shift in the d-band center indicates that the Pt d orbitals move closer to the Fermi level, thereby raising the energy of the antibonding states formed with adsorbates, reducing their electron occupancy, and facilitating stronger adsorption and activation72,73. The d-band center of the Pt cluster in Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) lies between Pt5/Al2O3(100) and Pt5/TiO2(001), leading to a compromise in adsorption strength for adsorbates. Subsequently, the adsorption behaviors of the reactant and intermediates on the surfaces of Pt5/TiO2(001), Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100), and Pt5/Al2O3(100) were investigated. The corresponding adsorption configurations and adsorption energies are presented in Supplementary Figs. 19–21, Supplementary Table 10, and Fig. 7a. The results showed that CH4 adsorption was strongest on Pt5/Al2O3(100) (–0.39 eV), weakest on Pt5/TiO2(001) (–0.14 eV), and intermediate on Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) (–0.23 eV). Regarding the adsorption of intermediates, Pt5/Al2O3(100) exhibited stronger binding with CH3*, CH2*, and CH* species, whereas Pt5/TiO2(001) showed the weakest adsorption overall, suggesting poor stabilization of dehydrogenated intermediates. Notably, Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) exhibits intermediate adsorption strengths toward these species, consistent with the trend observed in the d-band center analysis.

Figure 7c illustrated the energy barrier profiles for the stepwise dehydrogenation of CH4* to C* on the Pt5/TiO2(001), Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100), and Pt5/Al2O3(100) model surfaces. In these systems, CH4 first adsorbs onto the Pt cluster sites, followed by successive C–H bond cleavage steps to form CH3*, CH2*, CH*, and eventually C* species (Supplementary Fig. 22). Specifically, on the Pt5/Al2O3(100) surface, the intrinsic activation energy for the initial C–H bond cleavage of CH4 is 0.23 eV, and the subsequent barriers for CH3* → CH2*, CH2* → CH*, and CH* → C* steps are 0.33, 0.10, and 0.48 eV, respectively. In contrast, on the Pt5/TiO2(001) surface, although the first C–H bond cleavage remains facile (0.22 eV), the CH3* → CH2* step faces a substantially increased barrier (1.35 eV). The subsequent CH2* → CH* and CH* → C* steps also encounter high barriers (0.63 and 1.12 eV, respectively). The higher dehydrogenation barrier of CH4 and intermediates on Pt/TiO2 explains its lower initial activity than that of Pt5/Al2O3(100).

Notably, on the Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) surface, the activation energy for the first C–H bond cleavage remains low (0.24 eV), the energy barriers for the CH3* → CH2*, CH2* → CH*, and CH* → C* steps increased significantly to 0.87, 0.34, and 0.82 eV, respectively. This indicated that the Pt sites modulated by the TiO2-Al2O3 interface can retain initial activity while substantially suppressing deep dehydrogenation. Taken together with the structural and Bader charge analyses, the dual effects of strain-induced lattice contraction and interfacial oxygen-mediated charge redistribution by Al2O3 both activate TiO2 lattice oxygen to suppress carbon deposition and optimize the electron density of Pt clusters, delivering a balanced trade-off between activity and coke resistance on Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100).

To further clarify the role of oxygen species generated from CO2 dissociation in carbon oxidation, we calculated the energy barriers for CO2 dissociation and the subsequent C–O coupling reactions on Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) and Pt5/Al2O3(100) models (Supplementary Fig. 23).

In the Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) model, the O atom released from CO2 dissociation at an interfacial Pt site preferentially fills an oxygen vacancy on the TiO2 surface, forming lattice oxygen. A lattice oxygen atom located near the interface can then react with a neighboring C* to produce CO, while simultaneously regenerating the oxygen vacancy, thereby establishing a dynamic cycle between oxygen vacancies and lattice oxygen, which resembles a Mars-van Krevelen-type oxidation mechanism74. In contrast, in the Pt5/Al2O3(100) model, the O atom produced from CO2 dissociation directly oxidizes the surface C* to form CO.

The calculated activation barriers for CO2 dissociation and the subsequent oxidation step on Pt5/TiO2(001)-Al2O3(100) are 0.66 eV and 0.97 eV, respectively, which are significantly lower than those on Pt5/Al2O3(100) (0.88 eV and 1.91 eV). Combined with the in situ DRIFTS results, these findings suggest that the dynamic cycle between oxygen vacancies and lattice oxygen enables continuous regeneration of active oxygen species during the reaction, thereby promoting the oxidation of carbon deposits. This beneficial behavior is attributed to the ultra-thin anatase TiO2 overlayer, which provides accessible vacancies together with mobile lattice oxygen.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a Pt/TiO2-Al2O3 catalyst by employing an in situ growth method to construct an ultra-thin anatase TiO2 overlayer on Al2O3, followed by Pt deposition via incipient wetness impregnation. Structural and spectroscopic characterizations combined with DFT calculations reveal that the interface between TiO2 and Al2O3, through strain-induced lattice contraction and Ti–O–Al electronic modulation, optimizes the electronic environment of Pt clusters. This interfacial engineering enhances CH4 C–H bond activation while increasing the dehydrogenation energy barrier of the critical CH* → C* step, suppressing the formation of surface carbon species. As a result, the Pt/TiO2-Al2O3 catalyst exhibits excellent coke resistance and long-term stability in DRM. Under reaction conditions at 800 °C for 100 h, CH4 conversion remained around 91% with an H2/CO ratio close to 0.97, showing negligible deactivation and highlighting the effectiveness of interfacial electronic modulation for designing thermally stable, coke-resistant catalysts.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

The TiO2 and TiO2-Al2O3 supports were synthesized through a one-pot hydrothermal method. Under magnetic stirring, Tetrabutyl titanate [Ti(OC4H9)4, TBT] was added to a solution containing glycerol and ethanol, followed by the addition of an appropriate amount of Al2O3. After stirring for 1 h, the solution was transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 24 h. The resulting white powder was then collected, washed several times with anhydrous ethanol, and dried overnight at 60 °C. Finally, the powder was calcined at 400 °C for 1 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. The nominal molar ratios utilized were TBT/glycerol/ethanol = 1:100:60. The solids prepared were named T-xA, where x is the atomic ratio of Al2O3/TiO2. In this experiment, four supports, T, T-3A, T-10A, and T-20A, were prepared separately.

The Pt catalysts were prepared by incipient wetness impregnation (IWI) using Al2O3, TiO2, or T-xA as supports. Chloroplatinic acid hexahydrate (H2PtCl6·6H2O, Pt content 37%) was used as the Pt precursor. Prior to impregnation, the pore volume of each support was determined by measuring its saturated water uptake. The resulting pore volumes were 0.83, 1.62, 1.80, 1.72, and 1.74 mL g−1 for TiO2, T-3A, T-10A, T-20A, and Al2O3, respectively. For each catalyst, 1 g of support and an aqueous H2PtCl6·6H2O solution with a concentration of 100 mg mL−1 were employed. To achieve a nominal platinum loading of 3 wt% (Supplementary Fig. 24), 835 μL of the H2PtCl6·6H2O solution was first transferred into a 5 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of deionized water to adjust the total solution volume to match the pore volume of the corresponding support. Specifically, additional water volumes of 785, 965, 885, and 905 μL were added for T-3A, T-10A, T-20A, and Al2O3, respectively. The resulting solutions were ultrasonicated for 10 min until a homogeneous solution was obtained. The impregnation solution was then added dropwise to the support under continuous manual stirring with a glass rod to ensure uniform distribution of the precursor throughout the pore network. After impregnation, the samples were kept under static conditions at room temperature for 2 h. The impregnated solids were subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 10 h, followed by calcination in a muffle furnace at 350 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 2 °C min−1 under static air. The resulting catalysts were denoted as Pt/A, Pt/T, and Pt/T-xA, respectively.

Catalytic characterization

The reduced, fresh, and spent catalysts were characterized by using X-ray diffraction (XRD), grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM). The specific area and pore’s structure were obtained by nitrogen adsorption-desorption techniques on Micromertics ASAP 2460. H2 temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), EPR, the DRIFTS experiments of CO adsorption (CO-DRIFTS), NH3 temperature programmed desorption (NH3-TPD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and Raman. For the DRM mechanism study, the temperature-programmed surface reaction of methane (CH4-TPSR), in situ DRIFTS was conducted. The details of characterization techniques and procedures, including the DFT method, are given in Supplementary Information.
