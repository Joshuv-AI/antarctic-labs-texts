Use of the Peptide Barcode Method for Efficient Screening of mRNA Expression Control Sequence
Authors: Shun Kumano, Kazuki Tanaka, Akihiro Nojima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The precise control of specific protein expression in target cells remains a critical challenge for the advancement of messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics. While incorporating regulatory sequences, such as microRNA-responsive RNA switches, is a promising approach for cell-type-specific targeting, optimizing these sequences requires extensive and time-consuming screening using conventional evaluation methods. In this study, we applied a multiplexed evaluation methodology utilizing peptide barcodes coupled with liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to improve the screening and design efficiency of mRNA expression control sequences. We designed mRNAs equipped with cell-specific expression control sequences and fused unique peptide barcodes to the encoded target proteins. By comparing conventional individual transfections with simultaneous co-transfections, we demonstrated that our LC-MS-based method can successfully distinguish and quantify protein expression derived from individual mRNA candidates within a single mixed sample. The simultaneous evaluation using peptide barcodes yielded results equivalent to the individual evaluations but required a reduced number of samples. This multiplexed technology would accelerate the screening process for regulatory sequences and is expected to enhance the development of mRNA therapeutics with high targeting specificity.

1. INTRODUCTION

Messenger RNA (mRNA) technology has rapidly advanced in recent years, becoming a major platform for therapeutic interventions and vaccine development.1–3) The clinical success of mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccines has driven further exploration into applications such as personalized cancer vaccines and protein replacement therapies.4–6) A key advantage of mRNA therapeutics is their programmability: by simply modifying the nucleotide sequence, mRNA can be designed to express virtually any therapeutic protein within a patient’s cells. However, realizing the full clinical potential of these treatments requires precise control over protein expression within specific target cells and tissues.

To address this, significant research has focused on improving drug delivery systems (DDS), particularly lipid nanoparticles (LNPs). Active targeting strategies, such as modifying the LNP surface with specific ligands, antibodies, or peptides, are continuously being investigated to direct them toward target cell receptors.7–11) However, because achieving absolute cell specificity with LNPs alone remains challenging, an alternative approach involves designing mRNA regulatory sequences to control expression intracellularly. RNA switch technologies, for instance, utilize cell-specific microRNAs (miRNAs) to turn protein expression “on” or “off” depending on the cellular environment using an miRNA-mediated gene silencing mechanism.12–15) Specifically, in a basic OFF-switch, an endogenous miRNA binds to a complementary sequence on the mRNA, triggering translational repression. Conversely, an ON-switch is structurally designed so that the binding of the specific miRNA triggers the cleavage and removal of an extra inhibitory sequence, enabling the target protein to be expressed exclusively in the target cells. While these sequence-based control systems are effective, optimizing the regulatory elements requires screening a vast number of candidate sequences across multiple target cell types. Conventional optimization relies on trial-and-error and individual evaluations (e.g., fluorescence detection). These methods are time-consuming and resource-intensive, requiring sample preparations proportional to every combination of candidate sequence and cell type.

To overcome these throughput limitations, multiplexed evaluation using mass spectrometry (MS) offers a practical solution. We previously developed a high-throughput method that couples peptide barcoding with liquid chromatography-MS (LC-MS) to simultaneously evaluate multiple mRNA sequences and their resulting protein expression levels.16,17) In this approach, unique 10- to 20-amino-acid peptide barcodes are fused to the target proteins encoded by candidate mRNAs. Following cellular expression, these barcodes are enzymatically cleaved and quantified via LC-MS. This enables the simultaneous evaluation of multiple mRNAs in a single measurement, drastically reducing the experimental burden.

In this study, we applied this peptide barcoding method to accelerate the design of mRNA regulatory sequences. As a proof of concept, we addressed the bottlenecks in RNA switch screening. Conventionally, evaluating N candidate miRNAs across M target cell types requires individual transfections, resulting in N × M sample preparations. By co-transfecting multiple mRNA candidates into a single cell type and distinguishing their expression via LC-MS, the number of required sample preparations can be reduced to M. The concept is illustrated in Fig. 1. As a model system, we used an ON-switch design to evaluate two candidate mRNAs (mRNA_ON and mRNA_Shuffle) containing different miRNA binding sites in their 3ʹ-untranslated regions (3ʹ UTRs) across two cell lines (HeLa and 293FT).14) mRNA_ON was designed to express the target protein in HeLa cells but not in 293FT cells, whereas mRNA_Shuffle was designed to suppress expression in both. We constructed plasmids encoding a target protein fused with a C-terminal peptide barcode, ensuring the barcode sequences differed between mRNA_ON and mRNA_Shuffle. To normalize for transfection efficiency, we included a control plasmid encoding a different protein fused to a third, unique peptide barcode. After synthesizing mRNAs from these plasmids, the three transcripts (mRNA_ON, mRNA_Shuffle, and control) were mixed and co-transfected into HeLa and 293FT cells. Following intracellular expression, the cells were lysed, and the barcodes were enzymatically cleaved for LC-MS analysis. The ratio of the MS signal intensities of the target barcodes relative to the control barcode reflects the comparative expression levels. If the RNA switch functions correctly, the mRNA_ON barcode will be detected only in HeLa cells, while the mRNA_Shuffle barcode will be undetectable in both cell lines. The primary objective of this study is to experimentally validate this multiplexed evaluation framework outlined in Fig. 1.

2. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

2.1 Sequence design

To evaluate the targeted expression control of mRNA, three types of mRNA sequences were designed: mRNA_ON, mRNA_Shuffle, and Control mRNA. Enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) was employed as the target protein for mRNA_ON and mRNA_Shuffle, while red fluorescent protein (RFP) was used as the control protein to correct for gene transfer efficiency. For mRNA_ON, an “ON switch” sequence containing an miRNA binding site specific to HeLa cells was inserted into the 3ʹ-UTR. For mRNA_Shuffle, a shuffled sequence of the ON switch was placed in the 3ʹ-UTR. Those UTRs were designed based on the previous studies.14) The Control mRNA incorporated α-globin-derived sequences for both its 5ʹ- and 3ʹ-UTRs.

To enable multiplexed evaluation using MS, unique peptide barcode sequences were fused to the C-terminus of each protein gene. The “ON barcode” was assigned to mRNA_ON, the “Shuffle barcode” to mRNA_Shuffle, and the “Control barcode” to the Control mRNA. Those barcodes are identical to those used in our previous study.16,17) Plasmid DNAs containing these insert sequences were synthesized by a commercial gene synthesis service (Azenta, Burlington, MA, USA). 2.2 Synthesis of mRNA

Using the synthesized plasmid DNAs as templates, the DNA regions from the T7 promoter to the 3ʹ-UTR were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the primers shown in Table 3. For the Control sequence, a poly-A tail was directly added during the PCR amplification step. The PCR products were purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and their lengths were confirmed by electrophoresis using a TapeStation system (Agilent). Subsequently, in vitro transcription was performed to synthesize mRNAs using the HiScribe T7 mRNA Kit with CleanCap Reagent AG (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), which simultaneously added a 5ʹ cap structure. Residual template DNA was removed by DNase I (New England Biolabs) digestion. The synthesized mRNAs were then purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), and their yield and length were verified by electrophoresis.

2.3 Cell culture, mRNA transfection, and fluorescence detection

HeLa and 293FT cell lines were utilized for mRNA transfection. The cells were seeded into 6-well plates (NuncTM Cell-Culture Treated Multidishes; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and, upon reaching 70%–90% confluency, were transfected with mRNA using Lipofectamine MessengerMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For each well, 0.2 μg of either mRNA_ON or mRNA_Shuffle and 1.25 μg of the Control mRNA were used. The specific combinations of mRNAs for the eight prepared cell samples are summarized in Table 4. As described above, the Control mRNA was included to normalize transfection efficiency across the samples. Samples transfected with either mRNA_ON or mRNA_Shuffle individually were used to verify whether the RNA switch functioned as expected (hereafter referred to as the “individual evaluation”). Conversely, samples co-transfected with both mRNA_ON and mRNA_Shuffle were used to demonstrate that the characteristics of two different mRNAs could be evaluated simultaneously by leveraging the peptide barcodes (hereafter referred to as the “multiplexed evaluation”).

Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the expression of eGFP and RFP was visually confirmed using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X710; Keyence, Osaka, Japan). The excitation and emission wavelengths were set at 470 and 525 nm, respectively. This microscopy analysis was performed solely for initial qualitative visual confirmation. Subsequent quantitative comparisons were conducted using a flow cytometer (FCM). After microscopic analysis, the cells were detached using trypsin, harvested, and pelleted by centrifugation. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) by pipetting. Each cell suspension was then divided into two equal aliquots, one of which was centrifuged again to pellet the cells. Next, this pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin by pipetting. The fluorescence intensities of eGFP and RFP in each sample were then measured using an FCM (ZE5 Cell Analyzer; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Data were acquired for 10000 events (cells) per sample. The ratio of the mean fluorescence intensity of eGFP relative to that of RFP was treated as a single data point. Specifically, the eGFP/RFP ratio in the FCM analysis was calculated as follows:

The remaining cell aliquot was subjected to another round of centrifugation to pellet the cells. Cell Lysis Buffer M (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) was then added to the pellet to induce cell lysis. The total protein concentration in the resulting lysate was quantified using the Takara BCA Protein Assay Kit (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). Finally, 10 μg of the extracted protein was recovered via acetone precipitation and prepared as the sample for LC-MS analysis.

2.4 Sample preparation and LC-MS analysis

To acquire the calibration curves required for the MS-based quantification of the peptide barcodes, the peptides listed in Table 1 were custom-synthesized (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan). The synthesized peptides were dissolved in a mixture of 0.1% aqueous formic acid and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (5:95, v/v). This solution was continuously infused into the mass spectrometer at a concentration of 10 μg/mL and a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min using a syringe pump, and the spectra were confirmed via full MS scans. For the calibration curve acquisition, an XBridge Premier Peptide BEH C18 130Å 2.5 μm column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was utilized. Mobile phases A and B consisted of 0.1% aqueous formic acid and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, respectively. The injection volume was 2 μL. The chromatographic separation was achieved using a gradient elution profile. The proportion of mobile phase B was programmed as follows: the run started with an initial isocratic hold at 0.5% B from 0 to 0.20 min, followed by a linear increase to 95.0% B at 8.00 min. This concentration was maintained until 10.00 min. At 10.01 min, the composition was sharply reverted to the initial condition of 0.5% B, which was then maintained until the end of the 20.00 min run for column re-equilibration. MS measurements were performed in full MS scan mode. Peak areas were integrated from the extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) for the target m/z values specified in Table 1.

To measure the peptide barcodes from the lysates of mRNA-transfected cells, the protein precipitates were dissolved in an enterokinase reaction buffer containing urea at a final concentration of 1.5 M to cleave the peptide barcodes from eGFP and RFP. The mixture was then incubated with 1 μL of enterokinase at 25°C for 2 h. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding 1 μL of 200 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, and the resulting solution was prepared as the sample for LC-MS analysis. The released peptide barcodes were analyzed via LC-MS under the same conditions used for acquiring the calibration curves. The signals of the peptide barcodes were distinguished from those of endogenous peptides and proteins based on their specific retention times and m/z values. The amount of peptide barcodes in the solution was calculated using the measured EIC peak areas and the standard calibration curves. The quantified value of eGFP relative to that of RFP was treated as a single data point. Specifically, the eGFP/RFP ratio determined by LC-MS was calculated as follows:

3. RESULTS

3.1 Fluorescence analysis of protein expression

The validity of the multiplexed evaluation method using peptide barcoding was assessed by comparing it with the results of the conventional individual evaluation method. Fluorescence microscopy images of HeLa and 293FT cells transfected with mRNA for the individual evaluation are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. The Control mRNA was transfected into all samples, and RFP was detected at approximately equivalent fluorescence intensities across them. In contrast, the intensity of the green fluorescence from eGFP varied depending on the sample. The results of the FCM analysis, performed to quantitatively evaluate these differences in fluorescence intensity, are presented in Fig. 4. Furthermore, the eGFP/RFP ratios calculated from the Fig. 4 data according to Eq. (1) are compared for each sample in Fig. 5. In the HeLa_ON sample (i.e., HeLa cells transfected with mRNA_ON), the detected green fluorescence was more than twice as strong as that in the other samples. In the HeLa_Shuffle, 293FT_ON, and 293FT_Shuffle samples, the green fluorescence was weaker than in the HeLa_ON sample, but stronger than in the HeLa_Blank and 293FT_Blank samples, which were transfected with the Control mRNA only. These results indicate that although the RNA switch driven by the miRNA binding sequence located in the 3ʹ-UTR is functioning, the expression is not completely controlled (i.e., there is some leaky expression).

For all peptide barcodes, distinct peaks were detected at their expected m/z values (Table 1). The calibration curves generated by targeting these specific m/z values are presented in Fig. 10. Linearity was observed for all peptide barcodes, indicating that reliable quantification was highly feasible.

4. DISCUSSION

The further advancement of mRNA therapeutics will heavily depend on the continuous improvement of DDS.7–11) Alongside the development of technologies designed to deliver mRNA to specific cells, techniques for rigorously controlling expression exclusively within those target cells are garnering significant attention.12–15) When incorporating regulatory sequences into mRNA, these sequences must be custom-tailored for each target cell type. Conventionally, screening such regulatory sequences using techniques like fluorescence observation required individual evaluations, necessitating a total number of samples equal to the number of candidate sequences multiplied by the number of target cell types. We hypothesized that the peptide barcoding method could significantly improve this screening process. Peptide barcoding is a technique already utilized in the screening of antibodies and LNPs to evaluate multiple candidates simultaneously.18–21) Furthermore, our previous studies have demonstrated that peptide barcoding enables the parallel analysis of the correlation between mRNA sequences and their corresponding protein expression levels.16,17) In this study, we aimed to apply the peptide barcoding method as a tool to improve the design efficiency of mRNA expression control sequences, attempting to validate the proof of concept illustrated in Fig. 1. To achieve this, we first confirmed the efficacy of the regulatory sequences using the conventional individual evaluation method, and then sought to demonstrate that comparable results could be obtained with a reduced number of samples by employing a multiplexed evaluation approach via peptide barcoding.

For this study, we utilized sequences previously designed to be expressed exclusively in HeLa cells. As observed in the individual evaluation results (Figs. 2–5), the eGFP/RFP ratio was highest when mRNA_ON was transfected into HeLa cells. This outcome was expected, as mRNA_ON incorporates a sequence designed to turn expression “ON” in response to miRNAs endogenous to HeLa cells. Consistent results were also observed in the LC-MS-based individual evaluation using peptide barcodes (Fig. 11). However, HeLa cells transfected with mRNA_Shuffle, as well as 293FT cells transfected with either mRNA_ON or mRNA_Shuffle, also exhibited higher eGFP/RFP ratios than the blank samples. As discussed in the Results section, this indicates that the expression control is not absolute. To further enhance targeting specificity for robust DDS applications, it will be necessary to combine this approach with complementary strategies, such as OFF-switches or technologies that improve the active targeting capabilities of LNPs.

As shown in Figs. 6–8, conventional fluorescence detection during multiplexed evaluation cannot distinguish whether the observed fluorescence originates from mRNA_ON or mRNA_Shuffle. In contrast, as demonstrated in Table 5 and Fig. 12, the peptide barcoding method successfully distinguishes and quantifies the protein expression derived from each respective transcript, even when mRNA_ON and mRNA_Shuffle are co-transfected into the cells. As shown in Fig. 11B, the individual FCM results and the result of the LC-MS-based individual evaluation were highly correlated. A potential limitation of the peptide barcoding method is the quantitative error introduced by differences in ionization efficiencies among the distinct barcode sequences. However, this strong correlation clearly indicates that such differences did not critically disrupt the overall quantitative trends in this study. It is worth noting that a variation in the rank order among the lower-expressing samples was observed when comparing the multiplexed results to the individual evaluations. This discrepancy may be attributed to the potential influence on expression efficiency caused by co-transfecting multiple mRNAs. Although the absolute values of the eGFP/RFP ratios differ between Figs. 11 and 12, the overall trend—where the ratio derived from mRNA_ON in HeLa cells is the highest—remains identical, thereby strongly validating the peptide barcoding approach.

In this study, we prepared two types of mRNA as expression control sequences and evaluated them across two distinct cell lines. The conventional individual evaluation method required the preparation and analysis of four separate samples. Conversely, by leveraging the peptide barcoding method, we were able to simultaneously evaluate the two mRNAs, successfully verifying the efficacy of the regulatory sequences using only two samples. Based on these findings, we conclude that the proof of concept illustrated in Fig. 1 has been successfully demonstrated. However, we acknowledge the limitation of the current study in that the multiplexed evaluation was demonstrated using only a single pair of constructs (mRNA_ON and mRNA_Shuffle). Evaluating only two constructs is inherently limited in fully demonstrating the large-scale multiplexing capability required for practical screening. Nevertheless, our previous study17) has shown the basic utility of the peptide barcoding method for the parallel analysis of mRNA sequences, indicating the potential that this specific approach can be scaled up to simultaneously evaluate a larger library of RNA switch candidates. Moving forward, we aim to fully establish this multiplexed screening framework, thereby continuously enhancing its utility and broader applicability in the development of highly specific mRNA therapeutics.
