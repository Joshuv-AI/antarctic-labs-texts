Clinical advantages of percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy combined with lumbar hyperextension pressurization in the treatment of L4/5 single-segment lumbar disc herniation
Authors: Si-ping Zhang, Min Tong, Jun Mo, Huan-wen Zhang, Zhen-yu Dong, Yi-fei Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) has become a classic operation for the treatment of lumbar disc herniation (LDH), its high recurrence rate remains a major problem that needs to be addressed. lumbar hyperextension pressurization (LHP) can promote the further herniation of LDH. The purpose of this study was to explore the application advantages of LHP in PELD and to clarify whether LHP-PELD is better than traditional PELD.MethodsPatients with L4/5 single-segment LDH who underwent surgical treatment were retrospectively analyzed. 23 cases were treated with LHP-PELD and 28 cases were treated with traditional PELD, and were followed up for at least 12 months. The intraoperative blood loss (IBL), operation time (OT), postoperative hospital stay (PHS), visual analogue scale (VAS), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), recurrence rate, MacNab score, intervertebral disc height (IDH), the cross-sectional area of the spinal canal (CSAC), the cross-sectional area of the dural sac (CSAS), the residual rate of the nucleus pulposus (RRNP), and the residual area of the nucleus pulposus (RANP) were compared between the two groups.ResultsThere were no significant differences in OT, IBL, and PHS between the two groups (p > 0.05). The VAS-Leg of the LHP-PELD group was significantly lower than the traditional PELD group on the postoperative 3rd day (p < 0.05), but there was no significant difference between the two groups at 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively (p > 0.05). There was no significant difference in VAS-Back, ODI, MacNab, recurrence rate, and IDH between the two groups (p > 0.05). The CSAC and CSAS in the LHP-PELD group were significantly higher than the PELD group (p < 0.05). However, the RRNP and RANP in the LHP-PELD group were significantly lower than the PELD group (p < 0.05).ConclusionCompared with traditional PELD, LHP-PELD can significantly reduce the residual nucleus pulposus, expand the area of the spinal canal and the dural sac, and significantly reduce the pain of patients in the short term postoperatively. It has better clinical efficacy and radiological outcomes and may to some extent reduce recurrence rates and the need for secondary surgery.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is one of the most common clinical conditions, with approximately 70% of adults experiencing LBP in their lifetime, and 537 million people living with LBP worldwide through 2017 alone [1]. Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is one of the most common causes of LBP [2]. Although most patients with LDH can achieve good curative effects with conservative treatment, for patients with ineffective conservative treatment, complicated with neurologic impairment, or severe pain that cannot be relieved, surgical treatment is often able to provide faster and more complete symptomatic relief and avoid further exacerbation of pain and neurologic deficits [3]. With the rapid development of minimally invasive spinal surgery, percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) has become a classic operation for the treatment of LDH [4]. Compared to open surgery, PELD can be performed under local or epidural anesthesia, with less bleeding, less trauma, less damage to bone structure, and less impact on spinal stability, which can significantly shorten the postoperative hospital stay (PHS) and facilitate the patient’s early return to work [5–7]. Although PELD has many advantages, its postoperative recurrence rate is as high as 5%–15%, which is still a major problem to be solved urgently [8]. Using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine, several studies have found that the standing weight-bearing condition promotes further herniation of the degenerated intervertebral disc (IVD) or nucleus pulposus (NP) [9, 10]. Although the lumbar spine cannot be pressurized by standing weight-bearing during PELD, we believe that intraoperative hyperextension of the lumbar spine combines anterior-to-posterior application of thrusts can effectively simulate a standing weight-bearing state, thereby promoting further herniation of degenerated IVD. Therefore, we applied lumbar hyperextension pressurization (LHP) to PELD for the first time and retrospectively compared LHP-PELD with traditional PELD in terms of perioperative indicators, clinical outcomes, and radiological outcomes, so as to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of LHP-PELD and, in particular, whether the changes in radiological outcomes were superior to those of traditional PELD, thereby significantly reducing the risk of distant recurrence rate of LDH.

Materials and methods

Study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria

This retrospective study included patients with LDH treated by LHP-PELD or traditional PELD from February 2021 to February 2022. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows: inclusion criteria: (1) L4/5 single-segment LDH; (2) ineffective with conservative treatment for more than 3 months; (3) patients who did not choose fibrous annular suture intraoperatively; (4) extrusion/sequestration-type LDH; and (5) Having sufficient physical strength can effectively complete the LHP; (6) follow-up for at least 1 year; exclusion criteria: (1) previous history of lumbar spine surgery; (2) combination of other lumbar spine disorders such as lumbar spondylolisthesis, lumbar scoliosis, or lumbar spinal stenosis; (3) patients with severe multisegment LDH; and (4) patients with combination of other disorders that could not be treated with surgery.

According to the above inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 51 patients with LDH were included, of which 23 were treated with LHP-PELD and 28 were treated with traditional PELD. All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team, and demographic data such as gender, age, body mass index (BMI), and follow-up time were counted and analyzed in both groups.

Operative technique

PELD

The patient was placed in the prone position and anesthetized with local infiltration of 1% lidocaine or continuous epidural anaesthesia employed. The midline of the lesional spinous process, the level of the intervertebral space, and the height of the intervertebral foramina were determined under X-rays. After the Kirschner’s needle was marked for the path of entry and the entry point, the 18G puncture needle was used to puncture the posterior upper part of the intervertebral foramen along the direction of the marker line. Anesthesia was infiltrated layer by layer in the process of needle insertion. The guidewire was placed and the puncture needle was withdrawn after confirming the position and inserting the guide rod and catheter along the guidewire, and then the visual trephine was inserted. After confirming the position under X-ray, a spinal endoscope was inserted through the working channel. Under endoscopic surveillance, the bone mass was abraded and the herniating IVD was removed, the nerve root was explored and loosened. Local hemostasis, disc ablation decompression and fibrous annuloplasty using bipolar radiofrequency. The working channel was removed at the end of the operation and the incision is glued with biomedical glue (Fig. 1).

LHP-PELD

To ensure the successful implementation of intraoperative LHP, patients are typically provided with preoperative education and guidance. Concurrently, sedative drugs are usually not required during the operation or only a small dose is needed to ensure that the patient has a good intraoperative experience while actively cooperating to complete LHP. The first part of the surgical technique is consistent with traditional PELD. After performing the discectomy and decompression of IVD under traditional PELD, The working channel and camera system of the endoscope remain positioned within the surgical field, ensuring that the LHP is performed under endoscopic monitoring. This facilitates continuous observation of changes in the herniated disc while simultaneously reducing the risk of infection. The patient is asked to place both hands on the surgical bed in front of the chest and keep both upper limbs fully straightened, ensuring that the angle between both upper limbs and the surgical bed is about 70°–90°, and the angle between both surgical bed and the upper trunk is about 45°–60°, with the hip joints and both lower limbs tightly attached to the surgical bed. The lumbar spine was subjected to a dynamic pressurization process from prone to hyperextension and maintained in the maximal hyperextension for 3 s, with an anterior-to-posterior thrust applied from both upper limbs, and the thrust intensity should be sufficient to sustain the lumbar hyperextension posture. The prone position was maintained after repeating the above maneuver 3 times. After completing the LHP maneuver endoscopic inspection and removal of secondary herniated NP was performed. After decompression is completed the working channel is withdrawn and the incision is glued with biomedical glue (Fig. 2). For elderly or weak patients, the procedure is conducted under the supervision of surgical assistants, circulating nurses, and anaesthetists to ensure safety and efficacy during LHP, thereby preventing unforeseen incidents.

Postoperative management

Postoperative standardized use of antibiotics to prevent infection, requiring patients to wear a external fixation brace fixed within 1–3 months after surgery and get out of bed appropriately. Patients were instructed to perform postoperative functional exercises for the rehabilitation of lower limbs and lower back muscles. Postoperative lumbar spine X-ray, CT and MRI should be reviewed to clarify the change of intervertebral disc height (IDH), decompression of the intervertebral space and the residual NP.

Perioperative indicators and clinical outcomes

Differences between perioperative indicators such as intraoperative blood loss (IBL), operation time (OT), and postoperative hospital stay (PHS) were recorded and compared between the two groups. Follow up and compare the differences in the visual analogue scale (VAS), and Oswestry disability index (ODI) of the lower back and legs in the two groups in the preoperative period, postoperative 3 days, 3 months, 6 months and 12 months and compare the differences in the recurrence rates between the two groups. The MacNab score was applied to compare the difference between the surgical excellence/good rates of the two groups.

Radiological outcomes

X-rays and MRI of the patient’s lumbar spine were performed preoperatively and within 3 days postoperatively, and the average IDH was measured on lateral lumbar spine X-rays using Farfan’s method, with IDH = [anterior intervertebral disc height (ab) + posterior intervertebral disc height (cd)]/2 [11]. The cross-sectional area of the spinal canal (CSAC) and cross-sectional area of the dural sac (CSAS) were measured on MRI cross-sectional T2-weighted images. The residual rate of nucleus pulposus (RRNP) was calculated for both groups and the residual area of nucleus pulposus (RANP) was measured on MRI cross-sectional T2-weighted images. Differences in radiological outcomes between the two groups were compared [12, 13]. Radiological outcome measurements are shown in Fig. 3.

Comparisons between the two groups were made using the two independent samples t-tests, and comparisons between postoperative time points and preoperation were made using the paired t-test. Perform a Shapiro-Wilk test to assess the normality of the data, for non-normally distributed quantitative data, the median (P25, P75) is used for presentation. Intergroup comparisons are conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparison of count data was performed using the chi-square test. A difference of P < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance. Perform statistical power analysis using gpower, with power > 0.8 considered to indicate adequate statistical power.

Perioperative indicators and clinical outcomes

The VAS and ODI were significantly improved in two groups at 3 days, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively compared with the preoperative period (p < 0.05). The VAS-Leg in the LHP-PELD group was significantly lower than that in the traditional PELD group of 3 days postoperatively (p < 0.05), but there was no significant difference at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively (p > 0.05). There was no significant difference in VAS-Back and ODI between the two groups preoperatively and postoperatively (p > 0.05). Although the OT and IBL in the LHP-PELD group were higher than those in the traditional PELD group, and the PHS was shorter than that in the traditional PELD group, there was no significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05). Although the excellent/ good rate of MacNab in the LHP-PELD group was slightly higher than that in the traditional PELD group, there was no significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05). The recurrence rate of the LHP-PELD group was lower than that of the traditional PELD group, but there was no significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05), In the power analysis for recurrence rate, post-hoc analysis using GPower revealed that power (1 − β) < 0.8. No complications such as dural tear, intervertebral space infection, and nerve injury occurred in the two groups. Discussion

The main findings of this study were that LHP-PELD significantly reduced short-term postoperative leg pain compared with traditional PELD, as well as having a lower postoperative RRNP and RANP, and significantly increasing the CSAC and CSAS without significantly decreasing the IDH. Although there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of clinical outcomes (VAS-Back, ODI) and perioperative indicators (OT, IBL, PHS).

LDH is mainly caused by the herniated NP compressing the spinal nerve or the cauda equina nerve, which leads to pain and neurological dysfunction, thus seriously affecting the patient’s daily life and work [2]. As a standard procedure for the treatment of LDH, the recurrence rate of PELD is still high, and its high recurrence rate is related to various factors, such as the type of herniation of the LDH, experience of the operator, age of the patient, fatty infiltration of the paraspinal muscles, obesity, and inappropriate physical labor, but all of these factors are causative factors for the re-herniation of the NP. Therefore, we believe that the recurrence of PELD is closely related to postoperative NP remnants [14, 15]. Due to access limitations, traditional PELD may make it difficult to completely remove residual NP fragments that are hidden or free outside the endoscope [16]. A retrospective study that included 10,228 patients found a 4.3% unsuccessful rate of first-time traditional PELD, primarily due to incomplete removal of herniated or degenerated IVD (2.8%) [17]. Due to the increased somatic stress, the area of the IVD in patients with LDH is significantly increased in the standing position, which can aggravate the degree of nerve root compression, and the hyperextension produced by the lumbar pillow also increases the area of the herniated discs [18]. The lumbar spine from the prone position to hyperextension generates a high-stress region in the process of front-to-back dynamic change, and at the same time, the application of anterior-to-posterior thrust can simulate the lumbar spine weight-bearing and thus can produce a kind of front-to-posterior pressure on the IVD. Therefore, we believe that the LHP is capable of simulating somatic stresses in the standing position. In PELD, repeated LHP helps degenerated discs or residual NP fragments to be herniated from the deeper region of the intervertebral space, which increases the resection rate of LDH, reduces the RRNP and indirectly expands the range of decompression, which ultimately reduces further the recurrence rate of LDH. Since intraoperative LHP needs to be repeated three times, thus it will prolong the OT to a certain extent, and its repetitive squeezing effect may lead to a mild increase in bleeding, but there is no significant difference in the OT and IBL between the two groups, thus the LHP does not cause any unfavorable effect in PELD. Due to the squeezing effect of LHP, the LHP-PELD group had a more complete intraoperative NP removal, whereas the residual NP fragments in the traditional PELD group might have caused irritation and squeezing of the oedematous nerve roots, and thus, the early postoperative period of VAS-Leg in the LHP-PELD group was significantly lower than that in the traditional PELD group, and with the postoperative subsiding of the oedema of the nerve roots and the surrounding soft tissues, there was no significant difference between the two groups in the long-term VAS-Leg postoperatively [17]. The recurrence rate in the LHP-PELD group was lower than that in the traditional PELD group, and although the difference between the two groups was not significant, we still consider the low recurrence rate in LHP-PELD a favorable result, because inadequate disc resection in traditional PELD can lead to an elevated rate of long-term recurrence [19]. Furthermore, our power analysis revealed that the power for recurrence rate < 0.8, indicating that its statistical power remains limited, with the potential for false negatives. Considering the small sample size of this study and the short follow-up period, this may be the main reason for the non-significant difference between the recurrence rates of the two groups.

Healthy young adults aged 25.5 ± 5.6 years had L4/5 IDH ranging from (10.56 ± 1.164) to (12.31 ± 1.511). However, IDH decreased with age and degenerative disc disease, and there was a significant negative correlation between IDH and the degree of IVD degeneration [20, 21]. The mean age of the patients in this study was 47.45 ± 12.87 years, and the mean preoperative IDH was 9.97 ± 1.07, which also reflects the fact that with age, degenerated IVDs may herniate under prolonged abnormal stress, which may lead to a decrease in IDH. The change in IDH after LHP-PELD was not significantly different from that of the traditional PELD, indicating that even though more IVD was removed during LHP-PELD than traditional PELD, the IVD removed was already degenerated or between healthy and degenerated, and its removal would not result in an excessive loss of IDH, and thus would not adversely affect the stability of lumbar spine. CSAC and CSAS effectively reflect dural sac and nerve root compression and are important radiological measures of the efficacy of PELD [22]. In this study, we found that both LHP-PELD and traditional PELD could effectively decompress the spinal canal, as both CSAC and CSAS increased significantly compared with preoperative, but the increase in CSAC and CSAS of LHP-PELD was more significant than that of traditional PELD, suggesting that its decompression of the spinal canal was more complete, which resulted in more significant increase in the intravertebral space, contributing to a good recompensation of the dural sac and relaxation of the nerve roots. Higher RRNP of traditional PELD may be an important cause of LDH recurrence [23]. We found that RRNP and RANP of LHP-PELD were significantly lower than traditional PELD, suggesting that adequate LHP during PELD helps the degenerated NP to be herniated from the deeper region of the intervertebral space and reduces the residual NP, thus significantly reducing the incidence of long-term recurrence rate.

In conclusion, we found that LHP-PELD has better short-term clinical efficacy and radiological outcomes, but its difference between long-term clinical efficacy was not significant. This may be attributable to the fact that LHP-PELD preserves greater effective space for the spinal cord and nerve roots in the early postoperative period due to more thorough removal of the degenerated IVD. In contrast, traditional PELD, which involves discectomy of only the herniated IVD, yields less significant pain relief during the early postoperation of nerve root oedema compared to LHP-PELD. In terms of long-term efficacy outcomes, both LHP-PELD and conventional PELD achieved comparable clinical outcomes over the long term, as both techniques successfully removed the herniated IVD. However, the higher rate of discectomy of the diseased IVD demonstrated by LHP-PELD in imaging changes may contribute to reducing the long-term recurrence rate and decreasing the likelihood of secondary revision surgery. Therefore, the primary advantages of LHP-PELD may lie in the early alleviation of lower limb pain symptoms, alongside a long-term reduction in the recurrence rate of LDH and the rate of revision surgery.There are still many limitations in this study, firstly, although we clarified that LHP-PELD could theoretically achieve a better long-term prognosis and lower recurrence rate than traditional PELD, the difference was not significant, which was considered to be related to the shorter follow-up time and insufficient sample size, the advantages of LHP application should be confirmed at a later stage by multicentre, large-sample, long-follow-up prospective studies or randomised controlled trials; secondly, LHP could promote residual NP re-herniation in PELD, but we failed to clarify what magnitude of LHP could produce the optimal effect to more completely remove degenerated NPs; finally, although we confirmed the advantages of LHP-PELD application in terms of clinical efficacy, safety, and radiological outcomes, we failed to analyze it in terms of mechanisms, such as biomechanical testing and finite element analysis, which led to an insufficient level of evidence.

Conclusion

LHP-PELD has comparable safety with traditional PELD, but more significant postoperative short-term relief of leg pain, greater CSAC and CSAS, and lower RRNP and RANP, which can effectively reduce the rate of NP residueand may offer certain advantages in reducing long-term recurrence rates and secondary surgery rates, thus providing a new concept and technological improvement method for minimally invasive treatment of LDH.
