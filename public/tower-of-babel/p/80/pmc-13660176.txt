Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) attenuates TLR4/NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammation in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis: a candidate molecule associated with neuro-pathology
Authors: Ming-An Li, Yi-Zhi Song, Ting Li, Jian Wu, Yue Tao, Rui Hu, Chen-Meng Qiao, Chun Cui, Wei-Jiang Zhao, Yan-Qin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive and fatal neurodegenerative disorder driven by neuroinflammation involving activated microglia and astrocytes, which accelerates the loss of motor neurons. While Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) is known for its immunomodulatory properties, its specific role in ALS pathogenesis has not been fully established. This study aimed to characterize the expression patterns and functional significance of SLPI in ALS models.MethodsThe study utilized SOD1G93A mice to analyze the spatiotemporal dynamics of SLPI expression in the gastrocnemius muscle, lumbar spinal cord, and serum across different disease stages. In vitro functional assays were conducted using siRNA-mediated knockdown of SLPI in BV2 (microglia), MA (astrocytes), and NSC-34 (motor neurons) cell lines. Additionally, recombinant SLPI protein was applied to LPS-stimulated BV2 cells to investigate its effect on the TLR4/ NF-κB signaling pathway.ResultsIn SOD1G93A mice, SLPI was significantly upregulated in the gastrocnemius muscle from the pre-symptomatic stage (60 days) through the late stage (130 days). In the lumbar spinal cord, SLPI showed a transient initial increase but declined sharply by the end-stage; a similar significant reduction was observed in late-stage serum levels. In vitro, SLPI knockdown exacerbated pro-inflammatory cytokine production in all three cell types and impaired the antioxidant capacity of NSC-34 motor neurons. Mechanistically, recombinant SLPI attenuated inflammation in BV2 cells by modulating the TLR4/NF-κB pathway.ConclusionsThe dynamic changes in SLPI levels suggest its potential relevance as a candidate molecule for disease staging. Meanwhile, its protective effects in regulating inflammation suggest that it could be a promising therapeutic candidate for mitigating ALS-associated neuroinflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01572-8.Keywords: ALS, SLPI, Neuroinflammation, Motor neurons, SOD1G93A mice, Oxidative Stress, Astrocyte, Microglia

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal, progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by the selective loss of upper and lower motor neurons in the brainstem, cerebral cortex, and spinal cord (Wei et al. 2024). This progressive neurodegeneration leads to muscle atrophy, dysphagia, and ultimately respiratory failure (Xie et al. 2025). Among familial cases, approximately 20% are linked to mutations in the superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene (Mead et al. 2023). Current therapeutic options for ALS remain limited, highlighting the urgent need to identify more effective diagnostic biomarkers and targeted therapeutic strategies.

A hallmark of ALS progression is chronic neuroinflammation, driven by a complex non-cell-autonomous mechanism. In this process, activated glial cells undergo a deleterious phenotypic transition from a neuroprotective M2/A2 state to a pro-inflammatory M1/A1 profile (Van Harten et al. 2021; Zelic et al. 2025; Philips and Robberecht 2011; Tang et al. 2023). This shift triggers the release of neurotoxic mediators, including IL-1α, TNFα, and C1q, which synergistically amplify oxidative stress and excitotoxicity, ultimately leading to motor neuron apoptosis (Guttenplan et al. 2020). Within this cascade, the pattern recognition receptor Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) serves as the primary sensor of pathological insults. Activation of the TLR4 signaling pathway triggers a robust intracellular cascade that ultimately leads to the activation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), a master transcriptional regulator of global inflammation (Grottelli et al. 2019). Concurrently, this transcriptional priming is essential for the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex, which is a multiprotein complex composed of NLRP3, ASC, and pro-caspase-1. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome further induces caspase-1 cleavage, maturation of IL-1β, ultimately aggravating motor neuron degeneration (Deora et al. 2020; Cihankaya et al. 2024). Collectively, this tightly regulated cascade spanning from upstream TLR4/NLRP3 signaling to downstream A1-inducing elements constitutes a crucial molecular mechanism driving non-cell-autonomous motor neuron degeneration.

The SOD1G93A mouse is the most widely utilized ALS model (Martinelli et al. 2025). Pathological changes predominantly occur in the lumbar spinal cord and skeletal muscles (Wei et al. 2024; Faller et al. 2025). Its defined progression—pre-symptomatic (~ 60 days), symptomatic onset (~ 90 days), and late-stage (~ 130 days)—provides a robust framework for investigating pathology and evaluating therapeutic interventions (Xin et al. 2024; Figueroa-Romero et al. 2019).

Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) is a serine protease inhibitor widely expressed in mucosal tissues, immune cells, and various secretory fluids (Scott et al. 2011). SLPI is best characterized by its multifunctional roles in anti-inflammatory responses (Ghasemlou et al. 2010) and antimicrobial defense. Previous studies have reported that serum levels of the endogenous protective factor SLPI are elevated in patients with acute ischemic stroke (Ilzecka and Stelmasiak 2002). In rodent models of cerebral ischemia, adenovirus-mediated overexpression of SLPI in the cortical region has been shown to reduce infarct size and confer neuroprotection (Wang et al. 2003). This observation prompted us to investigate whether SLPI can modulate glial cell activation to mitigate neuroinflammation in ALS.

In this study, we found that SLPI expression was significantly upregulated in the gastrocnemius muscle as the disease progressed, while in the lumbar spinal cord, SLPI levels were markedly elevated during the pre-symptomatic and symptomatic phases, but significantly decreased in the late stage, mirroring the substantial decline observed in serum SLPI levels at the late stage. Mechanistically, SLPI deficiency exacerbated glial cell activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, while impairing the antioxidant capacity of NSC-34 cells. In contrast, exogenous recombinant SLPI significantly suppressed LPS-induced inflammation in BV2 cells. These findings identify SLPI as a candidate molecule functionally correlated with neuroinflammatory responses, suggesting that late-stage decline accelerates ALS progression. Considering the combination of both peripheral and central pathology, this study indicates that SLPI might serve a potential candidate molecule in ALS neuroinflammation, worthy of further translational investigation.

Materials and methods

Animals

SOD1G93A transgenic mice (B6. Cg - Tg (SOD1*G93A)1Gur/J, stock no. 004435) were originally obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). All animals were housed under specific pathogen–free conditions at the Animal Experimental Center of Wuxi School of Medicine, Jiangnan University, in a controlled environment (24 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 10% relative humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle). Transgenic males were bred with wild-type C57BL/6J females (Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China), and genotyping of the offspring was performed via PCR analysis of tail-tip biopsies. All procedures were approved by the Jiangnan University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. JN. No 20231230m0090715[618]) and conformed to international guidelines for the ethical use of animals.

Male SOD1G93A mice were randomly divided into three groups according to disease stage: pre‑symptomatic (60 days), symptomatic onset (90 days), and late‑stage (130 days). Age and sex matched wild‑type littermates served as controls in each group.

Behavioral tests

Motor performance was evaluated at 60, 90, and 130 days using the rotarod (Techman, Software Co., Ltd., ZB-200, Chengdu, China) and traction tests. Mice were trained daily for three consecutive days, followed by behavioral testing on the fourth day. After testing, mice were euthanized, and tissues were collected for further analysis. Before testing, animals were allowed at least 30 min to acclimate to the experimental environment. Each behavioral test was performed in triplicate, and the average value was used for data analysis. All behavioral assessments were conducted under double-blind conditions.

In the traction test (Wu et al. 2025), mice were allowed to grasp a 5-mm diameter horizontal rope with their front paws, and their performance was monitored for 10 s. We assigned scores based on the following criteria: 4 points for full four-limb grasping; 3 points for both forepaws and a single hind limb; 2 points for dual forepaw contact; and 1 point for utilizing only one forepaw. The final result for each mouse was determined by averaging the scores from three trials, each conducted 15 min apart.

In the Rotarod test (Qiao et al. 2025), mice initially underwent a 3-day training regimen at a constant speed of 4 rpm for 300 s per day. During the formal testing phase, the apparatus was set to accelerate from 4 to 40 rpm. The latency to fall was documented for each animal across three trials, which were separated by 15-minute rest periods. The final data were expressed as the mean latency to fall.

Dataset acquisition and processing

To evaluate the clinical relevance of SLPI in human ALS muscle pathology, transcriptomic data from the public dataset GSE3307 were analyzed (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). In this analysis, expression profiles from 9 patients with sporadic ALS and 18 normal human skeletal muscle controls were retrieved. To avoid user-introduced processing bias, the fully pre-normalized and quality-controlled Series Matrix File, which had undergone baseline background adjustment and quantile normalization by the original depositors, was directly obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The standardized expression values for the SLPI gene were directly extracted from the matrix. Since the GEO datasets are publicly accessible, this analysis did not need additional ethical approval.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cortex, lumbar spinal cord, gastrocnemius muscle, as well as the MA, BV2, and NSC-34 cell lines using RNA Isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme, China). The purity and integrity of the extracted RNA were assessed by measuring the A260/280 ratio with a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (ND-ONEC-W, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Kit (11120ES60, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (11202ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) on a Light-Cycler 480 II system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) to determine relative mRNA expression levels. The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

Mouse cortex, lumbar spinal cord, gastrocnemius muscle, as well as MA, BV2, and NSC-34 cells, were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with PMSF (ST506, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and a specific phosphatase inhibitor (P1081, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The homogenates were centrifuged at 15,616 xg for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (BL521A, Biosharp, Hefei, China). The prepared protein samples were then denatured by heating in a metal heating block for 10 min. Subsequently, 20 µg of total protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk or 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-Chat (1:1000, 20747-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-SLPI (1:1000, NBP1-76803, Novus, USA), rabbit anti-NLRP3 (1:500, 19771-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-TLR4 (1:1000, 19811-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-iNOS (1:1000, ab178945, Abcam, MA, USA), rabbit anti-NF-κBP65 (1:1000, 10745-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-phospho-NF-κBP65 (1:2000, ab86299, Abcam, MA, USA), rabbit anti-IL-6 (1:1000, ab6672, Abcam, MA, USA), rabbit anti-IL-1β (1:1000, 516288, Zenbio, Chengdu, China), rabbit anti-TNF-α (1:1000, 346654, Zenbio, Chengdu, China), mouse anti-ARG1 (1:500, sc-47715, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), mouse anti-GFAP (1:1000, GB12096-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), mouse anti-Iba1 (1:1000, GB12105-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-SOD1 (1:10000, F1214, Selleck, Houston, Texas USA), rabbit anti-β-Tubulin (1:2000, 10068-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:8000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, including goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:8000, BA1054, Boster, Wuhan, China) and goat anti-mouse IgG (1:8000, BA1051, Boster, Wuhan, China). Finally, protein bands were visualized using ECL detection reagent (36208ES60, Yeasen, China), and densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

To collect whole lumbar spinal cord tissue, mice were transcardially perfused with sterile Phosphate Buffer Solution (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.01 M PBS (pH 7.4). The lumbar spinal cord tissues were fixed in 4% PFA overnight. Subsequently, the tissues were immersed in 20% sucrose solution at 4 °C for one day, followed by transfer to 30% sucrose solution until the tissues sank. The spinal cord tissues were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) compound (Tissue-Tek, SAKURA, USA). The embedded spinal cord blocks were sectioned using a cryostat (CM3050 S, Leica, Germany) set at − 20 °C, with a section thickness of 10 μm. For antigen retrieval, the spinal cord sections were immersed in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 45 min and subsequently washed three times in PBS. The sections were then blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min at 37 °C. Primary antibodies—including rabbit anti-SLPI (1:250, NBP1-76803, Novus, USA), mouse anti-Neun (1:200, MAB377, Merck, Kenilworth, NJ, USA), mouse anti-GFAP (1:200, GB12096-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-Olig2 (1:200, 15Z2, EnkiLife, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-Islet-1 (1:200, bs-7532R, Bioss, Beijing, China), and mouse anti-Iba-1 (1:200, GB12105-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China)—were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing off the primary antibodies, secondary antibodies conjugated with FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, A0568, Beyotime, China) and Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, A0516, Beyotime, China) were applied for 30 min at 37 °C. DAPI was used to stain nuclei, and fluorescence images were acquired using an Axio Imager Z2 microscope (Zeiss, Axio Imager Z2, Oberkochen, Germany). For each animal, at least five non-consecutive sections (with a 100 μm interval between sections) were collected for analysis (n = 3 mice per group). High-magnification images were captured from the lateral horn of the spinal cord (the region of interest, ROI) using a Zeiss Axio Imager Z2 microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Quantitative analysis was performed using ZEN 2.3 (Blue Edition) and ImageJ software. To eliminate potential bias, all image acquisition and quantitative measurements were conducted in a double-blind manner, with investigators remaining blinded to both genotype and disease stage. When analyzing the results, uniform thresholds were established in ImageJ based on a fixed brightness/contrast baseline. The percentage of positive cells within the lumbar spinal cord ventral horn was quantified for each animal, and data are presented as the average percentage calculated across sections for each mouse. Antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay

The Mouse SLPI ELISA Kit was purchased from Boster Biological Technology (EK1996, Boster, Wuhan, China), and the levels of SLPI in mouse serum and cell culture supernatants were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a multi-mode microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Synergy H4, USA).

Cell culture and drug treatment

The MA astroglial cell line was purchased from Jennio Biotech Co., Ltd. (JNO-M0088). The BV2 microglial cell line (Cat# GDC0311, RRID: CVCL_0182) was obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and its identity was verified by Shanghai Biowing Applied Biotechnology Co.

Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The motor neuron-like cell line NSC-34 was kindly donated by Guangdong University of Chinese Medicine and was originally derived from the fusion of mouse neuroblastoma cells with spinal cord motor neurons. In attempts to slow cell proliferation and enhance their maturation towards a differentiated state, before confluence, cells were grown in a 1:1 mixture of DMEM plus Ham’s F12 medium supplemented with low serum (1% fetal bovine serum; FBS) and 1 µM RA. After 6 days of RA treatment, their morphology changed and became neuron-like cells with interconnected neurites (Chen et al. 2018; Vitale et al. 2026). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 11965092, Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (A5669701, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Cell Transfection and Treatment: MA, BV2, and NSC-34 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/well in 12-well plates (for protein extraction) or 8 × 10⁴ cells/well in 24-well plates (for RNA extraction) (CellPro Biotechnology, Suzhou, China). Cells were cultured to approximately 70–80% confluence before transfection. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were synthesized by Genecreate Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The sense sequence of the non-targeting control siRNA (si-NC) was 5′-UUC UCC GAA CGU GUC ACG UTT-3′, and the antisense sequence was 5′-ACG UGA CAC GUU CGG AGA ATT-3′. The sense sequence of the siRNA targeting SLPI (si-SLPI) was 5′-CCAGUGCCUUAAGCUUGAGAATT-3′, and the antisense sequence was 5′-UUCUCAAGCUUAAGGCACUGGTT-3′. Transfection was performed using Hieff Trans® in vitro siRNA/miRNA Transfection Reagent (40806ES02, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The siRNA-transfection reagent complexes were added to MA, BV2, or NSC-34 cells and mixed gently by shaking the plates. Total RNA was harvested 24 h post-transfection to assess gene knockdown efficiency, and total protein was collected at 48 h for protein expression analysis.

For recombinant human SLPI (rhSLPI; MedChemExpress, USA) treatment, BV2 cells were cultured to 70–80% confluence after being seeded at densities of 5× 103 cells/well in 96-well plates (for CCK-8 assays, C06030, New Cel1 & MolecularBiotech, Suzhou, China), 8 × 10⁴ cells/well in 24-well plates (for RNA extraction), and 5 × 10⁵ cells/well in 6-well plates (for protein extraction). For subsequent LPS stimulation, cells were first starved in serum-free DMEM for 8 h, and then treated with serum-free DMEM containing 1 µg/ml LPS and various concentrations of rhSLPI (125, 250, 500, and 1000 ng/ml) for 24 h. Protein and RNA were subsequently extracted using the protocols described above for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data are represented as means ±standard error of means (SEM). The unpaired Student’s t-test was used to analyze the differences between the two independent groups, and differences among the three or multiple groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test by GraphPad Prism 10.1 Software (San Diego, CA, USA). The significance levels were defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P<0.001. Quantitative statistical graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 10.1 software.

Results

Progressive motor dysfunction and motor neuron loss in SOD1G93A mice

To establish a baseline for subsequent research, we characterized the stage-dependent pathological progression of SOD1G93A mice at 60 (pre-symptomatic), 90 (onset), and 130 days (late); furthermore, genotyping confirmed that all SOD1G93A mice carried the human SOD1G93A transgene (Figs. 1 A and S1A). While no significant abnormalities were observed at 60 days, SOD1G93A mice exhibited progressive weight loss, hindlimb muscle atrophy, and tremors starting at 90 days, culminating in severe paralysis by 130 days (Fig. 1B, S1B). Motor deficits followed a similar trajectory: rotarod performance declined at 90 days, with both rotarod and wire-hang scores significantly impaired by 130 days (Fig. 1C). Molecularly, motor neuron loss was localized to the lumbar spinal cord rather than the cortex, as evidenced by significantly reduced Chat and Islet-1 expression at 130 days (Fig. 1D-F, S1C-D).

Spatiotemporal dynamics of SLPI expression in central and peripheral tissues of SOD1G93A mice

We characterized SLPI expression in the lumbar spinal cord, cortex, and gastrocnemius muscle across disease stages (Fig. 2A-I). At 60 days (pre-symptomatic), SLPI mRNA was specifically upregulated in the gastrocnemius muscle (Fig. 2C). By 90 days (onset), SLPI protein and mRNA levels significantly increased in both the lumbar spinal cord and gastrocnemius muscle, while cortex levels remained stable (Fig. 2D-I). Notably, at 130 days (late), while SLPI mRNA remained elevated in the gastrocnemius muscle and cortex, lumbar spinal cord protein levels significantly declined—a finding further confirmed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 2D-J). This peripheral upregulation was corroborated by clinical data from the GSE3307 dataset, which showed increased SLPI in the skeletal muscle of ALS patients (Fig. 2K). Finally, serum SLPI levels were significantly reduced at 130 days (Fig. 2L). Collectively, these results indicate that SLPI exhibits dynamic alterations throughout the disease progression and could be considered a candidate protein associated with ALS pathology.

Spatiotemporal correlation between systemic inflammation and the dynamic SLPI response

To investigate the relationship between SLPI and ALS-associated inflammation, we assessed inflammatory profiles in the lumbar spinal cord, cortex, and gastrocnemius muscle. At 60 days (pre-symptomatic), the mRNA levels of the A1-type astrocyte marker C3 and the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 were upregulated in the lumbar spinal cord, suggesting astrocyte activation and increased inflammation (Fig. 3A); at this timepoint, no significant changes were observed in the expression of TLR4 and p-P65 within the lumbar spinal cord (Fig. 3D). While cortical Iba1 was significantly increased (Figure S2A-B), SLPI levels in the lumbar spinal cord and cortex remained unchanged. Similarly, inflammation was already evident in the gastrocnemius muscle, with a robust induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-β, and IL-6) (Fig. 3G). alongside significant SLPI upregulation (Fig. 2C).

As the disease progressed to Day 90 (onset), a synchronous inflammatory response was observed: SLPI protein levels in the lumbar spinal cord increased (Fig. 2D), accompanied by a sustained elevation of the astrocyte marker C3, indicating astrocytic activation (Fig. 3B). Concurrently, no significant alterations were detected in the expression levels of TLR4 and p-P65 within the lumbar spinal cord region (Fig. 3E). In the gastrocnemius muscle, SLPI levels continued to rise, occurring synchronously with the upregulated expression of iNOS and IL-1β (Fig. 3K). This parallel increase suggests a potential correlation between endogenous SLPI and the inflammatory response. However, by Day 130 (end-stage), despite the persistence of spinal glial activation, characterized by increased GFAP protein expression and elevated levels of the A1-type marker C3 (Fig. 3C, F), SLPI protein levels in the lumbar spinal cord exhibited a prominent decline (Fig. 2D). At this point, the levels of TLR4 and p-P65 in the lumbar spinal cord region were markedly increased, indicating a pronounced inflammatory response (Fig. 3F). Despite the persistent peripheral muscle inflammation and elevated SLPI levels, this central nervous system-specific depletion of SLPI suggests a dysregulation of central homeostatic mechanisms, occurring concomitantly with terminal neurodegeneration.

Glial activation precedes motor neuron and oligodendrocyte loss in SOD1G93A mice

To determine the cellular consequences of the evolving inflammatory milieu, we performed immunofluorescence staining in the lumbar spinal cord. At 90 days, despite the robust activation of astrocytes (with increased GFAP expression) and microglia (with increased Iba1 expression) (Fig. 4A, B), the populations of Neun+ neurons and Olig2+ oligodendrocytes remained comparable to WT (Fig. 4C, D). This indicates that glial activation in the lumbar spinal cord occurs synchronously with SLPI upregulation during the disease onset (as shown in Figs. 2 and 3), preceding the onset of massive neuronal death.

However, by 130 days, the landscape shifted dramatically. While glial activation persisted and intensified (Fig. 4E, F), we observed a significant loss of both motor neurons and oligodendrocytes (Fig. 4G, H). Furthermore, co-localization analysis revealed that SLPI co-localized with astrocytes (GFAP+), microglia (Iba1+), and neurons (Neun+), whereas no such co-localization was observed in oligodendrocytes (Olig2+) (Fig. S3A-D), demonstrating a diverse cellular source of SLPI in vivo. Our findings differ from a previous study which suggested that human astrocytes, rather than microglia, are the primary source of SLPI (Suh et al. 2012). These results demonstrate that neuronal loss in the lumbar spinal cord at the late stage of the disease is congruent with the previously observed depletion of SLPI (Fig. 2J). This indicates that SLPI undergoes a dynamic transition, characterized by an initial elevation followed by a subsequent decline, during the persistent inflammatory response. This implies that it may serve as a critical molecular player associated with neuroinflammatory pathology in ALS.

Endogenous SLPI maintains microglia homeostasis and suppresses inflammatory signaling

In vivo, we found that SLPI dynamic changes in SLPI are accompanied by the activation of glial cells and the occurrence of inflammation. To mechanistically explore SLPI’s role in glial activation, we employed an LPS-stimulated BV2 microglial model.

LPS challenge triggered a rapid induction of SLPI, initially peaking alongside pro-inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) at 6–12 h (Fig. 5A-D). Notably, by 24 h, while the pro-inflammatory response had largely subsided, SLPI protein levels remained plateaued and were significantly elevated in the culture supernatant (Fig. 5E, F, and J). To determine the functional significance of this induction, we silenced SLPI using siRNA. Notably, SLPI knockdown markedly exacerbated the microglial inflammatory response, evidenced by the upregulation of the M1 marker CD16 and increased expression of IL-1α and TNF-α (Fig. 5G, I). Mechanistically, the knockdown of SLPI significantly potentiated the activation of the P65 and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways, as evidenced by a substantial increase in downstream IL-1β levels (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these in vitro findings corroborate our in vivo observations and identify SLPI as a critical negative regulator of microglial-mediated neuroinflammation.

Endogenous SLPI maintains astrocyte homeostasis and suppresses inflammatory signaling

We first evaluated the responsiveness of MA cells to inflammatory stimuli. While direct LPS exposure only transiently decreased SLPI levels without eliciting a robust inflammatory response (Figure S4A–D)—consistent with the known hyporesponsiveness of astrocytes to direct TLR4 ligation (Chang et al. 2023)—stimulation with BV2-conditioned medium (BV2-CM) significantly modulated SLPI expression, triggering a more pronounced dynamic response (Tang et al. 2023). SLPI mRNA levels were significantly up-regulated at 6 and 12 h of CM stimulation, accompanied by astrocyte activation and an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 6A and C). However, by 24 h, SLPI expression declined, coinciding with elevated levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and a reduction in the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 (Fig. 6E). Protein analysis showed that SLPI was upregulated at 6 h, downregulated at 12 h, and re-elevated at 24 h (Fig. 6B, D and F). These changes coincided with increased expression of inflammatory cytokines. ELISA confirmed that secreted SLPI was significantly elevated in the MA cell supernatant at 24 h following BV2-CM stimulation (Fig. 6J).

To define the functional role of SLPI under basal conditions, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown (Fig. 6G, I). Loss of SLPI was sufficient to disrupt astrocyte homeostasis, inducing a spontaneous pro-inflammatory transition. SLPI-deficient MA cells exhibited significant upregulation of the A1 marker Serping1 and the cytokines IL-6 and IL-10 (Fig. 6G and I). Furthermore, SLPI silencing activated the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB pathways, as indicated by elevated NLRP3 and p-P65 levels (Fig. 6H). These results demonstrate that endogenous SLPI is essential for suppressing spontaneous astrocyte activation and inflammatory signaling.

Endogenous SLPI maintains motor neuron homeostasis and suppresses inflammatory and antioxidant ability

To investigate the role of SLPI in motor neurons, we utilized differentiated NSC-34 cells, confirmed by the expression of the motor neuron marker ChAT (Fig. 7A). siRNA-mediated silencing was employed to evaluate the functional impact of SLPI loss. SLPI knockdown triggered a robust pro-inflammatory response, characterized by significantly elevated mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-1α, and iNOS (Fig. 7B, D), alongside the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB pathways (Fig. 7C).

Beyond its immunomodulatory role, SLPI is essential for maintaining redox balance. While SOD1 protein levels remained stable, SLPI-silenced cells exhibited a marked accumulation of intracellular ROS (Fig. 7C, E), indicating that SLPI contributes to neuronal homeostasis by bolstering oxidative stress defenses and limiting inflammatory signaling.

rhSLPI inhibits LPS-induced inflammatory response in BV2 Cells

To evaluate the potential therapeutic effect of SLPI, we assessed the impact of recombinant human SLPI (rhSLPI) on inflammatory responses in BV2 microglial cells. A CCK-8 assay confirmed that rhSLPI exhibited no significant cytotoxicity across a concentration range of 125–1000 ng/ml (Fig. 8A). Further analysis showed that treatment with 250 ng/ml rhSLPI preserved cell viability while significantly suppressing inflammatory gene expression, identifying this as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments (Fig. 8B). In an LPS-induced inflammatory model, 24-hour treatment with rhSLPI markedly reduced both mRNA and protein levels of key inflammatory mediators, including NLRP3, TLR4, p-P65, and IL-6 (Fig. 8C and D). These findings indicate that rhSLPI effectively attenuates LPS-induced microglial activation and inflammatory signaling.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the spatiotemporal expression profile of SLPI in both central and peripheral tissues of the SOD1G93A mouse model, elucidating its potential role in ALS neuroinflammation. Our central finding demonstrates a compelling spatiotemporal correlation between the decline in central SLPI levels and the progression of neuroinflammation in SOD1G93A mice. However, whether this central depletion directly drives terminal motor neuron death or is merely an advanced byproduct of it remains unresolved. Furthermore, although our loss- and gain-of-function assays in BV2, MA, and NSC-34 cells demonstrate that SLPI possesses a potent capacity to modulate the TLR4/NF-κB axis in vitro, these isolated cellular microenvironments cannot fully represent the entire pathogenic scenario of chronic ALS.

A notable observation in our study is the biphasic expression of SLPI in the lumbar spinal cord, which increased at 90 days but significantly declined by 130 days, a trend mirrored by systemic serum SLPI levels (Fig. 2). However, its levels in the gastrocnemius muscle showed an early, sustained upregulation. Conversely, cortical SLPI levels remained stable despite late-stage mRNA elevation. This spatiotemporal divergence indicated that SLPI alterations predominantly occur in tissues undergoing active pathological remodeling, which is consistent with the early upregulation of SLPI reported in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells from ALS patients (Lilo et al. 2013). Given that glial activation in the lumbar spinal cord persists throughout the disease course (Fig. 3), the initial upregulation of SLPI likely reflects a reactive response to neuroinflammation. Crucially, however, the late-stage depletion of circulating SLPI must be interpreted with extreme caution. The current data represent a descriptive correlation rather than a validated diagnostic or staging tool. Unlike established biomarkers such as Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL), serum SLPI fluctuations reflect systemic inflammatory dynamics. Thus, combining SLPI with a structural damage marker like NfL might be a much better way to predict the disease progression.

Immunofluorescence colocalization analysis revealed that SLPI colocalizes with GFAP, Iba1, and Neun (Figure S3), demonstrating a diverse cellular source of SLPI in vivo. While our in vitro data confirmed SLPI expression in both BV2 and MA cells, a previous study identified astrocytes as the primary source of SLPI in the human central nervous system (Suh et al. 2012). This discrepancy may be attributed to species-specific regulatory mechanisms. We found that LPS-stimulated BV2 cells upregulated both SLPI and pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 5), positioning SLPI as a stress-responsive regulator of microglial inflammation, likely via NF-κB inhibition (Adapala et al. 2011). Conversely, the weaker response of astrocytes to direct LPS stimulation, combined with the biphasic SLPI expression observed upon exposure to BV2-conditioned medium (Fig. 5), suggests that astrocytic SLPI is indirectly regulated by microglia-derived mediators.

Our functional assays revealed that SLPI knockdown in glia promotes a phenotypic shift toward pro-inflammatory states, evidenced by increased markers of A1-type astrocytes (Serping1) and M1-type microglia (CD16) (Fig. 6). These results corroborate earlier findings that SLPI suppresses NF-κB activation and reduces the production of nitric oxide and TNF-α in myeloid cells (Jin et al. 1997). Given that microglia-derived factors like IL-1α, TNF, and C1q are known to drive neurotoxic A1 astrocyte polarization (Guttenplan et al. 2020; Liddelow et al. 2017), SLPI may serve as a critical checkpoint in this inter-glial signaling axis.

Regarding neuronal protection, SLPI deficiency in NSC-34 cells led to elevated ROS and cytokine levels (Fig. 8), confirming its role in maintaining redox balance and limiting neuroinflammation. This is consistent with the known antioxidant properties of SLPI in models of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury (Mongkolpathumrat et al. 2022; Schneeberger et al. 2008). Interestingly, our results suggest that SLPI’s antioxidant effects may be SOD1-independent, mirroring findings in Drosophila where the SLPI homolog IM33 regulates systemic ROS levels and lifespan (Xu et al. 2023).

Furthermore, rhSLPI significantly reduced inflammatory responses in LPS-treated BV2 cells without cytotoxicity. It downregulated the expression of TLR4, NLRP3, and IL-6, highlighting the therapeutic potential of exogenous SLPI in modulating central nervous system inflammation (Fig. 8). LPS exposure predominantly recapitulates classical TLR4-mediated immune activation. It does not fully mirror the chronic, multi-factorial microglial phenotypes or phenotypic transitions inherent to ALS pathology. Therefore, caution must be exercised when extrapolating these BV2 findings to motor neuron degeneration in vivo. To bridge this gap, future investigations are warranted to clarify whether SLPI exhibits consistent homeostatic or neuroprotective modulatory properties under true ALS-specific toxic insults, such as mutant SOD1 aggregation, incubation with soluble factors derived from the spinal cord of SOD1G93A mice, or in microglia derived from patient-induced pluripotent stem cells. Given that TLR4 deficiency extends survival in SOD1G93A mice (Lee et al. 2015), the inhibition of this pathway by rhSLPI highlights its therapeutic potential as an anti-inflammatory modulator in ALS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the dynamic changes of SLPI in SOD1G93A mice, which may represent a pathology-related factor. Furthermore, its capacity to modulate inflammatory responses in astrocytes, microglia, and motor neurons in vitro positions SLPI as a promising candidate molecule associated with ALS-related neuroinflammation (Figure S5).

Limitations and future directions

We must acknowledge several limitations in the present study. First, although we identified a robust stage-dependent alteration of systemic SLPI in SOD1G93A mice, direct validation utilizing clinical serum or cerebrospinal fluid samples from cohort patients with ALS was not performed. Second, while exogenous rhSLPI exhibited profound anti-inflammatory capacities in BV2 cells in vitro, its therapeutic efficacy has not yet been evaluated in SOD1G93A mice. Consequently, SLPI should currently be considered a candidate molecule for ALS-associated neuroinflammation. However, the generalizability of our in vivo findings is inherently limited by the exclusive reliance on male SOD1G93A mice. Additionally, relying on a single genetic model precludes us from concluding that SLPI dysregulation is a universal hallmark of ALS pathology. Future studies incorporating alternative genetic models (e.g., TDP-43) and both sexes are essential to comprehensively validate these findings. An upcoming phase of our research group’s work will evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of AAV-mediated SLPI overexpression via intrathecal injection in SOD1G93A mice.
