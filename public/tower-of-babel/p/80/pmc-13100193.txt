A dual-branch deep learning framework for emotion recognition from EEG signals
Authors: Debam Saha, Asfak Ali, Vycheslav Gulvanskii, Dmitrii Kaplun, Ram Sarkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mental health monitoring through emotion recognition plays an important role in early intervention and personalized healthcare systems. Traditional EEG-based emotion recognition approaches have encountered significant limitations, including heavy reliance on manual feature engineering, poor generalization across datasets, and computational complexity that restricts real-world deployment. This study introduces a novel hybrid dual-branch deep learning architecture that integrates temporal and spectral feature extraction for robust EEG-based emotion recognition, while minimizing preprocessing requirements. The proposed framework integrates a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to capture temporal dependencies directly from raw EEG signals, while concurrently leveraging Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to extract spatial features from Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) representations. This architecture further incorporates innovative cross-modality enhancement mechanisms, such as inverse MFCC computation and LSTM-to-MFCC projection, which facilitate bidirectional feature learning between temporal and spectral domains. Subsequently, feature fusion is achieved through element-wise multiplication and concatenation, and the integrated representations are classified using an Artificial Neural Network (ANN). The evaluation has been conducted on three benchmark datasets: Brainwave EEG, WESAD, and SWELL, achieving remarkable performance with accuracies of 96.49%, 99.99%, and 99.99%, respectively. The model has attained perfect precision, recall, and F1-scores on the WESAD and SWELL datasets, setting new state-of-the-art standards. Comparative analysis has demonstrated the method’s superiority over existing machine learning and deep learning techniques, while preserving computational efficiency that supports real-time applications. These results have confirmed the framework’s strong potential for practical use in mental health monitoring and affective computing. The source code of this work is available at: https://github.com/DebamSahaCS/Dual-Branch-DL-Framework

Introduction

Mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, and stress-related disorders1 have been recognized as significant public health challenges across the globe, impacting the emotional stability and daily lives of millions of people. The timely identification and continuous tracking of emotional states have been regarded as vital for ensuring effective mental health care and intervention strategies. Nevertheless, conventional approaches to emotion evaluation, including self-reports and standardized questionnaires, have been constrained by issues of subjectivity, recall bias, and their inability to provide continuous assessment, thereby reducing their utility in real-time monitoring of mental well-being.

Within this framework, emotion recognition2 based on electroencephalogram (EEG) signals has received considerable attention in the fields of affective computing and brain–computer interface (BCI) research. EEG signals record the brain’s electrical activity, providing a direct and objective reflection of the cognitive and affective mechanisms associated with various emotional states. Utilizing EEG for emotion recognition has enabled the design of systems that can provide continuous and objective monitoring of emotional conditions, thereby supporting early diagnosis and promoting personalized interventions for mental health management.

Despite its promising potential, EEG-based emotion recognition3 has encountered several challenges that have limited its practical application. EEG signals are highly non-stationary and exhibit substantial variability across individuals as well as recording sessions, which has made the development of models with strong generalization capabilities across subjects and datasets particularly difficult. Furthermore, many existing emotion recognition approaches have relied extensively on manual feature engineering, where domain experts extract handcrafted attributes such as band power or statistical measures from EEG recordings. This process has not only proven to be time-consuming. However, it has also often failed to adequately capture the intricate spatiotemporal dynamics embedded in EEG signals that are associated with diverse emotional states.

Moreover, deep learning techniques4, which have achieved remarkable success in fields such as image and speech recognition, have also been employed for EEG-based emotion recognition or EEG-Based stress detection1 to overcome the limitations of manual feature engineering. However, these approaches have often required substantial computational resources and involved large model architectures, making them less suitable for real-time and portable applications in mental health monitoring. In addition, although many deep learning models have reported high accuracy on specific datasets, they have frequently struggled to generalize across diverse datasets and recording conditions, thereby restricting their practical effectiveness in real-world scenarios.

To overcome these challenges, this study has introduced a hybrid dual-branch deep learning framework for EEG-based emotion recognition, aiming to enhance accuracy, generalization, and computational efficiency while minimizing dependence on manual feature engineering. The proposed model has integrated temporal and spatial feature extraction within a unified structure. Specifically, the temporal branch has employed a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network5 directly on raw EEG signals to capture temporal dependencies and dynamic variations linked to emotional states. In parallel, the spatial branch has extracted Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC)6,7 from EEG signals and applied a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for spatial feature learning, leveraging the capability of MFCC8 to represent frequency-domain characteristics that are highly relevant for emotion recognition.

This work has proposed a hybrid dual-branch deep learning model for EEG-based emotion recognition designed to overcome the existing limitations in accuracy, generalization, and computational efficiency while reducing reliance on manual feature extraction. The architecture integrates two complementary branches: the temporal branch utilizes a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network applied directly to raw EEG signals to capture temporal dependencies and dynamic emotional patterns, while the spatial branch extracts Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) from EEG signals and employs a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to learn spatial features, leveraging MFCC’s ability to represent frequency-domain information relevant to emotion recognition. Beyond these primary branches, the model innovatively implements cross-modality feature learning by calculating inverse MFCCs from the CNN outputs and generating MFCCs from signals reconstructed using LSTM outputs. This approach enriches feature representations and enables the model to capture complementary spatiotemporal information inherent in EEG signals. Outputs from both branches have been combined using multiplicative fusion and concatenation, followed by flattening and classification through an Artificial Neural Network (ANN). This unified end-to-end framework allows for continuous emotion recognition from raw EEG data with minimal preprocessing, marking a significant advancement in model design and practical applicability for mental health monitoring systems.

The proposed model’s effectiveness has been demonstrated on three publicly available EEG emotion datasets: Brainwave EEG9, WESAD10, and SWELL11, achieving an impressive classification accuracy of 99% on all datasets. This performance highlights the model’s superior capability in EEG-based emotion recognition. By integrating a hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture with MFCC-based feature extraction, the system has significantly reduced computational complexity while attaining state-of-the-art accuracy. These characteristics make the approach highly promising for practical implementation in real-time emotion recognition systems aimed at mental health monitoring and brain–computer interface applications.

Literature survey

Emotion recognition from physiological signals, especially EEG and other biosensors, has gained significant prominence in affective computing, human–computer interaction, and mental health monitoring. Over the last decade, research has progressed through diverse methodologies for emotional state classification, transitioning from classical machine learning systems based on handcrafted features to deep learning architectures capable of end-to-end feature extraction. More recently, fusion and ensemble-based techniques have been developed to enhance robustness.

Initially, studies have relied on statistical, spectral12, spatial-temporal13, frequency-domain14, and CNN-based features15 manually extracted from physiological signals like EEG, ECG, GSR, skin temperature, and respiration. These features were processed via conventional classifiers, including Support Vector Machines (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forests (RF), and Logistic Regression, achieving effective performance in controlled experimental setups. However, these methods have been limited by scalability challenges, heavy dependence on expert-driven feature engineering, and diminished robustness in broader evaluations. With advancements in deep learning, architectures such as CNNs, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), LSTM models, and Transformer-based networks have been increasingly applied to extract discriminative features directly from raw or minimally processed signals. These models have excelled in capturing complex temporal and spectral patterns in physiological data, minimizing the need for manual feature extraction while substantially improving multi-class emotion recognition accuracy.

More recently, research has concentrated on multimodal fusion16,17 for emotion recognition and ensemble learning18 methods that combine different signal sources and classifier outputs to improve stability and reliability, especially under noisy real-world conditions. These approaches exploit complementary information from multiple modalities (e.g., EEG combined with ECG or EDA) and integrate classifiers at feature or decision levels to address sensor-specific variability. Concurrently, efforts have been directed towards building adaptable models across diverse experimental protocols, hardware setups, and demographics. Reflecting this evolution, there has been a clear methodological progression in EEG emotion recognition: beginning with feature-engineered machine learning, advancing to end-to-end deep learning architectures, and most recently focusing on fusion and multimodal strategies. This trajectory is well supported by diverse datasets, including Brainwave EEG, SWELL, and WESAD, which have collectively facilitated these advances. In contrast to wavelet-based multiscale time–frequency modeling (e.g., multiscale CNN kernels operating on wavelet features), our framework employs a compact MFCC-based spectral representation and introduces an inverse-MFCC reconstruction to couple spectral and temporal representations. Moreover, while dual-path attention/graph-fusion networks integrate local spectral patterns with graph-based spatial dependencies via attention mechanisms, our approach implements cross-modality enhancement through explicit LSTM-to-MFCC projection and reciprocal feature enrichment, enabling interaction between temporal dynamics and spectral cues within a unified dual-branch19 architecture.

Machine learning approaches

Early and foundational studies predominantly employed machine learning algorithms to classify emotional states from EEG data. For the Brainwave EEG dataset, Qing et al.20 utilized an activation mechanism within conventional machine learning pipelines, but reported a modest accuracy of 75.00%, highlighting the constraints of shallow models in capturing subtle affective features. Bird et al.21 extended this investigation by deploying classical classifiers, marginally raising the accuracy to 94.90%, yet still facing limitations in generalizability and robustness. Machine learning techniques have also contributed significantly to the SWELL and WESAD datasets. Sriramprakash et al.22 applied SVM and KNN for SWELL, attaining an accuracy of 86.36%—a foundation upon which subsequent enhancements were built. Gedam et al.23 introduced ensemble learning through XGBoost for both SWELL and WESAD, achieving 92.36% in both cases, demonstrating that boosting strategies can capitalize on weak learners to capture more complex signal patterns. Hybrid neuro-fuzzy methods, such as the ANFIS model in Bardak et al.’s24 work on Brainwave EEG, produced a strong accuracy of 95.97%, leveraging the adaptability of fuzzy rules for decision boundaries. Feature selection methods proved crucial in Dahal et al.’s25 random forest (RF) with mRMR features for SWELL, yielding near-perfect accuracy (99.00%) and equally high precision, recall, and F1-score (99.00%). On the WESAD dataset, Bajpai et al.26 and Ciccarelli et al.27 combined physiological signals (HRV, EDA) with SVM classifiers, resulting in consistent performance improvements—accuracies of 90.49% and 96.12%, alongside comprehensive high-scoring precision, recall, and F1-measures, underscoring the essential role of feature engineering in traditional models.

Deep learning-based approaches

The field has undergone a marked transition towards deep learning, which has revolutionized accuracy and feature learning capabilities. Manoharan et al.28 deployed wavelet transforms followed by CNN classification on Brainwave EEG, leading to an accuracy of 91.72%, thereby enabling hierarchical spatial feature extraction unavailable in shallow models. Zhong et al.29 capitalized on the recurrent graph neural network (RGNN) architecture to reach 94.24% accuracy, suggesting the importance of modeling both temporal and spatial dependencies within EEG signal graphs. Significant advances are evident in the SWELL dataset research. Liakopoulos et al.30 converted EEG data to 2D spectrograms and processed them with CNNs, achieving 97.64% accuracy, while Sarkar et al.31 pushed further using self-supervised learning to obtain 98.4% accuracy—demonstrating that unsupervised and hybrid models can leverage large unlabelled datasets for improved representation learning. Mortensen et al.32 excelled with a 1D CNN architecture harvesting HRV features, achieving a near-perfect 99.9% accuracy and flawless evaluation scores (100 for precision, recall, and F1). Benita et al.33, investigating CNNs on WESAD, reached 98.55% accuracy, though precision and F1-score lagged, illustrating that high accuracy alone may not reflect overall classification reliability. Hybrid architectures combining convolutional and recurrent layers—such as CNN-LSTM—have also exhibited superior performance (Ali et al. for Brainwave EEG with 95.55% accuracy and F1-score, Nazarova et al.34 for WESAD with 97.00% accuracy), confirming that complex temporal-spatial dependencies can be more effectively modeled with such deep structures.

Multimodal approaches

Multimodal and sensor-fusion techniques have been increasingly adopted for robust emotion classification. Kumar et al.35 integrated EDA, ECG, CWT, and VGG16 neural network features for WESAD, with an accuracy of 86.66%, establishing the foundation for more diverse signal utilization. Kasnesis et al.36 developed TranSenseFuser—a novel fusion framework—reaching 98.46% accuracy and an F1-score of 97.03%, which showcases the value of concurrent data streams from multiple sensor modalities. Liao et al.37 explored a variety of machine learning algorithms for SWELL, obtaining a flawless 100% accuracy, albeit omitting precision, recall, and F1 reporting. Feature fusion strategies are exemplified across datasets where HRV, EDA, mRMR, and other physiological indicators are jointly processed by classical or deep learning models, as shown by Bajpai et al.26, Ciccarelli et al.27, and Dahal et al.25, consistently driving up recognition rates and metric reliability. These studies underline the rapidly advancing paradigm of multimodal analysis in affective computing.

Motivation behind this work

Emotion recognition using EEG signals has attracted significant attention, as it has provided more objective and reliable insights into human affective states in the domains of mental health assessment, stress detection, and affective computing. Although the field has witnessed growing interest supported by the availability of multiple public EEG datasets, existing models have still faced critical limitations that have hindered their applicability in real-world environments. Many approaches have remained dependent on manual feature extraction, which has required domain expertise, offered limited scalability, and has often failed to exploit the complex spatiotemporal dynamics that are embedded in EEG recordings. In an attempt to overcome these challenges, deep learning methods have been proposed, yet they have frequently encountered issues such as high computational demands and weak generalization performance across heterogeneous datasets. These shortcomings have posed substantial challenges, especially when EEG data have been acquired using diverse sensor configurations, varying sampling rates, or different experimental conditions.

A considerable number of previous studies have evaluated emotion recognition models using a single dataset, often under tightly controlled laboratory conditions. While such settings have yielded artificially high accuracy, they have offered limited practical relevance, as real-world applications demand robustness across heterogeneous populations, devices, and environments. Consequently, there has been a growing need for models that have sustained high performance across multiple datasets while remaining computationally efficient and adaptable to diverse EEG data characteristics. Motivated by these challenges, the present work has introduced a hybrid deep learning framework that has combined LSTM and CNN architectures with MFCC-based cross-modality feature fusion, thereby enabling the joint extraction of temporal and frequency-domain representations from EEG signals. Through systematic evaluation on three widely used benchmark datasets—Brainwave EEG, WESAD, and SWELL—our study has demonstrated the feasibility of developing emotion recognition models that are generalizable, resource-efficient, and deployable in real-world mental health support and monitoring contexts.

Methodology and implementation

This section outlines the complete workflow adopted to develop and evaluate the proposed EEG-based emotion recognition model. It begins with the data preprocessing pipeline, which standardizes and prepares raw EEG data from multiple public datasets for efficient model training and evaluation. Following this, we describe the proposed hybrid deep learning architecture, which integrates both temporal and spectral feature extraction through LSTM, CNN, and MFCC-based processing to enhance the representation of emotional states. Finally, the experimental setup is detailed, including hardware specifications, training parameters, optimization strategies, and evaluation metrics, to ensure transparency and reproducibility of results across all three datasets—Brainwave EEG, WESAD, and SWELL.

Datasets used for experimentation

To assess the performance and generalization ability of the proposed hybrid deep learning model for EEG-based emotion recognition, experiments have been conducted on three widely recognized public datasets: Brainwave EEG9, WESAD10, and SWELL11. These datasets are extensively utilized in the affective computing and mental health monitoring fields and encompass a broad spectrum of emotional states recorded under diverse experimental conditions, devices, and participant demographics. Employing multiple datasets has allowed a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s robustness across varying recording environments and emotional labeling methodologies, which is essential for creating practical emotion recognition systems deployable in real-world mental health monitoring applications.

Brainwave EEG Dataset: The Brainwave EEG dataset records emotional states—positive, neutral, and negative—using EEG measurements obtained from a Muse EEG headband equipped with dry electrodes positioned at TP9, AF7, AF8, and TP10. Data acquisition included three-minute sessions for each emotional state alongside a six-minute resting baseline, enabling a structured analysis of emotional brain activity. The dataset consists of 2,132 samples, with emotional stimuli carefully chosen from films like La La Land and Marley & Me to elicit specific emotional responses during recording. To facilitate a detailed examination of the neural correlates of emotion, statistical features were extracted from the EEG signals. The dataset categorizes emotions into three classes: 0 for negative, 1 for neutral, and 2 for positive, providing a solid foundation for studying emotion-related brain dynamics in EEG-based emotion recognition systems.

WESAD Dataset: The WESAD (Wearable Stress and Affect Detection) dataset is a multimodal resource created for emotion and stress detection, comprising recordings from 15 participants using a chest-mounted RespiBAN Professional device (sampling at 700 Hz) and a wrist-worn Empatica E4 sensor. It includes physiological signals such as ECG, EDA, EMG, respiration, body temperature, blood volume pulse (BVP), and accelerometer data to capture responses during neutral (baseline), stress (induced by the Trier Social Stress Test), and amusement (funny video clips) conditions, with recovery facilitated through guided meditation. Ground truth labels for valence, arousal, and stress were validated via self-report questionnaires, including PANAS, STAI, SAM, and SSSQ. Providing approximately 36 minutes of labeled data per subject, the dataset is extensively utilized in affective computing as a benchmark for emotion recognition systems, particularly in mental health monitoring applications.

SWELL Dataset: The SWELL-Knowledge Work (SWELL-KW) dataset was created to investigate stress and user behavior in realistic office settings. It collected data from 25 participants engaged in typical knowledge work tasks such as report writing, presentations, and email management under three conditions: neutral, time pressure, and email interruptions. Multimodal sensors—including computer logging, facial video recordings, Kinect 3D body posture tracking, and physiological measures such as heart rate and skin conductance—were employed to capture behavioral and physiological responses during approximately three-hour sessions per participant. Subjective measures of task load, mental effort, emotion, and perceived stress were obtained via validated questionnaires, ensuring reliable ground truth labels for stress and affective state estimation. Overall, the SWELL-KW dataset offers rich, naturalistic multimodal data to support advances in affective computing, stress detection, and mental health monitoring in practical knowledge work environments.

Data pre-processing

Effective data preprocessing is essential for building a robust EEG-based emotion recognition system, particularly when working with multiple datasets containing varied emotional labels and experimental conditions. In this study, we applied a unified preprocessing pipeline across all three datasets—Brainwave EEG, WESAD, and SWELL to ensure consistency and enhance the model’s generalization ability.

Initially, each dataset was loaded from its respective CSV format using pandas.read_csv() function. To prepare the data for classification, non-informative or metadata columns, such as subject identifiers, condition descriptions, or label strings, were excluded from the feature set to retain only meaningful EEG-derived attributes. Emotion or stress-related labels were extracted as the target variable, with the exact label column varying across datasets depending on the emotion annotation scheme.

The datasets were then inspected for missing values, and if any were detected, the pipeline was configured to raise an error, preventing silent corruption of training quality. Next, class labels were numerically encoded using LabelEncoder38 method from Scikit-learn to convert categorical targets into a format compatible with PyTorch-based classification.

To ensure that all input features contributed equally during model training, we applied feature standardization using StandardScaler39 method, which transforms the data to have zero mean and unit variance. This step is particularly crucial for models that involve gradient-based optimization, such as CNNs and LSTMs.

The preprocessed data were then partitioned into training and test sets using train_test_split(), with stratification applied to preserve the class distribution within both subsets. Finally, to enable seamless integration with PyTorch’s training framework, each dataset was wrapped in a custom EEGFeatureDataset class that converts features and labels into PyTorch tensors and supports efficient batch processing through the DataLoader interface.

This standardized preprocessing pipeline enables consistent handling of diverse EEG datasets, reduces bias introduced by varied label formats or feature scales, and prepares the data for robust model training and evaluation.

Proposed methodology

This work has proposed a hybrid dual-branch framework that has jointly exploited temporal and spectral representations of EEG to achieve robust emotion recognition with minimal preprocessing and strong cross-domain feature40 enrichment. The architecture comprises two primary streams—a temporal branch operating directly on raw or reconstructed EEG via Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units, and a spectral branch operating on Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) via CNNs—that have been coupled by bidirectional cross-modality enhancement and gated fusion before a compact ANN-based classifier. By enabling both direct sequence modeling and localized spectral–temporal pattern extraction, the framework has captured complementary evidence of affective dynamics while preserving computational parsimony suitable for near real-time scenarios.

Rationale for dual-branch and bidirectional enhancement and mathematical view of the full model

The dual-branch design has stemmed from the observation that time-domain sequence modeling and MFCC-domain convolution capture different aspects of EEG dynamics: LSTMs have tracked long-range temporal dependencies and nonstationarities, while CNNs on MFCCs have detected local spectral–temporal motifs robust to minor deformations. Without cross-modality enhancement, branches may learn partially disjoint features; the inverse MFCC and LSTM-to-MFCC transformations have encouraged agreement and knowledge sharing, enabling spectral features to be tested for temporal plausibility and temporal patterns to be re-expressed spectrally for convolutional scrutiny. The multiplicative fusion component has emphasized co-occurring evidence across branches, whereas additive and concatenative components have preserved complementary information, collectively producing a rich yet compact representation. Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted on the Kaggle cloud platform in a computational environment equipped with 32 GB RAM and an NVIDIA Tesla P100 GPU, which substantially accelerated model training and evaluation. The implementation was developed in Python using PyTorch for deep learning, Scikit-learn for preprocessing and evaluation, and NumPy/Pandas for data handling. The proposed hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture was extensively validated using the Brainwave EEG dataset, which includes three emotional categories: Negative (0), Neutral (1), and Positive (2). Observations and discussion

The proposed model demonstrates consistently high performance across all three datasets. Under the single stratified split as seen in Table 1, the model achieves test accuracies of 96.49% on Brainwave EEG and 99.99% on both WESAD and SWELL. To ensure reproducibility beyond a single split, we further reported Monte-Carlo cross-validation results (30 stratified 70/30 runs) with distribution-free 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, yielding mean test accuracies of 95.48% (95% CI: 95.18–95.78) on Brainwave EEG, 99.93% (99.92–99.93) on WESAD, and 99.99% (99.99–99.99) on SWELL as depicted in Table 2. Collectively, these results highlight the architecture’s strong ability to maintain stable performance across diverse data sources, sensor types, and experimental conditions.

The predicted class distributions across datasets closely align with the true class distributions, indicating unbiased predictions even in the presence of mild class imbalance (e.g., WESAD’s relatively smaller positive class). The effectiveness of the proposed cross-modality enhancement—via inverse MFCC computation and LSTM-to-MFCC projection—likely contributes to this robustness by enriching temporal and spectral feature learning and promoting stronger cross-domain representations. The comparatively lower performance on Brainwave EEG may be attributed to the limited number of electrodes (four channels from the Muse headband), shorter recording durations, smaller dataset size, and potentially higher noise levels typical of consumer-grade EEG devices. Overall, the comparative evaluation confirms that the hybrid CNN–LSTM model with MFCC-based cross-modality learning not only performs strongly within each dataset but also remains stable under repeated resampling, underscoring its potential for real-world deployment in mental health monitoring and affective computing applications.

Comparison with past methods

The performance of the proposed hybrid CNN–LSTM model with MFCC-based cross-modality learning was compared against existing methods reported in the literature for the Brainwave EEG, SWELL, and WESAD datasets, which is clearly depicted in Table 4, 5, and 6. This comparison highlights the contributions of our approach in terms of accuracy, generalization capability, and computational efficiency.

Brainwave EEG

The proposed method has demonstrated superior performance compared to previous works for EEG-based emotion recognition using the Brainwave EEG dataset. Past studies, such as Qing et al.20 have applied activation mechanism models with machine learning and reported an accuracy of 75.00%. Manoharan et al.28 have utilized wavelet transform with CNN, resulting in an accuracy of 91.72%. Zhong et al.29 have implemented RGNN techniques, achieving a 94.24% accuracy, while Bird et al.21 have employed classical classifiers with a slightly higher accuracy of 94.90%. Ali et al.41 have adopted a hybrid approach using CNN, LSTM, and ANN algorithms, which has achieved an accuracy of 95.55% and an F1-score of 95.55%. Bardak et al.24 have explored ANFIS models, reporting an accuracy of 95.97%, and Reddy et al.42 have utilized the GRNN method with a recorded accuracy of 95.99%. In comparison, the proposed work—leveraging combined signal and MFCC feature extraction—has achieved a notably higher accuracy of 96.49%. Moreover, it is distinguished by its detailed evaluation results, including a precision of 97.01%, a recall of 98.23%, and an F1-score of 96.12%, surpassing all previously reported metrics. The comprehensive set of evaluation metrics provided by the proposed approach further underscores its effectiveness and serves as a new benchmark for emotion recognition in EEG signal processing. This substantial performance improvement validates the robustness of the model and its methodological advances over previous works as depicted in Table 4.

SWELL dataset

The proposed method has achieved outstanding results in comparison with previous works on the SWELL dataset depicted in Table 5, establishing a new benchmark for emotion recognition performance. Among earlier studies, Sriramprakash et al.22 employed SVM and KNN classifiers, obtaining an accuracy of 86.36%. Gedam et al.23 reported a moderately improved accuracy of 92.36% using XGBoost, while Liakopoulos et al.30 leveraged 2D spectrograms with CNN architectures to achieve an accuracy of 97.64%. Sarkar et al. adopted self-supervised learning, resulting in 98.4% accuracy, and Dahal et al.25 utilized RF (mRMR features), reaching 99.00% accuracy along with a precision, recall, and F1-score of 99.00%. Mortensen et al.28 explored a 1D CNN with HRV features, achieving an accuracy of 99.9% and perfect scores for precision, recall, and F1-score (100% each). Liao et al.37 applied various machine learning algorithms, achieving 100% accuracy, although their precision, recall, and F1-score values were not specified in the comparison. The proposed work, by harnessing signal and MFCC feature fusion, has surpassed all reported results with an accuracy of 99.99%, and perfect precision, recall, and F1-score measures, each at 100. This comprehensive achievement highlights the effectiveness of the methodology and underscores its superiority over all previously published approaches for the SWELL dataset, validating its robust generalization and discriminative capability in emotion recognition from EEG signals.

Ablation study

We examined four model configurations to quantify the contribution of the proposed IMFCC-based cross-modality enhancement: (i) a temporal-only LSTM branch, (ii) a spectral-only CNN operating on MFCC features, (iii) a dual-stream architecture with cross-modality removed (i.e., without IMFCC reconstruction/projection), and (iv) the full proposed model. Results indicate that the dual-stream formulation constitutes a strong baseline, whereas the incremental benefit of IMFCC is dataset dependent. For WESAD and SWELL, incorporating IMFCC yields consistent improvements relative to the corresponding dual-stream model without cross-modality, and paired McNemar tests confirm statistical significance (median p-values = 0.0003163 and 0.0008617, respectively). Conclusion

This study presented a novel hybrid CNN–LSTM architecture enhanced with MFCC–based cross-modality learning for EEG-based emotion and stress recognition. The proposed framework introduces two key innovations: (1) an inverse MFCC computation mechanism that reconstructs spectral features back into the temporal domain, enabling the LSTM branch to capture deeper sequential dependencies, and (2) an LSTM-to-MFCC projection that maps learned temporal representations into the spectral space for CNN-based processing, thereby enriching spatial–spectral feature learning. This dual-pathway interaction between temporal and spectral domains allows the model to leverage the complementary strengths of CNNs and LSTMs, ultimately producing more robust and discriminative features. The model was evaluated on three benchmark datasets — Brainwave EEG, WESAD, and SWELL — that vary significantly in signal quality, sensor configurations, and experimental protocols. Despite these differences, the proposed approach consistently delivered superior performance, achieving test accuracies of 96.49%, 99.99%, and 99.99%, respectively, with minimal misclassification and balanced per-class accuracies. The comparative analysis against related work demonstrated that the proposed model either surpasses or matches the state-of-the-art in all three datasets, outperforming conventional machine learning approaches that rely on handcrafted features and rivaling advanced deep learning models, including CNN-Transformer hybrids and multimodal fusion frameworks. Our design differs from wavelet multiscale kernels and graph/attention fusion methods by relying on MFCC-derived spectral compactness and explicit inverse-MFCC cross-modality coupling; benchmarking on other standard datasets like DEAP and SEED will be included in future work for direct comparison. Moreover, the unified architecture required no dataset-specific hyperparameter tuning, underscoring its adaptability and generalization capability. These results indicate that the proposed framework is a highly promising solution for real-world applications in mental health monitoring, affective computing, and adaptive human–computer interaction systems.

Limitations and future work

Although the proposed approach achieves notable results, several avenues remain open for further research and development. First, the current work did not consider any cross-dataset transfer learning and domain adaptation that allows models trained on one dataset to generalize effectively to another without extensive retraining. This will address one of the persistent challenges in physiological signal analysis — the variability introduced by different hardware setups, subject demographics, and recording environments. Second, there is significant scope for lightweight model optimization through techniques such as pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation, which could make the model suitable for deployment on wearable devices and edge computing platforms where computational resources are limited. Third, extending the framework to incorporate multimodal fusion with other physiological signals such as ECG, EDA, PPG, and respiration could further improve robustness, particularly in cases where EEG data may be noisy or partially missing. We also try the Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) methodology in the future. Finally, adapting the system for real-time, online emotion recognition using streaming EEG data will open up possibilities for applications in driver monitoring, adaptive e-learning, workplace stress management, and continuous mental health assessment.
