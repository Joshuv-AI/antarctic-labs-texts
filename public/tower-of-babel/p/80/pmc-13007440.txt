The Syn-sleep trial protocol: detection of cutaneous phosphorylated alpha-synuclein in REM sleep behavior disorder
Authors: Sarrah Marcotte, Todd Levine, Roy Freeman, Christopher H Gibbons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Idiopathic rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) is a prodromal neurodegenerative disease of misfolded alpha-synuclein (P-SYN) with a high risk of phenoconversion to a clinically apparent synucleinopathy (including Parkinson’s disease, multiple system atrophy, or dementia with Lewy bodies) over 15 years.Objectives:To determine rates of cutaneous P-SYN deposition in iRBD, to quantify changes in PSYN deposition over time, and to determine if P-SYN deposition patterns and amounts predict phenoconversion to a specific type of synucleinopathy.Clinical trial protocol:In a prospective, blinded study we will recruit 80 individuals with polysomnography confirmed iRBD or probable RBD using standard diagnostic criteria. Skin biopsies with dual immunohistochemical immunostaining for nerve fibers (protein gene product 9.5) and P-SYN will be completed at 3 sites using standard methodology. Quantitative measures of P-SYN and nerve fiber density will be measured blinded to any clinical data and will be followed longitudinally to determine the final clinical diagnosis.Discussion:Patients with iRBD are an important population to study due to the high rates of phenoconversion to clinically apparent synucleinopathy. Defining the frequency of P-SYN deposition and the risk of phenoconversion will aid in the development of future clinical trials that seek to alter the natural history of synucleinopathies.Keywords: Dementia with Lewy bodies, Alzheimer’s disease, mild cognitive impairment, skin biopsy, phosphorylated alpha-synuclein

Introduction

A group of neurodegenerative diseases, collectively known as synucleinopathies, have no effective treatments and share an underlying pathology – progressive deposition of the misfolded protein, phosphorylated alpha-synuclein (P-SYN), within the central and peripheral nervous systems [1]. Synucleinopathies, which include Parkinson’s disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), pure autonomic failure (PAF), and multiple system atrophy (MSA), affect over 2 million people in the US and carry significant morbidity and high mortality [2]. Recently, detection of cutaneous P-SYN deposition has been established as a sensitive and specific marker for synucleinopathy that correlates with disease severity [3–9]. In addition, cutaneous P-SYN is also observed in individuals with prodromal disease, including isolated rapid eye movement sleep disorder (iRBD), which manifests as dream reenactment with vocalizations and uncontrolled movements [10]. While iRBD typically has no cognitive, motor, or autonomic features of the synucleinopathies at initial presentation, 75% of iRBD patients will progress to a clinically apparent synucleinopathy (defined as meeting clinical criteria for PD, DLB, or MSA) within 10 years of the diagnosis, and >90% within 15 years [11]. However, the clinical and research implications of cutaneous PSYN deposition in iRBD patients have not been fully elucidated [12]. At present, methods for predicting the disease trajectory to clinically apparent synucleinopathy in patients with iRBD are limited. The development of a diagnostic tool to evaluate and predict the probability of phenoconversion at prodromal stages of synucleinopathy is an urgent and unmet need. The primary objectives of this study are:

To define the rates of P-SYN deposition in patients with the prodromal disorder isolated REM sleep behavior disorder (polysomnography confirmed) and in probable RBD (pRBD, without polysomnography)

To define the rates of P-SYN deposition in patients with the prodromal disorder isolated REM sleep behavior disorder (polysomnography confirmed) and in probable RBD (pRBD, without polysomnography)

To quantify cutaneous P-SYN deposition and determine rates of change over time.

To quantify cutaneous P-SYN deposition and determine rates of change over time.

To identify the patterns of P-SYN deposition across biopsy topography and nerve fiber subtypes to predict phenoconversion to PD, DLB, or MSA.

To identify the patterns of P-SYN deposition across biopsy topography and nerve fiber subtypes to predict phenoconversion to PD, DLB, or MSA.

To achieve these goals, we will conduct a prospective, blinded longitudinal study of patients with iRBD who will undergo annual clinical evaluations and skin biopsies with P-SYN quantitation to monitor rates of clinical and pathological change over time.

Methods: clinical trial protocol

Study design & cohort

The study is a multi-center, blinded, longitudinal, prospective observational study to evaluate P-SYN deposition in individuals with iRBD. A total of 80 individuals with iRBD and pRBD will be recruited into the trial (either clinically defined or confirmed by polysomnography). Study participants will be recruited from approximately 10 medical centers that specialize in patients with iRBD and pRBD and will include a mixture of academic and private practice sites.

Subjects will be included with iRBD and pRBD in the study based on the following definition: subjects will have repeated episodes of sleep-related vocalizations and/or complex motor behaviors. Approximately half of the study subjects (ñ = 40) will require polysomnographic recording to demonstrate REM sleep without atonia (iRBD); the other half will require confirmation with a clinical history and by a REM Behavior Disorder Sleep Questionnaire (RBDSQ) score ≥ 5 (pRBD) [13]. In addition, the RBD symptoms in both groups are not better explained by another sleep disorder, mental disorder, medication, or substance use based on the International Classification of Sleep Disorders version 3 criteria [14].

Study visits and participant data collection

Trial synopsis

In collaboration with approximately 10 centers that specialize in RBD, we will recruit a total of 80 individuals in a 2-year longitudinal study where skin biopsies will be performed at 3 sites on each patient at 12-month intervals (baseline, year 1, year 2). Plasma blood collection will be performed at 12-month intervals (baseline, year 1, year 2). Detailed quantified neurological examination, cognitive evaluation, medical history, and questionnaires will be performed at each visit.

Additional biomarkers, imaging, and clinical information (if available) will be obtained for the purpose of determining phenoconversion to clinically apparent synucleinopathy. Subjects enrolled in the study will have baseline evaluations and follow-up visits at 12 and 24 months to define any changes to clinical diagnosis (clinical phenoconversion). Skin biopsies will be repeated at the 12- and 24-month follow-up visits to determine the rate of P-SYN accumulation over time and the rates of nerve fiber degeneration within punch skin biopsies.

Standardization of data and tissue collection

To minimize potential variability in study outcomes, all tissue and clinical data will be obtained using standard methods that we have utilized in multiple prior studies [5,6].

Skin biopsy samples

All subjects will have 3-millimeter punch skin biopsies taken from the distal leg 10 cm above the lateral malleolus, the distal thigh 10 cm above the lateral knee, and the posterior cervical region 3 cm lateral to the C-7 spinous process. Biopsy locations are selected based on prior publications that demonstrate differential phosphorylated alpha-synuclein deposition in proximal and distal locations across the different synucleinopathies. The three sites we have selected provide maximal sensitivity and specificity across all synucleinopathies [21]. Local analgesia will be provided using lidocaine with epinephrine (1:100,000). Skin biopsy samples do not require sutures, and band aids will be provided for wound care. Skin biopsy specimens will be fixed in Zamboni paraformaldehyde solution in pre-labeled tubes, placed into a standard shipping pack, and shipped overnight for central processing. At the central processing site, biopsies will be washed and placed in a 20% glycerol cryoprotectant solution.

Skin biopsy processing and immunostaining

After storage in cryoprotectant solution for at least 24 hours, skin samples will then be sectioned into 50 μm thick samples using a cryostat. Tissue will be immunostained with antibodies against protein gene product 9.5 (PGP9.5: a pan-axonal marker) and with antibodies to phosphorylated alpha-synuclein using free-floating sections in our standard protocols. Six skin biopsy specimens from each biopsy will be dual immunostained with rabbit anti-protein gene product 9.5 (Cederlane: 1:1000) and phosphorylated alpha-synuclein (Wako: 1:1000), mounted on glass slides as previously reported and seen in Figure 1 [21]. An immunofluorescent microscope will perform an initial review of slide quality. In all experiments, positive and negative controls are run to confirm the results.

Biopsy analysis

All biopsies will be evaluated in a blinded fashion using standard protocols [22]. Pathologist reading will include a semi-quantitative analysis of each biopsy site, with a composite score that includes that sum of all 3 biopsies as previously reported [21,23]. The sum score is a scale ranging from 0 to 36 SU’s (synuclein units) that has previously been shown to correlate with disease severity and aid in differentiation between disease subtypes [21,23]. All slides will undergo complete digitization (VS200, Evident). A series of optical images will be acquired at 2 μm intervals through the depth of the section as a Z-stack (VS200, Evident). Digital analysis of tissue will be performed to detect and quantify intra-axonal P-SYN (NerValence, CND Life Sciences). Quantitation of P-SYN (um2 of P-SYN per mm2 of tissue analyzed) will be reported for each biopsy specimen. A composite score for all 3 biopsies would therefore be equal to the sum of the P-SYN measured across all 3 biopsies divided by the area of tissue measured. The amount of P-SYN will be noted for each cutaneous dermal sub-structure (blood vessels, nerve bundles, pilo-arrector muscles, sweat glands, and sub-epidermal plexus) in each tissue section as seen in Figure 2. Intra-epidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD) will be calculated using standard methodology for immunostained nerve fibers, reported as nerve fibers/millimeter. Pathological review and grading of P-SYN deposition for all tissue sections will follow strict blinding measures, including slide randomization, random number generation, and blinding between pathology reviewers and study staff [21].

Definition of “positive biopsy results”

Only biopsies expressing P-SYN co-localized within a peripheral nerve axon (confirmed using immunostaining for anti-PGP9.5 and anti-P-SYN) will be considered positive. Any equivocal section will be considered negative. If any of the three biopsy sites contains P-SYN then the case is considered ‘positive’ (e.g., if one of 3 biopsies is positive, the case is positive) [24].

Clinical data capture

All study data, including questionnaires, examination scores, and demographic information, will be input using electronic data capture. Ancillary data (including prior medical records, test results, and polysomnography) will be uploaded into the electronic data capture system.

Blinding and review of data

Study blinding

All investigators reviewing biopsies will be blinded to clinical data, and all clinical evaluators will be blinded to pathology data. The initial clinical assessments will occur before biopsies, so all investigators will be blinded to skin biopsy results at that time.

Study objectives

Primary objectives

This is a prospective longitudinal study of individuals with iRBD and pRBD to determine the diagnostic utility of skin biopsy detection of P-SYN in a prodromal disease state. The following primary objectives are included in the study:

To define the rates of P-SYN deposition in patients with iRBD and pRBD,

To define the rates of P-SYN deposition in patients with iRBD and pRBD,

To determine the topographic patterns and specific nerve fiber subtypes that contain P-SYN to predict both the time to phenoconversion and the subtype of synucleinopathy (PD, DLB, or MSA).

To determine the topographic patterns and specific nerve fiber subtypes that contain P-SYN to predict both the time to phenoconversion and the subtype of synucleinopathy (PD, DLB, or MSA).

Secondary objectives

To enhance pathological reading, digital quantitative analysis of cutaneous P-SYN deposition will be aided using a novel machine learning algorithm (NerValence, CND Life Sciences). Extracted data will include hole slide imaging with analysis of representative neural structures, target stain detection, segmentation, stain quantification, and pattern recognition will be performed using deep learning algorithms. Results will be compared against pathologist readings and actual follow-up data to further refine model accuracy.

Statistical analysis

We anticipate, based on preliminary data and publications by other groups, that 1) >50% of individuals with iRBD and pRBD will have P-SYN present on skin biopsy, 2) P-SYN will be present in >80% of those that phenoconvert to clinically apparent synucleinopathy, 3) that P-SYN across the group will increase by >10% during annual follow-up visits, and 4) P-SYN deposition within sensory predominant fibers with widespread deposition will phenoconvert to MSA, while autonomic predominant P-SYN deposition will be either PD or DLB.

We further anticipate that DLB will be distinguished from PD based on a combination of pathological features that include 1) more widespread topographical deposition of P-SYN (e.g., more biopsies positive), 2) greater total amount of P-SYN deposition, 3) greater vasomotor deposition (e.g., a larger percentage of P-SYN within vasomotor fibers) and 4) lower densities of unmyelinated nociceptive C-fibers within the epidermal layer of the leg and thigh biopsies.

General statistical considerations: For the primary objectives, the presence or absence of P-SYN will be reported as a binary outcome for the first objective. Quantitation of P-SYN will be measured as a continuous variable, and phenoconversion in P-SYN-positive compared to P-SYN-negative biopsies in patients with RBD. We predict that a threshold of P-SYN > 0 is a ‘positive’ result and will separate RBD using dichotomous measures, but ROC curve analysis will be performed to maximize sensitivity and specificity using continuous quantitative measurement of P-SYN. Initial positive biopsy results will be compared against future biopsy results using Krippendorff’s alpha reliability estimate. Reliability will be measured by quantified continuous P-SYN values between visits using Bland-Altman plots, with the change in continuous values used to determine mean change over time. Additional endpoints will include positive predictive value and negative predictive value of P-SYN deposition and IENFD as continuous variables for statistical analysis in predicting phenoconversion. Data will be compared first using repeated measures. Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA), with phenoconversion as the predictor variable. The primary dependent variable is the quantitative measure of P-SYN deposition. Other key covariates will include disease duration, polysomnography confirmation of iRBD, examination scores, and autonomic nerve fiber density (pilomotor nerve fiber density and sudomotor nerve fiber density) [25]. These will be evaluated individually and together with descriptive and multivariate statistics to identify potential violations of statistical assumptions; transformations or bootstrap methods will be used as needed to address results that violate normality. For within- and between-group comparisons of demographic and laboratory data, nonparametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis) will be used. If the data cannot be transformed, we will apply the GEE (general estimating equation) method. As appropriate for study design, raw data will be reported as mean ± standard deviation and interquartile range using absolute values and change from baseline data for all tests of structure and function.

Sample size justification

Power analysis is based on the minimum number of subjects across the study to reach completion required for endpoint objectives. The expected dropout rate for subjects in our 2-year study is ~5% per 12-month period based on our prior natural history data within existing RBD studies. The required number needed to obtain sufficient objective pathological data (based on prior work in patients with PD, MSA, and DLB) from phenoconverted P-SYN positive RBD subjects is 20. We estimate that P-SYN will be detected in a minimum of 50% of iRBD subjects at baseline, where there will be a minimum of 15% annual phenoconversion rate in P-SYN-positive subjects to a clinically apparent synucleinopathy. We predict <10% of the P-SYN negative cases to become P-SYN positive per annual follow-up visit. Individuals who phenoconvert, or are lost to follow-up, will be replaced with newly recruited subjects each year to maintain the same number of iRBD subjects in total. We estimate that 80 iRBD subjects total will result in a total of 67 P-SYN positive cases throughout the study, and 18 cases of phenoconversion in the P-SYN positive group and 2 cases of phenoconversion in the P-SYN negative group. These numbers will provide >95% power, with a 2-sided alpha of 0.01 to detect differences in phenoconversion rates among patients with iRBD who are P-SYN positive compared to those who are P-SYN negative.

Discussion

REM sleep behavior disorder affects 0.5–1% of the US population, with significantly greater prevalence in the older populastion [26]. However, confirmation of an iRBD diagnosis requires polysomnography, which is particularly challenging to incorporate into population-based studies. Probable RBD (pRBD), with a diagnosis based on clinical symptoms of clear dream enactment-type, has prevalence estimates of over 5% of the general population [26]. These studies raise concerns about the number of people at risk for future neurodegenerative disease. The need for dependable, accessible, and accurate tools to detect the presence of P-SYN in patients with clinical features of RBD is an urgent priority. We and others have demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity of cutaneous P-SYN detection in patients with established synucleinopathy [10,21,27]. The primary goals of this longitudinal, prospective study are 1) to determine rates of P-SYN deposition in iRBD and pRBD, 2) to quantify changes in P-SYN deposition over time, and 3) to determine if the patterns of P-SYN deposition are a predictor of phenoconversion from iRBD to a clinically apparent synucleinopathy.

The confirmation of P-SYN deposition in patients with iRBD is an important inflection point in ongoing prodromal disease studies, as it confirms the pathologic basis of the disease [4,28,29]. There is a strong need for reliable predictors for phenoconversion to clinically apparent synucleinopathy. The presence of cutaneous P-SYN in a patient with iRBD has previously been shown as a predictor for phenoconversion [10], and therefore, the absence of P-SYN might correlate with a lower risk of phenoconversion within a set period of time. Establishing phosphorylated alpha-synuclein status will be necessary in studies that seek to alter the natural history of disease through synuclein-targeted therapies.

The goal of P-SYN quantitation has not been fully realized. To date, most diagnostic tools offer binary outcomes reporting the presence or absence of the abnormal protein [30], leading biologic definitions of disease to be tied to the presence or absence of a misfolded synuclein [28,29]. However, a truly quantitative measure of phosphorylated alpha-synuclein could offer meaningful utility to clinical trial design and predictive models of phenoconversion in RBD. To date, several publications have suggested quantitation is possible, and in cross-sectional studies, these quantitative measures aid in distinguishing disease subtypes [21,23]. If synuclein quantitation is confirmed in longitudinal follow-up, P-SYN deposition may offer a potential biomarker of target engagement that could be leveraged in synuclein-targeted therapies.

The ability to quantify P-SYN in prodromal disease states also has important implications for prognosis. Patients with RBD have a high likelihood of progressing to clinically apparent synucleinopathy over 15 years. However, not all synucleinopathies are created equal. There are very different rates of clinical decline in patients with MSA, DLB and PD. Developing algorithms to measure amounts and distribution of P-SYN deposition that could predict the development of PD, DLB or MSA in patients with RBD would offer significant prognostic value and substantially modify our approach to clinical trial design. At present, pathological features that distinguish synucleinopathy subtypes are noted in established disease states, and this study will determine if such features can be identified in prodromal conditions.

There are certain limitations to this study design. First, only half of the study subject will have polysomnography confirmation of RBD. Those that have PSG will have completed it as part of the historical workup, not at the time of study entry, so standardization of results is not possible. Second, the other half of recruited patients will have clinical confirmation placing them into the ‘probable’ RBD category. Given the frequency with which clinical symptoms are present in the population without PSG confirmation, we feel this is an important question requiring investigation. Third, rates of phenoconversion may not occur at the estimated rates proposed in this trial. Longer durations of follow-up will be considered if necessary to ensure adequate rates of phenoconversion. Finally, neuronal specific markers for nerve fiber subclass identification are available but are difficult to perform in a study of this size.

Conclusion

In summary, this prospective longitudinal study of RBD will move the field toward the unrealized goal of individualized precision diagnostic medicine. Early and accurate confirmation of pathology will support disease definitions, while quantitative analysis of longitudinal change will offer insights into phenoconversion risk and timelines. The results will aid in improving prognostication at the earliest disease stages. Further, the patterns of P-SYN deposition and rates of change over time may allow for the prediction of subcategories of synucleinopathy, e.g., MSA, DLB, or PD. Defining disease-specific prognostication with biomarkers would offer an important diagnostic step forward, theoretically reducing the patient’s diagnostic journey for a diagnosis by several years. Finally, the study results will support cutaneous P-SYN detection as a necessary part of the framework for developing disease-modifying clinical trials at an early stage of disease, which is critical for the future development of therapeutic advances.
