Elevated Interleukin‐6 Levels in Socioeconomically Disadvantaged Children With Borderline Subclinical Rheumatic Heart Disease in São Paulo, Brazil: A Prospective Cohort Study Highlighting Early Detection and Treatment Opportunities
Authors: Carlos Eduardo de Barros Branco, Alessandra Santos Lima Gazola, Carlos Augusto Homem de Magalhães Campos, Samar Freschi de Barros, Simone Santos, Karen Francine Kohler, Marcelo Luiz Campos Vieira, Luiza Guilherme Guglielmi, Paulo Pinto Alves Campos Vieira, Samira Saady Morhy, Ranna Santos Pessoa, Alberto Rodolpho Hüning, Vitor Emer Egypto Rosa, Jorge Kalil, Flavio Tarasoutchi, Roney Orismar Sampaio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubclinical rheumatic heart disease (SCRHD) is the precursor to symptomatic disease, yet the cytokine profile of its earliest borderline stage remains uncharacterized. It is unknown whether inflammatory markers persist longitudinally in subclinical rheumatic heart disease. This study characterized the cytokine profile in borderline SCRHD at baseline and evaluated its longitudinal evolution over a 2‐year period in the combined SCRHD (borderline and definitive).MethodsIn a prospective cohort study, 1026 children (5–15 years) from disadvantaged areas of São Paulo, Brazil, underwent echocardiographic screening. Participants were classified as normal, borderline SCRHD, or definite SCRHD according to World Health Organization criteria. Plasma levels of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), tumor necrosis factor‐alpha, and other cytokines were measured at baseline (T0) and after 2 years (T1).ResultsThe prevalence of SCRHD was 7.6% (95% CI, 6.0%–9.2%), with 6.7% borderline (95% CI, 5.2%–8.2%) and 0.9% as definite (95% CI, 0.3%–1.5%). At baseline, IL‐6 levels were significantly higher in the borderline SCRHD group compared with controls (P=0.048), while tumor necrosis factor‐alpha showed a trend toward elevation (P=0.067). No significant differences were observed for interleukin‐2 (P=0.067), interleukin‐4 (P=1.000), interleukin‐10 (P=0.690), interleukin‐17A (P=0.067), or interferon‐gamma (P=0.456). Among 44 participants followed for 2 years, IL‐6 and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha levels remained persistently elevated, with no significant change over time (P=0.626 and P=0.144, respectively), indicating ongoing inflammatory activity.ConclusionsBorderline SCRHD is marked by elevated IL‐6 levels that last for at least 2 years, indicating a sustained proinflammatory state from the earliest detectable stage of the disease. These findings identify interleukin‐6 as a potential early biomarker and therapeutic target to prevent the progression of SCRHD.

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) remains a leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality among children and young adults in low‐ and middle‐income countries, affecting an estimated 33 million people worldwide. 1 , 2 It is a chronic sequela of acute rheumatic fever, an autoimmune response to infection with group A streptococcus. 3 , 4 Despite advancements in health care, RHD persists because of socioeconomic disparities and inadequate prevention strategies. 5 , 6

Subclinical rheumatic heart disease (SCRHD), detectable only through echocardiography, precedes symptomatic disease and is categorized into borderline and definite stages based on the World Heart Federation (WHF) criteria. 7 Borderline SCRHD represents the earliest stage, characterized by subtle valvular abnormalities without overt clinical symptoms, whereas definite SCRHD indicates more pronounced echocardiographic changes consistent with established disease. 7

However, studies analyzing cytokine alterations in the borderline group, which represents the earliest detectable phase of SCRHD, are lacking. Early detection of borderline SCRHD is critical for timely intervention to prevent progression to definite SCRHD and irreversible cardiac damage. 8 Understanding the immunopathogenesis at this initial stage can provide insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

This study aims to assess interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), and other cytokine levels in children with borderline and definite SCRHD at baseline (T0) and after 2 years (T1).

Proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α, mediate the inflammatory processes that lead to valvular damage. 9 Elevated levels of these cytokines have been reported in acute rheumatic fever and clinical RHD, correlating with disease activity and severity. 10 , 11 Conversely, anti‐inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin‐4 (IL‐4), interleukin‐10 (IL‐10), and interleukin‐2 (IL‐2), play a crucial role in regulating immune responses and maintaining homeostasis. 12

METHODS, STUDY DESIGN, AND POPULATION

The data sets used or analyzed in this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

A prospective cohort study was conducted from June 2019 to February 2022 in the districts of Jardim Ângela and M'Boi Mirim, located in the southern region of São Paulo, Brazil. These densely populated urban areas are marked by significant socioeconomic vulnerability and limited access to specialized health care. 13 , 14 Children aged 5 to 15 years who were registered at 1 of the 14 participating Basic Health Units were eligible for screening. Inclusion required written informed consent from a parent or legal guardian. To establish a well‐defined cohort and minimize potential confounding, children with a previous diagnosis of acute rheumatic fever, known congenital or acquired heart disease, or established immunologic disorders were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included living outside the predefined area of the Basic Health Units, displaying clinical signs of acute infection at enrollment, or having recently used immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory medications (within the past 30 days).

Sample Size Calculation

Based on results from previously published studies, 15 an estimated prevalence of SCRHD of 12.9% was used to calculate the sample size. Therefore, a minimum of 1027 individuals was required to identify the prevalence of SCRHD in the studied population. With a desired precision of 2.15% and a maximum Type I error rate of 5%, this calculation included a margin for potential dropouts of up to 10%.

Ethics Statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hospital Israelita Albert Einstein, Heart Institute (InCor), and the São Paulo Municipal Health Secretariat. The protocol was approved by the ethics committee under opinion number 1.966.008. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all legal guardians.

Echocardiographic Screening and Classification

All participants underwent echocardiographic screening performed by an experienced cardiologist using portable echocardiography equipment (VIVID I GE with a 3SC‐RS GE transducer). The same echocardiographer performed all examinations, which were subsequently reviewed by a second, independent echocardiographer who was blinded to the participant's clinical and laboratory data. In cases of discordant interpretations, a third expert reviewer was consulted to reach a consensus. Participants were classified as normal, borderline, or definite SCRHD based on the 2012 WHF criteria, which incorporate specific morphological and Doppler‐based valvular findings (Table 1). 7 Borderline SCRHD: defined by at least 2 echocardiographic findings suggestive of RHD but insufficient to meet the criteria for definite SCRHD. This includes pathological mitral regurgitation and morphological features of RHD in a single valve 7 (Figure 1). Definite SCRHD: characterized by more pronounced echocardiographic abnormalities, including pathological regurgitation and morphological changes in at least 2 valves or severe morphological changes in a single valve 7 (Figure 2).

To assess the reproducibility of echocardiographic classification, we performed a formal analysis of inter‐ and intra‐observer agreement using the weighted Cohen's Kappa coefficient with squared weights. A total of 59 echocardiographic studies were independently reviewed by 2 experienced echocardiographers who were blinded to clinical and laboratory data. For intra‐observer reproducibility, the same evaluator re‐assessed the echocardiographic studies after a fixed 2‐year washout period, ensuring complete temporal separation and eliminating recall bias.

Control Group Selection

Controls were matched to SCRHD cases based on age, sex, and body mass index using propensity score matching. Propensity scores were calculated with logistic regression incorporating the matching variables, and a nearest‐neighbor matching algorithm was applied without replacement to select controls. The effectiveness of the matching was evaluated by comparing baseline characteristics between groups to ensure balance.

Cytokine Measurement

Blood samples (20 mL) were collected from participants diagnosed with borderline or definite SCRHD and from a matched control group without RHD. Controls were matched based on age, sex, and body mass index using propensity scores.

Plasma levels of interleukin‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐2, IL‐4, IL‐10, IL‐17A, and IFN‐γ were measured using the BD™ Cytometric Bead Array (CBA) Human Th1/Th2/Th17 Cytokine Kit, following the manufacturer's protocol. Analyses were performed at the Immunology Laboratory of the Heart Institute (InCor).

Cytokine measurements were taken at baseline (T0) and after 2 years (T1). Of the 69 children diagnosed with borderline SCRHD, 66 (95.6%) had blood samples collected at T0. Blood was not collected from 3 participants: 1) because of unsuccessful venipuncture and 2) because of acute infections at the time of evaluation, to reduce potential confounding from systemic inflammation.

Because of logistical challenges and participant attrition, 44 participants (40 with borderline SCRHD and 4 with definite SCRHD) returned for the T1 evaluation. Since group A streptococcal infections are often subclinical in children, the interval between infection and biomarker sampling could not be determined, and serologic confirmation of group A streptococcus exposure was not feasible. Importantly, no participants with active or recent infections were included in the study. Treatment and Follow‐Up

Children classified as borderline or definite SCRHD, based on the 2012 WHF echocardiographic criteria, 5 were referred for specialist cardiology evaluation. At enrollment, international guidelines—including those from Australia 16 and the World Health Organization 17 —recommended secondary prophylaxis with monthly intramuscular benzathine penicillin G only for individuals who met the criteria for a definite diagnosis. Prophylaxis was not advised for borderline cases during the study period. All participants with definite SCRHD were appropriately referred for cardiology follow‐up and initiation of prophylaxis. However, despite formal recommendations, none of these patients received penicillin at any point during the 2‐year follow‐up (T1). Following the publication of the Gwoko Adunu pa Lutino (GOAL) trial in 2022, which provided strong evidence supporting prophylaxis for both borderline and definite cases, all previously identified patients were recontacted and counseled to begin secondary prophylaxis in accordance with the updated international guidelines, in coordination with local public health authorities.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or mean±SD, as appropriate. The normality of distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with P<0.05 indicating departure from a normal distribution. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for 2 groups or the Kruskal–Wallis test for 3 or more groups, as appropriate. Following significant Kruskal–Wallis results, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn's post‐hoc test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d for continuous variables, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Changes in cytokine levels between T0 and T1 were assessed with the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for paired non‐parametric data. Inter‐rater reliability for echocardiographic assessments was evaluated using Cohen's Kappa coefficient. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 1026 participants were included, of whom 78 (7.6%) were diagnosed with SCRHD: 69 (6.7%) with borderline SCRHD and 9 (0.9%) with definite SCRHD (Table 2). The main echocardiographic findings are detailed in Table S1. The median age was 9 years (IQR 7–11), with no significant differences in age, sex distribution, or body mass index between the SCRHD and control groups (Table 3). At T0, IL‐6 levels were significantly higher in the borderline SCRHD group compared with controls (median: 49.9 pg/mL [IQR 5.8–1165.4] versus 15.9 pg/mL [IQR 2.7–80.5]; P=0.048) (Figure 4). The definitive SCRHD group also showed elevated IL‐6 levels relative to controls; however, the small sample size limited statistical significance (Tables 4 and 5).

TNF‐α levels showed a non‐significant trend toward elevation in the borderline SCRHD group compared with controls (median: 3.7 pg/mL [IQR 3.7–6] versus 3.7 pg/mL [IQR 3.7–3.7]; P=0.067). No significant differences were observed in IL‐2 (P=0.067), IL‐4 (P=1.000), IL‐10 (P=0.690), IL‐17A (P=0.067), or IFN‐γ (P=0.456) levels among the borderline SCRHD, definitive SCRHD, and control groups at T0. In the subgroup of participants with borderline and definitive SCRHD who returned for follow‐up (n=44), cytokine levels remained unchanged over the 2 years. IL‐6 (Figure 5) and TNF‐α levels remained elevated, with no significant changes between T0 and T1 (IL‐6: median 31.5 pg/mL[IQR 5.1–398.4] versus 42.3 pg/mL [IQR 3–854.5]; P=0.62; TNF‐α: median 3.7 pg/mL [IQR 3.7–3.7] versus 3.7 pg/mL [IQR 3.7–22.1]; P=0.144) (Table 6). Similarly, no significant changes were observed in IL‐2 (P=0.317), IL‐4 (P=1.000), IL‐10 (P=0.080), IL‐17A (P=0.715), or IFN‐γ (P=0.686) levels between T0 and T1 in the borderline plus definitive SCRHD group.

DISCUSSION

Rheumatic fever is a neglected and underdiagnosed disease, with no available laboratory tests to diagnose it or monitor its progression. Until now, there have been no studies analyzing cytokine changes in the borderline group, which represents the earliest detectable phase of subclinical rheumatic heart disease. It is important to emphasize that RHD remains a significant public health problem in developing countries, causing more than 200 000 deaths per year, 15 and efforts in primary and secondary prevention are highly effective. 18 In this context, echocardiographic screening with early diagnosis, as recommended by the WHF, may help reduce these rates.

RHD is a chronic sequela of rheumatic fever characterized by an autoimmune inflammatory response that progressively damages the heart valves, often necessitating surgical intervention. 11 Although the inflammatory nature of RHD is widely recognized, the immunological mechanisms underlying its development and progression remain uncertain. 3 , 11 This knowledge gap limits the development of effective immunomodulatory therapies to halt the progression from rheumatic fever to rheumatic heart disease, representing a significant challenge in disease management.

Cytokines, essential mediators of the immune response, play a vital role in the inflammatory process associated with RHD, both in initiating and regulating immune responses. 19 A detailed understanding of cytokine changes in the early stages of SCRHD can provide valuable insights into potential therapeutic targets. Severe valvular damage is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in RHD, and the absence of reliable markers to differentiate disease stages or to monitor the progression of the subclinical phase poses a significant challenge for effective clinical management. In this context, cytokines function as potential biomarkers to distinguish between disease stages, which is highly relevant to clinical practice. Accurate inflammatory markers are essential for risk stratification and guiding treatment decisions, ultimately enhancing patient management.

Our study highlights that borderline SCRHD represents the earliest detectable stage of subclinical disease. The borderline category encompasses subtle echocardiographic abnormalities that may precede the development of more severe valvular damage, as seen in definite SCRHD. 7 Early detection of borderline SCRHD is crucial, as a timely intervention could prevent progression to advanced disease stages and related complications. 20 Our findings suggest that IL‐6‐mediated inflammation might contribute to the clinical symptoms of valvular damage.

The significant increase in IL‐6 in children with borderline SCRHD suggests that inflammatory processes are active from the earliest stages of subclinical disease. IL‐6 is a multifunctional cytokine involved in immune regulation and inflammation, and elevated levels have been associated with disease activity in acute rheumatic fever and clinical rheumatic heart disease (RHD). 10 , 12 Studies show that elevated IL‐6 levels are detected upon admission of children hospitalized with acute rheumatic fever, 10 , 21 correlating with traditional inflammatory markers such as C‐reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate. Elevated IL‐6 levels have also been reported in patients with clinical 22 and severe RHD. 11 Diamantino et al. 11 suggested that the coordinated production of IL‐6 and IL‐17A in patients with less severe RHD may exert a protective effect. In contrast, the concurrent elevation of IL‐6 and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) is associated with severe RHD, indicating distinct patterns of immune response correlated with disease severity. Additionally, Silva et al. 23 demonstrated that reducing circulating IL‐6 levels after percutaneous mitral commissurotomy is associated with hemodynamic improvement and favorable outcomes in patients with rheumatic mitral stenosis, suggesting that IL‐6 may be a marker of inflammatory activity and a predictor of treatment response.

Although TNF‐α levels did not reach statistical significance, a trend toward elevation was observed in the borderline SCRHD. We observed this trend compared with controls, suggesting that TNF‐α may be involved in the early stages of the disease. Studies with larger samples may be necessary to confirm its relevance as a biomarker in SCRHD. The literature reports elevated TNF‐α in patients with acute rheumatic fever, 10 , 21 and those with clinical rheumatic heart disease (RHD), 9 which is associated with ongoing inflammatory processes.

These findings suggest that TNF‐α remains elevated throughout the entire spectrum of rheumatic heart disease, persisting in both subclinical and clinical phases, as well as in the valvular tissue of patients undergoing surgical interventions. Given its role in the more aggressive forms, this cytokine may be a potential therapeutic target. Interestingly, in rheumatoid arthritis, significant benefits have been observed with TNFα blockade, resulting in reduced disease activity. 21 This parallel suggests that TNF‐α inhibitors could be explored in SCRHD to prevent disease progression. Therefore, more detailed studies are justified to investigate therapeutic interventions based on TNF‐α blockade in RHD.

On the other hand, the levels of inflammatory cytokines IL‐17A and IFN‐γ did not show significant differences among the groups analyzed at the initial evaluation (T0), despite previous studies indicating a prominent role for these cytokines in inflammatory 24 , 25 and autoimmune responses. 25 Our findings suggest that, at the first assessment, their concentrations do not differ between healthy individuals and those with borderline or definite SCRHD. Middleton et al. 21 reported elevated interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ) levels during tonsillitis, indicating early involvement in inflammatory processes. Moreover, Guilherme et al. 9 demonstrated an elevation of IFN‐γ in the valvular tissue of patients undergoing valve replacement surgery for mitral stenosis. 9 , 26 Other studies reported a reduction in circulating IFN‐γ levels after percutaneous mitral commissurotomy, 23 , 27 which is associated with hemodynamic improvement. This may indicate that the expression of these cytokines occurs at distinct phases of disease progression or that they are not sensitive markers in the early stages of SCRHD.

The lack of significant changes in anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐2, IL‐4, IL‐10) in the SCRHD groups indicates a possible failure in regulatory mechanisms that counterbalance. IL‐4 and IL‐10 play a crucial role in regulating immune responses and facilitating the resolution of inflammation. 25 Our baseline assessment did not reveal any significant group differences. Compared to controls, the absence of elevation in their levels in the borderline and definite stages suggests that the anti‐inflammatory and immune responses are inactive in the early stages of the disease and remain stable between the borderline and definite stages of SCRHD.

The similarity in the behavior of these interleukins suggests that they act in a coordinated manner, possibly in a common regulatory mechanism or cascade, contributing to the perpetuation of the cardiac damage characteristic of the disease. The absence of significant increases in these anti‐inflammatory cytokines may indicate a deficiency in the regulatory response, allowing inflammation to persist and promote valvular injury.

We also compared IL‐2 levels among the different groups. This cytokine is multifunctional, exhibiting both proinflammatory and anti‐inflammatory functions in the immune system. 25 Notably, IL‐2 promotes the proliferation and activation of T cells, while also inducing the proliferation of regulatory T cells. Although many studies have investigated the regulation of regulatory T cell numbers, particularly concerning IL‐2, the regulation of their function remains an area of intense research. Previous studies have observed an elevation of IL‐2 upon hospital admission in patients with acute rheumatic fever 28 , 29 and active RHD. Additionally, elevated levels have been found in patients with clinical RHD compared with healthy individuals. 22 Furthermore, specific IL‐2 genotypes have been shown to predict the development of clinical RHD. 30 However, our study did not observe significant differences among the control, borderline SCRHD, and definite SCRHD groups.

IL‐4 regulates the humoral immune response, promotes tissue repair, and induces fibrosis. 25 Silva et al. 23 confirmed the elevation of this cytokine in severe RHD. They observed that its levels decrease after percutaneous mitral commissurotomy, associating this reduction with hemodynamic improvement and favorable outcomes. Diamantino et al. 11 showed that IL‐4 predicts adverse outcomes (death or need for valvular intervention) during the follow‐up of patients with subclinical and clinical RHD. In contrast, Guilherme et al. 31 demonstrated that the scarcity of IL‐4 in the valvular tissue of patients undergoing valve replacement surgery may contribute to the progression of RHD, suggesting a protective role for this cytokine.

Regarding IL‐10, available data in the literature also present heterogeneous results. Guilherme et al. 31 and Diamantino et al. 11 demonstrated no significant differences in IL‐10 levels between patients with acute rheumatic fever and clinical RHD, nor between stable and severe patients. These findings corroborate the present study's findings, which did not show differences between the borderline and definite phases. In contrast, research conducted by Diamantino et al. 11 demonstrated that elevated IL‐10 levels predict adverse outcomes during follow‐up, suggesting that, although there is no difference between disease stages, elevated levels may be associated with a worse prognosis. The imbalance between proinflammatory and anti‐inflammatory cytokines may be a critical factor in the pathogenesis of RHD.

Detecting and monitoring IL‐6 levels in children with borderline SCRHD could provide a means of identifying those at risk of progression to definite SCRHD. Early intervention strategies, including prophylactic antibiotics and regular follow‐up, may be more effective when initiated early. 20 Anti‐IL‐6 therapies, such as tocilizumab, have effectively treated inflammatory conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, by inhibiting IL‐6 signaling pathways. 21 In the context of SCRHD, targeting IL‐6 could reduce inflammation and prevent progression from borderline to definite SCRHD. However, clinical trials are necessary to evaluate the safety and efficacy of such therapies in pediatric populations with subclinical rheumatic heart disease.

Implementing cytokine‐targeted therapies in RHD presents challenges, particularly in resource‐limited settings where RHD is most prevalent. The high cost of biological agents and potential side effects, including immunosuppression, must be considered. Additionally, long‐term safety data for such interventions in children are limited. Our findings underscore the need for further research to explore the role of cytokine imbalances in the progression of RHD. More extensive longitudinal studies are necessary to confirm these results and to understand the mechanisms underlying the lack of elevation in anti‐inflammatory cytokines. Investigating interventions that enhance anti‐inflammatory responses may offer alternative therapeutic strategies.

LIMITATIONS

This study has limitations, including a smaller sample size at T1 because of participant attrition, which may affect the generalizability of the results. The small number of participants classified as having definite SCRHD limits the conclusions that can be drawn about this subgroup. Additionally, since this was a community‐based screening program, the exact timing of the antecedent streptococcal infection could not be determined for all participants. While this is a limitation, it is important to note that previous influential studies examining immunologic and echocardiographic markers in RHD cohorts have also not reported intervals between streptococcal exposure and assessment. 11 , 23 , 30 , 31 Therefore, the present findings align with the methodological standards of the field.

CONCLUSIONS

This study shows that children with borderline SCRHD have elevated IL‐6 levels from the earliest detectable stage of the disease. During a 2‐year follow‐up, consistently high IL‐6 and TNF‐α levels highlight a sustained proinflammatory state, indicating that chronic immune activation is already present during the borderline phase. The lack of increases in anti‐inflammatory cytokines suggests a possible failure in immune regulation that may contribute to disease progression.

Importantly, demographic and socioeconomic variables were not significantly associated with SCRHD in our cohort, highlighting the need for universal screening in similar populations, regardless of these variables. Early identification of borderline SCRHD and monitoring of cytokine profiles could provide critical opportunities for timely intervention and potentially alter the disease trajectory.

Therapeutic strategies targeting IL‐6 and TNF‐α show promise in preventing disease progression. Our findings indicate that anti‐IL‐6 and anti‐TNF‐α therapies deserve further investigation as potential treatments for SCRHD. Exploring ways to boost anti‐inflammatory responses might provide additional approaches to regulate the immune dysregulation seen in rheumatic fever.

Future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow‐up are essential to validate these findings and assess the efficacy and safety of targeted cytokine therapies in pediatric populations. Such research could greatly improve the management of rheumatic heart disease, ultimately reducing its global burden.
