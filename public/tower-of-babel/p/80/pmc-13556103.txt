Prevalence and associated factors of anxiety and depression among hemodialysis patients in Sudan during armed conflict: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Yasir Abdelrafi Dafalla Fadlelmoula, Mohamed Adil Ahmed Mohamed Ahmed Madani, Israa Alamin Mohammed Hussein, Moram Elfadel Abdelrhaman Gasmalha, Nadine Mohieldin Ibrahim Gasmelseed, Alzaki Joda Alzaki Hmidah, Marwan Abdulazeez Abdullah Mohammed, Tasneem Mohamed Abuobeida Mohamed, Hind Musa Mana Mohammed, Sohaib Mohammed Mokhtar Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) receiving hemodialysis are at high risk of depression and anxiety, especially in conflict settings where healthcare access is disrupted. No large-scale study in Sudan has simultaneously assessed depression and anxiety during armed conflict. This study aims to estimate the prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms and assess their associations with sociodemographic characteristics, dialysis duration, and comorbidities among hemodialysis patients in Sudan during the ongoing armed conflict.MethodsA multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted across 13 dialysis units in nine Sudanese states between April and December 2025. A total of 811 adult patients (≥ 18 years) on maintenance hemodialysis were recruited via convenience sampling. Depression and anxiety were assessed using the validated Arabic versions of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, respectively. Binary logistic regression identified factors associated with depression and anxiety.ResultsThe prevalence of depressive symptoms was 40.3% (n = 327) and anxiety symptoms 28.6% (n = 232). Comorbid diabetes mellitus (Depression: OR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.71–4.13; Anxiety: OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.28–3.14) and use of neurotoxic medications were associated with higher odds of both conditions. Longer dialysis duration (> 12 months) was associated with lower odds. Female sex and lower dialysis frequency (one session/week) were uniquely linked to higher odds of depression, while older age (≥ 74 years) was protective against anxiety. Geographic variation was observed, with higher odds in Gadarif state.ConclusionDepression and anxiety are highly prevalent among hemodialysis patients in Sudan, exacerbated by ongoing conflict. Diabetes, neurotoxic medications, and regional disparities are key associated factors. Targeted mental health screening and support should be integrated into nephrology care, focusing on high-risk subgroups and conflict-affected regions.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Hemodialysis, Depression, Anxiety, Armed conflict, Sudan, Mental health

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive and usually irreversible loss of kidney function, defined by a sustained reduction in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) to < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or evidence of kidney damage for at least three months [1]. Patients with CKD commonly experience a range of distressing symptoms, including fatigue, pruritus, irritability, anxiety, and nausea [2]. Globally, CKD affects about 13–14% of the adult population and is a major contributor to disability, cardiovascular complications, and premature death [3].

When CKD progresses to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), typically defined by an eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m², patients require renal replacement therapy, including dialysis or kidney transplantation. Kidney transplantation offers the best survival and quality-of-life outcomes; however, haemodialysis remains the most widely used modality worldwide, accounting for nearly 90% of dialysis treatment [4, 5].

Despite its life-prolonging role, haemodialysis substantially alters daily life through strict treatment schedules, dietary and fluid restrictions, and long-term dependence on medical care. These constraints contribute to reduced physical functioning, social isolation, and high rates of psychological distress, particularly anxiety and depression [6, 7].

Anxiety is a mental health disorder characterized by excessive fear, worry, and apprehension, often accompanied by somatic symptoms such as palpitations, dyspnea, and restlessness [8]. Depression is characterized by persistent low mood, loss of interest or pleasure, and cognitive and functional impairment lasting for at least two weeks [9]. These conditions are highly prevalent among patients with CKD, particularly those receiving dialysis, with reported rates approaching 40–50%, exceeding those observed in many other chronic medical illnesses [10].

Empirical studies consistently show a high burden of anxiety and depression among patients receiving hemodialysis across different regions of the world. Studies from Nigeria, Egypt, Lebanon, and Greece report anxiety prevalence ranging from 39.6% to 49.6% and depression ranging from 38.2% to 55% among hemodialysis patients [11–14].

The psychological vulnerability of patients with CKD is further amplified in settings affected by armed conflict. Evidence from Ethiopia and Syria shows that disruption of dialysis services, forced displacement, and loss of continuity of care significantly worsen psychological distress among dialysis-dependent patients [15, 16].

In Sudan, the armed conflict that began on April 15, 2023, between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) led to the closure of more than two-thirds of health facilities in conflict-affected regions. Furthermore, hemodialysis services were severely disrupted, resulting in treatment interruptions, displacement, and increased mental health burden among patients with CKD [17, 18]. At the population level, the war, displacement, and economic collapse have produced extremely high rates of psychological distress, with studies reporting depression and anxiety symptoms in more than 90% of affected individuals [19]. Depression and chronic medical illness have a bidirectional relationship, where each can worsen the course and outcomes of the other [20].

Patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis represent a highly vulnerable population during armed conflict because of their dependence on continuous medical care combined with high physical and psychological burden. Although previous studies in Sudan have examined depression or the general impact of war on dialysis patients, none have evaluated anxiety and depression together across a wide national, multicenter sample during an active conflict. This study therefore uses a large multicenter sample from public and private dialysis centers across nine states to systematically assess anxiety and depressive symptoms alongside key demographic and clinical factors.

This study aims to estimate the prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms and assess their associations with sociodemographic characteristics, dialysis duration, and comorbidities among hemodialysis patients in Sudan during the ongoing armed conflict.

Methodology

Study design

This was a multicenter, descriptive cross-sectional, health facility–based study conducted to assess the prevalence of anxiety and depression among patients with chronic kidney disease receiving maintenance hemodialysis. Data were collected between April and December 2025.

Study setting

The study was conducted in renal dialysis centers across Sudan. It included 13 dialysis units distributed across nine states: Khartoum, Al Qadarif, Kassala, Red Sea, White Nile, Sennar, Al Gezira, Northern State, and North Kordofan. These states represent major geographic regions of the country and were variably affected by the ongoing armed conflict, which disrupted healthcare delivery and dialysis services in several areas through reduced accessibility, intermittent supply chains, and population displacement. Patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis were recruited from these centers during routine dialysis sessions. The participating centers contributed variably to the study sample, with higher recruitment from Gezira State due to its larger functioning dialysis capacity during the study period.

Study population

The study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of chronic kidney disease who were receiving regular maintenance hemodialysis at the participating centers. Patients with known psychiatric disorders, acute kidney injury, cognitive impairment, or inability to communicate were excluded.

Because a complete national sampling frame of dialysis patients was not available during the conflict, a convenience sampling approach was used. This allowed broad inclusion across multiple centers but may introduce selection bias.

Data were collected through structured face-to-face interviews conducted by trained medical students. Interviewers received standardized training on ethical conduct, interviewing techniques, and administration of the study instruments to reduce interviewer bias and improve data reliability.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and mental health outcomes. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations.

Associations between anxiety or depression and categorical variables were examined using chi-square tests, and comparisons of continuous variables were performed using independent-sample t-tests. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of anxiety and depression, including sociodemographic factors (age, sex, marital status, education, occupation, income, and living situation) and clinical variables (duration of CKD, duration of dialysis, diabetes, and hypertension). Results were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 811 participants were included in the study. The majority were male (62.0%), with most participants aged 46–59 years (29.1%). Secondary education was the most commonly reported educational level (32.1%). Most participants had not been displaced in the past six months (78.9%) and reported regular access to healthcare services (87.9%). Regarding dialysis characteristics, the highest proportion of participants had been on dialysis for 12–35 months (25.4%) and received two dialysis sessions per week (85.2%). Nearly half of the participants experienced kidney failure for less than three months prior to initiating dialysis (49.8%). Hypertension was highly prevalent among participants (73.4%), while diabetes mellitus affected nearly one-third of the study population (31.4%) (Table 1).

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants

The distribution of participants across states showed that the majority were from Gezeira, accounting for 44.6% (n = 362) of the sample. This was followed by White Nile, which represented 20.0% (n = 162) of participants. Gadarif ranked third, contributing 11.8% (n = 96) of the study population. The remaining states accounted for smaller proportions of participants (Fig. 1).

The prevalence of depression among the study participants showed that 40.3% (n = 327) were classified as having depression (positive), while the majority, 59.7% (n = 484), were classified as non-depressed (negative) (Fig. 2).

The prevalence of anxiety among the study participants indicated that 28.6% (n = 232) were classified as having anxiety (positive), while the majority, 71.4% (n = 579), were classified as non-anxious (negative) (Fig. 3).

Prevalence of Anxiety

Binary logistic regression analysis identified several factors significantly associated with depression (positive vs. negative as the reference category). Male gender was associated with lower odds of depression compared with females (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.41–0.89, p = 0.011). State of residence showed significant associations, with lower odds of depression observed among participants from Gezira (OR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.24–0.90, p = 0.023), Kassala (OR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.06–1.00, p = 0.049), Northern State (OR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.06–0.90, p = 0.034), Red Sea (OR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.08–0.65, p = 0.005), and White Nile (OR = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.02–0.12, p < 0.001), compared with Gadarif. Longer dialysis duration was inversely associated with depression, particularly among patients on dialysis for 36–59 months (OR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.12–0.57, p < 0.001) and 60–119 months (OR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.18–0.65, p = 0.001), compared with those on dialysis for less than six months. Receiving two dialysis sessions per week was also associated with reduced odds of depression compared with one session per week (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.21–0.76, p = 0.006). In contrast, diabetes mellitus was strongly associated with higher odds of depression (OR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.71–4.13, p < 0.001). Additionally, the use of medications potentially harmful to the nervous system was significantly associated with increased odds of depression among those reporting definite use (OR = 2.67, 95% CI: 1.43–4.98, p = 0.002), possible use (OR = 5.14, 95% CI: 1.52–17.40, p = 0.009), or uncertainty about use (OR = 2.95, 95% CI: 1.41–6.17, p = 0.004), compared with non-users. Other variables included in the model did not show statistically significant associations with depression (Table 2).

Discussion

This study found a high prevalence of depressive (40.3%) and anxiety (28.6%) symptoms among patients with chronic kidney disease receiving hemodialysis across multiple centers in Sudan. These findings are consistent with reports from other low- and middle-income countries, including India, where the prevalence of depression and anxiety among patients with CKD has been reported at approximately 66% and 61%, respectively [24], as well as studies from Sudan, Pakistan, and Nigeria [11, 25, 26]. The high burden of psychological distress in these settings likely reflects the combined effects of socioeconomic hardship, limited access to dialysis and mental health services, late presentation of kidney disease, high comorbidity burden, and fragile health systems [27].

In Sudan, the ongoing conflict further exacerbates psychological distress by disrupting health services, worsening socioeconomic conditions, and creating uncertainty about treatment continuity and daily life [28]. However, despite the armed conflict, the prevalence of depression in our study was comparable to rates reported in some non-conflict hemodialysis populations, while anxiety prevalence remained lower than some international meta-analysis estimates. This may reflect the already high baseline psychological burden among hemodialysis patients, as well as cultural coping mechanisms, strong family support systems, adaptation to prolonged adversity, possible resilience among Sudanese patients, and potential underreporting of psychological symptoms.

Several factors were associated with both depression and anxiety in our study. CKD patients with diabetes had significantly higher rates of both conditions, consistent with findings from China and India [27, 29]. This supports the view that comorbidities such as diabetes increase psychological distress by adding to overall disease burden and treatment complexity [30]. In addition, longer dialysis duration (> 12 months) was associated with reduced odds of depression and anxiety, aligning with findings from Indonesia [31]. This likely reflects psychological adaptation over time and highlights the early dialysis period as a particularly vulnerable phase requiring targeted mental health support [31]. In Sudan, the severe shortage of mental health services leaves most CKD patients without psychological care, a situation worsened by the ongoing conflict [32].

Use of neurotoxic medications was the strongest factor associated with both depressive and anxiety symptoms. Neurotoxic drugs may exert direct neuropsychiatric effects, and CKD patients are particularly vulnerable due to impaired drug clearance and alterations in the blood–brain barrier [33]. In Sudan, this association may be intensified by limited medication counseling, fragmented follow-up, and disrupted health services in conflict-affected areas, which increase the risk of improper dosing and adverse drug effects [34, 35]. Geographic variation was also observed, with higher odds of depression and anxiety among patients in Gadarif and lower odds in Gezira and White Nile. These differences are likely associated with regional disparities in dialysis availability, travel burden, and continuity of care, which may influence both physical strain and psychological stress.

Our study also identified factors uniquely associated with depression. Female sex was significantly associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms, but not anxiety. A study from Pakistan reported higher prevalence of both depression and anxiety among females [24]. In contrast, a previous single-center study from Gadarif, Sudan, found no significant association between sex and depression [36]. The multicenter design of our study may explain this difference by capturing a more representative patient population.

Moreover, we found that CKD patients undergoing two dialysis sessions per week were associated with lower depression scores compared with those receiving only one session. This may be due to improved clearance of toxins and waste products, which reduces the physical symptom burden [37]. Interestingly, a study from India reported the opposite, where two dialysis sessions were associated with higher depression risk [38], possibly due to differences in patient populations or treatment protocols.

Factors uniquely associated with anxiety showed a different pattern. Older age (> 75 years) was associated with lower anxiety levels among CKD patients. This finding is consistent with a study from China, which also reported lower anxiety prevalence among older patients [39]. In contrast, a study from Algeria did not find a significant association between age and anxiety [40]. The lower anxiety observed in older patients may be related to greater illness acceptance, improved emotional regulation, and reduced future-oriented worries compared with younger patients, who often face greater social and economic pressures [41].

In contrast, no association was found between several factors including education level and depression or anxiety. While a study from India reported that 55.9% of patients with depression were illiterate, suggesting a protective role of education [42], in Sudan the high disease burden and health-care limitations may overshadow this association. Similarly, recent displacement was not independently linked to psychological distress, possibly due to a uniformly high baseline stress among CKD patients [43]. This may reflect a uniformly high baseline of psychological stress among CKD patients, which could mask additional effects of displacement. Despite its high prevalence, hypertension was also not associated with depression or anxiety, likely because it is often clinically silent, unlike diabetes, which involves complex self-management and symptomatic fluctuations [44].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. Its multicentre design, covering diverse regions in Sudan, enhances the generalizability of the findings to a broader CKD population. The use of validated Arabic versions of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 allowed for reliable assessment of both depression and anxiety, providing a comprehensive evaluation of psychological distress.

However, there are limitations to consider. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between CKD and psychological outcomes. Reliance on self-reported symptoms may introduce reporting bias, and the limited access to psychiatric evaluation in Sudan means that clinical confirmation of diagnoses was not always possible. The use of convenience sampling may limit representativeness, and interview-based data collection could introduce social desirability bias. Additionally, the ongoing conflict may have affected patient participation and access to dialysis, which could influence the observed prevalence estimates. Furthermore, the duration of displacement was not consistently documented due to the rapidly changing wartime conditions, and no minimum displacement period was defined. Finally, unequal geographic representation, with nearly half of participants from Gezira, may limit the generalizability of findings across all regions of Sudan.

Recommendations

Early screening for depression and anxiety should be prioritized among CKD patients, particularly during the first year of dialysis, which appears to be a critical period of vulnerability. Mental health services should be integrated into nephrology care, including counseling and, when indicated, pharmacological support, to ensure that psychological distress is addressed alongside physical health needs. Patient and caregiver education programs are essential to improve awareness of the potential risks of neurotoxic medications and to enhance understanding of mental health. Given the ongoing armed conflict, mental health interventions should be adapted to conflict-affected settings. This includes providing mobile or telehealth counseling, ensuring continuity of dialysis services through coordination with humanitarian agencies, prioritizing high-risk regions with disrupted healthcare, and training dialysis staff to recognize and support patients experiencing psychological distress. Finally, further research is needed to examine the longitudinal trajectory of psychological distress in CKD patients and to assess how conflict and resource limitations influence mental health outcomes.

Conclusion

Depression and anxiety are highly prevalent among CKD patients in Sudan. Factors associated with both conditions include diabetes, neurotoxic medication use, and geographic location, while female sex and dialysis frequency were uniquely associated with depression, and older age appeared protective against anxiety. These findings highlight the urgent need for integrated mental health care, early screening, and tailored interventions, particularly during the initial stages of dialysis and in regions affected by armed conflict. Addressing psychological distress alongside CKD management has the potential to improve patient outcomes and quality of life, especially in resource-limited and conflict-affected settings.
