Determinants of mortality in carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae ICU Infections: a multivariable clinical and predictive modeling analysis
Authors: Davut Ipek, Yusuf Ipek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study primarily aimed to identify independent predictors of ICU mortality among adult patients with microbiologically confirmed carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) infection. Secondary objectives included characterization of infection types, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and ICU length of stay within this CRKP cohort.MethodsThis retrospective study included 180 adult ICU patients with microbiologically confirmed CRKP infection between January 2020 and December 2024. Demographic and clinical variables—including comorbidities, vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, septic shock, and ICU length of stay—were extracted from electronic hospital records. Antimicrobial regimens and susceptibility profiles were recorded. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to determine independent predictors of mortality.ResultsThe overall ICU mortality rate was 75% (135/180). Non-survivors were significantly older than survivors (75 vs. 61 years, p = 0.003). Vasopressor use (83.7% vs. 24.4%, p = 0.001) and mechanical ventilation (99.3% vs. 75.6%, p = 0.001) were markedly more common among non-survivors. Multivariate analysis identified age (OR 1.04; p = 0.001), vasopressor use (OR 13.42; p = 0.0001), and mechanical ventilation (OR 14.19; p = 0.018) as independent predictors of mortality. ROC analysis showed good discriminatory performance for vasopressor use (AUC 0.810), mechanical ventilation (AUC 0.780), and age ≥ 68 years (AUC 0.720). No statistically significant association between specific antimicrobial combination regimens and survival was observed.ConclusionsCRKP infections in the ICU are associated with extremely high mortality, primarily driven by advanced age and acute clinical severity indicators. In this cohort, antimicrobial regimen type was not independently associated with survival, although treatment subgroup sizes were limited. Introduction

Intensive care units (ICUs) concentrate the most vulnerable hospital populations and serve as a major epicenter for healthcare-associated infections and antimicrobial resistance. Critically ill patients are predisposed to infection due to advanced age, impaired immunity, invasive procedures, mechanical ventilation, and prolonged exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics [1–6]. In this context, infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) microorganisms have become a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in ICUs where antimicrobial pressure is high [7, 8].

Among MDR organisms, carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria—especially Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae—represent an urgent global threat and have been classified as WHO “critical priority pathogens” due to their limited therapeutic options and high mortality burden [9]. Several recent ICU-based studies confirm that carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae are independently associated with increased mortality, prolonged length of stay, and higher rates of septic shock [10–12]. Similarly, P. aeruginosa demonstrates multidrug resistance patterns that significantly complicate empirical treatment strategies in critically ill patients [13–16].

While Gram-negative MDR pathogens dominate global concern, Gram-positive pathogens also pose substantial challenges. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) remains a common cause of ventilator-associated pneumonia and bloodstream infections in ICUs, and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) are increasingly associated with invasive infections and worse clinical outcomes [17–19]. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that VRE bacteremia is associated with significantly higher mortality compared with vancomycin-susceptible strains [20].

Although antimicrobial resistance patterns are broadly characterized, resistance epidemiology varies geographically and even between different ICUs within the same hospital. International guidelines therefore emphasize the importance of using local microbiological data and unit-specific antibiograms to guide empirical antibiotic therapy [21, 22]. Moreover, delays in appropriate empirical treatment—often driven by unexpected resistance—are consistently shown to worsen ICU outcomes, especially in cases of sepsis and septic shock [23, 24].

Beyond microbiological factors, prognostic biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), along with clinical severity scores such as APACHE II and the Charlson Comorbidity Index, increasingly aid in stratifying risk and predicting mortality in MDR infections [25–31]. However, there remains a need for integrated analyses that combine initial culture results, detailed resistance profiles, and clinical outcomes (mortality and ICU length of stay) within a single ICU cohort.

Therefore, the primary aim of the present study was to identify independent clinical predictors of ICU mortality among adult patients with microbiologically confirmed carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) infection. Secondary objectives included evaluation of infection characteristics, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and ICU length of stay within this defined CRKP cohort. By generating unit-specific data from a tertiary-care ICU, the study aimed to support risk stratification and antimicrobial stewardship strategies in CRKP management.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Mardin Training and Research Hospital, a 750-bed tertiary-care institution located in southeastern Turkey. The hospital comprises both medical and surgical intensive care units (ICUs) with a total capacity of 21 adult ICU beds. The study period extended from January 2020 to December 2024. Data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and microbiology laboratory database.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Mardin Artuklu University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2024/4–31, Date: April 16, 2024) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). All patient data were anonymized before analysis.

Study population and inclusion criteria

The study population consisted exclusively of adult ICU patients with microbiologically confirmed CRKP infection, and analyses were restricted to this defined cohort. During the study period (January 2020–December 2024), all adult ICU admissions were screened through the hospital microbiology database to identify patients with Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates. Among these, cases with carbapenem-resistant strains were evaluated. Patients aged ≥ 18 years with microbiologically confirmed ICU-acquired CRKP infection (defined as isolation ≥ 48 h after ICU admission) were included. Only cases with microbiologically confirmed CRKP isolation accompanied by compatible clinical signs of infection were included. Cases considered colonization without clinical evidence of active infection were excluded. After applying exclusion criteria, a total of 180 eligible patients constituted the final study cohort. Exclusion criteria were: (i) age < 18 years, (ii) isolation of K. pneumoniae within the first 48 h of hospital admission (community-acquired cases), and (iii) non-ICU hospitalization at the time of positive culture (Fig. 1).

Clinical data collection

Demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded, including age, sex, and comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease). Data on immunosuppression, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, presence of septic shock, surgical-site infection, and duration of ICU stay were also collected. Septic shock was defined according to the Sepsis-3 criteria as the need for vasopressor therapy to maintain a mean arterial pressure ≥ 65 mmHg and serum lactate > 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Because APACHE II scores were unavailable for all patients, we prespecified a severity proxy index to capture acute critical illness. The index combined three binary indicators—mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, and septic shock. A logistic regression model including these variables was constructed, and the estimated regression coefficients were used to calculate predicted probabilities of ICU mortality, which constituted the weighted severity index. This index was developed as an exploratory proxy measure due to the absence of complete APACHE II data and has not been externally validated. It should not be considered a replacement for established severity scoring systems such as APACHE II or SOFA. For transparency and clinical interpretability, an unweighted 0–3 score (simple sum of the three indicators) was also calculated and analyzed. For transparency, an unweighted 0–3 version was also computed. Because the weighted severity index was mathematically derived from vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, and septic shock variables, it was not simultaneously entered into the final multivariable regression model in order to avoid multicollinearity and circular modeling effects. Instead, the index was evaluated separately as an exploratory composite severity measure for discrimination and calibration performance. Model discrimination was quantified by the area under the ROC curve (AUC), and calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (10 deciles) and calibration plots (observed vs. predicted risk). Internal validity was examined with bootstrap resampling. The primary outcome was all-cause ICU mortality, defined as death occurring during the ICU stay. Secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay, distribution of infection sites (defined as ventilator-associated pneumonia, bloodstream infection, urinary tract infection, or surgical-site infection based on clinical and microbiological criteria), and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of CRKP isolates. Microbiological identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

All clinical specimens (blood, respiratory secretions, urine, and wound swabs) were cultured using standard microbiological techniques. Identification of Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using an automated system (VITEK® 2 Compact, bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem, ertapenem) were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [32]. Susceptibility to colistin and tigecycline was evaluated according to European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoints (version 13.0, 2023) [33]. Quality-control strains Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 were included in each run [32].

Antimicrobial therapy

Patterns of administered antimicrobial therapy were analyzed descriptively. Due to limited subgroup sizes, treatment regimens were not evaluated as primary outcome predictors but explored to provide contextual clinical information. Empirical therapy was initiated according to local infection-control guidelines and modified based on susceptibility results. Colistin was administered at a dose of 9 × 10⁶ IU intravenously every 8–12 h. Tigecycline was given as a 100-mg loading dose followed by 50 mg every 12 h. Meropenem (1 g every 8 h) and imipenem (500 mg every 6 h) were used according to clinician discretion, adjusted for renal function. Combination therapy was considered when clinically indicated, in line with international consensus recommendations [34]. Because of the retrospective design, the appropriateness of empirical antimicrobial therapy could not be systematically assessed for all patients. Specifically, detailed information regarding timing of therapy initiation, in vitro susceptibility concordance at the time of empirical treatment selection, and time-to-appropriate therapy was incomplete in the medical records. Therefore, antimicrobial regimens were analyzed descriptively and should not be interpreted as indicators of comparative therapeutic efficacy.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons between survivors and non-survivors were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of ICU mortality. This threshold was selected to avoid premature exclusion of potentially clinically relevant variables that might demonstrate independent effects after adjustment for confounding. Given the total number of mortality events (n = 135), the events-per-variable ratio in the multivariate model was considered adequate for reliable estimation of primary predictors. A Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the mortality prediction performance of significant variables identified in multivariate analysis. Area under the curve (AUC) values, optimal cut-off points, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated to assess discriminative ability. Additional exploratory analyses, including ROC curve analysis, calibration assessment, and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, were performed to descriptively evaluate discrimination and outcome patterns within the cohort. These analyses were considered supportive and exploratory given the relatively limited cohort size. A Cox proportional hazards model was also applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for mortality. The discrimination and calibration of the weighted severity index were assessed using AUC, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and calibration plots. Internal validation was performed by bootstrap resampling. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics of Patients with Carbapenem-Resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae Infection were shown in Table 1. Non-survivors were notably older than survivors (75.00 vs. 61.00 years, p = 0.003), indicating age as an important prognostic factor. Male sex was also more common among non-survivors (57.8%) compared with survivors (75.6%) (p = 0.033). Hypertension was significantly more prevalent in the non-survivor group (45.2% vs. 24.4%, p = 0.013), whereas diabetes mellitus showed a trend toward higher prevalence in non-survivors but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.067). Chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and COPD distributions were similar between the two groups. Immunosuppression was more frequent among non-survivors (10.4% vs. 2.2%), though not statistically significant (p = 0.162). ICU length of stay did not differ significantly between groups (25.00 vs. 19.00 days, p = 0.126). Importantly, vasopressor use (83.7% vs. 24.4%, p = 0.001) and septic shock (27.4% vs. 8.9%, p = 0.010) were markedly higher in non-survivors (Table 1).

Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis for Mortality in ICU Patients with Carbapenem-Resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae was shown in Table 6. Age was significantly associated with increased mortality (OR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.07, p = 0.001). Although hypertension and diabetes mellitus were prevalent comorbidities, neither showed a statistically meaningful association with mortality in the adjusted model (p = 0.236 and p = 0.795, respectively). In contrast, vasopressor use emerged as one of the strongest predictors of death (OR: 13.42, 95% CI: 4.49–40.08, p = 0.0001), underscoring the prognostic importance of hemodynamic instability. Mechanical ventilation was also significantly associated with ICU mortality (OR: 14.19, 95% CI: 1.56–129.06, p = 0.018); however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because mechanical ventilation intrinsically reflects advanced critical illness severity and is closely linked to ICU mortality itself. Septic shock did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with mortality in the multivariate model (OR: 0.92, p = 0.899) (Table 6; Fig. 2).

ROC Curve Analysis for Mortality Prediction was shown in Table 7. Age showed a moderate predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.720 (95% CI: 0.64–0.80) and an optimal cut-off value of ≥ 68 years, yielding a sensitivity of 71.9% and a specificity of 63.4%. Vasopressor use demonstrated the highest discriminative power among the evaluated variables, with an AUC of 0.810 (95% CI: 0.75–0.88), achieving a sensitivity of 83.7% and specificity of 75.6%. Mechanical ventilation also showed good predictive ability, with an AUC of 0.780 (95% CI: 0.70–0.86) and a notably high sensitivity of 99.3%, although specificity was comparatively low (24.4%) (Table 7; Fig. 3).

A Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using ICU length of stay as the time-to-event variable and ICU mortality as the outcome. Survival curves were compared according to vasopressor use and mechanical ventilation status. Patients receiving vasopressor support showed visibly lower survival probabilities over time compared with those not receiving vasopressors, with a borderline significant difference on log-rank testing (χ² = 3.74, p = 0.053). Discussion

In this study, we evaluated clinical characteristics, infection patterns, and mortality predictors among ICU patients diagnosed with carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) infection. Our findings demonstrate that advanced age, vasopressor use, and mechanical ventilation were the most significant contributors to mortality, while combined antimicrobial regimens showed limited impact on survival. These observations reflect the known clinical complexity of CRKP infections and align closely with the global literature on MDR Gram-negative pathogens.

Consistent with our findings that non-survivors were significantly older than survivors, Deshwal et al. reported that advanced age is an important independent predictor of mortality in patients with resistant Acinetobacter baumannii infections [10]. Epidemiological studies in critically ill populations show that older adults exhibit impaired immune responses and higher rates of organ dysfunction, contributing to worse outcomes in MDR infections [1]. Our results reinforce the role of age as a major risk factor and emphasize the vulnerability of older ICU patients in regions with high CRKP prevalence. Recent geriatric critical care literature further emphasizes that older ICU patients with sepsis represent a uniquely vulnerable population due to frailty, immunosenescence, reduced physiologic reserve, and increased susceptibility to organ dysfunction. Bosnak and Akova recently highlighted that optimization of outcomes in elderly septic ICU patients requires not only appropriate antimicrobial therapy but also comprehensive supportive care strategies, including early recognition of deterioration, individualized hemodynamic management, minimization of invasive procedures whenever feasible, and multidisciplinary nursing-centered care approaches [35]. These considerations may be particularly relevant in CRKP-associated sepsis, where mortality remains exceptionally high among older adults.

Mechanical ventilation—present in 99.3% of non-survivors in our cohort—was strongly associated with mortality. Grasselli et al. noted a comparable association between ventilator dependence and secondary infections in the ICU, demonstrating that prolonged ventilation significantly increases the risk of MDR bacterial colonization and sepsis [5]. Pickens et al. emphasized that ventilator-associated pneumonia caused by Gram-positive or Gram-negative MDR organisms remains a significant contributor to mortality in ICUs [17]. Taken together, these findings highlight the crucial role of respiratory failure severity and ventilator-associated complications in shaping outcomes in CRKP infections. Nevertheless, interpretation of mechanical ventilation as an independent mortality predictor requires caution. In ICU populations, mechanical ventilation is intrinsically linked to severe respiratory failure and end-of-life critical care management, creating a close association with ICU mortality itself. Therefore, mechanical ventilation in the present study should primarily be interpreted as a marker of advanced illness severity rather than a fully independent causal determinant of mortality.

Vasopressor requirement was another powerful predictor of mortality in our study, consistent with the understanding that hemodynamic instability reflects severe systemic infection and high mortality risk. Hwang et al. reported that patients requiring vasopressors for septic shock had markedly elevated mortality, particularly when appropriate antibiotic therapy was delayed [23]. Our multivariate model found vasopressor use to be one of the strongest predictors of death (OR: 13.42), confirming that acute circulatory failure is a dominant driver of negative outcomes in CRKP infections.

It is important to emphasize that the absence of an independent association between septic shock and mortality in the multivariable model should not be interpreted as evidence that septic shock is clinically unrelated to mortality. Septic shock and vasopressor use represent closely overlapping manifestations of severe circulatory failure, and vasopressor therapy constitutes a defining component of the Sepsis-3 septic shock criteria. Therefore, the attenuation of the septic shock effect after adjustment likely reflects collinearity and clinical overlap between these variables rather than a true absence of prognostic relevance. The significant univariate association observed in our cohort further supports the established relationship between septic shock and adverse outcomes in critically ill patients.

Interestingly, surgical-site infections were not significantly different between survivors and non-survivors, mirroring global trends showing that CRKP predominantly causes pneumonia and bloodstream infections rather than soft-tissue infections [11]. Respiratory-origin infections—frequently ventilator-associated—continue to be the leading infection site in CRKP cases, in line with our cohort’s high mechanical ventilation rates.

The high mortality observed in our cohort is consistent with multiple international studies reporting poor outcomes for CRKP infections. Giacobbe et al. recently highlighted that KPC-producing K. pneumoniae bacteremia carries a persistently high mortality rate despite improved therapeutic options [34]. Kumar et al. emphasized that MDR Gram-negative sepsis is associated with significant mortality due to limited treatment efficacy and rapid progression of septic shock [7]. Together, these studies underscore the severity of CRKP infections and the need for early recognition and intervention.

Regarding treatment, interpretation of antimicrobial regimen effectiveness in this study requires substantial caution because the appropriateness and timing of empirical antimicrobial therapy could not be systematically evaluated retrospectively. Consequently, the treatment analyses presented here should be considered primarily descriptive rather than definitive comparative assessments of regimen efficacy. Within these limitations, no statistically significant survival advantage was observed for any specific combination regimen used in this cohort. Although no statistically significant association between combination therapy and survival was identified in this cohort, the small number of patients in each treatment subgroup precludes definitive conclusions regarding therapeutic efficacy. Cincotta et al. reported that clinicians often favor combination therapy due to concerns about resistance, yet survival benefits remain inconsistent in real-world practice [21]. Paul et al. also cautions that evidence supporting combination therapy for CRKP is variable, suggesting that therapeutic success depends heavily on infection site, organism load, and timely optimization of therapy [22]. The absence of benefit in our cohort may reflect late presentation, high severity of illness, or intrinsic limitations in available antimicrobials.

Inflammatory biomarkers showed moderate mortality prediction performance. Age, vasopressor use, and mechanical ventilation were the strongest predictors in ROC analysis—supporting the notion that acute physiologic derangement rather than baseline comorbidity burden drives outcomes in CRKP infections. Similar findings were reported by Liang et al. and Zhou et al., who observed that PCT, CRP, and NLR correlate with disease severity but are often overshadowed by clinical instability and organ failure as mortality predictors [25, 26]. Our findings are consistent with this, as vasopressor use and mechanical ventilation demonstrated superior discriminative value (AUC 0.810 and 0.780).

The weighted severity index used in our study performed robustly (AUC 0.810) and showed good calibration. This mirrors the findings of Ali et al. and Tian et al., who emphasized that multivariable indices integrating acute physiologic variables outperform single biomarkers in predicting mortality in sepsis and MDR infections [28, 30]. Given its predictive accuracy and interpretability, this index may represent a practical tool for CRKP risk stratification in resource-limited settings. Although vasopressor use, septic shock, and mechanical ventilation reflect overlapping dimensions of critical illness, multivariate modeling allowed independent estimation of their prognostic contributions, minimizing confounding between severity indicators. However, this proxy index remains exploratory and lacks external validation against standardized severity scoring systems. Therefore, its clinical application should be interpreted cautiously. Importantly, because the weighted severity index was constructed directly from variables already included in the multivariable mortality model, it was intentionally analyzed separately rather than re-entered into the final regression model to avoid multicollinearity and overfitting effects.

Compared with previously published studies on CRKP, which often focused either on resistance mechanisms or isolated clinical outcomes, our study provides a more integrated analysis by simultaneously examining early culture results, detailed resistance patterns, clinical severity indicators, and both mortality and ICU length of stay within the same cohort. While prior research [10, 34] primarily analyzed bloodstream or specific-site infections, our work includes a broader range of infection types and incorporates a weighted severity index, offering a more comprehensive risk-stratification approach. Additionally, unlike studies that compare MDR versus non-MDR infections, our cohort consists solely of confirmed CRKP cases—allowing clearer identification of independent prognostic factors such as vasopressor use and mechanical ventilation. This positions our study as one of the few providing unit-specific, real-world ICU data that integrates microbiological, clinical, and predictive modeling elements.

Importantly, our analysis focused on all-cause ICU mortality rather than infection-attributable mortality. In critically ill patients, separating mortality directly attributable to CRKP infection from mortality related to underlying disease severity or multiorgan failure is methodologically challenging in retrospective designs. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the context of overall ICU mortality risk rather than strictly infection-specific fatality.

Another important consideration is the adequacy and timeliness of appropriate antimicrobial therapy. Delayed initiation of effective treatment is a well-established predictor of mortality in critically ill patients with multidrug-resistant infections. In the present retrospective study, detailed data on time-to-appropriate therapy and empirical treatment adequacy relative to susceptibility profiles were not systematically available; therefore, these variables could not be incorporated into the mortality model. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the observed associations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its single-center retrospective design limits generalizability, as microbiological ecology and clinical practices vary widely across ICUs and regions. The relatively small ICU capacity (21 beds) may further limit extrapolation of findings to larger tertiary referral centers with different case-mix characteristics. In addition, the inability to formally adjudicate infection-attributable mortality represents an important limitation, as deaths may have resulted from underlying critical illness rather than CRKP infection alone.

Second, although detailed clinical and microbiological data were collected, complete APACHE II or SOFA scoring data were unavailable, necessitating the use of a proxy severity index. The weighted severity index was not externally validated against established severity scoring systems, and its performance in other ICU populations therefore remains uncertain. Moreover, because the index was derived from variables already associated with advanced critical illness, it should be interpreted as an exploratory composite severity measure rather than a fully independent prognostic tool.

Third, the study could not systematically evaluate the appropriateness of empirical antimicrobial therapy, concordance with in vitro susceptibility results, or time-to-appropriate treatment initiation. This represents a major limitation because delayed or inappropriate empirical therapy is a well-established determinant of mortality in multidrug-resistant infections and substantially limits interpretation of treatment-regimen analyses. Similarly, antimicrobial selection was not randomized and may have been influenced by clinical severity (confounding by indication), further limiting causal interpretation of treatment-related findings.

Fourth, molecular characterization of resistance mechanisms (e.g., carbapenemase genes) was not performed, preventing deeper insights into the genetic determinants of CRKP virulence and treatment failure. In addition, the relatively small number of patients receiving specific antibiotic combinations substantially limited statistical power for subgroup analyses and restricted definitive conclusions regarding regimen effectiveness. Although the number of mortality events was adequate for multivariable modeling, the relatively small number of survivors may also have reduced class balance and limited discrimination stability in certain comparative analyses.

Furthermore, inclusion of mechanical ventilation in mortality modeling may partly reflect the intrinsic relationship between ventilator dependence and ICU death in critically ill populations, potentially limiting interpretation of mechanical ventilation as a fully independent prognostic factor. Lastly, unmeasured confounders—such as timing of antibiotic initiation, source control adequacy, prior colonization status, and differences in supportive care practices—may have influenced clinical outcomes. Although key severity indicators were incorporated into the multivariable model, residual confounding and overlap between severity-related variables cannot be entirely excluded. Despite these limitations, the study provides clinically relevant real-world ICU data regarding mortality-associated factors in CRKP infections and highlights the ongoing challenges posed by multidrug-resistant pathogens in critically ill populations.

Beyond mortality prediction, our findings strongly emphasize the critical importance of infection prevention and antimicrobial stewardship strategies in ICU settings with high CRKP burden. Colonization with multidrug-resistant organisms frequently precedes invasive infection and may provide a valuable opportunity for early intervention before the development of severe disease. Recent studies have highlighted that colonization by multidrug-resistant bacteria in critical care units is associated with multiple modifiable risk factors, including prolonged ICU stay, invasive device exposure, prior broad-spectrum antibiotic use, and mechanical ventilation. Early surveillance, contact precautions, antimicrobial stewardship programs, and rapid microbiological diagnostics may therefore play a crucial role in limiting CRKP transmission and progression from colonization to invasive infection. In this context, the work of Garcia-Parejo et al. and the accompanying editorial by Zahar and Moing-Locoz further underline the continuing global urgency of antimicrobial resistance and the need for proactive prevention-focused ICU strategies [36, 37].

From a clinical perspective, the findings of this study further emphasize the urgent need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship programs, rapid microbiological diagnostics, early identification of high-risk ICU patients, and strict infection-control measures to limit CRKP transmission. Optimization of empirical antimicrobial strategies based on local resistance epidemiology may help reduce delays in appropriate therapy. In addition, minimizing unnecessary invasive procedures and reducing prolonged mechanical ventilation exposure whenever feasible may contribute to lowering the burden of CRKP-related complications in critically ill patients. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating standardized severity scores and treatment-timing variables are needed to better define optimal management strategies for CRKP infections in ICU settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae infections remain a major clinical challenge in the ICU, driven by high rates of mechanical ventilation, vasopressor requirement, and advanced patient age—all of which emerged as strong independent predictors of mortality in our cohort. In this cohort, no statistically significant association between specific combination antimicrobial regimens and survival was observed; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited subgroup sizes. The strong association between clinical severity indicators and mortality highlights the importance of early recognition of high-risk patients and rapid optimization of supportive care. Our findings underscore the need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship, active colonization surveillance, early infection-control interventions, and prevention-focused ICU strategies, and the development of new therapeutics to combat the rising burden of CRKP in critical care settings.
