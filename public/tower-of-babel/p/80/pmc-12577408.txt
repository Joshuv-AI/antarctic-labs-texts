Comparison of fully-vaccinated and non/partially-vaccinated critically-ill COVID-19 patients in terms of disease severity: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Guillaume Louis, Vincent Dinot, Thibaut Belveyre, Ophélie Dirand, Paul Dunand, Cyril Cadoz, Rostane Gaci, Sébastien Gette, Pascale Perez, Christophe Goetz, Yoann Picard, Nouchan Mellati

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCritical care admissions due to breakthrough COVID-19 in fully vaccinated patients remain rare. Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has posed an unprecedented global health challenge, with critical illness and intensive care unit (ICU) admissions contributing disproportionately to mortality. Despite advances in supportive care, including corticosteroids and non-invasive oxygenation strategies, critically ill COVID-19 patients continue to experience high rates of invasive mechanical ventilation and death [1, 2]. These poor outcomes are partly explained by profound immune dysregulation, including excessive cytokine release, aberrant neutrophil activation, and T cell dysfunction, which together contribute to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and multi-organ failure [3, 4]. These immune abnormalities highlight the importance of preexisting immunity in the progression of the disease. Vaccination has proven to be the most effective intervention to prevent severe COVID-19 not only by inducing neutralizing antibodies but also by generating durable memory B and T cell responses that contribute to limiting viral replication and immunopathology during breakthrough infections [5–7]. Large cohort studies have confirmed that mRNA vaccination is strongly associated with reduced hospitalization and severity of disease, further supporting its critical role in mitigating COVID-19 outcomes [8]. However, variants of concern (VOCs) have continuously evolved and may erode previously induced immunity [9]. While most COVID-19 vaccines were initially developed as two-dose regimens, waning immunity over time and the circulation of novel VOCs have led to the recommendation of booster doses in many countries.

Although breakthrough infections requiring ICU admission remain relatively rare, they represent a clinically relevant scenario where vaccine-induced immune memory may influence disease trajectory. Data on the characteristics, management, and prognosis of such patients are limited, particularly in studies with well-defined vaccination status (including type, timing, and booster administration) and systematic assessment of the circulating variants [10]. Understanding whether vaccination attenuates disease progression once ICU admission is required is crucial not only for patient care but also for guiding public health strategies in the context of evolving VOCs. The present study therefore aims to describe the characteristics, management, and prognosis of fully vaccinated patients admitted to ICUs and to compare them with a non- or partially vaccinated population, while also evaluating the influence of VOCs.

Methods

Study design and ethics

The COMETE-19 registry is a near real-time observational database of COVID-19 patients in the ICUs of Mercy Hospital (Metz) and Bel Air Hospital (Thionville), which together form the Metz-Thionville Regional Hospital Centre. This tool was launched on 1 March, 2020. The study was conducted in accordance with the reference methodology MR-004 (N°2208231v0) of the French National Commission on Information Technology and Liberties (CNIL), and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04430322, registration date 2020-06-12). The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was designed in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies. It was approved by the ethics committee of the French Intensive Care Society (CE SRLF 22–062).

Patient selection

The registry contained data from 1,059 patients across two centres as of June 1, 2022. All patients were exported from the registry and divided into two groups: the fully vaccinated group and a control group composed of unvaccinated or partially vaccinated patients. Patients were considered unvaccinated if they had not received any dose of COVID-19 vaccine and partially vaccinated if they had received a dose but did not complete the full vaccination scheme. Another group, called “at least one dose”, included both fully vaccinated and partially vaccinated groups. Patients were considered fully vaccinated if they had completed a two-dose schedule with a time since vaccination of at least 14 days (or 28 days after a single dose of the Ad26.COV2S vaccine) but less than 6 months (reduced to 3 months for individuals aged ≥ 80 or immunocompromised individuals), prior to booster administration. The same 6- or 3- month timeframe was applied for a second or third booster. This threshold was chosen based on guidelines from the French Department of Health and Prevention, which recommended a booster dose beyond this period [11]. The remaining patients (i.e., unvaccinated or partially vaccinated) made up the control group. During the study period, all vaccines authorized in France were used among patients. These included mRNA-based vaccines (BNT162b2, Pfizer-BioNTech; mRNA-1273, Moderna) and adenoviral vector vaccines (ChAdOx1 nCoV-19, AstraZeneca; Ad26.COV2.S, Janssen). Data regarding COVID-19 vaccination status, including the number of vaccinations, vaccine products used, and dates of administration were obtained from AmeliPro, a centralized COVID-19 vaccine information system in France. To maintain comparability with the control group of non-vaccinated patients in light of the changing patient characteristics over the course of the pandemic, patients admitted to the ICU before the second wave (March-June 2020) were excluded from the analysis, since the characteristics and management of patients varied between the first and subsequent waves. During the first wave, our institution and the French Grand Est region overall were heavily affected by the COVID-19 pandemic. ICUs were overwhelmed, which is of relevance given that there appears to be a link between ICU patient load and COVID-19-associated mortality [12]. Additionally, nearly 20% of our patients considered the most stable and transportable were transferred to other ICUs outside the region to cope with this massive patient flow [13]. Besides, patient management improved during the second wave, with a widespread use of corticosteroid therapy and non-invasive oxygenation strategies [14–16]. Patients were treated according to the standard of care. Dexamethasone has been systemically administered for 10 days at 6 mg per day to all patients admitted to ICU since the second wave in July 2020, whereas it was rarely used before. Patients were admitted based on the French Society of Intensive Care (SRLF) guidelines for patient admission in a pandemic context [17].

Variables and outcome measures

The variables in the medical charts and electronic reports were recorded by the responsible critical care physicians at each ICU. The primary outcome was a binary measure that divided critically ill patients into those who needed organ support (mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [ECMO], dialysis, vasopressors) or died, and those who did not. High disease severity corresponded to a level ≥ 7 based on the World Health Organization COVID-19 Clinical Progression Scale. Specifically, Level 5 corresponds to “oxygen by mask or nasal prongs”, Level 6 to “oxygen by non-invasive ventilation (NIV) or high flow”, Level 7 to “intubation and mechanical ventilation with pO2/FiO2 ≥ 150”, Level 8 to “mechanical ventilation with PaO2/FiO2 < 150 or vasopressors”, Level 9 to “mechanical ventilation with pO2/FiO2 < 150 and vasopressors, dialysis, or ECMO”, and Level 10 to “death” [18].

Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, the need for mechanical ventilation (MV) and ECMO, and the need for NIV, high-flow nasal cannula, tracheostomy, vasopressors, or renal replacement therapy.

Statistical analysis

Patients’ characteristics were described for the entire study population and according to vaccination status. Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as n (%). Patient groups were compared using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Fisher’s exact test. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent risk factors for progression to high disease severity in patients according to vaccination status. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, as all eligible patients during the study period were included. Based on the empirical rule of 10 events per covariate, our cohort size was adequate for the multivariable logistic regression models. The data were expressed as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The significance level was set at 5%. All analyses were performed by using SAS 9.4 (SAS Inst., Cary, NC).

Results

Patient characteristics

From July 1, 2020 to June 1, 2022, 805 COVID-19 patients met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The median age of the analysed population was 65 (56–71) years, and 33% were women. Patients spent a median of 9 days in the ICU, MV was required in 50% of cases, and 175 (26%) died. Among the 805 admitted patients, 126 (16%) received at least one vaccine dose and 41 (5%) were fully vaccinated. The control group comprised 85 partially vaccinated (11%) and 679 (84%) unvaccinated patients. Table 1 shows baseline characteristics of the patients. Of the 41 patients in the fully vaccinated group, 34 (83%) received the BNT162b2 vaccine (Pfizer BioNTech) (Table S1). All had received two doses; 21/41 (51%) had received a booster and 6/41 (15%) a second booster (Table S1). The fully vaccinated and control groups did not differ in terms of age, gender, severity scores or comorbidities, except for chronic renal failure and ischemic heart disease. Fully vaccinated patients required less IMV (27% vs. 51%, p = 0.003) and had a lower mortality rate (15% vs. 26%, p = 0.026). Patients in the fully vaccinated and control groups spent a median of 6.8 and 8.9 days in the ICU, respectively (p = 0.22). ECMO was required in 5% and 4% of cases for median durations of 11.9 and 12 days, respectively (p = 0.67). Renal replacement therapy was conducted in 7% in each group (Table 1).

Overall, 128 patients were excluded from the analysis because of missing PaO2:FiO2 ratio (n = 18), variant of concern (n = 100) and BMI (n = 16) data. Subsequently, 372 patients with high disease severity (level ≥ 7) were compared to 305 patients with better outcomes (level ≤ 6). In univariate analyses, age, immunosuppression, higher SOFA score, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus were associated with high disease severity, while full vaccination status was associated with low disease severity (Table 2). In the multivariate model, older age, immunosuppression, and higher SOFA score remained independently associated with worse outcomes, while full vaccination status was associated with a significantly lower risk of progression to high severity (OR 0.30, 95% CI [0.11–0.74], p = 0.011) (Table 3). Sensitivity analyses excluding BMI and variant data (n = 801) yielded consistent results, confirming the robustness of these associations.

Variants of SARS-CoV-2 and vaccination status

Sequencing was performed in 705 (88%) of 805 patients. Among them, 176 (25%) were infected with the Delta variant, whereas wild-type, Beta, Alpha, and Omicron variants were responsible for 174 (25%), 125 (18%), 118 (17%), and 112 (16%) cases, respectively. Omicron was the most frequent variant in vaccinated individuals, while the Alpha and Beta prevailed in unvaccinated subjects (Fig. 3). In multivariate analysis, no variant strain was associated with higher disease severity or mortality, regardless of the vaccination status, with the Alpha variant serving as the reference.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that complete COVID-19 vaccination was associated with a reduced risk of progression to severe disease compared with partial or no vaccination. Fully vaccinated patients required invasive mechanical ventilation less frequently and showed a consistent trend toward lower ICU mortality.

Vaccination status was rigorously defined and verified through the French national registry, which provided exact information on vaccine dates, vaccine types, and booster status. This methodological rigor represents a strength of our study compared with previous ICU cohorts, which often relied on incomplete or self-reported vaccination data [23]. Our analyses also covered all major variants of concern (VOCs), none of which were independently associated with outcomes after adjustment for patient characteristics and vaccination status. These findings are consistent with population-level studies showing that vaccination significantly reduces the risk of hospitalization, ICU admission, and death [8, 24]. At the ICU level, similar results have been reported in large cohorts: a French nationwide analysis demonstrated that vaccination decreased the need for invasive mechanical ventilation and in-hospital mortality [25], and an Italian multicenter study confirmed the protective effect of vaccination even among patients requiring respiratory support [10]. The biological plausibility of this protective effect is supported by recent immunological studies. Booster doses induce strong neutralizing antibody responses that decline over time, but memory B cells persist and rapidly recall effective antibodies during breakthrough infections, limiting viral replication [5, 26]. In parallel, vaccine-induced CD4 + and CD8 + T cells are durable and broadly cross-reactive across SARS-CoV-2 variants, thereby preventing progression to respiratory failure despite reduced neutralizing activity [6, 7]. Vaccinated individuals who experience breakthrough infections also display attenuated systemic inflammation and faster immune resolution, reducing the likelihood of cytokine storm, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and multi-organ failure [27]. Moreover, the combination of natural infection and vaccination—known as hybrid immunity—confers particularly broad and durable protection, approaching complete efficacy against severe disease [28]. Although VOCs have modified the epidemiology of COVID-19, they did not independently influence outcomes in our cohort. Omicron lineages, for example, are more transmissible but intrinsically less virulent than Delta [29]. ICU data suggest that outcomes are primarily determined by host factors such as age, comorbidities, and immune status rather than the specific variant [30]. Breakthrough infections are more frequent in elderly and frail individuals, but vaccination and booster doses continue to substantially reduce the risk of severe outcomes, even in these vulnerable groups [31, 32].

The efficacy, immunogenicity, and safety of COVID-19 vaccines must also be considered. Large-scale studies confirm sustained protection against severe disease after booster doses, although protection against infection wanes over time [24]. Immunocompromised patients have lower responses, but repeated doses improve both antibody and T-cell immunity [33, 34]. Safety data are reassuring: myocarditis after mRNA vaccination is rare and less frequent than after SARS-CoV-2 infection [35] while neurological complications and thrombosis with thrombocytopenia after adenoviral vector vaccines remain exceptional [36, 37]. Although case reports have suggested that molecular mimicry may rarely trigger autoimmune events, systematic reviews confirm their extreme rarity, and the overall benefit–risk balance of vaccination remains overwhelmingly favourable [38]. Importantly, our ICU cohort did not reveal any unusual safety signals.

Finally, the strengths of our study include the precise determination of vaccination status, the systematic identification of SARS-CoV-2 variants, and the availability of detailed clinical information. Importantly, sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings, even when variables with missing data were excluded from the models. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design inherently carries a risk of bias. Second, the number of fully vaccinated patients was relatively small, reflecting both the real-world ICU epidemiology during the study period and our strict definition of “fully vaccinated.” In addition, missing data on serology and reinfections precluded a direct assessment of hybrid immunity, and the potential presence of false vaccination passes—although impossible to verify—cannot be entirely excluded [39].

Conclusions

This cohort study demonstrated that full vaccination was associated with a reduced risk of progression to severe disease and showed a protective trend against ICU mortality, regardless of the circulating variant of concern. These findings support the clinical relevance of vaccination even among patients who develop critical illness, reinforcing the importance of maintaining high vaccination coverage and timely booster campaigns in populations at risk.
