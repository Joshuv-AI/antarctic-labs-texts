The role of pre-operative sit-to-stand assessment in robotic hysterectomy surgical outcomes: An observational cohort study
Authors: AR Walker, YS Chen, S Leite, K Durning, L Alvarado, D Negatu, SP Huepenbecker, N Taylor, Israel Zighelboim, A Graul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A sit-to-stand (STS) assessment is a simple test and surrogate marker of frailty.•Below average STS is associated with more complications, re-operations, readmissions.•STS may to help risk-stratify patients preoperatively.Keywords: Robotic hysterectomy, Pre-operative testing, Frailty, Readmission, Complication

Introduction

The World Health Organization classifies the sit-to-stand (STS) maneuver as a measure of functioning, disability, and overall health (International Classification of Functioning, 2024). This short examination can be performed in the office setting by measuring the number of times a patient can repeatedly stand up from a seated position within 30 s (McAllister and Palombaro, 2020, Bohannon et al., 2010). Though healthy adults are normally capable of the STS maneuver, performance has been shown to be decreased in frail patients (Tang et al., 2021, Park et al., 2021). In the context of surgery, regardless of approach, frailty is known to be associated with longer hospital stays, higher rates of post-operative complications, poor discharge disposition, and increased mortality (Buigues et al., 2015, Lin et al., 2016).

Across all surgical specialties, and specifically within gynecologic oncology, there has been an increase in the practice of minimally invasive surgery (MIS). Compared to laparotomy, MIS has the advantage of lower rates of complications such as blood loss, ileus, and post-operative arrhythmias as well as shortened hospital length of stay (LOS), lower readmission rates, decreased hospital costs, and improved post-operative pain control (Walker et al., 2009). Furthermore, robotic-assisted laparoscopy has a lower rate of conversion to laparotomy, shorter operative times, and further reduction in hospital stay compared to traditional laparoscopy (Corrado et al., 2018). Outside of the gynecologic literature, other studies have found that frail patients may stand to benefit the most from MIS approaches with less complications, shorter procedure times and decreased blood loss (Maegawa et al., 2022, Li et al., 2024).

Prediction of poor surgical outcomes for elderly or frail patients is an opportunity for surgeons to decrease morbidity and mortality for patients with high surgical risks and improve the utilization of hospital resources. Although frailty has been linked to adverse postoperative outcomes in gynecologic oncology and other surgical specialties, there is scarce evidence evaluating simple physical performance tests such as STS in patients undergoing gynecologic hysterectomy (RH), benign or oncologic. We therefore aimed to determine if preoperative STS testing is associated with postoperative complications, re-operation, and readmission after RH. We hypothesized that poor performance on STS testing will correlate with poor outcomes after RH.

Methods

Study design

This study was a prospective, observational study of women aged 18 years and older who underwent a RH within our community-academic hybrid institution from January 2022 to June 2023 by one of the 4 gynecologic oncologists in our practice. The study was powered to investigate the relationship between patient anthropomorphic measurements, specifically increased waist-to-hip ratio versus a normal ratio, with a primary outcome of operative time. Concurrently, data was collected to assess for frailty using the STS maneuver with comparison to collected independent variables. For the STS analysis, we excluded patients with an inability to perform the STS maneuver, as the clinical suspicion for frailty would at baseline be higher in this patient population. Thus, excluding this population would allow the study to focus on recognition of more occult patient frailty in whom providers may have had a lower pre-test suspicion. Additional exclusion criteria included metastatic disease (as this is an independent predictor of outcomes) and decisional impairment. Surgical data were collected including intra-operative and 30-day postoperative Clavien-Dindo grade II – IV complications (Dindo et al., 2004) (hemorrhage, bowel injury, vascular injury, bladder/ureteral injury, incisional/vaginal cuff infection and/or dehiscence, anesthetic complications), re-operation, LOS, and readmission. Demographic, comorbidity, and clinical characteristics were obtained via electronic medical record review and stored in a REDCap database. This study was approved by the institutional review board (SLIR #2021-116) prior to recruitment and written informed consent was obtained by participants.

Sit to stand maneuver

A 30 s STS test was performed at patients’ preoperative appointment. To standardize the maneuver, we utilized a slightly padded standard armrest chair with a seat height of 17 in., which was stabilized against the wall. For patients who were unable to use this chair due to obesity or other reasons, a wheelchair was used with the brakes engaged. With feet flat on the floor, patients were instructed to rise from a seated position to stand with their hips fully extended. After confirming the patient’s movement ability, testing was performed by counting and recording the number of sit-to-stand repetitions completed in 30 s (McAllister and Palombaro, 2020, Bohannon et al., 2010).

Statistical analysis

The number of STS repetitions was categorized for each participant as above or below average for age per CDC guidelines (Table 1) (CDC, 2024). Patients younger than 60 years-old were not included in the CDC guidelines and we opted to categorize these younger patients in the 60 to 64 years-old group, as younger patients are more likely to have a higher number of repetitions, we reasoned that a below average value in this category would represent significant frailty, effectively increasing the test’s specificity. The primary outcome examined in these two groups was a composite of postoperative complications, re-operation, and readmission. The initial clinical study was powered to detect the rarer outcome of a 30-minute operative time difference among anthropomorphic differences with a power of 80% and an alpha of 0.05.

The data was aggregated and summarized using mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables while categorical variables were summarized using frequency and percent. Comparisons between binary outcomes were conducted using an unpaired t-test, Fishers exact test, and chi square tests. Comparison across groups of 3 or more were compared using ANOVA, Wilcoxon Ranked sum test, and chi-square tests. These analyses were conducted using Stat V.17.

Due to the low number of events in the composite outcome, we applied Firth’s penalized logistic regression, which is designed to mitigate small sample and event bias and yield finite, more stable estimates when standard maximum‑likelihood models can be unstable (Heinze and Schemper, 2002, Doerken et al., 2019). This approach has been increasingly utilized in recent clinical publications facing similar sparse data and low event challenges (Yakob et al., 2025, Marchincin et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2025). This analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.5.1), as this feature is not available in Stat. Firth’s method addresses the issue of small-sample bias by introducing a penalization term, which helps produce more reliable estimates when event counts are sparse (Heinze and Schemper, 2002, Doerken et al., 2019).

Five binary variables were derived for use in the analysis. The primary predictor, STS performance, was coded as a binary variable indicating below average or above average based on predefined age classifications. Race was dichotomized as Caucasian/White versus all other racial categories. The presence of any comorbidity and any prior abdominal surgery were each coded as binary variable. The primary outcome was a composite binary variable, defined as the occurrence of one or more of the following events: 30-day readmission, re-operation, or any post-operative complication. The association between the predictor variables and the composite outcome was evaluated using the firth's penalized logistic regression model by installing and loading the ‘logistf’ package in R. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 190 patients consented to participate in this study. One hundred seventy-five completed the STS maneuver, underwent RH with a gynecologic oncologist at our institution during the 18-month collection period from 01/2022 through 06/2023, and were included in the analysis. Fifteen patients were unable to complete the maneuver and were thus excluded.

The average age was 60 years (SD = 12 years) and patients were predominately white/Caucasian (87%), and obese (70%) (Table 2). Patients were largely American Society of Anesthesiology class II or III (95%), undergoing RH for uterine indications (73%), and most commonly found to have benign pathology (46%). One hundred thirty-seven patients (78%) were categorized as above average STS performers for their age while the remaining 38 participants (22%) were classified as below average (see Supplemental Fig. S1). There were no significant differences in demographics including smoking status, body mass index, medical comorbidities, or surgical history between above average and below average performers. There were no significant differences in procedure performed or procedure-related times (Table 3).

The primary composite outcome of complication, re-operation, or readmission within 30 days was statistically significant with more events occurring in the below average STS group compared to the above average group (16% versus 2%, p = 0.004). The overall complication rate in the cohort was 3% (n = 6); significantly more complications occurred in the below average STS group compared to the above average group (13% versus 0.7%, p < 0.001). The overall rate of reoperation within the cohort was 2% (n = 3), all of which occurred in the below average STS group (8% versus 0%, p < 0.001). Most complications and re-operations were related to pelvic abscesses and vaginal cuff dehiscence (Table 4). The overall readmission rate was 5% (n = 8) with significantly more readmissions occurring in the below average group (16% vs 2%, p = 0.004). Reasons for readmission included ileus, nausea and vomiting, acute kidney injury, abdominal pain, sepsis, and incisional infection. There was no difference in LOS between the two groups.

The multivariable Firth’s logistic regression model was statistically significant overall (p = 0.011), indicating that the set of predictors was significantly associated with the composite outcome. After adjusting for other covariates, only the STS variable was statistically significant (Table 5). Patients who performed below average on the age-specific STS had over nine times the odds of experiencing an adverse outcome compared to those who performed above average (OR = 9.50; 95% CI: 2.29–56.5; p = 0.002). None of the other variables in the model showed a statistically significant association with the outcome (p > 0.05 for all).

Discussion

In this exploratory, prospective cohort study of women undergoing RH with a gynecologic oncologist, the STS assessment was a significant predictor of a composite of post-operative complication, re-operation, and readmission rates after adjusting for common factors associated with peri-operative morbidity including obesity, previous surgery, race, and age.

The extent of surgical literature exploring the correlation between frailty and poor surgical outcomes has increased over the last decade. A large retrospective cohort study of more than 400,000 patients undergoing laparotomy, laparoscopy, or orthopedic procedures conducted by Shinall et al found that frail patients had a 30-day mortality risk of 1.5%, which was 47.8 times higher than patients without frailty (Shinall et al., 2020). Emergent cases have shown even worse outcomes; Collins et al found that in frail patients, emergency general surgery procedures that are generally considered low-risk posed similar postoperative complications, failure-to-rescue, and readmission as that of high-risk procedures (Collins et al., 2023). These trends among gynecologic oncology patients have proven congruent with the general surgery literature, where frailty has been shown to serve as a marker for higher rates of complications, myocardial injuries, prolonged LOS, non-home discharge, and non-initiation/non-completion of chemotherapy in both laparotomies and laparoscopic approaches (Uppal et al., 2015, Mah et al., 2021). This STS study is consistent with these trends in that below average STS performance as a surrogate marker for frailty was predictive of adverse post-operative outcomes, even in MIS cases which are generally considered lower risk.

Given observed trends, including our own findings, the question of how best to assess frailty pre-operatively has become critical. The 5-Factor Modified Frailty Index (mFI) has been demonstrated to be predictive of Clavien grade III to V post-operative complications, non-home discharge, and non-completion of chemotherapy in a cohort of patients ≥ 70 years old undergoing laparotomy (Mah et al., 2021). This trend has also been demonstrated specifically in MIS cohorts; Schipa et al and Hermann et al found the mFI to be associated with re-admission, complications, and mortality specifically in gynecologic MIS cohorts (Schipa et al., 2023, Hermann et al., 2022). In our study, the STS was similarly correlated with complications and readmissions, as well as re-operation.

Though the mFI is the most widely evaluated tool in assessing frailty in gynecologic oncology patients, other instruments have also been assessed. Sawada et al found that in a retrospective cohort of 65 patients undergoing MIS, the Geriatric 8 screening tool was predictive of perioperative complications (Sawada et al., 2024). Likewise, the Fried Frailty Criteria and Johns Hopkins Adjusted Clinical Groups index both predicted complications, though both were nearly all or exclusively evaluated in laparotomies (Courtney-Brooks et al., 2012, Sia et al., 2020). Lastly, the Driver’s tool, which is a collection of clinical markers of frailty rather than an instrument, was shown to be predictive of decreased disease free and overall survival; however, the route of surgery wasn’t specified in this cohort and all patients received both adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation (Driver and Viswanathan, 2017). It is difficult to compare the Driver study directly, as our cohort was mostly comprised of benign pathology and earlier stage malignancies. Our cohort’s rate of frailty of 21% is congruent with the rates in the aforementioned studies, which ranged from 6 to 27% (Schipa et al., 2023, Hermann et al., 2022, Sawada et al., 2024, Sia et al., 2020, Driver and Viswanathan, 2017, Donato et al., 2021). In aggregate, the evidence strongly supports integration of a pre-operative frailty assessment into routine clinical practice to help identify risk and counsel appropriately.

While there is no standard validated measure of frailty in gynecologic patients undergoing MIS RH, the STS evaluation has multiple advantages compared to other measures. As time with patients becomes increasingly limited in the office setting, the need for easily and rapidly administered frailty assessments is increasingly important. On average, oncologists report spending 17–24 min with each patient while OB/GYNs spend around 13–16 min (Report, 2017). Pre-operative visits require thorough medical, surgical, and social history taking, as well as physical exam and surgical counseling with informed consent. This easy maneuver can be used to help identify and triage patients at-risk for adverse outcomes after surgery even when low risk MIS is being performed. Compared to measures used in the studies discussed above, including the mFI, Geriatric 8, Fried Fraility Criteria, Johns Hopkins Adjusted Clinical Groups index, and Driver’s tool, the STS does not require questionnaire administration or collection, uses fewer variables, only takes 30 s to administer, and can be completed in a small examination room.

Strengths of this study include that this cohort is a representative sample of patients in our community-academic hybrid institution. It also includes both benign and oncologic indications, increasing the chance that the findings will be generalizable to a wide range of both gynecologic and gynecologic oncologic patients. The STS is validated and easy to administer as noted above. Additionally, we included a multivariate regression which provides increased validity to our findings.

Limitations of our study include a mostly white/Caucasian cohort which may not accurately represent a more racially diverse patient population. The benign and oncologic patients were analyzed as one population to preserve the total cohort size; conclusions about STS as a predictor specifically oncology patients cannot be drawn. The model detected a strong association between STS and adverse outcomes; however, the sample size may have been insufficient to identify more modest effects from other predictors. Patients who were not able to perform the STS maneuver as part of the larger primary study were excluded; conclusions about this maneuver and correlation with postoperative outcomes in this population cannot be drawn. One key limitation is the low number of composite events (n = 9), which resulted in a low EPV for the multivariable model. To reduce small sample bias, we used Firth’s penalized likelihood. While this approach yields more stable estimates than standard logistic regression, the confidence intervals remain wide, reflecting the inherent uncertainty due to the limited number of events. Accordingly, these results should be considered hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory, and they provide a preliminary foundation for future, adequately powered studies. The wide confidence interval for the STS odds ratio indicates imprecision in the effect estimate.

Because this study is hypothesis generating, further research is needed to identify methods to reduce readmission and complication rates in those higher risk, frail patients prior to undergoing surgery, including MIS surgery. Once risk is identified, other potential interventions could include a pre-habilitation, comprehensive geriatric assessment, nutrition supplementation and weight management, evaluation of mood and cognitive disorders, osteoporosis screening and treatment, reducing polypharmacy, and other clinical testing, such as spirometry, as appropriate based on a patient’s medical history (Dhesi et al., 2019).

In conclusion, a modified 30 s STS test in the preoperative setting is predictive of complications, re-operation, and readmission rate after RH even after adjusting for other factors associated with postoperative complications. While further research is needed to address how preoperative frailty can be mitigated before surgery, frailty assessment can help inform treatment planning decisions and risk discussions between physicians and patients.
