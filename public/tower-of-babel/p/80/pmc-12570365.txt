Endolysosomal dysfunction impairs proteostasis and induces neurodegeneration in vivo

ABSTRACT
SummaryTransactive response (TAR) DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) inclusions are a pathological hallmark of the frontotemporal dementia (FTD)-amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) spectrum. Dysfunction of the endolysosomal system, which plays a crucial role in protein trafficking and maintaining proteostasis, has been implicated in FTD-ALS pathogenesis. While the impact of endolysosomal dysfunction on TDP-43 pathology remains unclear, we demonstrated that disrupting the endolysosomal pathway by expressing the constitutively active endosomal protein, Rab5Q79L, induces TDP-43 aggregation in cultured cells. Here, we generated a mouse model expressing GFP-tagged Rab5Q79L, demonstrating that GFP-Rab5Q79L mice exhibit early motor deficits and endolysosomal dysfunction, including enlarged endosomes, abnormal lysosome morphology, and p62- or ubiquitin-positive inclusions. These mice also developed significant neuronal loss, neuroinflammation, phosphorylated TDP-43 (pTDP-43) inclusions, and nuclear envelope and nuclear pore structural defects reminiscent of FTD-ALS. Accordingly, GFP-Rab5Q79L mice will prove useful in expanding our understanding of endolysosomal dysfunction in proteostasis and pTDP-43 pathology.

Introduction

Endolysosomal system dysfunction has been implicated in many neurodegenerative disorders including the frontotemporal dementia (FTD)-amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) spectrum and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), among others.1,2,3 These diseases are often characterized by protein aggregates arising from disrupted protein trafficking and proteostasis. These aggregates are composed of proteins such as the transactive response (TAR) DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43), tau, α-synuclein, or amyloid beta, among others, contributing to cytotoxicity and cell death, exacerbating disease.4,5 Expanding our understanding of the endolysosomal system and its contribution to protein aggregation in neurodegenerative diseases is essential to identifying therapeutic avenues for treatment.1,3

Recent studies explored the molecular underpinnings of these disorders to establish how the endolysosomal system modulates disease pathology.6,7,8,9,10,11,12,13 Our work and that of others have pinpointed several endolysosomal genes that exacerbate neuropathology in FTD-ALS and AD including TRAF family member-associated NF-κB activator (TANK) binding kinase 1 (TBK1),6,7 chromosome 9 open reading frame 72 (C9orf72) bearing a G4C2 repeat expansion,6,14 progranulin (GRN),8 valosin-containing protein (VCP),9 charged multivesicular body protein 2B (CHMP2B),10,11 and the transmembrane protein 106B (TMEM106B).12,13 Notably, many of the protein products arising from these endolysosomal system-related genes are modulated in the early stage of disease,15 underscoring the endolysosomal system as a valuable therapeutic target for treating neurodegenerative diseases.16

One early neuropathological marker of endolysosomal dysfunction is endosomal enlargement.1 Endosome trafficking and fusion are regulated by the small guanosine triphosphatase, Rab5.17 Rab5 dysfunction is implicated in several neurodegenerative diseases, leading to enlarged endosomes, hindering the trafficking of key disease-causing proteins.18 Overexpression of wild-type Rab5 in mice recapitulates a fraction of these phenotypes, leading to mislocalized and enlarged endosomes and ablating synaptic plasticity.16,19 Rab5Q79L, a constitutively active Rab5 mutant, plays an imperative role in studying the endolysosomal pathway. This mutant form of Rab5 is locked in an activated (guanosine triphosphate-bound) state, thereby prolonging its activity and accelerating early endosome formation and maturation.20 Its presence in cells mimics conditions where Rab5 is constitutively active, affecting processes such as endocytosis, membrane trafficking, and signal transduction.15,19,21,22 Studies employing Rab5Q79L have provided insight into how prolonged activation of Rab5 impacts cellular functions, including modeling endolysosomal dysfunction in diseases such as FTD-ALS and AD.23 How Rab5Q79L contributes to endolysosomal dysfunction and protein aggregation in vivo is yet to be elucidated.

Our recent work uncovered endolysosomal dysfunction as an upstream driver of TDP-43 proteinopathy in FTD-ALS.6 Using a cellular model, we previously observed that Rab5Q79L induces TDP-43 aggregation.6 To elucidate how an impaired endolysosomal system impacts TDP-43 proteinopathy, behavioral abnormalities, and other aberrant traits, we developed a mouse model expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged Rab5Q79L using the adeno-associated virus (AAV). Our GFP-Rab5Q79L mice displayed early-onset motor deficits that progressively worsened with age. Additionally, GFP-Rab5Q79L mice exhibited enlarged endosomes and endolysosomes, as well as abnormal lysosomal morphology and p62- or ubiquitin-positive inclusions, indicating endolysosomal dysfunction and impaired proteostasis. Moreover, these mice displayed increased neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and aggregates composed of phosphorylated TDP-43 (pTDP-43). Lastly, we observed defects in nuclear envelope morphology and nuclear pore structures, suggesting that endolysosomal dysfunction disrupts nuclear envelope integrity. By recapitulating endolysosomal deficits observed in FTD-ALS, data from our novel GFP-Rab5Q79L mouse model indicate that endolysosomal dysfunction is sufficient to induce motor deficits, pTDP-43 pathology, neurodegeneration, and neuroinflammation in vivo.

Results

GFP-Rab5Q79L mice exhibit motor deficits

To investigate the ramifications of Rab5 dysfunction in vivo, the Nixon group generated a mouse model overexpressing wild-type Rab5.16 These mice exhibited a 2.5-fold increase in Rab5 expression compared to control mice, manifesting mild endosomal dysfunction and AD-like phenotypes between 6 and 15 months of age.16 To create a mouse model with more robust endosomal deficits, we utilized a constitutively active form of Rab5, Rab5Q79L, described in 1994 and predicted to be synonymous to the constitutively active Q61L mutation in Ras.20 Since then, Rab5Q79L has been well characterized in cellular models and found to enlarge endosomes.23 Moreover, Rab5Q79L induces TDP-43 mislocalization and aggregation in vitro,6 recapitulating observations in TDP-43 proteinopathies.24 To understand how Rab5Q79L modulates the endolysosomal system in vivo, we generated AAVs expressing either GFP-tagged Rab5Q79L or GFP alone to drive expression in the mouse central nervous system. Consistent with our previous findings,6,25,26,27,28 GFP-Rab5Q79L was expressed mainly in MAP2-positive neurons rather than in GFAP-positive astrocytes and Iba1-positive microglia (Figures S1A–S1C).

We first performed a simple phenotypic assessment of our GFP-Rab5Q79L mice by conducting a hindlimb clasping assay at 30, 45, and 60 days of age, observing significant motor defects in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice at 30 days, which progressively worsened (Figure 1A). No motor defects were observed in GFP control mice. Given these initial observations, subsequent studies were conducted using 3-month-old mice. First, we measured the motor coordination and strength of GFP-Rab5Q79L mice using the hanging wire assay. Compared to mice in the control group averaging two falls in 2 min, we noted a significant increase in the frequency of falls (13 falls in 2 min) in the GFP-Rab5Q79L group (Figure 1B). Following behavioral testing, we harvested brains from 3-month-old mice for further analysis. We performed immunoblotting to detect GFP-Rab5Q79L expression (Figures 1C and S5A–S5C), observing that total Rab5 protein levels (endogenous combined with exogenous) were moderately increased in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice, being approximately 2-fold higher compared to GFP mice (Figure 1D). Notably, GFP-Rab5Q79L expression correlated with behavioral performance, with mice expressing higher GFP-Rab5Q79L protein performing worse in the hanging wire assay than mice with lower expression (Figure S2). Rab5Q79L induces endosomal enlargement and abnormal lysosome morphology, recapitulating endolysosomal dysfunction

We next analyzed the endolysosomal system in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice. Considering that Rab5 overactivation leads to enlarged endosomes in vitro,22 we measured endosome size in the cortex of GFP-Rab5Q79L mice. We observed a significant 10-fold increase in endosome size in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice compared to endosome size in GFP control mice (Figures 2A and 2B). Although Rab5 is an early endosome marker, constitutively active Rab5Q79L has been implicated in premature endosomal maturation to lysosomes.23 Therefore, we examined whether GFP-Rab5Q79L mice exhibit altered lysosome morphology and function. We labeled the cortex of GFP-Rab5Q79L and GFP control mice with an antibody recognizing the lysosomal luminal protease, Cathepsin D (CatD), observing abnormal CatD-positive puncta in approximately 30% of total cells (Figures 2C and 2D). Double labeling for CatD and GFP revealed that approximately 60% of GFP-Rab5Q79L-expressing cells exhibited abnormal CatD-positive puncta (Figures 2E and 2F). To further explore this phenomenon, we used electron microscopy to visualize Rab5Q79L-induced endolysosomal morphology. We observed enlarged early endosomes and endolysosomes, suggestive of severe endolysosomal disruption, in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice but not in GFP control mice (Figure 2G). GFP-Rab5Q79L mice exhibit proteostasis defects

The endolysosomal system is responsible for the transport, recycling, and degradation of proteins within a cell, the improper function of which can result in protein mislocalization, aggregation, and cytotoxicity, disrupting cellular proteostasis.29 Accordingly, we evaluated whether the endolysosomal dysfunction observed in Rab5Q79L mice contributes to aberrant proteostasis in vivo. We first assessed whether ubiquitin aggregates, an indicator of defective protein degradation, are present in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice. In comparison to GFP controls, we observed a significant increase in ubiquitin in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice (Figures 3A and 3B). While almost no ubiquitin-positive aggregates were detected in GFP controls, approximately 20% of cells in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice exhibited ubiquitin puncta. Double labeling for ubiquitin and GFP revealed that approximately 85% of GFP-Rab5Q79L-expressing cells showed ubiquitin-positive puncta (Figures 3C and 3D). Of note, cells with ubiquitin aggregates exhibited significant endosome enlargement, suggesting disruption of both protein degradation and endosomal function (Figure 3C). Given the essential role of autophagy in modulating proteostasis,30 we additionally measured p62 aggregates in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice, observing a striking increase in these aggregates in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice compared to GFP control mice, with approximately 45% of cells exhibiting p62 aggregates (Figures 3E and 3F). Double labeling for p62 and GFP revealed that approximately 95% of GFP-Rab5Q79L-expressing cells exhibited p62 aggregates (Figures 3G and 3H). Notably, some of the p62 aggregates in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice were localized within enlarged endosomes (Figure 3G), supporting a synergistic dysfunction in both endosome morphology and proteostasis. Rab5Q79L induces neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and TDP-43 proteinopathy in vivo

Given the presence of endolysosomal dysfunction and proteostasis defects in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice, we examined whether these mice developed neurodegeneration and gliosis. GFP-Rab5Q79L mice exhibited a reduction in brain weight, suggesting significant neurodegeneration as early as 3 months (Figure S3), and this was corroborated by a significant decrease in NeuN staining and cortical thinning (Figures 4A and 4B). Also observed in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice were increases in glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, Figures 4C and 4D) and Iba1 immunoreactivity, indicative of gliosis (Figures 4E and 4F). We previously observed that Rab5Q79L induced TDP-43 mislocalization and aggregation in vitro.6 Therefore, we evaluated whether GFP-Rab5Q79L mice similarly exhibited TDP-43 pathology. We used immunohistochemistry to measure pTDP-43 in both the cortex and the thalamus; pTDP-43 pathology was observed in both brain regions in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice but not in GFP control mice (Figure 5A). We further quantified these data, scoring the severity of pTDP-43 pathology on a scale of 0–4 (score 0: none, 1: sparse, 2: mild, 3: moderate, 4: severe), based on previous methods.31 We noted that the pTDP-43 pathology in our GFP-Rab5Q79L mice scored 1, indicating sparse pTDP-43 pathological burden (Figure 5B). Moreover, co-labeling of pTDP-43 and GFP revealed that cytoplasmic pTDP-43 aggregates were present exclusively in GFP-Rab5Q79L-positive cells (Figure 5C), confirming that the expression of Rab5Q79L induces pTDP-43 pathology in vivo. To investigate whether these mice exhibit TDP-43 loss of function, we first assessed TDP-43 subcellular localization using immunohistochemistry with the anti-total TDP-43 antibody. We observed no clear mislocalization of TDP-43 in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice compared to GFP controls (Figure 5D). Moreover, we directly measured TDP-43 loss of function by examining the inclusion of cryptic exons in TDP-43 target transcripts. We conducted qPCR to assess the levels of cryptic exon inclusion of TDP-43 RNA targets Sortilin1 or Ap3b232,33; however, we did not observe any significant changes in the splicing of these two targets (Figure 5E). To assess whether the observed endosomal defects are specific to TDP-43 pathology, we investigated tau pathology (pTau) in these mice, another major pathological hallmark of FTD.34 However, we did not observe any phosphorylated tau (AT8) in the brain of GFP-Rab5Q79L mice (Figures S4A and S4B). Rab5Q79L induces nuclear envelope and nuclear pore complex morphological alterations

Given previous findings of nuclear envelope and nuclear pore defects in TDP-43 proteinopathies such as FTD-ALS,35,36 we examined nuclear envelope integrity and nuclear pore abnormalities using immunofluorescence staining for Lamin B1 and nuclear pore complex (NPC), respectively. In GFP-Rab5Q79L mice, we observed impaired nuclear envelope integrity, as evidenced by nuclear lamina invaginations (Figures 6A and 6B). Moreover, using an NPC antibody that detects multiple nucleoporins, we observed that NPCs were irregularly distributed around the nuclear envelope in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice (Figures 6C and 6D), further confirming nuclear envelope dysfunction and nuclear pore defects. Discussion

Previous studies have examined the impact of the Rab5Q79L mutation on endolysosomal dysfunction in vitro,6,23 but no Rab5Q79L in vivo model has been generated to accurately assess neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration upon endolysosomal dysfunction. Studies conducted by the Nixon group demonstrated that overexpressing wild-type Rab5 recapitulated prodromal and degenerative features of AD in aged mice, including mild endosomal dysfunction as determined by approximately 2-fold increases in endosome size.16 Strikingly, our GFP-Rab5Q79L mice exhibited more robust endolysosomal defects, which lead to severe neurodegeneration, behavioral deficits, and TDP-43 pathology at an early developmental stage with a lower expression level (approximately 2-fold increase in Rab5 compared to approximately 2.5-fold in this previous model16).

Other groups have also enlisted Rab5 to modulate endolysosomal function and to recapitulate disease pathology. The Zerial group generated Rab5 knockout mice to study endosomal dysfunction, observing loss of endosomes and lysosomes in addition to disrupted protein processing, although the downstream consequences were not fully explored.17 Most other endolysosomal mouse models focus on downstream lysosomal dysfunction but lack defects in upstream endocytosis, trafficking, and sorting.37,38,39,40 These lysosomal dysfunction models are largely knockout models of lysosomal genes including the lysosome-associated membrane protein 2 (LAMP2) and ceroid lipofuscinosis neuronal 7 (CLN7),39,40 although knockin models41 share similar phenotypes including defects in autophagy and neurodegeneration.39,40,41 As for our GFP-Rab5Q79L mouse model, it exhibited endolysosomal dysfunction with robust neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and motor decline. With endolysosomal dysfunction being an early sign of disease,15,16 our model accurately recapitulates these early phenotypes, rendering it ideal for the discovery and testing of early interventions and possibly the identification of diagnostic markers. Strikingly, our novel model of endolysosomal dysfunction also manifested pTDP-43 cytoplasmic inclusions, as observed in TDP-43 proteinopathies such as FTD-ALS and AD. Combined, these robust characteristics create a powerful model to study endolysosomal phenotypes and downstream consequences in pTDP-43 pathology.

TDP-43 pathology is a hallmark feature of FTD-ALS, but the molecular underpinnings resulting in TDP-43 mislocalization and aggregation are poorly understood.6 Overexpression of TDP-43 has been shown to hinder endocytosis and to exacerbate motor deficits in a Drosophila model of ALS,42 whereas upregulating either endocytosis or autophagy reduces TDP-43 aggregates and ameliorates ALS pathology in a dose-dependent manner.42,43 Consequently, the bidirectional repercussions of TDP-43 pathology and endolysosomal dysfunction require further analyses. Our prior work explored this relationship and demonstrated that Rab5Q79L induces TDP-43 pathology in vitro.6 Similarly to our cellular model, in the present study, we observed pTDP-43 pathology in the cortex and thalamus of GFP-Rab5Q79L mice but not GFP control mice. Moreover, we did not observe any pTau in these mice, indicating that our GFP-Rab5Q79L model specifically induces pTDP-43, but not pTau, at the examined time point. Together, these findings support that endolysosomal dysfunction induces pTDP-43 pathology in an in vivo GFP-Rab5Q79L model.

In addition to facilitating the trafficking of disease-causing proteins such as TDP-43, endosomal proteins are required for nuclear envelope structure and integrity.44 Fusion and trafficking of endosomes is modulated by endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-III proteins including CHMP2B.45 CHMP2B dysregulation contributes to elevated ubiquitin and p62 labeling, coupled with enlarged endosomes in neurodegenerative diseases such as FTD.46,47 ESCRT-III proteins are involved in maintaining nuclear envelope integrity,44 with neurodegenerative diseases such as FTD-ALS and AD exhibiting defective nuclear envelope morphology, disrupting nuclear-cytoplasmic transport of proteins, and contributing to cellular stress and dysfunction.48,49 To assess whether our GFP-Rab5Q79L mice disrupted nuclear envelope integrity, we visualized the nuclear pore structure and morphology of cells in the GFP-Rab5Q79L mouse brain. We observed changes in morphology of the nuclear envelope in addition to the NPCs in our GFP-Rab5Q79L mouse model. The precise nuclear pore proteins and/or nuclear envelope components specifically impacted by constitutively active Rab5Q79L are yet to be elucidated. Moreover, whether nuclear pore defects are up- or downstream of neurodegeneration and pTDP-43 pathology remains poorly understood. Extensive studies in cellular and murine models support a role for TDP-43 pathology in disrupting nuclear pore homeostasis,36 while additional studies observe defects in the nuclear pore drive TDP-43 mislocalization and dysfunction.50 This chicken-or-egg phenomenon is yet to be fully elucidated, and therefore we cannot state whether the NPC defects we observe in our model precede or follow our observed neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory phenotypes. However, our results support the notion that endolysosomal dysfunction leads to aberrant proteostasis and pTDP-43 pathology, which are either cause or consequence of nuclear envelope integrity abnormalities that disrupt the transport of TDP-43 and other cargo proteins, thereby exacerbating disease severity.

Limitations of the study

Along with the knowledge gained from our findings earlier, limitations with the present study must be recognized. We used P0 injections to generate our GFP and GFP-Rab5Q79L mice, which leads to transgene expression during development, raising a concern that developmental defects may contribute to phenotypes observed in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice. Future investigations using tail vein or stereotaxic injections in adult mice will rule out the involvement of developmental defects. Moreover, we focused our studies on the brain, rather than the spinal cord, because AAV9 intracerebroventricular injection primarily targets neurons in the mouse brain, with minimal expression in the spinal cord.6,25,26,27,28 Future investigation is needed to ascertain the expression of Rab5Q79L and its associated pathologies in the spinal cord to determine its potential involvement in the behavioral deficits we observed in our GFP-Rab5Q79L mice. Furthermore, while pTDP-43 cytoplasmic inclusions were detected in these mice using immunohistochemical staining, this pathology is sparse. Consistent with this finding, we did not observe the accumulation of pTDP-43 using biochemical analysis, obvious TDP-43 mislocalization, or TDP-43 loss of function. Given that these observations were made at 3 months of age, further investigation in aged mice would be needed to determine whether aged GFP-Rab5Q79L mice develop more robust TDP-43 pathology and TDP-43 loss of function. Lastly, while tau pathology was not detected in our 3-month-old GFP-Rab5Q79L mice, we cannot rule out the possibility that tau pathology may be observed at elevated levels in aged mice, as previously observed in aged transgenic mice expressing wild-type Rab5.16 Therefore, future investigation should focus on later time points to more thoroughly characterize the presence of the co-pathologies including tau and TDP-43 in GFP-Rab5Q79L mice.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal studies

All procedures involving mice were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for Care and Use of Experimental Animals and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol number A00005547-20-R23). Mice were maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle in standard housing at Mayo Clinic Jacksonville with ad libitum access to standard mouse chow and water. All mice were of the same genetic background (C57BL/6J; The Jackson Lab) and were assigned to experimental groups based on the AAV received (AAV9-GFP or AAV9-GFP-Rab5Q79L). All mice underwent the same procedures in the same order and when possible, individual litters were randomly divided among experimental groups to prevent any litter-specific confounding effects. No further randomization was undertaken. Roughly equal numbers of males and females were included in each experimental cohort and no differences between sexes were observed, except for expected differences in total body weight. Mice were aged to specific time points as indicated in the text, methods, and figure legends. No animals were excluded from the study.

Method details

GFP-Rab5Q79L construct generation

mCherry-Rab5Q79L was a gift from Sergio Grinstein53 (Addgene). We cloned Rab5Q79L into recombinant AAV9 constructs in-frame with GFP to generate GFP-Rab5Q79L. We inserted GFP-Rab5Q79L and GFP cDNA into the HindIII and XhoI restriction sites of the AAV expression vector pAM/CBA-pl-WPRE-BGH, containing the CMV-enhanced chicken β-actin promoter.

Adeno-associated virus production and neonatal mouse injections

AAV9 was produced as previously described27,54 and purified using standard methods.55 Briefly, AAV9 vectors encoding GFP-Rab5Q79L or GFP were co-transfected with the cis plasmid pCap9 (a gift from Dr. Todd Golde, University of Florida) into HEK 293T/17 cells (ATCC). Cells were harvested 72 hours after transfection, treated with 50 units/ml Benzonase endonuclease (Sigma-Aldrich), and lysed by freeze-thaw. The virus was isolated using a discontinuous iodixanol gradient and the buffer exchanged for phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco) using an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugation filter concentrator (Millipore). The genomic titer of each virus was determined by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using the Quant Studio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), and primers specific to the woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element. Samples were compared against a standard curve of supercoiled plasmid and diluted to their final concentrations in PBS. Intracerebroventricular injections of virus into post-natal day zero (P0) C57BL/6J wild-type pups were performed as previously described.27,54 This injection method primarily targets neurons in the mouse brain, with minimal expression in the spinal cord.6,25,26,27,28 Pups were cryoanesthetized on ice until they exhibited no movement and 2 μL AAV (7.5×1012 genomes/mL) solution was injected into each lateral ventricle using a 32-gauge needle (Hamilton Company) fitted to a 10 μL syringe (Hamilton Company). The needle was inserted at a 30-degree angle from the surface of the head at a point approximately two-fifths the distance between the lambda suture and the eye and held at a depth of approximately 2 mm when injecting. Pups were then allowed to recover on a heating pad before being returned to their home cages.

Mouse behavioral analyses

Wild-type mice expressing either GFP-Rab5Q79L or GFP alone were analyzed for hindlimb clasping every 15 days beginning at 30 days of age. Then, at 3 months of age, all mice underwent the hanging wire test. All mice were acclimated to the testing room for 1-2 hours prior to analysis. All behavioral equipment was cleaned with 30% ethanol prior to use with each animal. All mice were returned to their home cages and home room after each day of testing. Researchers performing behavioral analyses were blinded to the AAV status of the mice.

Clasping test

Hindlimb clasping behavior was scored at 30, 45, and 60 days for each mouse according to a standard rubric. Mice were lifted from their cage by the base of their tails and hindlimb position was observed for 10 seconds. If the hindlimbs were splayed outward, the mouse received a score of 0. If a single hindlimb was retracted toward the abdomen for more than 50% of the time suspended, the mouse was assigned a score of 1. If the hindlimbs were partially retracted for more than 50% of that time, it was assigned a score of 2, and if the hindlimbs were completely retracted for more than 50% of the time suspended, the mouse received a score of 3. If the hindlimbs were completely retracted for more than 90% of the time and the torso was twisted, the mouse received a score of 4. This test was repeated 3 times, and each mouse was assigned an average score.

Hanging wire test

A 55 cm long, 2 mm thick plastic-coated wire was secured to 2 vertical stands at a height of 35 cm. A layer of soft pads was placed beneath to prevent injury to the animals. Mice were weighed at three months-of-age prior to beginning the assay to account for differences in body weight. Each mouse was suspended from the tail and lowered towards the wire, allowing the animal to grasp the wire with its forefeet. The tail was then gently released, a timer was started, and the mouse was allowed to hang from the wire until it fell onto the soft padding below. The mouse was then returned to the wire and the total number of falls over a 2-minute testing period was recorded. This test was repeated 3 times per mouse, with rest periods in between, and the scores were averaged.

Tissue processing and generation of lysates

After all behavioral analyses were completed at three months-of-age, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation and the brain was harvested. Each brain was weighed and bisected at the sagittal midline. One hemibrain was immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 hours before being rinsed with PBS and embedded in paraffin. Fixed hemibrains were sectioned into 5 μm midsagittal slices, mounted on positively charged glass slides, and dried overnight. These paraformaldehyde-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) brain sections were used in subsequent immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses. The other hemibrain was regionally dissected and frozen on dry ice. To prepare brain homogenate, the entire hemi-cortex was homogenized in cold TE buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM PMSF, 1x protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails) at a ratio of 5 μL of buffer per milligram of tissue. To prepare protein lysates, an aliquot of brain homogenate was diluted with 2x co-immunoprecipitation buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 300 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 5 mM EDTA) and 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Samples were briefly sonicated then cleared by centrifugation at 16,000 x g for 20 minutes. A bicinchoninic acid assay was performed on the supernatant to determine protein concentration (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR

To extract total RNA, mouse brain homogenates were mixed with Trizol LS (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research). cDNA was synthesized from 250 ng of RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA transcription kit (Applied Biosystems). Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR GreenER qPCR SuperMix (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each sample was run in triplicate and processed on a Prism 7900HT Fast Real-Time system (Applied Biosystems). Samples were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to the endogenous controls (Rplp0 and Gapdh). The following primers and their sequences were used: ap3b2 forward: 5′- AGCCAGAATATGGCCACGAC -3’. ap3b2 reverse: 5′- CACTATGATGGGCACACGGA -3’. sortilin forward: 5′- CAAATGCCAAGGTGGGATGAA -3′, sortilin reverse: 5′- TTGAATCCAAAGCCTCTACGCC -3′. Gapdh forward: 5′-CATGGCCTTCCGTGTTCCTA-3′, Gapdh reverse: 5′-CCTGCTTCACCACCTTCTTGAT-3′. Rplp0 forward: 5′-ACTGGTCTAGGACCCGAGAAG-3′, Rplp0 reverse: 5′-CTCCCACCTTGTCTCCAGTC-3′.

Western blot analysis and quantification

Equal amounts of protein were used in each assay. Tissue lysates were diluted with 2x SDS-loading buffer at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio and heated at 95°C for 5 minutes. Samples were run on 4-20% Tris-glycine gels (Novex) and proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Non-specific sites were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBS plus 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 hour, then incubated with primary antibodies, GFP (1:2000, Invitrogen), Rab5 (1:1000, cell signaling), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:5000, Meridian Life Science) overnight at 4°C. Membranes were washed in TBST and incubated with donkey anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (1:5000; Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 hour. Membranes were washed again, and protein expression was visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence treatment and exposure to film. Bands were quantified using Scion Image software by analyzing pixel density, and protein levels were normalized to GAPDH as the protein loading control. Experiments were repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility and associated N numbers are listed in the figure legends.

Immunohistochemistry in mouse brain

FFPE tissue sections from mice were deparaffinized in xylenes and rehydrated through a series of ethanol solutions. To detect early endosome antigen 1 (EEA1) (1:400, Cell Signaling), cathepsin D (CatD) (1:500, ProteinTech), ubiquitin (1:10000, Invitrogen), p62 (1:500, cell signaling), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (1:5000, Biogenex), ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1) (1:500, Wako Chemicals), or neuronal nuclei (NeuN) (1:4000; Chemicon International), slides were steamed in distilled water or citrate buffer pH 6.0 for 30 minutes, then incubated in Dako Peroxidase Block (DAKO, S2001). Sections were immunostained with the primary antibody using the DAKO Autostainer (Universal Staining System) and the DAKO EnVision + HRP system. Following labeling, all sections were stained with hematoxylin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), dehydrated through ethanol and xylene washes, and coverslipped with Cytoseal mounting media (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To detect pTDP-43, antigen retrieval was performed by steaming in 1 mM sodium citrate pH 6.0, 0.05% Tween 20 buffer for 30 minutes. Tissues were incubated with Dako Peroxidase Block (DAKO, S2001) and blocked in 2% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. Slides were then incubated with rabbit anti-TDP-43 pS409/410 (1:500, rb3655, developed in-house25) overnight at 4°C. After washing in PBS, sections were incubated with an anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200, Jackson ImmunoResearch) at room temperature for 2 hours. Peroxidase labeling was visualized with the chromogen solution 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB-Plus, DAKO) and the VECTASTAIN Elite-ABC kit (Vector Laboratories). Slides were then stained with Lerner 1 hematoxylin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and coverslipped with Cytoseal mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). IHC-stained slides were scanned with a ScanScope® AT2 (Leica Biosystems) at either 20x or 40x magnification, and representative images were taken and analyzed with ImageScope® 7 and Aperio ePathology software (v12.1; Leica Biosystems). GFAP and Iba1 were quantified by dividing GFAP- or Iba1-positive area by the total area of the cortex. CatD, ubiquitin, and p62 were manually scored by quantifying puncta and/or accumulations in the cortex of GFP-Rab5Q79L mice in comparison to GFP controls using ImageScope.

Immunofluorescence staining in tissues

FFPE sections of mouse brain tissues were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a series of ethanol solutions, and steamed in Dako antigen retrieval solution for 30 minutes. Slides were blocked in Dako All Purpose Blocker for 1 hour and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, including: rabbit anti-EEA1 (Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-GFP (Life Technologies), rabbit anti-TDP-43 pS409/410 (1:500, rb3655, developed in-house25), phospho-tau (1:500, Ser202, Thr205 (AT8), Invitrogen), MAP2 (1:4000, Thermo Fisher Scientific), CatD (1:500, ProteinTech), ubiquitin (1:10000, Invitrogen), Lamin B1 (1:500, Abcam), and nuclear pore complex (NPC) (1:500, Abcam). Slides were then washed and incubated with corresponding Alexa Fluor 488-, 568- or 647-conjugated donkey anti-species (1:500, Molecular Probes) for 2 hours. DAPI (Vectashield) was used to stain cellular nuclei. Images were obtained on a Zeiss LSM 980 laser scanning confocal microscope. For the analysis of immunofluorescence images, cells were visually scored as positive or negative for EEA1, CatD, ubiquitin, p62, or pTDP-43. To quantify CatD, ubiquitin, or p62 co-labeling with GFP or GFP-Rab5Q79L, 100 total cells from 4 to 5 images were quantified for each mouse. The percentage of positive cells is shown. For all analyses, researchers were blinded to injected viruses while performing the quantification.

Electron microscopy

To examine the morphology of endosomes and endolysosomes in the mouse brain, electron microscopy (EM) was performed as previously described.56 Mice used for EM studies were transcardially perfused with EM fixation buffer containing 2% glutaraldehyde and 2% PFA in 0.M phosphate buffer (PB). The cortex was dissected from the fixed mouse brain and placed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4 overnight. After PBS washing, tissue was post-fixed in 1% OsO4, en bloc, washed three times in pure water, stained with 1% uranyl acetate and 50% ethanol, dehydrated in 70, 80, 95, and 100% ethanol and propylene oxide, and infiltrated and embedded in Epon 812 (Polysciences). Ultrathin sections were cut from the Epon 812-embedded samples by Leica Ultramicrotome, counterstained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged with a Philips 208S electron microscope at 1,000x magnification.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification methods are described above. No animals or individual data points were excluded from the reported analyses. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism. The statistical test used and the definition of statistical significance in each case is included in the figure legends. Unless otherwise noted, graphs comparing multiple cohorts or datasets were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s post-hoc multiple comparisons test. Comparisons between two datasets were carried out using an unpaired two-tailed t-test. Data are presented as the mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise noted. Whenever possible, investigators were blinded to the genotype of each mouse.
