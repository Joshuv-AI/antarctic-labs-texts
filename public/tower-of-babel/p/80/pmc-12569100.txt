Therapeutic comparison of MitoTEMPO and rosmarinic acid as mitochondria targeted and non targeted antioxidants evaluated in rat sciatic nerve crush injury model
Authors: Ahmet Akkoca, Burcu Gültekin, Aysu Şen, Selim Kutlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria are key organelles involved in various pathophysiological processes. As the primary site of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, they represent a major target for many therapeutic agents. In this study, the therapeutic effects of a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant (MitoTEMPO, MT) and a conventional yet potent antioxidant compound (rosmarinic acid, RA) were compared in a rat model of sciatic nerve crush injury, focusing on nerve conduction parameters and histological structures. A total of 28 adult Wistar Albino rats were used in the study, with seven animals assigned to each group: SHAM, crush injury (CI), RA, and MT. The CI model was induced by applying a mosquito-type clamp to the sciatic nerve for 40 s. Nerve conduction parameters were evaluated using compound action potentials (CAPs) analyzed through comprehensive mathematical methods. In addition, structural alterations were assessed via histological staining and scoring. Both RA and MT treatments exerted therapeutic effects on the CAP parameters, which were progressively suppressed by CI. However, the efficacy of MT was more pronounced-particularly in nerve fibers with conduction velocities between 65 and 69 m/s, where significant improvements were observed. Histological evaluation of CI group preparations revealed neuronal loss between nerve fascicles, axonal degeneration, increased collagen deposition, cytoplasmic vacuolization, and myelin sheath degeneration. MT treatment was more effective than RA in alleviating these pathological alterations. In conclusion, this study underscores the importance of mitochondria-targeted antioxidant strategies in reversing electrophysiological suppression and structural damage following peripheral nerve injury.Keywords: Antioxidants, Crush injury, Mitochondria, Nerve conduction velocity, Sciatic nerveSubject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNI) can occur due to causes such as trauma or iatrogenic interventions, leading to partial or complete loss of motor and sensory functions, physical disability, and neuropathic pain1. Every year, more than a million patients worldwide are diagnosed with PNI2. Although there is a wealth of information about regeneration mechanisms following PNI, there are limitations regarding reliable treatment methods that guarantee full recovery in all patients3. Deciding on the surgical treatment of PNI involves complex processes, such as determining the timing of the surgery. Performing the surgery too early may result in the complete loss of the potential for spontaneous recovery1, It has been noted that PNI treatment through electrical stimulation alleviates axonal regeneration and neuropathic pain; however, the specific mechanisms underlying successful PNI treatment through electrical stimulation remain unclear4. For these reasons, the contribution of pharmacological treatment methods to PNI therapy, through their ability to enhance myelination and provide neuroprotective effects, continues to be a focal point of interest for researchers5.

Compound action potential (CAP) recordings of the sciatic nerve provide information about nerve conduction velocity and nerve viability following the applied procedures6. Thanks to numerous studies conducted to date, advanced mathematical models have been developed, making it possible for CAP recordings to provide more detailed information about conduction parameters7.

The sciatic nerve crush injury (CI) model is widely used experimentally to explore the mechanisms of traumatic damage in the peripheral nervous system and to investigate alternative treatment methods8. Studies show that the amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS) increases following sciatic nerve CI, and this increase negatively impacts the recovery processes and electrical activities of the sciatic nerve9. Considering that the mitochondria are the major site of ROS production in biological systems and that mitochondria are an important target for ROS, mitochondria-targeted therapeutic strategies are of significant importance. Because mitochondrial damage caused by increased ROS triggers the production of even more ROS, leading to a vicious cycle10. The researchers have clearly reported the contributions of various antioxidants to the recovery process after PNI11,12. For example, in a study conducted by Kocaoğlu et al. (2017) using the PNI model, the investigators examined the effects of the antioxidant α-lipoic acid on sciatic nerve parameters. They reported that α-lipoic acid improved the Sciatic Functional Index (SFI) and increased the levels of antioxidant enzymes in the same group13.

In our sciatic nerve CI model, we aimed to compare the therapeutic effects of MitoTEMPO, a mitochondria-specific antioxidant that we have previously used in different studies and observed its positive effects in various pathophysiological conditions14–17, with rosmarinic acid (RA), a powerful molecule known for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antibacterial, antitumor, and antiangiogenic effects18. Thus, it was targeted to reach a conclusion on the effectiveness of mitochondria-targeted treatment methods in PNI.

Materials and methods

Animals, experimental groups, surgical procedures and drug applications

In the experiments, 28 adult (14-week-old) female Wistar Albino rats with body weights between 195 and 246 g were used. Rats were housed at constant ambient temperature and humidity on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with no restriction on access to feed and water, and 5 rats per cage. All procedures were approved by Necmettin Erbakan University, Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (July 11, 2024, Approval no: 2024-054). Every step of the experimental process carried out during the entire course of the study was supervised by the authorities of the Experimental Medicine Application and Research Center of Necmettin Erbakan University to ensure compliance with animal welfare standards.

Rats were randomly divided into 4 groups. In group 1 (SHAM, n = 7)), the right sciatic nerve of the rats was accessed through a 1 cm skin incision approximately 1,5 cm above the knee joint and then the opened tissues were closed and the SHAM operation was completed. After operation, this group received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of %0.9 saline (195/81, Osel A.Ş., Türkiye) in a volume of 0.1 ml per day for 14 days. Rats in group 2 (CI, n = 7)) were subjected to crush injury (CI) model according to Angelis’ description19. Briefly, the sciatic nerves were crushed with a mosquito-type clamp for 40 s through the incision and then the tissues were closed. After surgical procedures, this group also received 0.1 mL i.p. saline injection daily for 14 days. Rats in group 3 (RA, n = 7) were injected i.p. with RA (C18H16O8, R4033, Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals, USA) dissolved in saline at 40 mg/kg daily after CI surgery20. Rats in group 4 (MT, n = 7)) were injected i.p. with MitoTEMPO (C29H35N2O2P·Cl, SML0737, Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals, USA) dissolved in saline at 0.7 mg/kg daily after CI surgery16. All surgical procedures were performed under anesthesia with i.p. injection of a combination of 80 mg/kg ketamine + 10 mg/kg xylazine. The sutured tissues were dressed daily with antiseptic solution.

Compound action potential recording and analysis

The right sciatic nerves of the rats sacrificed by cervical dislocation were quickly isolated to a length of approximately 5 cm and placed in modified Krebs solution (composed of mM: 119 NaCl, 4.8 KCl, 1.8 CaCl2, 1.2 MgSO4, 1.2 KH2PO4, 20 NaHCO3 and 10 glucose, pH 7.4, and gassed with a mixture of 95% O2 and 5% CO2). It was transferred to a nerve chamber (Agent Well, Biopac Systems, Inc., USA) with steel pins spaced 5 mm apart after the unwanted tissues around the sciatic nerve were removed. The stimulus that would generate the compound action potential (CAP) was delivered as a 1 ms supramaximal square stimulus using a stimulus isolation unit (MAY ISO150-A, Commat Ltd. Şti., Türkiye) from the distal region of the sciatic nerve15. CAPs were transferred electronically via Biopac Student Lab 4.1.7 software using SS2L Lead Set on BIOPAC MP36 system (Biopac Systems, Inc., USA).

Maximum depolarization (mV) indicating the peak value of CAP, conduction velocity (m/s), maximum depolarization velocity (m/s), maximum and minimum time derivatives corresponding to the velocity of the rising and falling phases (+ dV/dt and − dV/dt; V sec) and CAP integral (mV ms) were measured from the CAP traces. The conduction velocity was calculated by dividing the distance between the stimulation and recording electrodes [35 mm) by the delay time between the stimulation and the first depolarization point. Similarly, the maximum depolarization velocity was calculated by dividing the distance by the time elapsed until the point of maximum depolarization.

The relative contributions of nerve fiber groups with different conduction velocities to the CAP were calculated using a modified model by Tuncer et al.21, based on the advanced mathematical modeling of Cummins et al.22,23. To enhance the visual impact and clarity of the estimated conduction velocity distribution (CVD) results, velocity groups were defined (slow: <40 m/s, medium: 40–60 m/s, and fast: >60 m/s), and the relative contributions of these velocity groups to the CAP were visualized separately (Fig. 3B). The relative contributions of nerve fiber groups were calculated using MATLAB software (R2025a, MathWorks, USA).

Conduction velocity distribution (CVD) data. (a) Distribution representing the percentage of fiber count relative to each conduction speed for all experimental groups. (b) The distribution representing the percentage of fiber speed groups (slow: <40 m/s, medium: 40–60 m/s, and fast: > 60 m/s) contributing to the compound action potentials recorded from all experimental groups. Values are given as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Histological experiments

After the sciatic nerve tissues were fixed with buffered neutral formalin, the tissue processing was carried out using a tissue processor (Leica TP1020, Leica Biosystems, USA). The processed tissues were then embedded in paraffin using tissue embedding equipment (Leica HistoCore Arcadia H, Leice Biosystems, USA). Sections with a thickness of 5 micrometers were taken from the sciatic nerve blocks of all groups using a microtome and were placed on slides after being flattened in a water bath.

The sections were stained with toluidine blue (T3260, Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals, USA) and Masson’s trichrome (CB6095-0100, ChemBio Laboratry Research, Türkiye) stains. The stained nerve sections were qualitatively analyzed under a light microscope (Axiocam Erc 5s, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). These histological changes were graded as degree 0 (absent), degree 1 (mild), degree 2 (moderate), and degree 3 (severe)24.

Statistical analysis

All data in the manuscript were given as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). The conformity of the data to normal distribution was analyzed by Shapiro-Wilk test. One Way ANOVA and post-hoc test Tukey was used to compare the significance of the data between groups. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and Microsoft 365 software.

Results

Our findings showed that the CI model suppressed the CAPs of isolated rat sciatic nerves, and that RA and MitoTEMPO had therapeutic effects on the suppressed CAP parameters. When all our data are evaluated as a whole, it is concluded that the therapeutic effect of MitoTEMPO on the injured sciatic nerve is more effective compared to RA. Sample CAP traces for each group are shown in Fig. 1.

Representative compound action potential (CAP) recordings from all experimental groups. The curves were plotted so that the stimulation points align. The distance between the recording and stimulating electrodes was 35 mm.

When the data obtained from the CAP curves were analyzed, significant suppression was observed in all of these data with CI compared to the SHAM group. It is noteworthy that in the RA and MT treatment groups, MitoTEMPO produced statistically more significant therapeutic effects compared to RA in terms of maximum depolarization, maximum depolarization velocity, +dV/dt, −dV/dt, and CAP integral values (Fig. 2).

Data obtained from compound action potential recordings. (a) maximum depolarization (mV), (b): conduction velocity (m/s), (c): maximum depolarization velocity, (d) +dV/dt (V.sec), (e) −dV/dt (V.sec), and (f): compound action potential (CAP) integral (mV.ms). Indicator ‘s’ represents a comparison to the SHAM group, ‘c’ to the CI group, and ‘r’ to the RA group (p < 0.05). Values are given as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Our estimated CVD data for all experimental groups showed that in the CI group, nerve fibers with conduction velocities faster than 60 m/s did not contribute to the CAP recordings. The speed groups that contributed the most to the CAP data for the SHAM, CI, RA, and MT groups were 37.5 m/s, 31 m/s, 37.5 m/s, and 43.5 m/s, respectively. Notably, MitoTEMPO treatment provided significant recovery in nerve fibers with conduction velocities of 65–69 m/s. Although the therapeutic effect of RA was observed at the same speeds, it was limited (Fig. 3A). In Fig. 3B, which further clarifies the relative percentage contributions of the velocity groups classified as slow, medium, and fast to the CAP curves, shows that RA exerts its therapeutic effect primarily through slow-conducting nerve fibers, whereas MitoTEMPO acts through medium- and fast-conducting nerve fibers (Fig. 3B).

In our histological experiments, Toluidine Blue staining revealed that the nerve fibers in the SHAM group had an orderly structure, with clearly visible axonal structures and surrounding myelin sheaths in their natural form. In the CI group, neuronal cell loss was observed between the nerve fascicles, resulting in gaps between the nerve fibers. Additionally, axonal degeneration, intracytoplasmic vacuolization, and myelin sheath degeneration were prominent in the CI group. Although RA exhibited some therapeutic effects against these damages, this effect was more pronounced in the MT group (Fig. 4).

The images and histopathological scores of sciatic nerve sections stained with Toluidine Blue and Masson’s Trichrome. Indicator ‘s’ represents a comparison to the SHAM group, and ‘c’ to the CI group, (p < 0.05). Values are given as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

As a result of Masson’s Trichrome staining, while the connective tissue layers of the nerve (epineurium, perineurium, endoneurium) appeared normal in the SHAM group, an increase in collagen content was observed in the endoneurium and perineurium of the CI group. In the RA and MT groups, this increase was found to be reduced (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Oxygen is essential for mitochondrial function and energy supply. The distinction between physiological and pathological nerve regeneration depends on a delicate balance between the production of ROS and antioxidant activity25. Oxidative stress is among the primary causes of neuronal damage following injury26. The main source of free radicals responsible for oxidative stress is the electron transport chain located in the inner mitochondrial membrane27. In this study, the effectiveness of the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MitoTEMPO and RA, a non-mitochondria-targeted but potent antioxidant, on regeneration processes following PNI was compared using electrophysiological and histological methods.

Given that a substantial portion of ROS production occurs within mitochondria, a widely accepted strategy involves neutralizing these free radicals at their source—within the mitochondria—without disrupting mitochondrial integrity or allowing their diffusion into the bloodstream, where they could damage other cells and tissues. In our previous study using an abdominal ischemia-reperfusion model, we observed that the total oxidant levels in the MitoTEMPO-treated group were even lower than those measured in the control group14. However, in another study employing an ischemic stroke model, the oxidative stress index calculated for the MitoTEMPO group was comparable to that of the control group16. RA, which we compared against MitoTEMPO in this study, has been featured in numerous studies due to its strong antioxidant properties. Zych et al. (2019) demonstrated that RA is effective against oxidative damage parameters caused by type 2 diabetes28. Areti et al. (2018) suggested that RA could be used as a therapeutic agent in oxaliplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy due to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties29.

Electrophysiological and structural changes in the sciatic nerve have been reported in numerous models of sciatic nerve crush injury2,30,31. Although the findings of these studies are consistent with those of the present study, our use of isolated sciatic nerve provided the advantage of attributing the results specifically to the sciatic nerve rather than to the activity of the entire motor unit. Moreover, our estimated CVD results, calculated from the recorded compound action potentials (CAPs), represent a well-established method that offers interpretable information regarding nerve conduction. This approach, originally discovered by Cummins et al. (1979a, b) and later refined by Tuncer et al. 21, enables detailed understanding and interpretation of how fibers with different conduction velocities are affected by a specific event21–23. In summary, this study provides highly original findings in comparison to many existing studies on the sciatic nerve, particularly regarding changes in nerve conduction velocity and the accompanying structural alterations, as well as the implications of these parameters for mitochondria-targeted oxidative stress treatment strategies.

In the study by Salehi et al. 32 using the chronic ischemia (CI) model, the decrease in the sciatic functional index following injury is consistent with our findings32. The progressive suppression observed in maximum depolarization in our data parallels the findings of Raducan et al. (2009), who conducted a study using a model similar to ours33. Suppression of maximum depolarization should be interpreted together with the area under the CAP curve, since in some cases, the maximum depolarization may be small due to the dispersion of conduction velocities among fibers contributing to the CAP. However, if both maximum depolarization and the CAP integral decrease simultaneously, it can be concluded that some nerve fibers are definitively blocked15. In the present study, nerve fiber blockade resulting from CI is clearly demonstrated by decreases in both maximum depolarization and CAP integral values. The simultaneous increase in these parameters observed with MitoTEMPO treatment indicates an increase in the number of functional nerve fibers in the MT group (Fig. 2A, B).

In our sample traces, where the stimulus times were overlaid, there is consistency among our findings of delayed CAP formation (reduced CAP conduction velocity), slower rise to peak and recovery slopes, maximum depolarization rate, +dV/dt, −dV/dt, and conduction velocity as a result of CI. Additionally, our estimated CVD results further support these observations. Specifically, CAPs recorded from the CI group lacked contributions from fast-conducting fibers (> 60 m/s), while slow (< 40 m/s) and intermediate (40–60 m/s) conduction velocity fibers contributed. Previous studies have demonstrated that nerve conduction velocity is determined by fiber diameter, axon diameter, myelin thickness, and internodal length34. Ikeda and Oka (2012) stated that the most reliable recovery index for spontaneous regeneration in peripheral nerves is not the restoration of the myelin sheath layer, as commonly believed, but rather the nerve conduction velocity and the average fiber diameter35. Our histological findings indicated improvements in myelin degeneration in both the RA and MT groups. When considered together, these results suggest that antioxidant treatment contributes to myelin regeneration following PNI. Furthermore, the observed improvements in nerve conduction parameters in the RA and MT groups may be partially attributed to the recovery of the myelin sheath.

The number and morphological structure of mitochondria maintain a delicate balance in terms of function (mitochondrial homeostasis), which plays a crucial role in preserving cellular homeostasis36. In neurodegenerative diseases, the ATP required for neuronal regeneration must be supplied by healthy mitochondrial functions37. Yu et al. 2 reported increased oxidative damage in a sciatic nerve crush injury model2. Damaged mitochondria lead to increased ROS production, and the elevated ROS levels, in turn, drive mitochondrial dysfunction38. In this study, we associate the protective effect of MitoTEMPO against RA in CI with its ability to rescue sciatic nerve cells from the vicious cycle of mitochondrial dysfunction and increased ROS production. This, in turn, enables mitochondria to function properly and synthesize the ATP required for regeneration.

Since this study was conducted only on adult female rats, the generalization of the results with respect to sex- and age-dependent biological differences is limited. Considering that differences in nerve regeneration and antioxidant responses may vary by age and sex, it is necessary to validate our findings in male rats and different age groups as well. Moreover, since our study focused on electrophysiological and histopathological parameters, it lacks a detailed analysis of oxidative markers and biochemical pathways. This constitutes a limitation in clarifying the direct mechanisms of the tested antioxidants on cellular redox balance. Therefore, the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of mitochondria-specific and non-specific molecules in the PNI model can only be discussed indirectly. Nevertheless, our study demonstrated, through electrophysiological and structural parameters, the potential effects of MitoTEMPO—a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant—on nerve regeneration compared with RA, a non-mitochondria-targeted molecule that has shown strong therapeutic effects in the literature. Future studies including biochemical analyses and different age/sex groups will further support our findings and strengthen their translational validity.

In conclusion, this study reported electrophysiological suppression and structural changes in nerve conduction velocity and the activity of nerve fibers contributing to the CAP in a sciatic nerve CI model. Additionally, we highlighted that mitochondria-targeted antioxidant treatment strategies may offer a more effective therapeutic approach based on electrophysiological and histological findings. Ultimately, MitoTEMPO was evaluated as a more effective treatment alternative compared to RA, a well-known potent antioxidant, in improving conduction parameters and structural alterations in the crushed sciatic nerve.

Future directions

When the findings, propositions, and limitations of this manuscript are considered as a whole, the need for further research becomes evident. In particular, it is crucial to delineate the specific signaling pathways involved in the regeneration processes of the injured sciatic nerve and to clarify how mitochondria-targeted antioxidant strategies modulate these mechanisms. A comprehensive understanding of these aspects may pave the way for considering such strategies as promising therapeutic options and informative tools for future clinical applications.
