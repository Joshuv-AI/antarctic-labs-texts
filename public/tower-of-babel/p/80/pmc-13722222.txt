Comparative analysis of adenosine deaminase (ADA) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR/GeneXpert) for diagnosing pleural and peritoneal tuberculosis
Authors: Aparnna Unnikrishnan Nair, Sanjana Laishram, Shoraf Pascal, Muskan Joshi, Gayathri Pramil Menon, Lakshmi Murugesan, Nithila Sivakumar, Lakshiya Ramamoorthy, Ruchi Kothari, Roshan Prasad, A Prashanth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Tuberculosis (Tb) is one the most prevalent diseases, with a huge disease burden in various developing countries, such as India. Given the large burden of TB cases, the proportion of extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) cases has also risen in recent years. The diagnosis of EPTB poses a significant challenge, owing to its atypical characteristics. Adenosine deaminase (ADA) and GeneXpert/PCR (NAAT) are the primary tools for EPTB diagnosis currently in practice. The aim of the study was to contrast and highlight the efficacy of the two tests, investigating the utility of both, alone and in combination.Methods and Material:This study was a cross-sectional comparative study with a total of 77 samples included. Every diagnosis was documented to generate the values based on predictive power, which include sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV).Results:In all the samples, ADA was first compared to GeneXpert in ascitic fluid (sensitivity 75%, specificity 6.9%, PPV 10% and NPV of 66.67%) and in pleural fluid (sensitivity 94.11%, specificity 3.70%, PPV 38.09% and NPV 50%). GeneXpert compared to ADA in ascitic fluid (sensitivity 10%, specificity 66.67%, and PPV 75%, and NPV 6.89%) and in pleural fluid (sensitivity 38.10%, specificity 50%, PPV 94.12% and NPV 3.70%).Conclusions:ADA can be used with relative confidence to test EPTB, due to its high sensitivity as well as reliable negative predictive value, in contrast GeneXpert/PCR is strongly recommended in the detection of pre-existing Tb cases, due to its high specificity. Further studies and investigations are required to help health care providers establish the collective use reducing both morbidity and mortality. This study reinforces the use of ADA in cases of EPTB.

Introduction

Tuberculosis remains one the most frequently encountered diseases, posing a significant public health concern in most developing nations, such as India. Despite recent breakthroughs, the disease continues to affect millions globally, with an estimated 13 million cases and 2.5 million cases reported annually.[1] According to a WHO report, India carries the highest burden of tuberculosis, accounting 2 to 2.5 million cases.[2]

Mycobacterium tuberculosis clinically presents as pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) and extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB). Pulmonary tuberculosis is the most common clinical form, which involves the lungs. On the other hand, extrapulmonary TB can occur anywhere outside the lungs, including lymph nodes, kidneys, abdomen, intestine, skin, genitourinary tract, bone, and serous membranes, such as pleural, pericardial, and meninges, which causes significant disabilities if left undetected. In 2013 alone, nearly 2.1 million cases of tuberculosis were detected in India, of which 16% were new EPTB cases, totalling approximately 336,000 individuals with EPTB.[3] The escalation of HIV infections has led to a concurrent increase in EPTB cases. Immunocompromised individuals, particularly those infected with HIV, are more susceptible to EPTB. EPTB constitutes about 15-20% of all TB cases, with HIV-infected individuals accounting for 50% of EPTB cases.[45]

Tuberculosis pleural effusion stands out as one of the most common types of EPTB, accounting for most cases of pleural effusion occurring in developing countries. Pleural effusion usually presents bilaterally, with a predominance of the right lung due to the impaired lymphatic drainage and higher oxygen tension.[6] Tuberculous peritonitis, which is the inflammation and fluid collection around the peritoneum, is the most common form of abdominal EPTB. The clinical features include fever, abdominal distension, abdominal pain, unintentional weight loss, chronic diarrhoea, or an abdominal mass. It has, however, been detected that about 15%-25% of patients affected with abdominal TB also have pulmonary tuberculosis.[7]

In India, conventional tests for detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) include sputum culture in Lowenstein Jensen (LJ) medium and sputum smear microscopy (also known as AFB stain test or ZN test. Diagnosis of EPTB poses a significant challenge, owing to its ambiguous clinical presentation, challenges in obtaining specimens/samples from internal organs, and the lack of access in resource-limited settings. Hence, ADA testing serves as the cornerstone in the detection of Tb/EPTB., ADA is essential for breaking down adenosine and deoxyadenosine to inosine and deoxyinosine, and its levels peak during tuberculosis infection. Pleural or peritoneal fluid samples are collected via thoracentesis or paracentesis, respectively. An ADA value >40 U/L in pleural fluid or >30 U/L in ascitic fluid is indicative of tuberculosis.[2]

A study of 63 patients showcased that the ROC (receiver operating characteristic curve) for the sensitivity and specificity of ADA in the pleural fluid were 92% and 90%, respectively. The authors found the positive likelihood ratio to be 9.03, the negative likelihood ratio of 0.10, and the odds ratio of 110.08.[8] ADA level in ascitic fluid has been reported to be a sensitive test for tuberculous periotonitis, with a 95% confidence interval.[9] It was noted among children and adults age >50 that ADA may be elevated to proportions that can result in false positive tests.[10] Hence, ADA alone cannot help confirm pleural TB.

Over the last few decades, a rise in molecular techniques has helped in the precise and accurate detection of Tb/EPTB. Traditional methods often fail to diagnose Tb/EPTB due to a low number of pathogens in diagnostic samples. However, the technology incorporating nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) to amplify the amount of Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA in diagnostic samples has emerged. The WHO has endorsed the Xpert MTB, which uses PCR to amplify MTb DNA, where the diagnostic sample may be too little for diagnosis using the traditional method. It provides more accurate, faster results within 2 hours and is more sensitive than AFB smear microscopy. It gives very few chances of cross-contamination and is safe.[11] All these advantages make it a novel and unique tool for diagnosing EPTB. The diagnostic tools GeneXpert/PCR and ADA are the most commonly employed and productive techniques for a conclusive diagnosis of EPTB. However, there is inconclusive evidence over which technique yields better results in clinical practice, leading to delays in the diagnosis of EPTB.

This study aims to conduct a comprehensive comparison between PCR/GENEXPERT vs. ADA to determine the superiority in the investigative diagnosis of EPTB, ultimately contributing to the growing discourse and confusion in the field of EPTB diagnosis, with the ultimate goal to be providing focused and timely treatment in resource-limited areas.

Subjects and Methods

Ethical Considerations: The study was conducted and appropriate samples were collected from patients admitted to the Pulmonary Medicine, Internal Medicine, and Surgery Departments at the Index Medical College, Hospital and Research Centre, after receiving appropriate sanction from the ethical committee for the study from January 2017 to April 2019. Appropriate and necessary formal permissions along with informed consent were obtained from all the subjects included in this study.

Study design

This research incorporated a cross-sectional observational design, with a study population consisting of 77 individuals meeting the inclusion criteria. 44 samples were obtained from pleural fluid, while 33 samples were collected from peritoneal fluid.

Inclusion criteria

Patients included in the study were clinically diagnosed with either pleural or abdominal TB, above 12 years old, culturally positive for TB in the respective fluids, and without comorbidities that could confound the diagnosis.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with malignancy, heart failure, liver disease, pleural effusion due to other conditions, negligible amounts of pleural fluids, previous opportunistic infections, refusal to provide consent, age <12 years and HIV-positive status were excluded. Individuals with TB infection in bones, joints, kidneys, lymph nodes, or fluids from other spaces were not included.

Demographic characteristics: 59% of the study population belonged to the 20–40 years age group, with around 60% educated college educated, and 66% having a reported average monthly income above 125 USD.

Study procedure

Patients previously positive for TB, now presenting with clinical features suggestive of tuberculous pleural effusion or abdominal TB, underwent thoracentesis or paracentesis, respectively. All the study participants had not been previously diagnosed with EPTB.

Data collection

Each diagnosis was documented and classified for further analysis, and the study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, ensuring transparency and completeness in reporting [Figure 1].

STROBE flow diagram

Statistical analysis: The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of using either pleural or peritoneal fluid samples for TB diagnosis were calculated based on the compiled results. These statistical measures shed light on the diagnostic accuracy and reliability of the fluid samples.

ADA measurement

Adenosine deaminase (ADA) activity was assessed using a spectrophotometric methodology. The ADA-MTB (R) kit was used and the ADA activity was determined by the conversion of adenosine to inosine, observed at 546nm. Pleural and peritoneal samples were placed in sterile or heparinised tubes and treated for 2 hours at room temperature. Reagent 1, consisting of TRIS HCl pH 8.0, 4-AA 2 mM, PNP 0.1 U/ml, XOD 0.2 U/ml, and peroxidase 0.6 U/ml, and Reagent 2, containing TRIS HCl pH 4-50 mM, adenosine 10 mM, and Ehstp 2 mM, were used for the analysis.

GeneXpert/PCR analysis: The Xpert Mtb/RIF kit (CGXM.tb/RIF-10) was used for the GeneXpert/PCR analysis. Pleural or peritoneal fluid sediments were prepared by adding 1.5 ml Xpert M. Tb/RIF sample reagent to 0.5 ml of resuspended pellet and incubating for 15 minutes at room temperature. After vigorous shaking, the specimen was transferred to the Xpert M. Tb/RIF cartridge, labelled with the lab accession number, and analyzed in the GeneXpert instrument. The test was initiated using the GX2.1 software, and results were obtained within 12 hours.

Cartridge preparation and test initiation: Each Xpert M. tb/RIF cartridge was labelled with the lab accession number, and the liquefied specimen was aspirated into the transfer pipette until the meniscus reached the minimum mark. The sample was then transferred into the open port of the cartridge, and the lid was firmly closed. The cartridge was loaded into the GeneXpert instrument, and the test was initiated using the GX2.1 software. The system was checked for the assay and software version compatibility before proceeding. The test was started by scanning the cartridge barcode, entering the sample ID, and initiating the test through the software interface.

Safety precautions: Various safety precautions were followed during cartridge handling and disposal, such as discarding used cartridges according to safety guidelines to prevent the potential transmission of infectious agents.

In summary, this study reaffirms the conventional practice of conducting ADA tests for pleural effusion or ascites to detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis. ADA proves to be cost-effective, easily accessible, and exhibits reliable sensitivity and negative predictive value when compared to GeneXpertPCR. However, it is crucial not to rely solely on ADA for diagnosing Mycobacterium tuberculosis due to its low specificity in disease detection. GeneXpert, with its high specificity and positive predictive value, serves as a valuable tool for confirming established TB cases. Despite these advantages, using GeneXpert as a routine test for determining EPTB cases is not recommended due to its poor sensitivity in uncertain cases and those without a conclusive diagnosis. Combining ADA with GeneXpert/PCR proves more effective than using them individually for identifying MTb in pleural and ascitic fluids. Therefore, it is suggested to initially perform ADA and subsequently conduct PCR to specify tuberculosis.

Discussion

Despite advancements in tuberculosis treatment, EPTB poses diagnostic challenges, emphasizing the crucial need for timely determination to enhance the effectiveness of TB drugs. This study aims to compare the efficacy of ADA and GeneXpert/PCR in determining EPTB from pleural and peritoneal fluids and assess whether their combined use offers additional benefits. Additionally, the study aims to correlate sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value of PCR/GeneXpert and ADA for detecting extrapulmonary tuberculosis, evaluating 77 clinically diagnosed cases of tuberculosis involving peritoneal and pleural fluids.

Both ADA and GeneXpert were applied to collected fluids, with pleural fluid ADA set at a cut-off of 40 U/L and ascitic fluid ADA at 30 U/L based on prior studies. The comparative analysis revealed ADA’s high sensitivity in pleural and peritoneal fluids, detecting numerous truly positive EPTB cases, despite its lower specificity. Previous literature supports ADA’s sensitivity and specificity, particularly in pleural fluid cases. In pleural fluid, GeneXpert demonstrated a sensitivity of 38.10%, lower than its specificity of 50%, as observed in prior studies. Despite its poor sensitivity, GeneXpert’s extremely high specificity in determining tuberculous pleural effusion aligns with a systematic review by Sehgal et al.[12] The validity of the study is further substantiated by its results resembling those of previous reviews, emphasizing GeneXpert’s use in EPTB diagnosis.

In contrast, the statistical analysis of ADA versus GeneXpert in ascitic fluid revealed ADA’s sensitivity of 75%, specificity of 6.9%, positive predictive value of 10%, and negative predictive value of 66.67%. These findings coincide with Gupta et al.’s[13] study. Despite the high-sensitivity ADA exhibits in previous studies, GeneXpert’s clinical utility is limited when used independently.

Statistical analysis indicates that the combined use of ADA and GeneXpert significantly improves EPTB diagnosis. The study’s strengths include enrolling all 77 clinically diagnosed tuberculosis patients, utilizing non-invasive tests with a quick turnaround time, and ensuring stringent sample collection and immediate transportation to the laboratory. Limitations included the short duration of the study, a small sample size, and geographical restrictions to South Asian countries. The findings of this study lack generalisability since it was conducted at a single centre. Furthermore, the investigation could be extended to a larger cohort of suspected cases.

Conclusion

The burden of EPTB significantly impacts health outcomes in India, with GeneXpert/PCR and ADA being the predominant diagnostic tools. However, prior to this study, uncertainty existed regarding the preferred diagnostic approach, causing delays and missed cases of EPTB. This research aims to contribute clarity to this issue. The study reinforces the practice of performing ADA in all cases of pleural effusion or ascites for detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis. ADA proves to be cost-effective, easily accessible, and exhibits reliable sensitivity and negative predictive value compared to GeneXpert PCR. However, it is emphasized that ADA should not be the sole diagnostic test due to its low specificity. GeneXpert, with its high specificity and positive predictive value, is valuable in confirming established tuberculosis cases. Despite these advantages, using GeneXpert as a routine test for EPTB is not recommended due to its poor sensitivity in doubtful cases.

In conclusion, choosing the better test remains challenging, as both have their pros and cons. However, a combined use of both tests yields improved outcomes. Further investigations, including other forms of EPTB like Tb meningitis, are essential to establish the collective use, aiming to reduce morbidity and mortality. Larger study groups, including TB HIV co-infection cases, are necessary for confirming efficacy in diverse patient population.
