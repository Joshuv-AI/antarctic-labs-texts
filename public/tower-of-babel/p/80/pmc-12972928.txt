The correlation between triglyceride-glucose index and the risk and severity of obstructive sleep apnea in the general population: a single-center cross-sectional study
Authors: Junwei Yang, Zong-An Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common disorder characterized by airway obstruction. Recent evidence suggests that insulin resistance (IR) may significantly impact sleep health. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a non-invasive marker of IR, potentially linked to OSA risk. However, existing studies often featured small sample sizes and limited analyses. This study aimed to evaluate the association between the TyG index and the risk and severity of OSA in the general population.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, single-center, retrospective study involving 600 adult patients with complete sleep respiratory monitoring results at West China Hospital from December 2024 to May 2025. The TyG index was treated as the exposure variable, while the risk and severity of OSA were the outcome variables. The TyG index was calculated as follows: TyG = ln [fasting triglycerides (TG) (mg/dL) × fasting blood glucose (FBG) (mg/dL)/2]. To assess the relationship between the TyG index and OSA, we performed multivariate logistic regression, subgroup analyses, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses.ResultsAmong the total population, 515 (85.8%) were diagnosed with OSA and 247 individuals (41.2%) had severe OSA. After adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, antihypertensive drugs, hypoglycemic drugs and various biochemical markers, multivariate logistic regression revealed that a one-unit increase in the TyG index was associated with a 168.9% higher risk of OSA (P<0.001), while a one-standard deviation (SD) increase corresponded to a 99.9% higher risk (P<0.001). Additionally, participants in the high-TyG group had a 2.954-fold higher risk of OSA compared with those in the low-TyG group [95% confidence interval (CI): 1.634–5.341; P<0.001]. In the fully adjusted model, each one-unit and one SD increase in the TyG index was associated with a 47.5% (P=0.003) and 31.3% (P=0.003) increase, respectively, in the risk of severe OSA. Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the association between the TyG index and both OSA risk and severity remained significant across several subgroups (P<0.05). Sensitivity analyses excluding individuals with chronic kidney disease confirmed the robustness of these associations (P<0.05). Operating characteristic curves (ROC) analyses indicated that the TyG index was a moderate predictor of OSA risk [area under the curve (AUC): 0.704; 95% CI: 0.674–0.762; P<0.001] and a modest predictor of severe OSA (AUC: 0.573; 95% CI: 0.524–0.622; P<0.001).ConclusionsElevated TyG index levels are significantly associated with increased risk and severity of OSA, highlighting the potential utility of the TyG index as an initial screening tool in the diagnostic pathway for OSA. Future research should involve larger, prospective, multicenter trials and external validation.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common chronic sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of partial or complete airway obstruction, leading to repetitive apneas and hypopneas (1). If left untreated, OSA increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, and stroke (2,3). The primary mechanism underlying OSA is the collapse of the upper airway (4). Among the contributing anatomical factors, adenoid hypertrophy is a clinically significant abnormality that can induce nasal obstruction, obligate mouth breathing, and ultimately disrupt normal respiratory patterns (5). In patients with fluid retention, fluid may shift from the legs to the neck during the night, contributing to upper airway collapse (6). Additionally, during wakefulness, neuronal activity maintains the tone of the muscles that keep the airway open, preventing collapse. However, during rapid eye movement sleep, reduced muscle activation can allow airway collapse to occur (7). Obesity, especially visceral fat, is a known risk factor for OSA (8). Other common risk factors for OSA include obesity, age, male gender, craniofacial and soft tissue abnormalities that narrow the upper airway, nasal congestion, smoking, family history, menopause or post-menopause, comorbid conditions such as hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, acromegaly, hypothyroidism, and chronic kidney disease (CKD), as well as exposure to certain medications or substances, including anesthetics, benzodiazepines, and alcohol (9). OSA-induced hypoxemia and micro-arousals not only disrupt sleep quality but also negatively affect overall health, contributing to hypertension, insulin resistance (IR), metabolic syndrome, and neuropsychiatric complications such as depression (10). These complications significantly increase disease burden, making treatment and rehabilitation more challenging (11). The prognosis of OSA is closely linked to disease severity. Sleep monitoring allows clinicians to classify OSA severity and develop appropriate treatment plans. The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), which measures the average number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep, is commonly used for this purpose: an AHI of 5–14 indicates mild OSA, 15–29 indicates moderate OSA, and 30 or higher indicates severe OSA (12). While lifestyle interventions may suffice for mild cases, moderate to severe OSA often requires more aggressive treatments, such as continuous positive airway pressure. Consequently, early identification of modifiable risk factors is crucial for improving disease risk, severity, complications, and overall prognosis.

Current evidence suggests that IR may play a significant role in sleep health. Archontogeorgis et al. [2017] reported that OSA was independently associated with IR in non-diabetic patients (13). Furthermore, several experimental studies using methods to induce sleep fragmentation have consistently shown that reduced deep sleep leads to decreased insulin sensitivity (14-16). Various indicators are available to assess IR, among which the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has gained considerable attention due to its widespread use, reproducibility, and non-invasive nature. Previous studies have suggested that the TyG index is not only associated with metabolic disorders and cardiovascular diseases but may also be linked to OSA risk. For instance, Kang et al. demonstrated an independent association between the TyG index and OSA risk in 180 Korean adults attending a sleep clinic (17). Similarly, Bikov et al. enrolled 132 patients with OSA and 49 non-OSA, reported elevated TyG values in non-diabetic, non-obese OSA patients, independent of age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) (18). Zou et al. further showed that the TyG index exhibited moderate diagnostic accuracy for OSA in both males and females (19). Despite these findings, there are several limitations in previous studies: they involved small sample sizes, considered few confounding factors, only alcohol consumption, smoking and BMI, did not perform in-depth subgroup analyses. IR may lead to increased OSA-related risks, such as diabetes and hypertension, which in turn affects the severity of the disease. However, no studies have simultaneously evaluated the relationship between the TyG index and both OSA risk and severity within a single dataset.

Based on the aforementioned background, this study aimed to investigate the association between the TyG index and both the risk and severity of OSA in the general population, while exploring potential population-specific characteristics, in order to provide new insights and a theoretical basis for the clinical management and prognosis of OSA. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1625/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional, single-center, retrospective observational study consecutively collected patient data from West China Hospital between December 2024 and May 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (I) age ≥18 years; and (II) complete sleep respiratory monitoring results. Exclusion criteria included: (I) insufficient sleep monitoring data to assess OSA risk and severity; (II) missing fasting triglycerides (TG) or fasting blood glucose (FBG) data (n=3); (III) presence of malignant tumors (n=11); (IV) severe kidney or liver failure (n=4); and (V) severe hematological or immune system disorders (n=1). The flow diagram detailing participant recruitment and exclusions is presented in Figure 1. Following the application of these criteria, 600 adults were included in the study.

Flow diagram of recruitment and exclusion of participants.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital (No. 2024233) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Determination and evaluation of TyG

In this study, the TyG index was calculated as follows: TyG = ln [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2] (20), where TG and FBG were measured using the glycerol phosphate oxidase method and venous plasma glucose measurement, respectively. To facilitate analysis of the association between TyG and OSA risk, the TyG index was standardized as: standardized TyG = (original TyG − mean)/standard deviation (SD). Based on the median TyG value, all participants (n=600) were divided into two groups: a low-TyG group (≤8.74, n=299) and a high-TyG group (>8.74, n=301). Among participants with OSA only (n=515), the low-TyG group included 258 individuals (≤8.79), and the high-TyG group included 257 individuals (>8.79).

Diagnosis and evaluation of OSA

All patients underwent overnight laboratory-based polysomnography, during which electrooculography, electroencephalography, submental and anterior tibialis electromyography were recorded. In addition, chest and abdominal movements, oxygen saturation, and oronasal airflow were continuously monitored. Peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2) was tracked using a pulse oximeter, and changes in body position during sleep were recorded. Apnea was defined as a ≥90% reduction in airflow from baseline lasting 10 seconds or longer, while hypopnea was defined as a ≥30% reduction in airflow lasting 10 seconds or longer, accompanied by either a ≥3% decrease in oxygen saturation or an arousal. Patients with an AHI ≥5 were classified as having OSA (21). Based on this definition, participants were divided into an OSA group (n=515) and a non-OSA group (n=85). Among patients with OSA, severity was further classified using AHI into severe (n=247) and non-severe (n=268) groups.

Collection and definition of variables

This study collected comprehensive data on demographics, anthropometric measurements, comorbidities, prior medication use, and blood biomarkers. Demographic data included age, gender, smoking status (defined as having ever smoked or currently smoking, regardless of cessation), and alcohol consumption (defined as having ever consumed alcohol or currently consuming it, regardless of cessation). Anthropometric and basic clinical data included BMI, height, weight, heart rate, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and systolic blood pressure (SBP). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). DBP, SBP, and heart rate were measured at the patients’ initial hospitalization. Comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes, obesity, CKD, COPD, atrial fibrillation, hyperuricemia, and gout. Hypertension was defined as SBP/DBP ≥140/90 mmHg, a previous diagnosis of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medications (22). Diabetes was defined as a prior diagnosis, current use of antidiabetic medications, FBG ≥7.0 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5% (23). Obesity was defined as BMI ≥28 kg/m2 (24). Hyperuricemia was defined as serum uric acid ≥420 µmol/L (25). Prior medication use included antihypertensive and antidiabetic medications. Blood biomarkers included platelet count, hemoglobin, white blood cell count (WBC), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin, total bilirubin, uric acid, FBG, TG, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), serum electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium), fibrinogen, and D-dimer. All biomarkers were obtained from fasting (≥8 hours) antecubital vein blood samples, collected by trained nursing staff and analyzed in the central laboratory.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As none of the continuous variables followed a normal distribution, they were presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Differences between two groups were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To identify factors associated with OSA risk and severity, univariate logistic regression analyses were initially performed. Variables with P<0.05 were subsequently included in multivariate logistic regression models to assess the independent association of the TyG index with OSA risk and severity. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on gender, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and hyperuricemia to evaluate the stability of these associations. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients with CKD was performed to further validate the robustness of the observed associations. Additionally, 170 patients were randomly selected from the OSA group and analyzed alongside the non-OSA group using multivariate regression. The predictive ability of the TyG index for OSA risk and severity was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The sample size for this study was determined based on practical considerations and available resources, aiming to ensure sufficient statistical power for detecting meaningful associations between TyG index and OSA. While a priori sample size calculations were not performed due to the exploratory nature of the study, we conducted a post hoc power analysis to evaluate the adequacy of the sample size for detecting the observed effect sizes. Using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6), we estimated the statistical power for the primary outcome (OSA diagnosis) based on the observed effect size and the sample size (n=600). The analysis assumed a two-tailed significance level (α =0.05) and a power threshold of 80%. The results indicated that the study had sufficient power (>80%) to detect the observed effect size.The Little’s MCAR test was conducted on the collected dataset. The pattern of missing data met the criteria for Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), and the number of missing values for each biomarker was ≤10 (representing <5% of the total sample size), Consequently, the mean imputation method was employed to replace all missing values.

Results

Baseline characteristics were organized based on the classification of OSA

As shown in Table 1, a total of 600 patients were included, of whom 515 (85.8%) were diagnosed with OSA. Compared with the non-OSA group, patients with OSA were older, predominantly male, and had higher proportions of smokers and alcohol consumers. They also exhibited higher prevalence rates of diabetes, hypertension, obesity, hyperuricemia, and gout, along with increased use of antihypertensive and antidiabetic medications. Moreover, the OSA group had elevated levels of BMI, DBP, SBP, hemoglobin, ALT, AST, total bilirubin, uric acid, FBG, TG, and TyG index, while displaying lower levels of HDL-C, eGFR, and serum calcium (all P<0.05).

Among the total population, 247 individuals (41.2%) had severe OSA. Compared with the non-severe group, patients with severe OSA were older, more likely to be male, and had higher proportions of smokers and alcohol consumers. Baseline characteristics grouped according to median TyG

As shown in Table 2, the study population was divided into low-TyG and high-TyG groups based on the median TyG index. Compared with the low-TyG group, the high-TyG group included a higher proportion of men and exhibited increased prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, obesity, hyperuricemia, and gout. Importantly, as shown in Tables 2,3, the high-TyG group had a significantly higher prevalence of both OSA and severe OSA compared with the low-TyG group (P<0.05).

Discussion

This single-center, cross-sectional, real-world study demonstrated that higher TyG index levels are significantly associated with increased risk and severity of OSA in the general population. These associations remained consistent across most subgroup and sensitivity analyses, highlighting their robustness. Furthermore, ROC analysis indicated that the TyG index has predictive value for both OSA risk and severity. Collectively, these findings provide evidence supporting the use of the TyG index as a practical marker for routine screening and risk stratification of OSA.

Current evidence suggests that IR plays a significant role in sleep health. For example, Punjabi et al. recruited 150 healthy men and assessed polysomnography, oral glucose tolerance, fasting insulin, and lipid levels (26). They reported that the prevalence of sleep-disordered breathing ranged from 40% to 60%, and multivariable linear regression analysis indicated that increases in the AHI were independently associated with worsening IR, regardless of obesity. This finding highlights that OSA is common among mildly obese men and is independently linked to glucose intolerance and IR (27). Among the various metrics for evaluating IR, the TyG index has gained attention as a widely used, reproducible, and non-invasive biomarker. Several studies have examined the relationship between the TyG index and OSA. Bianchi et al. [2015] investigated 302 elderly patients with small abdominal aortic aneurysms and categorized them into low- and high-risk OSA groups using the Berlin Questionnaire (28). The high-risk OSA group exhibited significantly higher levels of multiple biomarkers, including the TyG index. In 2020, Zou et al. studied 4,703 patients suspected of having OSA, reporting a significant correlation between TyG and OSA severity in linear regression analysis (19). Multivariable logistic regression confirmed that TyG was significantly associated with OSA in both male and female patients, and ROC analysis indicated moderate diagnostic accuracy, suggesting that TyG may serve as a straightforward screening tool for OSA. Kang et al. [2020] retrospectively analyzed 180 suspected OSA patients in Korean sleep clinics and found an independent correlation between TyG and OSA risk (17). The optimal threshold for predicting OSA was a TyG index of 8.83 (specificity: 69.0%, sensitivity: 61.6%, AUC: 0.688), with predictive accuracy improving in individuals aged ≥55 years or those with obesity, indicating enhanced utility in older, obese populations (17). Similarly, Bikov et al. included 132 non-obese, non-diabetic OSA patients and 49 non-OSA controls, finding that TyG values were significantly higher in the OSA group, independent of age, sex, and BMI, and were independently correlated with OSA risk (18). These studies collectively suggest a significant association between the TyG index and OSA risk. However, no prior research has systematically examined the relationship between TyG and both the risk and severity of OSA within a single dataset. The present study addressed this gap, demonstrating that higher TyG levels were significantly associated with increased OSA risk and severity in the general population. These associations remained consistent across most high-risk subgroups. Moreover, ROC analysis indicated that TyG is a predictor of both OSA risk and severity, warranting further investigation into the underlying biological mechanisms.

Despite the significant findings of this study, the underlying biological mechanisms linking the TyG index and OSA remain unclear. Previous literature suggests several potential pathophysiological pathways. First, IR leads to elevated blood glucose levels, which activate various inflammatory pathways (28). Hyperglycemia also induces oxidative stress, generating free radicals that damage cells and tissues and further exacerbate inflammation (29). Studies have confirmed significant associations between inflammation, oxidative stress, and both the onset and progression of OSA (30). Therefore, IR may indirectly contribute to OSA development through inflammatory and oxidative stress mechanisms. Second, IR is closely linked to cardiovascular disorders such as hypertension (31), and hyperinsulinemia can promote hyperuricemia (32), both of which are recognized risk factors for OSA (9). IR may also disrupt lipid metabolism, leading to fat accumulation (33), which has been shown to substantially increase the risk of moderate to severe sleep-disordered breathing (34). These findings suggest that IR may exacerbate OSA severity indirectly via its effects on lipid metabolism. Third, OSA itself contributes to metabolic disturbances. Recurrent episodes of apnea and intermittent hypoxia during sleep can alter metabolic patterns in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, disrupting insulin signaling and promoting IR (35). This bidirectional relationship creates a vicious cycle, wherein IR worsens OSA through lipid dysregulation and inflammatory activation, while OSA further amplifies IR. Future research should focus on cellular and animal studies to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which IR influences the onset and progression of OSA, which may provide new targets for prevention and therapeutic interventions.

Limitations

In addition to the unresolved biological mechanisms linking IR and OSA, there are several limitations in this study. First, as a cross-sectional, single-center, retrospective study, the sample size is relatively limited, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Future research should involve larger, prospective, multicenter trials to validate these associations. Second, as an observational study, genetic factors were not explored, and thus the causal relationship between the TyG index and OSA risk or severity remains unestablished. Third, although multiple confounding factors were considered, some important variables, such as genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, dietary influences, and other health-related factors, may have been overlooked. Fourth, the TyG index was measured at a single time point, which does not account for dynamic changes or long-term exposure. Consequently, the relationship between sustained or fluctuating TyG levels and OSA risk or severity remains unclear. Fifth, among the 600 patients included, only 85 were non-OSA, which may introduce selection bias. Future studies with larger and more balanced populations are warranted. Sixth, the definition of obesity was based on criteria for the Chinese population rather than the World Health Organization standards, which may limit the applicability of the results to other ethnicities. Broader studies across diverse populations are needed. Seventh, the AUC value is 0.704, the clinical utility is limited, and there is a lack of external validation. Current findings supported further research rather than immediate clinical application. Finally, because TyG is closely associated with age, obesity, and diabetes, factors that are themselves risk factors for OSA, it is possible that TyG indirectly influences OSA risk through these pathways. As a cross-sectional observational study, causality cannot be established. Nevertheless, ROC analysis indicated that TyG exhibited the strongest predictive value among the confounding factors, highlighting its potential utility as a screening biomarker.

Despite these limitations, the clinical significance of our findings remains clear. Future research should aim to address these shortcomings, refine study designs, and further validate the robustness of the observed associations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that elevated TyG index levels are significantly associated with increased risk and severity of OSA. These associations were consistent across multiple high-risk populations, highlighting the potential role of the TyG index and IR in guiding OSA prevention and management strategies. Future research should focus on larger, multicenter, prospective clinical trials to further elucidate the relationship between TyG levels and OSA development. Additionally, interventional studies targeting elevated TyG indices are warranted to determine whether reducing TyG levels can improve OSA risk and severity.
