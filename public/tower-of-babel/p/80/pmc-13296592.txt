Factors Influencing the Maintenance of Advanced Life Support Competencies Among Critical Care Professionals: A Scoping Review
Authors: Sónia Ferreira de Sousa, Maria do Céu Mendes Pinto Marques, Guida Maria Marques da Silva Amaral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdvanced life support (ALS) competence may deteriorate within months after certification, but what supports ongoing competence in critical care remains unclear.AimTo map factors influencing maintenance of ALS competence among critical care professionals working in critical care settings.Study DesignScoping review conducted in accordance with Joanna Briggs Institute guidance and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. MEDLINE, CINAHL and the Cochrane Library were searched (from February to March 2025). We included peer‐reviewed and grey sources from hospital‐based critical care settings that assessed ALS competencies over time. Data were synthesised using inductive thematic analysis.ResultsTwenty‐five sources were included (19 peer‐reviewed, 6 grey). Factors were mapped to three domains. Educational factors were most frequently reported and centred on simulation‐based rehearsal, structured feedback and debriefing and spaced refreshers delivered in brief, frequent sessions. Institutional determinants related to protected training time, access to resources, standardised roles and content and monitoring through audit and feedback. Individual determinants included clinical exposure and experience, perceived preparedness and self‐confidence, stress and cognitive load, engagement in ongoing learning and non‐technical behaviours. Outcomes were largely simulation‐based or self‐reported, and longer‐term durability was inconsistently assessed.ConclusionsMaintenance of ALS competence appears to require coordinated educational and organisational supports; longitudinal evaluations with explicit definitions and validated outcomes are needed.Relevance to Clinical PracticeCritical care services may strengthen ALS competence maintenance by embedding recurrent, context‐aligned rehearsal with feedback, supported by protected training time, accessible training infrastructure and ongoing performance monitoring.Review RegistrationThe review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/PEQJH.

Introduction

ALS is central to the management of life‐threatening deterioration and cardiac arrest, where timely, coordinated actions are associated with improved outcomes [1, 2]. In hospital‐based critical care settings, physicians and nurses involved in ALS care must respond to deterioration and cardiac arrest under time pressure and uncertainty, relying on integrated competence that includes knowledge, psychomotor performance and non‐technical team behaviours such as leadership, communication and situational awareness [3, 4]. This scoping review maps the factors reported to influence the maintenance of ALS competence among hospital‐based critical care professionals.

Background/Justification for the Study

ALS knowledge and performance commonly decline within months after initial training or certification, particularly for infrequently performed tasks and time‐critical actions [5, 6, 7]. International guidance therefore emphasises deliberate practice, feedback, team‐based training and regular reassessment [4, 8, 9, 10]. Despite this, recommended retraining intervals vary across organisations and are often based on consensus rather than comparative evidence [4, 8, 11, 12]. Some studies suggest that refreshers may be needed within 2–6 months, and low‐dose high‐frequency (LDHF) models, involving brief, recurrent refresher practice distributed over time, have been operationalised in programmes such as Resuscitation Quality Improvement (RQI), but their implementation and the competence‐related outcomes used to evaluate them remain heterogeneous [8, 13, 14]. Clinical exposure may also contribute to sustained performance, but this relationship is not well characterised [15, 16].

Maintaining ALS competence is therefore a patient safety and service delivery concern because deterioration in competence may compromise timely recognition and coordinated delivery of resuscitation care [17, 18]. In this review, maintenance of ALS competence refers to the sustained capability to recognise the deteriorating or arrested patient and to apply ALS algorithms over time following initial training, including rhythm recognition and management, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), defibrillation, post‐resuscitation care, and non‐technical team behaviours such as leadership, communication and situational awareness. Retention is used more narrowly in the literature to describe preservation of specific post‐training performance at defined follow‐up time points, rather than the broader multicomponent construct captured by maintenance. Evidence in this area is dispersed across studies focussed on educational formats and simulation outcomes, alongside guidance that stresses organisational enablers such as protected training time, standardised processes and audit with feedback [4, 18, 19]. Individual determinants, including confidence, stress responses and cognitive load, are also described as relevant but are often not evaluated longitudinally or with validated outcomes [20, 21, 22].

Accordingly, this scoping review maps the factors reported to influence maintenance of ALS competence among hospital‐based critical care professionals.

Aim and Research Question

The aim of this scoping review was to map the factors reported to influence the maintenance of ALS competencies among critical care professionals in hospital‐based critical care settings. The research question was ‘What factors influence the maintenance of Advanced Life Support competencies among healthcare professionals in critical care settings?’

Design and Methods

We conducted this scoping review in accordance with Joanna Briggs Institute guidance for scoping reviews [23] and reported it in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) [24].

Setting and Sample

Eligibility criteria were developed according to the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework, as recommended in JBI scoping review guidance [23].

Population

We included physicians, nurses and students in these professions when training or practice occurred in critical care settings. Studies of mixed professional teams were eligible when findings were clearly applicable to, or could be interpreted for, physicians and/or nurses.

Concept

The concept of interest was maintenance of ALS competence, which in this review was treated as a broad construct encompassing ALS‐related knowledge, technical and non‐technical performance and their preservation over time following initial training. For the purposes of this review, ‘over time’ referred to any follow‐up assessment beyond immediate post‐training or post‐course measurement; no single minimum interval was imposed because the aim was to map how maintenance was assessed across the available literature. Studies were eligible if they reported any ALS‐related knowledge, skill or performance over time (including retention or decay outcomes) and described factors reported to influence those trajectories. We used ‘factors’ as an umbrella term for individual, educational or institutional elements described as potentially shaping competence maintenance, including tested variables, qualitative influences and normative recommendations. Competence‐related outcomes were charted as defined by the original authors and were commonly assessed using knowledge tests, simulation‐based metrics, observed performance or self‐report; therefore, we did not assume direct equivalence between these measures and longer‐term clinical performance, and we anticipated heterogeneity in outcome measures.

A small number of studies focussed on Basic Life Support (BLS) or on broader resuscitation programmes (e.g., Advanced Trauma Life Support) were included only when participants were critical care professionals with ALS level responsibilities and when outcomes or factors were directly relevant to ALS competence maintenance in critical care. Studies limited to technical BLS measures without a clear link to ALS decision making or practice in critical care settings were excluded.

Context

We included adult hospital‐based critical care contexts where ALS‐level interventions are routinely delivered, such as Intensive Care Units (ICU), Emergency Departments (ED) or Operating Rooms (OR). Studies conducted in broader hospital populations or mixed acute care settings were eligible when results for high‐risk areas were reported separately, or when data relevant to these settings were clearly extractable, ensuring applicability to the target context. When the unit type was not explicitly labelled as ICU, ED, or OR, we retained studies only if participants' clinical roles and the training or assessment content clearly involved ALS level responsibilities in hospital‐based high acuity care. This approach aimed to avoid excluding potentially relevant evidence due to incomplete reporting while maintaining alignment with the review context.

Source Types and Limits

We included original studies, systematic reviews, and scoping reviews published in English, Portuguese, or Spanish between March 2019 and March 2025. Grey literature was eligible when it provided transparent methods and extractable findings relevant to the PCC criteria. We limited inclusion to this period to prioritise contemporary evidence relevant to current resuscitation education practice while maintaining feasibility for mapping within a rapidly evolving literature.

Data Collection Tools and Methods

Information Sources

The databases consulted included MEDLINE Ultimate, CINAHL Ultimate and the Cochrane Library (via EBSCOhost). Searches were conducted between February and March 2025. Full‐text access was obtained through institutional subscriptions. When full‐text articles were not readily available, assistance was sought from academic and hospital librarians.

Search Strategy

The search strategy combined three concepts: ALS competencies, healthcare providers, and hospital‐based critical care settings, using Boolean operators and database specific indexing. Complete search strategies are detailed in File S1.

Selection of Sources of Evidence

The screening process was conducted manually. Titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by two authors. Full text screening and data extraction were carried out by all three authors. Disagreements were resolved through discussion among all authors.

Data Charting Process

A structured data extraction form was developed to chart study characteristics, competence‐related outcomes, and reported influencing factors. Extracted data were cross‐checked and consolidated. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with re‐examination of the full texts when necessary.

Data Items

The data extraction table was designed to ensure comprehensive information addressing the review objectives. Data were charted on publication characteristics, setting and participants, competence domain and outcome measures, factors reported to influence competence maintenance, and key author conclusions. Factors were extracted in their original descriptive form, without prior categorisation, to minimise interpretation bias and preserve analytical neutrality. The full extraction framework is presented in File S2.

Data Analysis

Influencing factors were extracted using the original terminology reported by the authors. Data were synthesised using an inductive, iterative thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns and group related concepts across sources. Three overarching domains (individual, institutional and educational) were developed through successive rounds of refinement. Sources contributing to multiple domains were coded across domains. The final thematic structure was applied across the full dataset to support the synthesis presented in Section 4.3.

Critical appraisal was not undertaken, as the purpose of this review was to map the nature and range of evidence rather than to estimate effects or certainty.

Ethics Statement

Ethical and institutional approval were not required for this scoping review because it synthesised published and publicly available literature.

Results

Selection of Sources of Evidence

Database searching identified 249 records (MEDLINE n = 169, CINAHL n = 76, Cochrane Library n = 4). After removing 39 duplicates, 210 records were screened by title and abstract, and 68 full texts were assessed for eligibility. Forty‐nine reports were excluded, primarily due to wrong outcome (n = 28), wrong population (n = 10), wrong setting (n = 8), or wrong context (n = 3), with one primary exclusion reason assigned per report. Twenty‐five sources were included. The selection process is summarised in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1), and excluded full texts are listed in File S3.

Characteristics of Sources of Evidence

Twenty‐five sources published between March 2019 and March 2025 were included, comprising 19 peer‐reviewed studies and 6 grey literature documents. Most sources originated from high‐income settings, particularly North America, Australia, and Europe, with additional contributions from Asia and the Middle East. Study participants were primarily nurses and physicians working in hospital‐based high acuity environments, including ICU's, ED's, and OR's. Sample sizes ranged from 15 to 888 participants.

Peer‐reviewed sources predominantly reported quantitative outcomes, with fewer qualitative designs and evidence syntheses. Grey literature sources were largely policy, guideline, consensus, or regulatory documents and were not consistently presented with comparable study‐level methodological reporting.

Overview of Synthesis and Evidence Type

Across sources, competence maintenance was most commonly operationalised using knowledge tests, simulation‐based performance metrics, or self‐report measures, with follow‐up most often limited to weeks or months. Factors were charted using the authors' original terminology and synthesised inductively. Three domains emerged (individual, institutional, educational), with sources coded across domains when multicomponent initiatives were described. Given the heterogeneous evidence base and the inclusion of grey literature, the Results narrative distinguishes empirical findings from normative recommendations, with detailed source to cluster mapping presented in Tables 1, 2, 3 and File S4.

Thematic Clusters of Influencing Factors

The synthesis identified a multilevel set of factors reported to influence ALS competence maintenance. Educational factors were most frequently reported, followed by institutional determinants and individual‐level influences. Hybrid initiatives were coded across domains rather than generating additional categories, reflecting the integrated structure of several programmes. Thematic clusters within each domain are presented in Tables 1, 2, 3.

Individual Factors

Individual influences on ALS competence maintenance were mapped into 13 thematic clusters (Table 1). The narrative highlights recurring patterns across clusters, focussing on clinician background and exposure, motivation and engagement with training, cognitive and emotional demands during performance and non‐technical behaviours.

Two cross‐sectional surveys reported correlates of theoretical knowledge. Pérez‐Ibáñez et al. [26] associated higher knowledge with postgraduate education, male gender and recent ALS training, whereas Vidmanić et al. [27] reported higher knowledge among nurses with greater professional experience and additional resuscitation training. These findings describe associations and do not establish maintenance trajectories.

Readiness factors were primarily described in simulation‐based work. Campbell and Clark [28] reported stress, cognitive overload and limited prior exposure among new graduates and identified unfamiliarity with essential equipment, particularly the defibrillator, as relevant to time‐critical actions. Related sources discussed stress and anxiety as potential constraints [29]. Professional role and experience [30], prior ALS exposure [31] and scenario‐related cognitive load and comfort [32] were described as additional learner level considerations.

Across interventions, confidence and self‐assessed preparedness were frequently reported, whereas objective maintenance outcomes were less consistently measured. Mastoras et al. [33] described perceived benefits of repeated practice for high‐acuity, low‐opportunity procedures, with outcomes reported descriptively. In a cross‐sectional evaluation of a gamified digital tool, Chen et al. [34] reported that, among the technology‐related variables examined, perceived usefulness was the only independent predictor of nurses' self‐assessed competence. Marler et al. [35] reported knowledge gains that remained above baseline at 3 months and high self‐reported confidence, although confidence did not correlate with time to task performance. Armstrong et al. [31] similarly reported short‐term increases in confidence and empowerment following simulation, without evaluating longer‐term maintenance.

Au et al. [36] synthesised resuscitation training trials and noted that in some included studies, younger age, greater intensive care exposure and completing ALS training after a period of clinical experience were associated with higher scores on follow‐up simulated resuscitation assessments.

Guideline and strategic documents emphasised well‐being, psychological safety, resilience, engagement in continuous learning and non‐technical skills such as leadership, teamwork and closed‐loop communication. These sources primarily provide recommendations and trial summaries, with limited and inconsistent evidence for sustained gains beyond 1 year [4, 8, 17, 18, 19]. Resuscitation Council UK ALS Provider Course Regulations [37] specify eligibility and recertification prerequisites but do not evaluate these as predictors of long‐term competence.

Institutional Factors

Institutional influences on ALS competence maintenance were mapped into six thematic clusters (Table 2). The narrative highlights recurring patterns across clusters, focussing on training access and resourcing, governance and standardisation, team structures and leadership roles and monitoring through assessment and feedback. Across the included empirical studies, institutional factors were mainly described as implementation enablers or constraints, rather than evaluated as predictors of longer‐term retention.

Several studies described how organisational arrangements shaped opportunities for reinforcement. Campbell and Clark [28] situated ALS reinforcement within a graduate nurse residency context, whereas Marler et al. [35] highlighted unit mandates and stakeholder support as contextual conditions for implementing ICU simulation programmes. Barile et al. [38] reported that a policy requiring quarterly RQI‐based refreshers during shifts was perceived as helpful for keeping skills current but was constrained by equipment and connectivity problems and by competing clinical demands, without objective competence measures. Mastoras et al. [33] described departmental funding, accreditation and leadership encouragement as supports for ongoing simulation‐based Continuing Professional Development (CPD).

Institutional culture and team design were also emphasised. Pallas et al. [32] described improved task clarity and reduced cognitive overload when a designated nurse leader role was assigned during simulated ALS, whereas Armstrong et al. [31] described hierarchical team culture as a barrier to shared leadership and highlighted the need for sustained interprofessional simulation and audit cycles to support leadership behaviours in practice. Li et al. [39] and Rolston et al. [40] described organisation‐level redesigns that formalised roles, workflows and review processes, including a standardised nurse‐led resuscitation team with video review in the emergency department. Across nursing‐focussed reviews, outdated protocols, inconsistent standards and system barriers to ongoing education (including staffing, funding and rural access constraints) were also described [41, 42].

Guidance and consensus documents largely described organisational prerequisites for ALS training and maintenance, including quality assurance processes, faculty support, defined roles and infrastructure for access to training resources but did not evaluate these features as determinants of ALS competence maintenance over time [4, 8, 17, 18, 19, 37].

Educational Factors

Educational influences on ALS competence maintenance were mapped into six thematic clusters (Table 3): simulation and scenario‐based training, instructional design and performance support; spaced repetition and LDHF models; gamification and digital strategies; multimodal and blended learning approaches; and curricular variation and standardisation gaps.

Across empirical studies, educational effects were predominantly assessed using simulation‐based metrics or self‐report, with follow‐up typically limited to weeks or a few months. Within course improvements in leadership ratings were reported after Crisis Resource Management (CRM) and TeamSTEPPS teaching embedded within repeated resuscitation scenarios and debriefing [43]. In a systematic review of advanced structured resuscitation courses, Au et al. [36] reported that simulation‐based approaches, scheduled refreshers and modified content delivery were the most consistently associated with improved medium‐term skills retention, whereas the isolated use of high‐fidelity manikins produced mixed findings, and no single optimal strategy could be identified.

Within primary studies, spaced reinforcement showed selective benefits and uneven durability. Campbell and Clark [28] reported that spaced simulation reinforcement improved some psychomotor measures, while written knowledge declined, and time to shock performance did not improve. Introducing task interruptions to increase realism in ALS simulation did not improve technical or non‐technical performance compared with standard simulation. In Truchot et al. [29], these interruptions included repetitive phone calls and interruptions from relatives or other clinicians, and no between‐group differences were observed, including at 3‐month retention. By contrast, Finstad et al. [30] reported improved non‐technical skills in clinical anaesthesia teams after in situ simulation‐based team training. Multicomponent programmes combined rehearsal with structured review and feedback. Rolston et al. [40] reported higher return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after implementation of an ED bundle combining mechanical chest compression device training, in situ simulation for standardised roles, and multidisciplinary video review with feedback. Marler et al. [35] reported short‐term knowledge gains, with scores at 3 months remaining above baseline following a multimodal programme.

Digital and gamified formats produced mixed short‐term findings and were rarely linked to maintenance trajectories. In a cross‐sectional evaluation of a gamified digital tool, Chen et al. [34] reported that perceived usefulness was associated with self‐assessed competence, without longitudinal retention outcomes. In a cluster randomised trial, Kim et al. [44] reported similar post‐test scores for basic content but lower scores for knowledge related to ALS algorithms in the gamification group. Barile et al. [38] described perceived benefits of quarterly LDHF RQI refreshers, alongside practical barriers, and without objective competence measures.

Evidence syntheses and grey literature converged on recurrent, structured practice with feedback, distribution of training over time and alignment with updated guidance, while also highlighting variability in retraining intervals and the need for standardised curricula and evaluation systems [4, 8, 17, 18, 19, 37, 41].

Integrated Strategies for Competency Maintenance

Integrated strategies were coded across the existing individual, institutional and educational domains rather than presented as a separate table. These initiatives combined simulation, feedback, debriefing or spaced refreshers with organisational mechanisms such as mandated participation, role definition, workflow integration, audit and video review. Overall, evidence remained heterogeneous and was often short term, self‐reported or not specific to sustained ALS competence maintenance. Reported barriers included competing clinical demands, infrastructure constraints and limited objective longitudinal measurement.

Discussion

The mapped evidence portrays competence maintenance as a multilevel phenomenon shaped by educational design, individual readiness and organisational infrastructure. Most empirical studies operationalised ‘maintenance’ through short interval simulation outcomes, written knowledge tests or self‐report, with limited evaluation of subsequent clinical performance or patient outcomes. Educational determinants dominated empirical evaluation, whereas institutional determinants were more often recommended than tested.

Evidence on maintenance trajectories remains limited because competence was often inferred from pragmatic proxies. Cross‐sectional surveys linked theoretical knowledge to training recency, resuscitation exposure and professional background, but could not establish durability [26]. Some studies also labelled self‐assessed outcomes as ‘competence’; in a gamified ALS based digital tool, perceived usefulness was most strongly associated with self‐assessed emergency care competence, suggesting that reported gains may partly reflect usability or engagement [34].

Durability is also difficult to infer from the intervention literature because improvements were uneven across competence domains and may decay rapidly, particularly for time‐dependent behaviours and algorithm integration. In an ED nurse residency programme, spaced simulation‐based assessments after ALS training were associated with improvement in most timed skill measures after repetition, whereas written test scores declined, and time to first shock did not improve [28]. Increasing operational realism through task interruptions did not improve technical or non‐technical performance compared with standard simulation, including at follow‐up [29]. Gamified learning may increase engagement, but objective learning gains were inconsistent. In a cluster randomised trial, overall post training knowledge did not differ between conventional and gamified learning, but knowledge related to ALS algorithms was lower in the gamified group [44]. At an aggregate level, Au et al. [36] reported substantial heterogeneity in intervention content, outcomes, and follow‐up across trials, with signals of advantage for interactive and structured refreshers over passive approaches but limited longer‐term measurement and inconsistent alignment across outcomes. Taken together, these data support deliberately targeting high‐consequence behaviours, selecting outcomes that extend beyond immediate post‐course competence, and avoiding an implicit ‘more is better’ logic when redesigning refresher formats [36].

Most evidence on team processes and leadership is simulation‐based and time‐limited. In the CANLEAD randomised simulation trial, introducing a designated nursing team leader was associated with lower cognitive load for the medical team leader and improvements in objective performance metrics [32]. Beyond resuscitation specific contexts, in situ simulation‐based team training in Norwegian anaesthesia teams was followed by higher Anaesthetists Non‐Technical Skills scores during clinical induction [30] However, evidence for sustained effects beyond short follow‐up remains limited. A randomised trial of brief mental skills training prior to simulated resuscitation assessment reported no significant differences in subjective or physiological stress measures, or performance outcome, despite high perceived relevance among participants [45].

System level determinants were repeatedly framed as foundational but seldom tested as intervention targets. Grey literature recommended governance, protected training time, standardised roles, audit and feedback and training resources, but these should be interpreted as recommendations rather than empirical demonstrations of effect [4, 8, 17, 18, 19, 37].

Where examined, implementation depended on alignment between educational design and operational realities. A pre‐ and post‐evaluation reported improved resuscitation process measures and ROSC after a team‐focussed, video‐reviewed CPR programme, although causal attribution was limited [40]. Programme evaluations described perceived value but persistent barriers and limited objective longitudinal outcomes [33, 38].

Limitations

Several limitations constrain inference. In most primary studies, maintenance was defined using short follow‐up simulation metrics, written tests or self‐report, with limited linkage to subsequent clinical performance or patient outcomes. Definitions and measures varied, and many studies were small, single centre evaluations with short follow‐up or uncontrolled before and after designs. Randomised trials were uncommon, and few studies examined scalability, resources, sustainability or cost effectiveness. Institutional determinants were frequently described as essential but rarely evaluated as the unit of intervention.

This scoping review was restricted to publications in English, Portuguese or Spanish from March 2019 to March 2025 and searched MEDLINE Ultimate, CINAHL Ultimate and the Cochrane Library; relevant education or implementation literature indexed elsewhere may have been missed. Most sources came from high‐income settings, limiting transferability to resource‐constrained contexts. Because no critical appraisal was undertaken, findings should be interpreted as descriptive mapping rather than evidence of effectiveness.

Implications for Practice and Further Research

Implications to Clinical Practice

The mapped evidence supports treating ALS competence maintenance as an ongoing workplace capability rather than episodic recertification. Critical care programmes should embed recurrent rehearsal with feedback, prioritise high consequence actions and reinforce role clarity, leadership and closed loop communication. Psychological readiness may be included within broader programmes but should not be presented as a proven standalone mechanism. Organisational support remains essential: protected time, governance, standardised roles, audit with feedback and accessible training infrastructure are necessary conditions for feasible, measurable rehearsal in routine care.

Future Research Directions

Future research should evaluate multicomponent maintenance programmes that align educational design, individual readiness and institutional infrastructure. Studies should use explicit definitions, validated context specific outcomes, longer follow‐up and measures of clinical transfer. Mixed methods, implementation and economic evaluations are needed to examine feasibility, reach, fidelity, sustainability, resource implications and team‐level competence over time.

Conclusion

This scoping review mapped 25 sources on factors influencing maintenance of ALS competence in hospital‐based critical care. Evidence was heterogeneous and clustered across educational, individual and institutional domains. Most studies used short follow‐up simulation metrics, knowledge tests or self‐report, with limited assessment of clinical transfer or patient outcomes. Educational factors dominated the evidence (simulation refreshers, structured reassessment and LDHF practice) and may improve selected process or performance measures, but durability remains uncertain. Team role clarity and distributed leadership appear relevant, although evidence is mainly simulation‐based. Organisational enablers, including protected time, governance, standardised roles and audit with feedback, are widely recommended but seldom tested.

Future research should use explicit definitions, validated longitudinal outcomes and multilevel implementation designs.
