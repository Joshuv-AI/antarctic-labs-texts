Real-world operational characteristics of perovskite photovoltaics
Authors: Xingtao Wang, Chi Zhang, Congbo Shi, Chuanke Chen, Lichuang Wang, Zizhen Lin, Chao Dong, Dongming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved remarkable progress in power conversion efficiency, approaching 27% for single-junction device. As this photovoltaic technology advances toward industrial implementation, operational characteristics are becoming increasing critical. Although considerable efforts have been devoted to studying stability under laboratory conditions, the operational characteristics of PSCs in real-world differ markedly and remain insufficiently explored. Specifically, the power output of perovskite modules under outdoor conditions is strongly influenced by dynamic weather parameters, yet their behavior in these uncontrolled environments is far less understood compared to controlled laboratory evaluations. This review highlights the phenomena unique to PSCs, including seasonal efficiency variations and diurnal cycling, which distinguish them from convention PV technologies. We first discuss the operational behavior of PSCs observed in controlled laboratory test, with emphasis on light exposure, temperature cycling and bias to elucidate the underlying mechanism. Then, we summarize the works on long-term outdoor behavior of both single junction PSCs and perovskite/silicon tandem photovoltaics. Finally, we provide perspective on bridging the gap between fundamental mechanism and real-world applications.Keywords: Perovskite solar cells, Outdoor behavior, Operational characteristics

Introduction

Over the past decade, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved remarkable progress in power conversion efficiency (PCE), with laboratory PCE approaching 27% under standard test conditions (STC, 25 ℃, 1000 W m⁻2, AM 1.5G) [1–4]. As perovskite photovoltaic (PV) technology advances from laboratory research toward commercialization, realistic energy yield has become as critical as laboratory efficiency [5, 6]. A clear understanding of the operational characteristics of perovskite PVs under outdoor conditions is therefore essential for their industrialization.

Indoor experiments provide valuable insights into the behavior of PSCs under specific and isolated conditions (e.g., controlled light soaking, temperature cycling) [7–9]. These laboratory studies elucidate fundamental mechanisms such as the influence of illumination on trap states, lattice dynamics, and metastability, or the temperature dependence of carrier transport and recombination [10–13]. In practice, the power output characteristics of PV modules under outdoor conditions strongly depend on actual weather conditions, which couples multiple factors including light intensity, spectral variation, temperature fluctuations, and environmental stressors such as humidity and soiling [14]. These dynamic outdoor conditions are fundamentally different from the static STC environment. Complementarily, outdoor testing reveals emergent behaviors—such as seasonal efficiency variations, diurnal cycling phenomena, and current-matching challenges in perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells (TSCs)—that only appear under realistic operating conditions [15–17]. Unlike conventional PV technologies, PSCs exhibit unique outdoor signatures such as reversible light-induced metastability, temperature-dependent degradation pathways, and spectrum- or bandgap-related current mismatch in tandem architectures. Capturing and understanding these real-world operational characteristics is essential to bridge the gap between laboratory efficiency records and reliable field deployment.

Stability has been identified as a key obstacle toward PSC commercialization. To date, outdoor studies of perovskite PVs mainly focus on stability [18–20]. However, practical operation behavior of PSCs such as variations in PV parameters under light exposure, temperature cycling and revere bias is equally important, as they directly influence the realistic energy yield. In this review, we highlight the phenomena unique to PSCs, including seasonal efficiency variations and diurnal cycling, which distinguish them from conventional PV technologies. We first discuss the operational behavior of PSCs observed in controlled laboratory test, focusing on light exposure, temperature cycling and bias to elucidate the underlying mechanism. Then, we summarize the works on long-term outdoor behavior of both single junction PSCs and perovskite/silicon TSCs. Finally, we provide perspective on bridging the gap between fundamental mechanism and real-world applications.

Operational insights from laboratory stress testing

Perovskite PVs are exposed to multiple environmental stressors during outdoor operation, including light, temperature, electrical bias and other factors, which complicates the understanding of failure mechanisms [21, 22]. Laboratory tests under controlled conditions provide mechanistic insights into how these individual factors affect device performance and stability, offering indispensable guidance for interpreting real-world operational behavior.

Characteristics under light exposure

Light is one of the most critical environmental factors governing the performance and stability of PSCs. Under outdoor environment, lighting conditions are inherently dynamic and can vary across multiple timescales. For example, the solar spectrum itself is not constant. It shifts with atmospheric composition, humidity, and air mass, as well as solar elevation angle that changes throughout the day and across seasons. Relative contribution of ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared photons differs between morning, noon, and evening, and this spectral evolution has a direct impact on light absorption and carrier generation in PSCs. In addition to spectral variations, the intensity of incident light is highly variable. It fluctuates between day and night, across different seasons, and depending on weather conditions such as cloudy, sunny. Therefore, understanding how PSCs respond to realistic light environments is essential. Laboratory simulations that replicate diurnal and seasonal light variations provide valuable mechanistic insights into how devices adapt or degrade under fluctuating illumination, ultimately guiding strategies for improving long-term stability and outdoor reliability.

Light intensity-dependent PV parameters

The performance parameters of PSCs exhibit complex response patterns to changes in illumination intensity. At low light intensity, the PCE of PSCs decreased less than that of the silicon heterojunction (SHJ). However, for light intensities in the 0.5–1.0 Sun range, in which most electricity is produced, this difference is not crucial. The current at the MPP scaled linearly with light intensity (Fig. 1a–d) [23]. The log–log linearity of the Jsc indicates that the trap-assisted recombination dominates under short-circuit (SC) conditions. Moreover, the recombination mechanism of PSCs dependents on light intensity: bulk Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination predominates under low light conditions, while interface recombination becomes dominant at high light conditions [24]. Light intensity regulates the Voc performance of PSCs by affecting the rate of carrier generation, interface recombination, and band bending. Under high light intensity, the improvement of quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) directly drive the Voc increase, but this enhancement is limited by interface defects and non-radiative recombination [25]. When the light intensity exceeds 1 sun, the Voc of PSCs initially rises, then approaches saturation, and in some devices may even begin to decline (Fig. 1e) [25, 26]. The fill factor (FF) is sensitive to light-intensity-dependent changes in defect states and ohmic losses [27]. At high light intensity, FF is primarily reduced by series resistance, while at low light it is affected by the shunt resistance (Fig. 1f, g). Trap-assisted recombination degrades FF across the entire intensity range, with interface recombination exerting a particularly impact under high light intensity [28].

Light-Soaking Effect (LSE)

The LSE refers to the phenomenon in which the PV parameters of PSCs fluctuate over time under continuous illumination. The underlying mechanisms are multifaceted, involving photo-induced ion migration [29, 30], lattice expansion [31], charge carrier accumulation, and trap defect passivation [32].

Under continuous illumination, majority-carrier accumulation at selective contacts forming interfacial space-charge regions with exceptionally large capacitances, a phenomenon directly linked to hysteresis and capacitive behavior to PSCs [33]. Specifically, ion/vacancy migration toward the TiO₂/perovskite interface under light induces positive charge accumulation and photoinduced upward band bending (Fig. 2a), generating electrostatic potentials that enhance photovoltage at low intensities but promote interfacial recombination at higher intensities (Fig. 2b) [34]. In particular, under open-circuit (OC) condition, PSCs exhibit the “drop-in-dark” effect after the light source turning off, manifesting as further performance degradation rather than recovery [35].

The extent of light-induced ion migration is composition-dependent. MA-based perovskites exhibit pronounced light-enhanced ion migration, whereas Cs incorporation stabilizes the ionic landscape and suppresses halide segregation, enabling faster photovoltage stabilization and improved operational robustness. For example, CsPbI₂Br exhibits a constant ion migration barrier (~ 0.45 eV) under varying light intensities, in contrast to MAPbI₃, where light reduces the barrier dramatically from 0.62 eV to 0.07 eV (Fig. 2c, d) [36, 37]. Similarly, CsFAMA triple-cation absorbers suppress non-radiative recombination more effectively and display superior photoluminescence responses under illumination compared to double-cation counterparts, highlighting the composition-dependent nature of LSE in PSCs [38].

At the defect scale, illumination promotes transitions between shallow and deep defect configurations, which modulate carrier lifetimes and account for the bidirectional nature of the LSE behavior (Fig. 2e, f) [39]. The light-activated, meta-stable deep-level trap states within the perovskite layer can be reversible through rapid self-healing in dark, highlighting the strong coupling between illumination and defect energetics (Fig. 2g, h) [40]. Importantly, reducing surface defect density not only improves baseline performance but also enhances the beneficial side of LSE, reflected in higher photoluminescence intensity and extended carrier lifetimes under illumination [41].

Continuous illumination induces uniform lattice expansion that reduces strain and lowers energetic barriers at interfaces, thereby enhancing carrier extraction and boosting efficiency without compromising long-term stability [31]. However, some works reported that this lattice expansion is mainly caused by the radiative heating [42]. In MAPbI₃, this beneficial evolution is further coupled with thermally driven phase transitions, where balanced electron and hole mobilities emerge around 310–330 K, coinciding with peak performance [43]. A-site cation plays a key role in photoinduced strain and lattice expansion. Among four cations of MA, FA, Cs, and PEA studied, FA is different from the other three cations, where FA-based FAPbI3 does not show lattice expansion upon illumination while the other three cations exhibit considerable lattice expansion. Hydrogen-bonding interactions and ionization energy of the A-site cation may be potential reasons responsible for the light-induced lattice strain in MHPs [44].

Illumination-induced stability issues

Device stability has attracted growing attention in recent years, as photodecomposition of PSCs represents a key challenge. Illumination-induced instabilities are associated with ionic redistribution and defect evolution, which over extended timescales cause performance fluctuations that reflect daily and seasonal variations in outdoor operation [45].

Illumination could trigger reversible changes, such as photo-induced halide segregation, and irreversible degradation, due to material decomposition. For example, MAPbI3 decomposes into PbI2, MA+, HI, and I2 gases [46, 47]. The release of volatile species damage other layers, such as metal electrode. All-inorganic halide perovskite also suffers from light-driven degradation. CsPbI3 readily transitions from its black photoactive phase to a yellow non-perovskite phase under illumination and thermal stress, accompanied by a significant loss in photoluminescence [48–50]. In hybrid halide perovskites, halide segregation under light exposure further accelerates performance deterioration and reduced stability [51].

Photo-induced degradation exhibits wavelength dependence. High-energy photons (300–500 nm) induce severe instability, while long-wavelength light induces milder degradation [52, 53]. UV-B irradiation (310–317 nm) is particularly detrimental, causing more than 50% efficiency loss after prolonged exposure, whereas UV-A photons (360–380 nm) exert minimal damage and may even slightly enhance performance [54]. This light-induced degradation is directly linked to both decomposition of the perovskite absorber into PbI2 and the high susceptibility of the electron-transfer layer (ETL) to photochemical reactions [55]. Moreover, UV irradiation can also deteriorate encapsulation materials, further compromising device reliability [56].

Light additionally acts synergistically with environmental stressors such as humidity. Simultaneous exposure to illumination and moisture accelerates perovskite decomposition, generates sub-bandgap states, and promotes grain fragmentation, leading to enhanced nonradiative recombination and irreversible performance decline [57, 58]. These degradation pathways emphasize the importance of evaluating illumination not as an isolated stressor but in conjunction with other environmental conditions.

Temperature effects on PSCs

Temperature plays a considerable role on optoelectronic properties and stability of PSCs. While moderate thermal tuning could enhance charge transport and device performance, both cryogenic and elevated temperatures induce performance losses through trap activation, ion migration, and structural degradation. In practical operation, PV devices are continuously exposed to temperature fluctuations, making it essential to understand the behavior of PSCs under temperature thermal cycling. In this section, we discuss the influence of temperature cycling on device performance.

Temperature-dependent device performance

Charge transport in PSCs shows dependence on temperature, which is associated with trap dynamics, phase transitions, and ionic motion. Carrier lifetimes increase markedly at low temperatures, particularly under high light intensity (Fig. 3a). Charge extraction efficiency declines in planar devices upon MAPbI₃ transition from a tetragonal to an orthorhombic phase, highlighting temperature-dependent trapping/detrapping mechanisms [59]. Temperature-dependent studies reveal that MAPbI3 devices show enhanced performance at lower temperature (~ 220 K), which can be explained by the enhanced interfacial charge transfer, reduced charge trapping with suppressed nonradiative recombination and narrowed optical bandgap at lower temperature [60]. MAPbI₃ devices exhibit dramatic changes and decreased in a reversible manner for temperatures both higher and lower than room temperature. From temperature up to 80 °C and down to − 80 °C, PCE losses of up to 25%, which can be attributed to a temperature induced limitation in the transport and extraction of the photo generated charge carriers [61]. Comparative analysis of photocurrent and absorption spectra reveals distinct mechanisms at different temperatures: exciton formation dominates at low temperatures (> 9.1 meV binding energy), mixed phases cause discrepancies near the orthorhombic-tetragonal transition, and enhanced longer-wavelength photons collection occurs at elevated temperatures, potentially due to optical cavity effects [62]. Investigations at 175 K further demonstrated that current–voltage hysteresis persists even in “hysteresis-free” devices, with relaxation times increasing from less than 1 s at room temperature to over 15 s at low temperature, reflecting slowed ionic equilibration rather than the elimination of underlying processes [63].

For mixed-cation and MA-free systems, negative temperature coefficients of power output have been observed, dominated by thermally activated recombination associated with defects and lattice strain, with an average value of − 0.44%/°C up to 85 °C [64]. Perovskite PVs exhibit exceptionally low temperature coefficients of PCE, in some cases as low as − 0.08 rel %/°C at 80 °C, outperforming Si, GaAs, and CIGS technologies (Fig. 3b). This unique behavior originates from the anomalous bandgap widening with temperature (Fig. 3c), which reduces Jsc but partially offsets losses by improving Voc. As a result, perovskite modules are projected to retain 95–97% of the energy yield of silicon modules across diverse climatic conditions, despite intrinsic stability challenges [65]. In tandem architectures, the temperature coefficient of Jsc is dependent on current matching and spectral conditions, switching between positive and negative depending on which subcell is limiting [66]. Temperature-dependent optical modeling has further provided predictive tools for device optimization, enabling accurate reproduction of measured Jsc and EQE across diverse perovskite compositions [67].

Thermal stability and operational reliability

Beyond transient charge transport, temperature also influences on the long-term stability of PSCs [68]. Thermodynamically, the cubic to tetragonal phase transition at lower temperature facilitates the elimination of intrinsic point defects in perovskite films, thereby extending the carrier lifetime and enhancing the Voc in devices (Fig. 3d–f) [69]. Interestingly, device performance is recoverable after a limited amount of temperature cycles, and the PCE even improves after a single low-temperature cycle [69, 70]. This behavior can be contributed to that perovskite film experiences an increasing strain as the temperature is lowered below and above room temperature. When passing through room temperature, the strain is almost entirely released regardless of the cooling or heating history of the sample (Fig. 3g, h) [65]. However, prolonged cycling eventually leads to irreversible structural and gradual PCE decline.

Operando studies revealed that light soaking induces reversible halide demixing, whereas thermal stress at 65 °C drives irreversible phase segregation and amorphization [71]. Under elevated reverse bias, thermal stress could also generate localized heating, hot-spot, and electrode degradation via defect-induced shunts and metal ion migration, posing significant risks under shading or mismatch conditions [72]. Moreover, temperature cycling between 10 and 60 °C has been shown reported to cause severe but reversible Jsc losses (> 80%) due to ion accumulation at selective contacts, a failure mechanism absent under constant thermal exposure but highly relevant to outdoor operation [73].

Influence of electrical bias on the perovskite PVs

In real outdoor environments, partial shading or module mismatch often introduces reverse bias on individual sub-cells, critically impacting both the short-term power output and long-term stability of PSCs. To understand the outdoor operational characteristics of PSCs, it is essential to investigate their behaviors and degradation mechanisms under electrical bias.

Bias-dependent device behaviors

Bias stress influences a wide range of abnormal behaviors and degradation pathways in PSCs, from reversible hysteresis and photodegradation to catastrophic reverse bias (RB) breakdown under shading. The origin of these bias-dependent phenomena lies in ion migration and interfacial electrochemical reactions [74–76]. EBIC mapping and capacitance measurements reveal that voltage biasing enhances charge extraction primarily through ion redistribution, with migration pathways mediated by grain boundaries and extended defects, leading to transient capacitance buildup and hysteresis [77, 78]. For example, NiOx-based devices show abnormal hysteresis specifically when I–V scans start from RB, arising from ionic accumulation at the NiOx/perovskite interface, which induces tunnel junction formation and transient negative resistance [79]. ETL-free PSCs exhibit dynamic reverse bias (DRB) behavior with variable breakdown voltages, where Zener breakdown dominates below – 1 V, Zener–avalanche mechanisms appear between – 1 and – 4 V, and irreversible thermal breakdown occurs beyond – 4 V, all fundamentally driven by ion migration under bias [80]. More broadly, RB breakdown is typically governed by tunneling currents enabled by ion accumulation at interfaces, followed by slower electrochemical processes that increase breakdown voltage and series resistance over time [81]. Structural defects such as pinholes further amplify RB risks, creating PN-like diode shunts that trigger abrupt low-voltage breakdown (< – 2 V) and hot-spot formation due to localized current densities [82].

At the module and tandem levels, bias resilience diverges across architectures. Monolithic perovskite/silicon TSCs demonstrate exceptional RB tolerance, maintaining stability even under higher RB stress, as the Si subcell absorbs most of the reverse voltage and shields the perovskite top cell (Fig. 4a–d) [83, 84]. In contrast, perovskite-only modules are prone to severe RB degradation under partial shading due to low breakdown voltages (~ 2 V) and poor thermal conductivity, leading to localized heating and permanent failure [85, 86]. These observations highlight the importance of module design, including bypass diode integration and optimized thermal management, to ensure reliable outdoor performance.

Bias conditions during illumination further influence device behavior. Under OC conditions, PSCs undergo irreversible degradation manifested as the “drop-in-dark” effect due to ion accumulation at the ETL interface, whereas under short-circuit (SC) conditions, the devices show reversible recovery (Fig. 4e); applying a mild negative bias during recovery further accelerates performance restoration [87]. The degradation rate in the OC case is faster than that in the SC case, with slowest degradation rate under maximum power point (MPP) [53]. Similarly, under natural and concentrated sunlight, SC degradation is dominated by light intensity, while OC degradation depends on cumulative illumination dose, reflecting distinct defect-generation pathways [88]. Photodegradation is also bias-dependent: triple-cation PSCs degrade fastest at OC (77% PCE loss), slowest at SC (34%), and intermediately at maximum power point (57%), with high-energy photons accelerating decomposition into PbI₂ and trap formation [55]. Moreover, device degradation shows a significant correlation with applied current under reverse bias, as degradation rates and RB currents follow the same exponential dependence on normalized voltage, indicating that current-driven processes play a primary role in failure even at relatively low biases [89].

Bias stress additionally drives electrochemical halide oxidation, where ion-induced band bending enables hole tunneling into the perovskite bulk, producing neutral halogen species that diffuse to contacts and form deep recombination centers (Fig. 4f) [90]. Grain boundaries play a dual role in bias-induced dynamics. While they enhance charge transport at forward bias due to favorable band bending, they act as preferred sites for ion accumulation and decomposition under low bias, contributing to instability [91]. RB also initiates iodide oxidation at the cathode, causing Cu electrode corrosion and filament formation. Reinforced barriers such as LiF/SnO₂/ITO effectively suppress these reactions and dramatically extend RB lifetimes [92]. Similarly, interfacial electrochemistry at positive-bias interfaces drives electrode diffusion and oxidation, with Au-induced degradation in n–i–p devices and Ag-induced reactions in p–i–n architectures, underscoring the importance of stabilizing these interfaces [93, 94].

Operational characteristic under real-world

Evaluating operational characteristic under real conditions is essential before advancing perovskite PV toward commercialization. Different from laboratory tests, outdoor operation exposes perovskite PVs to simultaneous and dynamic variations in spectrum, irradiance, temperature, and bias from partial shading. The coupling of these factors governs both degradation pathways and power generation, making outdoor studies indispensable for evaluating device reliability and bridging the gap between laboratory predictions and real-world performance. Whereas existing reports on outdoor testing of perovskite photovoltaics are very limited compared to the extensive data available for mature crystalline silicon PV, and most have been conducted on lab-scale devices. Nevertheless, these limited outdoor test results remain critically important for understanding the actual power generation behavior of perovskite solar cells under real-world conditions.

Seasonal outdoor performance of perovskite PVs

Seasonal variation is a distinctive feature of PSCs under real-world outdoor operation, arising from the combined effects of temperature fluctuations, spectral shifts, and intrinsic device metastability. Unlike controlled indoor tests, outdoor environments impose dynamic day–night and seasonal cycles that couple reversible and irreversible processes, producing performance behaviors not captured in laboratory stability protocols. Gaining a comprehensive understanding of these phenomena requires integrating empirical seasonal performance data with mechanistic insights into their driving factors.

PSCs display a distinctive seasonal dependence in outdoor environments, with power output and stability influenced by ambient climate conditions. Unlike conventional PVs, they may show performance decline in hot seasons or, in some cases, improvements under intense summer irradiation, reflecting the interplay between external stressors and the intrinsic metastability of the perovskite absorber. One-Year outdoor monitoring in a Berlin climate showed that PSCs retained nearly 100% of their initial performance through winter and spring but declined markedly during the hotter summer months, indicating a dominant role of device temperature in accelerating degradation under warm conditions (Fig. 5a, b) [95]. Multi-season studies in a hot–arid climate revealed that lifetime indicators (T80/T50) are climate-dependent with systematically shorter in summer–fall than in cooler seasons. Diurnal analysis demonstrating that daytime maximum temperatures drive irreversible losses, while nighttime minima govern reversible recovery and initial fast degradation (Fig. 5c, d) [96]. Complementary outdoor and indoor light cycling stability tests indicated that ambient temperatures could have a potentially greater effect than the irradiance on the PSC’s lifetime and degradation, with daytime maximum temperatures playing a major role in long-term degradation and nighttime minimum temperatures potentially influencing reversible and early-stage degradation processes.

Khenkin et al. tracked MPPT output of devices on a rooftop test field in Berlin for ~ 2.5 years, revealing distinctive seasonal behavior in PSCs. Unlike silicon and other low bandgap PV, the PSC show a summer improvement. This behavior may be attributed to the intrinsic metastable characteristics of PSCs (Fig. 6a) [16]. The contradictions between reports of summer decline and summer improvement may be contributed to the intrinsic stability of PSCs, which could have been enhanced by advances in fabrication processes. In a subsequent four-year outdoor study in Berlin of encapsulated p-i-n PSCs, the group reported pronounced seasonal performance variations, with winter performance during the first year up to 30% lower than in summer, and this performance gap increased with aging (Fig. 6b). They identified four main factors as drivers of this seasonality: (I) solar spectrum, (II) device temperature, (III) MPPT losses, and (IV) metastability effects. Solar spectrum changes in summer and winter could result in a 10% difference in generated current at the same irradiance levels (Fig. 6c, d). Temperature affects the observed PSCs in different ways depending on the state of the device, with initially negative temperature coefficients becoming positive in degraded devices. MPPT losses are linked to the magnitude of J-V hysteresis, which increased significantly in aged devices and low operating temperatures. Metastability in PSCs, particularly light-soaking dynamics, suppressed winter performance in cold, low light winter conditions (Fig. 6e, f) [97].

Diurnal outdoor performance of perovskite PVs

Daily light–dark cycling introduces additional complexity to the outdoor behavior of PSCs, which cannot be captured in static indoor stability protocols. Unlike relatively stable output of conventional PV technologies, PSCs exhibit pronounced diurnal fluctuations, reflecting the interplay between illumination intensity, temperature variations, and intrinsic metastability. These fluctuations manifest not only as reversible recovery processes between night and day but also as asymmetric morning–afternoon behaviors under equivalent irradiance, underscoring the unique coupling between ionic and electronic transport in PSCs under realistic conditions.

Outdoor monitoring consistently reveals that PSCs degrade during dark periods and recover under illumination, producing a characteristic morning recovery effect. Sara Pescetelli et al. installed the solar farm in Heraklion, Crete and studied their outdoor PV performance. The total ISC increased almost linearly with the irradiance level, whereas VOC data indicate a logarithmic dependence. Differently, the solar farm FF was almost unaffected by the irradiance. The PV performance during the morning is higher than during the afternoon even under same irradiance mainly contribute to the improvement of Voc (Fig. 7a–d). They attributed this characteristic to the higher temperature of panel in the afternoon compared with the morning [98]. Two-year outdoor tracking in Berlin and Ljubljana revealed that both single-junction and tandem PSCs undergo overnight degradation followed by substantial recovery from morning to noon or evening (Fig. 7e), This phenomenon can be attributed to the light-soaking effect of PSCs, which depends not only on the daily irradiation but also on the device temperature [17]. Similarly, two-year monitoring of perovskite mini-modules in Nicosia, Cyprus revealed daytime performance degradation and overnight recovery with statistical analysis showing declining diurnal current and PCE during the day but recovery at overnight. Conversely, the voltage and fill factor tended to improve during the day but decreased overnight [99]. Importantly, the role of light intensity is also critical—under low irradiance, ionic transport dominates the device response, while under high irradiance, electronic transport takes over, minimizing ionic contributions. [100] Collectively, these diurnal trends point to a fundamental departure from conventional PV behavior, where power output is expected to be symmetric with respect to irradiance and time of day.

Outdoor performance of perovskite/silicon TSCs

The outdoor behavior of perovskite/silicon TSCs exhibit distinctive features compared with their single-junction counterparts, as their performance is jointly governed by the interplay between the perovskite and silicon sub-cells. One critical factor is current matching, which depends on temperature, spectrum, and cell architecture. Research results show that the temperature dependence of the two absorbers follows opposing trends: while the bandgap of crystalline silicon narrows with increasing temperature, that of perovskites widens (Fig. 8a). This mismatch shifts tandem devices away from the optimal current matching point under realistic hot outdoor conditions, suggesting that perovskites with narrower bandgaps (< 1.68 eV at 25 °C) are more favorable for tandems deployed in sunny, high-temperature climates [101].

Architectural innovations such as bifacial tandems further highlight the importance of bandgap selection. Michele De Bastiani et al. studied the effect of bifaciality to perovskite/silicon TSCs. Benefiting from albedo-driven current enhancement in the silicon bottom cell, the bandgap of perovskite used in top cells is narrower than monofacial TSCs. A five days outdoor test in Jeddah and Karlsruhe shows that the bifacial tandem benefit from an additional reduction of perovskite bandgap performed significantly better than the monofacial one (Fig. 8b), particularly during midday when the light intensity was close to 100 mW cm–2 or 80 mW cm–2. And this enhancement is more pronounced on sunny days [102]. They further studied the diurnal and long-term evolution of bifacial perovskite/silicon TSCs over a six months period outdoors in Saudi Arabia with a hot and humid outdoor conditions. The results demonstrated that the FF is the primary performance-limiting parameter (Fig. 8c). Daily FF loss was found to be largely reversible, driven by ion migration within the perovskite that recovers overnight, whereas irreversible FF degradation originated from corrosion of silver contacts, which progressively reduced the maximum attainable efficiency (Fig. 8d, e) [103].

Extended outdoor evaluation has also clarified the broader environmental stressors influencing tandem stability. A year-long study in the Red Sea region identified three primary factors affecting long-term current matching and energy yield: module temperature, solar spectral variations, and surface soiling. High module temperatures shift the balance of sub-cell currents, while spectral shifts alter the effective photocurrent distribution between the perovskite and silicon layers. Meanwhile, dust accumulation introduces additional current mismatches by preferentially attenuating short-wavelength light, further complicating the outdoor stability landscape [104].

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the outdoor performance of perovskite/silicon TSCs cannot be reliably predicted from standard indoor testing alone. Temperature-dependent bandgap shifts, spectral variability, bifacial operation, and environmental degradation pathways interact to define both the short-term efficiency and the long-term stability of TSCs. These insights highlight the necessity of outdoor-specific optimization strategies, including bandgap engineering, contact stabilization, and anti-soiling measures, for achieving reliable deployment of perovskite/silicon TSCs in diverse climates.

Outlook

As perovskite PVs advance rapidly toward industrialization, understanding and managing their outdoor operational behavior becomes increasingly critical. Unlike laboratory conditions, outdoor environments expose devices to multiple stress factors that drive complex degradation pathways. To ensure reliable large-scale deployment, future research must bridge the gap between fundamental mechanism studies, outdoor demonstration power station, and the development of dedicated reliability standards tailored to perovskite technology.

Demonstration power station under representative climatic conditions

Establishing outdoor demonstration facilities in regions characterized by strong UV irradiation, elevated humidity and heat, or extreme cold is essential. Leveraging recent advances in large-area perovskite module fabrication, these sites will generate empirical data on performance and degradation under diverse stress environments. Complementing these outdoor investigations, accelerated aging tests are performed in simulated indoor environments to rapidly evaluate module performance under extreme yet reproducible conditions. By comparing the power generation dynamics and degradation kinetics from both natural and accelerated aging, a performance comparison model could be established to correlate indoor acceleration factors with outdoor long-term outcomes, thereby quantifying the equivalence between accelerated and real-time aging. Based on the integrated dataset, the power generation behavior of modules in specific climates can be identified, along with climate-dependent failure mechanisms. Such studies are indispensable for elucidating climate-specific degradation mechanisms and for guiding the design of materials, device architectures, and encapsulation strategies optimized for targeted deployment scenarios.

Long-term outdoor monitoring databases

Developing large-scale, geographically distributed outdoor monitoring platforms is essential to enable statistically robust evaluation of perovskite device and module stability. Through the continuous, long-term collection of performance metrics, environmental conditions, and degradation signatures, the platform can establish comprehensive databases that support accurate lifetime prediction. Comparative analyses across diverse climatic zones will further help distinguish universal degradation mechanisms from site-specific stress responses. Achieving this vision requires close collaboration between academia and industry. Academic institutions can lead fundamental research on degradation mechanisms using high-precision characterization tools, while industrial partners contribute scalable module fabrication, real-world deployment experience, and data from diverse climatic zones. By integrating field data with academic modeling expertise, the community can effectively bridge the gap between accelerated laboratory testing and real-world performance. This synergy will ultimately yield crucial insights for evaluating the global deployment potential of perovskite PV technologies.

Refinement of testing protocols and standards

Insights from outdoor demonstration projects should be fed back into laboratory testing to improve the relevance of accelerated stress protocols. Current International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) standards (e.g., IEC 61215), designed for mature and stable technologies like silicon photovoltaics, fall short in capturing the metastable nature of PSCs. Unlike covalently bonded silicon, perovskites are ionic crystals that exhibit metastable behavior under light and heat. In real world outdoor environments, continuously varying light intensity, spectral distribution, and temperature-humidity conditions collectively affect both the photoelectric output and long-term stability of perovskite devices. However, existing test standards are largely based on constant or simplified stress conditions, which do fail to represent dynamic environments. Therefore, it is necessary to establish stability assessment protocols that more accurately reflect realistic service conditions.

For example, solar irradiance varies continuously over the course of a day, whereas current steady-state tests cannot capture the dynamic performance evolution and degradation pathways under fluctuating illumination. Developing standardized stability test protocols incorporating time-dependent irradiance profiles is therefore essential to reliably evaluating device evolution under realistic conditions. In addition, the organic components in perovskite materials are particularly sensitive to short-wavelength photons, particularly in the UV and blue-violet light regions. In high UV radiation environment, such spectral effect can significantly accelerate material degradation. Accordingly, it is essential to formulate stability testing standards should incorporate controlled spectra conditions, including high-intensity UV exposure, to assess wavelength-dependent degradation and spectral stability.

Moreover, IEC protocols employ single or sequential stress application methods, which does not account for the synergistic effects arising from simultaneously applied stressors in real-world environments. To bridge this gap, it is imperative to develop accelerated aging protocols that explicitly consider coupled multiple stresses. Representative scenarios include UV‑humidity‑thermal stress, thermal cycling‑mechanical load, and coupled temperature‑irradiance fluctuation. Systematically investigation of degradation patterns under these combined stresses will provide a more rigorous and physically grounded basis for evaluating the long‑term reliability of perovskite photovoltaics in practical outdoor applications.
