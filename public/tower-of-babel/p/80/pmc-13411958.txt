Subtype-specific HER3 enrichment in basal-like breast cancer is regulated via the GATA2/GATA3–FOXA1 axis
Authors: Congcong Tan, Hui Lyu, Sanbao Ruan, Yakun Wu, Margaret E Larsen, Shou-Ching Tang, Bolin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBasal-like breast cancer (BLBC) is a major subtype of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), characterized by aggressive behavior, limited treatment options, and poor prognosis. While HER3 overexpression is frequently observed in TNBC and associated with poor outcomes, its subtype-specific expression and therapeutic potential remain unclear. Here, we demonstrated that HER3 signaling is selectively hyperactivated in BLBC compared to claudin-low breast cancer (CLBC) using transcriptomic profiling. Histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi), Romidepsin and Panobinostat, exerted potent antitumor effects on BLBC by selectively downregulating HER3 expression. HER3 levels were positively correlated with FOXA1, a key transcriptional activator. Mechanistically, we identified GATA2 and GATA3 as upstream regulators of both FOXA1 and HER3. HDACi disrupted the GATA2/GATA3-FOXA1-HER3 axis by suppressing GATA2 and GATA3 expressions. Specific knockdown of FOXA1, GATA2, or GATA3 enhanced HDACi-induced apoptosis and growth inhibition, while ectopic expression of any of these transcription factors restored HER3 levels and mitigated the effects of HDACi. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays confirmed direct binding of GATA2 and GATA3 to the FOXA1 promoter. In BLBC xenograft models, GATA2 overexpression markedly attenuated the antitumor activity of Panobinostat and substantially reversed its effects on FOXA1 and HER3 expression, tumor growth, and apoptosis. Clinically, high expression of GATA2, GATA3, FOXA1, or HER3 is significantly associated with poor outcomes in BLBC patients. Collectively, our studies established a previously unrecognized GATA2/GATA3-FOXA1-HER3 axis as a key regulatory network in BLBC progression. We provide strong experimental data supporting that the two HDACi, Romidepsin and Panobinostat may be repurposed as effective therapeutic agents for BLBC.Keywords: HDACi, HER3, FOXA1, GATA2, GATA3, BLBC, CLBC

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a heterogeneous and aggressive subtype of breast cancer [1], which is further stratified into distinct molecular subtypes, including basal-like breast cancer (BLBC) and claudin-low breast cancer (CLBC), each with unique molecular profiles and clinical behaviors [2]. BLBC is characterized by the expression of genes typical of basal or myoepithelial cells of the mammary gland. BLBC constitutes approximately 15–20 % of all breast cancers and is frequently, though not exclusively, classified within TNBC [3]. BLBC is associated with high proliferative rates, aggressive clinical behavior, and poor prognostic outcomes, underscoring the critical need for innovative and effective therapeutic strategies tailored to this challenging subtype [4]. Our previous research revealed that the histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor (HDACi) Panobinostat effectively induced growth inhibition and apoptosis in TNBC cells [5]. However, in CLBC, Panobinostat treatment upregulated receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), such as human epidermal growth factor receptor 3 (HER3), contributing to adaptive resistance. Combining Panobinostat with gefitinib to target HER3-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling significantly enhanced its efficacy in CLBC cells [5]. We previously reported that HER3 was expressed in approximately 50 % of TNBC tissues and that elevated HER3 levels were significantly associated with poor clinical outcomes in TNBC patients. Specific knockdown of HER3 expression markedly inhibited TNBC cell proliferation in vitro and repressed tumor growth in vivo [6], suggesting that HER3 represents a promising therapeutic target in HER3-high TNBC. In our earlier analyses, HER3 expression appeared relatively low in CLBC cell lines and elevated in BLBC cell lines [6]. However, a systematic comparison of HER3 expression between these TNBC subtypes has not yet been conducted. Moreover, whether the anti-tumor activity of HDACi involves modulation of HER3 expression in HER3-high BLBC and the underlying mechanisms governing this regulation remain unresolved.

Forkhead box protein A1 (FOXA1), a pioneer transcription factor, plays a critical role in regulating hormone-responsive genes in breast cancer [7], particularly in the luminal subtype, where it facilitates chromatin remodeling for estrogen receptor (ER) binding and transcriptional regulation, driving tumor growth and progression [8,9]. Dysregulation of FOXA1 has been implicated in multiple oncogenic signaling [9]. FOXA1 overexpression suppresses interferon signaling and immune response in androgen receptor (AR) positive prostate cancer and ER positive luminal breast cancer [10]. Recent evidence has also identified FOXA1 as a regulator of HER3 expression in ER-positive MCF7 cells [11]. Although traditionally considered low in TNBC [12], FOXA1’s expression and role in TNBC, especially in BLBC, has not been systematically studied. Additionally, the pioneer transcription factors GATA Binding Protein 2 and 3 (GATA2 and GATA3), members of the GATA family, are crucial in regulating physiological and pathological processes [13], with gene or transcriptional abnormalities closely linked to the development and progression of various cancers, particularly breast cancer [14]. GATA2 has been recognized as a key oncogenic biomarker in breast cancer, promoting cell proliferation, invasion, migration, angiogenesis, and resistance to endocrine and chemotherapy [15–18]. GATA3 is a critical regulator of luminal cell differentiation in the mammary glands and is strongly associated with luminal breast carcinomas [13]. It interacts with ER and FOXA1 to form a chromatin remodeling complex at enhancer regions, promoting chromatin relaxation and epigenetic modifications to drive transcription [19–21]. Interestingly, the prognostic significance of GATA3 in breast cancer remains controversial [22]. Previous studies showed that GATA3 promotes ERα expression and maintains a differentiated phenotype, which is linked to a favorable prognosis [20,21], especially in ER + breast cancer patients [14,23]. Conversely, elevated GATA3 is observed in invasive subtypes [24] and correlates with poor prognosis in triple-positive breast cancer (TPBC) [25]. Moreover, GATA3 enhances doxorubicin resistance by inhibiting CYB5R2-mediated ferroptosis [26]. Notably, GATA3 expression is also frequently observed in TNBC, with Byrne et al. reporting 48 % of 98 primary TNBC tissues, 21 % of which showed high levels [27]. Another study found strong GATA3 positivity in 73.3 % of 50 TNBC specimens [28]. However, a systematic analysis of the roles of GATA2 and GATA3 in different subtypes of TNBC and their contributions to therapeutic resistance has yet to be conducted.

In this study, we analyzed public transcriptomic data to compare gene expression and perform functional enrichment between BLBC and CLBC subtypes of TNBC. We further investigated the therapeutic potential of HDACi, Romidepsin and Panobinostat, in BLBC via potent inhibition of HER3. Through clinical and experimental evidence, we elucidated the underlying mechanisms of HDACi-mediated selective downregulation of HER3 and identified a key regulatory axis. These findings highlight the potential of targeting this axis to enhance the efficacy of HDACi, providing a foundation for therapeutic repurposing of HDACi against BLBC.

Material and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Romidepsin and Panobinostat were from Selleck Chemicals, Inc. (Houston, TX), and were diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for cell culture experiments. Antibodies used for Western blot assays were from the following: HER3 (12708), p-HER3 (Y1286), EGFR (4267), IGF- 1R (3027), FOXA1 (53528), DNMT1 (5032), DNMT3A (32578), DNMT3B (57868), GATA2 (79802), GATA3 (5852), PARP (46D11), caspase-8 (9502), caspase-3 (8G10) (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Danvers, MA); β-actin mouse mAb (clone AC-75) (Sigma-Aldrich, Inc., St. Louis, MO). Antibodies used for immunohistochemistry (IHC) assays were from the following: HER3 (12708), GATA2 (79802), GATA3 (5852), Ki67 (9027), C-caspase3 (9661) (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.) and FOXA1 (ab173287, abcom). Antibodies used for ChIP assays were from: GATA2 (79802), GATA3 (5852), rabbit IgG (2729) (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.). All other reagents were from Sigma-Aldrich, Inc. (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless otherwise specified.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human breast cancer cell lines (HCC1143, BT20, MDA-MB-468, HCC70, HCC1806, HCC1937, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-157, BT549, SUM159PT, HCC1395, and Hs578T) and the non-tumorigenic breast epithelial cell line MCF-10A were from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA). MCF-10A was cultured in DMEM/F-12 (1:1) medium (Sigma) supplemented with 5 % Horse Serum (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 20 ng/mL Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF) (Sigma), 10 μg/mL Insulin (Sigma), 0.5 mg/mL Hydrocortisone (Sigma), 100 ng/mL Cholera Toxin (Sigma). Other cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 (1:1) medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma) and cultured in a 37 °C humidified atmosphere containing 95 % air and 5 % CO2. Cell line authentication was performed via short tandem repeat (STR) DNA profiling between 2016 and 2018. Mycoplasma testing was conducted biannually using the MycoAlert™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), and all cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and purified with the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), and mRNA levels of FOXA1, HER3, GATA2, and GATA3 were normalized to GAPDH using the ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Protein extraction and Western blot assay

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Protein lysates were quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein were separated via SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with primary antibodies as described previously [5]. Signals were visualized using ECL detection reagent (GE Healthcare).

Lentiviral-mediated knockdown and overexpression assay

Knockdowns of FOXA1, GATA2, and GATA3 were performed using two independent shRNAs for each gene delivered via a lentiviral vector system. All lentiviruses shRNAs were purchased from Sigma and validated for specificity and efficiency. The lentivirus production in HEK293T cells and transduction of targeted cells were carried out as described previously [5]. For overexpression studies, cells were transfected with specific FOXA1 (#153109), GATA2 (#144474), or GATA3 (#141984) cDNA expression vectors (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA), or a control vector, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). The efficiency of knockdown or overexpression was validated by qPCR for mRNA levels and Western blot analysis for protein expression.

Flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis

Apoptosis was evaluated using the Muse™ Annexin V & Dead Cell Assay (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA) and the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) following the manufacturers’ protocols. Flow cytometry analysis was performed on the Guava® Muse® Cell Analyzer (Luminex) and the BD FACSCanto II cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were quantified using the respective instrument software and analyzed with GraphPad Prism.

Live/dead cell staining

Live/Dead cell staining was performed using the Live/Dead Imaging Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and as previously described [6]. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated with the kit’s reagents at 37 °C for 15 min. Stained cells were visualized using the EVOS FLoid Cell Imaging Station (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The quantification was performed by analyzing three randomly selected fields per group.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using MTS assays with the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Kit (Promega, Madison, WI) as previously described [5]. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with the appropriate HDAC inhibitors, while control cells received DMSO. Absorbance at 570 nm was measured 72 h post-treatment.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500–1000 cells per well and treated with Panobinostat or Romidepsin. After 10–14 days of incubation, colonies were fixed with methanol for 20 min and stained with 0.5 % crystal violet for 15 min at room temperature. Colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

Protein binding to gene promoters was analyzed using the Simple ChIP® Plus Kit (Magnetic Bead) (Cell Signaling Technology). Chromatin proteins were crosslinked to DNA, and cell lysates were sonicated to fragment DNA to sizes ranging from 150 to 900 base pairs. Anti-GATA2 (#79802, CST), anti-GATA3 (#5852, CST), and a control rabbit antibody (#2729, CST) were used to immunoprecipitate DNA-protein complexes. Real-time PCR was performed using primers targeting the core promoter regions of FOXA1. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Tumor xenograft model

Athymic nu/nu mice (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA, USA) were maintained under IACUC guidelines. HCC1806 cells (5 × 106), transduced with control or GATA2-overexpressing vectors, were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of 6–8-week-old female nude mice. When tumors reached ~80 mm3, mice were randomized into groups and treated with Panobinostat (5 mg/kg) or vehicle (DMSO) via intraperitoneal injection, every other day for 4 weeks. Drug formulations were prepared using the MedChemExpress in vivo formulation calculator. Tumor volumes were calculated as: Volume = (Length × Width2)/2. Body weights were monitored thrice weekly. At the endpoint, tumors were harvested, imaged, and weighed.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and staining quantification

IHC analyses of a TNBC tissue microarray (TMA) (BR1301, US Biomax, Inc., Rockville, MD) and the tumors obtained from animal experiments were conducted as previously described [29]. In brief, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against GATA2 (1:100), FOXA1 (1:100), HER3 (1:200), Ki67 (1:400), and cleaved Caspase-3 (1:400). Staining was visualized using a DAB substrate kit, counterstained with hematoxylin, and imaged under a bright-field microscope. For TMA samples, IHC staining was evaluated manually using a standardized semi-quantitative scoring system as follows: Score 0: no staining; Score 1+: weak nuclear staining in <10 % of invasive tumor cells; Score 2+: moderate nuclear staining in >10 % and <30 % of invasive tumor cells; Score 3+: strong nuclear staining in >30 % of invasive tumor cells. All TMA slides were independently reviewed and scored by two investigators. For the xenograft tumor sections, we performed digital quantification using ImageJ software with the IHC Profiler plugin. Three randomly selected fields per group were analyzed to calculate the mean staining intensity of each target protein [5].

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was assessed using two-way ANOVA for group comparisons or Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0.0). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

HER3 signaling is specifically enriched in BLBC cells and selectively suppressed by HDACi

We previously reported that HER3 was expressed in approximately half of TNBC tissues and that elevated HER3 levels were significantly associated with poor prognosis in TNBC patients [6]. However, its expression pattern across TNBC subtypes remains unclear. To address this, we performed transcriptomic profiling and functional enrichment analysis in BLBC and CLBC cell lines. Cell lines were classified into BLBC and CLBC subtypes based on transcriptomic data from public datasets, using hierarchical clustering with established subtype-specific gene signatures, as described in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and related studies [30–32]. Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed clear separation between BLBC and CLBC samples, reflecting distinct gene expression signatures (Fig. 1A). Volcano plot analysis further identified 741 significantly up- and downregulated genes between the two subtypes, with ERBB3 (HER3) prominently upregulated in BLBC cells (Fig. 1B). To gain functional insights, we conducted kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis. The ErbB signaling pathway emerged as one of the top pathways enriched in BLBC (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Consistent with this, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis highlighted significant overrepresentation of molecular functions including protein tyrosine kinase activity, kinase activity, and receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) activity (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 1B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further confirmed the significant enrichment of the KEGG_ERBB_SIGNALING_PATHWAY in BLBC compared to CLBC (Fig. 1E), supporting the activation of ErbB-driven oncogenic signaling in BLBC. We next assessed HER3 mRNA expression in TNBC subtypes among our cell lines. qRT-PCR analysis revealed significantly higher HER3 transcript levels in BLBC compared to CLBC cell lines (Fig. 1F), further supporting HER3 as both a potential subtype-specific biomarker and a therapeutic target in BLBC.

Although several HER3-targeted therapies are under development, only one bispecific antibody has received FDA approval—for the treatment of non-small-cell lung and pancreatic cancers harboring neuregulin 1 (NRG1) fusions [33]. In contrast, multiple HDACi have been approved for hematologic malignancies, and their potential in solid tumors, including breast cancer, is actively being explored [34]. Our previous study revealed that HDACi induced HER3 expression as an adaptive response in CLBC cells [5]. However, it remained unclear whether HDACi also affect HER3 expression in BLBC. To assess the therapeutic relevance of HDACi and HER3 signaling in BLBC, we first evaluated the effects of Romidepsin (Romi) and Panobinostat (Pano) on cell viability. MTS assays were performed in BLBC cell lines and compared to the normal-like breast epithelial cell line MCF-10A. Treatment with Romidepsin or Panobinostat significantly reduced the viability of BLBC cell lines (MDA-MB-468, HCC1806) in a dose-dependent manner, whereas the non-tumorigenic mammary epithelial cell line MCF-10A remained relatively resistant (Fig. 1G). These results suggested that HDACi selectively targeted cancerous cells while sparing normal breast epithelial cells, underscoring their therapeutic potential for BLBC. We then examined whether HDACi modulate HER3 expression in BLBC cells. Treatment with Romidepsin or Panobinostat significantly downregulated HER3 mRNA levels in MDA-MB-468, HCC1806, and HCC70 cells compared to untreated controls (Fig. 1H). Western blot analysis confirmed a dose-dependent decrease in HER3 protein, while the levels of other major RTKs, including EGFR and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R), remained unchanged (Fig. 1J). These results demonstrate that HDACi selectively suppress HER3 expression in BLBC cells. Collectively, these findings indicate that HER3 signaling is hyperactivated in BLBC and can be selectively downregulated by HDACi, supporting the therapeutic potential of repurposing HDACi for HER3-positive BLBC.

The expression of FOXA1 is highly enriched in BLBC cells and positively correlated with HER3 expression in TNBC cell lines and specimens

To explore the underlying mechanism by which HDACi altered HER3 expression in BLBC cells, we first examined HER3 promoter methylation by comparing the two TNBC subtypes: BLBC and CLBC cell lines. Methylation analysis of the human breast cancer cell lines DNA methylation dataset (GSE78875) revealed no significant differences between the subtypes (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B), suggesting that the differences in HER3 expression between BLBC and CLBC cells are not primarily driven by promoter methylation.

We next explored the transcriptional mechanisms underlying HDACi-mediated regulation of HER3. Our previous data showed that Panobinostat induced both HER3 and FOXA1 expression in MDA-MB-231 cells (data not shown). Additionally, volcano plot analysis revealed that FOXA1 mRNA expression was significantly higher in BLBC compared to CLBC cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S2C). These findings prompt us to hypothesize that FOXA1 may transcriptionally regulate HER3 in TNBC. To investigate the relationship between FOXA1 and HER3, we examined their expression across breast cancer subtypes. Analysis of gene expression data from the DepMap portal revealed strong co-expression of FOXA1 and HER3 across breast cancer cell lines, with notable enrichment in BLBC compared to CLBC subtypes (Fig. 2A). Pearson’s correlation analysis confirmed a remarkable positive correlation between FOXA1 and HER3 mRNA levels in 47 breast cancer cell lines (R = 0.8848, Fig. 2B) and 21 TNBC cell lines (R = 0.7489, Fig. 2C). This positive correlation was further validated at the protein level in 21 breast cancer (R = 0.8127, Fig. 2D and E) and 10 TNBC cell lines (R = 0.6701, Fig. 2F).

We then assessed FOXA1 expression in our TNBC cell line panel. Consistent with the higher expression of HER3 observed in BLBC cells (Fig. 1B), FOXA1 mRNA expression was also highly enriched in BLBC cells compared to CLBC cells (Fig. 2G). Protein expression analysis further confirmed strong co-expression of FOXA1 and HER3 in BLBC cell lines, while both proteins were low or undetectable in CLBC and MCF-10A cells (Fig. 2H). Moreover, bioinformatic analysis of clinical datasets revealed a positive correlation between FOXA1 and HER3 mRNA expression in both TNBC (R = 0.42, Fig. 2I) and BLBC patients (R = 0.43, Fig. 2J). To further validate FOXA1 protein expression in TNBC tissues, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis using a tissue microarray (TMA) of TNBC specimens (n = 125). Elevated expression of FOXA1 (++/+++) was observed in approximately half of the samples, which agreed with our findings of HER3 expression in the TNBC specimens [5]. Representative IHC images demonstrated a progressive increase in FOXA1 staining corresponding to HER3 levels (categorized as negative (HER3−), low (HER3+), medium (HER3++), and high (HER3+++), Fig. 2K), with a statistically significant positive correlation (Fig. 2L). Taking together, these findings indicated that the expression of FOXA1 was highly enriched in BLBC and positively correlated with HER3 expression in BLBC, supporting a potential regulatory role for FOXA1 in control of HER3 expression in BLBC.

HDACi downregulate HER3 and induce BLBC cell growth inhibition and apoptosis via suppression of FOXA1 expression

Given the positive correlation between FOXA1 and HER3 expression in BLBC cells (Fig. 2), we next investigated whether HDACi inhibited HER3 expression and induced BLBC cell death via downregulation of FOXA1. Treatment of BLBC cell lines (MDA-MB-468, HCC1806, and HCC70) with Romidepsin or Panobinostat significantly reduced FOXA1 mRNA and protein levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3A and B; Supplementary Fig. S3A and S3B). To assess the functional relationship between FOXA1 and HER3, we performed FOXA1 knockdown experiments using two independent shRNAs (shFOXA1#1 and shFOXA1#2). Specific knockdown of FOXA1 significantly reduced HER3 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3C and D), recapitulating the suppressive effects of HDACi on HER3. These data strongly support that FOXA1 positively regulates HER3 expression in BLBC cells. Conversely, the ectopic expression of FOXA1 restored HER3 protein expression, even in the presence of Panobinostat treatment, demonstrating that inhibition of FOXA1 expression was crucial for HDACi-mediated downregulation of HER3 in BLBC cells (Fig. 3E).

To assess the role of FOXA1 and HER3 in HDACi-induced apoptosis, flow cytometry analysis was performed in MDA-MB-468 cells treated with Romidepsin or Panobinostat. Both HDACi significantly induced apoptosis (early and late), as shown by Annexin V/PI staining (Fig. 3F). Ectopic expression of FOXA1 markedly attenuated these apoptotic effects and mitigated the HDACi-mediated reduction in cell viability (Fig. 3G) and clonogenicity (Fig. 3H). Consistent with our previous report [35], Panobinostat induced PARP cleavage and activated Caspase-9 and −3, while concurrently reducing HER3 and FOXA1 protein levels. Notably, FOXA1 overexpression partially rescued HER3 downregulation and suppressed PARP cleavage and caspase activation (Fig. 3I, Supplementary Fig. S3C). Enhanced exogenous FOXA1 expression upon HDACi treatment was likely due to activation of the CMV promoter in the expression vector, a known effect of HDACi [36]. To further validate the role of FOXA1 in mediating HDACi-induced HER3 downregulation and apoptosis, we then examined the effects of FOXA1 knockdown on HDACi response in BLBC cells (Supplementary Fig. S3). Specific knockdown of FOXA1 enhanced Romidepsin- and Panobinostat-induced apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S3D), reduction of cell viability (Supplementary Fig. S3E), and inhibition of colony formation (Supplementary Fig. S3F). FOXA1 knockdown also augmented HDACi-induced HER3 downregulation, PARP cleavage, and activation of Caspase-3 (Supplementary Fig. S3G). Collectively, our data demonstrated that HDACi suppressed HER3 expression and potently induced BLBC cell growth inhibition and apoptosis via downregulation of FOXA1.

GATA2 and GATA3 are highly co-expressed with FOXA1 and HER3 in BLBC and elevated expression of any of these four genes is significantly associated with poor prognosis in BLBC patients

To define the mechanism by which HDACi regulate the FOXA1-HER3 signaling axis, we first analyzed the FOXA1 promoter methylation levels in BLBC and CLBC cell lines using the human methylation dataset (GSE78875). FOXA1 promoter methylation levels were significantly higher in CLBC compared to BLBC cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B), suggesting that reduced FOXA1 expression observed in CLBC may result from promoter hypermethylation. In mammals, DNA methylation patterns are established and maintained by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), primarily DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B. DNMT1 preserves existing methylation during DNA replication, whereas DNMT3A and DNMT3B are responsible for de novo methylation during development [37]. Dysregulation of these enzymes contributes to tumorigenesis [38]. Interestingly, treatment with Panobinostat decreased DNMT1 and DNMT3A protein levels while increasing DNMT3B expression in CLBC cells, indicating a potential role for HDACi in reversing FOXA1 promoter hypermethylation via DNMT modulation. In contrast, Panobinostat had minimal effect on DNMT (DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B) expression in BLBC cells, even at higher doses, suggesting that HDACi-mediated FOXA1 regulation in BLBC might not involve direct regulation of FOXA1 promoter methylation (Supplementary Fig. S4C).

We next explored potential transcription factors that might mediate HDACi-induced reduction of FOXA1. Using The Signaling Pathways Project (SPP), a bioinformatics platform integrating transcriptomic and epigenomic data to analyze signaling pathways and identify therapeutic targets, ChIP-seq analysis revealed several transcriptional modulators—GATA2, GATA3, CTCF, BRD3, CEBPB, SRF, and MYC—with multiple binding sites in the FOXA1 promoter. We then examined the expression patterns of these factors alongside FOXA1 and HER3 using curated breast cancer omics data. Among them, GATA2 and GATA3 exhibited expression patterns closely resembling those of FOXA1 and HER3 across breast cancer subtypes (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Pearson’s correlation analysis confirmed strong positive correlations between GATA2 or GATA3 and both FOXA1 and HER3 in TNBC and particularly in BLBC tissues (Supplementary Fig. S5B–D; Fig. 4A), with correlation coefficients of R = 0.61 (FOXA1-GATA2) and R = 0.74 (FOXA1-GATA3) in BLBC. These results suggested that GATA2 and GATA3 might serve as key transcriptional regulators of FOXA1 and HER3 in BLBC cells.

To validate the biological relevance of our findings, we analyzed RNA-seq data from the DepMap portal to assess the mRNA expression patterns of FOXA1, HER3, GATA2, and GATA3 in breast cancer cell lines. Heatmap analysis revealed co-expression of GATA2 and GATA3 with FOXA1 and HER3 across breast cancer subtypes, with notably higher expression in BLBC compared to CLBC (Fig. 4B). Quantification of elevated mRNA levels of all four genes were confirmed in BLBC compared with CLBC cells (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S5E). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further demonstrated that high expression of FOXA1, HER3, or GATA2 was significantly associated with poor relapse-free survival (RFS) and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) in BLBC patients (Fig. 4D and E). Although GATA3 expression data were unavailable for BLBC, high GATA3 expression in TNBC patients correlated with worse overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) (Supplementary Fig. S5F and G). These results underscore the prognostic significance of FOXA1, HER3, GATA2, and GATA3 in BLBC. Their co-expression, along with the presence of GATA2/3 binding sites in the FOXA1 promoter, supports the role of GATA2 and GATA3 as key transcriptional regulators of the FOXA1-HER3 signaling axis and highlights their potential as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in BLBC.

HDACi downregulates FOXA1 and HER3 expression via suppression of GATA2 or GATA3-driven FOXA1 transcription in BLBC cells

To determine whether GATA2/3 are involved in HDACi-mediated downregulation of the FOXA1-HER3 signaling axis in BLBC cells, we first examined the effects of HDACi on the expression of FOXA1, HER3, GATA2, and GATA3. Treatment with Panobinostat or Romidepsin significantly reduced both mRNA and protein levels of FOXA1, HER3, and GATA2 in HCC1806 and HCC70 cells, or GATA3 in MDA-MB-468 and HCC70 cells (Fig. 5A and B; Supplementary Fig. S6A and B), suggesting that HDACi might inhibit FOXA1-HER3 expression via suppression of GATA2 and/or GATA3. To directly examine their regulatory roles, we performed targeted knockdown of GATA2 or GATA3 in BLBC cells. Silencing either factor led to a marked decrease in FOXA1 and HER3 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5C and D; Supplementary Fig. S6C), indicating that GATA2 and GATA3 are essential for maintaining FOXA1-HER3 signaling in BLBC.

Bioinformatic analysis identified eight putative GATA2/GATA3 binding sites (P1–P8) within the FOXA1 promoter region (−2000 bp to the transcription start site (TSS)), based on data from the Signaling Pathways Project (SPP) (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. S7C). Collectively, our data demonstrated that GATA2 and GATA3 transcriptionally activated FOXA1 expression in BLBC cells, thereby promoting HER3 expression. HDACi disrupted this regulatory axis by suppressing GATA2 and GATA3, providing a mechanistic basis for the control of FOXA1 and HER3 expression in BLBC. These findings reinforced the role of GATA2/GATA3 as key transcriptional regulators of FOXA1-HER3 signaling axis and potential therapeutic targets in BLBC.

Specific knockdown of GATA2 or GATA3 enhances HDACi-induced apoptosis and growth inhibition in BLBC cells

Next, we examined whether specific knockdown of GATA2 or GATA3 expression would enhance HDACi-induced anti-proliferative/anti-survival effects on BLBC cells. Treatment with Panobinostat or Romidepsin increased total apoptotic rates compared to DMSO-treated controls. Specific knockdown of GATA3 in MDA-MB-468 cells or GATA2 in HCC1806 cells further amplified HDACi-induced apoptosis (Fig. 6A). Similar results were observed in HCC70 cells, where knockdown of GATA2 or GATA3 markedly augmented apoptosis induced by Panobinostat or Romidepsin (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Fig. S7). Western blot analysis confirmed that HDACi suppressed FOXA1 and HER3 expression and increased PARP and Caspase-3 cleavage in MDA-MB-468 and HCC1806 cells. Silencing of GATA2 or GATA3 further reduced FOXA1 and HER3 levels and enhanced apoptotic marker activation, indicating additive pro-apoptotic effects (Fig. 6C). Cell viability and colony formation assays demonstrated that GATA2 or GATA3 depletion sensitized BLBC cells to HDACi, resulting in significantly reduced cell growth and clonogenic capacity (Fig. 6D and E). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that HDACi targeted the FOXA1-HER3 axis to induce apoptosis and inhibited BLBC cell growth by suppressing GATA2 and/or GATA3 expression. Depletion of GATA2 or GATA3 further enhanced HDACi-mediated anti-proliferative/anti-survival effects, highlighting a potential therapeutic strategy for BLBC.

Ectopic expression of GATA2 or GATA3 not only rescues FOXA1 and HER3 expression, but also attenuates HDACi-induced apoptosis and growth inhibition in BLBC cells

To determine whether suppression of GATA2 and/or GATA3 expression plays an essential role in HDACi-mediated inhibitory effects on BLBC cells, we performed rescue assays via ectopic expression of GATA2 or GATA3 in BLBC cells. Overexpression of GATA2 in HCC1806 cells or GATA3 in MDA-MB-468 cells significantly upregulated FOXA1 and HER3 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 7A). Importantly, overexpression of GATA3 or GATA2 effectively restored FOXA1 and HER3 mRNA and protein expression suppressed by HDACi treatment (Fig. 7B–H; Supplementary Fig. S8A). Flow cytometry and live/dead imaging assays revealed that overexpression of GATA2 or GATA3 significantly reduced HDACi-induced apoptotic cell death in BLBC cells (Fig. 7C–E; Supplementary Fig. S8B). Additionally, ectopic expression of GATA2 or GATA3 mitigated HDACi-mediated inhibitory effects on BLBC cell growth and colony formation (Fig. 7F and G; Supplementary Fig. S8C and D). Western blot analyses confirmed that overexpression of GATA2 or GATA3 clearly diminished HDACi-induced apoptosis evidenced by reduction of PARP cleavage and cleaved-Caspase-3 (Fig. 7H). Collectively, our data demonstrated that overexpression of GATA2 or GATA3 effectively rescued the expression of both FOXA1 and HER3. It also significantly attenuated HDACi-induced anti-proliferative/anti-survival effects on BLBC cells. These results highlighted the critical role of GATA2/GATA3 in modulating the therapeutic efficacy of HDACi in BLBC.

Overexpression of GATA2 markedly compromises the anti-tumor activity of panobinostat against BLBC and significantly abrogates panobinostat-induced downregulation of FOXA1 and HER3 in vivo

To determine the impact of GATA2 overexpression on Panobinostat-mediated anti-tumor activity in vivo, we established an orthotopic xenograft model with HCC1806 cells transfected with either control (Ctrl) or GATA2 cDNA expression vectors. Tumor-bearing mice were treated with DMSO or Panobinostat (5 mg/kg, i.p., every other day for 28 days). Panobinostat significantly inhibited tumor growth in the control group, whereas GATA2 overexpression markedly attenuated this effect, resulting in significantly larger tumor volumes in the Panobinostat-treated GATA2 group (Fig. 8A and B). Tumor images and weights measured at the endpoint further corroborated these findings, i.e. while Panobinostat potently decreased tumor size and weights in the control group, the HDACi had no significant effects on the tumor size and weights in the GATA2-overexpressing group (Fig. 8C). Mouse body weights remained stable across all groups, indicating no overt toxicity (Fig. 8D). IHC analysis revealed that GATA2 overexpression increased FOXA1 and HER3 expression compared to controls. In tumors with control vector, Panobinostat reduced FOXA1, HER3, and Ki67, and increased cleaved Caspase-3 expression—consistent with reduced proliferation and enhanced apoptosis. However, these effects were markedly diminished in GATA2-overexpressing tumors (Fig. 8E). Quantitative IHC analysis further confirmed sustained high levels of FOXA1, HER3, and Ki67, and reduced cleaved Caspase-3 in GATA2-overexpressing tumors, regardless of Panobinostat treatment.

Discussion

Both BLBC and CLBC are aggressive subtypes. They form the majority of TNBC and exhibit distinct characteristics and gene expression profiles. Clinically, their unique molecular features lead to different treatment responses [30,39,40]. Notably, the high heterogeneity of BLBC contributes to its highly proliferative and aggressive phenotype, resulting in a poor prognosis [39]. There is an urgent need to identify novel targets and develop tailored treatment strategies for each TNBC subtype, especially BLBC. Recent studies, including ours have demonstrated HER3 as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target in TNBC [5,6,41,42]. We observed an elevated expression of HER3 in approximately half of TNBC specimens and determined HER3 as an effective therapeutic target to enhance the antitumor activity of gefitinib and paclitaxel in TNBC [5]. Our observation is consistent with an earlier report showing positive expression of HER3 in 41 % of hormone receptor-negative breast cancer [41]. Here, we sought to examine the expression of HER3 across TNBC subtypes and found it especially enriched in BLBC with low expression or absent in CLBC, indicating HER3 as a potential subtype-specific marker for BLBC. Our findings align with a recent study demonstrating that the expression and dynamic upregulation of HER3 predominate in BLBC, contributing to drug resistance and microenvironment-induced adaptations [43].

Despite the emerging importance of HER3 in tumorigenesis and cancer therapy, no drug targeting HER3 had been approved until very recently [33]. This has spurred extensive efforts to develop diverse therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting HER3. Our previous data showed that the pan-HDACi Panobinostat effectively induced growth inhibition and apoptosis in TNBC cells. Interestingly, this effect was accompanied by the upregulation of HER3 in CLBC cells, causing adaptive resistance [5]. In the present study, we further investigated the therapeutic potential of two HDACi, Romidepsin and Panobinostat, against BLBC. We discovered that the HDACi selectively inhibited HER3 expression while exerting minimal effects on other RTKs in BLBC cells. In addition to their well-established role in histone modification [44], HDACi have been recognized for their ability to reverse DNA methylation and reactivate silenced genes [45]. However, analysis of HER3 promoter methylation in BLBC and CLBC subtypes showed no significant differences, suggesting that the differential expression of HER3 between these TNBC subtypes is driven by the mechanisms independent HER3 promoter methylation. Recent studies reveal a significantly positive correlation between FOXA1 and HER3 expression in invasive breast cancer tissue microarrays (n = 144) [46]. FOXA1 mediates adaptive resistance to HER2-targeted therapy by regulating HER3 in HER2-positive breast cancer [47]. Additionally, junctional adhesion molecule-A (JAM-A) has been shown to regulate HER3 expression in breast cancer via β-catenin and FOXA1 in MCF7 luminal cells [46]. Furthermore, putative binding sites of FOXA1 were identified in the promoter region of HER3 (data not shown). Thus, we hypothesized that FOXA1 might also regulate HER3 expression in the HER3-enriched BLBC cells. In support of this hypothesis, we found co-expression of FOXA1 and HER3 across all breast cancer cell lines. In fact, the expression pattern of FOXA1 closely mirrored that of HER3 in TNBC-highly enriched in BLBC but low/undetectable in CLBC. Analysis of the TNBC TMA (n = 125) also showed a strong positive correlation between FOXA1 and HER3. Moreover, both Romidepsin and Panobinostat inhibited HER3 expression by suppressing FOXA1. Studies with gain-of-function and loss-of-function approaches confirmed that FOXA1 played a crucial role in mediating the HDACi’s anti-tumor activity through downregulation of HER3 expression. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to demonstrate the regulatory interplay between FOXA1 and HER3 in BLBC. From a prognosis perspective, FOXA1 may have a diverse role in breast cancer depending upon the status of hormone receptors. In luminal breast cancers, elevated expression of FOXA1 contributes to a better prognosis, whereas FOXA1’s expression implies an adverse outcome in TNBC patients [48]. Mangia et al. [49] reported poorer disease-free survival in AR+/FOXA1+ TNBC, and another study of 333 non-metastatic TNBCs found that the AR+/FOXA1+ phenotype was associated with shorter recurrence-free and overall survival [50]. Adverse outcome in patients with FOXA1-positive TNBC could be attributed to their reduced chemosensitivity. Overexpression of FOXA1 has been shown to lead to an increase in drug resistance and anchorage independence in MDA-MB-231 cell model [51]. Consistently, our data showed that high expression of both FOXA1 and HER3 linked to a worse clinical outcome (RFS and DMFS) in BLBC patients. These findings provide new insights into the role of FOXA1 in TNBC and suggest that targeting the FOXA1-HER3 axis may be a promising therapeutic strategy for BLBC.

To determine the mechanism underlying HDACi-mediated FOXA1 downregulation, we first examined FOXA1 promoter methylation in BLBC and CLBC cell lines. CLBC cells exhibited significantly higher methylation levels than BLBC cells. Although HDACi have been reported to reverse CpG methylation by regulating DNMT1 expression [52], treatment of BLBC cells with panobinostat had little effect on DNMT1 expression. Screening of potential transcriptional regulators of FOXA1 based on ChIP-seq data from SPP curated ’omics database revealed a significant positive correlation between GATA2, GATA3, FOXA1, and HER3, especially in BLBC specimens. Intriguingly, a similar expression pattern of both GATA2 and GATA3 was also observed in TNBC cell lines. We then performed ChIP assays and confirmed that GATA2 and GATA3 directly regulated FOXA1 transcription. Our data indicate that both GATA2 and GATA3 function as oncogenic transcription factors to drive FOXA1 and HER3 expression, thereby promoting tumor growth in BLBC. These findings are in agreement with recent studies showing that GATA2 overexpression in MDA-MB-231 cells enhances cell proliferation and migration [16], and the oncogenic role of GATA3 is supported by its overexpression in HCC1806 (BLBC) cells promoting doxorubicin resistance [26]. Consistent with the prognostic role of GATA2 in other cancers [53,54], we found that increased expression of GATA2 was associated with poor RFS and DMFS in BLBC patients. However, the prognostic significance of GATA3 in breast cancer remains controversial [22,52,55]. While earlier studies reported that elevated expression of GATA3 predicted better survival in breast cancer patients, recent findings suggest a contrasting role. It has been shown that high expression of GATA3 is associated with poor prognosis, reduced immune infiltration, and increased PIK3CA and TP53 mutations in triple-positive breast cancer (TPBC) patients [25]. Similarly, our findings revealed that the elevated expression of GATA3 correlated with poor OS and PFS in TNBC; however, our analysis was limited by the absence of BLBC-specific data. This discrepancy may stem from the heterogeneity of breast cancer, as different subtypes likely exhibit distinct GATA3-mediated regulatory mechanisms. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the context-dependent role of GATA3 in BLBC progression and patient outcomes.

As pioneer transcription factors, FOXA1, GATA2, and GATA3 have been reported to form transcriptional complexes with various cofactors, especially hormone receptors and other pioneer factors [10,16,21]. GATA3 can assemble a chromatin remodeling complex with ER and FOXA1 to regulate target gene transcription [21]. Notably, GATA2 not only inhibits AR-induced PTEN transcription in AR-positive breast cancer cells but also directly represses PTEN transcription in AR-negative cells [16]. Given these interactions, it would be interesting to determine whether FOXA1, GATA2, and GATA3 form a cooperative regulatory network in control of HER3 expression in BLBC. Moreover, as glucocorticoid receptor (GR) is commonly expressed in TNBC cells, exploring potential crosstalk between those pioneer factors and GR represents another intriguing avenue for future investigations.

HDACi are known to exert broad biological effects by modulating both histone and non-histone protein acetylation, influencing transcription, chromatin architecture, and signaling pathways [56–58]. We found that Romidepsin and Panobinostat downregulated GATA2, GATA3, and HER3 expression in BLBC cells, consistent with their reported role in altering transcription factor function [59,60]. Mechanistically, HDAC3 has been shown to repress GATA2 transcription through direct interaction [61], while HDAC1/2 inhibition leads to GATA3 acetylation, impairing its DNA-binding capacity and transcriptional activity [62]. Moreover, HDACi can indirectly suppress transcription via disruption of key oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK [56]. These effects reflect the dual ability of HDACi to both activate and repress gene expression in a context-dependent manner [57,58], supporting our observed suppression of the GATA2/GATA3-FOXA1-HER3 axis in BLBC models.

While our findings demonstrate consistent transcriptional repression of GATA2, GATA3, and HER3 both in vitro and in vivo, the precise molecular targets of Romidepsin and Panobinostat in this axis remain to be defined. We acknowledge that future studies, such as ChIP-seq and global acetylome profiling, are necessary to determine whether these transcription factors are direct HDACi targets in BLBC [58]. Furthermore, HDACi-induced transcriptomic changes may extend beyond the GATA-HER3 pathway, potentially engaging immune, inflammatory, or stress-response networks [59]. This has important therapeutic implications for BLBC, a clinically aggressive triple-negative subtype with limited targeted options beyond chemotherapy [63,64]. HER3 is increasingly recognized as a key driver of resistance via PI3K/AKT signaling and is now being targeted by antibody-drug conjugates and bispecific antibodies in clinical trials, including a HER2/HER3 bispecific antibody approved for NRG1 fusion-positive tumors [33]. Our findings provide mechanistic rationale to explore HDACi as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or HER3-targeted agents to enhance efficacy and overcome resistance in HER3-expressing BLBC.

In summary, our findings identify HER3 as a potential subtype-specific marker for BLBC and establish a mechanistic basis for the therapeutic potential of HDACi in BLBC via selective suppression of HER3. These results also provide a strong rationale for combinatorial strategies targeting GATA2/GATA3-FOXA1 axis to enhance the efficacy of HDACi. Future studies should investigate the feasibility of the rational drug combinations and explore their impact on long-term patient outcomes in BLBC.
