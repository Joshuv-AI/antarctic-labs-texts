Explainable artificial intelligence on safe balance and its major determinants in stroke patients
Authors: Sekwang Lee, Eunyoung Lee, Kwang-Sig Lee, Sung-Bom Pyun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study develops explainable artificial intelligence for predicting safe balance using hospital data, including clinical, neurophysiological, and diffusion tensor imaging properties. Retrospective data from 92 first-time stroke patients from January 2016 to June 2023 was analysed. The dependent variables were independent mobility scores, i.e., Berg Balance Scales with 0 (45 or below) vs. 1 (above 45) measured after three and six months, respectively. Twenty-nine predictors were included. Random forest variable importance was employed for identifying significant predictors of the Berg Balance Scale and testing its associations with the predictors, including Berg Balance Scale after one month and corticospinal tract diffusion tensor imaging properties. Shapley Additive Explanation values were calculated to analyse the directions of these associations. The random forest registered a higher or similar area under the curve compared to logistic regression, i.e., 91% vs. 87% (Berg Balance Scale after three months), 92% vs. 92% (Berg Balance Scale after six months). Based on random forest variable importance values and rankings: (1) Berg Balance Scale after three months has strong associations with Berg Balance Scale after one month, Fugl-Meyer assessment scale, ipsilesional corticospinal tract fractional anisotropy, fractional anisotropy laterality index and age; (2) Berg Balance Scale after six months has strong relationships with Fugl-Meyer assessment scale, Berg Balance Scale after one month, ankle plantar flexion muscle strength, knee extension muscle strength and hip flexion muscle strength. These associations were positive in the SHAP summary plots. Including Berg Balance Scale after one month, Fugl-Meyer assessment scale or ipsilesional corticospinal tract fractional anisotropy in the random forest will increase the probability of Berg Balance Scale after three months being above 45 by 0.11, 0.08, or 0.08. In conclusion, safe balance after stroke strongly correlates with its initial motor function, Fugl-Meyer assessment scale, and ipsilesional corticospinal tract fractional anisotropy. Introduction

The International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health defines “mobility” as “moving by changing body position or location or by transferring from one place to another, by carrying, moving or manipulating objects, by walking, running or climbing, and by using various forms of transportation”1. Mobility impairment causes a significant economic burden2,3. Its indirect cost in the United States (productivity loss from mortality and morbidity) was 30,601 dollars per person as of 20102. Its total cost in Korea (direct medical, direct non-medical, and indirect cost) registered a rapid growth of 54% from 1.9 billion dollars to 2.9 billion dollars during 2008–20113. Specifically, impaired balance after stroke is a significant contributor to decreased mobility. Keeping one’s equilibrium when standing is essential for walking. Motor and sensory functions substantially affect balance after stroke, and the considerable role of demographic and behavioural factors is also well documented4–7. Existing literature evaluates motor and sensory functions based on motor evoked potential (MEP) (reflecting the corticospinal tract) and somatosensory evoked potential (SEP) (reflecting the proprioceptive pathway). Indeed, an increasing amount of recent examination requests due attention for DTI, highlighting the importance of the corticospinal tract’s structural integrity in safe balance after stroke8–10.

The emergence of this new tradition can be good news for clinicians trying to predict safe balance after a stroke. However, assessing the relative importance of different variables for predicting safe balance after stroke can be challenging. Artificial intelligence can benefit this purpose, given that it is reported to surpass conventional statistical approaches in performance measures for predicting various disease conditions11–21. Specifically, explainable artificial intelligence, so-called “Shapley Additive Explanations” (SHAP), is garnering significant attention on a global level21. This cutting-edge approach is well known for its innovative characteristic of combining the results of all possible real-world scenarios, which are ignored in linear or logistic regression with an unrealistic assumption of ceteris paribus, i.e., “all the other variables staying constant”21. To our best knowledge, however, no literature is available on the issue of safe balance after stroke in this new approach of explainable artificial intelligence based on various data types. In this context, this study utilises machine learning and hospital data with clinical, neurophysiological, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) properties for two purposes: (1) predictive artificial intelligence predicting safe balance after stroke and (2) explainable artificial intelligence identifying significant predictors of safe balance after stroke and analysing the directions of its associations with the predictors.

Materials and methods

Figure 1 shows the study design and analytic strategy.

Flow Chart. This study develops explainable artificial intelligence for predicting safe balance using hospital data. The dependent variables were Berg Balance Scales with 0 (below 45) vs. 1 (above 45) measured after three and six months. The twenty-nine predictors included demographic/socioeconomic determinants, disease information and diffusion tensor imaging parameters of corticospinal tracts in both hemispheres. Random forest variable importance and Shapley Additive Explanation values were calculated to analyse the strengths and directions of associations between safe balance and its predictors.

Participants

This study retrospectively analysed data from 92 patients admitted to the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at Korea University Anam Hospital, Seoul, Korea, from January 2016 to June 2023. The following inclusion criteria were used: first-ever stroke confirmation based on computed tomography or MRI; neurophysiological and DTI evaluations within one month of the onset; and initial clinical assessment within one month and its subsequent counterparts at three and six months after stroke. Likewise, the following exclusion criteria were employed: bilateral lesions in the hemispheres or cerebellum; consciousness disorders or acute confusional states; peripheral neuropathy, neuromuscular disorders or musculoskeletal deformities in lower extremities; abnormal or absent SEP responses in the unaffected lower limb; and diabetes diagnosis. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Anam Hospital (IRB No. K2022-1264-006). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed written consent was obtained from each participant to participate in this study.

Clinical and diffusion tensor imaging variables

Safe balance was measured by BBS three times, i.e., within one month (initial), three months, and six months after stroke (BBS_1, BBS_3, and BBS_6). BBS_3 and BBS_6 served as the dependent variables, and their original scales of 0–56 (lower score for the greater risk of fall and the greater level of assistance needed for walking in daily life) were recoded as binary with 0 (45 or below) vs. 1 (above 45)8. This popular cut-off point of 45 came from existing literature8. The 16 clinical predictors of BBS_3 and BBS_6 were included: (1) BBS_1 with its original scale of 0–56; (2) age, gender (female vs. male); (3) stroke side (right vs. left), stroke type (H vs. I), mini-mental state examination score, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, lower limb FMA score (motor function impairment after stroke); (4) muscle strength, i.e., hip flexor, hip extensor, knee flexor, knee extensor, ankle dorsiflexor, ankle plantar flexor; (5) neurophysiological information, that is, SEP amplitude ratio and MEP (Table 1).

We utilised only the lower limb FMA score, which assesses hip, knee, and ankle motor function with a maximum score of 34 points. The sensory component of the FMA was not included in the assessment. Manual Muscle Testing was conducted to evaluate muscle strength, referencing the Medical Research Council (MRC) grading scale. The specific muscle groups assessed included the hip flexors, hip extensors, knee flexors, knee extensors, ankle dorsiflexors, and ankle plantar flexors. Initial muscle strength measurements were taken from the affected limbs. The SEP amplitude ratio (peak-to-peak amplitude ratio of the cortical P-37 potential of the tibial nerve) was computed from the SEP response of affected and unaffected hemispheres stimulating the bilateral tibial nerves as a biomarker for assessing proprioception.

MEPs were elicited using a figure-of-eight coil (model C-B60) with a MagPro X100 magnetic stimulator (Medtronic, Skovlunde, Denmark). Surface electrodes were attached to the tibialis anterior muscle to record MEPs. The coil was placed tangentially on the scalp, and the participant remained at rest during stimulation. Stimulation was first applied to each hemisphere to identify the ‘hot spot,’ which was defined as the location where the highest MEP amplitude was consistently recorded. The Resting Motor Threshold (RMT) was established by determining the lowest stimulator intensity that produced an MEP of at least 50 µV in 50% of trials when the muscle was at rest, and it was expressed as a percentage of the stimulator’s maximum output. The stimulation intensity was set at 120% of the RMT for subsequent MEP recordings. MEPs were recorded across 5 to 10 trials to ensure consistent results, with measurements taken from onset to peak for amplitude, and latency was also documented. Data on the presence or absence of MEPs in the affected limb were collected.

Thirteen DTI parameters on corticospinal tracts of the ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres were selected based on a previous study9: fractional anisotropy, laterality index of fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, radial diffusivity, axial diffusivity, average length and fibre number of the corticospinal tract in the ipsilesional or contralesional hemisphere (Table 1). Laterality Index was defined as (Contralesional fractional anisotropy – Ipsilesional fractional anisotropy) / (Contralesional fractional anisotropy + Ipsilesional fractional anisotropy). The 3T Prisma MRI scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head coil was used to acquire DTI data using single-shot spin-echo planar pulse sequences. The imaging parameters for acquisition were set with a diffusion gradient direction count of 64, a matrix size of 112 × 112, a field of view measuring 224 × 224 mm², and a slice thickness of 2.0 mm. The voxel dimensions were 2.0 × 2.0 × 2.0 mm³, with an echo time of 55 ms, a repetition time of 6500 ms, a b-value of 1000 s/mm², and a flip angle of 90°. The DTI studio software (H. Jiang, S. Mori, Department of Radiology, Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, Maryland) was utilised for fibre tracking based on the fibre assignment continuous tracking algorithm. This process was guided by subject-specific regions of interest (ROIs) rather than a predefined CST template, ensuring that the analysis was tailored to the unique anatomical features of each participant10. In cases where the CST could not be reconstructed due to severe damage, the fibre number and the average length were recorded as 0, and the remaining DTI parameters were also set to 0.

Machine learning analysis

Six machine learning models were used to predict BBS_3/BBS_6: logistic regression, decision tree, naïve Bayes, random forest, support vector machine, and artificial neural network18–21. A random forest is a group of decision trees that make majority votes on the dependent variable (“bootstrap aggregation”). For example, 1000 new data are created based on random sampling with replacement, 1000 decision trees are created, 1000 out-of-bag (leftover) data are created, and the 1000 decision trees make majority votes on the dependent variable of every participant in the out-of-bag data18–21. The following default parameters were adopted for convenience: The splitting criterion was GINI, the max depth was not determined, and the number of trees was 1000 in the random forest; the radial basis function kernel was employed in the support vector machine; and the limited memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno algorithm served for the optimisation of the artificial neural network. The data of 92 cases with full information were split into training and validation sets with a 75:25 ratio (69 vs. 23 cases). Criteria for the validation of the trained models were accuracy (a ratio of correct predictions among 23 cases) and the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) (area under the plot of sensitivity vs. 1 - specificity). The random split and analysis were repeated 50 times, and the average was used for external validation.

Variable importance analysis

In practice, experts in artificial intelligence use random forest permutation importance to derive the rankings and values of all predictors to predict the dependent variable (e.g., BBS_3/BBS_6). Then, they employ the SHAP plots to evaluate the directions of associations between the predictors and the dependent variable18,21. Random forest permutation importance measures the overall accuracy decrease from the data permutation on the predictor. It is an average over all trees in the random forest with a value of 0 to 118. The SHAP value of a predictor for a participant measures the difference between what machine learning predicts for the probability of BBS_3/BBS_6 being above 45 with and without the predictor21. For example, let us assume that (1) the SHAP values of CST-IP-FA for BBS_3 have the range of (− 0.10, 0.10) and (2) these values increase as CST-IP-FA values increase. Here, some participants have SHAP values as low as − 0.10, while others have as high as 0.10. Including a predictor (CST-IP-FA) in machine learning will decrease or increase the probability of the dependent variable (BBS_3) being above 45 in the range of − 0.10 and 0.10. One way to interpret the results is to consider the max value (0.10), given that the SHAP values increase as the CST-IP-FA values increase21.

Notably, the SHAP approach has a vital strength compared to linear or logistic regression: the former considers all realistic scenarios, unlike the latter18,21. Let us assume that there are three predictors of BBS_3, i.e., CST-IP-FA, BBS_1, and FMA. As defined above, the SHAP value of CST-IP-FA for BBS_3 for a particular participant is the difference between what machine learning predicts for the probability of BBS_3 being above 45 with and without CST-IP-FA for the participant. Here, the SHAP value for the participant is the average of the following four scenarios for the participant: (1) BBS_1 excluded, FMA excluded; (2) BBS_1 included, FMA excluded; (3) BBS_1 excluded, FMA included; and (4) BBS_1 included, FMA included. In other words, the SHAP value combines the results of all possible scenarios, which are ignored in linear or logistic regression, with an unrealistic assumption of ceteris paribus, i.e., “all the other variables staying constant”21. Finally, it can be noted that R-Studio 1.3.959 (R-Studio Inc.: Boston, United States) and Python 3.8.8 (Van Rossum G, Drake FL.: Scotts Valley, United States) were employed for the analysis on November 1, 2023-December 31, 2023.

Results

Discussion

Summary

As predictive artificial intelligence, the random forest registered a higher or similar area under the curve compared to logistic regression, i.e., 91% vs. 87% (BBS_3), 92% vs. 92% (BBS_6). Based on the results of explainable artificial intelligence from random forest variable importance, BBS_3 has strong associations with BBS_1, FMA, CST-IP-FA, fractional anisotropy laterality index, and age; BBS_6 has strong relationships with FMA, BBS_1, ankle plantar flexion muscle strength, knee extension muscle strength and hip flexion muscle strength. These associations were positive in the SHAP summary plots. Including BBS_1, FMA, or CST-IP-FA in the random forest will increase the probability of BBS_3 being above 45 by 0.11, 0.08, or 0.08.

Contributions

This study has four clinical and research implications. Firstly, this study highlights the importance of initial safe balance and motor function in the lower limb for predicting safe balance after stroke. Safe balance is essential for walking22,23, and the BBS is its popular indicator, with a lower score for the greater risk of falls and the greater level of assistance needed for walking in daily life8. Based on random forest variable importance in this study, BBS_1 (initial safter balance) and FMA (motor function impairment in the lower limb) were top-2 predictors for BBS_3 and BBS_6. The results of this study agree with those of previous studies on the strong associations of initial safer balance with its later counterparts24–27. The findings of this study are also consistent with existing literature on the importance of FMA for predicting safe balance after stroke28–31. FMA, not designed to estimate safe balance after stroke, has been considered a good measure of movement quality and coordination, especially motor function impairment from synergistic limb movement after stroke28. This type of movement quality and coordination is reported to be vital for safe balance after stroke29. Two previous studies affirmed this evidence on the strong relationships of FMA with BBS within one month, at three months, and at six months after stroke30,31. To our knowledge, however, this study is the first explainable artificial intelligence study in this direction. Secondly, muscle strength predictors (hip flexor, hip extensor, knee flexor, knee extensor, ankle dorsiflexor, ankle plantar flexor) registered lower rankings than FMA and DTI predictors (CST-IP-FA, CST-Contralesional-FA, CST-IP-Average Length) for the prediction of BBS_3 in this study. The prediction of safe balance after a stroke would depend more on movement quality and coordination than on muscle strength as long as it is enough to overcome gravity. One previous study also reflected this explanation, i.e., using a straightforward binary indicator of his extensor motor function to predict independent walking at 12 weeks after stroke, i.e., whether the Medical Research Council (MRC) grade was above three32.

Thirdly, DTI and neurophysiological predictors were out of the top 10 for the prediction of BBS_6 in this study. Two explanations are plausible here. DTI predictors in this study involve the corticospinal tract, which has stronger associations with hand motor function than its leg counterpart33,34. Indeed, a time interval of six months would reduce the importance of DTI and neurophysiological predictors for the prediction of BBS_6. These predictors present structural information about the corticospinal tract or functional information about the somatosensory pathway. Still, they do not provide essential information such as joint spasticity35 or balance training36, which would significantly affect safe balance six months after stroke as a chronic phase. Fourthly, the importance rankings of age and the NIHSS were within the top 10 for the prediction of both BBS_3 and BBS_6. These two predictors were reported to be significant predictors for the activities of daily living three months after stroke37. Age affects safe balance after stroke based on its strong associations with neuroplasticity and pre-existing muscle conditions, including arthritis, osteoporosis, and musculoskeletal problems38–40. As a tool to assess the neurological severity of stroke41, likewise, the NIHSS would influence safe balance after stroke, given its strong relationship with movement quality and coordination. Little examination has been done, and more investigation is needed on this critical topic based on explainable artificial intelligence.

Strengths and limitations

Explainable artificial intelligence has garnered significant attention worldwide, and its substantial presence in top journals reflects this new trend11–20. The random forest delivers the best performance above conventional approaches while embracing various clinical and image characteristics in real-world scenarios without unrealistic distributional assumptions in traditional methods42. Moreover, the SHAP approach is well known for its unique advantage relative to linear or logistic regression, i.e., combining the outcomes of all possible scenarios, which are ignored in linear or logistic regression with an unrealistic assumption of “all the other variables staying constant”21,43. In this vein, experts in artificial intelligence use random forest variable importance and SHAP plots to evaluate the strength and direction of an association between the dependent variable and its significant predictor. This study benefited from these cutting-edge approaches, delivering excellent performance results and practical clinical implications. However, this study had some limitations. Firstly, this study employed the strict exclusion criteria, that is, bilateral lesions in the hemispheres or cerebellum, consciousness disorders or acute confusional states, peripheral neuropathy, neuromuscular disorders or musculoskeletal deformities in lower extremities, abnormal or absent SEP responses in the unaffected lower limb, and diabetes diagnosis. This is expected to bring certain restrictions on the clinical implications of this study. Secondly, data for this study came from a single hospital, which would limit the external validation of this study. Thirdly, the size of the data was small, which would bring an additional limitation on the external validation of this study. Fourthly, this study did not consider the multiple categories of Berg Balance Scales and the statistical comparison of model performance. Fifthly, extending this study to a multi-modal approach will be an excellent topic for future research. Twenty-nine clinical and diffusion tensor imaging variables took a numeric form for machine learning models in this study. Combining machine learning models with clinical numeric data and deep learning models with diffusion tensor imaging data is expected to improve performance, strengthen validity and further the horizon of research in this direction.

Conclusions

This study used machine learning and hospital data with clinical, neurophysiological, and DTI properties for predictive artificial intelligence to predict safe balance after stroke and explainable artificial intelligence to identify significant predictors of safe balance after stroke and analyse the directions of its associations with the predictors. Safe balance after stroke strongly correlates with initial motor function, the Fugl-Meyer assessment scale, and ipsilesional corticospinal tract fractional anisotropy. Diffusion tensor imaging information aids in developing explainable artificial intelligence for predicting safe balance after a stroke.
