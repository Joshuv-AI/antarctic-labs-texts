Migrant Work and Kidney Health in Nepal
Authors: Sanjib Kumar Sharma, Madan Kumar Upadhyaya
Published: 2024

The International Federation of Association Football World Cup has highlighted the human rights issues faced by migrants involved in construction activities in Qatar, particularly concerning exploitation and their physical and mental health. Any job that fits the criteria of four D’s—dirty, dry, dangerous, and demanding—are filled by migrant workers looking for higher wages. According to April 2023 poverty indicators, the unemployment rate in Nepal is 10.7%. Labor migration has been a common livelihood strategy for low-income households over the recent decades. Remittances from migrant laborers amounted to 24% of the country's gross domestic product.1 The mean age of migrant laborers is 29 years, with females making up 50% of migrants worldwide and 8.5% in Nepal. They migrate mostly to Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries and Malaysia. The occupational profile of workers are unskilled jobs (59%), with most migrants involved in manufacturing jobs (75%), and service sector jobs (21%). According to a qualitative study,2 the low-skilled workforce mostly work under adverse living and working conditions, exploitation by employers, congested accommodation, long hours of physical work without breaks, and unhealthy lifestyle.3 Considering this situation, we conducted a short survey at our institute, B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences, a tertiary care center in eastern Nepal. We found that 31% of the patients with kidney failure undergoing maintenance dialysis at our center are migrant workers. A pioneering and renowned surgeon at the Human Organ Transplant Center in Nepal has noticed labor migrant workers who have returned from abroad contribute to more than 30% of the transplant number in Nepal.4 We found this as an alarming situation in regard to health of migrant workers. With the rise in migrant worker throughout the world, this raises a question on work safety and health of migrants globally. It is of paramount importance to advocate for kidney health among the vulnerable and disadvantaged migrants because prevention is better than cure.

An International Society of Nephrology study in 2016 revealed high prevalence of CKD but low awareness in the Nepalese cohort.5 A community-based door-to-door survey from eastern Nepal performed among a subset of 1000 population using serum creatinine revealed the prevalence of CKD to be 10.6%.6 Age-standardized prevalence rate of CKD increased from 5979.1 patients per 100,000 population (95% confidence interval: 5539.7–6400.4) in 1990 to 7634.1 patients per 100,000 population (95% confidence interval: 7138.8–8119.4) in 2019.7 Research is increasingly highlighting the correlation between climate, extreme working conditions, and kidney diseases. Longitudinal assessments among migrant farmers have shed light on CKD of undetermined cause among men in their 20s–60s with no prior comorbidities who present often in the late stages in countries such as Mexico, Sri Lanka, India, Egypt, Costa-Rica, often with previous exposure to harmful pesticides, heavy metals, and dehydration. In a self-administered online survey with Nepalese nephrologists as participants, 92.1% of the nephrologists reported that the cause of CKD was unknown among their returnee migrant patients, most of whom were younger than 40 years without prior comorbidities.8

Nepalese migrant workers were engaged in heavy work or had strict rules regarding drinking water or toilet breaks. A qualitative study among Indonesian migrant workers found behavioral factors, such as daily consumption of cheap and readily available fast food, soft/energy drinks, and alcohol, along with hereditary hypertension were associated with kidney disease.3 Women account for 50% of all migrants worldwide. Female migrants are facing health issues because of exploitation. Other health issues include tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS, urinary tract infections, and skin diseases owing to unhygienic conditions. Similarly, occupational injuries and musculoskeletal pain are common among construction workers.

In Nepal, there is more focus on free dialysis services, renal transplantation, and health insurance schemes. Little priority is given to the prevention of renal impairment. Priorities with a captivating slogan “ABCD to save your kidneys (Check Your ABCD)” tests for urinary albumin (A-Albuminuria), BP (B-BP), serum creatinine (C-Creatinine), and blood glucose (D-Diabetes) among migrant workers before migration, during home country visits, and annually in the community and workplace could be implemented. The Ministry of Labour, Employment and Social Security could particularly focus on kidney health education programs and regular ABCD checkup of migrant workers in GCC countries and Malaysia for early detection and prevention of CKD. The ABCD approach is a basic approach of detecting noncommunicable diseases, which include basic physical examination, like measuring BP, and basic laboratory tests, like urinary albumin by dipstick, creatinine, and blood glucose by dipstick method. These tests can easily be performed at a community level. ABCD screening can be performed among the migrant workers and returnees in the community through the Ministry of Health and Population; the Ministry of Labour, Employment and Social Security; and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Raising awareness among female migrant workers is vital to prevent deterioration of health-related risks.9 International organizations and Non-Governmental Organizations should advocate for better working conditions and checkups. The Non-Governmental Organizations and civil societies currently working in this area include the National Network for Safer Migration, trade unions, media reporters focusing on migration issues, and the Non-Resident Nepali Association involved in fund-raising activities. Nonetheless, there are challenges. Nepal lacks government policies and sufficient implementation. There is lack of awareness about kidney health, poor access to health facilities, and favor for illicit migration. These make quality screening and monitoring of migrant workers very challenging.

Three key dimensions were recently identified as a conceptual policy framework to promote migrant worker health: poor employment conditions and lack of protection, disposability and abuse of power, and health and safety and psychosocial hazards9 (Table 1). The Ministry of Health and Population; the Ministry of Labour, Employment and Social Security; and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs can work cooperatively with an agreed consensus on prevention and early detection of CKD among migrant workers. Preventive measures to work on include kidney health education, ABCD screening, job safety, and quality assurance, as depicted in Figure 1. In addition, coordination with the private sector to promote economic opportunities would be valuable. In addition, governing authorities should work with the International Labor Organization, the World Health Organization, and GCC country leaders to protect migrant workers’ health during the job period in accordance to the recommendations mentioned in Table 1. The employers and employment agencies must adhere to the legal framework for ensuring the health and safety to migrant laborers, provide regular health checkups, and mandate health insurance.

CKD (Mesoamerican nephropathy) is seen in Central America (Mexico) among agriculture workers with extremes of heat. Our recommendations on kidney health education, ABCD screening, job safety, and quality assurance is also applied to this population. Apart from Nepal, other labor-sending countries include Bangladesh, Pakistan, India and Sri Lanka, the Philippines, Vietnam, Myanmar and Lao People's Democratic Republic, and countries in Africa. Collective actions by these labor-sending countries for kidney health rights and protection is important in the form of policy dialog meetings or regional forums. Finally, the contribution of science to end poverty cannot be overlooked. We advocate for studies on prevalence of CKD among migrants from high-risk countries (Gulf countries) in comparison with the general population. As the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals recommendations say, “no one should be left behind,” emphasizing the health and human rights of migrants.
