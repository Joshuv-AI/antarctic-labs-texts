Knowledge attitudes and practices regarding the use of prebiotics and probiotics for respiratory infections among Saudi healthcare students
Authors: Yousef S Aldabayan, Rafia Bano, Mashael Huwaikem, Sawsan Al-Hashim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 823 health science students to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of health science students regarding honey as a prebiotic and yoghurt as a probiotic, using an online questionnaire. Prebiotics and probiotics offer health benefits for the digestive system, respiratory health, and immune system, and healthcare providers should be well-informed to advise patients effectively. Data was analyzed using SPSS software. The results reveal significant differences in knowledge and practices across gender, geographical location, and smoking status. Females consistently outperformed males, with higher scores in knowledge assessment (3.9 vs. 3.4), practices and usage (3.7 vs. 3.1), awareness and attitudes (4.2 vs. 3.6), comparative perception (3.9 vs. 3.3), and recommendations (4.0 vs. 3.5). The data indicates that honey (39.5%) is the most preferred natural remedy for respiratory health among Saudi healthcare students, followed by yoghurt (26.9%). In comparison, 22.7% consider both equally beneficial. Using the Chi-square test (χ²) to determine statistical significance and Cramér’s V to measure effect size, the study found that 45.9% of participants demonstrated high knowledge of honey’s health benefits, with males (48.2%) exhibiting slightly greater awareness than females (44.2%). The findings indicated that healthcare students in Saudi Arabia have good knowledge about probiotics. A structured and inclusive health education approach can increase acceptance of natural health solutions, improving respiratory health outcomes across various populations. The study suggests that educational interventions on prebiotics and probiotics should be incorporated into health programs to help students manage respiratory health using safe, natural therapies.Keywords: Prebiotics, Probiotics, Respiratory health, Knowledge, AttitudeSubject terms: Health care, Nutrition, Public health

Introduction

Respiratory health remains a significant public health concern globally, with conditions such as influenza (flu) and asthma contributing to high rates of morbidity and mortality1. Honey and yoghurt, recognized for their natural therapeutic properties, have gained attention as complementary remedies for managing respiratory symptoms. Honey is valued for its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, which can help reduce cough severity and improve throat discomfort during respiratory infections2. Recent studies have highlighted honey’s effectiveness in reducing cough frequency and severity in children, often comparing it favorably to over-the-counter cough suppressants3. Moreover, honey’s role as a prebiotic is gaining attention, as it can promote the growth of beneficial gut bacteria, thereby supporting immune function and potentially reducing the incidence of respiratory infections4.

Similarly, yoghurt, particularly those containing live bacterial cultures, has been recognized for its probiotic properties. Probiotics are known to enhance gut health, which in turn can influence systemic immune responses. The gut-lung axis, a concept describing the relationship between gut microbiota and respiratory health, suggests that probiotics found in yoghurt may play a role in preventing or alleviating respiratory infections5. Studies have shown that regular consumption of probiotics can reduce the incidence and duration of upper respiratory tract infections, making yoghurt a valuable dietary component in respiratory health management6.

Najafi et al. (2020) conducted a study that demonstrated the use of probiotics in reducing bacterial pneumonia symptoms, such as dyspnea, cough, and fever, along with a reduction in hospitalization duration7. This highlights the potential of probiotics, like those found in yoghurt, to support respiratory health management by enhancing immune response and mitigating infection severity.

Rashidi et al. (2021) performed a systematic review and meta-analysis, revealing that probiotic fermented dairy products (PFDP), including yoghurt, had a significant protective effect against respiratory tract infections. Their findings suggest that regular consumption of PFDP can reduce the incidence of both upper and lower respiratory diseases8.

Probiotics are available as both pharmacological formulations and functional foods, commonly used to address general gastrointestinal issues or prevent antibiotic-related diarrhea9. Despite a wide range of probiotic products and substantial evidence supporting their health benefits, their use is not as widespread as other supplements10. This may be due to a lack of consumer knowledge and insufficient guidance from healthcare professionals. The knowledge of healthcare providers directly influences the choice and effectiveness of treatments, with evidence showing that the information provided by them is linked to better adoption of healthy dietary practices and a reduced risk of nutrition-related chronic diseases11. The knowledge of medical students about probiotics will impact their ability to offer accurate nutrition advice and may shape their future prescription practices.

Additionally, clinicians’ understanding of probiotics is influenced by their education, suggesting that gaps in knowledge could stem from deficiencies in the medical school curriculum. Studies from countries like India, Nigeria, Poland, and the US have evaluated the knowledge and attitudes of medical science students about probiotics12–16. According to these studies, most respondents were familiar with the term “probiotics”, with high awareness, but a gap remained in their knowledge and application.

Given the rising interest in integrative and complementary medicine, it is essential to understand how future healthcare providers and current healthcare students perceive and utilize these natural remedies. As these students will soon be at the forefront of patient care, their knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding honey and yoghurt can significantly impact their clinical decisions and patient recommendations. Despite the increasing use of these natural products, there remains a gap in the literature regarding the awareness and practices of healthcare students concerning honey and yoghurt for respiratory health management17.

This study aims to fill this gap by assessing the knowledge, practices, and awareness of Saudi healthcare students regarding the use of honey and yoghurt as natural remedies for respiratory health. By focusing on students from various healthcare disciplines, this research will provide insights into potential educational gaps and the need for curriculum development to better prepare students for incorporating complementary therapies into patient care. Additionally, understanding the differences in knowledge and practices across different regions and types of institutions (governmental vs. private universities) can help tailor educational strategies to specific needs.

The findings from this study will contribute to the broader discourse on the integration of natural remedies into mainstream healthcare, particularly in the context of respiratory health. By identifying areas where healthcare education may need to be strengthened, this research can inform future educational interventions, leading to improved patient outcomes and greater acceptance of evidence-based complementary therapies within the healthcare system.

Natural therapies like honey and yoghurt, recognized for their prebiotic and probiotic properties, offer potential benefits for respiratory and immune health. However, limited research exists on how future healthcare providers perceive and use these remedies. This study investigates the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of health science students in Saudi Arabia regarding honey and yoghurt, aiming to identify gaps and inform educational strategies. Understanding these perspectives is crucial for integrating evidence-based natural therapies into healthcare education and promoting holistic patient care.

The survey was developed and interpreted with guidance from the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) framework, which posits that people’s health behaviors — such as using natural treatments for respiratory ailments — are determined by their knowledge and attitudes. This model enhanced our understanding of how healthcare students’ awareness is translated to respiratory health behaviors. Keeping all these facts in mind, the present study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitude, and practices of Saudi healthcare students regarding the use of honey as a prebiotic and yoghurt as a probiotic for respiratory health management, particularly in the context of flu symptoms and asthma. The objectives of the study were also to compare the knowledge, practices, and awareness between healthcare students from different disciplines (e.g., medicine, nursing, pharmacy), regions (e.g., Central Province, Eastern Province), and types of institutions (governmental vs. private universities).

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional survey-based study was conducted among Saudi healthcare students to assess their knowledge, practices, and awareness regarding the use of honey and yoghurt for respiratory health management. All experimental protocols were approved by the deanship of Scientific research, King Faisal University, before the commencement of the study, Reference number (KFU-REC-2024-DEC-ETHICS2991).

Study population

A priori sample size was calculated using a 0.8 statistical power level, twenty-one observed variables, and a 0.05 probability level. The calculated sample size was 940. After sorting the incomplete responses, the total sample included 823 participants for further analysis. Survey link was distributed online without centralized tracking, it was not possible to determine the exact number of students reached and thus to calculate a precise response rate. The inclusion criteria were undergraduate healthcare students from various disciplines (e.g., medicine, nursing, pharmacy, clinical dietitians, respiratory therapists) enrolled in governmental and private universities across different regions of Saudi Arabia (e.g., Central Province, Eastern Province, Western Province). Those studying in other colleges and those who had already graduated were excluded from the study.

Sampling method

A convenient sampling method was used to recruit participants. The survey was distributed online, and participation was voluntary. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before they participated in the survey. The study ensured the confidentiality and anonymity of the participants’ responses. Google Form was set to allow one response per device to minimize duplicates.

Data collection

The questionnaire was distributed online via Google Form over 2 months (from January – February 2025). The content validity of the questionnaire for this study was established through a review by three academic experts in nutrition, respiratory health, and public health. The study was approved by the Deanship of Scientific Research, King Faisal University. All the methods were performed according to the relevant guidelines and regulations provided by the approving body. To establish reliability, a pilot study was conducted with thirty healthcare students. Cronbach’s alpha of respiratory health-related knowledge and attitude items was 0.82, suggesting high internal consistency. Data was collected using a self-administered online questionnaire. The questionnaire was divided into five sections: (I) Demographic information, (ii) Knowledge assessment, (iii) Practice and usage, (iv) Awareness {Awareness Level classification: High (≥ 70%), Moderate (50–69%), Low (< 50%)} and (v) comparative and attitudinal questions.

Data analysis and interpretation

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26 software. Descriptive statistics summarized the demographic data, knowledge scores, and practices. Comparative analysis was conducted to identify differences in knowledge, practices, and awareness between different student groups (e.g., by discipline, region, and type of institution). The appropriate statistical tests were used to assess statistical differences between groups, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant18,19.

The analysis of the collected data offers valuable insights into the knowledge, awareness, and attitudes toward honey and yoghurt for respiratory health across different demographic groups, including gender, location, and smoking status. Statistical tests, such as T-tests, were conducted to compare mean scores and examine whether gender or type of institution influences awareness, usage, or confidence in recommending honey or yoghurt. ANOVA and logistic regression were used to investigate predictive relationships between variables and identify which factors (e.g., education, awareness) influence practices or confidence in recommending natural remedies, ensuring the results’ accuracy20–22. This approach reveals significant disparities in health literacy and engagement, highlighting how demographic and behavioral factors influence knowledge and attitudes toward natural remedies. The findings provide a basis for evidence-based recommendations to improve public health interventions and educational strategies23,24.

Results

The following sections present a detailed examination of the results, their implications, and potential strategies to address identified gaps in health awareness and engagement. While some comparisons revealed statistically significant differences (e.g., p < 0.05), effect sizes (e.g., Cramér’s V = 0.07) were small, suggesting modest group differences in respiratory health-related behaviors. These findings reveal subtle but statistically significant trends in practice.

The study surveyed 823 Saudi healthcare students, as presented in Table 1; Fig. 1, with a higher proportion of females (56.6%) than males (43.4%). Most respondents (93.9%) were aged 18–24, indicating they are early in their academic careers. A small percentage were older students (25–30 years: 4.9%, 31–35 years: 0.9%, 36 years and above: 0.4%). Nursing students comprised the largest group (31.8%), followed by medicine (19.7%) and clinical dietetics (17.5%). Other disciplines included pharmacy, public health, and more. Most participants were undergraduates (96.8%), with first-year (28.1%) and fourth-year (26.7%) students being the most common. Geographically, most students were from the Eastern Province (57.2%), followed by the Central (15.3%) and Western (10.2%) Provinces. Although the majority (95.9%) of respondents attended public universities, this may reflect a sampling limitation rather than the actual distribution of healthcare students in Saudi Arabia, where numerous private universities operate across different regions.

Table 2 shows that many Saudi healthcare students demonstrated general awareness of the benefits of honey and yoghurt for respiratory health. In this study, general awareness was defined as ≥ 70% of participants providing correct or affirmative responses to individual knowledge items. Based on this criterion, 71.0% of respondents recognized honey as a prebiotic supporting immune function, while 76.2% identified yoghurt as a probiotic that can enhance gut health and potentially reduce respiratory infections. Additionally, honey was commonly associated with sore throat (81.7%) and flu symptoms (70.5%), and yoghurt with gut health (85.3%) and respiratory infections (53.5%). Gender-based analysis revealed that females consistently demonstrated slightly higher awareness than males, for example, 73.2% of females versus 68.1% of males identified honey’s prebiotic role, and 79.2% versus 72.3% identified yoghurt’s probiotic role. A greater proportion of males also selected “I don’t know,” suggesting comparatively higher uncertainty and knowledge gaps in this subgroup.

The data from Fig. 2 indicates that a considerable proportion of Saudi healthcare students incorporate honey and yoghurt into their practices for managing respiratory health. However, confidence in recommending these natural remedies varies. A majority (31.0%) reported using honey sometimes, while 26.5% used it often and 19.3% always, with similar trends observed across genders. Yoghurt consumption followed a comparable pattern, with 31.6% consuming it sometimes, 27.3% usually, and 20.2% always, with females exhibiting slightly higher consumption rates. Despite these usage trends, only 55.7% of students felt confident recommending honey, and 51.0% recommended yoghurt for respiratory health. Notably, a substantial proportion of respondents lacked confidence in making recommendations—25.7% for honey and 28.3% for yoghurt. These findings suggest a positive perception of honey and yoghurt as respiratory health aids but highlight the need for further education and training to strengthen healthcare students’ confidence in endorsing natural remedies as part of patient care.

Table 3 shows that many Saudi healthcare students have high confidence in honey (49.8%) and yoghurt (46.8%) as beneficial for respiratory health, with only a small percentage reporting low confidence (19.8% for honey and 21.6% for yoghurt). Confidence levels are similar between male and female respondents. The main barriers to recommending honey and yoghurt are a lack of evidence and limited knowledge, with patient preferences also playing a role. Cultural factors have a minor impact on hesitancy. These findings suggest that improving education and integrating clinical research could increase students’ confidence in using honey and yoghurt in patient care.

Data analysis from Table 4 indicates that honey (39.5%) is the most preferred natural remedy for respiratory health among Saudi healthcare students, followed by yoghurt (26.9%). In comparison, 22.7% consider both equally beneficial, and 10.9% do not feel confident recommending either. This suggests a general inclination toward honey but also highlights a lack of strong consensus. In terms of education preparedness, only 36.2% of students feel adequately trained to recommend honey and yoghurt for respiratory health, whereas 42.9% believe their education has not prepared them well, reflecting a significant gap in formal training on natural remedies. However, awareness of honey side effects varies, with 51.6% acknowledging potential risks, 28.2% unaware, and 20.2% uncertain, underscoring the need for better education on both the benefits and possible drawbacks of natural treatments to ensure informed clinical recommendations.

Table 5 revealed gender-based differences in knowledge and use of honey and yoghurt for respiratory health. While 45.9% had high knowledge of honey’s benefits, males (48.2%) had slightly higher knowledge than females (44.2%), with a statistically significant but small effect (p = 0.040, Cramér’s V = 0.07). Knowledge of yoghurt’s benefits was lower (37.9%), with no significant gender difference. Males reported more frequent honey use (51.5% vs. 48.9%, p = 0.015), while females consumed more yoghurt (40.3% vs. 35.6%), though the difference was not significant. Females (71.2%) were more likely to recommend honey for respiratory health than males (67.2%) (p = 0.009), despite males having higher knowledge and use. Confidence in recommending yoghurt was low overall (54.4%) with no significant gender difference (p = 0.097). These results suggest that although males have more knowledge and use honey more, females are more likely to recommend it, indicating a possible confidence gap among males. All genders underutilize yoghurt, indicating a need for targeted education to boost confidence in recommending natural remedies. Further research should explore barriers like perceived efficacy, cultural beliefs, and gaps in training.

Medical students demonstrated the highest levels of awareness regarding both honey (53.7%) and yoghurt (45.7%), and were also the most confident in recommending these remedies. Nursing students followed closely (50.0%), whereas health informatics, public health, and other non-clinical disciplines reported the lowest levels of awareness and confidence. In terms of confidence in recommending honey and yoghurt, males were slightly more confident overall than females (44.9% vs. 41.2%). However, only 30.8% of all students felt that their education had adequately prepared them to recommend these remedies, with medical and nursing students being 1.48 times more likely to report feeling prepared (p = 0.032).

Although previous sections noted that female students showed slightly higher awareness in self-reported responses (e.g., survey items on usage or perceived benefits), Table 6 reflects actual knowledge-based scores, where males scored marginally higher. This distinction suggests that female participants may exhibit greater subjective awareness or engagement, while male students demonstrated higher objective knowledge scores. This clarification helps resolve the apparent contradiction between the gender-awareness statement and Table 6 findings.

The results underscore significant differences based on both gender and academic discipline (p < 0.05), emphasizing the need for targeted educational enhancements, particularly in programs where awareness and confidence levels are lower.

The comparative analysis of gender and geographical location, as presented in Table 7, reveals significant disparities in knowledge, practices, awareness, and attitudes toward using honey and yoghurt for respiratory health. Females showed slightly higher awareness of honey’s prebiotic and yoghurt’s probiotic benefits, but their confidence in recommending these remedies was lower (Chi-Square, p < 0.05). Participants from the Eastern and Southern provinces had the highest knowledge, while those from the Western and Northern regions scored lower, suggesting gaps in health education. Males consumed more honey, while females consumed more yoghurt, with smokers reporting lower consumption of both (ANOVA, p = 0.048). Confidence in recommending these remedies was higher in males and those from the Central and Eastern provinces (T-Test, p < 0.05). Logistic regression indicated that those well-educated about natural remedies were 1.42 times more likely to recommend them (p < 0.05). These findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions, such as training and region-specific awareness campaigns, to enhance knowledge and confidence in using natural remedies in healthcare.

Discussion

Results of the present study depict several key insights regarding the knowledge, awareness, practices, attitudes, and comparative perceptions of honey and yoghurt for respiratory health across different demographic groups, including gender, geographical location, and smoking status.

Gender-based differences in knowledge and practices

Interestingly, male students had higher knowledge of the respiratory health benefits of honey and yoghurt; however, they showed less confidence in recommending these to patients.” It may also indicate that knowledge alone does not predict recommendation behaviour, and that gender-specific communication training may be necessary to facilitate effective respiratory health counselling. This distinction suggests that female participants may exhibit greater subjective awareness or engagement, while male students demonstrated higher objective knowledge scores. Chi-Square analysis (p = 0.001) confirmed that gender was a significant determinant of health awareness. Furthermore, logistic regression (OR = 1.82, p = 0.001) suggested that females were more confident in recommending these natural remedies than males. These findings align with existing research indicating that women generally demonstrate greater health consciousness and engagement in preventive healthcare measures11,16.

Impact of smoking status on health awareness and usage patterns

A comparison between smokers (n = 113) and non-smokers (n = 710) revealed substantial differences in health-related knowledge and practices. The t-test (t = 3.45, p = 0.003) indicated that non-smokers had significantly higher knowledge scores than smokers, suggesting that smoking behaviors may be associated with lower engagement in health literacy25–27. These studies consistently demonstrate a negative correlation between literacy (health, reading, numeracy) or cognitive function and various smoking behaviors, including initiation, continuation, and quitting. In essence, individuals with lower literacy and educational levels are more likely to smoke and less likely to quit. Non-smokers also had a higher mean score in health-related practices (3.9) compared to smokers (2.7), confirmed by ANOVA results (p = 0.004). These findings underscore the importance of targeted awareness campaigns to educate smokers about the benefits of natural remedies and preventive healthcare.

Regional variations in health awareness and attitudes

The study revealed notable geographical differences in knowledge and awareness, with participants from the Eastern Province scoring the highest in awareness levels (mean: 4.1). At the same time, those from the Northern Province had the lowest (mean: 3.0). One-Way ANOVA (p = 0.002) confirmed the statistical significance of these regional disparities. However, multivariate regression analysis (β = 0.35, p = 0.001) suggested that geographical location was a strong predictor of knowledge and awareness levels. These variations stem from differences in healthcare infrastructure, access to health education, and cultural influences across regions. Besides geography, differences were also observed across majors. Medical students exhibited the highest level of knowledge regarding honey and yoghurt for respiratory health (53.7%), followed by nursing (50.0%) and pharmacy (46.2%) students. Students from health informatics and public health reported comparatively lower scores. Anova tests confirmed these differences to be statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Confidence in recommending honey and yoghurt for respiratory health

In terms of comparative perception, females and non-smokers were significantly more confident in recommending honey and yoghurt for respiratory health than males and smokers. The Chi-Square test (p = 0.002) confirmed significant differences in perception across these groups. Moreover, logistic regression (OR = 1.82, p = 0.001) indicated that non-smokers and females were more likely to trust and recommend natural remedies, emphasizing the need for awareness initiatives targeted at males and smokers to boost their confidence in evidence-based natural treatments. The Chi-Square test (p = 0.002) confirmed significant differences in perception across these groups. Moreover, logistic regression (OR = 1.82, p = 0.001) indicated that non-smokers and females were more likely to trust and recommend natural remedies. At the same time, students from specific regions (e.g., eastern province) and medical disciplines were more likely to report confidence in recommending natural remedies compared to those in the northern areas or non-medical majors.

Educational background and institutional influence

Students from government institutions exhibited greater confidence in recommending natural remedies than those from private institutions, as indicated by ANOVA results (p = 0.003). This suggests that educational exposure to alternative and natural medicine may be more prevalent in government universities, highlighting the necessity for standardized curricular inclusion of evidence-based natural treatments across both public and private institutions.

It is well established that knowledge influences preventive health behavior28. In the present study, 54% of students demonstrated moderate understanding of honey as a prebiotic, and approximately 62% showed moderate knowledge of yoghurt as a probiotic. High knowledge levels were reported by 46% and 38% of students for honey and yoghurt, respectively. These findings are comparable to an Iranian study among medical students, where 43.1% and 50.7% demonstrated acceptable and good knowledge of probiotics, respectively10. In contrast, a study conducted in Indonesia among health science students reported that only 9.2% had good knowledge regarding the terms prebiotics and probiotics29. Similarly, 72% of students in our study correctly identified probiotics as live microorganisms, a rate higher than that observed in a Saudi survey, where 57.7% of healthcare practitioners recognized the definition of probiotics, and in a Jordanian study, where only 54.3% of students were familiar with it11,18.

A notable knowledge gap was highlighted in a study from Oman, where 89.3% of medical and 95% of dental students reported insufficient knowledge about probiotics9. This has been attributed to the lack of formal curriculum coverage, with fewer than 20% of students indicating that probiotics were included in their medical education30. Interestingly, Rahmah et al. found no significant difference in probiotic knowledge across majors, while our study showed variation: medicine students scored highest in attitude and knowledge, followed by nursing, pharmacy, and nutrition students (p < 0.001)29.

In terms of perceived benefits, most students in our study believed that probiotics were helpful for gastrointestinal health (81.2%), flu symptoms (58.3%), respiratory health (47.6%), and asthma (38.7%). These findings are aligned with prior research indicating that probiotics may offer benefits in managing gut health, irritable bowel disease, allergies, hyperlipidemia, and even cancer30. Our study is also the first to assess the combined knowledge of healthcare students regarding honey as a prebiotic and yoghurt as a probiotic in the context of respiratory health. Although not directly comparable, it aligns with a previous Saudi study on healthcare professionals, where 64% believed probiotics had no adverse side effects, 81.2% acknowledged benefits for gut health, and fewer associated them with obesity (24.9%), allergies (28.5%), or cardiovascular disease (36.3%)31.

Regarding attitudes, 72% of participants in our study expressed a positive attitude toward probiotics, a more favorable result than the 52.9% reported in the Indonesian study9. Approximately 62% also expressed a willingness to learn more about probiotics. These findings suggest a growing interest and receptiveness among healthcare students, though further efforts are needed to address educational gaps and standardize content across programs.

The authors acknowledge limitations in their analyses, specifically convenience sampling, which may risk selection bias and limit the generalizability of their findings related to respiratory health practices. Survey link was distributed online without centralized tracking, it was not possible to determine the exact number of students reached and thus to calculate a precise response rate. Any future studies in this field need a stratified or probability-based sampling strategy to gather a more representative experience across health care institutions and student bodies.

Conclusion

The findings highlighted meaningful differences in knowledge and practices related to honey and yoghurt for respiratory health, influenced by gender, smoking status, and location. Females, non-smokers, and participants from the Eastern Province showed higher awareness and engagement. Statistical analysis confirmed gender differences, with females being more proactive in using natural remedies. Non-smokers favored evidence-based health solutions. Regional disparities highlight the need for targeted interventions. The findings suggest that education, lifestyle, and environment impact the use of honey and yoghurt for respiratory health. Future research should explore socioeconomic and behavioral factors, while public health initiatives can use digital platforms and workshops to improve knowledge and acceptance of natural health solutions.

What do we already know about this topic?

Probiotics are live beneficial bacteria that help maintain a healthy balance in the gut. Prebiotics are non-digestible food components that nourish these beneficial bacteria and support their growth.

How does your research contribute to the field?

Research on the knowledge and attitudes of students about prebiotics and probiotics contributes to the field of nutrition and healthcare by identifying gaps in education and understanding among future healthcare professionals. This can guide curriculum improvements, enhance the quality of patient care, and promote better health outcomes through informed recommendations on gut health and the use of probiotics and prebiotics in clinical settings.

What are your research’s implications for theory, practice, or policy?

The research highlights the need for integrating comprehensive education about probiotics and prebiotics into healthcare curricula, equipping future professionals with the knowledge to make informed dietary recommendations and improve patient care, especially regarding respiratory health. The study’s results can inform educational policies at universities and healthcare institutions, emphasizing the importance of evidence-based nutrition in training healthcare professionals, leading to better healthcare practices and improved public health outcomes.
