Intrapartum vitamin D levels a predictor of new-born anthropometry: An observational study in a teaching hospital of Eastern India
Authors: Rubina Saha, Pragya Kumar, Shamshad Ahmad, Mala Mahto, Monika Anant, Anantha A Padmanabhan, Alok Ranjan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMaternal vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy is a common issue and a significant global public health concern. Over the years, fetal growth and development have been studied, and it has been discovered that they are linked to vitamin D.Aim and Objective:To estimate the vitamin D levels of mothers and neonates and determine an association between mothers’ serum vitamin D status and neonate anthropometry.Methodology:The research was performed between January 2020 and April 2021. A total of 97 participants—singleton (mother and neonate pair) at the time of delivery—were enrolled using convenient sampling. Five millilitres each of cord blood and maternal venous blood were taken at the time of delivery. Chemiluminescent immunoassay was used to analyse the vitamin D levels. One researcher measured the birth weight, length, head circumference, and chest circumference.Results:Mothers had 87.6% vitamin D deficiency. About 50.5% of newborns were vitamin D deficient. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the vitamin D levels of the mother as a predictor of neonate anthropometry. It was found to be significant with neonate length and neonatal head circumference. Supplementation with vitamin D and calcium during pregnancy was also found to predict the birth weight of neonates.Conclusion:Insufficient vitamin D was reported in 92.8% of mothers. Neonatal vitamin D insufficiency was found to be 94.8%. Neonatal vitamin D levels are substantially correlated with those of their mothers. Antenatal supplements of calcium along with vitamin D were seen to significantly improve birth weight and maternal vitamin D levels, while the extra supplementation of vitamin D during pregnancy significantly improved neonatal length and head circumference.

Introduction

Vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy is one of the most persistent public health challenges.[1] Global estimates of vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy vary from 40–98%, while 15–84% are severely deficient.[2] In India, vitamin D deficiency in pregnant women was around 85%.[3] Since the mother is the only source of vitamin D for the fetus and newborn, maternal vitamin D deficiency is a significant risk factor for vitamin D deficiency in infancy. Vitamin D status in cord blood has shown a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency among newborns, ranging from 28% in Poland to over 80% in Germany and Thailand.[456789101112] Various studies show that maternal vitamin D status affects child health, including foetal and postnatal growth.[1314] Vitamin D deficiency in mothers has been associated with low birth weight.[1516] There is a biological plausibility that vitamin D has a role in extraskeletal pathways involving fetal programming and gene expression.[17] Postnatal challenges in infancy may also be affected by pathways mediated by birth size or neonatal vitamin D status, which correlates with maternal status.[18]

A study shows that maternal vitamin D deficiency affects anthropometric measurements offspring.[18]

Evidence shows that vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy can lower symptomatic hypocalcaemia rates in neonates, potentially justifying routine use. In addition, it may play a role in offspring development and reduce maternal complications.[19] Timely Vitamin D screening and supplementation can prevent adverse neonatal outcomes. A recent study in the Asia-Pacific revealed significant clinical knowledge gaps in Vitamin D practices among healthcare providers professionals.[20]

The lack of research on vitamin D and its connection to neonatal anthropometry in Eastern India and at the national level, given the region’s varied geography, ethnic makeup, and dietary practices, presents an opportunity for this study to examine the various facets of vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy and neonatal anthropometric measurements. This study aims to investigate the relationship between mothers’ vitamin D status and the anthropometry of newborns. The objectives of this study were to estimate the vitamin D levels in mothers and their newborns and to examine the association between the vitamin D status of mothers and neonate anthropometry.

Methodology

A cross-sectional cohort study was carried out between January 2020 and April 2021 with the approval of the Institutional Ethical Committee. This study was conducted at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Patna, an institute of national importance that is situated in the eastern region of India, where the participants were recruited. To determine the appropriate sample size for our study, we used OpenEpi and considered a vitamin D deficiency proportion of 97% ± 5 based on a previous study.[21] We aimed for a confidence level of 95% and an absolute error of 5%, resulting in a sample size of 45. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, we arrived at a final sample size of approximately 50.

In our study, 134 singleton mothers were included using a convenient sampling technique. Based on the participants’ history of pregnancy (diagnosed with gestational and chronic hypothyroidism, Diabetes Mellitus Type 1 and Type 2, Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, diagnosed cases of mental illness, diagnosed cases of carcinoma, twin pregnancy, and laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2), twenty-two participants were excluded from the study. The exclusion was based on the fact that various interactions between medicines or pathologies could potentially lead to changes in neonatal anthropometry. The medical records of the 112 participants included participants’ addresses, the height of the mother, gestational age, and the intake of 500 mg of calcium supplements recommended during pregnancy by the National Health Mission.[22] An intake of 250 IU of vitamin D, iron, and folic acid tablets recommended by the WHO recommendation was assessed retrospectively.[23] Few participants had received vitamin D supplementation, which was recommended by their doctors and is referred to as extra vitamin D supplementation, that is not advisable during the average antenatal period. The evaluation of participants’ skin colour and sun reaction was conducted using the Fitzpatrick Scale, which considers genetics, sun response, and tanning habits.[24] It ranged from Type 1 to Type 6, and the tanning habits of the mother were noted accordingly. Aseptic techniques were used to collect 5 mL each of maternal venous blood and neonatal cord blood. Serum was stored at -80°C in the biochemistry department. At the time of delivery, the birth weights of the neonates were recorded. We also measured the neonatal length and head circumference in accordance with the guidelines provided by the “INTERGROWTH-21st course on maternal, fetal and newborn growth monitoring”.[25] Calibrations of equipment were conducted regularly to ensure accuracy. To minimise potential bias, a single researcher recorded all the anthropometric measurements. We obtained three measurements for each variable and calculated the mean for the final analysis to reduce any potential variation.

Vitamin D levels were estimated using a chemiluminescent immunoassay in April 2021. A chemiluminescence-based immunoassay analyser (ADVIA CENTAUR XP, Siemens) was used for estimation. In this study, we have taken the cut-offs as per the institutional guidelines followed in the Department of Biochemistry for adults, namely Vitamin D Deficiency (0-20 ng/ml), Vitamin D Insufficiency (20-30 ng/ml), and Vitamin D Sufficiency (above 30 ng/ml), which were referenced in a few literature reviews.[2627] According to the Canadian Pediatric Society, serum 25(OH) D concentration below 10 ng/ml is used as a cut-off for deficiency and between 10 and 30 ng/ml as a cut-off for insufficiency in newborns.[28] Figure 1 explains the study procedure.

Flow diagram of study procedure

According to average Indian standards, the cutoff for birth weight was ≥2500 gms, birth length was ≥50 cms, and head circumference was ≥33.9 cms.

Statistical analysis

We used IBM SPSS (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Frequency distributions were employed for qualitative variables, and mean (SD)/median (IQR) were utilised for quantitative variables after confirming the normality of continuous data. Through regression analysis, we established the capacity of maternal vitamin D to predict neonatal anthropometry and weight, while accounting for both known and unknown confounding factors.

Results

A total of 134 participants were recruited after obtaining their consent over the study period, out of which 22 participants were excluded (Hepatitis B, Hypothyroidism, HIV, Breast Malignancy, Chronic Mental Illness). A total of 112 (mother’s venous blood and neonate’s cord blood) sample pairs were collected at the time of delivery from the obstetrics and gynaecology department and sent to the Department of Biochemistry for analysis. Fifteen samples were lost during transport and storage. Finally, 97 samples were estimated for vitamin D levels in both mother and neonate pairs. Discussion

This study aimed to determine the association between vitamin D levels in mothers and neonate anthropometry. Singleton mothers participated in the study with a mean age of 25.98 years ± 4.11, bearing an average period of gestation of 38.29 (±2.34) weeks. Various Sociodemographic, clinical parameters and neonatal outcomes were observed [as in Supplementary file Tables 1-7].Vitamin D deficiency, insufficiency, and sufficiency were 87.6%, 5.2% and 7.2% in mothers, respectively, which was comparable to a study conducted in North India on pregnant women by Sharma et al.,[29] in which vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy was 93.5 %. In a recent publication, 95% of the pregnant females were vitamin D deficient.[30] Similar findings were also reported in a few other studies.[3132]

It was found that 50.5% of the neonates were born with vitamin D deficiency, 44.3% of the neonates were found to be insufficient, and only 5.2% had a sufficient amount of vitamin D. Various Correlations were found to be significant [Supplementary file Table 8], and the correlation between the mother’s serum vitamin D and her neonate serum vitamin D was found to be strongly correlated (r = 0.875) with a level of significance of P < 0.001, which was comparable to a study done by Arora et al.[32] that showed similar results. The findings suggest that maternal vitamin D deficiency is highest in the Indian population, and screening for the deficiency should be one of the important early interventions in the primary care health set up, as it is a predictor of low neonatal vitamin D levels.

The study conducted by Agarwal et al.,[33] showed contrary results, with no significant correlation between cord and maternal blood (P = 0.82). A significant correlation was also found in a study by Wierzejska et al.[9] and other studies found a reverse correlation between maternal and neonatal vitamin D levels.[583435] Alp et al.[12] found that maternal 25(OH) D levels are equal to cord vitamin D levels.

In our study, a tool named the Fitzpatrick Scale, developed by Thomas B. Fitzpatrick in the year 1975, assessed the individuals’ skin colour - based on genetic disposition [Supplementary file Figure 1], reaction to sun exposure [Supplementary file Figure 2], and tanning habits [Supplementary file Figure 3], this tool scores the individuals into Type 1 to Type 6 of skin colour [Supplementary file Figure 4], and it was found that in our study the population belonged to Type 3 (Fair), Type 4 (Light Brown Skin), and Type 5 (Dark Brown Skin).[3637] We investigated the association between skin colour and vitamin D levels, finding it to be significant [Supplementary file Table 9 and 10]. The findings aligned with the study by Khalid et al.[38] and Richard et al.,[39] revealing that women with dark skin had significantly higher chances of vitamin D deficiency than those with lighter skin tones (OR 2.60; 95% CI 1.08–6.22; adjusted for age, season, vitamin D supplement use, body mass index, smoking, parity). We also examined if Vitamin D levels in maternal blood predict neonatal anthropometry. [Supplementary Table 11].

Our study concluded that Vitamin D levels in mothers’ blood did not significantly affect birth weight, even after adjusting for confounders. However, mothers taking antenatal calcium with vitamin D had babies with better birth weights 12 times more often than those who did not (OR = 12.111, 95%CI [2.214, 66.266]). This finding aligns with a Cochrane review indicating that women given prenatal vitamin D supplements were less likely to have babies with low birth weight.[1] This finding contrasted with Rodriguez et al.[40] Mojibian et al.[41] found no effect of vitamin D supplementation on fetal outcomes. Khalessi et al.,[42] Lee, S.S et al.,[43] and Gernand et al.[16] found a link between vitamin D levels and birth weight. Larger studies are needed to confirm the association between vitamin D status and low birth weight.

It was also seen that maternal vitamin D levels were found to be significant predictors of neonatal length at birth, where an increase in maternal vitamin D levels (OR = 1.244, 95% CI [1.071, 1.445]) and additional intake of vitamin D during pregnancy (OR = 14.969, 95% CI [3.147, 71.202]) resulted in better outcomes in the length of the babies at birth. Similar findings were observed by Pérez-López et al.[44] and Karras, S. N et al.[45]

Mothers with higher vitamin D levels had babies with larger head circumferences than those with low levels (OR = 1.180, 95%CI [1.040, 1.337]). In addition, mothers taking extra vitamin D doses did not influence head circumference compared to those not taking them (OR = 0.347, 95%ci [347, 1.016]). This finding aligns with a single-centre randomized clinical trial in Pakistan, which reported no significant outcomes on fetal growth from vitamin D supplementation.[18] Many studies support the idea that vitamin D levels increase fetal growth.[464748] However, research shows adverse reactions to vitamin D regarding neonate anthropometry measures. In a cohort study by Francis et al.,[49] inconsistent associations were found with neonate anthropometry. This analysis included neonates born after thirty-seven weeks, examining birth length and head circumference. Thus, this study highlights that early detection and adequate vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy in primary health care setting may improve newborn anthropometry outcomes. Initially, we aimed for a larger sample size and planned subgroup analyses for various skin colours, but due to Covid-19 restrictions, the study concluded with a smaller sample. Nonetheless, we found that mothers’ vitamin D levels and prenatal supplements significantly influence neonatal anthropometry.

Limitations

Although the study was conducted with the initial intention of a larger sample size, due to COVID-19, our study was restricted to a smaller sample size as AIIMS Patna was converted into a COVID-19 dedicated hospital from July 2020 to June 2021. The study can be generalized to Indian skin tone and not to mothers across the world.

Conclusion

During pregnancy, low vitamin D levels in mothers can lead to vitamin D deficiency in neonates. Low vitamin D levels negatively affect neonate length at birth and birth circumference. Antenatal calcium and vitamin D supplements improved birth weight, suggesting that early detection and appropriate intervention of vitamin D levels and supplementation during the antenatal check-up period in the primary care may lead to better neonatal anthropometry outcomes. However, antenatal calcium and vitamin D supplements had no significant role in changing the length at birth and head circumference, suggesting a possible inadequacy in the dosage. Nevertheless, those who took an extra dosage of vitamin D had a better outcome in neonatal length at birth, whereas low vitamin D levels negatively affected both neonate birth length and head circumference after adjusting for confounding factors.

Recommendations

Vitamin D should be recommended in mothers for better growth of neonate anthropometry, as this study found that neonates’ vitamin D status reflects that of their mothers.

Further studies, particularly clinical trials, should be planned to analyze the association of vitamin D levels in mothers with neonate anthropometry.
