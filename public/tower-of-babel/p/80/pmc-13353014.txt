The diagnostic value of multiple schemes of MRI standard features combined with risk factors and ultrasound for Placenta accreta spectrum
Authors: Yanmin Shi, Huiru Chu, Haoran Wang, Hongyang Han, Runhua Li, Qingqing Pan, Yalong Chen, Haoxin Shan, Yafen Wang, Li Liu, Xinguang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to identify the most diagnostically valuable features among the 11 MRI features of Placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) described in the joint consensus statement by the Society of Abdominal Radiology (SAR) and the European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR), and to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of MRI, risk factors, and ultrasound (US) used alone or in combination for prenatal PAS diagnosis.MethodsThis retrospective study included 199 pregnant women who delivered at Zhengzhou Central Hospital from November 2018 to December 2022. Standardized evaluations of Risk Factors, US, and MRI features were conducted, and subjects were divided into PAS (89 cases) and non-PAS groups (110 cases). Chi-square tests, logistic regression, and the Bootstrap method were used to identify MRI features with intergroup differences. Diagnostic scheme efficacy was compared using the area under the curve (AUC), and improvements were assessed using the net reclassification improvement index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement index (IDI). The final diagnosis of PAS was based on surgical and/or pathological findings according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) criteria.ResultsThree Risk Factors (caesarean delivery (CD) history, placenta previa, advanced maternal age) and three MRI features (T2-dark intraplacental bands, Loss of retroplacental T2-hypointense line, and Asymmetric shape/thickening of the placenta) showed statistical differences. The AUC for Risk Factors, US, and MRI alone was > 0.8. Combined schemes (MRI + US and MRI + US + Risk Factors) had higher efficacy, with MRI + US + Risk Factors demonstrating the highest efficacy.ConclusionThis study identifies three key standardized MRI features and three key risk factors to simplify clinical application. For high-risk pregnant women, a multimodal approach combining risk assessment, ultrasound, and standardized MRI features produced the highest diagnostic performance, but prospective external validation is required before broader clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09268-x.Keywords: Placenta accreta spectrum, Diagnosis, Risk factors, Ultrasound, MRI

Introduction

Placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) is diagnosed at birth when part of the placenta is abnormally attached to the uterine wall. PAS is no longer viewed as a result of aggressive trophoblast invasion but rather as a failure of uterine regulatory mechanisms, with the scar tissue providing a permissive environment for partial abnormal placenta attachment at delivery [1]. PAS can significantly increase the rate of massive bleeding, hysterectomy and adverse neonatal outcomes [2], posing a significant risk to both maternal and fetal health. Accurate prenatal diagnosis and intervention are crucial for improving pregnancy outcomes. However, it has been reported that 1/2 to 2/3 of cases fail to receive a definitive diagnosis before delivery, missing the optimal intervention opportunity [3]. Notably, if PAS is managed by a multidisciplinary team with accurate prenatal diagnosis, conservative uterine surgery can be performed to improve prognosis [4]. With the increasing caesarean delivery (CD) rate in China, the incidence of PAS has risen, highlighting the need for effective diagnosis and unified management practices [5].

Currently, the diagnosis of PAS primarily relies on risk factors assessment and ultrasound (US). Although US is widely used due to its convenience and low cost, its diagnostic value for uterine wall and parametrial invasion in PAS is limited. MRI is increasingly used as a confirmatory diagnostic tool in tertiary centers, providing comprehensive placental morphology evaluation [6]. However, there is no unified MRI diagnostic standard for PAS, and diagnostic accuracy depends on radiologists’ experience, which is unfavorable for individualized diagnosis and clinical decision-making. There is a wide heterogeneity in the use of PAS reference standards among the studies included in the systematic review [7]. Therefore, standardizing PAS evaluation and developing efficient diagnostic plans is essential for improving PAS prognosis.

In 2020, the Society of Abdominal Radiology(SAR) and the European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) published a joint consensus statement on MRI features of PAS, including 7 “recommended” and 4 “uncertain” features. This provided a unified reference for prenatal MRI examinations [8]. However, the complexity of these features hinders practical application. In this study, we analyzed the diagnostic value of standardized MRI features from the consensus statement to identify the most discriminative features. We also evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of MRI and US used alone or in combination, as well as risk factors, aiming to provide a comprehensive basis for accurate prenatal PAS diagnosis, optimize the clinical diagnostic pathway, and improve patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This retrospective study included 203 pregnant women from November 2018 to December 2022. All participants delivered at Zhengzhou Central Hospital with complete US and MRI. Three cases of multiple pregnancy and one stillbirth were excluded, ultimately 199 patients were included in the analysis. Inclusion criteria were: (1) clinical patients suspected of PAS or with a posteriorly located placenta; (2) the interval between MRI and ultrasound examinations is less than 2 weeks; (3) complete clinical records from the perinatal period were available. Exclusion criteria were: (1) twin or multiple pregnancies; (2) incomplete clinical or imaging data; (3) poor image quality or incomplete MRI sequences; (4) missing delivery outcomes or unavailable surgical/pathological confirmation due to loss to follow-up; (5) stillbirth. Ultimately 199 patients were included in the analysis (The flowchart of study subject selection is shown in Fig. 1). This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Zhengzhou Central Hospital Affiliated to Zhengzhou University (approval number: 202241). Given the retrospective nature of the study and anonymization of patient data, informed consent was waived.

The flowchart of study subject selection

Diagnosis and grouping of PAS

PAS diagnosis was based on intraoperative findings as the primary basis, combined with histological examination as a supplementary confirmation, and obstetricians made diagnoses according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) standards [9, 10]. According to the existence of PAS, the subjects were divided into PAS group and non-PAS group.

Assessment of risk factors

The risk factors of PAS were collected and recorded according to FIGO’s risk factors classification and types by consulting the medical records [11].

US scoring method of PAS

Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound examinations were performed using a GE Voluson E8 color Doppler ultrasound system. The US diagnosis was made according to the PAS US sign rating scale [12, 13] (Supplementary Tables 1 and Fig. 2). The final diagnosis was made after a preliminary scoring by a US diagnostician and review by a senior radiologist.

MRI image acquisition and evaluation of standard features

All subjects were examined using a Siemens Aera 1.5T magnetic resonance imaging system. During the examination, the pregnant woman lies in the supine position with her feet entering first, wearing earplugs for sound insulation, and allowed to breathe naturally. An 18-channel abdominal phased array coil was employed, centered around the placenta, and the scan covered the area from the diaphragm to the pubic symphysis. The scanning image locations and sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

MRI image data were read by three radiologists with over ten years of work experience (Dr. H, Dr. W, and Dr. L) under blind conditions (unaware of US findings, patient’s placental pathology and surgical results), according to the 11 commonly used MRI features and the recommended standardized terminology (hereinafter referred to as standardized features) in “SAR and ESUR Joint Consensus Statement for MR Imaging of Placenta Accreta Spectrum Disorders”. These 11 MRI features include 7 “recommended” (at least 80% agreement in favor) features (T2-dark intraplacental bands, Placental/uterine bulge, Loss of retroplacental T2-hypointense line, Myometrial thinning, Bladder wall interruption, Focal exophytic mass, Abnormal vascularization of placental bed) and 4 “uncertain” (i.e., consensus was not reached, with less than 80% agreement) features (Placental heterogeneity, Asymmetric shape/thickening of the placenta, Placental ischemic infarction, Abnormal intraplacental vascularity). Inter-observer agreement for MRI feature interpretation was evaluated using Fleiss’ Kappa statistics, disagreements were resolved through collective discussion to reach consensus. The results demonstrated excellent inter-observer agreement, with Kappa values between 0.741 and 0.880 across all features.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). Qualitative variables were described with frequency and percentage, the χ2 test, Yates’s correction or Fisher’s was used for comparision between groups according to the theoretical frequency; quantitative variables were described as mean ± standard deviation, compared with the t-test between groups. Logistic regression was used for multivariate analysis of the MRI features with inter-group differences detected by the χ2 test to identify statistically significant MRI indicators. Given the low frequency of some MRI features, leading to a large estimated standard error, the effect sizes were calculated by the bootstrap method following stepwise selection (1000 resamples were performed to ensure model stability).

To compare diagnostic schemes, logistic regression (PAS as outcome; MRI, US scores, risk factors as predictors) generated predictive probabilities for ROC analyses (gold standard: surgical/pathological results). We reported AUC with 95% CI and used the Youden index for optimal cutoffs. Delong test was used for the comparison of AUC. For schemes with unclear AUC differences, NRI and IDI measured model improvements. All tests were two-tailed (α = 0.05).

NRI (net reclassification improvement index, reflecting the improvement in patient classification by the new model compared with the old model); IDI (integrated discrimination improvement index, reflecting the overall improvement in the predictive probability of the model). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General information of the study subjects

A total of 199 participants were included in the study, with an average age of 31.75 ± 4.27 years (range: 19–44 years). The PAS group comprised 89 participants (placenta accreta 34(17.1), placenta increta 17(8.5), placenta percreta 38(19.1)) with an average age of 32.69 ± 4.12 years, while the non-PAS group had 110 participants with an average age of 30.99 ± 4.26 years. The difference in average age between the two groups is less than 2 years. There were no statistically significant differences in other general data (see Table 1).

Discussion

This study identified three standardized MRI features and three risk factors with significant differences between PAS and control groups. Clinicians can prioritize these high-discriminative features to simplify diagnosis. Moreover, integrating risk factors, US scoring, and these MRI features into a multimodal framework overcomes single-modality limitations. This confirms the combined strategy is most effective, providing a theoretical basis for optimizing PAS diagnostic protocols.

Diagnostic value of risk factors for PAS

Using risk factors alone for prenatal PAS diagnosis yielded an AUC of 0.835, confirming the value of this traditional approach. Compared with US and MRI, it is more cost-effective and convenient, making it suitable for screening high-risk groups.

Possible risk factors for PAS and related research

Any factor causing endometrial damage can induce PAS. Injurious procedures (such as CD, myomectomy, uterine curettage, and in vitro fertilization (IVF)) trigger decidual-myometrial defects, which disrupt the basal plate and expose spiral arteries to trophoblast cells, leading to placental invasion [14]. In uterine scar areas, deficient decidua and damaged myometrium allow deeper extravillous trophoblast invasion.

According to FIGO consensus guidelines on PAS, most of the 17 risk factors (5 categories) correlate with direct or indirect uterine damage. Epidemiologically, PAS prevalence rises with CD rates; prior CD history is the most immediate and significant risk factor. Recent research shows that women with CDs have higher risk of PAS. Women with 4 or more CDs face a 9-30-fold higher risk, and those aged ≥ 35 years have elevated risk, likely due to higher CD rates in this group [1]. A retrospective study involving 68,944 delivery cases showed that the overall CD rate at the research center was as high as 55.0% [15]. Such a heavy burden of CDs further suggests the potential for a significant increase in the risk of PAS at the population level.

In contrast, PAS is rare in patients with uterine malformations, adenomyosis, or submucosal fibroids but with no history of uterine injury, so these are not major risk factors [9]. IVF embryo transfer is recognized as an independent risk factor [16], but lacks clear pathophysiological evidence.

Key Risk factors identified in this study

This study detected significant inter-group differences in three factors: prior CD history, placenta previa, and advanced maternal age. These findings align with current PAS epidemiological characteristics, while no significant differences were observed for other risk factors.

Standardized MRI features and their diagnostic efficacy for PAS

Significance of standardized MRI features

MRI is highly accurate for PAS diagnosis, but previous inconsistent descriptions of MRI features hindered inter-professional communication. Standardizing terminology and unifying diagnostic criteria are therefore critical. This study adopted SAR/ESUR joint consensus terms for PAS-related MRI features, to promote their clinical application and academic exchange.

Application of standardized MRI features

Since the 2020 SAR/ESUR consensus proposed standardized PAS MRI features, relevant diagnostic studies have emerged. Thiravit S. et al. [17] reported three widely used prenatal diagnostic features (the intraplacental low-signal band on T2WI, placenta/uterine bulging, and myometrial thinning), corresponding to the 1st, 2nd, and 4th consensus-recommended features. Romeo V. et al. [18] confirmed the 1st (the intraplacental low-signal band on T2WI) and 4th (myometrial thinning) features as independently associated with PAS.

In this study, 10 of 11 standardized MRI features showed significant inter-group differences. Considering feature frequency variation and single-feature diagnostic limitations, three key features were selected via bootstrap resampling: T2-dark intraplacental bands (1st feature), Loss of retroplacental T2-hypointense line (3rd feature), and Asymmetric shape/thickening of the placenta (9th feature). Consistent with other studies, these three features have high clinical utility; prioritizing their evaluation improves PAS diagnostic efficiency.

Their diagnostic values: T2-dark intraplacental bands result from fibrin deposition caused by repeated placental hemorrhage or infarction (sensitivity 0.83, specificity 0.80) [19]; Placental/uterine bulge is a severe PAS feature (accuracy 90.3%, sensitivity 94.4%, specificity 84.6%) [17]; Myometrial thinning indicates placental hyperplasia (high sensitivity 97.4%, low specificity 36.4%) [8].

Factors affecting the assessment of standardized MRI features

The accuracy of MRI feature assessment for PAS may be influenced by gestational age, maternal age, examination posture, and the radiologist. As pregnancy progresses, the signal contrast between the placenta and myometrium weakens [20]. Due to the potential for extreme thinning of myometrium in the third trimester, this feature (Myometrial thinning) should not be used in isolation but rather in conjunction with other features of PAS disorders. Therefore, identifying the most valuable diagnostic markers is one of the key objectives of this study [8].

Bladder wall interruption (indistinct visualization or traction/compression changes on T2WI) is affected by maternal age, body position, and bladder distension, reducing diagnostic accuracy. Placental tissue in the bladder is a rare but distinctive feature of penetrative PAS [8], seen only in severe cases. Non-PAS patients may also have asymmetric placental thickening or shape changes (e.g., thickness differences between primary and secondary placentas), requiring multi-angle observation.

Diagnostic value of combined MRI, risk factors, and US for PAS

Risk factors are key for identifying PAS high-risk groups. US and MRI are non-invasive (ionizing radiation-free) assessment methods: US is the first-line prenatal screening tool for its convenience and cost-effectiveness, while MRI provides valuable supplementary information, with the two complementing each other.

MRI offers higher diagnostic accuracy for PAS than US, consistent with most studies (e.g., Liu Q et al. [21]). MRI combined with US also has unique advantages in evaluating posterior uterine wall placental invasion and predicting intraoperative blood loss [22]. The US sensitivity may be affected by placental location, abdominal wall thickness, and PAS feature typicality, combining with MRI compensates for these limitations to enhance overall efficacy.

This study confirmed that MRI + US + risk factors outperforms MRI + US. Maurea S et al. [23] constructed a similar diagnostic scheme with 91% sensitivity and 88% specificity, matching our combined model’s 91.0% sensitivity but with our specificity slightly higher (97.2%). Sparse data in certain feature categories may have a minor impact on coefficient estimates. Due to variations in ethnicity, regions, and reproductive policies, further clinical validation across diverse populations is needed to confirm the model’s practical applicability.

Value of NRI and IDI in evaluating diagnostic schemes

NRI and IDI address limitations of traditional model evaluation by focusing on subtle changes in prediction probability and classification accuracy, enabling more nuanced assessments. These metrics are increasingly applied in high-precision fields like medical diagnosis [24–26]. NRI assesses reclassification improvements, while IDI evaluates overall discriminative enhancement, together providing a comprehensive assessment of model performance.

In this study, we used NRI and IDI to compare three diagnostic schemes (MRI+Risk Factors, MRI + US, MRI + US+Risk Factors) where AUC differences were not significant. Although the differences in AUC between the groups were not statistically significant, the NRI suggested improved risk stratification when risk factors were added to MRI + US, indicating incremental value.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is the use of standardized imaging features, which enhance diagnostic reproducibility, reduce inter-radiologist variability, and eliminate terminology ambiguities—enhancing diagnostic traceability and result comparability for clinical and academic use. Additionally, incorporating NRI and IDI to compare multi-method diagnostic efficacy improves the reliability and precision of evaluations, providing valuable references for prenatal PAS diagnosis.

The present study has certain limitations. No graded PAS diagnosis (failing to distinguish placenta accreta, placenta increta, and placenta percreta). The significance of relevant MRI features varies among different subtypes: the volume and distribution of T2-dark intraplacental bands increase with the depth of placental invasion, while Loss of retroplacental T2-hypointense line mostly presents as focal interface interruption in accreta and extensive loss accompanied by severe manifestations (e.g., myometrial disruption, bladder invasion) in increta/percreta [27, 28]. Single-center data and a study population limited to patients who underwent both US and MRI (introducing potential selection bias and limiting generalizability, though the predominantly high-risk cohort’s homogeneity strengthens applicability to this group); small numbers of severe implantation subtypes; and lack of long-term follow-up (preventing analysis of imaging-outcome associations). Since not all patients underwent hysterectomy, histopathological confirmation was unavailable in some cases. The use of a mixed reference standard may therefore affect diagnostic accuracy estimates. This is common in PAS research and reflects current clinical practice.

Radiomics and machine learning offer new opportunities for PAS diagnosis [29], as radiomics can overcome visual assessment limitations. However, US (the first-line modality) is rarely included in radiomics studies, and existing PAS radiomics studies have heterogeneous methodological quality (generally below ideal levels) [7], requiring more robust studies.

Future research should validate findings in prospective, multi-center, large-sample cohorts. Key directions include developing a PAS severity grading system through further quantitative standardization of MRI features, exploring artificial intelligence and radiomics analysis for objective quantitative diagnosis, and linking optimized diagnostic models to surgical strategies and maternal-fetal outcomes.

Practical considerations and clinical adaptation

This study systematically evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of risk factors, ultrasound, and MRI (used alone or in combination) for PAS, and the results confirmed that the combined diagnostic protocol achieved superior diagnostic efficacy. It should be noted, however, that the diagnostic efficacy of both ultrasound and MRI is influenced by multiple factors including team experience and patients’ clinical conditions. In particular, MRI examinations impose high equipment requirements on medical institutions and are associated with increased costs, longer examination times, and stricter demands on patient cooperation. Therefore, even if MRI performs well in tertiary centers, the availability, expertise, and costs of MRI may still vary considerably across different institutions.

Given that this study did not include patient populations across different countries and regions, it is recommended that PAS management strategies be flexibly adjusted in light of the actual clinical diagnosis and treatment needs and disparities in resource allocation among different institutions, with the adoption of the following stepwise diagnostic and management algorithm: risk factors combined with ultrasound serves as the initial screening method, and a definitive ultrasound diagnosis can directly guide subsequent clinical management. In cases of an inconclusive ultrasound diagnosis, further complementary MRI examinations are recommended, the results of which can assist in determining the need for surgical intervention or referral to medical institutions with better surgical conditions. For high-risk populations, even if the results of MRI are negative or the diagnosis is inconclusive, adequate preoperative preparation remains essential. This preparation includes establishing a multidisciplinary team and ensuring adequate blood reserve, so as to reduce surgical risks. For medical institutions with limited resources that are unable to perform MRI examinations, active completion of preoperative preparation or timely referral to higher-level medical institutions is advised if PAS is highly suspected clinically, in order to avoid diagnostic and therapeutic delays.

In addition, both ultrasound and MRI examinations are highly dependent on the professional experience of operators; systematic professional training or the establishment of a centralized reporting system is therefore required to improve the consistency and reliability of examination results. Overall, the core management strategy for PAS remains based on accurate prenatal diagnosis, with individualized delivery and surgical plans formulated through multidisciplinary collaboration, so as to ultimately reduce the risks of maternal and fetal complications [15, 30, 31].

Conclusion

This study identifies three key standardized MRI features (T2-dark intraplacental bands, Loss of retroplacental T2-hypointense line, Asymmetric shape/thickening of the placenta) and three key risk factors (caesarean delivery history, placenta previa, advanced maternal age) to simplify clinical application. For high-risk pregnant women, a multimodal approach combining risk assessment, ultrasound, and standardized MRI features produced the highest diagnostic performance, but prospective external validation is required before broader clinical implementation.
