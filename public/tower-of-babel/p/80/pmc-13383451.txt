Cultural adaptation of compensatory cognitive training for Nigerian mental health services: a participatory approach balancing fidelity and contextual fit
Authors: Olabisi E. Oladipo, Abiodun O. Adewuya, Bolanle A. Ola, Adeniran Okewole, Ayantunde Ayankola, Arit Esangbedo, Azizat Lebimoyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive impairment substantially constrains functional recovery in psychosis, persisting despite adequate symptom control. Although Compensatory Cognitive Training (CCT) demonstrates efficacy in high-income contexts, its direct transfer to resource-constrained settings encounters multiple barriers: linguistic complexity, cultural distance, extended session requirements, and specialist workforce dependencies. This study documents a systematic cultural adaptation process through which CCT was reconfigured for Nigerian mental health services whilst preserving core therapeutic mechanisms.MethodsA ten-day participatory workshop convened 28 multistakeholder participants (clinicians, researchers, service users, implementation scientists, designers, and language specialists) organised into four interlinked committees. The adaptation drew upon layered theoretical frameworks, including the Ecological Validity Framework, Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications-Enhanced, and principles balancing fidelity against contextual responsiveness. Modified Delphi procedures (80% consensus threshold) governed decisions according to hierarchical criteria: preserve cognitive principles, enhance accessibility, ensure feasibility. Post-adaptation assessment employed a five-domain fidelity matrix examining core model preservation, content validity, structural integrity, cultural fit, and implementation feasibility. Independent stakeholder validation involved both service users (n = 10) and mental health providers (n = 13) who had not participated in the adaptation process.ResultsThe adapted twelve-session Nigerian CCT manuals retained all five cognitive domains and compensatory philosophy whilst incorporating extensive modifications: linguistic simplification with strategic Nigerian Pidgin integration, contextually grounded examples reflecting Nigerian daily life, session condensation (90–120 min to 60 min), complete visual redesign, and comprehensive task-shifting supports. Fidelity assessment suggested strong preservation of essential intervention features (overall M = 4.76/5.0). Stakeholder validation yielded exceptional ratings for language clarity (M = 4.9/5.0), cultural relevance (M = 4.9/5.0), and acceptability (M = 4.8/5.0). All service users expressed willingness to participate; 92% of providers endorsed sixty-minute session deliverability. Implementation barriers appeared predominantly structural, concerning space (54%) and staffing (46%), rather than content-related.ConclusionsTheory-informed participatory adaptation appears capable of preserving intervention fidelity whilst enhancing both cultural appropriateness and implementation feasibility. The adapted CCT-Nigeria materials demonstrate content readiness for pilot implementation, though successful scale-up will likely require health system strengthening that addresses infrastructure, workforce capacity, and resource constraints. This methodology may offer a replicable framework for complex intervention adaptation across resource-constrained settings.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00965-4.

Background

Cognitive impairment constitutes a central feature of psychotic disorders, with consequences for functional capacity extending beyond positive symptom management. Deficits affecting attention, memory, organisation, and problem-solving persist despite adequate control of hallucinations and delusions, substantially limiting occupational functioning, independent living, and quality of life [1, 2]. Antipsychotic medications demonstrate efficacy in managing psychotic symptoms yet typically exert minimal impact on neurocognitive deficits [3]. Cognitive performance links to functional outcomes with effect sizes often exceeding those for positive symptoms [4, 5], positioning cognitive rehabilitation as critical for recovery-oriented care.

Cognitive remediation approaches such as CCT have demonstrated strongest efficacy for psychotic disorders, particularly schizophrenia-spectrum conditions, where neurocognitive deficits are most pronounced and persistent. In Nigeria, the lifetime prevalence of schizophrenia is estimated at 0.4–0.7% [6], while psychotic disorders collectively form a significant component of the mental health burden. Overall, mental disorders are estimated to affect 20–30 million Nigerians [7], representing a substantial and largely unmet public health need.

Implementing cognitive rehabilitation in Nigeria presents distinctive challenges reflecting global mental health inequities. The treatment gap for mental disorders exceeds 85%, with fewer than 300 psychiatrists serving over 200 million people [8, 9]. Mental health services operate under high patient volumes, severely limited consultation time, and acute staffing shortages, with specialist care concentrated within few tertiary facilities [10]. Variable literacy levels, infrastructural instability, and extended family structures shaping support networks compound these constraints. First-episode psychosis typically emerges during late adolescence or early adulthood, a critical window for educational, vocational, and social transitions [11]. Cognitive deficits emerging during this period can derail trajectories with cascading lifetime consequences [12]. Nigerian mental health services have historically prioritised acute symptom management over functional recovery, leaving cognitive rehabilitation largely unavailable outside research settings.

Compensatory Cognitive Training (CCT) emphasises compensation rather than restoration of neurocognitive deficits [13]. Unlike drill-and-practice methods targeting underlying cognitive processes, the intervention teaches individuals to utilise external aids, structured routines, and problem-solving strategies circumventing impaired capacities. Five core domains (organisation, attention, memory, learning, and problem-solving) are addressed through integrated psychoeducation, behavioural rehearsal, and systematic strategy application. Randomised controlled trials suggest CCT improves functional capacity and everyday functioning, particularly when delivered in group formats [14, 15]. Meta-analytic findings demonstrate medium effects on functioning (Hedge’s g = 0.46) with evidence of durability at follow-up (Hedge’s g = 0.36), with longer treatment duration associated with larger effect sizes [16]. Original CCT manuals, however, assume specific literacy competencies, material resources, extended session durations, and specialist provider availability: assumptions that may not obtain in many low- and middle-income country contexts.

Direct transfer of CCT into resource-constrained settings encounters substantive challenges beyond translation. Contemporary frameworks conceptualise cultural adaptation as systematic, theory-driven processes balancing fidelity with contextual responsiveness [17, 18]. The Ecological Validity Framework (EVF) [19] highlights multiple dimensions through which interventions interact with local meaning systems, arguing that surface adaptations require complementation by deep structure modifications addressing cultural values and social-structural forces [20]. Within implementation science, adaptation and fidelity are recognised as interdependent, with ‘core components’ (essential intervention elements producing outcomes) distinguished from peripheral features more amenable to modification [21, 22]. The Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications-Enhanced (FRAME) provides standardised taxonomies facilitating transparency and replication [23], whilst World Health Organization guidance emphasises participatory co-creation, transparent documentation, and systematic consideration of implementation feasibility alongside fidelity preservation [24].

Empirical examples of structured cultural adaptation for cognitive rehabilitation in sub-Saharan Africa remain notably scarce. Existing African mental health research has predominantly focused on task-shifting psychotherapy for common mental disorders rather than interventions for severe mental illness [25]. The few documented cognitive interventions for psychosis in African settings typically describe implementation without systematic adaptation methodology or fidelity assessment [26]. This gap assumes significance given evidence that cognitive deficits may manifest differently across cultural contexts [27], and that neuropsychological assessment tools standardised on Western populations may inadequately capture functioning where educational systems and valued cognitive skills differ substantially [28].

The present study undertook systematic cultural adaptation of CCT for Nigerian mental health services, grounded in the premise that intervention transportability requires theory-informed, auditable processes preserving core mechanisms whilst enabling contextual resonance and implementation feasibility. Three objectives guided this work: (1) documenting a transparent, participatory adaptation process integrating theoretical frameworks with multistakeholder expertise and structured consensus; (2) characterising adapted CCT-Nigeria properties through systematic fidelity assessment, demonstrating how functional fidelity can be preserved whilst representational and delivery features are redesigned; and (3) contributing to implementation science debates regarding how evidence-based interventions can be responsibly mobilised in resource-constrained settings without compromising therapeutic integrity. Methodological innovations include culturally grounded problem-solving scenarios, comprehensive task-shifting supports, strategic Nigerian Pidgin integration, and dual-stream stakeholder validation combining service-user and provider perspectives. These contributions may inform future adaptation efforts beyond Nigeria in contexts confronting similar challenges of linguistic diversity, resource scarcity, specialist workforce shortages, and cultural distance from intervention origins.

Methods

Study design and conceptual framework

This study employed a participatory, theory-informed cultural adaptation design with convergent qualitative and quantitative validation components. The analytic focus was the adaptation process and its outputs, specifically the development of culturally and contextually adapted Therapist and Client Manuals for CCT suitable for routine Nigerian mental health services. Original CCT manuals functioned as reference standards for identifying non-negotiable cognitive mechanisms, session objectives, and pedagogical sequencing. Fidelity was conceptualised as preservation of these core functions rather than replication of surface features [21].

The adaptation process was guided by a layered theoretical architecture integrating principles from cultural adaptation theory and implementation science. At its core was a fidelity-adaptation balance framework treating fidelity as preservation of intervention mechanisms rather than strict adherence to original format. For CCT, these mechanisms comprised five cognitive domains (organisation, attention, memory, learning, and problem-solving) delivered through cumulative strategy instruction and application. The Ecological Validity Framework (EVF) informed adaptation across domains of language, instructional methods, metaphors, and contextual framing, emphasising intelligibility and relevance within everyday Nigerian contexts [19]. Participatory co-creation principles recognised distributed contextual expertise among clinicians, service users, and implementation actors, whilst implementation considerations were informed by constructs from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), particularly those related to inner-setting constraints, workforce capacity, and intervention characteristics [29]. The Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications-Enhanced (FRAME) provided standardised taxonomies for characterising adaptations [23].

The study comprised two sequential phases. Phase 1 involved a structured participatory adaptation workshop resulting in draft adapted manuals. Phase 2 consisted of independent post-adaptation stakeholder validation, conducted with both intended recipients and implementers. This separation was intentional, designed to strengthen credibility by reducing confirmation bias and enabling appraisal from multiple perspectives. Participatory adaptation workshop (Phase 1)

Cultural adaptation of CCT for Nigerian contexts occurred through a structured ten-day participatory workshop conducted in October 2025, spanning two consecutive working weeks (Monday to Friday across weeks one and two). Each working day comprised approximately six hours of structured committee activity, including plenary sessions, committee-specific adaptation work, cross-committee review, and consensus voting, amounting to approximately 60 h of intensive collaborative work. Scheduling the workshop as a block rather than distributing sessions over a longer period was a deliberate design choice intended to maintain participant engagement, ensure contextual continuity across sessions, and preserve the cumulative logic of the adaptation process. Whether this format is replicable in other settings will depend substantially on institutional capacity and stakeholder availability, a point addressed further in the Limitations section.

The workshop brought together 28 participants representing diverse disciplinary backgrounds and professional roles essential for comprehensive adaptation: clinical psychologists (n = 2), psychiatrists (n = 2), psychiatric nurses (n = 6), occupational therapists (n = 2), medical social workers (n = 3), neuroscience researchers (n = 2), implementation scientists (n = 2), community mental health practitioners (n = 3), service-user representatives with lived experience of psychosis (n = 2), graphic designers and illustrators (n = 2), and language specialists (n = 2). This composition ensured representation of clinical expertise, implementation knowledge, cultural insight, service-user perspectives, and creative skills necessary for visual adaptation.

A committee-based structure with four interlinked sub-committees addressed distinct but interconnected adaptation domains. The Technical and Cognitive Adaptation Committee (n = 8) evaluated each session’s cognitive principles, assessed preservation of core therapeutic mechanisms, and ensured that modifications maintain alignment with CCT’s theoretical foundations in compensatory strategies and errorless learning. The Cultural and Language Committee (n = 7) reviewed all content for cultural appropriateness, linguistic accessibility, metaphor relevance, and contextual fit with Nigerian daily life. The Implementation and Training Committee (n = 7) evaluated feasibility within existing service structures, assessed resource requirements, designed facilitator training approaches, and developed implementation support tools. The Validation and Fidelity Committee (n = 6) included independent reviewers who had not participated actively in adaptation decisions, tasked with systematic fidelity assessment against original CCT materials: a separation between adaptation architects and fidelity evaluators that reduced confirmation bias whilst ensuring critical external review.

All twelve CCT sessions underwent sequential review using structured adaptation logs documenting four elements for each proposed modification: original content from source manuals, adapted content proposed for CCT-Nigeria, rationale explaining why modification was necessary or beneficial, and decision regarding acceptance, rejection, or further refinement. This systematic documentation created an audit trail enabling retrospective analysis of adaptation logic and facilitating replication. The complete adaptation decision log, recording original content, proposed adaptation, rationale, and committee decision for all 45 discrete modifications across the twelve sessions, is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Adaptation decisions were guided by three hierarchically ordered criteria: (1) preservation of cognitive principles required that modifications maintain CCT’s core theoretical mechanisms; (2) cultural and linguistic accessibility mandated that language, examples, visual materials, and metaphors be comprehensible and relevant to target populations including individuals with limited formal education; and (3) implementation feasibility necessitated that session formats, duration, material requirements, and facilitator demands align with realities of Nigerian mental health service delivery.

Decision-making followed a modified Delphi process requiring 80% consensus across relevant committees before proposed modifications could be incorporated. Linguistic simplifications proposed by the Cultural and Language Committee required Technical and Cognitive Committee confirmation that modifications preserved conceptual accuracy. This cross-committee review process occurred over multiple iterative rounds, with over 92% of proposals achieving required consensus following first or second-round review. The small minority requiring third-round discussion primarily involved conflicts between accessibility and fidelity, ultimately resolved through compromise solutions satisfying both priorities.

Fidelity and validity assessment

The Validation Committee developed a comprehensive five-domain matrix for quantitative fidelity assessment, ensuring adapted materials retained essential CCT features whilst documenting areas of intentional divergence. The matrix assessed: fidelity to core CCT model (preservation of compensatory strategies, errorless learning principles, graduated practice, and generalisation emphasis); content validity (retention of all essential cognitive domains with adequate coverage depth); structural integrity (maintenance of pedagogical sequencing, cumulative skill-building, adequate practice opportunities, and coherent session flow); cultural and linguistic fit (appropriateness of language complexity, cultural relevance of examples, accessibility for low-literacy populations, and visual representation accuracy); and implementation feasibility (realistic session duration, material availability, facilitator skill requirements, and compatibility with existing service delivery models). Each domain employed a five-point Likert scale (1 = poor; 2 = fair; 3 = acceptable; 4 = good; 5 = excellent).

Each of the twelve adapted sessions received independent review by at least two raters from different workshop committees who had not been primary architects of adaptations for that particular session, minimising confirmation bias. Raters provided quantitative scores across all five domains alongside qualitative justifications identifying specific strengths, flagging potential concerns, and suggesting refinements where appropriate. Discrepancies of two or more scale points between raters triggered structured discussion until consensus emerged. Fidelity assessment proved iterative: initial ratings identified areas requiring refinement, which prompted targeted revisions followed by re-assessment until satisfactory scores emerged.

Stakeholder validation (Phase 2)

Following workshop completion, adapted materials underwent independent validation by 23 stakeholders who had not participated in the adaptation workshop, providing external perspectives on accessibility, cultural appropriateness, and implementation feasibility. Service-user validation involved ten adults receiving care for psychosis, varying in age, gender, and primary language (English, Yoruba, Igbo). Provider validation involved thirteen mental health professionals (eight social workers and five psychiatric nurses), all with experience delivering psychosocial interventions but no prior involvement in adaptation activities.

Validation occurred in December 2025, with each stakeholder receiving complete sets of adapted Client and Therapist Manuals for independent review over one week. Stakeholders then completed structured questionnaires with both quantitative and qualitative components, using five-point Likert scales alongside open-ended questions. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics. Qualitative responses underwent thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s reflexive approach [30]. Two researchers independently familiarised themselves with the data, generated initial codes, and developed a codebook through iterative discussion. The codebook (provided as Supplementary File 2) contained 14 codes organised under four overarching themes. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion with a third reviewer. Themes were reviewed, refined, and integrated into the results narrative with illustrative quotations.

Ethical considerations

The study involved no clinical intervention and collected no personal health outcome data. Participation in the adaptation workshop and validation activities was voluntary with written informed consent obtained from all participants. Ethical approval was granted by the Lagos State University Teaching Hospitals Health Research Ethics Committee.

Results

The cultural adaptation process resulted in harmonised Nigerian editions of the Compensatory Cognitive Training Therapist and Client Manuals comprising twelve structured group sessions. The adapted manuals retained the full scope of the source intervention: all session numbers, targeted cognitive domains, and cumulative sequencing of skills were preserved. Adaptations clustered into five interrelated domains: linguistic and instructional clarity, contextual framing of examples and practice activities, session structure and visual representation, delivery supports for implementation feasibility, and systematic fidelity preservation validated through independent stakeholder appraisal. Linguistic and conceptual adaptation

Technical terminology underwent systematic simplification to enhance accessibility whilst preserving conceptual accuracy through conceptual translation rather than simple linguistic substitution. Complex neurocognitive terms were rendered in plain language reflecting observable behaviours: ‘prospective memory’ became ‘remembering things on time’; ‘self-talk’ transformed into ‘talking sense to yourself’; ‘cognitive flexibility’ became ‘having a Plan B’; ‘chunking’ became ‘break it down small small’; ‘errorless learning’ was explained as ‘practicing the right way from the start.’ These modifications maintained theoretical fidelity, with each simplified term preserving the underlying cognitive process whilst eliminating comprehension barriers for participants with limited formal education.

Session titles were revised to prioritise functional clarity over theoretical precision. Original titles employing cognitive psychology terminology were replaced with action-oriented alternatives directly communicating session purposes: Session 2’s ‘Prospective Memory (Calendars, Lists, Linking Tasks)’ became ‘Remember Things on Time’; Session 4’s ‘Conversational Attention’ became ‘Listen Well’; Session 9’s ‘Cognitive Flexibility’ transformed into ‘Have a Plan B.’

Nigerian Pidgin phrases were strategically incorporated where linguistic creativity could enhance engagement, reinforce key concepts, or reduce linguistic formality, following consultation with language specialists and service users. Representative integrations included: ‘Small small, brain go strong’ to convey progressive improvement; ‘No forget am o!’ as emphatic reminder; ‘You fit do am’ for encouragement; ‘E don finish’ celebrating completion; ‘Make we try again’ normalising repetition; ‘You sabi am well well’ for mastery recognition. These Pidgin elements appeared in Client Manual margins, facilitator scripts, and oral instructions, creating linguistic bridges between formal English content and vernacular communication whilst maintaining professional document standards. Glossaries provided translations of key terms into Yoruba, Igbo, and Hausa, enabling facilitators to code-switch appropriately based on participant language preferences.

To clarify the linguistic architecture of the adapted materials: both the Therapist and Client Manuals are written primarily in standard English, which was judged the most appropriate base language given its role as the official medium of education, healthcare documentation, and professional communication in Nigeria. Nigerian Pidgin English functions not as a parallel translation but as a supplementary register, strategically embedded in facilitator scripts, Client Manual margin callout boxes, and oral delivery prompts to reduce linguistic formality and strengthen facilitator-client rapport. Multilingual glossaries providing Yoruba, Igbo, and Hausa equivalents of key terms were appended to both manuals to support facilitators working across Nigeria’s linguistically diverse communities.

Contextual and cultural grounding

Examples were systematically replaced with authentic Nigerian contexts reflecting daily realities across urban and peri-urban environments. Memory prompts were reconfigured from Western domestic arrangements to Nigerian household patterns: ‘post-it notes on refrigerator’ became ‘paper near prayer mat or food container’; ‘alarm on bedside table’ transformed into ‘phone alarm or church/mosque bell.’ Communication examples were recalibrated to Nigerian social networks: ‘call your boss’ became ‘call your cousin after church or mosque’; ‘email colleague’ changed to ‘send WhatsApp message to prayer group.’

Transport scenarios incorporated Nigeria’s distinctive mobility ecosystem: danfo (commercial yellow minibuses), okada (motorcycle taxis), keke (motorised tricycles), and molue (larger commercial buses), enabling problem-solving practice around familiar transportation challenges. Daily living scenarios referenced immediately recognisable contexts: NEPA power outages requiring memory aids, market trading involving currency calculations, navigating prayer times or church schedules, fetching water during supply disruptions, and managing transport disruptions during rainy season. Characters throughout manuals received Nigerian names reflecting ethnic diversity: Ngozi, Chika, Ada (Igbo); Yusuf, Musa, Fatima (Hausa/Muslim); Bola, Ade, Tunde (Yoruba), creating cultural familiarity whilst avoiding ethnic favouritism through balanced distribution.

Structural and visual adaptation

The original CCT session structure comprising multiple discrete steps over 90–120 min was condensed to six integrated steps deliverable within 60 min, driven by implementation realities where consultation times rarely exceed one hour. The adapted structure integrated: Welcome/Review/Check-in (5–10 min); Introduce Goals/Concept (5–10 min); Teach/Demonstrate Skill (15–20 min); Practice with Integrated Feedback (20–25 min); Plan Daily Use (5–10 min); and Wrap-up/Homework (5 min). This preserved all essential pedagogical components (psychoeducation, modelling, rehearsal, feedback, and homework) whilst eliminating redundancy and enhancing temporal efficiency. Session-by-session testing confirmed trained facilitators could deliver all essential content within 60 min without rushing.

All Client Manual illustrations underwent complete redesign by Nigerian graphic designers depicting Nigerian people, clothing, settings, and cultural contexts. Characters were rendered with skin tones, facial features, and hairstyles reflecting diversity. Clothing incorporated Nigerian styles: gele (head wraps), iro and buba (wrapper and blouse), agbada (flowing robes). Settings depicted recognisable environments: outdoor markets, residential compounds, religious buildings, and transport scenes. Worksheets featured enhanced accessibility provisions: larger print, increased line spacing, visual icons supplementing text instructions, reduced text density, and colour-coding distinguishing exercise sections. Problem Cards, an innovation not present in original CCT materials, were developed for Sessions 9–10 comprising twelve scenario-based prompts across six thematic categories (Daily Living, Health Management, Work/School, Family Relationships, Transport, Flexibility).

Fidelity assessment outcomes

Quantitative fidelity assessment demonstrated excellent preservation of core CCT principles and pedagogical integrity. Overall fidelity achieved M = 4.76/5.0 (SD = 0.18), indicating strong preservation of essential features. Detailed fidelity and stakeholder validation ratings are presented in Table 4. Domain-specific scores revealed consistency: Fidelity to Core Model (M = 4.8, SD = 0.15); Content Validity (M = 4.8, SD = 0.16); Structural Integrity (M = 4.6, SD = 0.22), the lowest score reflecting temporal compression concerns whilst acknowledging successful pedagogical preservation; Cultural and Linguistic Fit (M = 4.8, SD = 0.14); and Implementation Feasibility (M = 4.8, SD = 0.17). All twelve sessions achieved individual means exceeding 4.6, with Sessions 1, 2, 5, and 11 receiving highest ratings (M ≥ 4.85).

Stakeholder validation outcomes

Independent stakeholder validation yielded overwhelmingly positive accessibility and acceptability ratings. Overall accessibility achieved M = 4.8/5.0 (SD = 0.19) across service users and providers, with service users rating M = 4.7 (SD = 0.22) and providers M = 4.8 (SD = 0.17).

Language clarity received exceptional endorsement (M = 4.9/5.0, SD = 0.08), with 21 of 23 stakeholders rating comprehensibility as very clear or extremely clear. Service users emphasised: “The language is clear and straightforward” and “Even people who did not go far in school will understand.” Cultural relevance achieved equally strong endorsement (M = 4.9/5.0, SD = 0.10). Service users validated: “The scenarios are typical of everyday living in Nigeria” and “Examples reflect Nigerian daily life.”

Nigerian Pidgin integration received robust endorsement: 72% rated it very helpful, 28% somewhat helpful, with none considering it unhelpful. Service users valued engagement-enhancing properties, noting it “adds some element of humour making it more interesting and enjoyable.” Providers observed: “The use of local phrases especially pidgin creates a more comfortable space for individuals to express themselves freely and authentically”, suggesting Pidgin may reduce power differentials that might otherwise inhibit participation.

Perceived usefulness received strong validation (M = 4.8/5.0) with all stakeholders affirming skills addressed practically relevant challenges. Service users specifically requested Problem Cards be discussed in detail: “Please I would like answers/discussion of the CCT problem cards. They show my daily struggles”, indicating culturally grounded scenarios resonated strongly.

Discussion

Principal findings and contributions

This study documents systematic cultural adaptation of CCT for Nigerian mental health contexts through participatory co-creation, multi-layered fidelity assessment, and independent stakeholder validation. The methodological approach demonstrates that rigorous, theory-informed adaptation can achieve dual objectives often construed as competing: maintaining intervention fidelity whilst enhancing cultural responsiveness, linguistic accessibility, and implementation feasibility. Quantitative fidelity assessment confirmed preservation of CCT’s essential features (M = 4.76/5.0), whilst stakeholder validation documented exceptional cultural appropriateness (M = 4.9/5.0) and strong implementation readiness (92% feasibility endorsement).

Three methodological innovations merit emphasis. First, participatory co-creation incorporated explicit separation between adaptation architects and fidelity validators, reducing confirmation bias. Second, fidelity was assessed post-adaptation as a property of adapted materials rather than through comparative analysis, operationalising contemporary implementation science frameworks distinguishing function from form [23, 29, 31]. Third, dual-stream stakeholder validation integrated experiential appraisal from service users with implementation perspectives from providers, enabling convergent validation. These findings challenge the perceived dichotomy between fidelity and adaptation, demonstrating their synergistic relationship when adaptation proceeds through disciplined, theory-informed processes.

Fidelity as function not form

Exceptional fidelity preservation despite substantial adaptation provides empirical support for contemporary frameworks distinguishing intervention ‘core components’ from peripheral features [22]. That all five cognitive domains, compensatory emphasis, errorless learning principles, and pedagogical sequencing remained intact (Fidelity to Core Model M = 4.8/5.0) whilst language, examples, visual materials, and structural format underwent extensive modification suggests CCT’s therapeutic mechanisms operate independently of their original cultural packaging. This dissociability has important implications for intervention transportability. Cognitive rehabilitation targeting universal neurocognitive processes through compensatory strategies may prove more amenable to cultural adaptation than psychotherapies embedding culturally specific assumptions about self, emotions, or interpersonal relationships [32]. However, this claim requires empirical validation through outcome studies demonstrating comparable effects to original formulations.

The structural condensation from 90 to 120 min to 60 min whilst preserving pedagogical integrity (Structural Integrity M = 4.6/5.0) represents a potentially significant implementation innovation. Time constraints constitute a primary barrier to intervention adoption in resource-limited settings where competing demands, high patient volumes, and limited staffing preclude extended consultations [10, 33, 34]. The 92% feasibility endorsement from providers familiar with Nigerian service realities provides encouraging preliminary evidence that this temporal compression aligns with implementation contexts. Fidelity was demonstrated through preservation of function rather than replication of form, positioning adaptation as essential implementation strategy enabling intervention enactment under real-world constraints [23].

Representational transformation and accessibility

Extensive linguistic simplification and visual scaffolding underscore the importance of representational transformation in enhancing cognitive accessibility [35]. By shifting explanatory burden from text-heavy instruction to facilitated interaction, modelling, and visual cues, adapted manuals redistributed cognitive load aligning with participants’ literacy levels and attentional capacity. Service-user validation confirmed this design logic with participants consistently reporting understandable language, clear instructions, and emotionally non-threatening materials. The absence of reported shame, distress, or confusion suggests representational transformation supported not only comprehension but also psychological safety and dignity.

Contextual grounding emerged as a key strength. Situating cognitive strategies within everyday Nigerian routines enhanced relevance and facilitated skill transfer. The service-user observation that Problem Cards ‘show my daily struggles’ validates the ecological validity of scenario-based practice grounded in authentic challenges, aligning with the EVF’s emphasis on matching intervention content to cultural meaning systems and social-structural realities [18, 19].

Qualitative data from the adaptation workshop itself further illuminate the deliberative logic underpinning specific representational choices. A community mental health worker who participated in the contextualisation process observed that “once we used market and church stories, everyone understood”, capturing how familiar scenario substitution transformed abstract cognitive exercises into recognisable problem-solving situations. A therapist participant noted that the shift from ‘self-talk’ to ‘talking sense to yourself’ was initially contested within the Technical and Cognitive Committee on theoretical grounds, but was ultimately endorsed after service-user representatives confirmed that the plain-language version conveyed the underlying concept more accurately for participants without a psychology background. These moments of productive disagreement (between clinical precision and communicative accessibility) represent the core dialectic of participatory adaptation and illustrate that qualitative deliberation, not merely quantitative consensus voting, shaped the final materials. A service-user representative captured the broader significance of the process: “This was not just a workshop, it was a shared building of something Nigerian”, characterising the collective authorship as co-producing local knowledge rather than importing external models.

Nigerian Pidgin integration (72% rating ‘very helpful,’ none finding it unhelpful) represents a potentially important linguistic innovation. The finding that Pidgin ‘creates a more comfortable space for individuals to express themselves freely and authentically’ suggests strategic vernacular integration may reduce communicative distance between providers and service users, particularly for populations with limited English fluency. The nuanced deployment (Pidgin in margins, oral instructions, and facilitator scripts rather than formal written instructions) demonstrates how engagement benefits can be balanced with professional presentation standards.

Task-shifting and implementation feasibility

Task-shifting potential emerged as critical given Nigeria’s severe specialist shortages (fewer than 300 psychiatrists serving 200 million people) [8]. Providers’ 85% endorsement of social worker and nurse delivery, coupled with universal affirmation (100%) of facilitator script utility, suggests comprehensive manualisation enabling step-by-step guidance may facilitate task-sharing more effectively than interventions requiring improvisation or advanced clinical judgment [25, 36]. Stakeholders identified variable supervision needs across sessions, with Sessions 5, 9–10, 11, and 12 most frequently flagged, suggesting tiered supervision models with specialist availability for complex content whilst routine delivery could occur independently. A recommended training curriculum and supervision protocol for task-shifted delivery is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

The critical finding that implementation barriers were predominantly structural, including space limitations (54%), staffing shortages (46%), resource constraints (38%), rather than content-related suggests CCT-Nigeria has achieved content readiness whilst highlighting that successful scale-up will require health system strengthening addressing infrastructure deficits, workforce development, and resource allocation rather than further manual refinement. This pattern reflects broader challenges in low- and middle-income country mental health service delivery where intervention effectiveness depends not only on clinical quality but on fundamental health system capacity [37]. Future implementation efforts should anticipate that effectiveness trials must account for health system variables alongside intervention characteristics.

Methodological contributions to adaptation science

By foregrounding adaptation as an analytic object, this study contributes to calls for greater transparency in reporting cultural adaptation [21]. The use of explicit decision rules, structured consensus procedures, fidelity assessment, and dual-stream validation demonstrates how adaptation processes can be documented systematically. The staged approach of participatory adaptation followed by independent validation offers a replicable model balancing inclusivity with methodological discipline.

Methodological innovations including culturally grounded Problem Cards, comprehensive facilitator scripts enabling task-shifting, temporal compression maintaining pedagogical integrity, and strategic Pidgin integration may inform future adaptation efforts extending to other sub-Saharan African contexts sharing similar implementation challenges. Systematic documentation through structured adaptation logs provides an audit trail enabling accountability and knowledge transfer.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the study documented intervention readiness but did not assess clinical or functional outcomes. Effectiveness requires prospective controlled trials demonstrating that adapted CCT improves functional outcomes for Nigerian young people experiencing psychosis. Second, validation occurred with stakeholders from southwestern Nigeria; regional variation in language, culture, and service organisation may necessitate additional modifications for northern or southeastern Nigerian regions or other sub-Saharan African contexts. Third, service-user validation involved only 10 participants; broader consultation including varied demographic groups would strengthen acceptability evidence.

Fourth, the intensive two-week block format (ten consecutive working days totalling approximately 60 h) enabled rapid progress and preserved contextual continuity but carries significant resource implications for institutions considering replication. Convening 28 participants across multiple disciplines for a concentrated period requires substantial institutional commitment: protected staff time, release from clinical duties, venue provision, catering, and in some cases participant transport. These demands may prove prohibitive for under-resourced settings, which is precisely the context where adaptations of this kind are most needed. Investigators planning comparable exercises might explore whether a distributed model, spreading equivalent working hours across several months with asynchronous review periods, achieves comparable adaptation quality at lower logistical cost. Additionally, block workshops may generate social momentum that accelerates consensus without allowing sufficient independent reflection; some of the minority of proposals requiring third-round discussion may have benefited from longer deliberation periods. Future adaptation science would benefit from systematic comparison of concentrated versus distributed workshop formats on both process quality and output fidelity.

Fifth, family member and caregiver perspectives were not incorporated; future iterations should include caregiver consultation given extended family significance in Nigerian contexts. Sixth, stakeholder feedback identified potentially valuable additions not currently included (standardised cognitive assessment tools, functional outcome questionnaires, family psychoeducation components, and structured follow-up protocols) suggesting opportunities for future enhancement. Seventh, cost-effectiveness analysis was not undertaken; empirical evaluation comparing costs against outcomes will be essential for policy decisions about resource allocation and scale-up investment. Finally, full translation into major Nigerian languages beyond glossaries was beyond scope; for participants with primary fluency in Yoruba, Igbo, or Hausa, the intervention would still require significant verbal mediation by facilitators.

Future directions and conclusion

Immediate next steps include pilot testing with embedded process evaluation assessing supervision requirements, non-specialist competency development, and session duration sustainability. Effectiveness evaluation through pragmatic randomised controlled trials comparing adapted CCT-Nigeria against usual care is essential for determining whether functional outcome improvements comparable to high-income settings can be achieved. Cost-effectiveness analysis will inform resource allocation decisions, whilst further linguistic translation and rural adaptation represent important expansion opportunities.

The adaptation of CCT for young adults with psychosis in Nigeria demonstrates that principled modification of neuropsychological interventions can preserve functional fidelity whilst enabling contextual responsiveness and implementation feasibility. By documenting a systematic, theory-informed process integrating participatory co-creation, explicit decision rules, fidelity assessment, and dual-perspective validation, the study provides both an implementation-ready intervention for Nigerian mental health services and a replicable methodological model for complex intervention adaptation in resource-constrained settings. The findings challenge false dichotomies between fidelity and flexibility, demonstrating instead that rigorous adaptation is scientifically necessary to preserve mechanisms, ethically required to ensure accessibility and dignity, and practically essential to enable implementation. Planned evaluation will determine whether these materials fulfil their promise of enhancing cognitive rehabilitation access and outcomes for young Nigerians experiencing psychosis, contributing ultimately toward a global neuropsychology defined not by direct importation of Western models but by rigorous dialogue between scientific evidence and local lived experience.
