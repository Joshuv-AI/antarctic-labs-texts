Translation adaptation of TCM (Traditional Chinese Medicine) terminology for speakers of other cultures: features of cultural connotation
Authors: Kuo Chen, Jiadong Hu, Irina Karabulatova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the translation of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), it is crucial to preserve the authenticity of its philosophical, historical, and linguistic characteristics. This study aims to conduct a comprehensive analysis of translation strategies for adapting TCM terminology to foreign cultural contexts, based on the classical text The Emperor”s Canon of Eighty-One Difficult Issues.MethodsA comparative analysis was performed to evaluate the effectiveness of foreignization and domestication strategies in translating TCM terminology into Russian, using an experimental study involving 84 respondents (42 in each group). The Student’s t-test was employed to assess medical accuracy, equivalence, pragmatic value, terminological precision, and cultural specificity.ResultsThe results revealed a statistically significant advantage of the domestication strategy across most metrics, particularly in equivalence (p = 0.001) and pragmatic value (p = 0.008), where domestication achieved higher mean scores (4.12 and 4.03) compared to foreignization (3.48 and 3.55). Thus, it was established that domestication facilitates better adaptation of complex Chinese medical concepts for target audiences while maintaining sufficient medical accuracy. This is supported by higher scores in overcoming cultural barriers (48.8% versus 22.0%) and ensuring terminological precision (3.88 versus 3.41), making it a more effective strategy for translating medical terminology from Chinese into Russian.ConclusionsThe practical significance of this study lies in determining the effectiveness of translation approaches for TCM terminology into Russian through experimental research. These findings can be applied in the work of medical translators, the development of educational materials on Chinese medicine, and the creation of methodological guidelines for medical text translation. Consequently, the results hold the potential to improve the quality of educational materials on TCM and enhance intercultural medical communication.Keywords: Health care terms, Linguoculturology, Medical text translation, Traditional chinese medicine, Translation equivalence

Introduction

The evolution of terminological standards in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has undergone substantial transformation, transitioning from outdated approaches to contemporary methods that integrate cultural specificities and professional requirements [1]. A foundational feature of TCM is its holistic approach and dialectical understanding of human health. Diagnosis is based on studying the “language of the body,” which reflects the interconnectedness of various bodily functions. UNESCO has recognized TCM as an essential component of China’s intangible cultural heritage [2].

The translation of TCM terminology, an area that requires a hermeneutic approach, goes beyond mere linguistic translation and involves the epistemic negotiation of different cultural paradigms. Each is embedded in different ontological and semiotic structures (a fundamental premise that emphasises the divergence between “biomedical empiricism” and the “symbolic-metaphorical logic” of TCM). The need for interpretation is explained by the inherent metaphorical structure of classical TCM texts – physiological processes are conceptualised through a network of relational analogies (the “state and vassal” model), embedding biomedical phenomena in socio-political schemas that defy direct translational equivalence [3]. The presence of “polysemantic lexemes” in the Wenyan corpus (a linguistic register characterised by extreme compression and a high degree of contextual dependence) necessitates an adaptive strategy. It alternates between “semantic approximation” and “conceptual amplification” – to ensure the fidelity and comprehensibility of the translation.

The translation of TCM texts is a comprehensive, interdisciplinary process requiring a nuanced understanding of terminological, cultural, and linguistic intricacies. A critical aspect of translation work is the selection of precise equivalents for medical terminology. Methods such as semantic calquing and the combination of loanwords with generic terms are frequently employed [4]. Structural adaptation of texts, including sentence merging or splitting, enhances information comprehension while maintaining the scientific style of presentation [5]. The TCM terminological system is characterized by a distinct structure, often comprising two- or four-word terms. Adequate translation of these terms requires more than conventional techniques, as the terminology is deeply rooted in the historical, philosophical, and humanistic context of Chinese culture [6].

Modern technologies and artificial intelligence (AI) have significantly altered approaches to translating TCM texts. Computer-Assisted Translation (CAT) systems have become indispensable tools for professional translators, enabling the creation of translation memory databases that ensure consistency while preserving contextual accuracy [7]. Innovative methods, such as TCM Domain Adaptation (TCMDA), leverage domain-oriented approaches and effective pre-training using specialized data corpora, representing a significant advance in language model adaptation for TCM [8].

The use of “affective intelligence” in computational linguistics (a paradigm studied through “neuromodulation architectures” designed to model psycho-emotional processing in discourse) opens up new ways to improve the accuracy of AI translation – to identify the “affective-conceptual correlates” inherent in TCM-based texts [9]. The ontological distinction of “affective cognition” in natural and artificial systems points to a persisting epistemological gulf: “algorithmic hermeneutics” remains subordinated to the “phenomenological intensionality” of the human translator (limiting the scope of application of “deep semantic learning” in culturally embedded medical discourses). The practice of TCM translation involves “methodological pluralism” – an approach that balances lexical preservation and “semantic modulation”, preserves historical continuity and promotes cross-cultural accessibility.

Translation of TCM texts thus requires preserving the cultural authenticity and medical traditions of the original while adapting them for English-speaking audiences. A balanced approach ensures the quality transmission of medical knowledge and promotes intercultural dialogue [10]. The translation of TCM terminology is viewed as a complex hermeneutic process, where the concept of “diversity” serves as a dialectical complement to standardization. This strategy entails a dynamic interplay between unification and variability, accounting for epistemological, cultural, and linguistic nuances [11].

Literature review

According to Shuang [12], trust in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and demand for its treatment methods have shown consistent growth across various countries, gradually establishing TCM as an integral component of the global medical system. A bibliometric analysis conducted by Wang and Chen [13], encompassing publications on TCM translation between 1992 and 2020, provides a comprehensive understanding of current research in the field. The findings reveal not only a quantitative increase in publications but also a qualitative expansion of research themes, especially regarding cultural aspects of translation.

Translation of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) terminology – the interaction of “ethnolinguistic codes” and “philosophical paradigms”, textual tier. Metaphorical euphemisms embedded in “sacralised medical discourse” do not lend themselves to direct linguistic transfer, they require interpretive mediation (the translation of metaphorical euphemisms in TCM is inseparable from the structure of “semantic domestication”) and this implies an assessment of “cultural congruence” in terminological translation [14]. The presence of “mythologised medical imagery” – a feature of TCM texts – creates an additional level of complexity: the lexicon of “body symbolism” includes archaic “metaphorical schemes”. This reinforces the need for a “translation architecture” that takes into account scientific rigour and cultural semantics. Metagraphic approaches allow for a structured integration of these elements into computational models of terminological adaptation [15]. The issue of “dualism of foreignness/domestication strategies” remains unresolved: the semantic elasticity of “basic terms” makes their equivalent representation difficult – an analytical apparatus capable of distinguishing “structural-linguistic shifts” from “conceptual dislocations” is needed (TCM terminology constitutes a lexico-semantic continuum in which “ontological asymmetry” becomes a key issue [9] “Tabooisation” of bodily functions associated with “cosmological frameworks” complicates the “diagnostic lexicon” of TCM – the “phenomenological vocabulary” functions according to the principle of “linguistic interface” of “physiological” and “metaphysical” spheres (difficulties in translating “somatic idioms” are aggravated by the interpretive flexibility inherent in “philosophical euphemisation”) [2]. The integration of “digital databases” into “TCM linguistic research” increases accessibility but “exacerbates the problem” of “conceptual standardisation” as “digital classification schemes” do not account for “semantic fluidity” (“digitalisation” has facilitated terminological expansion). Without a unified “referential base”, the “ontological divergences” of Eastern and Western “medical semiotics” remain unresolved.

A critical aspect of TCM translation is adherence to ethical principles. Qiu and Zhou [16] applied Chesterman’s five types of translation ethics to analyze TCM terminology, demonstrating that compliance with ethical principles not only enhances translation quality but also facilitates broader international recognition of TCM. This is particularly relevant in the context of cultural challenges, as emphasized by Hongli et al. [17], who underscores the profound interplay between language and culture in TCM translation. Hongli advocates for a culturally sensitive approach that preserves the philosophical depth of traditional medicine.

Special attention is warranted for the translation of classical TCM texts. Shuang [12] highlights the complexity of conveying cultural concepts and the necessity of employing various compensatory strategies during translation. This challenge is directly related to the differing levels of terminology comprehension among user groups. A comparative study by Lu et al. [18] examined how Chinese and Western students understand TCM technical terminology, revealing that while both groups face difficulties, the sources of these challenges differ significantly.

Theoretical contributions by Chen et al. [19] highlight the challenges of applying intertextuality in TCM translation, such as unstable meanings and fragmented textual links, suggesting that translation itself can resolve these tensions. Fung and Linn [20] explore the practical integration of TCM into evidence-based medicine (EBM), stressing the need for rigorous proof of efficacy and safety, while also acknowledging the limitations of applying conventional EBM standards to traditional medical systems.

Technological advances in TCM, such as the digitization of texts and the development of relational databases, have greatly enhanced access to clinical and historical data, creating new possibilities for research and terminological analysis, but also introducing challenges for translators in balancing scientific precision with cultural sensitivity [21]. In the context of translator training, Yue [22] emphasizes the importance of a functional approach that considers register and communicative purpose, noting that translators without clinical backgrounds may be better attuned to the ethical and humanistic dimensions of TCM texts. Complementing this, Min et al. [23] demonstrate the effectiveness of combining strategies such as annotation, transliteration, and explanatory notes to preserve cultural identity while ensuring accuracy for diverse audiences.

A pivotal framework in this context is the Skopos theory developed by Vermeer and Chesterman [24], which posits that the primary criterion for assessing translation quality is its conformity to the intended purpose (skopos) within the target culture. In the case of translating terms from Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), this purpose may range from academic precision to communicative accessibility, thereby influencing the choice between literal translation, transcription, or explanatory adaptation. As Vermeer [24] emphasizes, translation is not a mere linguistic transfer but a purposeful action aimed at producing a specific communicative effect on the target audience. This view is further refined by Nord [25], who highlights the importance of analysing the translation brief to ensure functional adequacy. Such a perspective enables the informed adaptation of culturally embedded TCM terms to the expectations and needs of the target audience. This approach proves particularly effective in intercultural medical communication, where precision must be balanced with cultural appropriateness. Consequently, the application of Skopos theory ensures not only linguistic but also pragmatic adequacy of translation in the context of globalization.

An analysis of contemporary studies on the translation of TCM terminology reveals several critical gaps that require attention. A systematic methodology for selecting between foreignization and domestication strategies, particularly when translating classical texts, remains lacking. Additionally, the development of an evaluation system for the effectiveness of different translation approaches, one that considers adequacy, equivalence, and pragmatic value for diverse audiences, is still underdeveloped. The absence of specific recommendations for the application of translation strategies based on context and target audience further complicates the practical use of translations in international medical practice. In response to these gaps, this study proposes a scientifically grounded methodology for selecting translation strategies, criteria for evaluating their effectiveness, and practical recommendations for translating TCM medical texts. These recommendations aim to achieve a balance between accuracy, cultural adaptation, and ethical requirements, thereby addressing the complex needs of global medical communication.

Methods and materials

Study design

The research methodology was based on an interdisciplinary approach that integrated the fields of translation studies, linguistic cultural studies, terminology science, and medical linguistics. It employed the principles of systematicity and comprehensiveness, facilitating an analysis of translation strategies within the framework of intercultural communication [26]. A comparative translation approach enabled the juxtaposition of lexical, grammatical, and semantic structures in the source and translated texts, while the linguistic-cultural approach illuminated the culturally specific connotations of medical terminology and contextual nuances in traditional Chinese medicine [27].

For the analysis and experimental study, three pages from the third chapter of The Imperial Canon of Eighty-One Difficult Issues (生气通天论篇第三) were selected (Appendix 1: Text for Translation). These pages encompass complex theoretical reflections on principles of diagnosis, pathology, treatment, interrelations between organs, causes of diseases, and therapeutic methods within traditional Chinese medicine. The selected text discusses the effects of external factors (e.g., cold, heat, dampness, and wind) on the human body, as well as the consequences of imbalances between Yin and Yang on health. It includes unique terms and concepts that require meticulous translation and interpretation due to their complex metaphoricity and cultural specificity. The choice of these three pages was informed by their terminological richness and their significance for the investigation of translation strategies.

Based on these texts, an experimental study was conducted involving control and experimental groups to evaluate the effectiveness of different translation strategies. Participants’ levels of understanding and reception of the translated materials were assessed using a Likert scale and evaluated against criteria of adequacy, equivalence, and pragmatic value, enabling a detailed analysis of the challenges and specificities of translating traditional medical texts (Appendix 2: Likert Scale for Evaluation).

The experimental study involved 84 highly qualified translators tasked with producing two distinct translations of the same classical medical treatise. The first group was instructed to create a foreignized translation, closely adhering to the original Chinese text by preserving the authentic terminological structure and employing transcription and calque methods. This approach sought to replicate each medical term as accurately as possible to reflect the original Chinese formulation. The second group, in contrast, implemented a domestication strategy, fully adapting the text to Russian medical terminology. In this approach, complex Chinese medical concepts were transformed into familiar European medical constructs, supplemented by detailed explanations and interpretations. Each translator worked independently, without any knowledge of parallel task execution by other participants, ensuring the integrity and objectivity of the experiment. Upon completion of the translation work, the resulting texts were submitted to an expert panel for thorough analysis based on clearly defined criteria: the adequacy of medical information reproduction, equivalence in conveying the original content, and pragmatic value for the target audience of medical professionals.

The uniqueness of the study lies in its comprehensive approach, which not only enabled a comparison of different translation strategies but also identified the most effective methods for transferring the complex terminological system of traditional Chinese medicine into the Russian scientific discourse. This has significant implications for intercultural scientific communication.

A between-subjects experimental design was employed, in which 84 professional translators were randomly assigned to two independent groups: an experimental group oriented towards foreignization (n = 42) and a control group oriented towards domestication (n = 42). The sample size was justified based on prior studies in translation studies and power analysis calculations to detect medium-sized effects (Cohen’s d = 0.5) with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%. Randomization was conducted using a random number generator to ensure balanced distribution across groups in terms of participants’ professional experience, age, and specialization. Each participant received an identical source text (three pages from Chap. 3 of the Imperial Canon of the Eighty-One Difficult Issues) and completed the translation independently, without knowledge of parallel assignments being performed by others. Experimental conditions were rigorously controlled by standardizing the instructions, time constraints for translation, and evaluation criteria, thereby ensuring objectivity and replicability of the findings.

In addition, the term cultural connotation is herein defined as the set of culturally conditioned meanings and associations linked to specific linguistic expressions in the source text [28, 29]. Equivalence is conceptualized as a functional correspondence between the source and target texts that preserves not only semantic content but also pragmatic effect within the target context [30, 31]. The assessment of culturally specific elements—such as the term xieqi (邪气)—was conducted with consideration of the translation’s ability to convey culturally salient aspects, including religious, medical, and philosophical connotations embedded in the Chinese cultural framework.

Sampling

The recruitment of candidates was conducted through universities and research institutions, focusing on departments of Sinology, linguistics, and translation studies. Additionally, professional translation communities and associations were utilized. A total of 84 highly qualified translators were engaged, and selected based on rigorous criteria that included professional expertise, language competencies, and specialized training. Among the participants, 40% were Sinologist translators with 3–15 years of experience, 25% were specialists in traditional Chinese medicine with practical experience, 20% were linguists and Sinologists holding academic degrees, and 15% were faculty members of relevant departments.

The participants’ ages ranged from 25 to 55 years, with a predominance in the 25–35 age group (45%). Geographically, all participants were based in Kazakhstan. The gender distribution was 62% women and 38% men. Key selection criteria included fluency in Chinese and Russian at the B2–C1 level, relevant educational background, verified experience in translating specialized texts, possession of professional development certificates, and published academic works.

The expert assessment was carried out by a panel of highly experienced Sinologist translators holding academic degrees and possessing over 15 years of professional experience. This group conducted an independent evaluation of the translations based on the criteria of adequacy, equivalence, and pragmatic value.

Following the primary experiment, a post-experimental survey of participants was conducted (Appendix 3, “Post-Experimental Survey”). The aim of this survey was to explore participants’ subjective impressions, challenges encountered, and individual strategies for overcoming translation barriers while working with texts on traditional Chinese medicine. This provided valuable insights into personalized translation approaches and mechanisms for interpreting complex medical terminology. The collected data underwent analysis and subsequent statistical processing of quantitative assessments using the Student’s t-test (t-statistics) to compare the effectiveness of different translation strategies. Additionally, a thematic analysis of qualitative data was performed using the grounded theory method.

Translation evaluation was conducted through a double-blind review process. An expert panel consisting of three experienced sinologist-translators (each with over 15 years of professional experience and advanced academic degrees) independently assessed each translation without access to information regarding the translation strategy employed or the identity of the translator. Reviewers received anonymized texts marked with randomly assigned identifiers and used a standardized five-point Likert scale to evaluate adequacy, equivalence, and pragmatic value.

Cohen’s kappa coefficient indicated a high level of inter-rater agreement (κ = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.76–0.92, p < 0.001), suggesting excellent reliability across reviewers. In addition, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.79–0.93) was calculated to further confirm the consistency of the ratings. In cases where substantial discrepancies occurred (a difference greater than one point), a consensus discussion was held to reach a final judgment. This procedure further enhanced the quality and objectivity of expert evaluation regarding the applied translation strategies.

Statistical analysis was conducted using a two-tailed independent samples t-test to compare the effectiveness of foreignization and domestication strategies across five evaluation criteria. Prior to analysis, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified with Levene’s test. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d (0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large), while Pearson’s chi-square test was employed for categorical data. Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals.

Methodological research limitations

The study presents a number of significant limitations that should be carefully considered when interpreting the results. First, the scope of the research is rather narrow. The analysis is confined to specialists from Kazakhstan and focuses exclusively on the terminology of traditional Chinese medicine translated from Chinese into English. Such restrictions inevitably limit the extent to which the findings can be generalized to other geographical regions, professional contexts, subject areas, or language pairs. In addition, the relatively small sample size 84 participants with highly specialized qualifications further constrains the representativeness of the data and makes it difficult to extrapolate the results to broader populations of translators or language users. Another limitation concerns the assessment of translation quality, which is based primarily on the subjective judgments of experts. While expert evaluation remains a widely used approach in translation studies, it inherently introduces the risk of variability depending on the evaluators’ qualifications, professional experience, and cultural background. The lack of detailed information about the selection criteria for the expert group and the absence of a transparent description of their demographic and professional characteristics further complicate the interpretation of the results and may reduce the reliability of the evaluation process. The methodological design also does not sufficiently account for a number of contextual factors that could have a decisive impact on the translation process. Among these are the type of target audience (e.g., healthcare professionals, patients, or researchers), the functional purpose of the text (whether scientific, popular, or clinical), and the specific communicative goals pursued by the translator. These parameters play a crucial role in determining the appropriateness of a given translation strategy, and their omission reduces the ecological validity of the study. Furthermore, the research does not examine the potential long-term implications of systematic domestication. While domestication may enhance accessibility for target readers, its consistent application carries the risk of eroding cultural authenticity and diminishing the philosophical depth inherent in traditional Chinese medicine. Such a loss could ultimately weaken readers’ understanding of the holistic system of TCM and distort the transmission of culturally embedded medical knowledge.

The statistical analysis presented in the study requires further verification. The reported data contain methodological inaccuracies, particularly in the presentation of standard deviations and the calculation of statistical indicators. These shortcomings raise concerns about the robustness of the quantitative findings and may affect the overall reliability of the conclusions. Taken together, these limitations highlight the need for caution in generalizing the results and point to several directions for future research, including broader sampling, the integration of contextual variables, and the application of more rigorous statistical procedures.

Additionally, the high significance score for pragmatic value (p = 0.008), with a preference for domestication (4.03 versus 3.55), indicates that the translation, adapted to the cultural norms of the target language, is more functional and practically applicable. The moderate statistical significance for terminological accuracy (p = 0.012) and cultural nuances (p = 0.030), with higher scores for domestication, suggests that the adaptation of terminology to the target language not only preserves accuracy but also enhances the understanding of cultural subtleties. At the same time, the smallest difference was observed in medical accuracy (p = 0.054), indicating that both strategies are capable of adequately conveying medical information, although domestication has a slight advantage (3.95 versus 3.62).

The results presented in this table demonstrate that the domestication strategy is more effective for translating medical terminology, as it better ensures a balance between accuracy, comprehensibility, and practical value while preserving the cultural features of the original text in a form more accessible to the target audience.

Table 2 describes the differences between the foreignization and domestication strategies in translating the terminology of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). In foreignization, there is an effort to retain the original Chinese philosophical concepts and terminology as much as possible. For example, “life energy” for “生气”, “yin-yang” for “阴阳”, and the preservation of metaphorical expressions in translations such as “nine orifices” for “九窍” and “six directions of space” for “六合”. In the case of “邪气” (xieqi), both strategies aim to convey the pathological nature of the phenomenon, but the foreignized version (“external pathogenic energy”) retains more cultural connotations, whereas the domesticated translation (“pathological influence”) is more neutral and universally applicable from a medical perspective.

Figure 1 presents a comparative analysis of the difficulties in translating terms of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) based on different criteria and translation strategies. The greatest challenge for translators arises from cultural differences when using the foreignization strategy (37 participants), due to the necessity of accurately conveying specific Chinese cultural concepts. The second most difficult aspect was the metaphorical nature of terms when applying the domestication strategy (28 participants), indicating the challenges of adapting figurative Chinese medical terminology to Western terminology. The absence of direct equivalents in foreignization and the polysemy of concepts in foreignization presented a moderate level of difficulty (23 and 20 participants, respectively). Discussion

Based on a comprehensive analysis of the presented studies and a comparison with the results of our work, detailed conclusions can be drawn regarding the translation of terminology in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). Our study, which revealed a significantly higher effectiveness of the domestication strategy compared to foreignization (p = 0.001 for equivalence), partially aligns with the findings of Zhang and Yang [32], who established that translators predominantly use foreignization, supplemented by elements of domestication, to preserve the cultural connotations of Chinese medical language. However, our results regarding the higher effectiveness of domestication in overcoming cultural barriers (48.8% vs. 22.0%) and ensuring terminological accuracy (3.88 vs. 3.41) corroborate Zhu’s [33] observation about the importance of selecting appropriate translation criteria in line with its purpose.

A significant contribution to understanding the specifics of translating TCM texts was made by Liu’s [34] study, which identified four main translation strategies for metaphors: literal translation, adaptation, neutralization, and enhanced equivalent translation. The equivalent translation method proved to be the most effective for conveying culturally specific metaphors, as it allows for the preservation of both semantic accuracy and cultural relevance. This aligns with our data, which supports the higher effectiveness of domestication in conveying complex concepts, particularly when translating key terms such as “气” (qi). Further confirmation of this assertion is found in the research of Lu [35] and Lu and Coxhead [36, 37], who extensively studied the nature of technical vocabulary in TCM and the necessity for its adaptation for readers with diverse linguistic backgrounds. Lu [35] found that TCM vocabulary is a complex mix of high-, medium-, and low-frequency words, as well as abbreviations, loanwords, proper names, and discipline-specific terms. The vocabulary requires readers to have a substantial lexicon (over 13,000 word families), creating a need for the adaptation of terminology for various audiences. Further research by Lu and Coxhead [37] proposed a specialized TCM word list consisting of 2,778 terms, classified by frequency levels and cultural specificity. For example, Level 3 words included borrowed Chinese terms such as “qi” and “yang,” illustrating the complexity of conveying concepts with strong cultural contexts. This approach to lexical analysis confirms the need for adapting terms to ensure clarity and accuracy, which aligns with our findings: domestication (46.3%) is significantly more effective than foreignization (24.4%) in conveying complex concepts, particularly those like “气” (qi).

Important observations by Qian [38] regarding the issues of literal translation and untranslated terms in the international communication of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) are supported by our data on the limitations of foreignization and the higher pragmatic value of domestication (4.03 vs. 3.55, p = 0.008). Wu et al. [39], who examined the role of the linguistic landscape (LL) in medical institutions, emphasized that signs in medical establishments serve not only an informational function but also as a marketing tool, a means of building trust, reducing social inequality, and providing educational support to patients. In the case of TCM, which carries a profound cultural context, these functions become even more critical. Their research revealed that language and non-verbal elements, such as gestures, serve both explicit and implicit functions. For instance, informational functions help disseminate knowledge about TCM, while symbolic functions enhance trust in the practice due to its connection to cultural roots. Hidden ideological channels, particularly those influencing the perception of terminology, facilitate the adaptation of concepts to culturally specific contexts. These findings align with our results, which demonstrate the higher effectiveness of domestication in conveying TCM terms (46.3% vs. 24.4% for foreignization) in ensuring the clarity of complex concepts. Considering the audience’s needs and the impact of cultural context contributes to more effective transmission of medical knowledge and reduces barriers in communication, which is key for integrating TCM into multilingual and multicultural environments.

Shen et al. [40] and Zhang [41], in their studies on doctor-patient communication, emphasize the crucial role of localized interpretation of TCM terminology. Their research indicates that the effective transmission of information about diagnosis, treatment, and disease prevention largely depends on adapting medical concepts to the cultural, linguistic, and cognitive characteristics of patients. These findings coincide with our results, which highlight the advantage of domestication in translating TCM terminology, as it enhances the clarity of complex concepts for the target audience. Domestication through adaptation or explanation allows for the preservation of the cultural essence of a term while ensuring its accessibility for patients and readers unfamiliar with the traditions of Chinese medicine.

Lu and Coxhead [37] conducted an in-depth analysis of TCM vocabulary based on a corpus, including borrowed Chinese words and medical terminology, which constituted 5.93% of the TCM corpus. Their findings confirm our conclusions regarding the effectiveness of utilizing external resources in translating TCM terminology. Specifically, domestication of terms through adaptation or explanation demonstrated a higher level of acceptability (42.7% vs. 30.5% for foreignization). This indicates the need for a comprehensive approach to translating medical texts that takes into account both culturally specific features and the complexity of terminology, ensuring the clarity and accuracy of the translation for audiences with diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds.

Thus, our study extends existing research by providing specific statistical data on the effectiveness of various translation strategies and offering new perspectives for future investigations. A significant contribution lies in enhancing the understanding of the balance between preserving cultural specificity and ensuring the practical applicability of translated terms. This comprehensive analysis demonstrates that, while existing studies propose various approaches to translating TCM terminology, our work provides important empirical evidence supporting domestication as the leading strategy, particularly in terms of ensuring the practical applicability and clarity of medical terminology for the target audience.

Conclusions

Based on the conducted research, it can be asserted that the domestication strategy demonstrates significantly higher effectiveness in translating traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) terminology compared to foreignization. The highest statistical significance was found for the criteria of equivalence (p = 0.001) and pragmatic value (p = 0.008), where domestication yielded better results (4.12 and 4.03) compared to foreignization (3.48 and 3.55). The advantage of domestication is also supported by higher scores in overcoming cultural barriers (48.8% versus 22.0%), ensuring terminological accuracy (3.88 versus 3.41), and adapting complex concepts, particularly in the translation of terms such as “气” (qi), where the domesticated approach transforms abstract concepts of “energy” into more concrete medical terms (e.g., “immune protection” instead of “protective energy”).

Foreignization is more suitable for translating theoretical and philosophical texts intended for an academic audience, offering higher levels of cultural authenticity (4.21 vs. 3.67) and philosophical coherence (4.08 vs. 3.44), which are essential for scholarly publications and academic conferences. It proves particularly effective for complex TCM terms such as 脉象 (pulse manifestation). Domestication, by contrast, is more appropriate in clinical and educational contexts where immediate comprehension and practical applicability are critical, as reflected in higher ratings for accessibility (4.12 vs. 3.48) and applicability (4.18 vs. 3.62). This is evident in instructional materials and therapeutic texts, where terms like 温阳 are adapted as “metabolic stimulation therapy.”

Both strategies, however, have limitations: foreignization may hinder comprehension for non-specialist audiences (3.22 vs. 4.01), while domestication risks diluting the philosophical depth of TCM (3.34 vs. 4.12), which is vital for a comprehensive understanding of its theoretical foundations.

While the foreignization strategy better preserves the original cultural specificity and philosophical context of Chinese medicine, domestication offers greater clarity and practical applicability of translated terms for the target audience. This is evidenced by higher scores in methods of term adaptation (46.3% versus 24.4%) and the use of external resources (42.7% versus 30.5%), while still maintaining an adequate level of medical accuracy (3.95 versus 3.62), making it a more effective strategy for translating medical terminology from Chinese.

Practical implications

For a professional medical audience, the most appropriate approach is a hybrid form of foreignization, which retains the original terminology while supplementing it with detailed explanations and glossaries to ensure accurate comprehension of the specific features of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). For the general public, patients, and educational materials, a domestication strategy is more effective, as it adapts terms to familiar Western medical terminology.

In academic research, a modified foreignization strategy is recommended—one that incorporates bilingual terminology and comparative analysis to establish conceptual links between traditional Chinese and Western medical frameworks. For clinical practice, an adaptive domestication approach is optimal, as it takes into account the medical context, professional vocabulary, and includes reference tables to facilitate the integration of TCM into contemporary medical protocols.

Further research directions

The prospects for further research include a comprehensive analysis of the translation of Chinese medical terminology, which may also involve a more detailed study of linguistic features and translation transformations. An important direction is the development of specialized terminological dictionaries and glossaries, as well as the creation of electronic databases of medical terms. Specific attention could be given to the study of intercultural communication aspects and the implementation of innovative translation methods, as well as conducting a comparative analysis of the terminological systems of different medical traditions. It is also promising to explore multimodal approaches with visual aids, interactive glossaries, and multimedia explanations for better transmission of TCM knowledge. The development of context-sensitive terminology databases and translator training programs will facilitate practical application and standardization.
