Ocular multimodal imaging of a patient with malignant atrophic papulosis (Degos disease)

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report multimodal imaging of a patient with Degos disease.ResultsA 37-year-old male presented with typical Degos disease skin lesions, blurred near vision, peri-central right scotoma along with right mid-dilated unreactive pupil, no afferent pupillary defect, left ptosis and exotropia. Introduction

Malignant atrophic papulosis (MAP) or Degos disease is a small vessel angiopathy targeting only the skin in the benign variant (70% of the cases). Still, it can also involve other organs (gastrointestinal and urogenital tracts, central nervous system, heart, and the eye1,2) and be responsible for 50% lethality within 2–3 years.3 Systemic manifestations can develop years after the occurrence of skin lesions leading to bowel perforation and peritonitis, thrombosis of the cerebral arteries, massive intracerebral hemorrhage, meningitis, encephalitis, radiculopathy or myelitis.3 It affects mostly males (approx. 80%) within their second or third decades.4 Vasculitis, coagulopathy or primary dysfunction of the endothelial cells have been implicated.3 Here, we present a well-documented case of this rare pathology with multimodal imaging.

Findings

A 37-year-old male presented with typical skin lesions (slightly umbilicated, porcelain-white centered macules or papules with telangiectatic borders, Fig. 1A) of the face and trunk attributed to Degos disease. Eighteen months after biopsy-proven diagnosis, he suffered from a right eye (RE) ‘kaleidoscopic vision’ (glow halos) rapidly followed by a peri-central scotoma and a blurred near vision on the same eye, then a permanent ptosis of his left eye (LE). Best corrected visual acuity was preserved in both eyes (BE, 20/20), intraocular pressure was normal in BE (9 and 10 mmHg, RE/LE respectively). At baseline, slit lamp examination revealed BE telangiectasic eyelid margins and a right painless porcelain white atrophic skin papule with telangiectasic borders (Fig. 1A). He presented with a right mid-dilated pupil unreactive to light (Fig. 1A and B, no afferent pupillary defect). The left pupil was reactive to light. Exotropia and ptosis of the LE (palpebral fissure height, PFH of the RE = 10 mm versus LE = 8.5mm) were noted (Fig. 1A). No complete oculomotor palsy was detected but a limited adduction of the LE was present, and no orbital infiltration or lesions responsible for a potential Claude-Bernard-Horner syndrome were found on cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Fundus was unremarkable in BE at baseline. The treatment consisted of eculizumab (600 mg intravenous (IV) weekly) and repeated 5-day cycles of treprostinil, a prostacyclin analog IV (70ng/min for 6 hours/day). Despite four months of this therapy, temporal retinal ischemia was detected with whitening and hemorrhages in the RE. Subsequent angiography and optical coherence tomography (OCT) were consistent with these findings. Fluorescein angiography (FA) revealed choroidal ischemia and preserved retinal blood circulation with intact capillary network (Fig. 2A and B). Indocyanine green (ICG) angiography also confirmed choroidal ischemia (Fig. 2D) with specifically arterial involvement, as veins were filled on intermediate frames (Fig. 2E). Late frames indicated damaged retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) due to complete (dark) or partial (grey) RPE atrophy (Fig. 2F), consistent with infrared findings (Fig. 2G). OCT showed the same pattern with RPE atrophy and mottled damages along with choroidal thinning (Fig. 2H). The inner retina was relatively preserved (Fig. 2H). As retinal, cerebral and intestinal infarcts were ongoing, eculizumab dosage was increased to 1200 mg every two weeks and in addition anifrolumab (900 mg IV every 4 weeks) and baracitinib (oral 4 mg/day) were given. After three months of treatment change, pupils and near vision were restored and left exotropia and ptosis resolved (Fig. 1C). Conjunctiva showed incomplete vascularization in the right eye (Fig. 1D). Fundus and FA depicted scars of Amalric triangular signs, representing choroidal sectorial infarction due to long posterior ciliary arteries hypoperfusion (Fig. 2B and C).

Discussion

Degos disease is a rare condition. The diagnosis is based on characteristic cutaneous lesions (porcelain-white, atrophic papules with peripheral erythema and telangiectases) and microscopic findings classically demonstrating a wedge-shaped zone of necrosis extending from the epidermis to the reticular dermis.5 It affects the eye in up to 7/27 (25.6%) of cases reported,4 mostly unilateral versus bilateral involvement.6 It usually produces ischemic necrosis of the conjunctiva (telangiectasia and microaneurysms of conjunctival vessels with or without atrophic plaques), choroid and retina as seen in our patient here.4,7,8 On FA, retinal circulation was preserved while choroidal arterial circulation was completely absent. This finding was not clearly noticed in prior reports and it questions why ciliary arteries (conjunctival, iridian and choroidal) seem preferentially targeted by the disease. Central nervous system disorders such as diplopia, and ophthalmoplegia have been described.6, 7, 8, 9 In this case, left exotropia and ptosis seemed to be due to a microvascular partial nerve III palsy, as suggested by the absence of an obvious cause on MRI findings and the reversibility of the phenomenon. Blurred near vision and unreactive mid-dilated right pupil might be linked with anterior ciliary artery involvement and subsequent ciliary body ischemia leading to a reduced accommodation, as the optic nerve did not seem to be affected.4,6,10 Indeed, no relative afferent pupillary defect, papilledema, optic nerve atrophy or retinal nerve fiber layer changes on OCT were seen here.6, 7, 8, 9 In summary, this disease might affect long posterior and anterior ciliary arteries and can have an impact on several eye structures, i.e conjunctiva, sclera, choroid, iris and retina. Skin and conjunctival lesions seem clinically analogous with central atrophic necrosis and peripheral telangiectasic borders.4,6,8

Some lesions were permanent (such as chorioretinal atrophy), but others could be reversed (pupil dilation, ptosis, conjunctival, and skin lesions) with prompt treatment. Many medications have been proposed with poor success: fibrinolytic and immunosuppressive therapeutic regimens like cyclosporine A, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide and corticosteroids.3 So far, eculizumab (antibody targeted against C5 complement pathway) and prostacyclin analogs (vasodilation and anti-platelet therapy) are the most promising options.3,11 In this patient, a high dose of eculizumab with the addition of anifrolumab (antibody targeted against type I interferon receptor) and baracitinib (inhibitor of the janus kinases proteins) had a drastic impact on the patient's prognosis with survival after two years of follow-up. This was consistent with C5b/interferon-alpha-mediated mechanism targeting the endothelium, altered fibrinolysis, and platelet functions identified as a cause for this systemic vaso-occlusive disorder.1

Conclusion

Degos disease can be a lethal vasculopathy that can involve several eye structures. It seems to have a peculiar tropism for conjunctival and choroidal vessels with severe infarctions. Lesions can be reversible if promptly managed in a tertiary specialized center.
