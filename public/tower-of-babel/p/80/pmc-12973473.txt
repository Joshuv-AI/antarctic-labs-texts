Real world incidence, predictors and outcomes of endocrine immune-related adverse events following immune checkpoint inhibitors
Authors: Shuwen Tan, Yuhan Wang, Yunjia Cai, Hongzhou Guo, Ying Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEndocrine dysfunction is one of the most common immune-related adverse events (irAEs) reported in immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) clinical trials. The aim of this research was to thoroughly assess the clinical features of endocrine irAEs, investigate the risk and predictive variables, and provide guidance for clinical therapy. This study performed a retrospective review of clinical data from 269 patients with malignant malignancies who underwent initial immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment at the First Bethune Hospital of Jilin University between May 2021 and October 2022.The incidence of endocrine irAEs was 31.6% (85/269), while the autoimmune polyendocrinopathy syndrome (APS) was observed in 1.1% (3/269). The median time for the first adverse reaction was 46 (33.5, 100.5) d. The occurrence of ICIs-related thyroid injury events was significant, with the incidence at 27.5% (74/269), whereas the frequency of grade 2 and higher adverse reactions was 33.8% (25/74). Female gender (OR = 2.723, 95 % CI: 1.447–5.125) and a history of chemotherapy (OR = 2.716, 95 % CI: 1.079–6.836) were distinct risk factors for thyroid injury associated with ICIs. In the 106/269 who had baseline antibody testing, the presence of anti-thyroglobulin antibodies (TGAb) (AUC = 0.717) and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) (AUC = 0.690) could aid in predicting this injury, with TGAb demonstrating greater reliability. The prevalence of ICIs-related diabetes was 3.7% (10/269), with a higher occurrence in male patients compared to female patients, and the rate of grade 3 and above adverse reactions was 10% (3/10). The occurrence of ICIs-related pituitary inflammation was 1.1% (3/269), primarily involving pituitary hormones such as thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). The incidence of ICIs-related adrenocortical hypofunction was 0.4% (1/269), with a grade 3 adverse event. The antibody testing was performed in a nonrandom subset and thus the predictive AUC results might be affected by bias.KEYWORDS: Immune checkpoint inhibitors, endocrine adverse reaction, thyroid injury, diabetes, hypophysitis, primary adrenocortical hypofunction

Introduction

In 2011, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) initially authorized ipilimumab, an inhibitor of the immune checkpoint cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4), for the treatment of metastatic or unresectable melanoma.1 Immunological checkpoints are present on the surface of T cells and are crucial for controlling immunological responses and preserving self-tolerance.2 CTLA-4 and programmed cell death receptor-1 (PD-1) facilitate the suppression of T cell activation. Tumor cells interact with immunological checkpoints (CTLA-4 and PD-1) on T cell surfaces via signaling molecules, therefore suppressing T cell anti-tumor activity and facilitating immune evasion of tumor cells.3 ICIs are monoclonal antibodies targeting specific cellular components that modulate the immune response, including CTLA-4, PD-1, and programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1). Consequently, T cell activation and antitumor efficacy are augmented.4 At present, ICIs are widely used in the treatment of malignant tumors such as metastatic melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, head and neck swelling tumor and non-small cell lung cancer,5,6 but with the accumulation of clinical practice data, immunotherapy has gradually exposed its shortcomings. While ICIs kill tumor cells by regulating immune response, the excessive activation of immune cells may also cause autoimmune damage, leading to irAEs.7,8 IrAEs involve multiple systems, among which endocrine glands are rich in blood supply and become one of the more common targets, causing corresponding endocrine dysfunction, mainly including thyroid dysfunction, hypophysitis, diabetes, adrenal insufficiency, and so on (Figure 1).9,10

Main endocrine immune adverse events related to ICIs.

Among patients receiving ICIs, 10% to 40% encounter endocrine immune-related side effects,7 with incidence rates differing based on the specific type of ICI administered.11 Endocrine disruption often manifests within 6 months of initiating medication, with a median onset of 9–11 weeks following the initial injection.12 The concurrent administration of anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 is linked to the early onset of endocrine irAEs.12–15 Thyroid-related irAEs are the most prevalent irAEs following ICI therapy.16 Thyroid dysfunction may occur in patients receiving ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) treatment (1–6%) and those undergoing anti-PD-L1 therapy (6–11%), with a higher prevalence observed in those post anti-PD-1 treatment (up to 40%).17 The prevalence of hypophysitis was greatest among patients receiving the ipilimumab and nivolumab combination (6.4%), whereas the incidence for anti-CTLA-4 was 3.2%, anti-PD-1 was 0.4%, and anti-PD-L1 was less than 0.1%.18 The reported incidence of ICIs-induced diabetes mellitus (ICIs-DM) is 0.9% to 2%.19–21 Primary adrenal insufficiency represents a rare complication associated with ICIs. To date, only a limited number of cases have been reported in the literature, which hampers a reliable estimation of its true incidence.1,22

According to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), the National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the United States has established a severity rating system for immune-related adverse events (irAEs) (ranging from grade 1 to grade 5).8 No to mild symptoms that do not require intervention are classified as grade 1; moderate symptoms that require minimum, local, or noninvasive action are classified as grade 2; severe symptoms but not life-threatening problems are classified as grade 3; life-threatening outcomes are classified as grade 4; and adverse event-related death is classified as grade 5. Generally, events of level 3 and above are regarded as serious adverse reactions. The diagnosis of endocrine-related adverse events is complex due to the vague clinical presentations influenced by the tumor and the challenges in biochemical evaluation.23 The National and International Endocrine and Oncology Science Associations have published English guidelines about endocrine problems associated with ICIs. Nonetheless, discrepancies and inconsistencies exist in the frequency of endocrine assessments and the diagnostic criteria for endocrine-related adverse events, and the predictive indications have not been incorporated among the guidelines. The European Society of Endocrinology (ESE) recommended that baseline basic testing be performed for all patients, including morning (8:00–9:00) measurements of TSH, free thyroxine (FT4), cortisol (considering recent or ongoing glucocorticoid therapy), glucose, and electrolytes (sodium, potassium, calcium). The tests are to be conducted every 4–6 weeks, ideally prior to the commencement of each treatment cycle.24 The common pathophysiological basis of immune checkpoint inhibitors-related thyroid dysfunction is destructive thyroiditis.25,26 The fundamental pathophysiology is regarded as an immune-mediated acute inflammation succeeded by the destruction of the thyroid gland. Autoantibodies may function as biomarkers for irAEs, with research indicating that anti-thyroid antibodies could act as possible biomarkers for thyroid irAEs.27 At present, most guidelines recommend baseline screening of fasting blood glucose (FBG), but systematic monitoring of blood glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1C), and C-peptide levels is not widely recognized. This might be due to the fact that currently, most cases of diabetes are reported as acute cases. However, it has been reported that the incidence of latent diabetes has increased.28 While adverse endocrine reactions have been thoroughly examined in clinical trials, the prevalence of thyroid-related adverse reactions was significant, reaching up to 40%,17 whereas research on diabetes remained scarce, usually less than 2%.29 The actual incidence rate in the real world requires further investigation and may exceed that of prior clinical research. This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of patients with solid tumors who received ICIs for the first time. It comprehensively evaluated the attributes of irAEs in clinical practice, examined the associated risk and predictive variables, and provided reference data for the diagnosis, treatment, and management of irAEs.

Research materials and methods

The clinical data of 269 patients with malignant tumors who were initially treated with ICIs at the First Bethune Hospital of Jilin University from May 2021 to October 2022 were selected. The inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with malignant solid tumors clinically or pathologically, who initiated ICIs treatment in our hospital and received at least one cycle of treatment, and had normal functions of the endocrine-related system at the initiation of ICIs treatment. The exclusion criteria were patients with endocrine system-related malignant tumors or metastases, patients with a surgical history of endocrine system-related diseases and requiring hormone replacement, patients with paraneoplastic syndrome, and patients who were lost to follow-up or had missing clinical data. Collect the age, gender, body mass index (BMI), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) physical status score, past medical history, tumor origin, distant metastasis, the type of ICIs used, the time of initiation of medication, the time of event occurrence, clinical manifestations, etc. at the beginning of the treatment. We gathered the serological levels of patients at the commencement of treatment with ICIs and subsequent to treatment, encompassing pituitary parameters including TSH, prolactin (PRL), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH) and growth hormone (GH); thyroid function indicators including TSH, free triiodothyronine (FT3), and FT4; thyroid autoantibodies including TGAb and TPOAb; blood glucose metrics including FBG and HbA1C; and cortisol levels and rhythm including ACTH, cortisol, and 24-hour free cortisol, among others. Due to the absence of existing standards advocating for baseline antibody screening, several individuals received antibody tests according to the treatment protocols of various physicians. We did not do systematic antibody testing for diabetes, lacked C-peptide data, and had incomplete HbA1c results.

During the entire treatment period, the attending physician and nurses conducted physical examinations and index evaluations of the patients every 1–2 cycles (3–6 weeks) and recorded the occurrence of ICIs-related endocrine adverse reaction events. According to whether ICIs-related endocrine adverse reaction events occurred, the patients were divided into the endocrine adverse reaction group and the non-endocrine adverse reaction group. The thyroid-related harm induced by ICIs is categorized into four classifications based on thyroid function30: (1) Hyperthyroidism: elevated FT3 or FT4 levels, with TSH at normal or subnormal levels; (2) Hypothyroidism: diminished FT3 or FT4 levels, with TSH at normal or supranormal levels; (3) Subclinical hyperthyroidism: normal FT3 and FT4 levels, but TSH subnormal; (4) Subclinical hypothyroidism: normal FT3 and FT4 levels, but TSH supranormal. Due to the absence of antibody testing in diabetes individuals, we could not establish a categorization. The endpoint of the study was the incidence of endocrine irAEs. This investigation continued until January 2023, utilizing pertinent data from our hospital. Throughout the follow-up period, if any adverse responses of grade 3 or above manifested, or if other patients discontinued ICI therapy, the follow-up persisted until the last recorded data.

Statistical approach

Statistical analysis was performed with the SPSS 27.0 program and R 4.5.1 software. The measurement results that adhered to a normal distribution were represented as mean ± standard deviation (x±s). For measurement data that deviated from a normal distribution, they were characterized by the median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. The count data were characterized by frequency (n) and percentage (%). The independent sample t-test was utilized to compare measurement data between two groups exhibiting a normal distribution. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare measurement data between two groups that did not adhere to a normal distribution. The chi-squared test was used to compare ranked data between two groups. One-way analysis of variance was employed to compare measurement data among three or more groups that adhered to a normal distribution. The Kruskal-Wallis test was employed to compare measurement data among three or more groups that did not conform to a normal distribution. The binary logistic regression approach was used to analyze risk factors. Survival analysis was conducted via the Kaplan-Meier method, and the predictive value was assessed by the the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. To evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of thyroid TGAb and TPOAb, the area under ROC curve analysis, confusion matrix index calculation were conducted successively. At the same time, the decision curve analysis (DCA) curve was drawn to comprehensively evaluate the clinical diagnostic value of the two indicators. P < .05 denotes statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of materials data

This study enrolled 269 patients with malignant tumors, with a mean age of 61.78 y (range: 34–85), comprising 207 males and 62 females. A subset of patients underwent surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or molecular targeted therapy prior to ICIs treatment. A total of 241 patients received PD-1 treatment, comprising 106 Sintilimab, 44 Camrelizumab, 36 Tislelizumab, and 30 Pembrolizumab. A total of 27 patients received treatment with PD-L1 inhibitors, comprising 14 patients treated with durvalumab and 9 patients treated with atezolizumab. In one instance, we administered PD-1 Nivolumab in conjunction with CTLA-4 Ipilimumab.

IrAEs were seen in 85 out of 269 patients, resulting in an incidence rate of 31.60% (85/269), which included 3 APS. The median duration before the initial adverse event occurred was 46 d (33.5, 100.5). The occurrence of be equal or greater than grade 2 and be equal or greater than grade 3 endocrine adverse events was 37.6% (32/85) and 2.4% (2/85), respectively. The prevalence of thyroid damage, diabetes, hypophysitis, and adrenal insufficiency was 27.5% (74/269), 3.7% (10/269), 1.1% (3/269), and 0.4% (1/269), respectively (Table 1).

ICIs-related thyroid injury

Clinical features

Of the 269 individuals exhibiting normal thyroid function at baseline, 74 experienced thyroid damage episodes linked to ICIs, resulting in an incidence rate of 27.5% (74/269). The clinical manifestations of thyroid injury were categorized as follows: 21 instances of hyperthyroidism, representing 28.4% (21/74); 17 cases of subclinical hyperthyroidism, constituting 23.0% (17/74); 17 cases of hypothyroidism, also accounting for 23.0% (17/74); and 19 cases of subclinical hypothyroidism, comprising 25.7% (19/74).

The severity of ICIs-related adverse events concerning thyroid injury was classified, with 49 cases categorized as grade 1 events, representing 66.2% (49/74); 25 cases as grade 2 events, constituting 33.8% (25/74); and no occurrences of grade 3 or 4 events. The majority of patients exhibited no apparent symptoms, while a minority presented with symptoms including fatigue and neck pain. The average values of TSH (range of normal value: 0.35–4.94 uIU/ml), FT3 (range of normal value: 2.43–6.01 pmol/L), and FT4 (range of normal value: 9.01–19.05 pmol/L) in hyperthyroid patients were 0.034 uIU/mL, 8.00 pmol/L, and 22.29 pmol/L, respectively. The average values of TSH, FT3, and FT4 in hypothyroid patients were 39.088 uIU/ml, 2.04 pmol/L, and 6.66 pmol/L, respectively. Five patients (6.8%) experienced concurrent adverse reactions related to ICIs, which included immune-related interstitial pneumonia, autoimmune liver injury, myocarditis, and cutaneous capillary hyperplasia. Among 74 cases of ICIs-related thyroid injury, 5 cases (6.8%) exhibited atypical imaging findings, characterized by an enlarged thyroid and extensive tissue alterations (Table 3).

Characteristics of occurrence

The median duration from the commencement of the first immune checkpoint inhibitor medication to the emergence of thyroid dysfunction was 46.5 d (range: 28 to 106.8 d), with the minimum delay being 4 d and the maximum period extending to 544 d. We observed significant individual variances. The median start time for hyperthyroidism was 42 d (95 % CI 38.6–45.4), while the median onset time for hypothyroidism was 136 d (95 % CI 94.3–177.7). The median duration to hyperthyroidism was much less than that to hypothyroidism, with a statistically significant difference (P = .0002) (Figure 2). The median time to the incidence of grade level 1 and grade level 2 of ICIs-related thyroid damage was 43 d (95 % CI 40.7–45.3) and 85 d (95 % CI 16.5–153.5), respectively, based on the severity of adverse events. A notable disparity existed in the median time across individuals with varying degrees of ICIs (P = .009) (Figure 3).

Evolution and outcome

Among the 21 patients initially diagnosed with hyperthyroidism, 9 subsequently developed hypothyroidism, resulting in an incidence rate of 42.9% (9/21). Several patients exhibited thyroid swelling and discomfort; their thyroid hormone levels indicated transient hyperactivity; the thyroid color Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated extensive alterations in the thyroid tissue; and the disease development corresponded with that of thyroiditis. The patient cohort exhibited a male-to-female ratio of 8:1, with a mean age of 66.7 ± 5.9 y. The minimum TSH level recorded was < 0.0025 uIU/mL during the hyperthyroid phase, and the median duration to get this lowest value was 47 (23, 49.5) d. The peak TSH value was 73.663 uIU/mL during the hypothyroid phase, with a median duration to attain this peak of 95 (64.5, 129.5) d. The median duration from hyperthyroidism to hypothyroidism was 67 d (30.5, 81.5). After the event endpoint, 3 of 21 patients with hyperthyroidism reverted to normal thyroid function, resulting in an incidence of 14.3% (3/21). Of the 17 individuals with subclinical hyperthyroidism, 9 (52.9%) reverted to normal thyroid function. The 17 individuals with hypothyroidism exhibited no difference in endpoint events. Of the 19 individuals with subclinical hypothyroidism, 9 (47.4%) reverted to normal thyroid function. Only one patient with hyperthyroidism and one patient with hypothyroidism ceased medication. The overall prognosis was favorable; nevertheless, the autoantibodies remained present. As the condition of these individuals improved, their antibody titers diminished.

Basic information of thyroid injury group and non-thyroid injury group

A total of 269 patients were studied. Average age of beginning ICIs therapy was 61.8 ± 8.3 y, with 207 males (77.0%) and ECOG scores of 0–1. The predominant tumor origins were non-small cell lung cancer (134), esophageal cancer (53), and small cell lung cancer (21). Most tumors were progressed, and 62.8% of patients (n = 169) developed distant metastases. Before ICIs, few patients had surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, or targeted treatment. The enrolled patients mostly utilized PD-1 ICIs (n = 241). Smoking, drinking, hypertension, surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation histories were also provided.

Further analysis revealed that patients with ICIs-related thyroid damage had a lower average age (60.6 ± 9.0) compared to those without thyroid injury (62.2 ± 8.0), however the difference was not significant (P = .154). Female patients (43.5%) had a significantly greater thyroid damage rate than male patients (22.7%) (P = .001). BMI (P = .703) and ECOG (P = .886) were similar between groups. No significant difference in smoking, drinking, or hypertension history across groups (P > .05). The incidence of ICIs-related thyroid damage in various malignancies was statistically significant (P = .003). No significant difference was seen between tumor PD-L1 expression and thyroid damage from prior treatments. ICIs combined with other medicines significantly increased thyroid damage (P = .015).

Thyroid function and antibody characteristics

The baseline TSH level in the thyroid injury group [2.03 (1.12, 2.87) uIU/ml] was somewhat elevated compared to the non-thyroid injury group [1.7 (1.14, 2.35) uIU/ml], although the difference lacked statistical significance (P = .132). The baseline thyroid hormone levels were comparable across the two groups (P > .05).

Only 106 individuals underwent testing for thyroid-associated antibodies, including TGAb (range of normal value: 0–4.11 IU/ml) and TPOAb (range of normal value: 0–5.61 IU/ml). There was no statistically significant difference in the basal thyroid function between the two subgroups that underwent thyroid antibody testing (P > .05). The baseline concentrations of TGAb [3.03 (1.89, 10.69) IU/ml] and TPOAb [1.40(0.71, 20.31) IU/ml] in the thyroid injury cohort exceeded those in the non-thyroid injury cohort. The difference was statistically significant (P < .05) (Table 4).

Analysis of risk factors

Certain research indicate that ICIs-associated thyroid damage correlates with gender, age, prior treatment history, ICIs medications, and baseline TSH levels. Data from 269 patients were assessed using chi-square and t-tests, along with P values; subsequently, covariates such as age, gender, chemotherapy history, treatment plan, distant metastasis, and TSH were further evaluated by binary logistic regression. The findings revealed that female gender (OR = 2.723, 95 % CI: 1.447–5.125) and a history of chemotherapy (OR = 2.716, 95 % CI: 1.079–6.836) were significant risk factors for thyroid damage events associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (P < .05) (Table 5).

Analysis of predictive factors

The analysis of 106 patients with baseline data for thyroid antibodies showed that there was a difference in antibody titer between the thyroid injury group and the non-thyroid injury group (P < .05). Further ROC curve verification showed that TGAb and TPOAb had predictive value for ICIs-related thyroid injury (AUC > 0.5). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for TGAb was 0.717, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.595–0.839, indicating that TGAb has a moderate predictive ability for the study subjects. The overall prediction accuracy was 0.679 (95 % CI: 0.582–0.767), the specificity was 0.679 (95% CI: 0.579–0.778), and the sensitivity was 0.682 (95 % CI: 0.487–0.876) (Figure 4).

The ROC curve of TGAb and TPOAb of 106 patients.

The positive predictive value (PPV) of TGAb was 0.891 (95 % CI: 0.814 - 0.967), indicating that when the test result of this indicator is positive, the probability of the subject being actually positive is relatively high; the negative predictive value (NPV) was 0.357 (95 % CI: 0.212 - 0.502), suggesting that when the test result is negative, the probability of the subject being actually negative is relatively low. Through the Youden index screening, the optimal diagnostic cutoff value (Cutoff) of TGAb was 2.255, and the Youden index at this point was 0.361. The PPV of TPOAb was 0.860 (95 % CI: 0.787–0.934), indicating a relatively high predictive value of the positive test result; the NPV was 0.500 (95 % CI: 0.281–0.719), which was higher than that of TGAb, suggesting that the predictive value of the negative test result of TPOAb was better than that of TGAb. After using the Youden index for screening, the optimal diagnostic Cutoff of TPOAb was 2.820, and the Youden index at this point was 0.431 (Table 6).

DCA curve analysis

The DCA results of TGAb and TPOAb show that within different high-risk threshold ranges, the clinical net benefits of the two indicators exhibit different trends. Compared with the full intervention and no intervention strategies, the net benefit curves corresponding to the model show differentiated characteristics, suggesting that TGAb and TPOAb both have certain clinical application values within the corresponding risk threshold ranges, which can provide certain reference for clinical decision-making. Moreover, the differences in net benefits at different thresholds also provide a threshold selection direction for the precise clinical application of the two indicators. The threshold range of TGAb is 11%−66% (Figure 5); the threshold range of TPOAb is 11%−99% (Figure 6).

ICIs-related diabetes

The study included 269 patients. During treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors, ten patients (3.7%) received a new diagnosis of diabetes. The male-to-female ratio was 9:1, the mean age was 60.8 y, and the mean BMI was 21.96 kg/m2. Of the cases, 7 were attributed to lung cancer, 2 to esophageal cancer, and 1 to liver cancer. ICIs included PD-1 in eight patients and PD-L1 in two patients. Adverse reactions were classified as grade 1 in five cases, grade 2 in four cases, and grade 3 in one case, with no treatments suspended or discontinued. The median duration until the onset of diabetes was 48 d (range: 40.8 to 112.8 d). Three out of ten patients experienced thyroid injury associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (Table 7).

Discussion

ICIs are becoming increasingly significant in tumor treatment, providing unparalleled therapeutic advantages to cancer patients. Nonetheless, owing to its side effects, over fifty percent of patients encounter irAEs that compromise quality of life and may result in treatment suspension or cessation.31 The consequences arising from these toxicities may result in long-term irreparable organ damage and further chronic health disorders. Endocrine adverse effects impact up to 40% of treated individuals and are one of the most prevalent toxicities associated with ICIs,32 with the thyroid, pituitary, adrenal glands, and islet β-cells being the most often impacted endocrine organs.18 In this study, the occurrence of endocrine adverse effects with ICIs was 31.6% (85/269). The prevalence of thyroid damage, diabetes, hypophysitis, and adrenal insufficiency was 27.5% (74/269), 3.7% (10/269), 1.1% (3/269), and 0.4% (1/269), respectively. The occurrence of adverse events exceeding grade 2 was 37.6% (32/85), while those surpassing grade 3 was 2.4% (2/85). The median onset of the initial adverse response was 46 d; the earliest reaction manifested 4 d post-treatment, while the latest reaction was observed 544 d after treatment started. Adverse effects manifested in certain instances six months post-drug cessation. The time of occurrence of endocrine-related adverse events varied greatly.

In this study, the incidence of ICIs-related thyroid injury was 27.5% (74/269). The percentage of medication kinds was PD-1 at 89.6%. No notable distinction was detected in thyroid damage induced by PD-1 and PD-L1. ICIs can cause a diverse array of thyroid dysfunctions, ranging from thyrotoxicosis to hypothyroidism, including both subclinical and overt forms, as well as a thyroid storm. Recent studies suggest that it primarily pertains to hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism. The majority of thyroid irAEs are asymptomatic, manifesting as moderate thyrotoxicosis or primary hypothyroidism due to thyroiditis. Thyrotoxicosis linked to autoimmune thyroid disease (Graves disease) occurs in a limited number of patients.26 Of the 21 patients initially diagnosed with hyperthyroidism in this research, 9 subsequently acquired hypothyroidism, resulting in an incidence rate of 42.9%. Several individuals further reported thyroid hypertrophy and discomfort. The alterations in thyroid hormones presented as transitory hyperthyroidism, and thyroid color ultrasonography scan revealed diffuse abnormalities in the thyroid parenchyma. The illness course features aligned with the clinical signs of thyroiditis, accompanied by a degree of hypothyroidism. This suggested a propensity for thyroid damage generated by ICIs to resolve spontaneously. The thyroid dysfunction linked to ICIs may be transient or enduring. Hypothyroidism may necessitate prolonged use of levothyroxine to substitute thyroid hormones. The general outlook is positive. The majority of thyroid toxicities connected to ICIs are mild or moderate (grade 1 or 2 according to CTCAE), but severe problems (grade 3 or 4) are few and mostly linked to combination therapies.33 Thyroid damage induced by immune checkpoint inhibitors often manifests during the initial weeks. Numerous case series documented in the literature revealed a median length ranging from 18 to 123 d.34 The median duration for the onset of thyroid dysfunction in this research was 42 d. The current risk factors for thyroid impairment induced by immune checkpoint inhibitors remain unclear. This study examined potential factors, including age, gender, type of immune ICIs, medical history, and TSH. Ultimately, it was determined that women and individuals with a history of chemotherapy exhibited a heightened susceptibility to immune checkpoint inhibitors-related thyroid damage, paralleling the overall population’s trend of increased vulnerability to autoimmune thyroid disorders among women. Thyroid-related irAEs are linked to antibodies against TPOAb and thyroglobulin TGAb, resulting from lymphocyte and cytokine infiltration into thyroid tissue.35 At present, thyroid antibodies are not incorporated into the baseline screening criteria. Autoantibodies may function as biomarkers for irAEs, with research indicating that antithyroid antibodies are viable biomarkers for thyroid-related irAEs.27 We performed a research involving 106 individuals with baseline antibodies. A statistically significant difference in the titers of TGAb and TPOAb was observed between the thyroid damage group and the non-thyroid injury group (P < .05). Subsequent analysis utilizing the ROC curve and DCA curve demonstrated that TGAb and TPOAb has predictive relevance for ICIs-related thyroid damage, with TGAb exhibiting superior sensitivity compared to TPOAb. The commencement of ICIs may lead to elevated thyroid autoantibody levels, maybe resulting from a humoral response that reveals thyroid antigens owing to destructive thyroiditis.36 A subset of the patients underwent thyroid-related antibody testing and lack of reporting of higher-grade thyroid events; This collection was a random selection; however, there may be underlying bias impacting the ROC analysis. This study found that autoantibodies persisted after thyroid damage, and for some patients, the antibody titer decreased as their condition improves. The necessity of thyroid antibody testing necessitates a meticulous evaluation of incidence rate variations, and additional clinical investigations are required for further validation.

A meta-analysis of 34 trials on ICIs therapies identified 85 instances of hypophysitis among 6472 individuals, with 34 cases (0.5%) classified as grade 3 or higher.18 The timing of hypophysitis is associated with the type of immune checkpoint inhibitor medications used. It often manifests after a few weeks to many months following the administration of the medicine. The onset of pituitary inflammation with a solitary CTLA-4 inhibitor occurs within 2–3 months. When administered PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, the occurrence is more probable within 3 to 5 months .1 Men are more likely to get pituitary inflammation due to ICIs, according to reports. Approximately four times as many men as women experienced pituitary inflammation as a result of anti-CTLA-4 medication, according to research by Caturegli et al.37 Iwama et al.38 discovered that those individuals treated with ipilimumab who experienced hypophysitis exhibited the presence of pituitary antibodies (APA). Cephalalgia and lethargy are the predominant complaints. Mild to moderate diffuse swelling of the pituitary gland is a sensitive and specific indicator of pituitary inflammation on imaging, and this enlargement often resolves within a few weeks.39 The treatment comprises supportive therapy and the replacement of levothyroxine, corticosteroids, and estrogen/testosterone hormones, contingent upon the type and degree of hormone deficiency.40 The recovery from central hypothyroidism appears to be prevalent, with a median duration of 10.5 weeks.41 The study comprised 269 patients. The final outcome indicated three cases of patients with hypophysitis, all of whom underwent the anti-PD-1 combined chemotherapy regimen. The incidence rate was 1.1% (3/269), accompanied by a male-to-female ratio of 2:1. The median duration of pituitary inflammation in this research was 124.3 d. The hormones that were lacking were thyroid-stimulating hormone and adrenocorticotropic hormone. The severity degree of adverse events ranged from 1 to 2. The pituitary MRI of the three individuals had no discernible abnormalities, aligning with a recent research. Johnson et al.42 discovered that just 2.3% of the individuals exhibited alterations in their pituitary MRI. Nonetheless, the sample size of this study was very limited and lacked representative significance.

The total incidence of ICIs-induced diabetic mellitus (DM) varies between 0.9% and 2%. The female to male ratio varies from 1:1.2 to 1:9, with a mean age of onset exceeding 60 y.19–21 There was also a study reporting an incidence rate of 3.5%.43 Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) may also worsen, but the frequency of occurrence is still unknown.44 Of all documented instances of ICIs-DM, over 97% transpired in patients undergoing anti-PD-1/PD-L1 or combination treatment, but occurrences of anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy were exceedingly uncommon.44 Approximately 40–50% of individuals with ICIs-DM exhibit detectable insulin autoantibodies upon diagnosis, whereas this percentage exceeds 90% in patients with Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM).45 The beginning period of ICIs-DM exhibited significant variability, with a median frequency between 7 to 17 weeks and a range spanning from 1 to 228 weeks.45 A review of reported cases indicated that a significant proportion of patients (38–71%) exhibited diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), alongside a relatively mild increase in HbA1C, implying a swift onset of impaired insulin secretion function.46 Individuals with ICIs-T1DM may exhibit hyperglycemia, characterized by symptoms such as thirst, polydipsia, and polyuria, as well as DKA, which involves hyperglycemia and ketosis. Should ketosis or ketoacidosis transpire, symptoms including nausea or abdominal discomfort may present, and in certain instances, indications of altered awareness or coma may be evident.23 In comparison to ICIs-T1DM, ICIs-T2DM exhibited a slower onset, effective blood sugar management through oral hypoglycemic agents, a reduced likelihood of acute complications, and does not interfere with the treatment regimen of ICIs.43 Certain individuals who encountered immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced diabetes mellitus also exhibited other endocrine immune-related side effects, including pituitary inflammation47 and thyroid dysfunction.48 This study detected 10 instances of diabetes associated with ICIs, with an incidence rate of 3.7% (10/26), surpassing that of other investigations. The male to female patient ratio for diabetes was 9:1, with an average age of 60.8 y. Of the 10 instances administered immune checkpoint inhibitors, 8 targeted PD-1 and 2 targeted PD-L1; 6 cases exhibited grade 1 adverse responses, 3 cases presented grade 2 adverse reactions, and 1 case suffered a grade 3 adverse reaction. None of these adverse effects resulted in the suspension or cessation of the therapy. The median duration for the onset of diabetes was 48 d. Of the 10 patients, 3 had thyroid impairment associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors. None of them had any significant adverse outcomes, such as DKA. The differences in the previous retrospective studies on ICIs-DM were significant (Table 10). A key discovery of our research is that the occurrence of ICIs was greater than what has been documented in the literature. This disparity may be attributed to the following factors: firstly, prior research predominantly concentrated on ICI-induced T1DM, with DKA frequently being the initial manifestation in case reports. Hyperglycemic patients without DKA may result in a missed diagnosis. In this study, the majority of the diabetic patients were found to be on combined chemotherapy, which might lead to an increase in the incidence rate. This study did not conduct antibody testing or typing, and the measured values of glycated hemoglobin were incomplete, so that the 3.7% incidence rate may have a potentially error and need further determination. Consequently, consistent blood glucose monitoring throughout the administration of immune checkpoint inhibitors is essential. The assessment of fasting blood glucose, C-peptide, and glycated hemoglobin is both convenient and efficacious. Simultaneously, imparting health education to patients facilitates early identification and intervention.

Primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI) is a rare complication of ICIs. There are few case reports of ICIs-associated PAI.54–56 Nevertheless, a study suggests that the recurrence rate is 1.2%, suggesting it may be more prevalent than previously assumed.57 The pathogenesis of ICIs-related PAI remains unclear, with elevated levels of 21-hydroxylase and adrenocortical antibodies observed in a patient experiencing pembrolizumab-associated PAI.56 Abdominal imaging for ICIS-related PAI may demonstrate signs of adrenalitis, characterized by bilaterally enlarged adrenal glands with relatively smooth margins. In the literature, three cases of ICIs-related PAI have been reported. One case exhibited normal bilateral adrenal gland appearance on abdominal CT,56 another case demonstrated a uniform increase in fluorodeoxyglucose activity in the bilateral adrenal glands,58 and the third case revealed bilateral adrenal enlargement on abdominal CT.54 A subsequent imaging examination revealed either normalization or atrophy of the adrenal glands. Adrenal insufficiency presents with nonspecific signs and symptoms, including fatigue, orthostatic hypotension, anorexia, weight loss, and abdominal discomfort.59 Adrenal crisis, a life-threatening disease characterized by severe weakness, syncope, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, altered mental status, and delirium, can progress to shock and death if left untreated.59 Hyponatremia and hyperkalemia frequently occur in primary adrenal insufficiency due to deficiencies in both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids.59 In this study, the limited sample size resulted in the identification of only one case of PAI associated with the combination of anti-PD1 and anti-CTLA4. A grade 3 adverse event was observed, leading to the termination of ICI treatment.

Regardless of the type of drug used, the clinical manifestations and management of endocrine irAEs caused by ICIs are similar. Despite the proposal of various diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in several guidelines, a consensus remains elusive due to the unique and diverse presentations of immune checkpoint inhibitors-related endocrine adverse effects. Moreover, although endocrine immune-related adverse events are predominantly chronic illnesses, mortality is an exceedingly rare occurrence.46,60 Accurate patient monitoring should consider common scenarios while ensuring cost-effectiveness. Prior to initiating ICI treatment, it is advisable to assess thyroid hormones (TSH, FT4, and TPO-Ab) initially, followed by monitoring thyroid function (TSH and FT4) with each treatment administration.60 Furthermore, it is essential to evaluate fasting blood glucose and electrolyte levels at baseline and throughout the treatment process. The evaluation of baseline pituitary function, including morning cortisol, ACTH, PRL, gonadotropins, and sex hormones, is beneficial for comparative analysis, particularly in the context of anti-CTLA-4 antibody administration.61 Regular monitoring of serum electrolytes and morning cortisol levels during the treatment period is recommended, as prevalent endocrine disorders such as pituitary inflammation, thyroid dysfunction, and primary adrenal insufficiency frequently present with nonspecific symptoms. If primary or secondary adrenal insufficiency is suspected, characterized by hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, fatigue, hypotension, or shock, an immediate examination is warranted, and corticosteroids should be administered promptly. Endocrine irAEs typically do not necessitate hospitalization; however, adverse reaction events classified as grade 3 or higher, such as diabetic ketoacidosis or adrenal crisis, are optimally managed in a hospital setting.62 Despite the critical implications of hormonal issues, prompt diagnosis and appropriate hormone replacement therapy facilitate continued cancer treatment, hence enhancing survival rates and treatment efficacy. Efficient communication and collaboration between oncologists and endocrinologists are essential for the care and prognosis of patients with immune checkpoint inhibitor-related endocrine disorders (Figure 7).

Conclusions and future directions

This study indicated that initial assessment of thyroid antibodies were associated with and may help predict thyroid irAEs in our subset; prospective validation is required. The baseline antibody testing was performed in 106/269 patients, which limits generalizability. The incidence of diabetes associated with ICIs may be lower than anticipated. Additionally, patients with pituitary inflammation may exhibit signs that could be easily overlooked, necessitating careful attention from physicians to prevent missed diagnoses. Future large-scale multicenter studies are necessary to validate these findings and to further investigate immune indicators, cytokines, and genetic factors associated with endocrine adverse events of ICIs, in order to enhance the prediction, identification, and management of such endocrine adverse events moving forward. Baseline measurements of TSH/FT4, glucose, and electrolyte levels should be conducted, with baseline antithyroid antibodies considered under a validation caveat. Glucose levels must be continuously maintained, and patients should be educated to enhance clinical actionability in accordance with established standards.
