Poly-ɛ-caprolactone/multi-wall carbon nanotube composites as osteoinductive scaffolds for alveolar bone regeneration
Authors: Chien-Yang Yeh, Firdiansyah Rasyid Thaha, Yung-Kang Shen, Wei-Chun Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeDesigning scaffolds to promote alveolar bone regeneration is a major challenge in dental practice. Among the desired properties of scaffolds, biocompatibility is crucial. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) possess high surface area and mechanical strength, enhancing the osteogenic properties of poly-ɛ-caprolactone (PCL). Therefore, this study evaluated the osteogenic potential of PCL/MWCNT scaffolds by incorporating varying weight percentages of MWCNTs (0.5–3 wt.%).Materials and methodsThis study fabricated the scaffolds using a solvent-casting/particulate-leaching technique. Their physical and chemical properties were evaluated using contact angle (CA), porosity, pore size distribution, and Raman spectroscopy. In vitro biocompatibility was assessed via 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazole-2-Y1)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) assays using osteoblast-like cells (MG-63), and cell morphology cell morphology was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).ResultsThe addition of MWCNTs to scaffolds improved their hydrophilicity and maintains a high porosity (>85 %) in most formulations. The Young’s modulus (0.15–2.32 GPa) of PCL/MWCNT scaffolds containing different weight percentages of MWCNTs was comparable to that of human alveolar bone (1.0–3.3 GPa). PCL/MWCNT scaffolds containing 3 wt.% MWCNT exhibited the highest biocompatibility across all time points (days 1, 4, and 7). ALP activity, a marker of osteogenic differentiation, increased significantly over time, particularly at higher weight percentages MWCNT.ConclusionThe PCL/MWCNT scaffold, especially at a 3 wt.% MWCNT, demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, porosity, and osteoinductive potential, making it a promising candidate for alveolar bone tissue engineering.Keywords: Poly-ɛ-caprolactone, Multi-wall carbon nanotube, 3D scaffold, Solvent-casting/particulate-leaching method, Osteogenic

Introduction

Dental implant placement requires a patient’s alveolar bone to be strong enough to complete the implantation process. When alveolar bone strength is insufficient, a porous temporary scaffold is needed to assist in bone reconstruction or regeneration. This porous scaffold must have sufficient mechanical properties to withstand bone damage. This porous scaffold is used to regulate the growth of cells migrating from surrounding tissues or to implant cells within the pores of porous scaffold, thus addressing the clinical challenge of alveolar bone repair during dental implant placement.1–3 Biomedical materials are essential elements in the construction of porous scaffolds. Numerous papers have explored the integration of various polymers with rigid materials (hyaluronic acid, carbon nanotubes, and graphene) to enhance the mechanical strength of scaffolds, while also exploring the biocompatibility of scaffolds through cell culture.4–6 Existing research has shown that the growth of bone and vascular tissue requires scaffolds with pore sizes greater than 100 μm, with pore sizes between 100 and 350 μm being optimal for bone formation.7

Poly-ɛ-caprolactone (PCL) is used as a biomedical material for porous scaffolds, but its strength is insufficient when used as a replacement scaffold for bone repair. Therefore, other materials (such as graphene,8 β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP),9 hydroxyapatite (HA)9) are added to form composite scaffolds suitable for bone repair or regeneration. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are nanoscale cylindrical structures composed of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice.10,11 Studies have shown that CNTs can cause immunotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and neurotoxicity in living beings.12–14 Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) hold great promise for applications in emitters, field-effect transistors, sensors, and biomedical products.15 Their biomedical applications include their use as novel nano-carriers for cancer therapy.16 MWCNTs offer a range of surface areas, ease of functionalization, electrochemical activity, and thermal and chemical stability.17 MWCNTs have demonstrated impressive self-healing capabilities under near-infrared radiation at 808 nm and 0.14 W.18,19 MWCNTs are composed of multiple layers of graphene wound together to form a tubular structure. They possess a large surface area, high mechanical strength, and excellent thermal conductivity.20 MWCNTs exhibit osteoconductive properties, which can influence genes involved in bone differentiation and accelerate mineralization.21

MWCNT composite scaffolds had been widely used in dentistry (alveolar bone) and orthopedics. The HA/CNT electrospun fibers showed well biocompatibility and improved mechanical properties of HA/CNT composites.22 Polymer-ceramic blended scaffolds containing carbon nanotube (different weight percentages) within PCL nano-fiber matrix were fabricated. The static culture of Schwann cells on these scaffolds demonstrated improved cell viability as the weight percentage of CNTs within PCL matrix increased to 5 wt.%.23 MWCNTs significantly improved the physical and mechanical properties of the prepared scaffolds ((PCL)/chitosan/CNT), and in-vitro evaluations showed that the prepared nanofibrous scaffold containing 4wt. % MWCNT could become very useful biocompatible material.24 Dexamethasone (DEX)-loaded polylactic acid (PLA)/MWCNT/polyethylene glycol (PEG) scaffolds not only enhanced mechanical strength but also promoted osteogenesis of rat bone marrow stromal cells (rBMSCs) via sustained release of DEX.25

This study anticipated that PCL/MWCNT scaffolds with higher MWCNT content would exhibit greater hydrophilicity and porosity, and their strength would closely match that of alveolar bone, making them suitable for alveolar bone regeneration. PCL/MWCNT scaffolds with high MWCNT content were expected to demonstrate higher MTT and ALP activity, exhibiting better biocompatibility. Therefore, when alveolar bone strength was insufficient during dental implantation, dentists could use them as porous temporary scaffolds to assist in bone reconstruction or regeneration.

Materials and methods

Poly-ɛ-caprolactone (PCL) and PCL/multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT) scaffolds were produced using a solvent-casting/particulate-leaching method. Initially, PCL was dissolved in chloroform at 20 °C–25 °C for 12 h. It was then mixed with different weight percentages of MWCNTs (0.5 %, 1 %, 1.5 %, 2 %, 2.5 %, and 3 % wt.%)22–25 and NaCl particles over a 2-h period. The final mixture was poured into a mold and allowed to solidify overnight at 20 °C–25 °C. Chloroform was removed using a 24-h evaporation in a vacuum oven IACF-DOV40 (Deng Yng, New Taipei City, Taiwan), maintained at 37 °C and 760 mmHg. The porogen was then removed from the scaffold using a water bath BH-130D (Yide Industry LTD., Taipei, Taiwan) with deionized (DI) water. Finally, the scaffold was dried in a 50 °C oven for 12 h. The PCL/MWCNT scaffold containing 3 wt.% MWCNTs had the smallest contact angle (76.87°), indicating the highest hydrophilicity among all samples (Fig. 2). Fig. 7 displayed the Raman spectra of PCL and PCL/MWCNT scaffolds. The PCL exhibited characteristic peaks at ~850 cm−1, ~1100 cm−1, ~1450 cm−1, and ~1720 cm−1, which correspond to C–C stretching vibration, C–O–C asymmetric stretching vibration, C–H bending vibration, and carbonyl stretching vibration, respectively. These peaks were presented in all composite scaffolds, demonstrating that the incorporation of MWCNTs does not alter the polymer’s chemical structure. Notably, the MWCNT-related D (~1350 cm−1) and G (~1580 cm−1) bands became more prominent with increasing MWCNT weight percentage. The presence of a 2D band (~2700 cm−1) at concentrations ≥2 wt.% further confirmed the presence of well-dispersed few-layer MWCNTs.27,31

Discussion

PCL scaffold was hydrophobic (contact angle >90°) in this study. However, MWCNTs addition to the PCL scaffolds resulted in hydrophilicity (contact angles between 76.87° and 84.23°), regardless of MWCNT weight percentages. The hydrophilicity of PCL/MWCNT scaffolds increased with increasing MWCNT weight percentages. This result was highly consistent with existing literature.25 PCL/MWCNT scaffold with 3 wt.% MWCNT exhibited the lowest contact angle, which generally promoted better cell attachment, proliferation, and tissue integration. Such modifications were often used to improve the biocompatibility, cell adhesion, and degradation rates of medical implants and scaffolds. The porosity variation among samples indicated that the effect of MWCNT addition on porosity of PCL/MWCNT scaffolds was nonlinear. High porosity was beneficial for bone tissue applications because it facilitated nutrient and gas exchange, enhances fluid absorption, and promoted cell infiltration and growth.8

Figs. 4–5 show the pore size distribution of different PCL/MWCNT scaffolds. The PCL/MWCNT scaffold with 1 wt.% MWCNT revealed the highest frequency of pores in pore size range >250 μm, indicating that this is most conducive to promoting osteoblast infiltration and growth. Scaffolds with 0.5 and 2 wt.% MWCNTs exhibited significant peaks in range <50 μm. The distribution profiles indicated that scaffolds with 1–1.5 wt.% MWCNTs were more conducive to cell proliferation due to their wider pores and greater connectivity. These results were highly consistent with those in existing literature.30

Porous scaffolds made from PCL lack enough strength for bone repair. Two-dimensional materials (such as carbon nanotube, graphene, HA, and β-TCP) had been used to enhance their strength. We used PCL/MWCNT as the porous scaffold material because MWCNT had been discovered for many years, its fabrication methods were mature and easy, its material was strong and used as a porous scaffold for a long time. MWCNT addition could enhance polymeric materials strength, although this could reduce their ductility. When the weight percentage of MWCNT and sintering degree were consistent within the scaffold, the PCL/MWCNT scaffold with 3 wt. % MWCNT exhibited the highest Young’s modulus of entire scaffold. In this study, the Young’s modulus (0.15–2.32 GPa) of PCL/MWCNT scaffolds with varying weight percentages of MWCNT were comparable to those of human alveolar bone (1.0–3.3 GPa).32 The Young’s modulus of PCL/graphene scaffolds with different graphene scaffold weight percentages ranged from 0.54 to 0.94 GPa,8 and the Young’s modulus results of PCL/MWCNT and PCL/graphene scaffolds were similar. However, the maximum Young’s modulus of the PCL/MWCNT scaffold was greater than that of the PCL/graphene scaffold. Therefore, this study’s results were suitable for future clinical applications.

The polymer matrix remained chemically stable across all formulations, with the position and intensity of PCL-related peak remaining constant (Fig. 7). Slight shifts in the C = O stretching region (~1720 cm−1) might indicate a weak interaction between carbonyl groups of PCL matrix and MWCNT surfaces, likely through hydrogen bonding or π-π interactions. The absorption band (2873 cm−1) at this location originates solely from the C–H vibrations of crystalline region of PCL molecule and MWCNTs. The gradual enhancement of G and 2D bands confirmed successful incorporation and enhanced dispersion of MWCNTs within PCL matrix. Furthermore, the coexistence of D and G bands within the composites indicated a mixture of ordered and disordered MWCNT structures, which might impact the mechanical and electrical properties of composite. In summary, Raman spectroscopy verifies the structural integrity of PCL matrix and provided clear evidence for the presence, dispersion, and quality of MWCNTs within the composite scaffolds. The emergence and evolution of D, G, and 2D bands with increasing MWCNT weight percentage highlighted the potential of Raman spectroscopy as a powerful tool for monitoring filler dispersion and structural characteristics in polymer nanocomposites.

The all tested PCL/MWCNT scaffold formulations were biocompatible and maintained cell viability for at least 7 days (Fig. 8). The high percentages (>90 %) for all samples indicated minimal cytotoxicity, which was crucial for biomaterials intended for tissue engineering or medical applications. Combined with the high porosity (Fig. 3), these materials appeared to possess a combination of physical structure (porosity) and biocompatibility sufficient for potential use as tissue engineering scaffolds or other biomedical applications.

In vitro, demonstrated that the PCL/MWCNT scaffold, particularly at 3 wt.% MWCNT, not only maintained but also enhanced cell viability, which was critical for successful bone regeneration applications.8,24,28 Increased ALP activity, particularly at higher MWCNT weight percentage, indicated enhanced osteogenic differentiation, confirming the scaffold’s potential to support bone formation.23,24,29,33 Furthermore, morphological results were consistent with the findings of biocompatibility and ALP activity, further demonstrating that 3 wt.% MWCNT provided an optimal microenvironment for osteoblast growth and differentiation.21,24,30

This study concluded PCL/MWCNT scaffolds with higher weight percentage MWCNT exhibited increased hydrophilicity and porosity. These scaffolds were also suitable for alveolar bone regeneration. PCL/MWCNT scaffolds with 3 wt.% MWCNTs exhibited the highest MTT and ALP assay on days 1, 4, and 7.23,24 In summary, PCL/MWCNT scaffolds with 3 wt.% MWCNT exhibited the highest biocompatibility. Furthermore, its strength closely matched that of alveolar bone. Therefore, dentists can use it as a porous temporary scaffold to assist in bone reconstruction or regeneration when alveolar bone strength is insufficient during implant placement.
