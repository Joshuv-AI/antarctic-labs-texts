Quality of Pulmonary Hypertension Care Within Expert PH Centers: Patient and Provider Perspectives From a Multi‐Site Qualitative Study
Authors: Michelle M. Hall, Seppo T. Rinne, A. Rani Elwy, Renda Soylemez Wiener, Kari R. Gillmeyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive disease associated with high morbidity and mortality. Despite advancements, gaps in PH care quality persist, though the drivers of these gaps are incompletely understood. Thus, we sought to explore patient and provider perspectives on high‐quality PH care. We conducted a multi‐site qualitative study at three expert PH centers in the United States, involving telephone interviews with 20 providers and 22 patients. We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis, using the Institute of Medicine's (IOM) six domains of healthcare quality (safe, effective, patient‐centered, timely, efficient, and equitable) as an a priori coding scheme, with subcodes developed under each domain. We then compared codes across IOM domains and patient and provider data to identify overarching themes. Our analysis revealed three cross‐cutting themes that encompassed all six IOM healthcare quality domains, covered all identified subcodes, and represented both provider and patient perspectives: (1) “It takes a village,” that is, the expansive team needed to provide care for a patient with PH; (2) The need for care coordination within a PH center, with specialty pharmacies, and with community providers; and (3) PH centers as a model to deliver high‐quality care. Finally, participants offered clear recommendations for addressing gaps in care quality, including expanding village members, such as the addition of social workers, improving care coordination by improving patient follow‐up and tracking, and expanding access to PH centers by having a PH center presence in the community. These findings highlight key areas for targeted improvements in PH care quality.

Caring for patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH) is complex, requiring mastery of complicated diagnostic and therapeutic algorithms, a nuanced understanding of pathophysiology to correctly identify PH subgroups or phenotypes, and navigation of a rapidly expanding landscape of new therapies and their indications [1]. Despite improved recognition of the disease and advancements in PH treatment, outcomes for patients remain poor, with a median survival of only 7 years after diagnosis and significantly reduced health‐related quality of life [2]. These poor outcomes may in part be due to gaps in quality of PH care, including delays in diagnosis and treatment, inadequate diagnostic evaluations, inappropriate use of PH therapy (including both undertreatment in patients likely to benefit and overuse in patients likely to experience harm), and racial and economic disparities in PH care [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8].

Recently, the European Society of Cardiology proposed quality indicators for PH care in the areas of structures, diagnosis and risk stratification, initial treatment, follow‐up, and outcomes [9]. This is an important step to improving PH care quality. Yet, little work has been done to explore patients' or providers' experiences with PH care quality, or perceived facilitators and barriers to high‐quality PH care, information that could build upon prior work and help identify effective strategies to improve PH care quality.

In 2001, the Institute of Medicine (IOM, now the National Academy of Medicine) developed a framework for healthcare quality and identified six quality domains: safe, effective, patient‐centered, timely, efficient, and equitable (Table 1) [10]. This landmark framework has been instrumental in identifying quality gaps and implementing efforts to close those gaps, and importantly aligns with patients’ experiences with healthcare delivery [11]. Guided by this framework, we explored patient and provider perspectives of high‐quality PH care within expert PH centers.

Methods

Participant Recruitment

We conducted a multi‐site qualitative study of patients and providers at three expert PH centers in the United States. We recruited providers using purposeful sampling [12] by contacting relevant section chiefs or PH clinical leads at each site and asking them to recommend relevant providers. We identified additional providers through chain referral [13]. We sought perspectives from all providers involved in PH care at each site, regardless of discipline, including cardiologists, pulmonologists, advanced practice providers (APPs), PH pharmacists, and PH nurses. We recruited patients at Sites A and C through recommendations from PH providers and by posting flyers in PH clinic exam rooms. We were unable to recruit patients at Site B due to that site's Institutional Review Board (IRB) restrictions. Patient inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18 years or greater, (2) English‐speaking, and (3) diagnosis of any World Health Organization (WHO) PH subgroup. Participant characteristics are shown in Table 2. Given small sample sizes across individual WHO PH groups, findings are presented in aggregate across groups rather than compared by PH subgroup. Provider types not represented at a site were due to the lack of that provider type involved in PH care at that site, rather than an inability to recruit that provider type (e.g., Site A did not have pharmacists or nurses directly involved in PH care).

Data Collection and Analysis

We conducted qualitative telephone interviews with patients and providers at each of the three PH centers between August 2022 and November 2023 using a semi‐structured interview guide (see Data S1). Interviews were conducted by a pulmonologist with experience in qualitative research (KRG), audio‐recorded, and transcribed.

We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis [14], following the six phases of analysis: familiarization with the data, creating codes, generating initial themes, and reviewing, defining, and naming themes. We used the IOM's six domains of healthcare quality to create an a priori coding scheme for both patient and provider data and developed subcodes under the IOM domains, with the equitable domain assessed in terms of geographic and socioeconomic variation in care (consistent with the IOM definition) rather than race‐ or ethnicity‐based disparities. The coding scheme covered: referrals to PH centers, structures, processes, and resources within PH centers, nursing support for PH care, pharmacist support for PH care, other key support roles, provider knowledge of PH care, provider availability/accessibility, PH medication management, geographic issues, communication (between providers, between patients and providers, across healthcare systems), patient tracking and follow up, relationships between PH centers and community providers, telehealth, insurance/financial issues, other facilitators/barriers to quality PH care, and recommendations for improvement. IOM domain definitions and associated subcodes are shown in Table 1. Two coders (MMH, KRG) read all transcripts to assign codes to each piece of text, with frequent team meetings among all team members to discuss codes and come to consensus. Next, we compared subcodes across all IOM domains as well as across patient and provider datasets to identify broader patterns and generate initial themes. Through team discussion, we iteratively refined the themes to determine the scope, focus, and boundary of each theme. Our resulting three themes, (1) “It takes a village,” (2) The need for care coordination, and (3) PH centers as a model to deliver high‐quality care, each represent all six IOM domains, integrate both provider and patient perspectives, and encompass all identified subcodes from our initial coding scheme. We used NVivo 14 software and Microsoft Word for data organization. The Boston University IRB approved this study (H‐41416).

It Takes a Village

Patients and providers both agreed that it “takes a village” to care for someone with PH. Participants highlighted multiple components of this village that work in tandem to care for patients. Within a PH center, the village includes PH physicians, APPs, nurse coordinators, administrative staff, pharmacists, and social workers, among others: “In the clinic, I have a fellow, scribe, nurse, pharmacist, nutritionist, and, if I need them, a case manager and palliative care person…the room looks like a stuffed Volkswagen” —Physician 4, Site B. The village within the PH center allows for more equitable care by addressing financial and insurance barriers: “Because the huge expense of [PH] medications, a lot of them are out of the reach of many patients and each drug company has their assistance programs. [The PH nurse] helps navigate those for the patient”—Physician 1, Site C.

Outside of PH centers, community providers, including both primary care providers and community pulmonologists/cardiologists, are essential to a patient's PH care: “Often times, we're sharing the care of the patients with either a community pulmonologist or a primary care doctor, who may see the patients more frequently than we do”—Physician 2, Site A. These visits with community providers allow for more frequent monitoring and minimize patient travel, examples of timely and patient‐centered care: “Once the patient is diagnosed, started on treatment, we coordinate care with a local physician very closely so [the patient] can be closely monitor[ed] and follow[ed]‐up locally, for example, with blood work.… [and] routine imaging studies”—Physician 2, Site B.

Unique to PH care, specialty pharmacies also play an integral role providing therapy and monitoring PH patients, and interact with both patients and providers: “We have a dedicated team that we work with through [specialty pharmacy]….they support us with a pretty robust weekly report”—Pharmacist, Site B. Outside of the medical team, patients noted that their village includes their families, caregivers, and PH‐specific support groups: “A friend…who also has PH put together a little support group of other young women who have this diagnosis…this little community of people has been really helpful”—Patient 2, Site A. Care Coordination

Beyond the need for a comprehensive, multi‐layered village of people to care for patients with PH, providers noted that delivering high‐quality PH care required close coordination within and amongst those various layers: “coordination…becomes a necessity…these patients are complicated, and they need high‐level coordination”—Physician 5, Site A. Patients noted when care coordination was done well: “Having a collaborative approach across all providers involved, whether they're directly related to [PH] or not…goes a long way in improving outcomes and improving the patient experience”—Patient 2, Site A. Patients also noted frustration when care coordination broke down between team members: “The running joke in this house is that if we ever won the lottery, we would get every single doctor paid for one day, get them into one room and have them duke it out”—Patient 6, Site A.

Careful coordination amongst different disciplines and across inpatient and outpatient settings within the PH center facilitated timely, effective, and efficient care: “within a week, the [patient] had a planned admission, got a procedure, got enrolled in a clinical trial and already has drug delivered to his house. So, it's just a very fast, closed‐loop system”—Physician 4, Site A. Patients also noted that coordination between providers impacted timeliness of diagnosis: “This cardiologist…and my pulmonologist worked together, and I think that he connected dots between scleroderma and pulmonary hypertension”—Patient 8, Site A.

Likewise, coordination between the PH centers and specialty pharmacies was required for safe, efficient, and effective care: “we have a system where you email the patient information to the coordinator and PAs, and then it all magically happens…they reach out to the specialty pharm[acies], get the drug approvals and then once the drug's approved, the PAs often will do the titrations”—Physician 8, Site A. Patients echoed the importance of this relationship with specialty pharmacies, noting that a breakdown in coordination could severely compromise the safety of PH care: “[The specialty pharmacy does] not have a good follow‐through system. I called to get my Tyvaso refilled…a week or two in advance… It did not come… [the specialty pharmacy said], ‘The doctor didn't send us a new script.’ They didn't call me to tell me that they were having an issue. It just fell through the cracks”—Patient 6, Site A.

Finally, coordination between PH centers and community providers facilitated safe and effective care for patients: “The key is interfacing with [community providers] and taking that burden off their shoulders but co‐managing with them. That's the way you avoid unnecessary medical therapy on the one hand and then missed opportunities to intervene and avert bad outcomes on the other”—Physician 7, Site A. Coordination across health systems was also required for efficient and equitable care by preventing repeat testing and allowing patients to receive care closer to home: “Oftentimes I'm scheduling and coordinating testing at outlying hospitals. A lot of the patients are coming in from all over the state, and they don't want to come here to get CAT scans, Echos, those types of things”—Nurse coordinator, Site C.

PH Centers as a Model for High‐Quality PH Care

Given the need for a comprehensive, well‐coordinated village to care for patients with PH, participants highlighted that dedicated PH centers, with providers who have PH expertise and the requisite resources and infrastructure, should serve as the ideal model of PH care delivery: “Once we grew our clinic…and we went through the accreditation process from the [Pulmonary Hypertension Association], it was clear that we needed much more infrastructure support for our patients to run things smoothly”—Physician 2, Site B. As one provider who had experience setting up a satellite PH site in the community noted: “It's difficult to have a PH clinic at a community hospital in isolation…I think the reason [Satellite Site] is successful is because there is the pharmacy support system that exists at [Site B]”—Physician 5, Site B.

The infrastructure that exists within a PH center directly addresses the six IOM domains of quality care. For example, efficiency: “Having all those people that know their roles and can not only communicate efficiently, but have the expertise to get things done, whether it's actually doing a procedure like a cath or being really familiar with these medications and how they're managed just allows for more efficient patient care”—Physician 4, Site A. This infrastructure also allows for more equitable care by addressing financial and insurance barriers: “Very few of our patients have such good insurance that we don't have to worry about what medications we're prescribing…Because of that, we've gotten very good at figuring out how to get the treatment that the patient needs, either because we know what alternative medication will work for them, what assistance program…they will qualify for, or how to manage the whole legal issue”—Physician 1, Site C.

Patients also noted the benefits of receiving care within a PH center, highlighting the patient‐centeredness of care (“I really trust my [PH] team and I know that they're doing what's best for me and take what I want into account”—Patient 2, Site A) and the equitable care: “I had an issue a few months back where Medicaid [wasn't] going to cover my medications anymore for the [PH]. So, I brought the letter into [my PH physician and nurse] and it was taken care of within a few days”—Patient 3, Site C.

Patients contrasted the high‐quality care received within PH centers to the gaps in care they experienced in other settings. One patient noted, “I was in rehab for 2 months. It was probably the worst time of my life…. Nobody there understood… what the diagnosis meant, how it functioned, or how it worked”—Patient 8, Site A. Another said, “I just know from my experience with medicine that…people who see a lot of the same type of medicine do better than people who only…see an issue occasionally”—Patient 1, Site C. Providers also noted gaps in care quality outside of PH centers: “When patients are sent from outlying communities, their care to date when we first see them is questionable…typically…there has been no PH management”—Physician 3, Site C.

Recommendations to Improve PH Care Quality

Patients and providers shared concrete recommendations to improve the quality of PH care by: (1) strengthening the care team composition, such as ensuring social workers are available for both patient and provider support, (2) improving care coordination, thereby reducing the burden on patients, and (3) expanding the reach of PH centers by either establishing local branches or increasing telehealth utilization. Discussion

Across three expert PH centers, patients and providers identified multiple key components vital for high‐quality PH care. Notably, our findings highlight strengths in current PH care delivery and offer specific solutions to improve PH care quality.

Participants in our study highlighted the substantial number of distinct roles required to provide high‐quality PH care, both within and outside of healthcare teams. PH guidelines and accrediting bodies such as the Pulmonary Hypertension Association (PHA) have set forth staffing requirements for PH centers, including core and extended members of multidisciplinary teams [1, 9, 15]. Certain roles are consistently emphasized, such as clinical coordinators, but other roles vary across guidelines. Two roles in particular, pharmacists and social workers, were highlighted by both patients and providers in our study as key members of the care team that either provided significant benefit when present or left a sizable gap if absent. Indeed, prior work has shown that the inclusion of clinical pharmacists into care teams reduces drug‐related hospitalizations and resolves more drug‐related issues for patients with PH, directly influencing safety and effectiveness of care [16]. The addition of social workers into palliative care teams has been shown to improve patient‐reported quality of life in patients with chronic cardiopulmonary disease [17].

Our study also identified key roles not currently included in PH guideline recommendations. First, patients noted that having access to mental health providers with specific PH knowledge would improve patient‐centeredness of care. Patients with PH suffer from high rates of anxiety and depression, with prevalence rates of 28% and 37%, respectively, highlighting the need for more closely integrated mental health care into PH teams [18]. The incorporation of mental health into the care of other complex conditions, such as heart failure and cancer, is recommended by professional societies [19, 20]. Second, providers noted that the integration of APPs into PH care teams could improve access and timeliness of PH care. Prior work by the American Heart Association found that care models that utilized APPs had improved outcomes related to adherence, self‐management, and even death [21]. Given the similarities between cardiovascular care and PH care, it is likely that the benefits of APPs seen in this work would translate to improved outcomes for patients with PH.

Our findings underline the need for better integration of pharmacists, social workers, mental health providers, and APPs into PH care teams. Given the consistency of this pattern across both patients and providers, these findings warrant consideration in future revisions of clinical guidelines and accrediting criteria, though confirmation across a broader range of PH centers would strengthen the evidence base for any specific recommendation. Notably, patients in our study received at least some of their PH care at expert centers yet still felt as though key members were missing from their care team. It is likely that patients receiving care outside of expert centers are suffering from an even greater deficit in their village. Importantly, we recognize that expansion is not feasible for all health systems with different resources and capabilities. Organizations such as the PHA work in tandem with PH practices and their associated hospital administrations to assist with personnel decisions and play an important role in maintaining and expanding villages.

Both patients and providers highlighted the extensive care coordination required to manage a patient with PH. Each step of a patient's care, from obtaining a diagnosis to initiating therapy to arranging follow‐up, requires substantial care coordination and is vulnerable to gaps. Mechanisms to improve care coordination identified by our participants include establishing clear roles and responsibilities of each PH care team member and developing open lines of communication, both within and outside a PH care center. As noted in guidelines and accrediting bodies, a clinical coordinator is required [1, 9, 15]. This dedicated team member provides value through patient education and support, as well as inter‐ and intradepartmental organization. While studies have not yet evaluated their impact on PH care specifically, evidence shows that patient navigators improve quality of care for patients with other chronic diseases, particularly through care coordination [22]. Additionally, EHR capabilities could be optimized to track patient follow‐up, monitor test results, and communicate with providers and pharmacies outside of PH centers. The use of EHRs to standardize care, optimize efficiency, and improve care coordination has been considered for over a decade, especially within the field of heart failure, but has yet to be studied in PH care [23, 24, 25].

Finally, patients and providers in our study described PH centers as providing many of the resources and infrastructure needed to deliver high‐quality PH care, consistent with current guideline recommendations regarding center‐based care [1]. Our study adds to the growing evidence that PH centers provide many benefits to patients, with prior studies showing better disease monitoring, treatment rates, and even survival for patients cared for within PH centers compared to non‐center‐based care [26]. Our findings suggest that PH centers, at least among the urban centers included in our study, may also improve the efficiency, timeliness, patient‐centeredness, and equity of PH care. Both patients and providers in our study noted multiple benefits to PH center‐based care, including how the systems in place at these centers allowed them to address significant financial and insurance barriers unique to the PH pharmacologic landscape. We expect this is in large part due to the work done by the PHA to address quality of care when moving through the accreditation process.

Despite guideline recommendations and the mounting evidence to support PH center‐based care, patients are not routinely receiving their care at PH centers. One significant challenge noted by our participants in promoting PH expert centers as the model of care is the distance many patients must travel to receive care at a PH center. There are significant geographic and economic barriers to receiving PH center‐based care, with 14 U.S. states having no PH expert center [27, 28]. Importantly, participants in our study offered potential solutions to help expand the reach and capacity of PH centers and overcome these geographic barriers. Two of the three sites had already established satellite locations, potentially increasing the number of patients served and minimizing patient travel, all while being supported by the systems and infrastructure of the PH center. Some providers in our study also utilized telehealth visits, particularly for short term follow‐up or routine check‐ins, allowing patients to remain local while still benefiting from expert PH care. This area needs additional study, given the complex nature of PH and variable utilization of this entity [29, 30]. Patients who had participated in telehealth visits remarked on their convenience, and recommended further use, as noted in Table 4.

Our study has many strengths, including a multi‐site design that captured a diverse range of patient and provider perspectives, representation from all five WHO PH groups, and input from multiple members of PH teams, including physicians, nurses, APPs, and pharmacists. Our study also has limitations. First, as all included sites were expert PH centers located within urban settings, our findings may not be transferable to other settings, including PH care in the community or in more rural areas. For example, because our themes were derived from three centers, it is possible that different or additional themes would emerge from PH centers with different geographic locations, patient populations, or institutional resources. As such, while our reflexive thematic analysis rigorously and systematically captured the perspectives of patients and providers at the centers we studied, the three cross‐cutting themes identified in this study should not be viewed as a generalizable model of PH care quality. Future work should explore patient and provider perspectives of PH care across a broader set of PH centers before these findings are used to inform national‐level clinical guidelines or accreditation standards. Second, the potential for selection bias exists as participants had to be willing and able to be interviewed, and we were unable to recruit patients at one of the sites. Additionally, our recruitment method for patient participants likely led to underrepresentation of patients with lower health literacy, less engagement with their care team, and greater language barriers to accessing PH centers, patients who may face even greater equity‐related barriers to care than those captured in our sample. As a result, the equity gaps described here may understate the true scope of the problem for the broader PH population. Finally, although our sample included three patients with CTEPH, our interview guide did not specifically capture whether these patients had been referred to a CTEPH center or evaluated for pulmonary thromboendarterectomy or balloon pulmonary angioplasty, outcomes that are central to guideline‐recommended CTEPH care and that our study was not designed to assess.

There are many opportunities to expand upon our findings in the future. While our study did include patients from all PH groups, we did not have the sample size to compare results across groups, which is an important study in the future given the different resources needed for each PH type. Additionally, we did not have access to clinical outcomes for our patients or their health systems. Our assessment of the timely and effective domains was therefore based on patients' and providers' perceived experiences of delay, access, and care coordination, rather than quantitative system‐level metrics such as time to appointment or wait times for urgent or routine visits. Further evaluation of specifics such as appointment wait times, clinic scheduling structures, and coverage models for urgent referrals would expand our understanding of quality of care, specifically in the timely and effective domains. Moreover, future work could partner directly with organizations such as the PHA to leverage deidentified, center‐level accreditation data alongside existing center resources and staffing patterns, an approach that may reveal patterns in quality gaps that a qualitative interview‐based approach alone cannot capture. Group 4 PH, for example, presents a distinct opportunity for this type of partnership, given the unique evaluation and treatment needs of this rarer diagnosis. Lastly, future work should specifically examine the role of race and ethnicity in shaping PH care quality and patient experience, an important dimension our study was not designed to capture.

Conclusion

Patients and providers acknowledge the complexity and nuance required to receive and deliver high‐quality PH care. Both groups highlighted themes of “it takes a village,” care coordination, and expert PH centers that encompassed high‐quality care. Though gaps exist within these realms, there are clear patient‐ and provider‐endorsed avenues to improving the quality of PH care.
