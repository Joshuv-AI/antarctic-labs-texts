Multidimensional determinants of depression and non-depression in older adults: evidence from national data
Authors: Kübranur Çebi Karaaslan, Esra Bayrakçeken, Ömer Alkan, Muhammet Talha Akgün
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global demographic transition indicates a rapid shift toward an aging population structure. Good mental health in advanced ages is critical for a healthy aging. Problems such as depression reduce life expectancy and impose a societal burden. This study aims to examine, through a multidimensional approach, the sociodemographic, economic, health-related, functional, and psychosocial factors affecting depression levels among individuals aged 65 and over in Türkiye.MethodIn this study, microdata from the Türkiye Older Adult Profile Survey conducted by Turkish Statistical Institute (TurkStat) in 2023 was utilized. The analysis included a total of 10,348 individuals aged 65 and over who personally responded to perception-related questions. The dependent variable, depression level, was categorized as “not depressed,” “mild,” and “severe” according to Geriatric Depression Scale scores. In the analysis of the data, a generalized ordered logit model and marginal effects were employed due to the violation of the parallel lines assumption.FindingsDepression was common among older adults, with 46.71% exhibiting mild depressive symptoms. Specifically, 34.26% were mildly depressed and 12.45% were severely depressed, while 53.29% were classified as non-depressed. According to the analysis results, female gender, living alone, low-income level, and experiencing financial hardship significantly increase the risk of depression. In terms of health status, poor self-rated general health, sensory impairments (vision/hearing difficulties), history of falls, and the need for home care are the main factors increasing the likelihood of depression. An increase in functional independence (KATZ index) and maintaining a physically active lifestyle exhibit protective effects. Additionally, strong neighbor support, a higher number of support people, and a sense of autonomy in decision-making processes significantly reduce the level of depression.ConclusionGeriatric depression in Türkiye is a multidimensional phenomenon with complex socioeconomic and environmental determinants, in addition to biological factors. In particular, older women, individuals living alone, and economically disadvantaged groups are at high risk. The findings indicate that, to support healthy aging, clinical interventions alone are insufficient; strengthening social support networks, enhancing urban accessibility, and urgently developing inclusive public health policies that promote older adults’autonomy are required.

Introduction

The global demographic transition points to a rapid structural shift toward an aging population [1]. Current projections suggest that by 2030, one in six people worldwide will be aged 60 years or older, with the number of individuals in this age group increasing from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion. By 2050, the number of individuals aged 60 and over is expected to double, reaching 2.1 billion. In particular, the population aged 80 and over is projected to triple between 2020 and 2050, reaching 426 million [2]. Although this increased longevity is an indicator of advancements in healthcare systems [3, 4], the mental health and well-being of the aging population present significant challenges for health systems [5]. Good mental health in older age is essential for healthy aging [6], as mental health problems not only reduce life expectancy among older adults [7] but also impose burdens on individuals, families, and society [8, 9].

Depression represents one of the most prevalent mental health challenges among older adults [10]. Standardized diagnostic frameworks, such as the DSM-5 and ICD-11, characterize a depressive episode by a persistent depressed mood or anhedonia lasting at least two weeks, accompanied by cognitive, psychomotor, and vegetative symptoms that impair daily functioning [11, 12]. The consequences of depression in older adults extend far beyond psychological distress. The consequences of late-life depression extend far beyond psychological distress, significantly impacting overall quality of life and clinical outcomes.

Geriatric depression is a critical pathology that exacerbates existing clinical outcomes [13] and systematically constrains quality of life [14].This condition, which is directly associated with functional decline [15], accelerated progression of chronic diseases [13], and increased mortality [16], imposes a substantial economic and operational burden on healthcare systems. Furthermore, the causal association between depression and cognitive deterioration as well as the development of dementia [17, 18] elevates it beyond a purely psychiatric diagnosis, positioning it as a focal concern in the management of neurodegenerative processes. In this context, elucidating the etiological factors and preventive strategies of depression in older populations constitutes a clinical imperative for advancing healthy aging objectives and sustainable health policies.

Geriatric depression represents a significant yet frequently underdiagnosed burden for global healthcare systems. The prevalence of clinical depression among populations aged 60 and over is reported to range approximately between 28% and 31% [10, 19, 20]. A similar epidemiological pattern is observed in Türkiye, which is characterized by a rapidly aging demographic structure; the prevalence of geriatric depression has been reported as 49.9% [15]. These findings underscore the urgent need to develop targeted interventions addressing the socioeconomic determinants of mental health and to establish proactive public health policies in aging societies.

The development of depression in older adults has a multifactorial structure. Genetic predisposition [21], neurodegenerative diseases [17], and various biological characteristics [22] are among the key individual determinants that increase the risk of depression. However, socioeconomic disadvantages such as low levels of autonomy [23], unemployment, and low income can also trigger the development of depression among older adults [24]. Environmental and psychosocial factors likewise play a significant role in this process. In particular, a positive housing environment has been shown to be associated with a reduction in depressive symptoms [25]. Conversely, loneliness and social isolation [26], as well as life transitions such as retirement [27], emerge as prominent psychosocial risk factors that may facilitate the onset of depression in later life.

Functional limitations in activities of daily living among older adults are considered a significant risk factor for the development of depression [28]. In Türkiye, depression is reported to be prevalent among the older adult population and is associated with functional impairments, adverse health behaviors, and sociodemographic vulnerabilities [15]. Similarly, studies conducted in China indicate that older adults without limitations in Basic Activities of Daily Living (BADL) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) exhibit a lower risk of depression, whereas disabilities restricting these activities increase the likelihood of depression, particularly in rural areas [29]. In Malaysia, it has also been reported that older adults experiencing functional limitations in BADL and IADL have a higher risk of depression [30].

Depression in older adults is closely associated with chronic diseases and multimorbidity [31, 32]. An increasing burden of chronic illness elevates the risk of depressive symptoms through functional losses such as limitations in daily activities, mobility difficulties, and disability [33–35]. These findings suggest that the burden of chronic disease in later life constitutes one of the key determinants of depression in old age.

Although the international literature includes a substantial number of studies examining the determinants of depression among older adults [36–38], studies investigating the factors that may protect older adults from developing depression remain relatively limited. Furthermore, many previous studies have examined social, functional, or health-related determinants in isolation rather than considering how these dimensions jointly influence mental health outcomes. Given the complex and interconnected nature of aging processes, a multidimensional approach that simultaneously examines multiple domains may enable a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying depression in later life. The identification of protective factors is particularly important from a public health perspective, as such factors can guide the development of preventive strategies aimed at enhancing psychological resilience and promoting healthy aging. Factors such as strong social ties, functional independence, and better self-perceived health may serve as significant buffers against the emergence of depressive symptoms. However, the extent to which these factors jointly operate to protect older adults from depression has not been sufficiently explored. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the factors that protect older adults from depression by examining the combined effects of social, functional, and health-related characteristics.

Despite the growing body of literature on depression in older adults, several critical gaps remain. First, most existing studies tend to examine the determinants of depression in isolation, focusing separately on socioeconomic, health-related, or psychosocial factors, thereby overlooking the inherently multidimensional nature of mental health in later life. Second, a substantial portion of the literature relies on binary modeling approaches that fail to capture heterogeneity across different levels of depression severity. This limitation restricts the ability to understand how risk factors may differentially influence mild and severe depressive conditions. Third, although the risk factors of depression have been extensively studied, comparatively less attention has been given to identifying protective mechanisms against depressive outcomes, such as social support, functional independence, and perceived autonomy. Addressing these gaps requires an integrated analytical framework that simultaneously considers multiple domains and accounts for variation across levels of severity.

In this context, the study examines the sociodemographic and economic factors determining depression levels (not depressed / mild / severe) among older adults, investigates how health status and geriatric syndromes are associated with depression levels, analyzes the ways in which functional limitations and health behaviors shape these levels, and evaluates the role of social support and participation factors as well as psychosocial-environmental determinants in this process. Accordingly, the study makes several important contributions to the literature. First, by jointly analyzing the socioeconomic, health-related, functional, and psychosocial determinants of depression among older adults, it adopts a multidimensional framework and offers a more comprehensive perspective than domain-specific approaches. Second, unlike traditional binary models, the study explicitly models depression severity using an ordered structure, thereby enabling the identification of heterogeneous effects across different levels of depressive symptoms. Third, methodologically, it employs a generalized ordered logit model to address the violation of the parallel lines assumption, thus providing more flexible and robust estimates. Finally, by utilizing nationally representative microdata from Türkiye, the study generates policy-relevant insights for designing targeted interventions to support healthy aging and mental health.

Materials and methods

Survey data

In this study, the microdata set of the Türkiye Older Adults Profile Survey conducted in 2023 by the Turkish Statistical Institute (TurkStat) was utilized. The dataset, published in 2024, includes detailed information on the demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of individuals aged 50 and over, as well as data on health, care and social support, environmental conditions, social participation, and life satisfaction, in addition to household-level variables such as education level, income structure, and housing characteristics [39]. The sampling frame of the study is based on the National Address Database. A multistage cluster sampling design was employed in the data collection process. In the first stage, blocks were selected as primary sampling units using the probability proportional to size sampling method; in the second stage, household addresses were determined from each block using systematic random sampling. The dataset was designed to be representative of the entire population of Türkiye. Within the scope of the analysis, a total of 10,348 individuals aged 65 and over who personally responded to perception-related questions were included. According to the classification of the World Health Organization, individuals aged 65–74 are defined as young-old, those aged 75–84 as old, and those aged 85 and over as oldest-old [40].

Variables

The dependent variable of the study is the individual’s level of depression. According to the Geriatric Depression Scale, individuals scoring between 0 and 9 are classified as “not depressed,” those scoring between 10 and 19 as “mildly depressed,” and those scoring 20 and above as “severely depressed” [39] (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

The dataset was organized using Microsoft Excel, and descriptive statistics as well as all analyses were conducted using Stata 16. A generalized ordered logit model was employed to examine the factors affecting depression levels among older individuals. In this model, which is frequently used when the dependent variable has an ordinal structure, parameters are estimated using the maximum likelihood method [41]. One of the fundamental assumptions of the ordered logit model is the parallel lines assumption, which states that the coefficients of the independent variables remain constant across all response categories. This assumption must be statistically tested to evaluate the adequacy of the model [42]. In cases where this assumption is violated, the generalized ordered logit model, which relaxes these constraints, emerges as a more appropriate modeling approach. This model enhances flexibility by allowing the independent variables to be estimated with different coefficients across category thresholds. Although the unconstrained generalized ordered logit model contains more parameters compared to the classical ordered logit model, it offers important methodological advantages in terms of the flexibility it provides at the variable level and its ability to maintain the efficiency of parameter estimates in small samples [43–45].

Key Findings on the Sample

The descriptive statistics presented in Table 2 provide a comprehensive overview of the sociodemographic, economic, and health characteristics of the older adults participating in the study.

When sociodemographic and economic factors are examined, 44.88% of the participants are male and 55.12% are female. In terms of age distribution, 64.51% of the participants are aged 65–74, 27.74% are aged 75–84, and 7.75% are aged 85 and over. Regarding educational attainment, 33.45% of the participants have not completed any formal education, 50.79% are primary school graduates, and only 0.57% have a university degree. While 19.63% of the participants live alone, 80.37% do not. In terms of regional distribution, 60.99% reside in the Western region, 15.81% in the Central region, and 23.20% in the Eastern region. In addition, 7.75% of the participants receive monthly social assistance, and 51.68% report experiencing financial difficulties, indicating a notable level of economic vulnerability within the sample. In terms of health status and geriatric syndromes, 22.06% of the participants evaluated their general health as good, 48.67% as moderate, and 29.27% as poor. It is noteworthy that 78.79% of the participants have at least one chronic disease. Regarding visual difficulty, 44.96% report no difficulty, 44.93% report some difficulty, and 9.73% report severe difficulty. Similarly, in terms of hearing difficulty, 59.77% report no difficulty, while 29.61% report some difficulty. Furthermore, 8.74% of the participants have a disability report, and 23.80% have experienced a fall. When functional limitations and health behaviors are examined, 79.64% of the participants can go outside without assistance. While 67.44% find access to hospitals easy, 34.04% report difficulties in pedestrian mobility. In addition, 59.49% of the participants describe themselves as physically active, whereas 16.75% report a need for home care. In evaluating social support and participation indicators, 38.11% of the participants report participating in park activities, while 84.26% state that they participate in artistic activities. Participation in volunteer activities is notably low at 2.77%. Furthermore, 65.38% of the participants report being able to receive help from their neighbors. Additionally, 21.08% believe that older adults are subject to social exclusion. While 54.42% state that age limits their desires, 62.88% report that they have been unable to realize their life plans. When psychosocial and environmental factors are examined, 63.68% of the participants believe that older adults have a say in decision-making processes, and 60.20% state that the state protects older individuals. The proportion of those who report hearing or witnessing violence is 2.53%. In addition, 20.93% of the participants report having experienced a loss of control, and 18.13% indicate that they have family responsibilities. The proportion of individuals who feel safe while walking at night is 71.87%.

Model estimation

In this study, the multidimensional determinants associated with depression levels among older adults were analyzed using a generalized ordered logit model due to the violation of the parallel lines assumption. Before estimating the generalized ordered logit model, the parallel lines assumption was evaluated using the oparallel procedure in Stata. This procedure reports several tests of the proportional odds assumption, including the Brant, Wolfe-Gould, Score, Likelihood Ratio, and Wald tests. All tests indicated a statistically significant violation of the parallel lines assumption (p < 0.001), suggesting that the effects of the explanatory variables were not constant across response categories. Therefore, the generalized ordered logit model was employed as a more appropriate modeling strategy. Furthermore, the presence of multicollinearity among independent variables was examined using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The fact that all VIF values are below 5 indicates that there is no multicollinearity problem in the model [46]. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the factors affecting depression among older adults in Türkiye. An examination of demographic determinants highlights a pronounced gender disparity, characterized by a significantly higher vulnerability to late-life depression among women than men. The significant gender inequality found in the study is consistent with international literature [49, 50]. This vulnerability among women is considered to be the cumulative result of biological, socioeconomic, and structural disadvantages accumulated over the life course. In particular, lower levels of education and income among older women, along with financial stressors such as economic dependency and unemployment, negatively affect mental health [51, 52]. In addition, the higher prevalence of widowhood among older women [53], as well as physical limitations such as chronic diseases, pain, sleep disorders, and frailty, which are more frequently observed in women, increase the psychological burden [52, 54, 55]. Consequently, the higher likelihood of depression among older women can be interpreted not only as a result of biological factors but also as a cumulative reflection of lifelong socioeconomic and psychosocial inequalities.

The age-associated trajectory of depressive symptomatology exhibits a non-linear pattern, consistent with established international empirical frameworks [56]. International studies report that depression becomes more pronounced after the age of 80 [57]. The lower rates of severe depression observed in very advanced ages may reflect a survivor selection effect, rather than a direct protective effect of age, whereby more vulnerable individuals are lost earlier, and relatively more resilient individuals survive to older ages [58, 59].

Regarding socioeconomic predictors, structural stratification dynamics reveal that higher educational attainment and robust financial resources function as critical protective buffers against late-life depression [60, 61]. Formal education equips older adults with enhanced health literacy, institutional navigation skills, and more resilient cognitive coping strategies, such as subjective memory preservation [61]. Because advanced education operates as a primary pathway to stable life-course earnings, the protective capacity of income further reinforces psychological well-being by reducing cumulative exposure to material hardships and acute economic stressors [62]. Conversely, the positive association between the receipt of state-sponsored social assistance and elevated depressive profiles warrants a nuanced structural interpretation. Rather than implying that institutional welfare dependency directly induces psychological distress, this correlation reflects a selection effect of severe systemic vulnerability. Individuals qualifying for old-age social welfare may be disproportionately exposed to compounding lifecourse disadvantages, including chronic poverty, multi-morbidity, functional dependency, and marginalization, which can collectively precipitate depressive pathways.

The intersection between living arrangements and psychological distress is distinctly manifested in the elevated vulnerability to depression observed among older adults residing alone, a pattern thoroughly validated across international empirical literature [63–66]. The micro-level mechanisms linking residential isolation to geriatric depression are fundamentally anchored in psychosocial disruptions, including the acute trauma of spousal bereavement [53], the contraction of social roles, and the erosion of proximate social support networks. These deficits trigger persistent feelings of hopelessness, cognitive vulnerability, and perceived social exclusion [67–69]. At the physiological level, sustained loneliness and objective social isolation operate as chronic stressors that dysregulate neuroendocrine and immune pathways, including the disruption of circadian cortisol rhythms, heightened systemic inflammation, and functional alterations in neural networks regulating emotional processing [70, 71]. Furthermore, this relationship may be characterized by a bidirectional trajectory, wherein depressive symptom clusters can promote behavioral withdrawal and social avoidance, thereby amplifying the severity of isolation in a compounding feedback loop [72].

Spatial and geographic disparities further shape the epidemiology of late-life depression, particularly within the distinct macro-regions of Türkiye. Although regional microclimates—ranging from temperate Mediterranean coasts to arid and cold continental steppe zones—can theoretically trigger somatic and seasonal depressive symptoms due to extreme thermal exposure [73–75], attributing geographic variations solely to climatic determinism is insufficient. This spatial distribution is heavily confounded by macroeconomic disparities across the East-West axis of the country. Regions characterized by higher depression prevalence often exhibit lower aggregate regional income, limited industrialization, and structural underdevelopment [76]. Therefore, the geographic variance in geriatric depression may be predominantly driven by underlying socioeconomic polarization and unequal resource distribution rather than macroclimatic variations alone.

Depression among older adults is closely associated with both perceived general health status and geriatric syndromes. The decrease in both the likelihood and severity of depression as perceived health status improves suggests that subjective health perception plays a protective role in mental health during old age [77]. Previous intervention studies conducted among older adults with chronic neurological conditions have similarly demonstrated that depressive symptoms are strongly associated with poorer quality of life and reduced psychological well-being [78, 79]. Conversely, the presence of chronic diseases is associated particularly with an increase in mild depression levels, which may stem from the functional limitations imposed by chronic conditions [80]. When examining the relationships between depression and geriatric syndromes such as sensory impairments (vision and hearing difficulties), disability status, and history of falls, it is evident that all these factors increase the risk of depression and, in some cases, are also associated with its severity [81, 82]. This can be explained by the fact that sensory impairments restrict individuals’ social interactions, leading to isolation and consequently exerting negative effects on mental health ([83]. On the other hand, the higher likelihood of severe depression among individuals with disability reports and those who have recently experienced falls suggests that functional loss and physical frailty may be associated with the severity of depression [84]. Factors such as mobility limitations following falls, fear of falling again, feelings of dependency, and life restrictions due to disability may negatively affect psychosocial adjustment and contribute to the emergence of more severe depressive conditions.

The ability to go outside and maintain an active lifestyle significantly reduces the likelihood of depression among older individuals. Findings based on the KATZ Activities of Daily Living scale indicate that as the level of independence increases, the risk of depression decreases. Older adults who experience difficulties in pedestrian mobility are more likely to develop both mild and severe depression compared to those who do not. Similarly, older adults engaged in active daily activities are less likely to experience depression compared to those leading sedentary lifestyles. Older adults who require home care are more likely to experience depression than those who do not. Overall, when these findings are considered collectively, it is evident that higher levels of functional independence and mobility are associated with a significant reduction in the likelihood of depressive symptoms among older adults [85–87]. Similar findings have been reported in intervention studies showing that improvements in psychological well-being and quality of life are accompanied by reductions in depressive symptoms among older adults with chronic conditions [78, 79, 88]. Increased functional limitations in older age reduce quality of life [89] and lead to social isolation [90]. It has been reported that both reduced quality of life [91] and social isolation [92] contribute to higher levels of depression among older individuals. Therefore, the association between functional limitations and depression in older adults may be explained by these mechanisms.

Longer nighttime sleep duration has a protective effect against depression among older individuals. This finding is consistent with the literature [93]. Sleep disturbances may contribute to the development of depression by causing circadian rhythm disruptions, as well as dysregulation in melatonin and neuroinflammatory processes [94].

Indicators associated with social support such as going to parks, engaging in artistic activities, participating in volunteer work, being able to receive help from neighbors, having a higher number of available support persons, lower perceptions of social exclusion, reduced perceived limitations of age on personal desires, and stronger beliefs in the ability to realize future plans are associated with a lower likelihood of geriatric depression among older adults. The literature similarly emphasizes that social support reduces geriatric depression [95–97]. Social support alleviates feelings of loneliness and hopelessness [98, 99]. Social networks also buffer individuals from psychological distress following life events such as divorce, bereavement, illness, and living alone [100], while simultaneously enhancing self-efficacy [101]. The protective role of social support in geriatric depression may be attributed to its stress-buffering function. From the perspective of the protective role of neighborly support, and considering the rapid urbanization and increasing number of single-person households in Türkiye, strengthening neighborhood-based social networks can be an effective strategy to support mental health later in life. Municipalities, local governments, and community centers can develop neighborhood engagement programs, regular social gatherings, volunteer visitor programs, and age-friendly community initiatives to encourage social interaction and mutual support among older residents.

Older adults who perceive themselves as capable of making independent decisions, who believe that the state protects older adults and takes measures against social exclusion, who feel a sense of personal control, and who feel safe when walking alone at night are found to have a lower likelihood of depression. These concepts are closely related to the notion of autonomy in older adults [102].This finding is consistent with the literature [103, 104]. It has been reported that declining levels of autonomy among older adults are associated with increased unhappiness and depression [103]. Furthermore, older adults who perceive that family responsibilities hinder their personal plans are found to have a higher likelihood of experiencing depression.

Limitations of the study

Although this study identifies factors affecting depression levels among the older adults population in Türkiye using a nationally representative dataset, it has several limitations. Since the research is based on cross-sectional data, it cannot establish causal relationships between variables; rather, it identifies strong associations and probabilities. Key variables such as depression levels and health status are based on self-reports of participants. This may have led to social desirability bias or inaccuracies due to recall errors among older adults. The dataset relies on scale scores rather than clinical examinations. Due to the exclusion of individuals facing transportation difficulties, the prevalence of depression in this group may be underestimated. Furthermore, the study is limited to the variables available in the dataset of the Turkish Statistical Institute, and therefore genetic predispositions or specific biological determinants could not be included in the analysis.

Conclusion and recommendations

Conclusion

This study reveals the multidimensional and interrelated determinants of depression among the older adults population in Türkiye based on a nationally representative large sample. The findings confirm that geriatric depression is not merely a consequence of biological aging, but rather a reflection of socioeconomic vulnerability, loss of functional independence, and inadequacies in social support mechanisms.

One of the most notable findings of the study is the impact of gender inequality on mental health. The higher risk of depression among older women compared to men reflects the psychological consequences of accumulated socioeconomic disadvantages over the life course. From an economic perspective, while the protective effect of increased income levels and financial security is clearly observed, the positive association between receiving social assistance and depression indicates that individuals in need of such support are already exposed to high-risk structural factors such as poverty and chronic illness.

From a functional perspective, independence and mobility in activities of daily living emerge as protective mechanisms against depression. The direct association between geriatric syndromes such as sensory impairments and history of falls with the severity of depression demonstrates that physical health and mental health constitute an inseparable whole. In addition, the ability of individuals to make their own decisions and the presence of a social support network in their environment are key elements that enhance psychological resilience.

Recommendations

In light of the study findings, the following strategic interventions are recommended to support healthy aging policies:

Special psychosocial support programs should be developed to enhance economic security and reduce social isolation among older women. Mental health screenings should be integrated into the assistance process for individuals receiving social aid, considering the elevated risk of depression within this group, rather than focusing solely on their financial needs. Considering the increased risk of depression among older adults experiencing difficulties in pedestrian mobility, local governments should prioritize age-friendly urban planning, including appropriate sidewalks and access to parks. In primary healthcare services, vision and hearing impairments as well as fall risk should be regularly screened, and the detrimental effects of these limitations on mental health should be minimized through rehabilitation. Given the strong protective effect of social participation, older adults should be encouraged to engage in volunteer-based projects and artistic activities, and such initiatives should be expanded at the local level. Efforts should be made to raise societal awareness to ensure that older adults actively participate in decision-making processes, and support systems should be established to alleviate the psychological burden of family responsibilities such as caregiving.

Since this study is based on cross-sectional data, longitudinal studies should be conducted to better understand the causal mechanisms between identified risk factors and depression. Furthermore, future research should be expanded using comprehensive datasets that include clinical diagnoses and biological determinants beyond self-reported data.
