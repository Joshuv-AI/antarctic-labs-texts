Myocarditis induced by durvalumab in a patient with hepatocellular carcinoma: A case report
Authors: Lama Alfehaid, Nada Alsuhebany, Maha Aldoughaim, Rawan Bukhari, Walaa Sait, Kanan Alshammari, Mohammed Aziz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Durvalumab is an immune checkpoint inhibitor that targets PD-L1 and is used for malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma. Although effective, it may cause immune-related adverse events, including rare cases of myocarditis (0.06% to 1%), which usually appear early. This complication results from immune response-induced damage to the myocardium, highlighting the importance of prompt detection and management to prevent potentially fatal outcomes. This report describes a 70-year-old woman with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma who developed myocarditis after receiving a single dose of durvalumab. The patient presented with atypical chest pain, shortness of breath, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Initial investigations revealed elevated cardiac biomarkers, including Troponin I and CK-MB, and an ECG showing sinus tachycardia and right bundle branch block. A cardiac MRI confirmed non-ischemic myocardial injury, leading to a diagnosis of ICI-induced myocarditis. The patient was treated with high-dose corticosteroids, resulting in rapid clinical improvement and stabilization of cardiac function. A multidisciplinary approach involving cardiology, oncology, and clinical pharmacy was crucial in managing this complex case. This case highlights the importance of heightened vigilance among healthcare providers when administering ICIs, particularly durvalumab, due to the risk of myocarditis. Early detection and prompt intervention are essential to manage ICI-related adverse events and prevent severe outcomes. The successful treatment of this patient demonstrates the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in tackling the complexities of ICI-induced myocarditis. Further research is necessary to optimize management strategies and follow-up protocols for patients experiencing this rare but serious adverse event.

Introduction

Durvalumab is an immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI), a monoclonal antibody targeting programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), that has emerged as an essential therapeutic agent in the treatment of various malignancies, including urothelial, non-small cell lung, extensive-stage small cell lung, and unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma. It works by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, thereby enhancing the immune system's capacity to identify and destroy cancer cells (Imfimzi 2020). In hepatocellular carcinoma, in particular, durvalumab has been evaluated in HIMALAYA, a phase III study that provided pivotal evidence showing that the combination of tremelimumab and durvalumab (STRIDE regimen) significantly prolonged overall survival compared to sorafenib in patients with unresectable disease who had not undergone prior systemic therapy.2 Furthermore, durvalumab monotherapy has demonstrated non-inferiority to sorafenib in terms of overall survival for these patients (Abou-Alfa et al. 2022; Greten et al. 2021).

Despite its proven efficacy in improving patient outcomes, the use of durvalumab is not without risk. Like many ICIs, it can lead to a spectrum of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). These include dermatologic reactions (1.6%), colitis (1.6%), hepatitis (1%), nephritis (0.3%), and various endocrinopathies such as hypothyroidism (7.3%) and hyperthyroidism (1.4%), adrenal insufficiency (0.4%), type 1 diabetes mellitus (< 0.1%), and hypopituitarism/hypophysitis (< 0.1%) (Abou-Alfa et al. 2022). These irAEs can complicate treatment and impact the quality of life for patients. Among these, myocarditis stands out as a particularly severe but rare irAE, with the potential for fatal outcomes ranging from 20 to 50% (Johnson et al. 2016; Moslehi et al. 2018; Salem et al. 2018; Mahmood et al. 2018). Myocarditis was rarely reported in early clinical trials. The exact incidence of ICI-associated myocarditis is not well-defined, but it has been reported to occur in approximately 0.06% to 1% of patients treated with an ICI (Abou-Alfa et al. 2022; Mahmood et al. 2018). The mechanism behind ICI-induced myocarditis is believed to involve an abnormal immune response, where the immune system erroneously targets and damages cardiac tissue. ICI-induced myocarditis typically occurs early, with a median onset ranging from 17 to 34 days after the initiation of ICIs and following 1 to 2 doses of immunotherapy. Administering more than one ICI concurrently is a well‑recognized risk factor for myocarditis (Mahmood et al. 2018; Aghel et al. 2021).

Only a few case reports have documented myocarditis associated with durvalumab when it is used as a monotherapy (Mahmood et al. 2018; Aghel et al. 2021; Maetani et al. 2022; Frascaro et al. 2023). In this case report, we provided a comprehensive account of a patient who developed acute myocarditis after receiving durvalumab. We examined the clinical characteristics, diagnostic challenges, treatment strategies, and outcomes related to this rare but serious adverse event. Through this case report, we aim to contribute to the growing literature on durvalumab-induced myocarditis and to emphasize the importance of early recognition and intervention in enhancing patient outcomes.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old woman with a medical history significant for diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and bronchial asthma was recently diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma and pulmonary metastatic nodules. She presented to the emergency department with atypical chest pain radiating to her back and worsening shortness of breath with exertion over the past four days. Additionally, she reported experiencing more than three episodes of watery diarrhea per day and reduced oral intake. The patient denied any prior history of ischemic heart disease or inflammatory bowel disease. Her medication history included perindopril 5 mg orally once daily, salbutamol 2 puffs every six hours as needed, 50 units of insulin glargine, and durvalumab 1500 mg, which she received as a single dose two weeks prior to her presentation. Upon examination, the patient appeared ill, with dry and cold extremities. Her initial systolic blood pressure was low in the 90 s but improved with the administration of 1 L of normal saline. She was noted to be tachypneic, with a respiratory rate of 34 breaths per minute, and tachycardic and a heart rate of 120 beats per minute. She was afebrile, with a temperature of 36.8°C. Lung sounds were clear, and her abdomen was soft and lax, with no tenderness or pain reported while in the room air.

Initial investigations included an ECG that showed sinus tachycardia and a right bundle branch block (Fig. 1). Relevant laboratory data revealed elevated levels of the cardiac biomarker Troponin I at 10084.1 pg/mL, creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB) isoenzyme at 133.5 µg/L, and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) level at 242 pg/ml. A loading dose of aspirin and clopidogrel was administered, and heparin infusion was started due to the initial suspicion of non-ST-segment myocardial infarction. Additional laboratory findings indicated acute kidney injury, with a serum creatinine level of 1.8 mg/dL (baseline 1 mg/dL), metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.22, pCO2 at 26, pO2 at 57, and HCO3 at 10.6, as well as elevated liver enzymes with AST at 258 U/L and ALT at 269 U/L. There was also evidence of hyperglycemia, with a random blood glucose level of 459 mg/dL. Urinalysis revealed urine glucose levels ≥ 1000 mg/dL, with negative ketone results; complete laboratory findings are included in the supplementary material. A chest CT angiogram ruled out pulmonary embolism but noted atelectatic changes in the lower lobes and mild enlargement of lymph nodes around the liver. The oncology and cardiology teams were consulted, and the patient was transferred to the cardiology intensive care team for further management of potential sepsis. Upon arrival at the medical cardiology intensive care unit, the patient continued IV fluids, resulting in improved urine output and blood pressure. An insulin infusion was initiated, and sodium bicarbonate was administered to correct her metabolic acidosis. Further history gathering was performed, with family members reporting that the patient had started feeling unwell two weeks before her presentation, with worsening fatigue the previous week. A clinical pharmacist reviewed the patient’s past medication history and noted that she received her first dose of durvalumab 1500 mg two weeks prior to her presentation. This raised the possibility of ICI-induced myocarditis, which the medical cardiology critical care team included as a differential diagnosis. A respiratory panel multiplex was requested to rule out any possible viral-induced myocarditis, which returned negative results. A coronary angiogram was performed, showing normal coronary anatomy, supporting the diagnosis of ICI-induced myocarditis. Methylprednisolone sodium succinate was started at a dose of 1000 mg IV daily for four days. The following day, the patient began to show clinical improvement, with laboratory results indicating a drop in Troponin I to 4053.3 pg/mL, BNP to 197.6 pg/mL, along with improved urine output and liver enzymes (AST 165 U/L and ALT 195 U/L).

Four days later, a cardiac MRI was performed to confirm the diagnosis, revealing faint mid-wall delayed myocardial enhancement in the basal to mid-septum and basal inferolateral wall, consistent with non-ischemic myocardial injury (Fig. 3). The function of the left and right ventricles improved and returned to normal, with an ejection fraction of 55% (Fig. 4). The patient was then switched to oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg divided into two doses per day (35 mg orally every 12 h) to be continued for four weeks. The patient was observed for 12 days, showing stable clinical, echocardiographic, and laboratory results throughout her hospital course. The oncology team has discontinued durvalumab permanently and is currently considering a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, either lenvatinib or sorafenib, for this patient. Discussion and conclusion

This case report describes a rare but serious case of myocarditis due to durvalumab in a patient with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma and highlights the complexity and challenge in managing ICI therapy. Immunotherapy, especially those targeting the PD-1/PD-L1, has revolutionized oncology by providing durable responses in cancer management (Imfimzi 2020); Shiravand et al. 2022; Tajiri et al. 2017; Thompson et al. 2024). However, their association with irAEs demands increased vigilance for early intervention to prevent potential life-threatening outcomes.

Multiple cohort and pharmacovigilance studies now identify patient‑ and treatment‑related factors that increase the risk of ICI‑associated myocarditis. The most substantial evidence comes from combination regimens that expose patients to more than one checkpoint target (adjusted reporting odds ratio ≈ 4–5 compared to monotherapy) (Salem et al. 2018). Additionally, consistently reported risk factors include pre‑existing metabolic‑cardiovascular conditions (such as diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia), prior or concurrent thoracic radiotherapy, older age (> 65 years), and very early onset of symptoms, typically within the first two treatment cycles (Salem et al. 2018; Mahmood et al. 2018; Aghel et al. 2021). Our patient exhibited several of these features: she was an older adult with long‑standing type 2 diabetes managed with basal insulin, and myocarditis developed after the first dose of durvalumab, meeting the “early‑onset” criterion. Although she received durvalumab alone (not a high‑risk combination regimen) and had no prior chest irradiation, the clustering of metabolic comorbidities may have lowered her threshold for immune‑mediated myocardial injury. Recognizing such risk patterns at treatment intiation can support closer biomarker monitoring and prompt cardiology referral, enabling timely intervention that proved lifesaving in this case (Thompson et al. 2024).

The clinical presentation of myocarditis in our patient, characterized by atypical chest pain, elevated cardiac biomarkers, and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, is in alignment with previous descriptions of ICI-induced myocarditis (Salem et al. 2018; Mahmood et al. 2018; Aghel et al. 2021; Robert 2020). The diagnostic pathway conducted in our report emphasizes the necessity of collaboration among multidisciplinary teams. Furthermore, this highlights the essential role of the interdisciplinary cardio-oncology discipline in the prevention, diagnosis, and management of cardiovascular sequelae in patients treated for cancer (Lyon et al. 2022).

Nevertheless, the normal coronary angiogram, along with the cardiac MRI findings of non-ischemic myocardial injury, points toward a diagnosis of ICI-induced myocarditis rather than ischemic or viral etiologies. This demonstrates the role of advanced imaging modalities in distinguishing myocarditis from other causes of myocardial injury (Aghel et al. 2021; Schneider et al. 2021; Axelrod et al. 2022). In terms of management, early use of high-dose corticosteroids was effective in reversing cardiac inflammation and stabilizing the patient's clinical condition, consistent with current management guidelines for ICI-induced myocarditis (Schneider et al. 2021; Axelrod et al. 2022; Palaskas et al. 2024). This confirms that myocarditis was related to ICI since it was reversed by the use of high-dose corticosteroids.

In patients being treated for severe ICI-induced myocarditis, monitoring should include echocardiogram, liver function tests, troponin levels, BNP, imaging, and creatine phosphokinase to rule out the presence of myositis. Ruling out myositis is necessary since ICI-induced myocarditis and myositis may present concomitantly. Once ICI-induced myocarditis is suspected, methylprednisolone should be initiated at a dose of 1 g/day for a duration of 3–5 days. However, close monitoring is necessary, and intensification of therapy may be warranted if symptoms do not improve within 24 h. Once cardiac function returns to baseline, a steroid taper over 4–6 weeks is recommended (Schneider et al. 2021). These measures were followed precisely with our patient, who experienced improvement within the first 24 h of receiving methylprednisolone.

The mechanism of ICI-induced myocarditis is not yet fully understood. A study examining peripheral blood samples from patients with ICI-induced myocarditis demonstrated higher levels of activated CD8 + cytotoxic T cells. This effect was consistent with findings from animal models where CD8 + cytotoxic T-cell infiltration was found in the myocardial tissue of mice (Axelrod et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2022). This may explain the efficacy of steroids in the treatment of ICI-induced myocarditis (Palaskas et al. 2024). Few cases in the literature have described ICI-induced myocarditis following durvalumab monotherapy. Similar to our patient, rapid clinical improvement was noted after administration of glucocorticoids (Mahmood et al. 2018; Aghel et al. 2021; Maetani et al. 2022; Frascaro et al. 2023).

Our case further sheds light on the importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention in ICI-induced myocarditis, especially since our patient experienced this life-threatening side effect after receiving a single dose of durvalumab. Late recognition of ICI-induced myocarditis can result in severe cardiac dysfunction and mortality, with fatality rates reportedly ranging as high as 50% (Johnson et al. 2016; Moslehi et al. 2018; Salem et al. 2018; Mahmood et al. 2018). There are no specific tests to distinguish ICI-induced myocarditis from acute coronary events. Troponin levels increase in patients with acute ICI-induced myocarditis; therefore, monitoring troponin trends along with clinical correlation is necessary to achieve an accurate diagnosis (Herrmann et al. 2022; Barac et al. 2024). The rapid improvement in troponin levels and ejection fraction after corticosteroid therapy in our case illustrates the benefit of early intervention in mitigating cardiac injury (Palaskas et al. 2024). The long-term cardiac outcomes of patients with ICI-induced myocarditis have not been fully clarified, and optimal management strategies and follow-up protocols require further research.

Durvalumab‑induced myocarditis is a rare yet potentially life‑threatening irAE that requires heightened clinical vigilance and prompt, multidisciplinary management. In this case, quick initiation of high-dose intravenous corticosteroids proved crucial, resulting in rapid clinical recovery. The long‑term cardiac outcomes of patients who experience ICI‑associated myocarditis remain uncertain and warrant systematic prospective investigation.
