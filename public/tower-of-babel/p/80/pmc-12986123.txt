Evaluation of an ESAT-6 Recombinant Skin Test Reagent for Bovine Tuberculosis Diagnosis in Guinea Pigs and Cattle
Authors: Matías Iván Gatto, Sol Ferrero, Florencia Tonini, Marcela Desio, Gerardo Rodríguez, Fernando Martino, Bernardo Alonso, Claudio Paolazzi, Claudia Argüelles, Gustavo Helguera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bovine purified protein derivative (PPD-B), a crude protein extract from Mycobacterium bovis cultures, has been the standard reagent for delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) testing in cattle, but its undefined composition and variability compromise reproducibility and specificity. To address these limitations, we developed and evaluated RRbTB-E, a recombinant fusion protein comprising ESAT-6, as a defined alternative for skin testing. RRbTB-E was produced in Escherichia coli, purified by affinity chromatography, and characterized by SDS-PAGE and Western blot. In M. bovis-sensitized guinea pigs, RRbTB-E induced robust DTH reactions comparable to PPD-B, with consistent performance across six independent experiments and long-term stability after storage at 4–8 °C for more than 900 days. Furthermore, RRbTB-E did not induce significant reactions in non-sensitized or Mycobacterium avium–sensitized animals, confirming antigenic specificity. RRbTB-E also elicited DTH responses in Mycobacterium tuberculosis–sensitized guinea pigs. In naturally infected cattle, it triggered responses similar in magnitude to PPD-B, while remaining negative in non-infected animals. These findings support RRbTB-E as a stable, reproducible, and specific candidate for standardized intradermal testing in bovine tuberculosis diagnosis.

1. Introduction

Bovine tuberculosis (bTB), primarily caused by Mycobacterium bovis, is a globally important disease affecting livestock health and agricultural economies [1]. This disease imposes substantial losses on cattle fertility and milk production on a global scale, which leads to economic burden of around US$ 3 billion per year due to the expenses of control programs, reduced productivity, and trade restrictions [2]. Although bTB is effectively controlled in most high-income countries through strict test-and-cull programs, it remains endemic across the majority of low- and middle-income nations, where socioeconomic constraints have prevented the systematic implementation of national control strategies, resulting in sustained economic impact and continued human morbidity and mortality [3,4].

The cornerstone of control programs for bTB has historically relied on the detection and removal of infected animals, predominantly through intradermal tuberculin skin test (TST), which assesses delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) responses to M. bovis antigens [5]. However, the traditional bovine purified protein derivative (PPD-B) used in these tests, despite its utility, suffers from inherent limitations due to its undefined composition and variability in antigen concentration [6]. Nevertheless, the most critical limitation of the TST lies in its potential for nonspecific reactivity in animals exposed to Mycobacterium avium subsp. paratuberculosis (MAP)—the etiological agent of paratuberculosis—as well as to several non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) [7,8]. In addition, its inability to effectively discriminate between M. bovis-infected animals and those vaccinated with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)—a deficiency referred to as the lack of DIVA (Detecting Infected among Vaccinated Animals) capability—poses a major constraint to the implementation of BCG-based vaccination programs for bTB control, despite evidence that BCG vaccination mitigates disease severity in cattle [9]. Defined antigens such as ESAT-6, which are absent from BCG strains, provide a strong conceptual basis for DIVA-compatible diagnostic approaches; however, experimental validation of this compatibility remains essential for each new diagnostic format. These challenges highlight the urgent need for more specific, sensitive, and standardized diagnostic reagents to accurately identify infected animals and support eradication efforts [10].

Over the last quarter of the century, novel skin tests based on defined and specific antigens have been developed. These reagents used Mycobacterium tuberculosis and M. bovis-specific proteins such as 6 kDa early secretory antigenic target (ESAT-6) and CFP-10, which are encoded by the Region of Difference 1 (RD1) genomic region, that is absent from BCG and most NTM [11,12,13], an important molecular distinction confers high specificity [14]. Two main antigen formats have been explored: recombinant fusion proteins that include ESAT-6 and CFP-10 [15], plus Rv3615c [9,16] expressed in Escherichia coli, and synthetic peptide cocktails of these proteins [16]. Preliminary studies show that both formats exhibit good diagnostic performance, with fusion proteins achieving high specificity in BCG-vaccinated animals and high sensitivity in M. bovis infections, while peptide cocktails display comparable sensitivity to the standard PPD-B test [9,16]. Additional advantages include lower production costs, ease of standardization, improved animal welfare due to milder reactions, and demonstrated safety in preliminary trials.

Expression of ESAT-6 protein fused to His-tag in E. coli has been reported to end up entrapped in inclusion bodies, independently whether they were expressed in shake flasks or bioreactors [17]. Moreover, approximately only two-thirds of ESAT-6 in E. coli inclusion bodies could be resolubilized to monomer molecules with high concentration of urea [18,19]. In contrast, soluble ESAT-6 and CFP10 were reported to be expressed in soluble form in E. coli BL21-DE3 [20], cloned into a plasmid coding for thioredoxin, which is generally used to improve stability and solubility of proteins [21].

As an alternative, in this work we show the generation of RRbTB-E, an ESAT-6 fusion protein engineered in a pMAL-c4 plasmid with a foldon sequence fused to Maltose Binding Protein (MBP) to improve its solubility and stability during expression and purification [22,23]. RRbTB-E was expressed in E. coli, mostly soluble, and could be purified by affinity chromatography. We evaluated its ability to elicit DTH responses compared to PPD-B in guinea pigs sensitized with M. bovis. Furthermore, we evaluated its antigenic specificity by testing for the absence of significant reactions in non-sensitized animals or those sensitized with M. avium. We also investigated whether RRbTB-E could induce DTH responses in M. tuberculosis-sensitized guinea pigs and, critically, whether it would trigger reactions of similar magnitude to PPD-B in naturally infected cattle while maintaining negative results in uninfected animals (Figure 1). Collectively, these findings provide evidence of RRbTB-E as a stable, reproducible, and antigen-specific candidate for standardized intradermal testing in bTB diagnosis, thereby advancing the development of diagnostic tools compatible with control programs that incorporate vaccination.

2. Results

2.1. Expression and Purification of RRbTB-E in E. coli

RRbTB-E was expressed in E. coli. The complete expression and characterization of the recombinant protein is shown in Figure 2. Induction of RRbTB-E expression was evidenced by SDS-PAGE, showing a clear band at 77 kDa, corresponding to the expected molecular weight. Furthermore, after cell disruption, densitometry analysis of the SDS-PAGE showed a markedly higher proportion of the recombinant protein in the soluble fraction from the supernatant (17%) compared to the insoluble fraction in the pellet (7%) with inclusion bodies (Figure 2A, Table S1).

The MBP affinity purification profile (Figure 2B) showed that the peak of the protein fractions obtained with the elution buffer was enriched in RRbTB-E, as evidenced by the presence of a unique protein band at 77 kDa. Identity of the recombinant protein was further confirmed by Western blot, using an anti-His antibody (Figure 2C).

2.2. Evaluation of Skin Test Reaction in Guinea Pigs

To evaluate the immunodiagnostic performance of RRbTB-E, a dose–response intradermal skin test was conducted in M. bovis-sensitized guinea pigs. Animals were inoculated with increasing doses of purified RRbTB-E (ranging from 0.5 to 32 µg), and skin reaction diameter was measured after 24 h. A clear dose-dependent response was observed, with induration diameters exceeding the 5 mm threshold (Figure 3A). The RRbTB-E reaction response at 8 µg and 32 µg was comparable in magnitude to that elicited by 1 µg of the standard PPD-B reagent.

In contrast, the MBP Fusion Protein Control (MFP Control), an irrelevant recombinant protein produced in the same plasmid backbone as RRbTB-E but lacking the ESAT-6 sequence, induced no significant skin reaction. Statistical analysis using Repeated Measure (RM) ANOVA showed significant differences between PPD-B or RRbTB-E (at 8 and 32 µg) and the MFP control (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was detected between RRbTB-E and PPD-B (Figure 3A). These results support the ability of RRbTB-E to elicit a specific DTH response in sensitized animals and validate its potential as a defined reagent for skin testing in bovine tuberculosis.

Based on these findings, 8 µg was established as the minimal RRbTB-E dose with biological activity equivalent to 1 µg of PPD-B in the DTH skin test. Since reference PPD-B preparations contain 32,500 International Units (IU) per mL and a protein concentration of 1 mg/mL, the administration of 1 µg corresponds to 32.5 IU per animal. Thus, 8 µg of RRbTB-E is equivalent to 32.5 IU of PPD-B.

Next, a potency assay was performed to compare the biological activity of RRbTB-E with that of the reference PPD-B reagent, using the equivalent bioactive doses. In M. bovis-sensitized guinea pigs, RRbTB-E induced skin reaction diameters that were comparable in magnitude to those elicited by PPD-B, yielding a relative potency of 123.8% (95% confidence interval (CI): 70.2–217.9%) in comparison to the reference PPD-B reagent, reflecting the inherent variability of in vivo biological potency assays (Figure 3B). This result indicates that 8 µg RRbTB-E provides biological activity within the acceptable range and supports its suitability for further diagnostic in field evaluation.

After confirming that RRbTB-E elicited a robust DTH response in the M. bovis-sensitized guinea pigs model and establishing a biologically equivalent dose to PPD-B, we next evaluated its antigenic specificity. RRbTB-E did not induce skin reactions in non-sensitized guinea pigs, showing no significant difference in comparison to the negative control MFP protein (Figure 4A). As expected, M. bovis-sensitized guinea pigs displayed clear RRbTB-E intradermal responses, consistent with the responses observed for PPD-B and PPD-A, which have been reported to cross-react with M. bovis (Figure 4B). Importantly, in animals sensitized with M. avium subsp. avium, RRbTB-E did not exhibit reactions above 5 mm, showing a significant difference compared to the positive control PPD-A (Figure 4C). These findings provide evidence that the recombinant reagent is antigenically specific and shows no significant cross-reactivity. Although M. avium has been reported to induce cross-reactive responses with PPD-B, potentially compromising the specificity of the tuberculin test, no such effect was observed with the PPD-B batch used here. Conversely, we established that animals sensitized with M. bovis cross-reacted with PPD-A, underscoring the complexity in the interpretation of results when using complex antigenic reagents.

Next, we tested whether RRbTB-E could elicit a DTH response in guinea pigs sensitized with M. tuberculosis. Animals were inoculated with the negative control MFP, the positive control Tuberculosis Purified Protein Derivative from M. tuberculosis (Batch I-94) (PPD-T), and RRbTB-E, with the skin reaction diameters measured after 24 h (Figure 4D). RRbTB-E induced a DTH response equivalent to PPD-T, and both reagents elicited significantly larger reactions than MFP. This finding is consistent with the presence of the RD1 region, which encodes the ESAT-6 antigen, in both M. bovis and M. tuberculosis.

We further evaluated RRbTB-E in the guinea pig model by assessing its stability and reproducibility. For the stability analysis, two production batches of RRbTB-E (I and II) were stored at 4–8 °C for over one year. Intradermal reactivity was measured in M. bovis-sensitized guinea pigs at multiple time points, and skin reaction diameters remained stable throughout the observation period, including after 2.5 years of storage (Figure 5A). These results indicate that RRbTB-E retains its biological activity during long-term storage under standard refrigerated conditions. Reproducibility was examined across six independent experiments comprising a total of 34 sensitized guinea pigs. A control chart confirmed the RRbTB-E consistency across tests (Figure 5B). The coefficient of variation (CV) was 10%, indicating high reproducibility in this biological assay, despite the inherent variability of intradermal skin testing. All animals tested across the six experiments developed a significant intradermal reaction to the recombinant reagent, further supporting the robustness of RRbTB-E in multiple in vivo functional biological testing.

The pooled data from these DTH assays (Figure 5C) demonstrated that RRbTB-E elicited a consistent and robust response, with all 34 sensitized guinea pigs exhibiting positive skin reactions (100% reactivity). This performance matched that of the standard positive control, PPD-B, which induced skin reaction diameters larger than the 5 mm threshold in all 34 tested animals (100%). In contrast, the MFP negative control failed to elicit any response, with all six animals remaining negative, confirming the specificity of the assay and supporting the proper antigenic activity of RRbTB-E.

2.3. Evaluation of Skin Test Reaction in Cattle

Having confirmed in guinea pigs that RRbTB-E is biologically active, specific, stable, and reproducible, we next evaluated its performance in naturally infected cattle, a clinically relevant setting. Using as reference the standard 3250 IU in 0.1 mL PPD-B dose in cattle [24], an initial RRbTB-E dose of 0.8 mg was selected. Two independent field trials were conducted in naturally infected animals, and in the first trial, a non-infected group was included to evaluate specificity. Animals were intradermally inoculated in the mid-cervical region and skin reaction diameter was measured after 72 h following the standard protocol [25].

In the first trial, non-infected animals (n = 3) displayed no significant skin induration after inoculation with either RRbTB-E or PPD-B, yielding an apparent specificity of 100% (3/3; 95% CI: ~29–100%) for the recombinant reagent (Figure 6A). In contrast, naturally infected cattle (n = 12) showed DTH responses to RRbTB-E comparable to those elicited by PPD-B, corresponding to an estimated sensitivity of 66.7% (8/12; 95% CI: ~35–90%) (Figure 6B). However, the small number of non-infected animals resulted in a wide CI for specificity, so this estimate should therefore be interpreted with caution. Although positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV) were calculated, these parameters were strongly affected by the high prevalence of infected animals and the small number of non-infected controls. Consequently, PPV and NPV were not considered reliable indicators of diagnostic accuracy in this proof-of-concept study.

To further improve sensitivity under real-world conditions, in the second trial the RRbTB-E dose was increased to 1 mg per animal (Figure 6C). Under these conditions, all naturally infected animals (n = 8) were identified by RRbTB-E, yielding a sensitivity of 100% (8/8), matching the performance observed with PPD-B. As no non-infected animals were included in this trial, specificity and predictive values could not be estimated. Importantly, the MFP control did not induce significant reaction, confirming the antigen specificity of RRbTB-E-induced responses in infected animals.

3. Discussion

The development of defined antigen reagents for DTH testing in bTB has become a necessary step toward improving diagnostic precision and enabling integration with vaccination programs. The conventional PPD-B TST, although widely used, is a complex and poorly standardized mixture of mycobacterial proteins that exhibits significant batch-to-batch variability and limited specificity—particularly in animals vaccinated with BCG or exposed to environmental mycobacteria [11,13].

ESAT-6 from M. bovis is one of the best characterized virulence associated antigens and a central component of defined diagnostic reagents. Encoded by the esxA gene within the RD1 region of virulent M. tuberculosis and M. bovis strains [26], ESAT-6 is not present in all attenuated M. bovis BCG substrains [27]. This absence has provided the biological rationale for its use in DIVA-oriented diagnostic strategies. Nevertheless, DIVA compatibility must be experimentally demonstrated for each specific diagnostic format and cannot be assumed solely based on antigen composition. As a potent T-cell antigen, ESAT-6 elicits strong IFN-γ responses, activates CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and promotes the maturation of Antigen Presenting Cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells [28,29]. These immunological properties have supported their incorporation into diagnostic platforms for both humans and animals [28,29,30]. However, recombinant production of ESAT-6 remains challenging due to its hydrophobicity, high α-helical content, and intrinsic tendency to form homodimers and higher-order oligomers, which contribute to aggregation and inclusion body formation in E. coli [18,31,32]. Moreover, efficient recovery of recombinant ESAT-6 typically requires solubilization under denaturing conditions, employing high concentrations of urea or guanidine-HCl to disaggregate the protein [18,19]. Although soluble expression can be achieved under specific conditions [20], these structural constraints underscore the need for optimized formats when developing ESAT-6–based diagnostic reagents.

To overcome these limitations, fusion to highly soluble carrier proteins has emerged as an effective strategy to improve the solubility and proper folding of recombinant targets [33]. In the present study, we engineered RRbTB-E by fusing ESAT-6 to MBP, a carrier known to enhance solubility, folding efficiency, and protein yield in E. coli expression systems [33,34,35,36]. The MBP intrinsic solubilizing properties can support proper folding either passively or via chaperone-like activity [37,38]. To further increase structural stability, we incorporated a foldon domain, which has been shown to promote trimerization, improve conformational integrity, and preserve antigenic epitopes in fusion proteins [39,40]. Using this design, RRbTB-E was successfully expressed in soluble form with limited retention in inclusion bodies, purified by affinity chromatography, and verified by SDS-PAGE and anti-His Western blot analysis.

After the reagent purification, the functional DTH activity of RRbTB-E was evaluated in guinea pigs sensitized with heat-inactivated M. bovis AN5, which is the standard model to perform PPD-B bio-assay potency testing [24,41,42]. A dose response activity was observed for RRbTB-E, while we did not detect a significant response with the highest dose of the negative control MFP that did not include the ESAT-6 sequence. Since RRbTB-E is a recombinant protein produced in E. coli, it is reasonable also to consider the potential contribution of residual endotoxin to the observed skin responses. This possibility, however, is unlikely to explain the DTH reactions, as the MFP control protein—produced under identical expression, purification, and formulation conditions—failed to elicit any significant cutaneous reactivity. Collectively, these data indicate that the responses induced by RRbTB-E reflect antigen-specific, cell–mediated immunity rather than nonspecific inflammation due to endotoxin contamination.

We also performed a parallel test to establish the RRbTB-E dose equivalent to PPD-B, in which 8 µg of RRbTB-E elicited an equivalent response to 1 µg of PPD-B. Although the estimated relative potency of RRbTB-E fell within the acceptable range defined by regulatory guidelines for biological assays, the associated confidence interval was relatively wide. This degree of variability is a well-recognized feature of in vivo tuberculin potency assays performed in small groups of sensitized animals, where biological heterogeneity, individual DTH responsiveness and limited group size contribute substantially to dispersion of the estimates. Importantly, the parallel-line model assumptions were fulfilled, with no significant deviations in linearity or parallelism between RRbTB-E and the PPD-B reference, supporting the validity of the potency estimation despite the broad confidence interval. Taken together, these results indicate that RRbTB-E displays biological activity comparable to PPD-B, while highlighting the intrinsic variability of guinea pig–based bioassays and the need for further evaluation in larger-scale or field-based studies.

Several factors likely account for the higher dose of RRbTB-E required to elicit a DTH response equivalent to that induced by PPD-B in denatured M. bovis-sensitized guinea pigs. PPD-B is a highly heterogeneous mixture containing hundreds of denatured mycobacterial proteins, providing a broad repertoire of T-cell epitopes that generates a stronger cumulative recall response than a single, structurally defined antigen [43,44,45]. In contrast, the ESAT-6 sequence in RRbTB-E represents approximately 13% of the total fusion protein. Therefore, administration of 8 µg of RRbTB-E delivers roughly 1 µg of ESAT-6, a mass comparable to that of PPD-B but without the extensive epitope multiplicity present in complex mycobacterial extracts [44,46]. Moreover, PPD-B contains proteins with diverse molecular weights and physicochemical properties, which are likely to enhance antigen uptake and processing by different antigen-presenting cell subsets and promote presentation through multiple MHC class II pathways, further increasing its T-cell stimulatory capacity per microgram compared to a single purified antigen. Therefore, its T-cell stimulatory capacity per microgram should be substantially greater than that of a single purified protein, even one such as ESAT-6 that retains a native conformation [45].

Following the confirmation that RRbTB-E induced a clear and dose-dependent DTH reaction in M. bovis-sensitized guinea pigs comparable to the reference PPD-B, we examined whether this response was specific. In non-sensitized animals, RRbTB-E did not trigger skin reactions, behaving similarly to the MFP negative control. This is expected because ESAT-6 contains only a few defined T-cell epitopes and requires prior exposure to M. bovis or other RD1-positive mycobacteria to induce a DTH response [46]. Likewise, guinea pigs sensitized with M. avium showed no significant reaction to RRbTB-E, even though they responded strongly to PPD-A. This agrees with studies showing that ESAT-6, encoded in the RD1 region absent from most NTM, is highly specific and does not usually cross-react with M. avium complex organisms [16]. Unexpectedly, we did not detect marked PPD-B cross-reactivity in M. avium–sensitized guinea pigs despite previous reports describing this activity in this model and in cattle [47,48,49]. However, differences in the detection of cross-reactivity of PPD-B with M. avium have been previously reported [8] and are likely due in part to variation in the manufacture of PPD products, an issue widely recognized in PPD production [50]. In contrast, M. bovis-sensitized animals displayed a strong skin reaction to RRbTB-E, PPD-B and, interestingly, also to PPD-A, highlighting the previously reported cross-reactivity of avian and bovine PPD reagents due to their many shared antigens [7,43,48,50].

To assess the positive cross-reactivity behavior of RRbTB-E, we tested its ability to trigger a DTH response in M. tuberculosis–sensitized guinea pigs. The protein induced a clear response, significantly higher than the negative control, but not significantly different than PPD-T. This result is expected because ESAT-6 is encoded in the RD1 genomic segment conserved in both M. bovis and M. tuberculosis, but not in the BCG strain [51]. These findings confirm that recombinant ESAT-6 retains the immunogenic properties of native RD1-encoded proteins and can reliably elicit DTH responses in animals sensitized to members of the M. tuberculosis complex. Overall, these findings show that RRbTB-E behaves as a specific, defined antigen that induces DTH reactions only in animals sensitized to M. bovis and M. tuberculosis, while avoiding the nonspecific responses commonly associated with complex PPD mixtures.

In addition, we assessed the stability and reproducibility of RRbTB-E in the guinea pig DTH model. We observed that they retained full biological activity for up to 2.5 years at 4–8 °C, indicating a level of stability that is uncommon among recombinant mycobacterial proteins, which typically require frozen storage to prevent degradation [52]. An important role in this stability could be due to the RRbTB-E fusion to the foldon trimerization domain, which has been shown to further improve the conformational stability of other fused protein moieties [23,53], suggesting that protein engineering strategies may extend these advantages. The absence of measurable loss of reactivity across production batches supports the suitability of the reagent for use in settings with limited cold-chain capacity. The RRbTB-E reagent also demonstrated high reproducibility, with a low coefficient of variation across batches in six independent experiments. This performance is relevant given the inherent variability associated with PPD-B intradermal testing [54,55]. Our observation shows an improvement over earlier reports using ESAT-6 alone or ESAT-6 and CFP-10 that describe a less consistent DTH responses [28], whereas in our study all sensitized animals responded robustly over the different batches tested. This diagnostic performance was comparable to PPD-B, and the absence of responses in MFP controls confirms the RRbTB-E specificity.

Finally, we evaluated RRbTB-E in naturally infected cattle. These experiments were designed as proof-of-concept field studies aimed at assessing the biological activity and preliminary diagnostic performance of RRbTB-E under real-world conditions. Although the number of animals included was modest, it is consistent with exploratory diagnostic studies in large animals and was sufficient to demonstrate that the recombinant antigen elicited DTH responses comparable in magnitude to those induced by PPD-B. At the initial dose of 0.8 mg, RRbTB-E correctly identified 8/12 of naturally infected animals, corresponding to an estimated sensitivity of approximately 66.7%, while no positive reactions were observed in the limited group of non-infected control cattle, yielding an apparent specificity of 100%. However, due to the small number of non-infected animals included, the CI for specificity was wide, and this estimate should be interpreted with caution.

Increasing the RRbTB-E dose to 1 mg markedly improved detection, achieving 100% sensitivity among naturally infected cattle and matching the performance of PPD-B. This result is highly relevant, since previous work in infected cattle showed that in vitro IFN-γ response comparing PPD-B and recombinant ESAT-6 resulted in equivalent values [56]. However, when compared in the context of a DTH skin test response, 0.4 mg of recombinant ESAT-6 achieved between 63 and 81% response [56]. To address this limitation, multi-antigen defined reagents have been developed to broaden epitope coverage and enhance response magnitude, as demonstrated by intradermal and IFN-γ–based assays using an M. bovis-specific antigen cocktail containing ESAT-6, CFP-10 and Rv3615c [57]. More recent studies have shown that this combinations of peptide antigens ESAT-6, CFP-10, and Rv3615c can achieve high diagnostic accuracy without compromising specificity in BCG-vaccinated animals [9]. Future studies using our fusion protein platform with ESAT-6 in combination with other specific M. bovis proteins as a multi-antigen reagent could result in improved performance over our current experimental skin test. However, the use of multicomponent reagents may introduce significant complexity in production, quality control, and regulatory validation.

A limitation of the present study is the small number of non-infected control cattle, which restricts robust estimation of specificity and results in wide CI. In addition, the use of the PPD-B-based TST as a reference standard, while widely accepted in the field, does not represent a definitive bacteriological confirmation. Larger-scale field studies including higher numbers of confirmed non-infected animals and comparison with bacteriological or molecular reference standards will be required to fully validate the diagnostic accuracy of RRbTB-E.

The observed DTH response in cattle to the ESAT-6 sequence in RRbTB-E could be attributed to the fusion of the MBP and the foldon components of this novel recombinant protein. One important factor is the relatively large molecular weight of MBP and foldon sequences, of approximately 67 kDa that adds to the 9.8 kDa of ESAT-6. When fused with the target protein, MBP and the foldon result in an increase in the molecular weight of the entire fusion protein, which may contribute to its recognition by the immune system. It has been reported that fusion to MBP significantly improves the immunogenicity of the target protein, resulting in the secretion of cytokines involved in the adaptive and innate immune response [58,59,60]. The absence of significant reactions in animals inoculated with the MFP negative control protein confirms that the detected skin responses were antigen-specific rather than attributable to nonspecific protein effects on the animals, suggesting that it should not interfere at this dose and route used. However, since it has been administered only once in the tested animals, further studies in a larger cohort of animals are necessary to confirm that it will not prime the animals for its response in recurrent DTH tests and cause false positive responses. Moreover, the DIVA potential of RRbTB-E remains hypothetical at this stage, as animals vaccinated with BCG were not included in the present study. Consequently, future validation in BCG-vaccinated and experimentally infected cattle will be critical to determine whether RRbTB-E can be integrated into vaccination-compatible bTB control programs.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of an MBP fusion protein with ESAT-6 used as a diagnostic reagent in the context of DTH response. These preliminary results highlight two strengths of RRbTB-E: its capacity to elicit robust DTH responses in infected animals and its lack of reactivity in non-infected cattle. The high specificity and adequate performance at elevated doses suggest that RRbTB-E is a good candidate for a practical defined-antigen alternative to PPD-B, warranting further trials in larger cohorts for optimization to balance sensitivity, dose requirements, and manufacturing scalability.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Expression and Purification of Recombinant Proteins

The sequence coding for the RRbTB-E fusion protein was synthesized by Genscript (Piscataway, NJ, USA) and inserted in an ampicillin-resistance pMAL expression plasmid with the MBP sequence at the C-term as reported in [23], with the ESAT-6 sequence Acc. Number WP_053892578. RRbTB-E was expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) under the control of an IPTG inducible promoter. Cultures were grown in 500 mL of Luria–Bertani (LB) medium at 37 °C with shaking at 250 rpm until 0.8 optical density units at 600 nm (OD600nm) was reached. Protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (Gen Biotech, Buenos Aires, Argentina) for 4 h at 37 °C under constant agitation. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 7000× g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting pellets were stored at −80 °C until use. For cell disruption, pellets were resuspended in disruption buffer (PBS supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and benzonase (in-house preparation), pH 7.4. Approximately 4 mL of disruption buffer per g of pellet was used. Cells were disrupted by sonication using Bioruptor Plus sonication system (Diagenode, Liège, Belgium) with 10 cycles of 30 s on and 30 s off. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by filtration through a 0.45 µm filter prior to chromatographic purification. The recombinant protein was purified by affinity chromatography on a 5 mL MBP-Trap column (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) using an ÄKTA Prime FPLC system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The column was equilibrated with a binding buffer consisting of 20 mM Tris-HCl, 200 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA (pH 7.4). Protein was eluted using the binding buffer supplemented with 10 mM maltose. The eluted protein was concentrated to approximately 5 mg/mL and stored at 4 °C. Protein concentration was measured using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and confirmed by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE performed using an omniPAGE Mini Vertical Protein Electrophoresis System (Cleaver Scientific, Rugby, UK). Western blot analysis was performed using an anti-His antibody that recognizes the His tag present in the RRbTB-E protein and visualized using a G:BOX imaging system (Syngene, Cambridge, UK), and image acquisition was performed using GeneSnap software version 7.12 (Syngene, Cambridge, UK). The overall yield of purified protein was approximately 0.1 g per liter of culture. A similar procedure was performed to obtain the control protein MFP.

4.2. Densitometry Analysis

NI, I, SF, and IF protein band intensities from Coomassie Brilliant Blue-stained SDS–PAGE gels from Figure 2A were quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software version 153a (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Briefly, high-resolution images of the stained gel were acquired and converted to 8-bit grayscale. Each lane was selected using the rectangular selection tool and analyzed with the Gel Analysis function in ImageJ with identical parameters applied to all lanes within a gel. All areas under the protein bands were integrated and values normalized as percentage area. Peak areas corresponding to RRbTB-E protein bands (~77 kDa) were integrated, and the percentage of the RRbTB-E band area relative to the total protein area was calculated. All analyses were performed using identical settings to ensure comparability between samples.

4.3. Animals

A total of 89 short-haired male guinea pigs (300–350 g) were purchased from the Animal Breeding Facility of the National Institute of Biological Production (ANLIS Dr. Carlos G. Malbrán). Animals were randomly allocated into groups of six and housed in separate cage racks, with ad libitum access to food and water. The number of animals per group was determined by the minimum required to implement a Latin Square design that allowed rotation of the injection site among six different samples within each group. Each flank was considered an independent experimental unit, which allowed a reduction in the total number of animals needed. The housing and care of the animals, as well as all experimental procedures, were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Committee for the Care and Use of Animals (CICUAL) of the Institute of Biology and Experimental Medicine (IBYME), under protocol 27/2025. All animals enrolled in the study were included in the analyses. Humane observation and inclusion criteria were predefined and applied uniformly. Guinea pigs were monitored for the following clinical signs: loss of >20% of initial body weight; prostration or lack of response to stimuli; severe respiratory signs (dyspnea or abnormal lung sounds); ulcerative or necrotic lesions at the inoculation site (left hind limb); hypothermia (rectal temperature < 36 °C); severe dehydration (reduced skin turgor and sunken eyes); and persistent abnormal behavior. These criteria were used to ensure consistent clinical monitoring and ethical oversight throughout the study.

4.4. Sensitizing Antigens

All animals underwent a two-week acclimatization period before sensitization. Sensitization and intradermal skin testing procedures followed the recommendations of the WOAH for tuberculin potency testing [61].

Different groups of guinea pigs were sensitized with M. bovis strain AN5, with M. avium subsp. avium strain D4ER or with M. tuberculosis strain H37Rv. Each antigen preparation was heat-treated, dried, and formulated in an oil adjuvant and administered by intramuscular injection into the right hind limb. All antigen preparations were provided by the Tuberculosis Laboratory of the National Service for Agri-Food Health and Quality (SENASA). A group of non-sensitized guinea pigs was included to evaluate reagent specificity.

4.5. Skin Test Reaction Procedure in Guinea Pigs

Intradermal skin testing was performed thirty days after sensitization. The guinea pig hair was shaved from both flanks, and three intradermal injections (0.2 mL each) were administered per flank, resulting in six independent test sites per animal. Injection sites were alternated according to a Latin square design to minimize positional bias. Reactions were evaluated 24 h after reagent administration; the length and width of each induration diameter were measured, and the indurated area was calculated. Responses with a diameter size above 5 mm were considered reactive, whereas smaller reactions were classified as negative for PPD-B. As PPD-B and PPD-A, we used the standard reagents produced by the DILAB-SENASA (Buenos Aires, Argentina). The tuberculin PPD-T reagent was obtained from the ANLIS “Dr. Carlos G. Malbrán” (Buenos Aires, Argentina). Each animal underwent a single intradermal skin test.

4.6. Potency Test in Guinea Pig

The in vivo potency of the RRbTB-E preparations was evaluated in three groups of six guinea pigs previously sensitized with the dry weight preparation of the AN5 strain of M. bovis. The assay was performed according to the SENASA guidelines for the evaluation of bovine tuberculin [25,62]. The SENASA Bovine Tuberculin Standard (Batches 90 and 91), at 1 mg/mL and 32,500 IU/mL, was used as reference preparation. Three five-fold dilutions of both the test reagent and the reference standard were administered intradermally according to a Latin square design. Relative potency was calculated using a parallel-line bioassay model [63,64]. The statistical analysis required the following design constraints: (i) an equal number of doses for the reference standard and the test preparation, and (ii) a constant dose ratio of 1:5 between adjacent dilutions. The sources of variation included: preparations, regression, parallelism, quadratic deviation, and deviation from linearity (difference of squares). For each source, degrees of freedom, reduced sum of squares, mean square, and the mean square ratio (F statistic) were calculated. The F value for each source of variation was compared with the F distribution at the corresponding error degrees of freedom to determine statistical significance. Purified RRbTB-E preparations were stored at 4–8 °C and used for in vivo assays over a period extending up to 2.5 years.

4.7. Skin Test Reaction Procedure in Cattle

A total of 23 adult dairy cattle (Bos taurus) from commercial herds located in Suardi, Santa Fe (Argentina), were included in the study. Two independent field trials were conducted, each involving different groups of animals selected according to their bTB status. In the first trial, 12 naturally infected animals were obtained from a sanitary-controlled herd that had previously tested positive for bTB using TST with PPD-B, together with 3 non-infected control animals from a herd officially free of bTB for more than five years, with all individuals testing negative using TST with PPD-B. The second independent trial included 8 naturally infected animals from a different sanitary-controlled herd with confirmed bTB cases based on TST with PPD-B. Antigens, including RRbTB-E and PPD-B, were administered intradermally at four sites on each side of the neck (eight injection sites per animal), with a volume of 0.1 mL per injection. Injection sites were alternated according to a Latin square design to minimize positional bias. Skin reactions were evaluated 72 h after antigen administration. The thickness of the skin at each injection site was measured using a calibrated caliper along both longitudinal and transverse axes, and the reaction size was calculated as the difference between the post-injection and baseline skin thickness. Reactions with skin response increase above 3 mm were deemed positive [25].

Diagnostic performance parameters were calculated using the PPD-B-based TST as the reference standard and a cut-off value of 3 mm. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated using standard 2 × 2 contingency tables. Exact 95% CI for sensitivity and specificity were estimated using the Clopper–Pearson method. PPV and NPV were interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size and the high prevalence of infection in the studied population.

4.8. Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.6.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A Latin square design was used to allocate intradermal injections, allowing each animal to receive multiple antigens or doses while minimizing potential site- and order-related bias. For all analyses, the animal was considered the experimental unit, and one response value per animal was used for each antigen or dose; injection sites were not treated as independent observations. Statistical comparisons between two paired groups were performed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way or repeated-measures ANOVA, as appropriate, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Linear regression analysis was used to compare dose–response curves. Data are presented as mean ± SD or as box-and-whisker plots, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Differences were considered statistically significant at * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01 and *** p < 0.001, while “ns” indicates non-significant differences, corresponding to p > 0.05. Exact p-values for each pairwise comparison are provided in Supplementary Table S2.
