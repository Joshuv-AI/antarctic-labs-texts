Bilateral association between muscle strength and depression: evidence from a prospective cohort study
Authors: Tong Guo, Fanchao Meng, Sixiang Liang, Guangwei Zhao, Jun Liu, Xue Wang, Ling Zhang, Gang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBoth low muscle strength and depressive symptoms are common among middle-aged and older adults. However, their bilateral association has not been thoroughly investigated. This study examined whether muscle strength and depression are bilaterally associated using data from a prospective cohort in China.MethodsA total of 6,961 participants without depression and 6,817 participants without low muscle strength from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study were included to investigate the association of muscle strength with depression and of depressive symptoms with low muscle strength, respectively. The 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) was used to assess depressive symptoms, with a cut-off score of 10. Handgrip strength and the time taken to complete the Five Times Sit-to-Stand Test (5TSTS) were used to evaluate muscle strength. Cox regression was applied to assess the associations after adjusting for relevant variables. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore potential non-linear relationships.ResultsThere was a significant association between handgrip strength (HR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.98–0.99) and time of 5TSTS (HR: 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.03) with the 7-year incident depressive symptoms. Similarly, depressive symptoms were significantly associated with the 4-year incident low muscle strength, as defined by both handgrip strength (HR: 1.04; 95% CI: 1.02–1.05) and time of 5TSTS (HR: 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.04). No evidence of non-linear associations was detected.LimitationDepressive symptoms were assessed using the CESD-10 scale rather than a clinical diagnosis.ConclusionsA bilateral longitudinal association exists between muscle strength and depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24915-2.Keywords: Muscle strength, Depression, Bilateral association, Cox regression, Prospective cohort

Introduction

Depressive symptoms are common among middle-aged and older adults. Globally, it is estimated that over 280 million people suffer from depression, contributing to approximately 47 million disability-adjusted life years in 2019 [1]. According to a recent cross-sectional survey, the prevalence of depressive symptoms among the general population in mainland China was 15.81% [2]. Low muscle strength is another widespread condition among older adults. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) in the United States showed that the prevalence of reduced muscle strength among adults aged 60 and above was 18% [3]. A meta-analysis reported that the overall prevalence of sarcopenia (defined as low muscle mass combined with low muscle strength and/or low physical performance) among community-dwelling Chinese adults aged over 65 years was 17.4% [4]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that both depression and low muscle strength are associated with a range of adverse outcomes and can significantly impair quality of life [5, 6].

Given that depression and low muscle strength are both common and share several risk factors [7, 8], numerous studies—both cross-sectional and prospective—have explored the association between muscle strength and depression. For instance, one NHANES-based study found a significant association between grip strength and depression [9]. Additionally, two large-scale prospective cohort studies from China and the UK also confirmed a longitudinal relationship between muscle strength and the risk of depression [10, 11]. However, the influence of depressive symptoms on the incident low muscle strength has been scarcely investigated, and the potential bilateral association between the two conditions has not been clearly established. This study aims to explore the bilateral relationship between muscle strength and depression among middle-aged and older adults using data from a prospective cohort in China.

Methods

Source of data

Our study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative, ongoing longitudinal cohort study in China. The survey began in 2011, with follow-up waves conducted in 2013, 2015, and 2018. It collects comprehensive information on respondents and their families, including demographic characteristics, intergenerational financial transfers, health status, medical care and insurance, employment, income, expenditures, and assets. In addition, CHARLS includes 13 physical measurements and the collection of blood samples. Further details about the study have been documented in previous research [12]. CHARLS was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University, and all participants provided informed consent prior to enrollment.

Assessment of muscle strength

In CHARLS, muscle strength was evaluated using both handgrip strength and lower limb muscle strength measurements in 2011, 2013, and 2015. Handgrip strength was assessed in both the dominant and non-dominant hands using a Yuejian™ WL-1000 dynamometer (Nantong, China). Participants were instructed to exert maximal force while squeezing the device, with two trials performed for each hand while maintaining a 90° elbow angle. For analysis, the average of the maximum values from each hand was used. If one hand could not be measured for any reason, the highest recorded value from the other hand was used. Low handgrip strength was defined based on the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) criteria: less than 28 kg for males and less than 18 kg for females [13].

Lower limb muscle strength was assessed using the Five Times Sit-to-Stand Test (5TSTS). Participants were instructed to cross their arms over their chests and perform five consecutive sit-to-stand movements as quickly as possible, without pausing or using their arms for support. Investigators used stopwatches to objectively record the time taken to complete the task. According to the AWGS 2019 criteria, a completion time of 12 s or more was considered indicative of low lower limb muscle strength [13].

Assessment of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed in 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018 using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), a shortened version of the original 20-item CESD. Participants reported the frequency of 10 symptoms experienced over the past week, with response options categorized as: rarely or never (< 1 day), sometimes (1–2 days), occasionally (3–4 days), and most or all of the time (5–7 days). Each item was scored from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating greater symptom frequency. Total scores ranged from 0 to 30. Consistent with previous studies, a score of ≥ 20 was considered indicative of significant depressive symptoms, while a cut-off of ≥ 10 was also used to identify participants with depressive symptoms; scores < 10 defined the no-symptom group [14]. The CESD-10 has been widely validated for use in general populations and has demonstrated strong reliability and validity among community-dwelling older adults in China [15].

Assessment of covariates

Sociodemographic variables included age, gender, education level, marital status, number of children, and household income. Education was classified into three categories: elementary school or lower, middle school, and high school or higher. Marital status was categorized as married (living together) or unmarried (including married but living apart, separated, divorced, widowed, or never married). Behavioral covariates included smoking status (smoker or non-smoker), alcohol use (drinker or non-drinker), and weight loss. Health-related covariates included obesity (body mass index ≥ 30), aging-associated cognitive decline [16, 17], hypertension (self-reported, systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medications), diabetes (self-reported, hemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%, or use of antidiabetic medications), dyslipidemia (self-reported, total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL, or use of lipid-lowering medications), and the self-reported number of comorbidities (excluding hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia).

Statistical analysis

The baseline survey recruited 17,705 respondents, covering 150 counties of 28 provinces in China. To evaluate the association of muscle strength with depression, participants at baseline without information on muscle strength were excluded. Those without information on depressive symptoms or diagnosed with depression at baseline were also excluded. In addition, we excluded participants without follow-up information on depression. Similarly, to evaluate the association of depressive symptoms with low muscle strength, participants without information on depression at baseline were excluded. Those without information on muscle strength or diagnosed with low muscle strength at baseline were also excluded. In addition, we excluded participants without follow-up information on muscle strength. The flow chart of sample selection was presented in Fig. 1. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations to reduce potential bias in statistical inference.

The flow chart of sample selection

We analyzed data with R software (R Development Core Team, Version 4.1.3) In the descriptive statistical analyses, continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile ranges, IQR), and they were compared by the student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared by the Chi-square test. P values less than 0.05 were considered of significance and Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple testing (0.05/2).

Cox proportional hazard models were employed to investigate the bilateral association between muscle strength and depression, using follow-up time as the time-scale variable. Results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three different Cox models were fitted. Model 1 was not adjusted for any confounders. Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, education, marital status, and income. Model 3 was adjusted for all the potential covariates. To assess potential non-linear associations, exposure variables were modeled using restricted cubic splines (RCS) within Cox models. The number of knots was selected based on the smallest Akaike Information Criterion. Non-linearity was tested using the Wald test for RCS coefficients.

We conducted several supplementary analyses to validate our findings. First, we applied a multivariable generalized linear mixed model with random intercepts and slopes to account for repeated measures and participant-specific trajectories. Second, we used the median follow-up time as the time-to-event in the Cox proportional hazards models. Third, we fitted an interval-censored survival model, given that the exact timing of events was unknown. Fourth, we performed subgroup analyses and tested interactions in vulnerable populations, including older adults (≥ 60 years) with multimorbidity (≥ 2 diseases), individuals with low income (< median household income), and those with fewer children (< 2 children).

Table 4 shows the association between depressive symptoms and incident low muscle strength. Discussion

In this study, we report, for the first time, a significant bidirectional longitudinal association between muscle strength and depression in a prospective cohort. This relationship remained robust after adjustment for relevant covariates and across multiple statistical models. Subgroup analyses indicated that the association was consistent across vulnerable populations, and RCS analyses confirmed its linearity.

Several prospective studies have reported associations between muscle strength and depressive symptoms. For instance, lower handgrip strength has been linked to the development of depressive symptoms in adults in cohorts from England (≥ 60 years) [18], Italy (≥ 65 years) [19], and China (≥ 45 years) [20]. Beyond that, there is evidence linking physical functioning with the trajectory of depressive symptoms, and vice versa. Brett et al. (2022) conducted a 19-year study of former NFL players, showing that declines in physical function predicted a steeper increase in depressive symptoms over time, independent of concussion history [21]. Similarly, Lever-van Milligen et al. assessed six-year trajectories of handgrip strength and physical function in individuals with depressive and/or anxiety disorders, finding that those with current depression and anxiety experienced greater declines in physical function compared with healthy controls [22].

Studies exploring the bidirectional relationship between muscle strength and depression remain limited, although low muscle strength is frequently observed as a clinical feature in individuals with depression. Bertoni et al. used SHARE (Survey on Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe) panel data to investigate whether the onset of low muscle strength at a 4-year follow-up could be explained by pre-existing depressive symptoms (either at baseline or at the 2-year follow-up), or vice versa [23]. Using multivariate logistic regression, they found that the onset of depressive symptoms increased the risk of low muscle strength. However, low handgrip strength at baseline was not associated with the risk of incident depression during follow-up. This insignificant association may have occurred due to the relatively short follow-up period or the use of only a single indicator of muscle strength.

The mechanisms by which muscle strength affects depression remain unclear, but several hypotheses involving neurophysiological and psychosocial pathways have been proposed. First, individuals with depression have consistently been reported to exhibit functional and structural brain alterations, with the hippocampus—an area involved in emotional processing and stress regulation—being among the most frequently affected regions [24, 25]. Levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a key marker of neuronal growth and plasticity, are reduced in individuals with depression [26]. In contrast, greater muscle strength has been positively associated with larger hippocampal volume, improved white matter integrity, and enhanced neurocognitive performance. Notably, resistance training—which is known to elevate BDNF levels—may play a protective role against depressive symptoms [27, 28]. Second, chronic low-grade inflammation may contribute to the development of depression [8, 29]. In response to muscle contractions, skeletal muscle tissue releases various cytokines and myokines into the bloodstream, some of which possess anti-inflammatory properties that may help protect against mood disorders [30].

Several mechanisms may explain the effect of muscle strength on depression. First, depressive symptoms are often linked to social isolation and loneliness, both of which are associated with poor physical performance and disability—conditions closely related to low muscle strength [31]. Second, malnutrition, which is common among older adults with depression [32], is a well-established risk factor for reduced muscle strength in the elderly [33, 34]. Third, depression is characterized by symptoms such as diminished interest, weight changes, and fatigue—all of which may contribute to the development of low muscle strength. Additionally, overlapping biological and physiological pathways, such as inflammation, may further clarify the link between depressive symptoms and reduced muscle strength [8, 29, 30].

This study has the advantage of using large-scale longitudinal data to explore the possible bilateral association between muscle strength and depression. It expands upon the findings of previous research by providing evidence of a two-way relationship between the two. In addition, the study used both handgrip strength and chair stand performance to represent upper and lower limb strength, respectively, which enhances the reliability of the findings. While our findings indicate a prospective association between muscle strength and incident depression, this does not necessarily imply a causal relationship. The association may reflect shared underlying biological or psychosocial mechanisms—such as chronic inflammation, neuroendocrine dysregulation, or socioeconomic and lifestyle factors—rather than a direct effect of muscle strength on depression. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted with caution.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, depression was assessed using the CESD-10, a screening instrument rather than a gold-standard diagnostic interview. Although the CESD-10 has been validated in Chinese and other populations [35, 36], it is not without limitations, and outcome misclassification is possible. In the CHARLS prospective cohort, the CESD-10 was administered blindly with respect to future outcome ascertainment and applied uniformly across exposure groups; therefore, any misclassification is most plausibly non-differential, as sensitivity and specificity are unlikely to vary systematically by exposure status. Under such circumstances, hazard ratio estimates are generally biased toward the null, suggesting that the true associations may be stronger than those reported [37]. Second, certain important covariates—such as physical activity level and specific medication use (e.g., antidepressants)—were unavailable. These factors could influence both exposure and outcome, introducing residual confounding that limits causal interpretation. Although our associations remained robust after adjusting for available covariates, future studies should incorporate more detailed assessments of these variables. Sensitivity analyses using E-values, which estimate the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to nullify the observed results [38], may further strengthen causal inference. Third, although the longitudinal design supports temporal sequencing, reverse causation cannot be entirely excluded. Subclinical depressive symptoms or early declines in muscle strength may have been present at baseline, potentially influencing both exposure and outcome and biasing the observed relationships. Finally, while the CHARLS cohort is nationally representative of community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults in China, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other populations. Cultural norms, institutional contexts, and healthcare systems may influence both muscle strength and depression risk. Replication in diverse settings is needed to assess external validity and to explore potential effect modification by societal and environmental factors.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a bidirectional longitudinal association between muscle strength and depression, underscoring a self-reinforcing cycle that may accelerate functional and psychological decline in older adults. Breaking this cycle will require integrated strategies that simultaneously target physical and mental health, particularly within primary care and community health systems where most older adults receive ongoing care. Combined exercise programs represent a promising dual-target intervention, with meta-analytic evidence showing benefits for muscle strength, depressive symptoms, and aspects of quality of life [39–41]. Defining the optimal modality, intensity, and duration of such interventions—and embedding them into routine care pathways—should be a priority for future research and policy.
