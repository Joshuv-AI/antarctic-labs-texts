Improving disinfection and packaging strategies for the effective preservation of welsh onion (Allium fistulosum L.)
Authors: Reena Gupta, Abdulsalam Abdulsattar Abdulazez, A. K. Kareem, Safia Obaidur Rab, Shirin Shomurotova, Fadhil F. Sead, Mustafa Nuhad Al-Darraji, Malik Bader Alazzam, Jamal K. Abbas, Hamza Fadhel Hamzah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to improve postharvest preservation strategies for Welsh onion (Allium fistulosum L.) by optimizing disinfection techniques and packaging methods. This study pioneers the combination of cold plasma treatment and nanocomposite packaging as a novel approach to enhance shelf life and preserve quality in Welsh onion. The research was conducted in two phases. In the first phase, disinfection was performed using gamma irradiation (1, 3, and 5 kGy) and cold plasma (15, 20, and 25 kV for 5, 10, and 15 min). In the second phase, four gas-barrier nanocomposite films were tested under both ambient and vacuum-sealed storage conditions for 4, 8, and 12 months. Volatile compounds were analyzed using gas chromatography (GC and GC/MS). Cold plasma treatment was more effective than gamma irradiation in preserving key postharvest quality parameters. The highest phenolic content and antioxidant activity were observed in samples treated with cold plasma at 25 kV for 5 min, with a phenolic content of 196.84 mg GAE/g DW and an IC₅₀ value of 78.74 μg/mL. Nonetheless, all packaging treatments showed declines in total phenolics, essential oil yield, and L* color values over time. The major volatile compounds in the essential oil included dipropyl disulfide (9.0%–18.3%), dipropyl trisulfide (9.4%–16.4%), dimethyl trisulfide (5.3%–11.1%), diallyl disulfide (6.8%–11.4%), and d-limonene (4.0%–9.1%). These findings provide valuable insights into the postharvest management of leafy vegetables such as Welsh onion.

Introduction

Allium vegetables, such as onions, garlic, and leeks, are essential to human health due to their nutritional value and medicinal properties1. Welsh onion (Allium fistulosum L.), also known as stone leek, Chinese onion, Chinese spring onion, and Japanese bunching onion, is a highly popular medicinal and functional vegetable in East Asian countries2,3. It has been reported to exhibit various biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antifungal, and antimicrobial effects4. Furthermore, the green pseudostems, leaves, and undeveloped bulbs of Welsh onion are commonly used as a seasoning in a variety of culinary applications, including cooked dishes, salads, and pickles5. Moreover, volatile compounds and extracts derived from Welsh onion have shown notable antimicrobial, antifungal, antioxidant, and antiparasitic properties6,7. The biological activity of Allium fistulosum is strongly associated with its total phenolic content8. Additionally, flavonoids such as quercetin and its glycosides have been linked to the antioxidant and anticancer properties of Welsh onion9.

Proper storage conditions are essential after harvesting vegetables and spices, with temperature and humidity being critical factors10,11. Maintaining low temperatures helps preserve quality and extend shelf life by slowing down deterioration processes12. Additionally, controlling relative humidity is vital because excessive humidity may lead to condensation, promoting microbial contamination and posing significant health risks13.

Consequently, implementing effective disinfection strategies is crucial to mitigate microbial contamination and ensure the safety of vegetables and spices after harvest14. With rising demand for safe, fresh-like produce, various disinfection technologies have been developed15. However, some methods may not fully meet food safety and quality standards, necessitating the identification of more effective solutions16.

Common disinfection methods for medicinal plants include irradiation, steam treatment, high hydrostatic pressure, high temperatures, ionizing radiation, infrared radiation, and the use of ethyl alcohol or formaldehyde17. Short shelf-life products are disinfected using physical methods (like irradiation and heat) and newer chemical alternatives (like ozone and essential oils) to meet modern safety standards18.

Irradiation is a highly effective physical method for disinfecting and preserving food and plants, and it has been shown to better maintain quality and properties compared to traditional chemical methods19. Recommended doses of gamma radiation for inhibiting sprouts doses used are < 0.5 kGy for hindering ripening; lower than or equal 1 kGy for pest disinfestations; and 3–5 kGy to increase shelf-life and for pathogenic microbes20. Studies have shown that gamma radiation at doses of 1 kGy and 5 kGy significantly reduces microbial contamination and extends the shelf life of various vegetables, particularly those that are dry, bulbous, or root-based21. In Iran, gamma irradiation at doses of 90 to 150 Gy applied to Iranian onion cultivars (White-Ghom, White-Neyshabour, Red-Ridge-Lump, and Red-Ray-Corrugated) significantly improved their post-harvest shelf life and quality22. Colletti et al.23 found that gamma irradiation treatments (up to 1.0 kGy), combined with modified atmosphere packaging, produced high-quality peach products with an extended shelf life. Adiani et al.24 reported that gamma irradiation at 10 kGy enhanced the extractability of oil and phenolic compounds by 80 and 50%, respectively. Low-energy e-beam irradiation at 18 kGy effectively reduced microbial contamination in black peppercorns without affecting their chemical or microbial integrity25. Although irradiation has demonstrated substantial effectiveness in food preservation, certain limitations such as consumer acceptance, regulatory concerns, and potential impacts on nutritional and sensory qualities have encouraged exploration of alternative technologies. Among these, atmospheric cold plasma (ACP) has recently emerged as a non-thermal, non-destructive method with promising potential for extending the shelf life of fresh produce26.

Atmospheric cold plasma is an emerging, non-destructive preservation technology with promising potential for extending the shelf life of fruits and vegetables26. Researchers reported that cold atmospheric plasma treatments reduced the total microbial count, as well as mold and yeast levels, in cherry tomatoes27, fresh-cut pears28, spring onion,29 and other consumed vegetables30. Kazemzadeh et al.31 found that plasma treatment (8 kV, 8.83 min, 6.6 cm) enhanced vitamin C retention and antioxidant activity in onion leaves during ten days of refrigerated storage. Senguler et al.32 showed that cold plasma treatment of onion skin waste reduced microbial counts while enhancing total phenolic content (TPC) and antioxidant activity. The cold plasma technique is particularly suitable for temperature-sensitive products and offers an eco-friendly33, cost-effective, and non-chemical alternative for preservation34. Although cold plasma preserves quality and bioactive compounds, proper packaging is needed to maintain these benefits during storage.

Due to their typical patterns of consumption, vegetables and spices are often stored for extended periods35. Therefore, selecting appropriate packaging materials is essential. Several studies have investigated how different packaging types influence the qualitative and quantitative attributes of these products36. Packaging plays a key role in maintaining the quality of vegetables and herbs during storage37. For example, vacuum packaging slightly increased flavonol content in onions, though anthocyanins declined38. Polyethylene covers and cold, dark storage preserved active compounds in dried purple coneflower39. In contrast, storing basil (Ocimum basilicum) in paper or plastic for up to 12 months had little effect on essential oil content40. Similarly, polyethylene packaging reduced moisture loss in garlic, and modified atmosphere packaging helped retain chlorophyll in coriander41. The selection of appropriate disinfection methods and storage conditions for vegetables and spices depends on their intended application, the specific plant parts used, and the nature of their bioactive compounds42. To date, limited research has explored the combined use of non-thermal disinfection methods and advanced packaging materials for leafy vegetables. The novelty of the present study lies in integrating cold plasma treatment with nanocomposite-based packaging as a sustainable strategy to extend shelf life and maintain the functional and sensory quality of Welsh onion. Accordingly, the present study aimed to optimize a disinfection approach and determine suitable packaging solutions for Welsh onion (Allium fistulosum), a plant valued both for its medicinal properties and as a culinary ingredient, particularly in pancakes.

Results

Disinfection

Total phenolic content

The results of the study indicated that the method of disinfection had a significant impact on the phenolic compounds present in A. fistulosum L. samples. Gamma irradiation, in particular, caused a notable decline in total phenolic content, with the lowest concentration measured at a dose of 5 kGy, reaching 19.5 mg GAE/g DW (Fig. 1). Conversely, samples exposed to cold plasma exhibited higher levels of total phenols compared to those treated with gamma rays. Antioxidant activity (DPPH)

Comparative evaluation of the antioxidant activity of A. fistulosum L. extracts using the DPPH assay revealed that cold plasma treatment at 25 kV for 5 min resulted in the highest radical scavenging capacity, with an IC₅₀ value of 78.74 μg/mL (Fig. 1). In contrast, the lowest antioxidant activity was observed in samples exposed to 5 kGy gamma irradiation, which also showed an IC₅₀ value of 78.74 μg/mL, indicating minimal improvement over the control. Notably, increasing the cold plasma intensity from 15 to 25 kV over a fixed 5-min treatment period led to a marked enhancement in antioxidant potential, as reflected by a significant reduction in IC₅₀ values from 168.36 μg/mL to 78.74 μg/mL.

Essential oil content and composition

Different disinfection techniques had a notable impact on the essential oil content of A. fistulosum L. Among the tested methods, cold plasma treatment at 15 and 20 kV for 15 min yielded the highest essential oil concentration, reaching 0.54% w/w (Fig. 2). This level was more than three times greater than that obtained from a 1-min exposure at the same voltages. Furthermore, gamma irradiation at doses of 3 and 5 kGy also enhanced the essential oil content compared to the lower dose of 1 kGy.Fig. 2Effect of different disinfection methods on essential oil content (%). Packaging

Total phenolic content

The application of various packaging methods had a significant impact on the phenolic compound content in A. fistulosum L. samples. A general decline in total phenolic content was observed across all packaging treatments as storage duration increased from 4 to 12 months (Fig. 3). Notably, treatments PF1A, PF3V, and PF4AV exhibited approximately a 50% reduction in phenolic content between months 4 and 8. Among the tested conditions, the highest phenolic content after 4 months of storage was recorded in treatment PF3A, with a value of 162.72 mg GAE/g DW. In contrast, the lowest phenolic content after 12 months was observed in PF4V, with 57.4 mg GAE/g DW (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Packaging × storage period interaction on the total phenolic content (TPC) and antioxidant activity (IC50). Antioxidant activity (DPPH)

Comparative analysis of the antioxidant activity of A. fistulosum L. extracts using the DPPH assay indicated that the PF3A treatment exhibited the highest radical scavenging capacity, with an IC₅₀ value of 84.36 μg/mL (Fig. 3). Conversely, the lowest antioxidant activity was observed in PF4V after 8 months of storage, with an IC₅₀ value of 262.0 μg/mL. On average, the PF3A treatment exhibited the highest antioxidant activity among all coatings across the three storage periods (4, 8, and 12 months), with a mean IC₅₀ value of 113.0 μg/mL.

Color characteristics

The analysis of color indices (L*, a*, and b*) in A. fistulosum L. revealed that storage duration had a significant impact across all packaging methods (Table 3). The L* index, representing lightness, consistently decreased with longer storage periods. The highest L* value (53) was observed in the PF1A sample at 4 months, while the lowest was recorded in the PF3V control at 12 months. The a* index, which reflects the red-green spectrum, reached its peak in the PF2V treatment after 12 months, with a value of 9.7 (Table 3). Discussion

The selected plasma operating conditions (15–25 kV for 5–15 min) and radiation doses (1–5 kGy) were carefully chosen based on literature data and preliminary experiments. Given the delicate nature of Welsh onion, moderate doses were applied to achieve a balance between effective microbial inactivation and the preservation of bioactive compounds, sensory attributes, and overall product quality15,17,21.

Various disinfection techniques markedly influenced the total phenolic content observed in the samples. This outcome is consistent with earlier research indicating that such methods including gamma irradiation can alter the phenolic levels in plant materials43. It has been proposed that the effectiveness of irradiation on plant constituents depends on several factors, such as the radiation dose, exposure time, and the characteristics of the raw material44,45.

In the present study, gamma irradiation at a dose of 3 kGy led to an increase in the phenolic content of A. fistulosum L., although noticeable differences were detected across the tested radiation levels. Gamma irradiation has been reported to enhance the accumulation of antioxidant compounds in vegetables. For instance, garlic sprouts irradiated at 1.25 kGy showed increased total phenol content and delayed the oxidation of total phenols and ascorbic acid during storage. These findings support the idea that gamma irradiation can effectively improve the antioxidant capacity of plant-based foods46. When cold plasma treatment was applied, higher intensities resulted in greater phenolic content compared to samples treated with gamma radiation. However, extending the duration of cold plasma exposure caused a decline in phenolic compound levels.

Cold plasma treatment of onion leaves at 8 kV for approximately 8.8 min effectively preserved vitamin C content and enhanced antioxidant activity compared to the untreated control during 10 days of storage at 4 °C31. These results are in line with previous studies showing that cold plasma reduces microbial load and increases total phenolic content and antioxidant activity in onion skin waste, highlighting the potential of cold plasma for improving the nutritional quality and shelf life of onion-derived products47. Additionally, in cherry tomatoes (stored at 4 °C and 90–95% relative humidity for up to 14 days) and fresh-cut pears (stored at 4 °C in sealed polyethylene containers for up to 7 days), plasma treatment decreased microbial counts and, in some cases, increased phenolic compounds and antioxidant capacity, although the effect depended on the type of produce and storage conditions48,49.

The observed increase in phenolic content and antioxidant activity in samples treated with cold plasma at 25 kV for 5 min compared to both the 15-min treatment and the control may be attributed to several factors. These effects can be attributed to several factors, including the potential degradation of phenolic compounds during extended exposure, the availability of sufficient energy to stimulate compound formation during shorter treatments, elevated temperatures associated with prolonged plasma application, and the dynamic, time-dependent fluctuations in the activity of reactive species50,51.

Previous research has demonstrated that cold plasma treatment can enhance the accumulation of phenolic compounds and flavonoids in plant tissues. One proposed explanation is that the UV radiation generated during plasma exposure stimulates the synthesis of phenolic compounds, particularly by affecting the epidermal cells of plant surfaces52. Conversely, the observed decline in phenolic levels during prolonged plasma exposure is likely due to compound degradation. This degradation process is thought to be initiated by the interaction of reactive oxygen species with the plant surface, triggering random bond disruptions53.

Among the evaluated color parameters, the b* value showed notable variation depending on the disinfection method, with observed values ranging from 19.8 to 22.39 across the different treatments. As for the L* and a* indices, the findings are consistent with those of Banerjee, et al.54, who reported that gamma irradiation did not produce statistically significant alterations in key sensory attributes, including color, aroma, texture, and flavor. Variations in color parameters are commonly attributed to intrinsic factors such as plant species, as well as extrinsic factors like the duration and intensity of exposure. These changes are often driven by oxidative reactions induced during processing, which can lead to the degradation of pigments and other color-contributing compounds. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for optimizing postharvest treatments, as maintaining visual quality is closely linked to both consumer acceptance and the preservation of bioactive compounds55.

Analysis of essential oils in the treated plants revealed that the applied disinfection methods had a significant impact on both the overall essential oil content and its individual components. Specifically, an increase in gamma radiation intensity corresponded with a notable enhancement in essential oil concentration within the plant tissues. This enhancement could be attributed to the stimulation of metabolic pathways responsible for essential oil biosynthesis triggered by gamma radiation56. Moreover, gamma irradiation may induce structural changes in plant cells, facilitating the release or synthesis of volatile compounds57.

The concentrations of dipropyl disulfide (ranging from 15.1 to 18.3%) and d-limonene (between 4.0 and 5.2%) in the essential oils showed a positive correlation with increasing gamma radiation intensity. Similar trends have been documented in previous research, where gamma irradiation enhanced the levels of bioactive compounds in essential oils. For instance, in this study, the cold plasma treatment at 15 kV led to increased levels of sulfur compounds, especially dipropyl disulfide, the dominant compound in the essential oil. Sulfur-containing compounds like dipropyl disulfide significantly contribute to the antioxidant and antimicrobial quality of Allium vegetables58. Our findings show that cold plasma treatment effectively preserves these volatile sulfur compounds by inhibiting microbial growth and reducing oxidative degradation during storage. This preservation helps maintain the nutritional and sensory properties of the vegetables, highlighting cold plasma as a promising postharvest method for quality retention59.

The use of various packaging methods was shown to affect both quantitative and qualitative indices of the packaged products. HDPE is an ideal packaging material due to its low permeability to water vapor. It prevents environmental moisture from reaching the product, protecting it from quality degradation. As a result, it helps maintain the product’s stability and extends its shelf life60. Findings from the present study indicate that both the duration of packaging and the environmental conditions during storage may play a role in modulating the phenolic profile and color characteristics of A. fistulosum L. Extended storage periods appeared to be linked with a gradual decline in phenolic content and antioxidant potential61, alongside shifts in color indices, specifically an elevation in the a* value and a reduction in the L* value62.

Vacuum packaging was associated with a decrease in phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity. Environmental factors including light exposure, temperature fluctuations, and atmospheric gas composition can influence the stability of phenolic compounds over time. Such degradation may be related to the disruption of cellular membranes63. These findings show that packaging features like material thickness and permeability play an important role in how quickly gases exchange and how the product breathes64. Vacuum packaging is essential for improving the quality and shelf life of vegetables like onions. By reducing oxygen exposure, it slows down oxidation and microbial growth that cause spoilage65. This method helps preserve important nutrients and antioxidants, maintaining freshness and color. Overall, vacuum packaging is an effective way to keep vegetables fresh longer and enhance their market value66.

The essential oil content varied depending on the packaging type. Specifically, packaging PF4 under normal air conditions resulted in higher essential oil content after 4 and 8 months, whereas packaging PF3 and PF2 under similar conditions showed a decrease in essential oil content after 4 months. Nanocomposite coatings improve shelf life by acting as barriers against moisture and oxygen, reducing spoilage67. They often contain antimicrobial nanoparticles that inhibit microbial growth and help control volatile compound release. These coatings also enhance mechanical strength, protecting products during storage and transport. Especially under vacuum conditions, nanocomposite packaging effectively preserves essential oils and maintains product quality68.

The results align with previous studies highlighting the effectiveness of active nanocomposite packaging, especially under vacuum, in reducing oxidation and preserving sensitive compounds. Importantly, this study introduces an innovative approach by combining cold plasma treatment with gas-barrier nanocomposite films, demonstrating a synergistic effect that not only minimizes degradation but also enhances the retention of phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity, and essential oil integrity69. These findings offer a novel strategy for the pharmaceutical and food industries aiming to extend the shelf life of vegetable essential oils. Furthermore, the study underscores the critical role of selecting suitable disinfection methods and packaging conditions in horticulture and postharvest handling of vegetables and herbs to simultaneously preserve functional quality, sensory attributes, and bioactive compounds over extended storage periods.

Methods

Plant material

Premium dried Welsh onions (Allium fistulosum L.) were sourced from local market in the city of Erbil (Iraq). Before the experiments, the plant material was botanically authenticated to confirm its purity.

Disinfection

An experiment employing a completely randomized design with five repetitions was carried out to evaluate different disinfection strategies. The treatments under investigation included gamma radiation and cold plasma. Gamma radiation was administered at levels of 1, 3, and 5 kilogray (kGy), whereas cold plasma was applied at intensities of 15, 20, and 25 kilovolts (kV), each for exposure times of 5, 10, and 15 min, accordingly70. Cold plasma treatment was carried out using a dielectric-barrier discharge (DBD) device (PlasmaBeam System, Relyon Plasma GmbH, Germany) equipped with a dual-voltage power supply. The system consisted of two parallel electrodes positioned 0.5 cm apart. Samples were arranged in a glass container, forming a 5-mm layer. Atmospheric air served as the feed gas, and the exposure duration was adjusted according to the experimental design.

Packaging

A factorial experimental design with five replicates was employed to assess the influence of various packaging materials and storage conditions. The study examined two key variables: the type of packaging film-comprising four nanocomposite films with gas barrier properties and the storage time, categorized into three durations: 4, 8, and 12 months. Welsh onion samples were sealed under two packaging environments: atmospheric (A) and vacuum (V), utilizing a locally available packaging system71. Throughout the storage period, qualitative characteristics of the plant material were monitored at each designated time point. Total phenolic content

Total phenolic content of A. fistulosum L. was measured using the Folin–Ciocalteu method72. A standard curve was prepared with methanolic gallic acid solutions (20–1000 μg/mL), mixed with 0.1 mL Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 0.27 mL of 7.5% sodium carbonate after 3 min. Absorbance was recorded at 765 nm after 2 h at room temperature using a Smart Spec Plus spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad). For analysis, 30 µL of extract (0.05 g/mL) was tested in triplicate. Results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry extract (mg GAE/g DW).

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant potential of the ethanolic extracts was assessed using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) free radical scavenging assay, following the procedure described by Bozin et al.73. The results were expressed in terms of IC₅₀ values, which indicate the concentration required to inhibit 50% of the DPPH radicals. Lower IC₅₀ values correspond to higher antioxidant capacity, allowing for a comparative evaluation of the extracts’ efficacy.

Color indexes

Color analysis was conducted using samples placed in a 50 × 50 cm lightbox. Images were captured with a Logitech HD Pro Webcam (Model C9020) and processed to extract RGB values using Average Color software. These values were converted to L*, a*, and b* indices using Colormine software, and additional color indices were calculated using specific formulas. The CIE Lab* color model (1976), a global standard for color measurement, was used: L* indicates lightness (0 = black, 100 = white), a* represents the red-green axis (positive = red, negative = green), and b* represents the yellow-blue axis (positive = yellow, negative = blue). Color changes were also tracked throughout the study using a ColorFlex colorimeter (HunterLab, USA), calibrated with a standard white tile. L*, a*, and b* values were recorded accordingly74.

Essential oil content and composition

A total of 100 g of dried, powdered plant material was subjected to hydro-distillation with 1500 mL of distilled water for 4 h using a Clevenger-type apparatus. The extracted essential oil (EO) was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and stored in dark vials at 4 °C75. For chemical analysis, the EO was analyzed using GC–MS (Agilent 5973, USA) equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m length, 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness). The column temperature was programmed to start at 70 °C (2 min hold), increase at 15 °C/min to 220 °C, and then rise to 300 °C with a 2 min hold. The injector temperature was 290 °C, and helium served as the carrier gas at 0.8 mL/min. The ion source temperature was 220 °C with 70 eV ionization energy76. EO yield was calculated as the percentage of oil extracted relative to the plant’s dry weight.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a completely randomized design (CRD) with five replicates. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using SAS software (version 9.0). Normality of residuals was verified with the PROC UNIVARIATE procedure. Mean comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a 5% significance level.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that disinfection methods, packaging, and storage duration significantly affect the phenolic content, antioxidant activity, color, and essential oil composition of the vegetable Allium fistulosum L. (Welsh onion). Cold plasma treatment, especially at 25 kV for 5 min, was the most effective in preserving and enhancing bioactive compounds, while high-dose gamma irradiation reduced these qualities. Packaging methods also influenced quality retention, with certain treatments like PF3A better maintaining phenolics and essential oils, though overall quality declined over longer storage. Vacuum packaging notably affected sulfur-containing compounds in the essential oils. Given the importance of quality and shelf-life in horticultural products, cold plasma combined with appropriate packaging offers a promising non-thermal technology to extend freshness and preserve the functional properties of fresh vegetables like Welsh onion.
