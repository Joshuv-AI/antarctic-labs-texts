PD-L1 in ICI Myocarditis
Authors: Adrian C Chen, Javid J Moslehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: cardio-oncology, endomyocardial biopsy, heart failure, immune checkpoint inhibitor, immunotherapy, myocarditis, onco-cardiology, PD-L1

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the treatment of many cancers, but their success has been accompanied by immune-related adverse events arising from unintended activation of host immunity. Among these complications, ICI myocarditis has emerged as one of the most feared. Although uncommon, myocarditis carries a disproportionate risk of morbidity and mortality compared with other immune toxicities.1 Early reports suggested mortality approaching 50%, creating the perception that the condition was uniformly catastrophic. More recent multicenter registries and improved recognition have shown that outcomes are heterogeneous, with many patients surviving when diagnosed early and treated appropriately.1 This evolving understanding has been paralleled by new therapeutic strategies. While corticosteroids remain first-line therapy, targeted immunomodulation is increasingly explored, including abatacept, which restores inhibitory signaling through CTLA-4 pathways, and ruxolitinib, which suppresses cytokine-driven inflammation through JAK inhibition.2 Despite these advances, one fundamental challenge remains: clinicians lack reliable tools to identify which patients with ICI myocarditis are at highest risk and require early escalation to aggressive immunosuppression. Identifying biomarkers that distinguish mild from fulminant disease therefore remains a critical unmet need in cardio-oncology.

In this issue of JACC: CardioOncology, Imaoka et al3 contribute to this goal by examining the relationship between myocardial PD-L1 expression and outcomes in patients with biopsy-confirmed ICI myocarditis. In a cohort of 20 patients undergoing endomyocardial biopsy, the investigators quantified PD-L1 expression using immunohistochemistry and evaluated its association with clinical severity and major adverse cardiotoxic events. They identified a threshold of approximately 8% PD-L1–positive cells that distinguished high- from low-expression groups. Patients with high myocardial PD-L1 expression experienced a markedly higher risk of adverse outcomes, including severe heart failure, arrhythmias, and cardiac death within 30 days. High PD-L1 expression was also associated with elevated troponin levels, prolonged QRS duration on electrocardiography, and greater inflammatory immune cell infiltration within the myocardium. Multiplex staining demonstrated PD-L1 expression not only on infiltrating macrophages, but also on cardiomyocytes. Collectively, these findings suggest that myocardial PD-L1 expression may serve as a histologic marker of inflammatory burden and identify patients with particularly severe immune-mediated myocardial injury.

The study has several strengths. First, the authors examined biopsy-proven cases of ICI myocarditis, providing histologic confirmation rather than relying solely on clinical criteria. Second, they used quantitative approaches to measure PD-L1 expression and correlated these findings with clinical outcomes. Third, multiplex immunohistochemistry provided mechanistic insight by identifying specific cell types expressing PD-L1 within the myocardium. At the same time, limitations merit consideration. The sample size is small, and the number of clinical events is limited. This suggests that the proposed PD-L1 threshold requires validation in larger cohorts. A further limitation is that multiplex immunohistochemistry cannot definitively attribute PD-L1 expression to specific cardiac cell populations, a concern that could be addressed with emerging approaches such as single-cell RNA sequencing or spatial transcriptomics. Endomyocardial biopsy is also subject to sampling variability given the patchy distribution of inflammatory infiltrates. The timing of biopsy varied across patients, which could influence measured PD-L1 levels. Finally, although the association between PD-L1 expression and outcomes is compelling, the study cannot determine whether PD-L1 contributes to disease progression or instead reflects underlying immune activation.

Understanding the biology of PD-1 and PD-L1 signaling helps interpret these findings. PD-1 is an inhibitory receptor expressed on activated T cells that regulates immune tolerance.4 Engagement of PD-1 by its ligand PD-L1 suppresses T cell receptor signaling, reduces cytokine production, and limits proliferation, preventing excessive immune-mediated tissue damage. In oncology, tumors exploit this pathway by expressing PD-L1, which attenuates antitumor immune responses and enables immune evasion. Therapeutic blockade of PD-1 or PD-L1 reverses this suppression and restores cytotoxic T cell activity against tumors. Importantly, PD-L1 is not constitutively expressed in most tissues but is typically induced as part of an inflammatory feedback loop. The dominant signal driving PD-L1 expression is interferon γ (IFN-γ), released by activated T cells during immune responses. IFN-γ binding activates JAK-STAT signaling, leading to STAT1 phosphorylation, induction of IRF1 (interferon regulatory factor 1), and transcription of the CD274 gene encoding PD-L1.5 At the same time, emerging data suggest that IFN-γ also contributes directly to myocardial injury by promoting the expansion of inflammatory macrophage populations that amplify cardiac inflammation.6 Thus, IFN-γ occupies a paradoxical position in myocarditis biology: it propagates pathogenic immune circuits while simultaneously inducing PD-L1 expression in the myocardium, which may function as a local negative feedback mechanism to restrain excessive T cell activation (Figure 1).

Within the cardiovascular system, PD-L1 expression has been observed in several inflammatory and immune-mediated conditions. Under normal physiological circumstances, cardiomyocytes show little PD-L1 expression. However, experimental and clinical studies demonstrate PD-L1 induction in cardiac tissue during immune activation. In models of autoimmune myocarditis, PD-L1 is upregulated on cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells in response to inflammatory cytokines.7 Similar patterns occur in viral myocarditis and transplant rejection, in which PD-L1 appears in both cardiac parenchymal cells and infiltrating immune cells.8 These observations suggest that PD-L1 expression in the myocardium represents a response to immune-mediated injury rather than a baseline feature of cardiac tissue. The findings of Imaoka et al therefore align with broader evidence that PD-L1 up-regulation accompanies inflammatory infiltration of the heart.

A key question raised by these observations is whether PD-L1 contributes directly to myocardial injury or represents a compensatory mechanism. Current evidence favors the latter. In many immune-mediated conditions, PD-L1 acts as a protective signal restraining T cell–mediated tissue damage.4 Increased PD-L1 expression correlated with greater inflammatory cell infiltration and more severe clinical manifestations, suggesting that it is induced in response to heightened immune activation. In this context, PD-L1 expression in cardiomyocytes may reflect the myocardium’s attempt to restrain immune injury. However, in the setting of checkpoint blockade, this endogenous brake becomes overwhelmed, leaving PD-L1 as a biomarker of immune escalation rather than protection. Thus, rather than driving disease, PD-L1 expression likely signals the intensity of immune attack within the myocardium.

Another observation by Imaoka et al provides further insight into PD-L1 biology. Myocardial PD-L1 expression was inversely correlated with the interval between symptom onset and biopsy and tended to be lower in patients who received corticosteroids before tissue sampling. This pattern suggests that PD-L1 expression is dynamically regulated and may diminish as inflammation resolves or is suppressed by immunosuppressive therapy. Clinically, this raises questions about the optimal timing of biopsy in suspected ICI myocarditis and how PD-L1 staining should be interpreted when tissue is obtained after treatment has begun. These findings also highlight that PD-L1 expression likely reflects a snapshot of ongoing immune activity rather than a static property of the myocardium.

How might these insights benefit patients? In principle, myocardial PD-L1 measurement could identify individuals with severe immune activation who may require early escalation of immunosuppressive therapy. However, endomyocardial biopsy is not routinely performed in most patients with suspected myocarditis. Two potential strategies could address this limitation. First, circulating soluble PD-L1 can be detected in blood and may reflect systemic immune activation, potentially serving as a noninvasive surrogate marker.9 Second, emerging molecular imaging approaches may allow visualization of PD-L1 expression in vivo.10 Positron emission tomography tracers targeting PD-L1 are already used in oncology to image tumor immune environments and could potentially be adapted to assess myocardial inflammation. Although these approaches remain investigational, they illustrate how mechanistic insights from studies such as this one may translate into improved risk stratification and personalized treatment for patients with ICI myocarditis.

In summary, one way to conceptualize the findings by Imaoka et al is that myocardial PD-L1 expression represents the biological footprint of myocardial immune regulation pushed to its limits. Inflammatory cytokines induce PD-L1 as a protective signal intended to restrain immune injury, yet in the setting of checkpoint blockade this pathway can no longer function effectively. The result is a paradox: higher PD-L1 expression reflects the myocardium’s attempt to defend itself while simultaneously identifying patients at greatest risk for immune escalation. The study by Imaoka and colleagues therefore may provide more than a prognostic biomarker; it may offer insight into the biology of ICI myocarditis. As the field moves toward precision cardio-oncology, integrating immune biology with clinical risk assessment may help identify patients who require aggressive immunomodulatory therapy while sparing others unnecessary treatment.11
