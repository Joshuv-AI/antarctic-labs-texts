Association of multiple trace metals in scalp hair with glioma risk: the mediating role of inflammation
Authors: Lingjun Yan, Honghai You, Huiying Wang, Chenyu Ding, Baochang He, Jing Wang, Wenhua Fang, Yuanxiang Lin, Dezhi Kang, Fa Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the relationship between 35 trace metals in scalp hair and the glioma risk as well as the potential mediating roles of 27 plasma inflammatory cytokines.MethodsA case–control study involving 228 participants was performed in southeastern China. Trace metals in scalp hair were analyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, and multiplex cytokines were detected based on Luminex® technology. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression in combination with four machine learning methods were used to select trace metals associated with gliomas. The joint exposure effect of trace metals was estimated using the generalized weighted quantile sum (gWQS) regression and quantile‐based g‐computation (qgcomp) algorithms.ResultsBoth LASSO regression and random forest algorithms identified five trace metals (gadolinium [Gd], lithium [Li], thulium [Tm], thorium [Th], and molybdenum [Mo]) associated with gliomas. After adjustments for potential confounders, Gd (odds ratio [OR] = 2.84, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.89–4.43) and Li (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.04–3.02) concentrations were positively associated with glioma risk, while Tm (OR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.17–0.73) and Th (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.28–0.71) exhibited inverse associations. Both gWQS and qgcomp algorithms showed Gd contributed most to the mixture effect. Moreover, there was a significant interaction between Gd and Tm or Th on glioma risk (p < 0.05). Notably, granulocyte–macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) mediated the association between Gd exposure and glioma risk by 25.75%.InterpretationThese findings suggest potential associations of certain trace metals, especially for Gd, with glioma risk, and may provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying from an inflammatory response perspective.

Introduction

Gliomas, the predominant malignant neoplasms within the central nervous system, exhibit an incidence rate of approximately 6.61 per 100,000 individuals, representing a substantial majority of primary intracranial tumors. 1 The WHO classification system delineates gliomas into low‐grade (Grades I and II) and high‐grade (Grades III and IV) categories. 2 Notoriously characterized by their infiltrative and aggressive behavior, high‐grade gliomas are marked by poor prognosis and limited survival outcomes. 3 Hence, the early detection of critical risk factors influencing gliomagenesis assumes critical importance in the prevention and therapeutic intervention of these aggressive tumors. Prior investigations have underscored the pivotal roles played by lifestyle variables (e.g., dietary patterns, tea, and coffee consumption), occupational hazards, and genetic predispositions in the etiology of gliomas. 4 , 5 , 6 Nevertheless, these identified risk factors elucidate only a fraction of the overall incidence of gliomas, implying the presence of additional undisclosed risk determinants.

In the past few decades, the acceleration of industrialization has led to a significant increase in metal pollution, posing a substantial threat to human health and becoming a critical environmental concern. 7 Trace metals pervade the environment, infiltrating the human body through inhalation, ingestion, and skin contact, thereby influencing the levels of trace metals within. 8 , 9 Essential trace metals like Mn, Cu, and Zn play pivotal roles in gene regulation, catalysis, and enzyme activation, crucial for sustaining normal brain function and growth. 10 , 11 Conversely, an excess of essential or toxic metals such as As, Cr, and Pb can compromise the blood–brain barrier, instigate cellular damage, thereby impairing the homeostasis of the central nervous system. 12 , 13 However, to date, there is limited research on the association of trace metals with the risk of gliomas, particularly in population‐based research. In contrast to the conventional use of blood samples, the analysis of trace metals in hair, a metabolic end product, provides a valuable insight into the enduring balance of these metals within the body over relatively prolonged durations. 14 Thus, the scrutiny of trace metal levels in hair samples have great implications in understanding their potential roles in the risk of gliomas.

Inflammatory cytokines, a diverse group of small molecules comprising glycoproteins and peptides with multifaceted pro‐inflammatory, anti‐inflammatory, and immunosuppressive properties, are susceptible to modulation by metal exposure. 15 Notably, metals such as Pb, Cd, Hg, and Ni exhibit varying degrees of influence on inflammatory cytokine levels. 16 Zn, an indispensable element crucial for enzymatic and transcriptional regulation, exerts inhibitory effects on the expression of TNF‐α, IL‐13, IL‐4, and IL‐6 at elevated concentrations. 17 Conversely, Zn deficiency impacts the production and bioactivity of diverse cytokines and antigen‐presenting cells, including IFN‐γ and TNF‐α, potentially leading to T‐cell immunosuppression. 18 On the other hand, inflammatory cytokines are believed to play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of gliomas. 19 A study from Norway revealed that heightened levels of IL‐4 and VEGF were associated with an increased risk of gliomas, while elevated levels of leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) and IL‐10 receptor were inversely correlated with glioma risk. 20 However, the potential role of inflammatory cytokines in mediating the interplay between trace metals and gliomas remains elusive.

Thus, the primary objective of this case–control study was to investigate the association between the concentrations of 35 trace metals in scalp hair and the risk of gliomas. A secondary goal was to explore the potential role of 27 plasma inflammatory cytokine levels as a mediator in the observed relationships.

Materials and Methods

Study design and study population

This study utilized a case–control research design. Between May 2021 and June 2022, a total of 100 patients diagnosed with gliomas were recruited from the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University in Fuzhou City, Fujian Province, China. Inclusion criteria comprised the following: (a) histological confirmation of primary gliomas; (b) age above 18 years and residency in Fujian Province for at least 10 years; and (c) Glasgow Coma Scale score of ≥13. Exclusion criteria for cases included the following: (a) recurrent gliomas or other non‐glioma brain tumors; (b) presence of distant metastases or secondary primary cancers; (c) recent use of cisplatin, ruthenium, antirheumatic, gastric drugs, or other metal‐containing medications within the last 3 months; (d) severe and persistent mental illness affecting questionnaire responses; and (e) prior magnetic resonance imaging before blood sampling.

During the corresponding period, 128 controls diagnosed with skull injuries, vascular malformations, and other non‐neoplastic disorders were included in the study. Inclusion criteria for controls stipulated (a) no previous malignancy diagnosis and (b) age above 18 years and residency in Fujian Province for at least 10 years. The exclusion criteria for controls were identical to those applied to the cases.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, Fuzhou, China (Approval ID: 2020005) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was acquired from each participant.

Epidemiological information and sample collection

Social demographics, lifestyle factors (including smoking and alcohol consumption), and recent medication use (including the types and durations) were collected using structured questionnaires conducted face‐to‐face. Fasting blood (5 mL) and scalp hair (1 g) samples were extracted from each case and control on the day of admission before the initiation of any therapies. Blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the plasma and then stored at −80°C until analysis. Hair samples were bagged in polyethylene bags and stored at room temperature.

Trace metals analysis

The hair samples were initially cut to 0.5 cm, washed thrice with 1% detergent, and subsequently subjected to ultrasonic cleaning for 15 min. Following this, the detergent was decanted, and ultrapure water was introduced, with ultrasonication at 80°C for 5 min, repeated three times, and finally dried at 80°C until a constant weight was attained. A quantity of 0.1 g of hair samples underwent digestion in a polytetrafluoroethylene digestion vessel with 2 mL of nitric acid and 3 mL of ultrapure water. Upon completion of digestion and neutralization of excess acid, the solution was diluted to 10 mL with deionized water, and analyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS, NexION 350X; Perkin‐Elmer, United States) for the quantification of 35 trace metals. Within the assay process, for quality control, the national standard substance GBW07601a (GSB‐20) human hair was employed as the quality control sample to oversee the determination results. Each batch of samples underwent analysis with a minimum of two experimental blanks and two quality control samples. Furthermore, 10% of the samples in the batch were subjected to parallel sample testing, ensuring that the relative standard deviation of the parallel sample test outcomes did not exceed 20%.

Details regarding the linear equations, correlation coefficients, detection limits, and detection rates of the trace elements are provided in Table S1. The r values of the linear equations all exceeded 0.99, indicating a strong linear relationship meeting the accuracy requirements. Elements Be, Sb, Tl, and V were excluded from subsequent analyses due to their non‐detection rate in hair exceeding 70%. In cases where the detection concentration of the remaining elements was below the limit of detection (LOD), 1/2 LOD was employed as a substitute.

Inflammatory cytokine array analysis

The inflammatory cytokine levels in the plasma were measured using a Luminex 200 system (Millipore) with a Bio‐Plex Pro Human Cytokine Grp I Panel 27‐Plex kit (Bio‐Rad, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. A standard curve consisting of 8 points was created by diluting the reconstituted stock standards provided by the manufacturer in a series of 1:4 dilutions. To ensure the accuracy of the assay, quality control samples provided by the manufacturer were analyzed concurrently with the test samples. Through age and gender frequency matching, 39 cases and 39 controls were selected for inflammatory cytokine testing (Table S2).

Data acquisition and processing were performed using Milliplex Analyst software. The undetectable rates of 27 inflammatory cytokines are given in Table S3. The cytokines (IL‐15, IL‐2, IL‐5, IL‐6, and VEGF) displaying undetectable rates exceeding 70%, were excluded from subsequent analysis. To address missing values concerning the remaining cytokines, a multiple imputation approach was employed, as depicted in Figure S1.

Statistical analysis

The distinction in baseline characteristics between the case and control groups was evaluated through chi‐squared analysis. The distributions of trace metals and cytokines were described by median and interquartile range, and compared by Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. They subsequently underwent natural log transformation to achieve normality. The selection of trace metals was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, in combination with four machine learning methods, namely random forest, support vector machines (SVM), neural network, and naive Bayes model (NBM). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to evaluate the predictive efficacy of selected trace metals. The association between trace metals or inflammatory cytokines and gliomas was examined through univariable and multivariable logistic regression, with odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) computed. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to display the association between trace metals and glioma risk. Additionally, the joint exposure effect of trace metals was estimated using the generalized weighted quantile sum regression (gWQS) and quantile‐based g‐computation algorithms. The gWQS effectively reduces dimensionality and addresses multicollinearity, but it remains constrained to detecting linear associations in one direction. The latter could further provide direct visualization of positive or negative effects of trace elements on glioma risk. Multiple linear regression was utilized to assess the relationships between trace metals and cytokines. We carried out all mediation analyses utilizing the “mediation” R package to estimate the average causal mediation (indirect) effects, the average direct effects, total effects, and the proportion of mediation effects, defined as the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect. Finally, E‐values were computed to evaluate the potential implications of unmeasured confounding factors. 21 Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.1.3), with statistical significance set at a two‐tailed p value below 0.05.

Results

The study encompassed a total of 228 participants meeting the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, with the mean age of 52.7 ± 12.4 years and 119 (52.2%) women. The basic characteristics of the cases and controls are shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences in general demographic characteristics between the glioma patients and controls (p > 0.05), including age, sex, education levels, residence, occupation, history of cancer, BMI, and tea consumption. The glioma patients, compared with their control counterparts, were more likely to have tobacco smoking (38.0% vs. 24.2%) and alcohol drinking (20.0% vs. 10.2%).

Selection of trace metals associated with gliomas by machine learning methods

Correlation analysis revealed a strong association among these trace metals (Fig. S2). To address multicollinearity, LASSO regression was employed for data analysis, and five trace elements (Gd, Li, Tm, Th, and Mo) associated with gliomas were then selected out of the 31 trace metals based on the minimum mean square error (Fig. 1A,B).

Screening of trace metals in scalp hair associated with gliomas. (A) The coefficient profiles of LASSO regression. (B) The tuning parameter selection plot of LASSO regression. The dotted lines represented the minimum and 1‐SE lambda for the optimal volume of variables, respectively. (C) The top five trace metals with the highest discriminatory power sorted by mean decrease Gini value in the random forest model. (D) Receiver operator characteristic curve of five trace metals.

Additionally, we randomly divided the data into training and test sets in a ratio of 7:3, and four other learning algorithms, including random forest, SVM, neural network, and NBM, were used for trace metals selection via developing prediction model. Notably, random forest exhibited the highest accuracy (75.0%) among the four algorithms (Table S4). Furthermore, through 10‐fold cross‐validation of random forest model, a subset of trace metals with the best predictive performance was identified, containing Li, Mo, Gd, Tm, and Th with an AUC of 0.742 (95% CI: 0.677–0.807) (Fig. 1C,D), which is consistent with the five elements screened out by the LASSO regression on described above, suggesting the robustness of our results.

Individual and combined effect of trace metals on glioma risk

The ORs and 95% CIs for the associations between the five identified trace metals and gliomas are presented in Figure 2. After adjustments for potential confounders, elevated concentrations of Li (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.04–3.02) and Gd (OR = 2.84, 95% CI: 1.89–4.43) in hair samples were positively linked to an increased risk of gliomas, while elevated concentrations of Tm (OR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.17–0.73) and Th (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.28–0.71) exhibited associations with a reduced risk of gliomas. Conversely, no significant association between Mo and glioma risk was observed. Furthermore, the RCS analysis also depicted the similar association patterns between these five trace metals and the risk of gliomas (Fig. S3).

Subsequent analysis using gWQS regression model revealed that the Gd contributed most to the mixture effect, constituting 59.59% (Fig. 3A). Additionally, the quantile‐based g‐computation analysis unveiled positive effects in the same direction for the Gd and Li, while Th and Tm showcased negative effects, with Gd and Th displaying the most substantial positive versus negative weights at 80.53% and 51.17%, respectively (Fig. 3B). The interaction analysis showed the interaction of Gd and Tm, as well as Gd and Th, indicating an escalated risk of gliomas at high Gd concentrations alongside low Tm or Th concentrations (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3C,D).

Associations of plasma cytokines with metal exposure and glioma risk

The results from the multivariate linear regression analyses concerning the levels of the five trace metals and the 22 cytokines are detailed in Table S5. Notably, the element Tm exhibited negative associations with FGF‐basic and MIP1‐α, whereas positive correlations were observed between element Gd and GM‐CSF, IFN‐γ, IL‐13, and IL‐1β, as well as between element Mo and FGF‐basic and eotaxin, and between element Th and eotaxin. The relationships between 22 cytokines and gliomas were evaluated through logistic regression model (Table S6). Following adjustments, elevated levels of G‐CSF, GM‐CSF, IL‐13, IL‐17A, IL‐1β, IL‐1Ra, IL‐4, and IL‐8 were linked to an augmented risk of gliomas, while increased levels of IP‐10 were associated with a reduced risk of gliomas.

Mediation effects

Following the correlational analyses, mediation analyses were conducted to further explore whether cytokine levels can mediate the trace metals impact on the gliomas. As presented in Table 3 and Figure 4, GM‐CSF emerged as a significant partial mediator in the association between Gd levels and gliomas (β = 0.033, 95%CI: 0.005–0.070, proportion mediated = 25.75%).

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between concentrations of 35 trace metals in scalp hair and the risk of gliomas using a case–control study design and explored the potential mediating roles of cytokines in plasma in this association. Our findings revealed a positive correlation between the concentrations of Li and Gd and an increased, whereas the concentrations of Tm and Th were linked to a reduced glioma risk. Notably, Gd emerged as the most influential factor in this mixture effect. Moreover, we observed that the cytokine GM‐CSF played a mediating role in the relationship between Gd levels and glioma risk.

Gd is a silvery white metal with atomic number 64 that reacts with oxygen and water. Leveraging its paramagnetic characteristics, it commonly features in chelated forms within magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents to enhance signal intensity. 22 A study explored the trace elements in the blood and tissues of 26 brain tumor patients and 21 controls, and found that Gd content of tumor patients was higher than that of the control group in both blood and tissues, 23 which is in line with the results of the study by Zhuang et al. 24 On the one hand, this implies that Gd can cross the blood–brain barrier and enter the brain. On the other hand, this may be related to the fact that Gd can regulate the turnover and release of neurotransmitters and promote the generation of reactive oxygen species by blocking different types of sodium, potassium, and calcium channels in human neurons, thus leading to oxidative neuronal damage and dysfunction and producing oxidative neurotoxicity. 25 , 26 Our investigation revealed a significant upsurge in elemental Gd levels in the hair of glioma patients compared to controls, correlating with an augmented risk of gliomas. This may be due to the incomplete removal of Gd complexes during sewage treatment, and the free Gd3+ ions generated by the degradation process can enter the human body through drinking water and food consumption, thus causing long‐term harm to human health. 27

Elevated levels of Th have been observed in astrocytoma tissues in comparison to normal brain tissue. 24 Interestingly, cellular experiments have demonstrated that the co‐administration of Th‐labeled antibodies with immune checkpoint inhibitors effectively impedes tumor progression. 28 Consistently, Lejeune et al. reported that Th conjugates stimulate the immune response, augmenting antitumor efficacy, 29 aligning with prior work by Hammer et al. 30 Additionally, Dai et al. delineated concentration‐dependent responses of Th3+ on mouse bone marrow stromal cell viability. 31 Our study revealed a negative correlation between Th and Tm levels and the glioma risk, possibly attributed to variations in Th and Tm accumulation across distinct brain regions and cancer subtypes, 32 with concentrations below their respective toxicity thresholds.

Furthermore, this study identified an interplay among the Gd and the Th and Tm. A previous investigation on hair rare earth elements and passive smoking highlighted a significant association between Gd and Tm. 33 Nevertheless, the current evidence base concerning the aforementioned metal interactions is insufficient. Our hypothesis posits that these interacting metals may influence glioma development by modulating shared pathways or environmental factors, potentially leading to synergistic or antagonistic effects. 34 Further in‐depth studies are imperative to elucidate the specific mechanisms and causes underlying these interactions.

Previous research has demonstrated that imbalance of trace metals can induce oxidative damage and chronic inflammation in biological systems. 35 , 36 Moreover, the development of gliomas is intimately associated with the immune‐inflammatory cascade. 37 GM‐CSF plays a pivotal role as a hematopoietic growth factor and a central mediator in inflammatory cascades. 38 Specifically, GM‐CSF has been shown to activate monocytes, triggering the production of cytokines such as TNF‐α and upregulating endothelial adhesion molecules and inflammatory profiles that disrupt the blood–brain barrier. 39 Elevated GM‐CSF levels have been observed in more aggressive gliomas compared to less aggressive counterparts. 40 Our study also identified a positive association between GM‐CSF levels and glioma risk, alongside a significant positive correlation between GM‐CSF and Gd levels. Mediation analysis indicated that GM‐CSF partially mediates the relationship between Gd levels and gliomas, suggesting that the inflammatory response could be a potential mechanism through which trace metal accumulation contributes to cerebral gliomas, warranting comprehensive exploration in future investigations.

The major strength of our study is that it is the first to utilize diverse statistical models scrutinize the impact of individual and combined trace metals on glioma pathogenesis, alongside an exploration of the potential mediating role of cytokines. However, several limitations are noteworthy. Firstly, this is a preliminary study that has a relatively small sample size. The lack of available specimens hindered a thorough examination of IDH‐1 gene mutations and their potential interactions with trace metals. Secondly, we only measured the total concentration of the trace metals, rather than specific metal forms and valence states, necessitating further detailed exploration of the relationship between each trace metal valence or form and glioma risk. Thirdly, despite adjustment for certain potential confounders, residual confounding such as dietary habits, background information on the regional trace elements, and genetic factors cannot be completely ruled out. However, the E‐values analysis (Table S7) suggested that unmeasured confounding factors were insufficient to fully explain away the association observed in the present study. Our results should be validated in future studies conducted in different regions and populations with diverse dietary habits and ethnicities. Finally, the reliance on a case–control design in this study weakens the causal argument, emphasizing the necessity for future prospective studies to validate our findings.

In conclusion, this study revealed that hair concentrations of Gd and Li were positively associated with risk of gliomas, while Tm and Th concentrations were associated to a reduced risk of gliomas, with Gd exerting the most substantial influence in the combined effect. Furthermore, the cytokine GM‐CSF was identified as a mediator in the relationship between Gd exposure and gliomas. Subsequent large‐scale prospective validation studies and animal experiments are essential to validate these results and unravel the underlying mechanisms.
