Cost‐Effectiveness of Automated Insulin Delivery Systems in Paediatrics, T1D and T2D Adults Across Four Nordic Countries
Authors: Johan Jendle, Kirsten Nørgaard, Elina Pimiä, Heiko Bratke, Aesha Khan Mirón, Maria‐Eleni Syleouni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study evaluated long‐term cost‐effectiveness of automated insulin delivery (AID) systems versus multiple daily injections (MDI) + CGM in children with type 1 diabetes (T1D), adults with T1D and insulin‐treated individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) across Denmark, Finland, Norway and Sweden.Materials and MethodsClinical outcomes and economic footprints were analysed for horizons up to 50 years from a healthcare system perspective using the IQVIA Core Diabetes Model. Five patient profiles were modelled: T1D paediatrics (T1D‐Paed), T1D adults with high baseline HbA1c (T1D‐Adult), real‐world evidence in T1D adults with lower baseline HbA1c (T1D‐AdultRWE), T2D adults (T2D‐Adult) and older T2D adults (T2D‐Elder). Costs were inflated to July 2024, with 3% annual discounting. Sensitivity analyses explored scenarios varying time horizons and HbA1c effect sizes and inclusion of hypoglycaemia rates.ResultsAID systems were estimated to lead to a reduction in diabetes‐related complications in all groups, most notably in the T1D‐Adult group (318 vs. 542 complications per 100 individuals). Improvements translated into lifetime cost savings (€1435 to €26 259), increased life expectancy (LE) and more quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs). Incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios (ICERs) remained below willingness‐to‐pay thresholds in all countries (from €14 695/QALY for T1D‐Adult in Finland to €56 711/QALY for T1D‐AdultRWE in Denmark), confirming AID is cost‐effective across all groups in the Nordic region.ConclusionsAID therapy consistently projected reduced diabetes‐related complications while remaining cost‐effective across a broad spectrum of diabetes patient profiles in the Nordic region. These findings strengthen the case towards widespread integration of AID technology into national diabetes care frameworks.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, including both type 1 (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D), is among the most prevalent and fastest‐growing chronic diseases worldwide [1, 2]. The Nordic region reports some of the highest incidence rates of T1D globally, particularly among children and adolescents [3]. Concurrently, the incidence of T2D continues to rise, mainly driven by an increase in obesity prevalence, sedentary lifestyles and an ageing population [4]. The increasing number of individuals living with diabetes and its associated long‐term complications challenges both the individuals and the publicly funded healthcare systems substantially. This underscores the need for health care strategies that ensure equitable and sustainable access to innovation [5].

Optimal glycaemic control, typically assessed by glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), is crucial for reducing the risk of both acute complications such as severe hypoglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis and chronic micro‐ and macrovascular complications, including myocardial infarction, nephropathy, or retinopathy [6]. International guidelines recommend clear therapeutic targets, aiming for an HbA1c of under 53 mmol/mol (7.0%) in adults [7, 8] and under 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) in children [9]. Yet, a substantial gap continues to exist between these recommendations and the clinical reality for T1D and T2D individuals in the Nordic region. In Sweden, for instance, ~45% of adults with T2D exceeded the HbA1c target in 2019 [10] and this percentage increased up to 57% among Norwegian adults with T1D in 2022 [11]. This gap is even more pronounced among children with T1D, as more than 60% of individuals in Nordic paediatric registries present HbA1c levels above 7.0% [12].

Automated insulin delivery (AID) systems have been a major advance in diabetes care. These systems integrate continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), insulin pumps, and algorithm‐driven insulin adjustments in real time [13]. Evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and real‐world data consistently show that AID systems improve glycaemic outcomes, such as increased time in range (TIR), and enhanced health‐related quality of life in individuals with diabetes [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Beyond clinical benefits, individuals living with diabetes and using AID reported reduced mental burden of diabetes management, improved sleep quality and reduced fear of hypoglycaemia, compared to multiple daily injections (MDI) used in combination with CGM—which require greater daily engagement and diabetes self‐management [19].

However, despite the well‐documented clinical benefits of AID systems, their widespread adoption has remained constrained by high device costs and heterogeneous reimbursement landscapes. An important barrier has been the limited availability of long‐term cost‐effectiveness evidence; without a clear demonstration that immediate device costs are offset by future complication avoidance, healthcare payers face uncertainty that restricts broad and sustainable funding [20]. Previous cost‐effectiveness studies for AID technologies for Sweden and Finland have only focused on the adult T1D population [21, 22]. For Denmark and Norway, comparable economic analyses have not been published so far. The lack of evidence regarding the long‐term clinical impact and the economic value of AID systems might affect budget and resource allocation decisions, potentially limiting patient access to these innovative therapies across the Nordic region. Therefore, country‐specific analyses addressing both the paediatric and adult populations are essential to support informed, equitable and sustainable health care policy decisions.

This study aims to provide evidence of the value of AID beyond the standard of care, by evaluating the long‐term cost‐effectiveness of AID systems compared to MDI + CGM. We analysed five different groups: children and adolescents with T1D, adults with non‐optimal glycaemic control (mean baseline HbA1c = 9.0%) and with closer to optimal glycaemic control (mean baseline HbA1c = 7.4%) T1D, and adults and elderly people with T2D using intensive insulin therapy in Denmark, Finland, Norway and Sweden.

Materials and Methods

Cost‐Effectiveness Analysis

Model Overview

This health economic evaluation employed the widely validated IQVIA Core Diabetes Model V.10.0 (CDM) [23], which simulates the disease progression for individuals with T1D and T2D, providing short‐ and long‐term clinical and economic outcomes. The IQVIA CDM integrates interlinked sub‐models governed by annual Markov cycles and Monte Carlo simulations, accounting for patient demographics, baseline risk factors, treatment effects and healthcare costs.

Among others, model inputs include changes in HbA1c, incidence of severe hypoglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis, as well as other clinical outcomes from cohort‐specific data source and literature [22].

Cohorts' Characteristics and Clinical Inputs

Data from five different patient groups were sourced from published real world evidence and clinical trials: (1) Children and adolescents (paediatric population) with T1D (T1D‐Paed) [24]; (2) Adults with T1D (T1D‐Adult) [25]; (3) Adults with T1D from real‐world evidence data (T1D‐AdultRWE) [26]; (4) Adults with T2D (T2D‐Adult) [16]; and (5) Older adults with T2D (T2D‐Elder) [27]. The main characteristics of each study cohort are provided in Table S1.

Simulations were run for both the intervention (AID systems) and comparator (MDI + CGI) groups. The HbA1c treatment effect was implemented as a one‐time reduction in the first year based on the respective study source, and was assumed to remain constant thereafter, in line with previous modelling conventions [28]. For the base case analysis, no hypoglycaemic events were considered due to the short follow‐up durations of the source studies, which limited the observation of these events. An exception was made for the T1D‐AdultRWE cohort, for which representative hypoglycaemia rates were reported in the study results.

Risk of Complications and Mortality

The following equations were applied to estimate the risk of complications arising from diabetes and their potential associated costs. The Swedish National Diabetes Register T1D model (SNDR T1) [29] and the T2D model (SNDR T2D) [30] were applied to estimate risk factors and outcomes for adult populations with T1D and T2D, respectively. SNDR T1 was also used to estimate cardiovascular complications in T1D‐Paed, although using adult registries [29]. All‐cause mortality across all cohorts was estimated using the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study 82 risk equations (UKPDS‐82), which are widely employed to model mortality in diabetes populations [31]. Consistent with the IQVIA CDM structure, complication risks were driven by mean HbA1c levels; HbA1c variability was not included as an independent parameter in the risk equations. The model assumes that the relationship between glycaemic exposure and long‐term outcomes is independent of the technology used to achieve glycaemic control.

Perspective, Costs and Time Horizon

The analyses were conducted from the perspective of each country's national healthcare system, considering direct costs only. These included costs associated with healthcare resources used in both primary and secondary care: treatment, patient management, cardiovascular disease, renal disease, ulcer treatment and amputations, eye disease and hypoglycaemia. All costs were sourced from published literature through targeted literature search and national tariff lists, using country‐specific reimbursement fees. A detailed breakdown of the total direct costs per patient population and unitary costs for complications and management used in the model is provided in Tables S2 and S3. Costs were updated to July 2024 values and were converted to euros (€) with the following currency conversion rates (July 2025): 0.13 (DKK to EUR); 0.085 (NOK to EUR) and 0.089 (SEK to EUR).

Regarding device costs, annual expenditures for AID treatment were estimated as the average cost across the following systems (mylife Loop powered by CamAPS FX AID system, MiniMed 780G). Furthermore, CGMs' costs were assumed to be identical for all individuals.

An annual discount rate of 3% was applied to both costs and utility outcomes. While national guidelines in the Nordic region vary slightly (ranging from 2.5% to 4%) [32], a unified 3% rate was selected to ensure comparability of results across countries and to align with international health economic standards. A 50‐year time horizon was used as the base‐case analysis to capture the long‐term effects of AID systems, which in adult cohorts approximates a lifetime horizon. Finally, all incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios (ICERs) were reported considering discounted quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs).

Health Utilities

QALYs were estimated using cohort‐specific baseline utilities and complications‐specific disutilities. Utility is a preference‐based measure of the health status, ranging from values below zero (worse than death) to one (best health status), where 0 represents death. Utility reflects the impact of a health state on an individual's quality of life, whereas disutility is the decrement of utility associated with that state [33]. An additional utility gain associated with reduced fear of hypoglycaemia (FoH) was applied to all AID groups as follows: 0.0576 for the T1D‐Paed cohort [24]; 0.0352 for the T1D‐AdultRWE population [26] and 0.0552 for the remaining T1D and T2D groups [22].

Sensitivity Analysis

To examine the base‐case model robustness, both univariate deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) with second order sampling were conducted. The one‐way deterministic sensitivity analysis explored six alternative scenarios by varying key model parameters: three alternative time horizons (5‐, 15‐ and 30‐year), ±20% variation in the HbA1c treatment effect, and inclusion of hypoglycaemic rates [34]. The latter was not applied to the T1D‐AdultRWE cohort, where hypoglycaemic events were already incorporated into the base‐case scenario. The PSA with second order sampling used a 5% uncertainty level for all model inputs to generate cost‐effectiveness acceptability curves and confidence intervals. This PSA was designed to assess model stability and decision robustness under simultaneous small parameter perturbations, rather than to fully characterise parameter uncertainty, given the heterogeneous nature of the input data sources and the absence of precise variance parameters. Both the ICERs and the cost‐effectiveness acceptability curves were interpreted using commonly cited country‐specific willingness‐to‐pay (WTP) reference values derived from published health economic literature: DKK 458134 (€59 557) for Denmark [35], €50 000 for Finland [36], NOK 814450 (€69 228) for Norway [37] and SEK 500000 (€44 500) for Sweden [38].

Cost Impact Analysis

Cost impact was estimated by applying unitary costs to the absolute reduction in diabetes‐related complications projected by the IQVIA CDM for each cohort. The model simulated the cumulative incidence of major complications per 100 individuals over a lifetime horizon under both treatment arms (AID and MDI + CGM). Differences in event rates were multiplied by the corresponding event‐specific costs to derive total complication‐related savings.

Included cost savings comprised cardiovascular disease, renal disease, ulcer treatment & amputation, eye disease, hypoglycaemia and general diabetes management. All savings were computed as direct medical costs, from the national healthcare payer perspective. A 3% annual discount rate was applied, in line with base‐case assumptions.

Results

Base‐Case Analysis

Across all T1D and T2D cohorts over the 50‐year horizon, AID therapy generated gains in both life expectancy (LE) and QALYs compared to MDI + CGM (Table 1). The greatest improvements were observed in the T1D‐Adult cohort, with an increase of 2.12 years in LE and 3.56 in QALYs (undiscounted values). The smallest gains in LE were noted in the T1D‐Paed and T2D‐Adult cohorts (0.29 and 0.37, respectively), while the lowest incremental QALYs were observed in the T2D groups (1.15 in T2D‐Adult and 0.96 in T2D‐Elder) (Table 1).

Complications

The use of AID systems was associated with a lower cumulative incidence of overall complications per 100 individuals compared to the MDI + CGM group. This clinical benefit was consistently observed across all five patient subgroups (Figure S1).

All cohorts showed a lower relative risk for diabetes‐related complications in the AID users, compared to the MDI + CGM users, with the most significant effects seen for proliferative retinopathy and end‐stage renal disease (Figure S2). The T1D‐Adult cohort showed the lowest relative risk for complications, particularly for proliferative diabetic retinopathy (−69.9%) and microalbuminuria (−57.1%). In contrast, the T2D‐Adult cohort showed the smallest relative reductions across diabetes‐related complications.

Cost savings associated with the reduction in diabetes‐related complications are reported in Table 3. While the savings were comparable across most cohorts and countries, they were notably higher in Finland for the cohorts with poorer baseline control: T1D‐Paed (baseline HbA1c 7.90%; savings of €23 434) and T1D‐Adult (baseline HbA1c 9.04%; savings of €26 259). Discussion

This study demonstrates that AID treatment is a cost‐effective intervention for children and adolescents with T1D, adults with T1D, even with slightly elevated HbA1c, and insulin‐treated adults with T2D across the Nordic region. Over a 50‐year time horizon, the use of AID was projected to increase LE and QALYs for all T1D and T2D groups. In line with the clinical outcomes, all ICERs indicated AID as a cost‐effective treatment across Denmark, Finland, Sweden and Norway.

The difference observed between T1D and T2D populations on quality‐of‐life metrics, especially LE, and costs, is a direct consequence of the different ages at diabetes onset. Because T1D is generally diagnosed at a younger age, this 50‐year metric for many falls within a normal lifespan and is sensitive to treatment improvements. For T2D, often diagnosed later in life [40], achieving a longer LE is not as feasible, due to later treatment start and subsequent benefit lag. Overall, the gains observed were driven by a substantial reduction in the cumulative incidence of diabetes‐related complications compared with CGM + MDI. Such evidence supports the broader and sustained use of AID for diabetes management.

In the base‐case analysis, AID systems improved both LE and QALYs compared to MDI + CGM across all study cohorts. As most Nordic countries lack formal, government‐mandated WTP thresholds, this analysis utilised commonly accepted, country‐specific benchmarks from published health economic literature [35, 36, 37, 38]. Under these assumptions, all ICERs in the base‐case analysis remained below the country‐specific WTP thresholds. These results are consistent with previous economic evaluations conducted in Sweden and Finland using the IQVIA CDM, which reported similar QALY gains and ICERs for T1D paediatric and adult populations [21, 22, 41].

The ICERs varied based on the baseline glycaemic control, reflecting the principle that the greatest health economic value is usually expected in populations with the largest potential for clinical improvement. Therefore, AID was most cost‐effective in the T1D‐Adult cohort, whose high baseline HbA1c (9.04%) allowed for the greatest health gains and resulted in the lowest ICERs. Conversely, while still demonstrating clinical benefits and remaining cost‐effective, the T1D‐AdultRWE cohort exhibited the highest ICERs among all T1D populations, which was an expected outcome given the closer to target baseline HbA1c (7.40%). Lower baseline HbA1c would mean that fewer long‐term diabetes‐related complications would be expected overall [42]. Additionally, individuals with near‐target glycaemic control may prioritise immediate symptom relief over long‐term outcomes [43]; however, the model does not account for these short‐term perceived benefits. Despite these factors, we still observe that AID is a clinically relevant and cost‐effective intervention even among near‐glucose‐target individuals, when used long‐term.

Indeed, sensitivity analyses further highlighted the importance of analysing long‐term scenarios, as for the near‐target T1D‐AdultRWE cohort, ICERs exceeded the WTP threshold in scenarios with shorter time horizons. In general, ICERs were lower with longer follow‐up, reflecting the chronic nature of the disease and the delayed benefits from preventing severe complications beyond 30 years. Thus, all analyses were conducted from the healthcare systems' perspective to ensure that the long‐term clinical and economic benefits of AID systems are adequately reflected, even in populations with lower baseline risk but persistent therapeutic needs. Importantly, the 50‐year horizon effectively represents a lifetime analysis for T2D and older adult cohorts, while for T1D‐Paed the analysis truncates at ~65 years of age. This approach is likely conservative; longer lifetime horizons would capture additional averted complications and yield more favourable ICERs in this group.

Our study not only is in line with prior published evidence for adult T1D populations but also provides novel health economic insights into T1D paediatric and T2D populations groups that have historically been underrepresented in AID assessment [20]. Additionally, it provides insight on the long‐term clinical and economic results, since such studies usually have short follow‐up periods.

In the T1D‐Paed cohort, AID systems lead to 3.6 additional undiscounted QALYs gained over the 50‐year period and demonstrated cost‐effectiveness compared to MDI + CGM as well as lower cumulative incidence of diabetes‐related complications. Given the early onset and lifelong nature of T1D [1], initiating AID systems in childhood is crucial for optimal diabetes management and offers considerable potential for long‐term health benefits as well as substantial cumulative cost savings from averted diabetes‐related complications. These outcomes are aligned with findings from a large paediatric real‐world study in England [15]. This study reported significant improvements in glycaemic outcomes such as HbA1c, TIR and hypoglycaemia frequency, and in quality‐of‐life metrics. While the lowest ICERs were observed in the T1D‐Adult cohort, the economic outcomes of our study confirm the perception of AID systems as a high‐value intervention for paediatric T1D populations. This reinforces the importance of early adoption despite country variations in local cost structures and WTP thresholds.

Although the short‐term benefits of AID systems are well‐established for T1D individuals [44], their impact on the insulin‐treated T2D population remains less explored [20, 45]. Our study demonstrated that AID systems were cost‐effective across all countries in both the T2D‐Adult and T2D‐Elder groups, although QALY gains were relatively modest. These results reflect the nature of T2D, which is typically diagnosed later in life, thereby offering a shorter time window to observe the same clinical benefits and cost savings seen in T1D cohorts. More specifically, a modest absolute reduction in cumulative diabetes‐related complications was observed in both T2D groups. However, given the higher prevalence and healthcare expenditure associated with T2D [1, 5, 46], these findings reinforce the relevance of AID systems as a strategic, value‐based therapeutic option for individuals with T2D who require insulin treatment in addition to non‐insulin glucose‐lowering treatments to optimise glycaemic control. The fact that AID is cost‐effective for individuals with T2D in all examined countries, mirroring results in T1D, highlights the importance of ensuring equitable and inclusive access to innovation for all intensively insulin‐treated persons, which is essential to fully capture the health benefits of AID therapy while minimising the economic burden on healthcare systems. A broader adoption of AID should be a priority in clinical decision‐making, in diabetes care budget planning and in health policy development.

In this study several limitations should be considered. The model assumes a one‐time HbA1c reduction during the first year of AID use, sustained over a lifetime horizon. While consistent with modelling conventions, this approach may not fully reflect real‐world dynamics such as treatment fatigue or improvements in device performance over time, considering the fast development of automated therapies. Variability in the comparator arms (MDI vs. pumps) across source studies may also influence incremental costs; however, this reflects real‐world practice variation. Second, data constraints precluded the inclusion of diabetes educator costs, specific drug‐device synergies (e.g., with SGLT2 inhibitors) and broader patient‐reported outcomes such as treatment satisfaction or sleep quality. Further clinical research should focus on capturing patient‐reported outcomes and quality of life metrics to fully reflect the impact that AID therapy can have in people living with diabetes and their caregivers' lives. Critically, the analysis was restricted to a healthcare payer perspective. The exclusion of indirect costs (e.g., productivity loss), alongside unquantified clinical benefits, suggests that the results presented here are likely a conservative estimate of the full value of AID therapy from a societal perspective. Finally, the absence of an official WTP threshold per QALY in Denmark, Finland and Norway requires interpreting cost‐effectiveness with some degree of uncertainty, relying instead on literature‐based benchmarks [35, 36, 37, 38].

However, this study has significant strengths. This analysis presents country‐specific evidence on the cost effectiveness of AID systems using a widely validated economic model across five distinct and clinically relevant patient populations. Therefore, covering a significant proportion of the population with insulin‐treated diabetes and various glycaemic profiles, including those often overlooked. The robustness of the base‐case findings was confirmed through comprehensive sensitivity analysis. The demonstration of consistent health gains, reduced complication risk and favourable cost‐effectiveness across diverse diabetes populations provides strong evidence to inform clinicians, payers and healthcare policy makers, further supporting the broader integration of AID therapy into diabetes care pathways as a sustainable standard of care for insulin‐treated individuals living with diabetes. Finally, the model relies on established relationships between glycaemic exposure and the risk of long‐term complications and mortality, assuming these are independent of the specific technology used.

Our analysis provides country‐specific evidence that AID systems are a cost‐effective therapy for diverse T1D and insulin‐treated T2D populations in the Nordic region, highlighting the long‐term value of AID systems. Additionally, findings confirm and extend existing health economic evidence on AID technologies and address critical gaps by including paediatric and T2D populations. Enabling broader adoption of AID with equitable access in diabetes care should be a priority in clinical decision‐making, in diabetes care budget planning and in health policy development.

In conclusion, this study provides comprehensive evidence that AID systems offer consistent short and long‐term clinical benefits, increase LE for individuals living with insulin treated diabetes, improve quality of life, and help to reduce the economic burden across diverse T1D and T2D diabetes populations in the Nordic region.

Given the magnitude and consistency of health gains observed—alongside their alignment with country‐specific WTP thresholds—the widespread integration of AID systems into national diabetes frameworks should be considered a priority, equitable and sustainable health care strategy.
