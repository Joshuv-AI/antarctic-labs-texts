Private Practice Dietetics Workforce Development: What 45 Thought Leaders Agree On
Authors: Donnelly Jennifer, Clark Peter, Walsh Louisa, Lane Rebecca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe growth of private practice dietetics has been driven by intrinsic and extrinsic factors. This expansion highlights the importance of appropriate training, preparation and support for new graduates entering private practice, yet existing literature identifies deficiencies across these areas. The aim of this study was to develop consensus amongst a panel of Australian Private Practice Workforce Development Thought Leaders on private practice dietetics workforce development aspects, including the challenges, function, training and preparation needs, support, and solutions.MethodsPreviously published literature informed a modified Delphi questionnaire conducted amongst private practice workforce development Thought Leaders, who had extensive private practice experience or substantial standing in the profession with regard to private practice, to seek agreement on private practice dietetics workforce development aspects. Content validity assessment was used to determine their rating. Items scoring excellent (index > 0.78) were added to the framework for the development of evidence‐based recommendations.ResultsForty‐six Thought Leaders consented and completed round one, 45 completed two‐rounds of the modified‐Delphi questionnaires and represented all states of Australia. After two rounds of iterative review, 116 items around workforce development aspects achieved excellent scale‐content validity. These included the key function and entry‐level competencies required for success in private practice dietetics, challenges and strategies to support training of current and future private practitioners. Key findings highlight that accrediting training programs should include more private practice placements and business training for dietetics students. Structured post‐graduation support is essential for new graduates entering private practice.ConclusionThis study provides expert agreement on workforce development aspects of private practice dietetics. A united approach to workforce development is needed, involving regulatory bodies, universities and private practitioners for capacity building. This work provides an evidence‐based framework to inform recommendations to support the training and preparation, policy development and strategic workforce capacity building planning for this crucial workforce.Keywords: competency, Delphi study, education, private practice dietetics, workforce development

Introduction

The scope of dietetics practice has evolved beyond its traditional training focus on hospital, food service management and public health, now encompassing private practice as an increasingly pivotal and dynamic domain within the profession [1, 2, 3]. Changes in reimbursement models [4, 5, 6], an increasing number of newly qualified dietitians [7, 8], and a growing drive for autonomy and innovation are collectively fuelling the growth of the private practice within the profession [9, 10, 11]. This, combined with a variety of extrinsic factors, including an aging population [12], increased prevalence of chronic disease [13], and Australians becoming more health‐conscious [14], has seen private practice become a significant employment opportunity for dietitians.

Currently, 30% of the dietetics workforce works in private practice [15], with 30%–50% of graduates in Australia entering this sector at the start of their careers [7, 16, 17]. Simultaneously, many face challenges related to insufficient business skills, limited professional support, and concerns about workforce sustainability [9, 18]. Studies consistently highlight gaps in tertiary training, preparation and mentorship, raising concerns around professional and reputational risks echoed by graduates and experienced practitioners [9, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Australian academic educators have identified structural barriers within accredited training programs, including curriculum crowding, university bureaucracy, and challenges initiating and sustaining private practice placements, which prevent adequate preparation for this employment sector [10].

Effective workforce development is critical to building and sustaining any workforce [23]. Workforce development involves meeting the training needs and supporting transitions into employment, and providing ongoing professional development throughout an individual's career [24]. Workforce development is critical for retention of skilled healthcare professionals, reducing attrition of key resources in the healthcare system [25, 26]. With the historical focus in workforce development in dietetics on hospital and public health, this leaves a gap in understanding the competencies, training and preparation needs and support required for success in private practice. As a result, this raises issues around the quality of care provided by early career practitioners, the risks of underprepared and unsupported practitioners and the resultant reputational damage to the profession [10, 21, 22, 27].

Identifying the pressures and unmet needs within private practice dietetics is a critical first step in establishing the areas where expert consensus is required to guide future workforce development. The Australian national data set for private practice dietetics [28] highlighted challenges reported for private practitioners, including financial insecurity, difficulty attracting new clients, cancellations, and non‐attendance, complicated by the effects of the cost‐of‐living crisis on client retention and profits. The data set highlighted that most practitioners were sole traders (68%) and generated less than AUS$149,999 in revenue (77%). Resultant burnout and professional isolation are also well‐documented challenges to private practice, complicated by a lack of formal workforce planning [15, 29, 30].

The Delphi technique offers a structured approach to capturing expert voices and perspectives to guide policy, training, and support frameworks [31]. It is particularly appropriate in contexts where there is limited consensus on key concepts or practices and involves an iterative process of initiating rounds of questionnaires to gain agreement on key items [32]. While the current evidence base provides valuable experiential insights among private practitioners, graduates and academics, there remains a lack of data‐driven studies that can inform workforce planning, training design and accreditation standards [28, 29]. A Delphi study fills part of this gap by generating expert agreement on empirical evidence. Gaining agreement around workforce development for private practice dietetics is imperative to build shared understanding and identify priorities for action. This, in turn, supports the development of actionable recommendations to guide key policy and decision makers, educators and practitioners, with the ultimate aim of strengthening workforce capacity in private practice dietetics. Accordingly, the aim of this study was to develop agreement amongst a panel of Australian Private Practice Workforce Development Thought Leaders on private practice dietetics workforce development aspects, including the challenges, function, training and preparation needs, support, and solutions. The resulting framework work will inform recommendations to support workforce development in the private practice sector and may provide a benchmark to inform accreditation policy and the alignment with entry‐level curricula.

Methods

This is a Delphi study based on the Delphi Plus method [33], and part of a mixed‐method study in a doctoral program. The researchers adopted a pragmatic theoretical perspective, which prioritises using multiple methods to solve real‐world problems over theory and abstract principles [34]. This Delphi study was conducted by a research team comprised of experienced dietitians and physiotherapists who work as academics or advisors and hold doctoral qualifications (P.C., L.W., R.L.) or are working towards one (J.D.). One author (P.C.) is the lead author of the Delphi Plus published method [33], used in this research. The protocol was approved by [anonymized]. This study was reported using the DELPHISTAR reporting guidelines [35].

The inclusion criteria for Thought Leaders in the study were:

Minimum of 5 years of experience as a private practice dietitian in Australia,AND/OR

Minimum of 5 years of experience as a private practice dietitian in Australia,

AND/OR

Hold an academic leadership or senior role,AND/OR

Hold an academic leadership or senior role,

AND/OR

A member or past member on a dietetics‐related governance body, organisation, or leadership position with a focus on private practice or primary care dietetics,AND/OR

A member or past member on a dietetics‐related governance body, organisation, or leadership position with a focus on private practice or primary care dietetics,

AND/OR

Authorship on a private practice or primary care dietetics‐related peer‐reviewed manuscript.

Authorship on a private practice or primary care dietetics‐related peer‐reviewed manuscript.

The inclusion criteria were designed to ensure that the Thought Leaders had rich insights into private practice for a duration consistent with business viability and with appropriate credentials [33, 36]. Private practice dietetics workforce development Thought Leaders were recruited via purposive sampling [37], using publicly accessible contact information (LinkedIn, Business websites) and complemented by snowball sampling. Snowball sampling assisted in enhanced diversification of experts [38] and reduced researcher bias [39]. Nominal saturation determined the completion of recruitment [36, 40].

The research team developed a 3‐min video recording detailing the study aims and requirements of the study. An email invitation with attached consent form, explanatory statement and the video recording was sent to prospective Thought Leaders. Where initial contact did not elicit a response, a second contact was made. Individual honorariums were not provided to participants. However, all participants went into a draw for the chance to win one of three $100 gift cards in recognition of their participation.

The first‐round questionnaire was developed by the research team in an online survey tool (Qualtrics) and was informed by previous literature [10, 21, 22, 29]. Items were developed by J.D. from the literature that may inform a framework for future recommendations. Core work items were separated into employee and business owner roles to reflect the dual clinical and business nature of private practice dietetics and differing role‐specific responsibilities. Questions were structured on 4‐point Likert scales, and also included free text fields to allow Thought Leaders to add additional items or make relevant comments [41]. Questions around private placement timing and their view on the feasible duration of a placement were added as checkboxes to allow for more than one choice. The first‐round questionnaire also included questions regarding the demographic characteristics of the Thought Leaders and two questions on their views on placement timing and duration. Two researchers, P.C. and L.W., reviewed the draft questionnaire and provided feedback. Duplicated items were removed, and items that required rewording were edited ahead of finalising the questionnaire. A private practitioner then piloted the questionnaire prior to commencing the study to gauge timeframes and adequacy of questions, with only minor changes made. The pilot responses were not included in the final data set. The full first round questionnaire is provided in Supporting Information S1. Items identified in the ‘free text’ field were included in the subsequent round. Items arising from free‐text responses were reviewed and refined by J.D. and P.C. to ensure clarity of wording prior to inclusion in the subsequent round. All participants took part voluntarily in the study, and informed written consent was obtained from all participants. After returning a signed consent form, participants were emailed a unique Qualtrics link to the first‐round questionnaire. Thought Leaders were given 2 weeks to respond to the questionnaire with reminders emailed approximately 1 week, 5 days, and 3 days prior to survey closure. All responses were anonymous throughout the Delphi process.

Data from the completed questionnaires were imported into an Excel (Microsoft Corporation) spreadsheet. Demographic characteristics of Thought Leaders were descriptively analysed. The Likert ratings were analysed using the content validity index (CVI) [41, 42], calculated as per the Delphi Plus method [33]. The probability of change agreement (p c) and a modified kappa statistic (k*) were calculated, strengthening the statistical validity and credibility of the item‐content validity index [33]. Items were rated as having an item‐content validity index of ‘Fair’: an item‐content validity index = 0.40–0.59, ‘Good’: item content validity index = 0.60–0.74 or ‘Excellent’: item‐content validity index > 0.74 [33, 43]. Items that were rated ‘Fair’ were removed after round one as per the Delphi Plus method [33], and those rated ‘Good’ or ‘Excellent’ were included in round two. The perceived number of placement days feasible for private practice placements and the year of training placements should be undertaken was reported as a percentage or participant agreeance.

In the second round questionnaire, all items that rated ‘Good’ or ‘Excellent’ in round one were included, and the results of round one were provided alongside each item in round two as percentage frequency of distribution and participants were asked to consider the retained elements according to the same Likert scale anchors as round one. Each Thought Leader was also asked to consider the group assessment of the item and then reassess the retained items on the same scale. The participants were given 2 weeks to complete the second‐round questionnaire, and reminders were emailed at week one, 5 days and 3 days prior to the questionnaire closure date. The second‐round data was again extracted, and the item‐content validity index for each item was calculated. An item‐content validity index of > 0.78 was regarded as providing strong evidence of excellent content validity [42], and, therefore for inclusion in the framework for recommendations. The number of rounds was defined in advance to be a maximum of two rounds as per the Delphi Plus method [33]. See Figure 1 for a diagrammatic representation of the methodology.

Results

Of the 65 Thought Leaders invited to participate, 46 (71%) agreed to participate. Competing professional and personal obligations were identified as the principal reason for non‐participation. One thought leader withdrew at the commencement of round 2. A total of 45 private practice dietetics workforce development Thought Leaders completed both rounds of an online modified‐Delphi questionnaire over the months of October 2025 and November 2025.

Demographic characteristics of Thought Leaders are described in Table 1. There was an overrepresentation of men (n = 10, 22%) compared to 7% across the entire Australian accredited dietetics population [44]. All states and territories were represented, with the exception of the Australian Capital Territory; with New South Wales, Queensland, and Victoria accounting for the majority of the thought leaders (n = 35, 78%). Thought Leaders reported to have roles as private practice dietitians or managers or those in leadership roles within private practice (n = 27, 60%), academics (n = 12, 27%), allied health educators (n = 1, 2%) and five held dual roles as academics and private practitioners (11%). The majority of Thought Leaders (n = 37, 82%) were APDs, with n = 6 (13%) advanced APDs. Only three Thought Leaders had never worked in private practice, with the majority having 6–19 years of experience in primary care (n = 30, 67%). Thought Leaders had diverse experience in workforce development in private practice including leadership, mentoring, supervision and training of early career dietitians; tertiary education from program direction, curriculum design, implementation and evaluation; placement coordination, facilitation, supervision and convenorship, roles in course advisory committees; leadership, management and advancement of continued professional development platforms; workforce development research and higher degree research supervision; digital health educators and advocacy roles including media engagements.

A consensus of evidence‐based items was systematically established through the application of a modified‐Delphi technique. This included n = 143 items; after two rounds, 116 achieved the a priori definition of agreement. All Thought Leaders (n = 46) completed round one of the modified Delphi. After round one, 99 items had an item‐content validity index of > 0.74 (‘Excellent’); 14 items had an item‐content validity index of 0.60–0.74 (‘Good’). Of those items remaining, 30 items (n = 1 testing propositions, n = 18 core dietetics work as employees, n = 2 core dietetics work as business owners, n = 7 private practice placements, professional support n = 2) had an item‐content validity index of < 0.60 (‘Fair’) and were removed. Twenty additional items proposed by Thought Leaders (testing propositions n = 1; competencies n = 8; tertiary education strategies n = 3; private practice placements n = 5; professional support n = 3) were incorporated into the second‐round questionnaire based on suggestions provided through the ‘free text’ field. Items that did not meet the pre‐specified criteria for agreement and were excluded following iterative review across Delphi rounds typically reflected areas where expert opinion was more variable, context‐dependent, or influenced by differences in practice setting, role, or career stage. The exclusion of these items does not imply a lack of importance; rather, it indicates that consensus was not achieved within this expert panel using the defined thresholds. This process aligns with the purpose of the Delphi methodology, which is to identify areas of shared agreement while distinguishing them from aspects of practice that remain contested or heterogeneous.

Thought Leaders were also asked to rate the number of placement days that would be feasible for a private practice placement. Thought Leaders rated numerous options, however 54% rated 1 day per week over several weeks as a feasible option, followed by 5 days (33%), 10 days (28%), ‘other’ (28%), 25 days (26%), 20 days (19.5%), 100 days (6.5%), ‘not achievable’ (4%). In terms of placement timing, Thought Leaders perceived multiple time points as appropriate including, second year postgraduate (72%), first year postgraduate (48%), fourth year undergraduate (61%), third year undergraduate (46%), with few Thought Leaders agreeing that the first 2 years of an undergraduate program would be appropriate (Year 1 and Year 2, both 9%).

Discussion

This Delphi study gathered the perspectives of 45 Australian experts in private practice dietetics workforce development to build an evidence‐based agreement on the challenges, function, competency needs, training solutions and support mechanisms pertinent for private practice. The resulting 116 evidence‐based items elucidate key propositions from the literature, delineate the core function of private practitioners as both employees and business owners and specify the key competencies beyond foundational clinical skills required for successful graduate‐entry into private practice. Additionally, the study proposes tertiary training strategies, placement challenges, models, solutions and approaches to post‐credentialling support. Collectively, this consensus provides a framework to inform future recommendations aimed at enhancing the preparation, training and capacity building of this workforce.

Private practice dietetics uniquely requires two specific groups of tasks and skill sets, clinical and business [3, 20, 45]. This study provides a consensus on the core tasks required by dietitians as employees and business owners in private practice and provides the key competencies beyond core clinical competencies to support the work of graduate‐entry private practitioners. Dietitians Australia's National Competency Standards [46] outline 55 criteria of observable and measurable actions covering professional practice, expert practice, research practice and collaborative practice. Although business management is recognised within the competency standards, there is a need for clearer guidance or possible curriculum standardisation to guarantee that every program offers thorough learning experiences to achieve competence in business management. Other health professions provide clear precedents for embedding business management within professional training through competency‐based frameworks. In medicine [47], physiotherapy [48], and health service management [49], business‐related capabilities, such as financial management, governance, workforce supervision and service sustainability, are articulated as core professional competencies rather than optional content. These models demonstrate that standardising business management expectations at the level of learning outcomes, while allowing flexibility in delivery, is both feasible and consistent with contemporary health professional education.

With business acumen identified as a key competency in private practice, entrepreneurship training was regarded as an essential inclusion in all tertiary training programs and should be prioritised in dietetics courses, with agreement that entrepreneurial competencies are relevant to all domains of dietetics practice. Insufficient business training is a well‐documented limitation of dietetics training programs and long‐term business sustainability in business generally [1, 7, 11, 16, 18, 19]. It is also a reported issue in other health professions nationally and internationally, including in physiotherapy [50, 51, 52, 53, 54], and dentistry [55, 56]. Fifteen of 22 programs in Australia included entrepreneurship elements in their training programs in our recent curriculum content analysis [21], but the depth and breadth of training is limited to publicly available curriculum information. Given the persistent challenge of curriculum crowding [10], Thought Leaders participating in this study—as well as in prior literature—have identified postgraduate micro‐credentials as a potential solution to enhance workforce readiness in private practice dietetics [28, 57, 58]. Nevertheless, the implementation of such credentials must account for financial accessibility, particularly in the context of the cost‐of‐living crisis and the substantial university debt already borne by graduates, which may limit their ability to pay for costly training post‐graduation [59, 60].

Tertiary education strategies have been proposed in this study to support filling the gaps in the preparation of students for private practice. These included more ‘real world’ exposure with experienced private practitioners presenting their experiences running and working in businesses to students, an inclusion in some existing programs and proposed in other studies [10, 21, 22, 28]. Thought Leaders in this study encouraged the engagement of experts in legal, finance, marketing, business coaching, and entrepreneurship. Universities could leverage their entrepreneurship/business/accounting/marketing schools to support curriculum development or teaching across schools, as seen in pharmacy training [61]. Authentic assessment tasks that simulate real‐world experience were also emphasised as important tertiary training strategies. Business plans, pitches and case studies form part of some dietetics programs, however, inconsistently [21]. These are innovative strategies to competency development in entrepreneurship and bring together key elements of business training, including business planning, finance and marketing strategies [62]. Nevertheless, the acquisition of business acumen through these assessment methods is highly advantageous for the preparation of dietitians entering private practice, irrespective of whether they work as employees or business owners [63], and highlighted in other health professions, including pharmacy [64] and physiotherapy [65]. Such competencies are transferable across all domains of dietetics work, fostering the development of essential entrepreneurial, business and leadership skills required within the healthcare profession [63], and align with the 16 critical capabilities for dietitians in the Reimaging the Future of Nutrition and Dietetics: Towards 2030 project [66].

Private practice placements are an opportunity for students to gain exposure and experience in this sector pre‐credentialling, however, they are limited in university dietetics programs in Australia [18, 21]. Thought Leaders in this study unanimously agreed that private practice placements should be included in all accredited dietetics programs and not merely as an elective, and are an opportunity for students to gain work experience in business management, whilst aligning with other dietetics placements in their ability for students to meet the required competencies for graduate‐entry practice [46]. However, consensus was not reached on making private practice placements a mandatory requirement. They provided agreement on multiple models and solutions to support private practice placement integration into programs; however, health insurance limitations and inadequate financial support are identified challenges that remain and are well recognised in the literature [10, 22, 67]. Another complication of the lack of exposure to private practice in tertiary programs is the reported challenge by business owners to attract quality graduates to private practice positions and the subsequent default graduate‐entry into the sector due to the competitive nature of hospital jobs [15], with 48.4% of graduates still desiring this sector over private practice (33.2%) [7]. Ongoing evaluation of accreditation standards and curriculum design is essential to ensure alignment with the private practice sector, and it is imperative that tertiary educators, practitioners, and governing bodies work together to establish innovative placement experiences to best fit this dynamic work environment. In Australia's Primary Heath Health Care 10 Year Plan 2022–2032 [68], a scaling up of allied health placements in rural, regional and remote areas is a medium‐term action aimed to improve access to primary healthcare in these areas. An increased capacity for primary care placements and removal of barriers to supervision in primary care placements were recommendations in the Scope of Practice Review [67], including a review of Medicare Benefits Schedule funding rules that act as a barrier to training students, and a recommendation that accreditation boards review and amend existing accreditation standards to achieve this. Health insurance systems were deemed prohibitive to private practice placements in this study, which is a significant hurdle to overcome without governments actioning change to funding and requirements for these schemes. However, more advocacy is needed by the professional body to ensure change is timely. Varying views on client burden and private practice placements have been raised in physiotherapy [69, 70] and dietetics literature [22], however, this item did not achieve consensus agreement as a limiting factor to students undertaking private practice placements in this study.

The Australian provisional mentoring model was previously identified as a promising solution to support; however, its discontinuation means that new graduates are no longer required to complete 12 months of mentoring with a fully qualified Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) [71]. Unsurprisingly, Thought Leaders were concerned about the termination of the provisional APD program for graduates entering private practice, and reiterated the ongoing call for better support [3, 10, 18, 20, 22, 45]. International studies have echoed these challenges with United States (US) dietitians reporting difficulties in sustaining successful entrepreneurial ventures due to a lack of business knowledge and mentorship [11]. Irish dietetics graduates called for mentoring to overcome self‐doubt and low confidence across all work areas [30]. Similar challenges have been observed by Canadian dietitians working in primary care [72].

A supervision model (mandated for graduates), more structured mentoring or an internship year were offered as solutions to support. This type of preceptorship is commonplace in other therapeutic areas like psychology and has been proposed by primary care dietetic Thought Leaders previously [20]. In New Zealand, mandatory supervision exists for dietitians in their first year of practice [73]. However, affordability for new graduates must be a key factor with any implementation of a formal program, as raised earlier and highlighted by Thought Leaders in this study. However, Thought Leaders in this study did not form a consensus on the concept that supervision should be free, but also that a fee would need to be attached to supervision, implying that it is up to the supervisor as to what type of supervision model they offer. A recent systematic review [74] found that there is a lack of published research on supervision in dietetics, but emphasised that supervision should be a routine aspect of dietitians' professional practice, which aligns somewhat with the agreement in this study that private practice is core dietetics work, and therefore post‐graduate support should align with other areas of dietetic practice. However, the Thought Leaders did not reach consensus on whether a mandatory supervision model should be applied uniformly across all work domains, rather than being specific to any single area of practice. Given that 30%–50% of dietetics graduates are moving into the private practice sector post‐credentialing [7, 16], a focus on adequacy of training and support for graduates entering this sector, is time‐critical, as ignoring or delaying action and having underprepared and unsupported health practitioners is a risk to the client, health practitioner and the profession [56]. The removal of the provisional APD program is an opportunity to embed current and future‐focused support for transition to employment for all graduates, tailored to the employment sectors of current and future graduates.

A systematic and strategic approach to workforce development of the private practice dietetics workforce must be prioritised, including the collective work of policy makers, governing bodies, training centres, and private practitioners, to ensure the capacity of this crucial workforce [67]. Further work is needed to develop recommendations to support tertiary training centres to implement some of the strategies outlined in this study and enable the relevant training and preparation of future private practice dietitians, whilst navigating some of the challenges to preparation currently experienced. Appropriate support for entry‐level private practitioners is needed to avoid unnecessary attrition of the workforce and loss of vital resources to support the health outcomes of the community [25, 75]. Further studies should evaluate non‐consensus items, comparative studies across practice domains, the current experiences of early career practitioners and models of supervision and mentoring.

A notable strength of this research lies in its implementation of the validated Delphi Plus methodology [33], which enhances the reliability and credibility of the agreement process. The recruitment of a large and diverse participant cohort, combined with an exceptionally high response rate of 98% completing both rounds of review, further strengthens the external validity of the findings. The inclusion of Thought Leaders across all Australian states and territories (except ACT), representing a broad spectrum of professional experience and adds depth to the richness of the data. A limitation of this study is that it may not fully capture the perspectives of all relevant policymakers, and early‐career private practitioners were not included in the inclusion criteria. Further consultation with these stakeholders is recommended to ensure that subsequent recommendations are contextually appropriate and can be effectively implemented within the private practice setting. Another limitation is the geographical focus on sector‐specific insights. The Australian focus may affect the generalisability of its findings to international settings, whilst the specificity of the findings to private practice dietetics may not be directly applicable to all aspects of the dietetics profession.

Conclusions

The present study has systematically identified and validated the principal challenges, preparation and training needs, strategies, and support mechanisms essential for effective workforce development in private practice dietetics. Through the agreement of Thought Leaders across Australia, a robust evidence base has been established, highlighting the dual need for clinical and business acumen in private practice and the importance of integrating entrepreneurship training, authentic curriculum and assessments, and the necessity of private practice placements within tertiary education programs, to equip graduates for the demands of this work domain. Furthermore, the study emphasises the need for structured transition to work support to ensure sustainable career pathways and mitigate professional risks. Collectively, these findings provide a framework for the development of recommendations for accreditation standards, curriculum design and implementation, professional support and strategic workforce planning to strengthen the capacity and resilience of the Australian private practice dietetics workforce.
