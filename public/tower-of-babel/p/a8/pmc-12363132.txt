The association between waist circumference, weight-adjusted waist index, waist-to-height ratio and waist divided by height0.5 and the prevalence of cardiovascular diseases in patients with diabetes
Authors: Jinhao Liao, Linjie Wang, Lian Duan, Fengying Gong, Huijuan Zhu, Hui Pan, Hongbo Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe waist circumference (WC), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), waist divided by height0.5(WHT.5R), and weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) are four anthropometric indices evaluating obesity, but the association with cardiovascular diseases in diabetic population remain unclear.MethodsWe extracted data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES1999-2016),and 7000 patients with diabetes were eligible. Weighted logistic regression and weighted restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to explore the association between WC, WWI, WHtR, WTH.5R and the likelihood of cardiovascular disease (CVD), congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary artery disease (CAD), and stroke. Stratified analyses and interaction tests were further conducted.ResultsThe WHtR index demonstrated a statistically significant positive linear relationship with the likelihoodof CVD [Quartile 4, full adjusted: OR 1.84(1.38,2.47)], CHF [Quartile 4, full adjusted: OR 2.35(1.51,3.66)], CAD [Quartile 4, full adjusted: OR 1.43(1.06,1.94)] and stroke [Quartile 4, full adjusted: OR 1.59(1.01,2.51)]. Moreover, the WC and WHT.5R were positively associated withCVD [Quartile 4, full adjusted: WC OR 1.95(2.44,2.65); WHT.5R OR 2.01(1.53,2.63)], CHF [Quartile 4, full adjusted: WC OR 2.83(1.79,4.47); WHT.5R OR 2.33(1.52,3.58)], and CAD [Quartile 4, full adjusted: WC OR 1.64(1.22,2.21); WHT.5R OR 1.43(1.06,1.94)], while WWI exhibited a similar association with the prevalence ratesof CVD [Quartile 4, full adjusted: OR 1.76(1.29,2.42)] and CAD [Quartile 4, full adjusted: OR 1.51(1.04,2.19)]. RCS analyses showed that all indices had a linear relationship with the probability of CVD, CHF, CAD, and stroke.ConclusionsThis study highlighted the application prospect of these four indicators in predicting cardiovascular disease ability in patients with diabetes, especially the WHtR index.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24068-2.Keywords: Waist circumference, Waist-to-height ratio, Waist divided by height0.5, Weight-adjusted-waist index, Cardiovascular disease, Congestive heart failure, Coronary artery disease, Stroke, Diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic, prevalent disease characterized by significant elevation of blood glucose concentration and insulin resistance associated with impaired pancreatic β cell structure and function [1]. Moreover, its epidemic has posed a substantial threat to global public health. There were 529 million patients diagnosed with diabetes with a global prevalence rate of 6.1% in 2021, and over 1.31 billion people are estimated to develop diabetes, with 89 of 204 countries and regions having an age-standard prevalence exceeding 10% by 2050 [2, 3]. In middle-aged individuals, diabetes reduces life expectancy by 4–10 years and is a significant risk factor for cancer, kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease [4].

A collection of heart and blood vessel-related conditions known as cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) includes coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, cardiomyopathy, and stroke [5]. The primary cause of death for patients with type 2 diabetes is cardiovascular disease [6]. At the same time, ischemic heart disease and stroke, its complications or comorbidities, ranked as the first and second leading cause of death, respectively [7]. Multitudinous modifiable risk factors contributed to this condition, including elevated glucose concentration, high blood pressure, inappropriate diet, tobacco smoking, alcohol use, and kidney dysfunction [8–10]. Moreover, a growing number of non-invasive indices have been identified as associated with cardiovascular diseases [11–14]. For instance, both hepatic steatosis index and body roundness index have been found to show significant associations with hypertensive patients suffering from obstructive sleep apnea [11, 12].

It is a well-known fact that patients with diabetes often have two or more risk factors for CVD, such as obesity and dyslipidemia [15]. Many studies have delved into the correlation between obesity indicators and the prevalence as well as mortality. Body mass index (BMI) is the classical indicator for obesity. Since BMI displayed a U-shaped or J-shaped association with the incidence of adverse events and mortality of cardiovascular diseases, it has raised controversy in the academic community with the famous “obesity paradox.” [16–18] The measurement indices of visceral adiposity, such as waist circumference (WC), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), waist divided by height0.5(WHT.5R), and weight-adjusted-waist index (WWI), showed an association with cardiovascular diseases independent of BMI and all the four indices exhibited good predictive power of cardiometabolic multimorbidity [13, 14, 19, 20].

While many studies have evaluated the anthropometric indices in various populations, very few have further examined the relationship between WC, WHtR, WHT.5R, and WWI with the risk of cardiovascular diseases in individuals with diabetes. Therefore, we aimed to conduct a cross-sectional study and provide more effective evidence for preventing and detecting CVD in individuals with diabetes.

Materials and methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is an ongoing, two-cycled, and nationally representative study employing a complex, multistage, probability sampling design developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). The survey’s primary objective is to evaluate the nutrition and health status. Every respondent was invited to participate in a personal interview at their home and physical examination at mobile examination centers (MECs), ensuring comprehensive data collection. The entire program will be implemented by the Declaration of Helsinki. The final outcoming data mainly encompassed demographic, dietary, laboratory, and questionnaire data. All data involved in the present study was extracted from the NHANES database(https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/) [21]. The program was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and informed consent was obtained from every respondent. Therefore, our study did not require further informed consent and ethical review.

We selected nine NHAES cycles from 1999 to 2016 and acquired data for the present study. Patients with diabetes were enrolled according to the following criteria:1) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level ≥ 126 mg/dL; 2) blood HbA1c concentration ≥ 6.5%; 3) 2 h oral glucose tolerance test(OGTT) ≥ 200 mg/dL; 4) self-reported diabetes through a “yes” response to the questions “Doctor told you have diabetes?” or “Taking insulin now?” or “Take diabetic pills to lower blood sugar?”. For further selection, we excluded those who (1) were without information about cardiovascular diseases, (2) missed data on anthropometric indices such as waist circumference, height, and weight, (3) were without sample weight. Ultimately, 6998 eligible participants were enrolled in the current study (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Definition of WC, WWI, WHtR and WHT.5R

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) health professionals conducted measurements for waist circumference (WC in centimeters), height (Ht in centimeters), and weight (Wt in kilograms) in the designated body measurement examination areas within the mobile examination centers. The following formulas can calculate the remaining three anthropometric indices: (1) Weight-adjusted waist index(WWI, cm/√kg) = WC(cm)/Wt(kg)0.5; (2) Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) = WC(cm)/Ht(cm); (3) Waist divided by height0.5(WHT.5R,) = WC(cm)/Ht(cm)0.5 [22, 23].

Assessment of outcomes

The cardiovascular outcomes of our study included CVD, CHF, CAD, and stroke. The respondents were classified into the CHF group by self-reported “yes” to the question “Ever told had congestive heart failure?”. CAD was defined as having coronary heart disease (CHD), angina pectoris, or heart attack confirmed by the questions “Ever told you had coronary heart disease?”, “Ever told you had angina/angina pectoris?” and “Ever told you had a heart attack?”. Similarly, stroke-related information was acquired through a “yes” confirmation of the question “Ever told you had a stroke?”. Patients were considered to have CVD if they suffered from any of the diseases mentioned above [24].

Baseline information and covariates

We extracted data from the NHANES person interview questionnaires encompassing: (1) demographic information such as age, gender, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, family income and etc.; (2) medical conditions; (3) lifestyles questionnaire, including alcohol use, smoking status and others; (4) laboratory sample data, including fasting blood glucose and insulin concentration, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), HbA1c, serum creatinine, urine albumin, and urine creatinine. Random urine albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR) was calculated by dividing urine albumin divided by urine creatinine. Alcohol use status was categorized into never drinking, 1–5 drinks/day, > 5–10 drinks/day, > 10 drinks/day. Smoking status was classified as never smoking (smoked less than 100 cigarettes in his/her entire life), former smoking (smoked more than 100 cigarettes but quit before the survey), and current smoking (smoked more than 100 cigarettes and not quit during the survey). BMI was calculated by weight dividing by the square of height. Patients were diagnosed with hypertension when they had a systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg or took medicines for arterial hypertension [25]. Chronic kidney dysfunction (CKD) was defined by: (1) previous medical history of CKD (answered “yes” to the question “Ever told you have a weak kidney?”); (2) eGFR<60 ml/min/1.73m2; (3) urinary albumin to creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g. Cancer condition was confirmed by the question, “Ever told you had cancer or malignancy?”.

Statistical analysis

Patients with diabetes were categorized based on the presence of CVD, CHF, CAD, or stroke. Within the intricate stratified sampling framework of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), suitable sample weights were ascertained for application during the analytical phase. A chart delineating the baseline characteristics of participants segregated them into groups with and without CVD. Continuous variables were depicted as mean and standard deviation (SD), with the differences between the groups tested by weighted Student’s t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as percentages, and weighted chi-square tests were employed to detect the differences. Weighted logistic regression models were conducted to explore the association between WC, WWI, WHtR, WHT.5R with the risk of developing CVD, CHF, CAD, and stroke. Four regression models were constructed, each progressively accounting for an array of confounding variables. Model 1 adjusted for no covariates. Model 2 included adjustments for age, gender, and race/ethnicity. Model 3 expanded the adjustments for age, gender, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, family income to poverty ratio, smoking status, alcohol use, and serum uric acid. Model 4 further refined the adjustment for serum creatinine, eGFR, UACR, CKD, hypertension, and cancer. To visually demonstrate the relationship between these four anthropometric indicators and cardiovascular risk and to detect potential non-linear relationships, a weighted restricted cubic spline analysis was applied subsequent to covariate adjustment in Model 4. Subgroup and sensitive analyses were performed to explore the correlation of the four indices with cardiovascular diseases in different populations, clustered or stratified by gender, race/ethnicity, PIR, alcohol use, smoking status, hypertension, CKD, and cancer condition. All data analysis processes were performed by R software (version 4.3.2).

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

According to the stratification by the condition of CVD with corresponding sample weights, the baseline characteristics of demographic, examination, blood samples, and urine sample data from 7000 patients with diabetes were demonstrated in Table 1. The average age of eligible patients was 59 years. Compared with the non-CVD group, patients with CVD were more likely to be male, older, non-Hispanic whites, and former smokers, and only slightly over 40% received college education. The CVD group had worse economic conditions and lived without a spouse due to widowhood, divorce, and separation. Regarding anthropometric indices, they tended to have wider waist circumference, higher WWI, WHtR, and WHT.5R (p<0.05). Meanwhile, they had higher hypertension and cancerprevalence rate, and worse kidney function. However, our study showed that patients with CVD exhibits lower serum TG and LDL levels (p<0.05).

Discussion

This study investigated the correlation between four key anthropometric indices, namely WC, WWI, WHtR, WHT.5R, and the likelihood of CVD, CHF, CAD, and stroke among individuals with diabetes, using weighted logistic regression. Our findings, which directly relate to clinical practice, indicated a pronounced elevation for the presence of CVD and CAD in patients with the highest quartile of these four indicators compared to those with the lowest quartile. Additionally, WC, WHtR, and WHT.5R were positively associated with CHF prevalence, and WHtR alone showed a significant correlation with stroke. Subsequent RCS analyses revealed a generally linear relationship between these indicators andCVD. As the values of these anthropometric measures increased, so did the propensity for disease development.

BMI is a widely used obesity evaluation index in clinical practice, but its construction is based on height and weight and cannot reflect fat distribution [26]. The WC, WWI, WHtR, and WHT.5R were utilized as adiposity measures complementary to BMI. And the relationship between these indices and the incidence of cardiovascular diseases and related adverse events have been the subject of extensive research. In our study, WC and WHT.5R demonstrated a significant positive linear correlation with the likelihoodof CVD, CHF, and CAD. Among these indices, WHT.5R is an emerging metric that has received comparatively less attention in the literature. Our research, with a clear aim to bridge this gap in knowledge regarding WHT.5R, contributes to a more nuanced understanding of its implications in cardiovascular health.

WC, a prevalent obesity metric often used alongside BMI, has been the subject of numerous studies. A Korean study for instance, found that those with high WC faced an elevated risk of experiencing composite outcomes such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and all-cause mortality, contrasting with the highest BMI category, which showed a lower prevalence. This suggested that WC might serve as a more potent predictive marker [27]. Czernichow et al. also reported that WC, rather than BMI, was associated with an increased probability of CVD mortality [28]. Our research, in line with this consensus, demonstrating a positive linear relationship between WC and CVD presence. However, a Swedish study involving 22,882 patients post their first myocardial infarction found that a wider WC was independently linked to recurrent atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with a U-shaped association observed in women [29]. The divergent outcomes may be partially ascribed to the varying definitions of outcome indicators and the distinct patient demographics involved in these studies.

The weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) is an innovative obesity metric developed by Park et al. which has been demonstrated robust predictive capabilities for CVD morbidity and mortality along with a positive association [14, 30, 31]. Prior studies have reported J-shaped associations between weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) and cardiovascular risk in hypertensive OSA patients, and a positive association between WWI and stroke risk in elderly hypertensives [32, 33]. In our study, an increase in WWI was significantly correlated with the presence of CVD and CAD, yet it did not show a significant relationship with CHF and stroke. Ye et al., using the NHANES database, identified a significant positive association between WWI and stroke [34]. which stands in contrast to our findings. In fact, aside from the WHtR, the other three indices did not yield consistent results in both primary and subgroup analyses (Supplementary Fig. 12–15), potentially due to variations in the study population. The reasons for these discrepancies are not entirely clear and may require further investigation for a more comprehensive understanding.

The WHtR emerged as the markedly effective indicator among the four anthropometric measures in our study, showing a significant positive linear association with all evaluated cardiovascular outcomes. A systemic review underscored the superior discriminatory power of WHtR compared to WC and BMI in identifying cardiometabolic risk factors [35]. Butt et al. noted that patient with heart failure in the highest quintile faced an elevated prevalenceof primary outcomes and hospitalization [36]. Ke et al. illustrated that WHtR had a distinct positive association with the prevalence of cardio-cerebrovascular events, with a stronger correlation than both WC and the waist-to-hip ratio in T2DM [37]. WC and WHtR are recognized as indices for measuring abdominal obesity, a condition that is prone to accumulating substantial visceral fat and is associated with higher concentrations of small dense low-density lipoprotein (LDL) in the blood stream. These small dense LDL particles possess a great capacity for penetrating arterial walls and are more susceptible to oxidation, which may consequently contribute to the development of atherosclerosis [38–40]. Consequently, it is not surprising that some researchers reported WC and WHtR exhibit a more robust predictive capacity than BMI for cardiovascular risks.

Strengths and limitations

There are some strengths of our study. Firstly, it pioneers the evaluation of the relationship between four anthropometric indicators and the risk of cardiovascular diseases among diabetic patients across all age groups in U.S. Furthermore, the study provides compelling evidence supporting WC and WHtR as predictive markers for the likelihood of cardiovascular disease, echoing the commendable performance of WHtR as previously documented in the literature. Secondly, by leveraging data from the NHANES database, the study ensures a substantial sample size that bolsters the reliability of the findings. Thirdly, the NHANES, being nationally representative of the US population, lends our results a broad generalizability, further enhanced by applying appropriate sample weights.

However, our study also has some limitations. From a methodological standpoint, it is a cross-sectional observational study, which may be subject to self-report and recall biases inherent in interview sessions. Additionally, while the study accounts for many covariates and confounding factors, unconsidered variables could influence the robustness of the research outcomes. Finally, as this study was based on the American population, future research is warranted to ascertain the applicability of these findings to other global demographics.

Conclusions

The study has shed light on the correlation between four obesity-related anthropometric indices and the risk of cardiovascular diseases in diabetic patients. The WHtR index demonstrated a significant positive linear association with the probability of CVD, CHF, CAD, and stroke. The WC and WHT.5R also showed positive associations with the likelihood of CVD, CHF, and CAD, while WWI indicated a similar correlation with the prevalence of CVD and CAD. Our study highlighted the promising potential of these four indicators in the predictive assessment of the prevalence of cardiovascular disease in diabetic patients, with a particular emphasis on the WHtR index. Future research is needed to verify the reliability of the results.
