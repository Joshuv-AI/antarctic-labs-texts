Investigation of the Crystallization Region for High-Quality GaN Growth in Na-Flux Method
Authors: Jineng Yao, Zhitao Yang, Mingbin Zhou, Xiujin Wang, Song Yang, Qixin Wan, Xia Yang, Juanli Zhao, Zhenrong Li, Zhihua Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Na-flux method is recognized as a highly promising technique for growing large-size, high-quality gallium nitride (GaN) single crystals due to its relatively mild growth temperatures and pressures. However, precisely identifying and effectively regulating the critical crystallization regimes, which dictate crystal quality, remains a fundamental challenge for scalable and stable growth. This study employs a synergistic multiscale approach integrating macroscopic numerical analysis, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and reported experimental data-to systematically investigate the crystallization kinetics of GaN within 973–1173 K and 1–7 MPa. By establishing a comprehensive thermodynamic model based on dynamic supersaturation, a precise temperature–pressure (T-P) phase diagram was constructed, successfully delineating the high-quality epitaxial growth regions. The accuracy of these optimal windows was rigorously verified through MD simulations and literature-based experimental growth outcomes. This multiscale framework elucidates the synergistic regulation mechanisms of T and P from both macroscopic and atomistic perspectives, providing robust theoretical guidance for optimizing process parameters in the growth of high-quality GaN crystals.

Introduction

Gallium nitride (GaN) is a cornerstone material in the field of compound semiconductors. , With its remarkable properties, including high electron mobility (1300 cm2/(V·s)), high electron saturation velocity (2.5 × 107 cm/s), excellent thermal conductivity (1.3 W/(cm·K)), and a wide bandgap (3.4 eV) that ensures superior resistance to high temperatures and high breakdown fields (3.3 MV/cm), GaN has emerged as a “star technology” in applications such as power delivery fast charging, electric vehicles, and solar photovoltaics. − These exceptional characteristics position GaN as a promising alternative to silicon-based semiconductors. As a result, it is increasingly regarded as one of the most promising technological directions in the field of power electronics. , Currently, commercial GaN crystals are predominantly grown using the Hydride Vapor Phase Epitaxy (HVPE) method. , However, the significant lattice and thermal mismatch between the heteroepitaxial substrate and GaN often leads to high density dislocations in the GaN epitaxial layer, with densities typically ranging from 108 to 1010 cm–2. Such high defect concentrations significantly degrade the internal quantum efficiency and long-term reliability of the resulting devices. −

As a result, this severely impacts the performance and reliability of GaN-based devices. Homogeneous GaN single-crystal substrates offer the most promising solution to address the issue of high defect density. The Na (sodium) flux method is a representative technique for growing homogeneous GaN crystals. This method benefits from relatively mild growth conditions (typically 1023–1133 K and 3–5 MPa) and enhanced cost-effectiveness, while also ensuring high-quality crystal formation characterized by low dislocation densities (104–106 cm–2) under near–thermodynamic equilibrium conditions. Unlike gas-phase epitaxial techniques such as MOCVD, MBE, or HVPE, these techniques face challenges of high strain and defect densities (108–1010 cm–2) due to heteroepitaxial constraints, while the liquid-phase Na-flux environment facilitates a more stable growth front and significantly improves crystal perfection. −

However, practical studies have revealed that the Na-flux method still presents several challenges during the growth process. − In particular, although the approximate temperature and pressure range is known, the specific temperature–pressure (T-P) combinations required to maintain stable supersaturation and achieve high-quality GaN growth have not yet been quantitatively clarified. When high temperature and pressure conditions are employed, spontaneous nucleation of polycrystals often occurs on the crucible walls. − The formation of polycrystals severely hampers nitrogen dissociation, which in turn inhibits the stable, long-term growth of GaN crystals. On the other hand, reducing the temperature and pressure can prevent successful epitaxial growth. − In early studies, Aoki et al. conducted experiments on GaN crystal growth under varying temperature and pressure conditions. They delineated the boundaries for GaN crystal formation region and examined the effects of temperature and pressure on crystal morphology evolution. Kawamura et al. developed a temperature-dependent metastable for crystal growth by quantitatively evaluating the nitrogen concentration and supersaturation in Ga–Na melts, thereby defining the kinetic boundaries for inhibiting spontaneous polycrystalline nucleation. These preliminary studies provide an important reference for GaN crystal growth. Based on the quantitative evaluation of nitrogen solubility and supersaturation, the optimal temperature–pressure (T-P) window for stable GaN epitaxial growth is determined to be 1073–1133 K and 3–5 MPa. Within this range, the supersaturation remains inside the metastable region, effectively suppressing spontaneous polycrystalline nucleation while maintaining sufficient driving force for single-crystal growth. Therefore, defining this quantitative T-P window is essential for achieving high-quality GaN epitaxy. To this end, the present study aims to delineate the optimal processing regime, defined as the T-P window in which the dissolved nitrogen concentration in the Ga–Na melt is sufficient to sustain continuous single-crystal growth while remaining below the critical threshold for spontaneous polycrystalline nucleation.

This study establishes a multiscale approach coupling macroscopic finite element analysis (FEA) with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. By cross-validating our simulation results with reported experimental data, we have precisely reconstructed the T-P phase diagram, thereby delineating the critical thermodynamic windows for synthesizing high-quality GaN crystals via the Na-flux method. These findings not only deepen the fundamental understanding of GaN crystallization kinetics but also hold promise for addressing critical technical bottlenecks in the growth of large-size, low-defect single crystals-such as parasitic polycrystalline nucleation and the precise control of growth conditions. By providing quantitative theoretical guidance on optimal T-P parameters, this work lays a robust foundation for suppressing spontaneous nucleation, stabilizing epitaxial growth, and ultimately facilitating the growth of high-quality GaN crystals.

Experimental Procedures and Computational Methods

GaN crystal growth is carried out using the Na-flux liquid phase epitaxy (LPE) method. The commercial GaN single crystals of size 10 × 10.5 mm2 are used as seeds. Starting materials, including metal Ga (99.9999%), Na (99.5%), and C (99.85%), are mixed in a molar ratio of Ga: Na: C = 27:73:0.5 (mol %) and placed into a boron nitride (BN) crucible, ensuring uniform mixing. The addition of carbon (C) is intended to suppress the formation of polycrystalline GaN on the surface of the Ga–Na solution. , The crucible is then rapidly transferred to a high-pressure, high-temperature reaction vessel, which is placed into an electric furnace.

During crystal growth, temperature induce thermal convection in the solution, while concentration gradients lead to diffusion, both of which influence the transport of nitrogen ions. Furthermore, material transport affects the temperature distribution. Steady-state numerical analysis is conducted on the heat transfer, thermal convection, and nitrogen diffusion interactions in the solution. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are widely employed in nanoscale research, owing to their capability to capture atomic-scale behavioral evolution within well-defined spatial and temporal regimes. For the simulations in this work, we employ the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS). The MD simulations are performed within the NPT ensemble, , maintaining a constant number of atoms, pressure, and temperature. Periodic boundary conditions (PBC) are applied in all three spatial directions (x, y, z) to mitigate finite-size effects and more accurately represent the bulk material behavior. The simulation cell for solution growth is set with dimensions of 1.9 × 1.6 × 10.0 nm3. The crystalline region consists of 207 Ga atoms and 207 N atoms, while the melt region is composed of 451 Ga atoms. In the Na-flux method for GaN growth, the primary role of sodium (Na) is to dissociate the nitrogen–nitrogen triple bond in N2 molecules. , As illustrated in Figure , to streamline the computational model, the nitrogen (N) source is represented by 100 discrete nitrogen atoms introduced directly into the gas phase. This simplification allows the simulation to focus specifically on the diffusion behavior of N within the melt and the subsequent growth kinetics. Consequently, the model facilitates a systematic investigation into the crystalline growth process under varying temperature and pressure conditions. Under our simulation conditions, the N concentration is substantially higher than that in practical scenarios. However, simulating solution growth under realistic nitrogen concentrations is infeasible, as this would require an excessively large simulation cell. , The number of atoms in the simulation cell is thus determined based on the constraints of our computational environment.

Schematic atomistic representation of the Na-flux growth model for GaN. Ga and N atoms are shown in green and blue, respectively.

Prior to the growth simulation, the solid–liquid interface model is equilibrated at 300 K to ensure system stability and improve interfacial coupling. The system is subsequently heated to the target temperature to facilitate crystal growth under high-temperature conditions. The growth process is monitored using a time step of 0.2 fs. Interatomic interactions in the GaN system are described by the Stillinger-Weber (S–W) potential, being critical for capturing the microscopic mechanisms governing crystal growth. −

Results and Discussion

The Na flux method relies on solution supersaturation as the dominant factor in crystallization processes, where the nucleation probability, growth rate, and ultimate crystal habit are all determined by the magnitude of supersaturation achieved in the system. As depicted in Figure , the critical nitrogen supersaturation required for spontaneous GaN nucleation in the Ga–Na melt is determined as a function of temperature and pressure using COMSOL simulations. These results show that increasing the temperature significantly lowers the minimum supersaturation needed for nucleation, a trend that aligns well with classical nucleation theory (CNT). According to CNT, enhanced thermal motion at elevated temperatures reduces both the critical nucleation work and the critical nucleus size, thereby promoting the formation of supercritical nuclei. This theoretical framework provides a consistent explanation for the simulation results presented in Figure . On the contrary, increasing the partial pressure of nitrogen enhances the concentration of dissolved nitrogen in the melt, which in turn increases the degree of supersaturation. The degree of supersaturation is a key parameter in controlling the nucleation rate and growth mode. This trend is consistent with the experimental observations reported by Yamane et al., who investigated the growth behavior of GaN in a Ga–Na melt under various temperature and pressure conditions. These results indicate that, at relatively low temperatures (973–1073 K) and elevated nitrogen pressures (approximately 5 MPa), the crystals predominantly exhibited a platelet morphology, reflecting a significant increase in spontaneous nucleation. In contrast, when the temperature was increased to around 1123 K under the same pressure, the growth tended to yield larger prismatic crystals, suggesting a shift in the growth mode toward anisotropic crystal facet growth dominated by pre-existing nuclei rather than the formation of new nuclei. Therefore, the combined regulation of temperature and pressure is crucial for striking a balance between nucleation rate and crystal quality.

Critical supersaturation for GaN nucleation dependent on temperature and pressure.

Moreover, the results presented in Figure show that the overall variation in the supersaturation required for GaN epitaxial growth by the Na-flux method under different temperature and pressure conditions closely parallels the trend observed for the spontaneous nucleation of GaN crystals. Under constant pressure, all curves exhibit a monotonic decrease in supersaturation with increasing temperature. This behavior originates from the critical role of Na at the gas–liquid interface: at elevated temperatures, Na effectively ionizes N2 molecules, significantly enhancing the equilibrium solubility of nitrogen in the Ga–Na melt. Since supersaturation is defined as the difference between the actual solute concentration and the equilibrium solubility, the thermodynamic driving force (supersaturation) required to sustain GaN epitaxial growth decreases continuously as temperature rises and solubility increases, approaching the thermodynamic equilibrium limit in the high-temperature range of 1123–1173 K. Concurrently, elevated temperatures strengthen nitrogen diffusion and interfacial atomic mobility, further reducing the dependence on high supersaturation from a kinetic perspective. At a constant temperature, supersaturation increases markedly with rising nitrogen pressure, a trend that strictly follows Sieverts’ law. The solubility of nitrogen in the Ga–Na solution is proportional to the square root of the nitrogen partial pressure; increasing pressure promotes N2 dissolution, thereby elevating the equilibrium solubility of nitrogen in the melt. To overcome the interfacial energy barrier and drive epitaxial growth, the system requires a higher absolute solute concentration, leading to a corresponding increase in the supersaturation threshold to compensate for the enhanced thermodynamic stability of dissolved nitrogen under high pressure.

The red curve delineates the spontaneous nucleation threshold: exceeding this threshold results in uncontrolled nucleation, which leads to polycrystalline growth. The blue curve represents the minimum supersaturation required for epitaxial growth. The region bounded by these two curves defines the high-quality growth window, wherein epitaxial growth can proceed smoothly, while polycrystalline nucleation is effectively suppressed. It is important to note that these boundaries do not strictly correspond to traditional saturation or spontaneous nucleation curves. Rather, they are semiempirical results derived from COMSOL simulations and constrained by experimental data, and therefore do have limitations. Nevertheless, this phase diagram region provides practical and engineering insights that directly guide actual crystal growth experiments, rather than serving merely as a universal boundary condition for spontaneous nucleation or epitaxial growth.

This range closely resembles the metastable region for GaN crystal growth investigated by Kawamura et al., mapping the metastable region by calculating the change in nitrogen solubility under various temperature and pressure conditions. Their finding that the metastable region expands with increasing growth temperature is consistent with our calculations. Concurrently, we delineate the high-quality crystallization zone based on the variation in supersaturation across different temperature and pressure values, which offers a more practical framework for adjusting the required temperature and pressure settings in experimental procedures.

Experimental data points reported in various literature sources are overlaid on the diagram to validate the phase diagram. The data (such as the red star points from ref ) is concentrated within the intermediate region between the two curves, which is essentially consistent with the reported results for Na-flux GaN crystal growth under the aforementioned conditions. The yellow data point at 1023 K, 5 MPa (ref ), is located above the spontaneous nucleation curve, indicating a high propensity for polycrystal formation under these conditions. This is consistent with the GaN crystal morphology published in the corresponding literature. At this temperature, Zhou et al. investigated GaN crystal growth at 7 MPa (pink data point), and their study demonstrated the possibility of growing hexagonal pyramidal GaN crystals, although the crystal surfaces were still covered by polycrystals. In the high-temperature regime, Tandryo et al. comparatively studied the effects of 1143 and 1173 K on crystal growth at 4 MPa (light blue data points). Their findings revealed that at 1173 K and 4 MPa, no polycrystal formation is observed on the GaN crystals. Concurrently, Wu et al. investigated the influence of varying temperatures 973 to 1123 K on the GaN crystal growth morphology at 4 MPa (red triangle data points), discovering that as the temperature increased, the crystal size gradually enlarged, and the content of polycrystals on the crystal surface progressively decreased. Furthermore, Wang et al. also investigated GaN crystal growth under different temperature and pressure conditions, concluding that the quality of GaN crystals exhibits a positive correlation with temperature and a negative correlation with pressure. These collective research findings substantially validate the accuracy of our calculated results.

Regarding the temperature–pressure (T-P) dependence, the critical pressures required for both spontaneous nucleation and epitaxial growth increase sharply with temperature. This trend is fundamentally attributed to the enhanced nitrogen solubility in the Na–Ga melt at elevated temperatures. Consequently, achieving the supersaturation threshold for epitaxial growth demands a higher nitrogen partial pressure. Furthermore, the critical supersaturation (and its corresponding pressure) for spontaneous nucleation increases even more dramatically at high temperatures. Thermodynamically, this occurs because the free energy difference between the gas and liquid phases diminishes, rendering embryonic nuclei highly unstable against thermal agitation. As a result, a substantially higher excess pressure is required to overcome the nucleation barrier. This temperature-induced expansion of the high-quality epitaxial growth window is highly advantageous; it enables the effective suppression of spontaneous nucleation through precise pressure modulation, thereby facilitating the synthesis of large, low-defect GaN single crystals.

However, a discrepancy exists between our calculated T-P phase diagram and certain reported literature data regarding the specific condition of 1123 K and 1.5 MPa While the referenced work suggests that GaN growth is achievable under these parameters, our theoretical model predicts a dissolution regime. To resolve this inconsistency and rigorously validate the accuracy of our computational framework, we perform a comprehensive re-evaluation of this specific T-P point. This is achieved through a dual-verification approach, integrating MD simulations to probe atomic-scale mechanisms with targeted experimental synthesis to confirm macroscopic outcomes. Such a multifaceted validation ensures the reliability of our established growth window.

Figure illustrates the simulation-based judgment of growth behavior at 1073 K and 1.5 MPa by employing the MD theoretical framework established in Figure . The dynamic evolution of the system was further examined through atomic-scale snapshots captured at the postequilibration and postgrowth (10 ns) stages, providing a direct visual correlation between the theoretical model and the actual crystallization process. Despite a sustained supply of monatomic nitrogen in the gas phase, no discernible epitaxial growth of GaN is achieved under the conditions of 1123 K and 1.5 MPa. This result is consistent with the experiments conducted by Aoki et al., where no crystal growth was observed at 1123 K and 1.5 MPa. From a structural perspective, the surface atoms of the seed crystal retain a disordered or semidisordered configuration throughout the 10 ns evolution (indicated by the purple frame), with no emergence of the characteristic hexagonal wurtzite lattice at the gas-seed interface. This structural stagnation suggests that the system fail to overcome the critical nucleation barrier, likely due to an insufficient degree of supersaturation at this relatively low pressure.

Consequently, the system tends toward crystal dissolution rather than precipitation. These microscopic observations are in excellent agreement with our calculated P-T phase diagram, which categorizes the 1123 K/1.5 MPa point within the growth inhibition regime. Ultimately, the MD simulation results confirm, from a microscopic perspective, the thermodynamic limitation associated with the nucleation free-energy barrier (ΔG*), which remains high at low nitrogen pressures due to insufficient supersaturation, thereby hindering GaN crystallization.

To evaluate the feasibility of GaN crystal growth at 1123 K and 1.5 MPa, a systematic analysis of the experimental results is conducted. As illustrated in Figure a,b, the postexperimental seed crystal surface exhibits no identifiable growth features; instead, it shows pronounced etching and dissolution morphologies. This lack of net growth is macroscopically evidenced by the change in crystal mass, which decreased slightly from 1.072 to 1.071 g (a loss of ∼ 0.09%). This conclusion is further corroborated at the microscopic scale by cross-sectional SEM images (Figure cd), which clearly reveal a reduction in seed thickness. Furthermore, no evidence of newly formed crystalline products is detected in the experimental residues. Such surface degradation behavior corresponds to the dissolution region situated below the blue curve in the P-T phase diagram, thereby providing qualitative experimental validation for the accuracy of our theoretical predictions.

To further evaluate the reliability of the established P-T phase diagram, a direct comparative analysis is performed between our simulation results and multiple sets of experimental data reported in ref. [21], as illustrated in Figure . Under a constant pressure of 3 MPa, the morphology and growth rate of GaN crystals exhibit a distinct temperature dependence. When the growth temperature is below 1123 K, the crystals exhibit hexagonal pyramidal growth with prominent ridge-like surface features. In the low-temperature regime (1073–1113 K), the system resides deep within the spontaneous nucleation region, where the high thermodynamic driving force significantly reduces the nucleation barrier. This leads to the disordered formation of numerous nuclei and subsequent competitive growth, characterizing a typical 3D island growth (Volmer–Weber) mode, as depicted in Figure a-1–c-1. As the temperature further increases to 1123 and 1133 K, the system crosses the nucleation boundary into the epitaxial growth region. Under low-supersaturation conditions, the enhanced surface diffusion length allows atoms to preferentially attach to energetically favorable step edges rather than forming new clusters. This facilitates a fundamental transition toward the step-flow growth mechanism, yielding the atomically flat surfaces shown in Figure d-1,e-1. Cross-sectional observations, as shown in Figure a-2–e-2, reveal that the crystal thickness increases and then decreases with rising temperature. This trend is attributed to the nonlinear variation of supersaturation in the Na-flux system: a peak supersaturation exists near the spontaneous nucleation boundary, beyond which supersaturation declines despite the increased solubility of nitrogen. This behavior perfectly aligns with our calculated T-P phase diagram. Furthermore, the reduced growth rate in the high-quality crystallization regime allows for sufficient structural relaxation, thereby significantly enhancing the crystalline perfection.

To further elucidate the physical nature of the bonding characteristics in crystal growth observed in Figure , molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are performed at a pressure of 3 MPa over a temperature range of 1073 to 1133 K. The radial distribution function (RDF) for GaN crystal growth is calculated and is shown in Figure . Specifically, as the temperature decreases from 1113 to 1073 K, the intensity of the first nearest-neighbor peak corresponding to the Ga–N bond also decreases slightly. This is generally associated with a lower degree of local atomic order, indicating that within this temperature range, lowering the temperature may results in poorer crystal quality. Furthermore, lower temperatures constrain the Ga–N collision frequency and prevent solute atoms from surmounting surface diffusion barriers. Such restricted mobility hinders adatoms from locating minimum-energy lattice sites, may lead to random nucleation-driven growth characterized by grain boundaries and high roughness, as seen at 1073 to 1113 K. In contrast, at the higher temperatures of 1123 and 1133 K, the intensity of the first nearest-neighbor peak corresponding to the Ga–N bond slightly increases. Concurrently, enhanced surface diffusion triggers a transition to step-flow epitaxy, where preferential attachment to steps suppresses new nucleation and elevates crystal quality. Notably, although the epitaxial layer is thinner at 1133 K than at 1123 K, it adopts a superior planar structure. This occurs because the elevated temperature weakens the c-direction growth rate, effectively shrinking hexagonal surface pits to produce a smoother morphology.

To comprehensively validate the accuracy of the T-P phase diagram, a boundary-verification strategy is adopted by selecting the two thermal extremes within the defined temperature range as representative conditions. By analyzing the growth behavior at these temperature limits under different pressures, the reliability of the phase diagram is assessed from an edge-condition perspective. Figure presents the atomic-scale evolution of the GaN epitaxial layer from the postequilibration stage to the postgrowth stage (10 ns). It should be noted that the simulations are performed with a pre-existing crystalline seed, therefore, the observed structural evolution corresponds to growth at the solid–liquid interface rather than homogeneous spontaneous nucleation. At a constant temperature of 973 K, increasing the pressure from 1 to 2 MPa significantly enhances atomic ordering and interfacial packing density. The transition from disordered atomic clusters to periodic lattice arrangements indicates the occurrence of ordered epitaxial propagation on the seed surface. In contrast, at 1173 K, no discernible growth is observed within the investigated pressure range. This behavior is attributed to the reduced thermodynamic driving force under high-temperature and relatively low-pressure conditions, where increased thermal fluctuations hinder the stabilization of ordered structures at the interface. These results are consistent with the T-P phase diagram, where the selected condition lies within (or near) the nucleation-favorable region, indicating that crystallization is thermodynamically permitted.

Summary

In this study, a synergistic multiscale framework coupling macroscopic FEA and MD simulations was established to systematically investigate the crystallization kinetics of GaN grown via the Na-flux method. By cross-validating the theoretical model with reported experimental data from the literature, the synergistic regulation mechanisms of T-P were elucidated. The main conclusions are as follows:(1)Construction of the T-P Phase Diagram: A precise T-P phase diagram based on dynamic supersaturation was successfully constructed for the range of 973–1173 K and 1–7 MPa. The exceptional agreement between our theoretical predictions and existing literature experimental results strongly validates the accuracy of this multiscale model.(2)Delineation of the High-Quality Growth Window: The thermodynamic boundaries for GaN crystallization were explicitly defined. A critical “high-quality growth window” was identified, which is strictly bounded by the minimum thermodynamic requirement for stable epitaxial growth (the lower bound) and the critical threshold for parasitic spontaneous polycrystalline nucleation (the upper bound).(3)Expansion of the Growth Window under High Temperature and Pressure: The study reveals that the high-quality growth window significantly expands as temperature and pressure increase. This phenomenon perfectly aligns with the fundamental thermodynamic principles of GaN crystal growth: elevated temperatures substantially enhance nitrogen solubility in the Na–Ga melt; meanwhile, as nitrogen pressure increases, a greater number of gaseous N2 molecules can overcome the gas–liquid interfacial barrier, dissociating into atomic nitrogen (N) and dissolving into the melt. Consequently, the increase in system pressure directly leads to an elevated effective nitrogen concentration within the melt. This not only dramatically broadens the allowable supersaturation range for stable epitaxial growth but also provides a wider operational space for the precise process regulation of GaN crystallization.
