LiMeNEx: an interactive webserver on lipid biochemical gene-regulatory network
Authors: Diksha Marwaha, Aishwary Sharma, Kartikeya Saini, Aryan K Singh, Akash Bhaskar, Arjun Ray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipid metabolism represents an intricate landscape of biochemical reactions catalyzed by enzymes and regulated by transcription factors (TFs) to maintain metabolic homeostasis. Disruption of the enzymatic or transcriptional machinery is associated with lipid imbalance, contributing to diverse pathological conditions across tissues and the physiological system. However, existing pathway resources predominantly focus on metabolic map reactions but lack integration of upstream regulatory components, limiting system-level interrogation of lipid-associated mechanisms. We present LiMeNEx (Lipid Metabolic Network Explorer), an interactive webserver for exploration of lipid biochemical gene regulatory networks that link lipids, enzymes, and TFs. LiMeNEx integrates carefully curated 504 biochemical reactions across 22 lipid pathways, involving 330 enzymatic genes and 345 TFs mapped across 50 human tissues and 11 physiological systems. The three consols of LiMeNEx allow users to query lipids for visualization of lipid pathways and genes involved in lipid–enzyme reactions, and to explore tissue and systems-specific transcriptional regulators of target genes. LiMeNEx provides a holistic view of lipid metabolism with comprehensive documentation for intuitive navigation. By integrating metabolic and regulatory layers in a tissue-resolved framework, it enables hypothesis-driven system-level investigation of lipid metabolism and related disorders. LiMeNEx is freely available at https://limenex.raylab.iiitd.edu.in/.

Introduction

Lipids exhibit a diverse class of hydrophobic molecules produced by enzyme-mediated metabolic reactions. They play primary roles in maintaining cellular membrane integrity, energy storage (triglycerides), cellular signaling (ceramides), and synthesis of complex lipid species (fatty acids) [1]. Maintenance of normal physiology and metabolic homeostasis depends on the coordinated regulation of lipid synthesis and turnover at the cellular and tissue levels. [2]. Lipid regulation is influenced by environmental factors, hormonal signaling, enzymatic catalysis, and transcription factor (TF)-driven gene control. Disruption of lipid metabolic regulatory mechanisms results in undesirable lipid accumulation in non-adipose tissues, lipotoxicity, and altered cellular composition, which contribute to oncogenic, metabolic, and neurodegenerative disorders [3].

Recent studies have demonstrated that upstream regulators are major contributors to lipid phenotypes and pathological outcomes. For instance, sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBP) influence cholesterol and triglyceride synthesis during the stages of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). [4], up regulation of fatty acid synthase (FASN) enzyme enhances phosphatidylcholine and fatty acid synthesis in colorectal cancer [5]. Additionally, impaired T3 hormone elevates low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and triglyceride in patients with hypothyroidism [6]. In contrast, most existing lipidomics investigations focus primarily on quantifying lipid species and correlating them with disease outcomes, such as upregulated saturated fatty acids and reduced oleic acid levels, which have been reported in serum profiles in psoriasis [7], and elevated LDL cholesterol is associated with women’s infertility risk. [8]. Likewise, existing pathway databases emphasize metabolic reactions rather than regulatory elements, whereas available gene regulatory networks rarely prioritize lipid metabolism.

To address this gap, we developed the LiMeNEx (Lipid Metabolism Network Explorer), for systematic exploration of TF-to-enzyme-to-lipid relationships, thereby facilitating the identification of upstream regulatory components of disease-relevant lipids across various tissues and the physiological system.

Materials and methods

Manually curated lipid metabolism pathways

For the development of LiMeNEx, 22 distinct pathways were meticulously curated using CellDesigner 4.4.2 [9] software to create a comprehensive biochemical network. Pathway data were collected from publicly available databases as illustrated in Fig. 1, including Reactome [10], KEGG [11], WikiPathways [12], and LipidMaps [13] (Supplementary Fig. S1). All the pathways were exported in XML format for integration within the LiMeNEx webserver. To enhance interpretability and consistency, metabolites were represented as oval nodes, while enzymatic genes and TFs via rectangular ones. Edges were defined as reaction edges, modifier edges, and regulatory edges. The rationale for using Cell Designer was its flexibility in representing customized biological elements beyond the constraints of standard pathway formats, such as SBML, KGML, or XML. These standard formats restrict the inclusion of custom entities crucial for LiMeNEx, such as explicit lipid–non-lipid distinctions and reaction–regulation hierarchies. Non-essential molecules (e.g. cofactors, water, ATP, GTP, NADPH) were excluded to prevent redundancy and ensure biological clarity. The curated pathways stored in XML format as a biochemical network provide the structural foundation of LiMeNEx.

Construction workflow of the webserver was developed through seven sequential steps: (1) Manual lipid pathways network curation; (2) network export in XML format; (3) TF regulatory data acquisition; (4) compilation of TF–enzyme gene pairs; (5) data standardization; (6) mapping pairs to tissues and physiological systems; and (7) integration of merged TF–gene pair datasets and 22 curated pathways into LiMeNEx.

Acquisition of transcription regulators for enzymatic genes

To incorporate transcriptional regulatory layer into LiMeNEx, experimentally validated TF–target gene interactions were obtained from TRRUST v2 [14], SIGNOR 3.0 [15], ChEA3 [16], and the signaling pathway project (SPP) [17]. TF–gene interactions from SIGNOR 3.0 were programmatically fetched via API, retaining 57 interactions annotated under the mechanism “transcriptional regulation.” From ChEA3, 854 TF–target pairs were retrieved under “Literature–ChIP-seq” category to ensure experimental validation. TRRUSTv2 contributed 193 literature-supported pairs, which were parsed to extract TF names, target genes, regulation type(activation, repression or unknown), and PubMed references. For the SPP Knowledge base, 28 739 interaction pairs were extracted through automated Python-based parsing, as API access was unavailable (Supplementary Fig. S2). These data were obtained under the “Cistromics(ChIP-seq)” category and included TFs, target genes, evidence type, binding score, tissue type, and species information. All datasets were standardized to resolve naming inconsistencies. A reference tissue-mapping file was constructed, which linked physiological systems, tissues, and cell types. New tissue entries identified during parsing were verified manually and appended. The final curated dataset included TFs, target genes, tissues, databases of origin, and evidence were compiled into a unified CSV for integration into LiMeNEx

Validation of TF–target gene interactions in tissues

To ensure the reliability of regulatory data, TF–target gene pairs from ChEA3, SIGNOR 3.0, and TRRUST v2 were manually verified. Each interaction was cross-referenced with experimental literature to validate the associated tissue-specific context. Since tissue annotation was missing in most of these databases (except for SPP), a literature review was conducted to identify the corresponding cellular context for every interaction pair. The identified cellular type was aligned with standardized tissue terminology to ensure consistency and accuracy. Pairs lacking strong experimental or literature evidence were excluded from integrating into LiMeNEx despite of annotated tissue information. To maximize data reliability, TF–gene pairs from ChEA3, SIGNOR 3.0, and TRRUST v2 were manually cross-referenced with primary literature. Pairs lacking strong experimental or literature evidence were excluded, prioritising precision over recall. ChIP-seq-derived pairs from SPP are tagged separately in the evidence table, allowing users to stratify results by evidence type and assess interaction reliability independently. All curated lipid metabolism pathways, along with their validated TF–gene interactions and associated tissues, are available in the Supplementary Data.

To quantify the robustness and recurrence of TF–target gene interactions across biological contexts, a confidence-based classification framework was introduced. Each interaction pair was assigned two independent confidence scores based on its frequency of occurrence across (i) distinct tissues and (ii) physiological systems. For tissue-based classification, interactions present in 1–3 tissues were assigned a low confidence score (Score = 1; 23 595 pairs), those occurring in 4–7 tissues were assigned medium confidence (Score = 2; 3989 pairs), and interactions observed in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\ge$\end{document}8 tissues (up to 23 tissues) were classified as high confidence (Score = 3; 600 pairs). Similarly, for physiological system-based classification, interactions present in 1–3 systems were categorized as low confidence (Score = 1; 23 954 pairs), those in 4–6 systems as medium confidence (Score = 2; 3159 pairs), and those occurring in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\ge$\end{document}7 systems (up to 10 systems) as high confidence (Score = 3; 1071 pairs). These dual classification schemes enable systematic assessment of interaction consistency across biological scales. Interactions with low confidence scores were interpreted as context-specific, indicating potential tissue or system-dependent regulatory relationships, whereas high-confidence interactions represent conserved regulatory events observed across multiple biological contexts, suggesting context-independent or broadly applicable regulatory roles. For downstream analyses and user interpretation within LiMeNEx, the confidence scores were further consolidated into three categorical labels: low, medium, and high confidence.

Webserver implementation

The LiMeNEx webserver is implemented via Dash framework, which combines a Flask-based backend with a React.js frontend to support interactive biological network exploration (Supplementary Fig. S3). The LiMeNEx network module operates using two primary input categories: (i) XML-formatted network files exported from CellDesigner, encompassing curated biochemical pathways, and (ii) the TF Mapping File derived from the TF–target gene pairs obtained from the SPP Knowledgebase across multiple tissues and physiological systems. The platform is organized into three query consoles, from which users can input, which are then passed to the pathway assembly module, reaction module, and regulatory module to dynamically assemble lipid-, gene-, and TF-centered graphs. Data parsing and conversions are handled by Pandas library. The webserver utilizes a reactive callback framework to enable real-time updates to both network graphs and their associated evidence tables when the user applies refinement actions in regulatory consoles.

This integrated pipeline is modular and scalable, designed to handle multiple biochemical pathways simultaneously while enabling seamless incorporation of additional pathway or regulatory datasets in future updates.

Results and discussion

Network features of LiMeNEx

LiMeNEx employs standardized visual conventions within network framework to distinguish biological entities. Lipid metabolites are represented as blue oval nodes; non-lipid metabolites as orange ovals; and initial metabolites of each pathway as cyan ovals. Enzymatic genes are displayed as green while TFs appear as red rectangular nodes (Fig. 2A).

Edges within the network are classified into distinct biochemical categories according to biological function. Solid arrows represent substrate-to-product conversions or biochemical reactions, catalysis edges are represented by lines with circular connectors, which connect enzymatic genes to their corresponding reaction units. TF–gene regulatory interactions are illustrated with dashed edges (Fig. 2B). Another distinguishing feature is the display of all possible reaction conversion patterns within the network (Fig. 2C). A network snippet at the reaction level demonstrates gene-specific catalysis. The selected enzymatic gene is shown with its associated substrate and product, along with the pathway label and additional enzymes to represent shared catalytic roles (Fig. 2D). Selection of metabolite nodes provides access to compound information from external databases, including ChEBI, PubChem, and KEGG. In contrast, enzymatic gene and TF nodes redirects to the respective UniProt entries (Fig. 2E). Reaction edge enable users to interpret biochemical nature of the substrate-product conversion (Fig. 2F).

LiMeNEx filters TFs for specific enzymatic genes within defined biological contexts using two filters: Physiological System and Tissue. Users can choose from predefined systems—such as metabolic, immune, and respiratory to view experimentally validated TFs regulating the enzymatic genes. Within each system, users can further refine by tissue (e.g. liver) to display only TFs with validated evidence in that tissue (Fig. 2G). Two-level filtering supports context-specific interpretation of regulatory interactions. The platform listed in all the consoles support evidence-based biochemical and regulatory information.Pathway Console evidence table enlists pathways and their database sources for the queried lipids (Fig. 2H). Query enzymatic gene reports bichemical conversion related substrates, products and its pathways in reaction Console table (Fig. 2I). The regulatory evidence table summarizes all TFs regulated by the enzymatic gene across tissues. Each entry specifies the supporting database sources—SPP, SIGNOR 3.0, TRRUST v2, and ChEA3—with corresponding PubMed identifiers for experimental validation (Fig. 2J).

User interface and functional modules

LiMeNEx features an intuitive, browser-based interface that requires no prior installation and a unified platform interface for lipid-centric investigation of metabolic and regulatory networks. It consists of three primary modules—Pathway Console, Reaction Console, and TF-Gene Console in a navigation panel. The Pathway Console enables lipid-centric exploration of metabolic pathways, visualizing the corresponding metabolites and the interconnected enzymatic genes (Fig. 3A). User selects lipids from the search panel to retrieve metabolic networks via the Fetch Network option. The resulting view supports lipid-corresponding pathways, direct biochemical reactions, enzymes that utilize the query lipid as a substrate, and highlights first-level chains of some target lipids within pathways, along with a pathway evidence table. The Reaction Console facilitates the exploration of biochemical reactions of query-specific enzymatic genes with detailed reaction information (Fig. 3B). The Fetch Reactions option allows users to examine substrate-product conversions, co-participating enzymatic genes, and reaction type annotations. Further, an evidence table in this console summarizes substrate, product, and pathways for query genes. TF–Gene Console renders experimentally validated transcriptional regulators of the target gene (Fig. 3C). The user can retrieve TFs via the Fetch TFs option for a query. TF–target gene regulatory networks are refined using system- and tissue-specific recurrence-based confidence score filters to obtain biological context-specific interactions. This is further supported by a table of TF-gene associations in tissues with supporting regulatory resources. All three consoles support interactive exploration of networks and their entities, exporting network graphs in SVG and JSON formats, and tabular outputs in CSV.

Critical insights into TF–gene relationships revealed in LiMeNEx

Manually verified reliability of the regulatory data for the TF–target gene pairs from ChEA3, SIGNOR 3.0, and TRRUST v2 revealed that among all the tissue types considered, leukocytes (15149) exhibited the highest transcriptional complexity in regulating enzymes involved in lipid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S4). This indicates that the immune system hosts the most extensive lipid metabolite regulatory hubs. The second-tier hubs of TF-enzymatic regulation of lipid pathways are located in the Liver (6666) and the Colon (6058), reflecting the pivotal roles of these metabolic tissues in systematically regulating lipid homeostasis. Dense TF–gene interactions observed in Leukocytes (immune cells), Liver (Metabolic hubs), and Colon (immune-metabolic junction) signify lipid regulatory hotspots, whereas low-count tissues (Stomach, Bone, Skin, Embryonic Tissue) show lower transcriptional activity and lipid metabolism in these tissues is less central or more cell-type-specific.

Highest TF-enzymatic gene pair-counts across the lipid metabolism-curated pathways were observed in Glycerophospholipid synthesis (5364), followed by Phosphoinositol (2662), cholesterol synthesis (2651), Lipoprotein (2560), and Arachidonic acid metabolism (2433), revealing multiple TF-enzyme pair checkpoints regulating lipid flux (Supplementary Fig. S5). Similarly, low-density TF–gene pairs in certain pathways, including triglyceride degradation, carnitine metabolism, and oxylipin biosynthesis, convey specialized regulatory roles for some TFs in enzymes involved in lipid metabolic mechanisms.

A closer inspection into the relative density of TF regulators per target gene across 22 lipid pathways and 43 tissues revealed that leukocytes, liver, and colon had the highest density of TF regulators per lipid-activating enzyme across all the tissues, highlighting them as central hubs of lipid regulation (Supplementary Fig. S6). A moderate density of TF regulators was observed in the uterus, stem cells, and mammary gland, underscoring the significance of hormonal regulation of lipid pathways in reproductive tissues. Upon analyzing issues in specific pathways, it was found that steroid hormone synthesis, lipoprotein pathway, and cholesterol degradation are strongly regulated by TFs in the liver tissue. oxylipin biosynthesis, triglyceride degradation, and sphingolipid degradation depict high TF density of regulation in the colon, uterus,the mammary gland, and lung. The relative density of TF regulators per target across the 11 physiological systems showed that the gastrointestinal, respiratory, and neurosensory systems exhibit intermediate regulation, whereas the cardiovascular and skeletal systems show low TF regulation density across all lipid pathways (Supplementary Fig. S7). To further examine the evidence structure underlying TF–target gene interactions integrated in Console 3 of LiMeNEx, we evaluated the overlap and distribution of pairs among evidence sources: SPP, ChEA, TRRUST, and SIGNOR (Supplementary Fig. S8). The analysis revealed that the majority of TF–gene pairs were supported by a single source, with SPP contributing the most evidence-based pairs (n = 8884), followed by ChEA (n = 1817), TRRUST (n = 559), and SIGNOR (n = 114). In contrast, comparatively fewer interactions were supported by multiple sources, indicating that although the integrated network is broad in coverage, the highest confidence in evidence is concentrated within a smaller subset of recurrently supported TF-gene relationships. To establish a recurrence-based confidence framework for TF–enzyme interactions across tissues, we analyzed the distribution of unique pairs according to the number of tissues in which each interaction was observed (Supplementary Fig. S9). The distribution was right-skewed, with the majority of interactions confined to a limited number of tissues, suggesting pronounced tissue-specificity in transcriptional regulation. Specifically, most interaction pairs were observed in only 1–3 tissues (n = 23 595), whereas a much smaller subset of interactions recurred across a broader tissue range, with only 600 pairs present in eight or more tissues. Based on this distribution, tissue-frequency thresholds were defined to classify TF–gene interactions into three confidence groups: low confidence (1–3 tissues), medium confidence (4–7 tissues), and high confidence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\ge$\end{document}8 tissues). This strategy enabled the systematic prioritization of broadly conserved regulatory interactions over tissue-restricted associations in Console 3 of LiMeNEx. A similar recurrence-based strategy was applied at the physiological system level to assess the broader conservation of TF–enzyme interactions across biological contexts (Supplementary Fig. S10). The distribution again showed a strong right-skewed trend, with most TF–gene pairs occurring in only one or a few physiological systems. In particular, 23 954 interaction pairs were observed across 1–3 physiological systems, whereas only 1071 pairs were detected across seven or more systems, representing the most recurrent and broadly conserved regulatory interactions. Based on this distribution, physiological system-based confidence thresholds were defined as low (1–3 systems), medium (4–6 systems), and high (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\ge$\end{document}7 systems). A comparative summary of the confidence class assignments derived from tissue-based and physiological system-based recurrence scoring (Supplementary Fig. S11). For the tissue-based framework, 23 595 TF-gene pairs were classified as low confidence, 3989 as medium confidence, and 600 as high confidence. Likewise, in the physiological system-based framework, 23 954 interactions were assigned to the low-confidence class, 3159 to the medium-confidence class, and 1071 to the high-confidence class. These results indicate that the majority of regulatory interactions in LiMeNEx are context-restricted, while a relatively smaller but important subset shows repeated occurrence across multiple tissues or physiological systems, thereby representing more robust and potentially conserved TF–enzyme regulatory relationships.

A biological case study: tracing transcriptional regulators of cholesterol synthesis in the context of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

NAFLD represents one of the most prevalent metabolic disorders globally, characterized by aberrant hepatic lipid accumulation driven by dysregulated cholesterol and fatty acid metabolism [18]. To demonstrate the end-to-end utility of LiMeNEx in a biologically grounded context, we present a case study that traces the transcriptional regulatory landscape of cholesterol synthesis in liver tissue, illustrating how a researcher can move systematically from a lipid of clinical interest to its upstream gene-regulatory determinants using the three consoles of LiMeNEx.

Step 1: Lipid-centric pathway retrieval via the Pathway Console A researcher investigating NAFLD initiates the analysis by querying cholesterol in the Pathway Console. LiMeNEx retrieves all biochemical pathways in which cholesterol participates as a metabolite, including cholesterol synthesis, cholesterol degradation, steroid hormone synthesis, and the lipoprotein pathway. The resulting network highlights cholesterol as a central metabolite node (blue oval), connected to its biosynthetic precursors, such as lanosterol and desmosterol, and to downstream products via enzymatic intermediates (green rectangular nodes). Among the enzymes displayed, HMGCR (HMG-CoA reductase), SQLE (squalene epoxidase), and FDFT1 (farnesyl-diphosphate farnesyltransferase 1) are identified as key enzymatic genes catalysing rate-limiting steps within the cholesterol synthesis pathway. The Pathway Console evidence table confirms that these reactions are supported across multiple curated databases, including KEGG and Reactome, providing immediate provenance for the retrieved network.

Step 2: Reaction-level dissection via the Reaction Console The researcher carries the identified enzymatic genes forward to the Reaction Console, querying HMGCR to examine its biochemical role in detail. LiMeNEx displays the reaction network centered on HMGCR, illustrating the irreversible conversion of HMG-CoA to mevalonate, the committed, rate-limiting step of the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway. The network additionally renders co-participating enzymatic genes that share catalytic roles within connected reactions, contextualizing HMGCR within the broader enzymatic machinery of the pathway. The reaction type is annotated as an irreversible conversion, and the associated reaction edge provides information on the biochemical nature of the substrate-product transformation. The Reaction Console evidence table summarizes the substrate (HMG-CoA), product (mevalonate), co-participating genes, and associated pathways for the queried gene, providing a structured, exportable record of the reaction-level information. This step confirms HMGCR as a high-priority enzymatic target for regulatory interrogation and motivates its selection for TF-level analysis in the subsequent console.

Step 3: Tissue-specific transcriptional regulatory network via the TF–Gene Console The researcher queries HMGCR in the TF–Gene Console and applies a two-level filter: Metabolic system \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\longrightarrow$\end{document} Liver, to retrieve only those TFs with experimentally validated regulatory interactions with HMGCR in hepatic tissue, the primary site of cholesterol biosynthesis and the tissue most directly implicated in NAFLD pathogenesis. LiMeNEx returns a tissue-resolved TF–gene regulatory network in which HMGCR is the central target node (green rectangle), connected to its transcriptional regulators (red rectangular nodes) via dashed regulatory edges. Among the retrieved TFs, SREBP1 and SREBP2 (sterol regulatory element-binding proteins) emerge as prominent regulators, consistent with their well-established roles in activating HMGCR transcription in response to intracellular cholesterol depletion [4]. Additionally, SP1 and NF-Y are identified as co-regulators, supported by ChIP-seq evidence from the SPP knowledgebase and literature-based evidence from TRRUST v2 and ChEA3, respectively. The regulatory evidence table lists each TF–gene pair with its associated tissue, supporting database sources (SPP, SIGNOR 3.0, TRRUST v2, ChEA3), and corresponding PubMed identifiers, enabling the researcher to directly access the primary experimental literature underpinning each interaction.

Biological interpretation and utility This three-step traversal, from cholesterol as a lipid query, through HMGCR as a rate-limiting enzymatic gene, to SREBP1/2 and SP1 as hepatic transcriptional regulators, recapitulates and extends a regulatory axis that is central to NAFLD pathogenesis (Fig. 4). Critically, this workflow required no prior knowledge of which TFs regulate HMGCR, nor any external database cross-referencing; LiMeNEx surfaced these relationships interactively, with full evidence provenance, within a single platform session. Beyond confirming known biology, this mode of exploration enables hypothesis generation: a researcher may, for instance, observe a lesser-characterized TF co-retrieved alongside SREBP2 in liver tissue and formulate a testable hypothesis regarding its contribution to cholesterol dysregulation in NAFLD. Similarly, the same gene set can be re-queried across alternative tissues, such as the colon or leukocytes, to explore whether the same enzymatic targets are governed by divergent transcriptional programs in different physiological contexts, supporting cross-tissue comparative analysis of lipid regulatory mechanisms. Taken together, this case study demonstrates that LiMeNEx enables end-to-end, hypothesis-driven exploration of lipid gene-regulatory networks in a biologically meaningful and clinically relevant context, a capability not supported by any existing standalone lipid or regulatory database.

Discussion

LiMeNEx is a comprehensive webserver for exploring lipid metabolism across pathways, biochemical reactions, and the regulatory layer. Unlike conventional pathway databases, which often restrict analysis to metabolic reactions alone, LiMeNEx bridges the gap between enzymatic control and transcriptional regulation, offering an interactive environment for mechanistic exploration of lipid dysregulation in health and disease. Functionally, LiMeNEx goes beyond static visualization, enabling users to navigate from lipids to regulatory TFs, visualize reaction-level interactions, and access literature-supported TF–gene relationships through an integrated evidence table. These interactive modules not only enhance data transparency but also support hypothesis generation by linking lipidomic signatures to gene-regulatory mechanisms. Several existing resources address aspects of lipid metabolism or gene regulation independently, but none provide an integrated, tissue-resolved framework spanning lipid metabolites, enzymatic genes, and TFs. Pathway databases such as KEGG, Reactome, and WikiPathways offer comprehensive maps of lipid biochemical reactions but do not incorporate upstream transcriptional regulatory components (Supplementary Table S1). LipidMaps provides rich annotation of lipid species and structural classification yet lacks pathway–gene–TF connectivity. On the regulatory side, general TF–gene interaction databases such as TRRUST v2, SIGNOR 3.0, and ChEA3 catalogue experimentally validated regulatory interactions across diverse biological contexts but are not tailored to lipid metabolism and do not resolve interactions at the tissue or physiological system level. LiMeNEx uniquely bridges these two worlds by integrating 504 manually curated biochemical reactions across 22 lipid pathways with experimentally validated TF–enzymatic gene interactions mapped across 50 human tissues and 11 physiological systems, all within a single interactive platform. This three-layer architecture—lipid metabolite → enzymatic gene → TF—with tissue-level resolution enables a mode of hypothesis-driven inquiry that is not supported by any existing standalone resource, positioning LiMeNEx as a complementary and novel addition to the lipid biology toolkit. The current version of LiMeNEx primarily focuses on human lipid metabolic pathways, but the underlying framework remains extensible to additional analytical modules. Taken together, LiMeNEx serves as both a reference resource and an exploratory analytical tool, enabling researchers to uncover novel regulatory associations and potential biomarkers of lipid-related disorders.
