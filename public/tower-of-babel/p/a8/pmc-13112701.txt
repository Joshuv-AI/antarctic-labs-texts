The GAP43/RAC1 axis drives glycolytic reprogramming and progression in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Kaidi Li, Cheng Shen, Yue Li, Qingqiong Zhang, Jinbao Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and poses a significant threat to public health. Traditional treatments often lead to poor prognoses and tumor recurrence. Glycolysis plays a crucial role in the initiation and progression of LUAD, making it an important area of research for the development of new therapeutic strategies.MethodsIn this study, we analyzed the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data to assess GAP43 expression and prognostic value in LUAD. Functional roles were investigated using GAP43 knockdown and overexpression cell lines through proliferation, apoptosis, colony formation, wound healing, and Transwell assays. The nude mouse xenograft tumor model was used to validate the effects of GAP43 knockdown on tumor growth in vivo. RAC1 was identified as a downstream target via gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and correlation analysis. Glycolytic activity (glucose uptake, ATP/lactate production, OCR/ECAR) and related protein expression were measured, and GAP43-RAC1 interaction was examined by co-immunoprecipitation.ResultsGAP43 expression was significantly upregulated in LUAD tissues compared with normal tissues and that high GAP43 expression was associated with poor overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). GAP43 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasive capabilities of LUAD cells in vitro and suppressed tumor growth in vivo. Conversely, its overexpression promoted malignant behavior of tumor cells. Mechanistically, GAP43 promoted the transcription of RAC1 by binding to the transcription factor CREB1, thereby enhancing aerobic glycolysis and facilitating the malignant progression of LUAD.ConclusionsThe results of this study suggest that GAP43 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for LUAD. The GAP43/RAC1 axis plays a crucial role in regulating glycolysis and malignant phenotypes in LUAD cells, offering new insights for the clinical management of LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03606-5.

Introduction

In recent decades, lung cancer has emerged as one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality, posing significant harm to and a burden on global public health. Histopathologically, lung cancer is classified into small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with NSCLC accounting for more than 80% of cases. NSCLC can be further categorized into lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and large cell carcinoma (LCC), among which LUAD is the most prevalent subtype, representing approximately 40%~45% of all patients [1]. The treatment of LUAD necessitates a personalized approach on the basis of individual patient tumor progression. Early-stage LUAD is primarily managed through surgical intervention; however, patients with advanced or metastatic disease require systemic therapies that may alleviate symptoms and prolong overall survival (OS) to some degree. Nonetheless, these treatments often lead to poor prognoses and tumor recurrence [2, 3]. Therefore, identifying effective therapeutic targets remains a core focus that cannot be overlooked in current research efforts.

Numerous studies have shown that cancer is essentially a metabolism-related disease characterized by dysregulation of glucose, glutamine, and ketone body metabolism, particularly aerobic glycolysis, which serves as a prominent hallmark of tumor progression [4]. Like other cancer types, LUAD cells, promote tumor growth and metastasis by altering metabolic pathways. Glycolysis is a cellular metabolic process regulated by multiple signaling mechanisms that enable cancer cells to rapidly generate ATP and biosynthetic precursors under hypoxic or nutrient-deficient conditions, which are essential for their proliferation and survival [5, 6].

Growth-associated protein 43 (GAP43) is a well-studied molecule in the context of neural development and regeneration, and its role in cancer has received increasing attention in recent years [7, 8]. In various cancers, GAP43 has been implicated in promoting tumor progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy [9–13]. Specifically, in lung cancer, GAP43 has been identified as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target. Studies have shown that GAP43 expression is upregulated in lung cancer tissues compared to normal lung tissues, and its overexpression is associated with poor prognosis and increased metastatic potential [10, 11]. Additionally, it has been suggested that GAP43 may be associated with signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT, which are essential for cancer cell survival and proliferation [14]. These findings highlight the potential role of GAP43 in lung cancer and underscore its value as a target for further investigation. However, the specific role of GAP43 in LUAD and whether it is related to LUAD glycolysis remains largely unexplored.

In this study, GAP43, a critical target in the progression of LUAD, was initially identified through bioinformatics methods. Subsequently, its downstream target gene RAC1 was discovered. In vitro experiments confirmed that GAP43 regulated glycolysis in LUAD cells through RAC1, and in vivo verified that GAP43 knockdown inhibited tumor growth. These findings confirmed the role of GAP43 in regulating glycolysis and tumor progression in lung adenocarcinoma.

Materials and methods

Gene selection analysis for LUAD

To identify genes associated with the occurrence and progression of LUAD, we performed analyses focusing on gene expression levels and prognosis. For expression analysis, RNA sequencing data from LUAD and normal tissue samples were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) through the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal. The raw count data were processed and normalized via the DESeq2 R package, which facilitated differential expression analysis. The Benjamini-Hochberg FDR was used for P value correction. And adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. For the prognostic analysis, we used clinical and RNA sequencing data of LUAD samples from the TCGA database via cBioPortal. The clinical data were subjected to preliminary cleaning and curation. The RNA sequencing data were transformed via a log2(x + 1) function, where x represents the RSEM (RNA-Seq by Expectation Maximization) value.

Histological staining

Hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining was performed to evaluate the histological features of the tumor tissues. The tissue sections were first dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin for 3 min, followed by differentiation in 0.25% hydrochloric acid alcohol and bluing in 0.1% ammonia water. After rinsing, the sections were counterstained with eosin for 25 s. Finally, the sections were dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral medium. This standardized HE staining protocol ensures clear visualization of cellular and tissue structures for histopathological analysis.

The immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed to validate the expression of GAP43 and its effect on tumor proliferation. The tissue microarrays (TMAs) containing LUAD (n = 88) and adjacent normal tissues (n = 90) from multiple patients were commercially available from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd (HLugA180Su11-M-088). The use of these TMAs was approved by the Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd ethics committee (Approval number: SHXC2021YF01). All tissue sections were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a high-pressure cooker at 120 °C for 5 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum in 0.1% Triton X-100. The sections were incubated with the primary antibody against GAP43 (Proteintech, 16971-1-AP, 1:500), RAC1 (Abcam, ab203884, 1:100) and Ki67 (Proteintech, 28074-1-AP, 1:1000) at room temperature for 1 h and then were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After being washed with 1× PBST, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP; Abcam, ab97080, 1:200) for 1 h. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used for chromogenic detection, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. The sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral medium. Pathological images were scanned via CaseViewer_2.4_RTM, a pathology slide image processing software. The IHC staining was quantified using the H-score method to assess the intensity.

Cell culture

Two types of human NSCLC cell lines, A549 and NCI-H1299, were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Stem Cell Bank. All the cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen, USA), 1× MEM nonessential amino acids (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 1× sodium pyruvate (Gibco, USA). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. To ensure the consistency of cell culture conditions, all cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling before the start of the experiments. Additionally, cells were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination to avoid any potential interference with experimental results.

Lentiviral vectors construction

To investigate the role of GAP43 in LUAD, we employed lentivirus-mediated short hairpin RNA (shRNA) to achieve stable knockdown of the GAP43 gene, and constructed overexpression vectors for both GAP43 and RAC1. The shRNA targeting GAP43 was synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China), with a scramble sequence serving as a negative control. For overexpression, the full-length coding sequences of human GAP43 and RAC1 were amplified and cloned. All sequences (shRNA and cDNAs) were cloned into the BR-V lentiviral vector system provided by BioBay (Suzhou, China). The recombinant plasmid was constructed via restriction enzyme digestion (using Age I and EcoR I), primer annealing, and T4 DNA ligase-mediated ligation. The plasmid was then transformed into DH5α competent cells, and positive clones were verified via colony PCR and Sanger sequencing.

For lentivirus packaging, a three-plasmid cotransfection system (BR-V-shGAP43, pMD2. G, and pSPAX2) was used in 293T cells. The lentiviral supernatant was collected at 48 and 72 h posttransfection for concentration and purification. The viral titer was determined via fluorescence and quantitative PCR (qPCR) methods. The lentivirus was used to infect A549 and NCI-H1299 cell lines, and the infection efficiency was monitored via fluorescence microscopy. The knockdown efficiency was validated via qPCR and Western blotting. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reliability and reproducibility. The 293T cells used for viral packaging and the A549 and NCI-H1299 cell lines were obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Life Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed via a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. At each indicated time point (days 1, 2, 3, and 4 post-transfection), 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2.5 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm via a SpectraMax M5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices LLC, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). All the assays were performed in triplicate.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis in A549 and NCI-H1299 cells was measured via an Annexin V/Propidium Iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (Nanjing KeyGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were harvested, washed three times with PBS, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Haimen, China) at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. A total of 300 µL of the cell suspension was stained with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Apoptosis was then analyzed via flow cytometry via a BD FACS Aria flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Colony formation assay

The colony formation ability of A549 and NCI-H1299 cells was assessed via a standard colony formation assay. After transfection, the cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS for 10‒14 days. The medium was replaced every 3 days. Once visible colonies formed, the cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted under a microscope. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Wound healing assay

A wound healing assay was performed to assess the migration ability of A549 and NCI-H1299 cells. The cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until they reached 90–100% confluence. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a scratch in the cell monolayer. The cells were then washed twice with PBS to remove debris and incubated in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 48 h via a light microscope. Wound closure was quantified by measuring the gap distance at these time points. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Cell migration assays

For the migration assay, we used Transwell chambers (Corning Costar Corp., Cambridge, MA, USA). 3 × 104 A549 and NCI-H1299 cells were suspended in 300 µL of serum-free RPMI-1640 medium and seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS. The cells were incubated under standard conditions for 24 h. After incubation, nonmigrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed via a cotton swab. The migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed and stained with 0.4% crystal violet.

Western blotting

Proteins are extracted from cells or tumor tissue using cell lysis buffer with protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein samples were mixed with 5×loading buffer (Beyotime, China) and subjected to heat denaturation under different conditions based on the target protein: samples for GLUT1 detection were heated at 70 °C for 10 min, whereas samples for the detection of other proteins were boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. Then, proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE on a 10% gel and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk (BD biosciences, USA) for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with TBST (Solarbio, China) containing 0.1% tween 20, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. Following three additional TBST washes, the bands were visualized, and their intensities were quantified via Image Lab software. The primary antibodies used included GAP43 (Proteintech, 16971-1-AP, 1: 2000); RAC1 (Abcam, ab203884, 1:1000); GLUT1 (Proteintech, 21829-1-AP, 1:1000); LDHA (Abcam, ab52488, 1: 3000), PKM2 (Proteintech, 15822-1-AP, 1:5000); HK2 (Proteintech, 66974-1-Ig, 1:3000) and β-Actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig, 1:20000). Goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated IgG (Beyotime, A0208, 1:3000) and goat anti-mouse HRP-conjugated IgG (Beyotime, A0216, 1:3000) were used as secondary antibodies. β-Actin was used as a loading control.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells via TRIzol reagent (Sigma, T9424-100 m; St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration, purity, and integrity were assessed via a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 2000/2000 C, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA via the SYBR® PrimeScript® PLUS RT Kit (Takara Bio, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The reaction was performed in a 20 µL volume containing 4 µL of 5x RT Buffer, 2 µL of dNTP mixture, 1 µL of PrimeScript RT Enzyme Mix I, 1 µL of RT Primer Mix, and 12 µL of RNase-free water. The reverse transcription reaction was carried out at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by incubation at 85 °C for 5 s to inactivate the enzyme. The synthesized cDNA was stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.

The relative expression of GAP43 was quantified via qPCR via the ABI Prism 7500 Sequence Detection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Reactions were prepared via SYBR® PrimeScript® PLUS Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan) in a 20 µL volume containing 10 µL of 2x Master Mix, 0.4 µL of forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL of reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 7.2 µL of RNase-free water. The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 31 s. A melting curve analysis was performed to verify the specificity of the PCR products. Gene expression levels were calculated via the 2^(-∆∆Ct) method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The primers used for qPCR were as follows: for GAPDH, the forward primer was TGACTTCAACAGCGACACCCA, and the reverse primer was CACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAAA, yielding a product size of 121 bp; for GAP43, the forward primer was GGCCGCAACCAAAATTCAGG, and the reverse primer was CGGCAGTAGTGGTGCCTTC, producing a 167 bp fragment; for RAC1, the forward primer was CCTGCATCATTTGAAAAATGTCCG, and the reverse primer was CCCACTAGGATGATGGGAGTGT, producing a 75 bp fragment. All primers were synthesized by Genewiz (Suzhou, China) and rigorously validated for amplification efficiency and specificity prior to use.

Co‑immunoprecipitation (Co‑IP)

To examine the protein‑protein interaction between GAP43 and RAC1, Co‑IP was performed. Cells were lysed in ice‑cold IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). For each IP reaction, 500 µg of total protein was incubated with 2 µg of anti‑GAP43 antibody (Proteintech, 16971‑1‑AP) or normal rabbit IgG (Beyotime, A7016, China) overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Then, 30 µL of Protein A/G PLUS‑Agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) were added to each sample and incubated for 4 h at 4 °C. The beads were collected by centrifugation at 2,500 × g for 5 min and washed three times with cold IP lysis buffer. The immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted by boiling in 5 × SDS loading buffer (Beyotime, China) for 10 min. The eluates were then subjected to Western blotting as described above, using anti‑RAC1 (Abcam, ab203884, 1:1000) and anti‑GAP43 (Proteintech, 16971‑1‑AP, 1:2000) antibodies for detection.

The dual-luciferase reporter assay

The dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega). The putative promoter region of the RAC1 gene was cloned into the pGL4.10[luc2] vector (Promega) to construct the firefly luciferase reporter plasmid. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and co-transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) with a mixture containing the RAC1 promoter reporter plasmid, expression plasmids for GAP43 or CREB, and the pRL-TK Renilla luciferase plasmid (Promega) for normalization of transfection efficiency. Luciferase activity was measured on a GloMax® Navigator Microplate Luminometer (Promega). The relative promoter activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase activity.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP was performed using a Magna ChIP™ Kit (Millipore, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with key modifications. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma, USA) for 10 min at room temperature before quenching with glycine. Chromatin was then sonicated using a ultrasonicator to shear DNA into fragments of 200–500 bp, which was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. For each immunoprecipitation, 5 µg of sheared chromatin was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg of a specific antibody against CREB1 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) or normal rabbit IgG (Millipore, USA) as a negative control, followed by capture using Protein A/G Magnetic Beads. The precipitated complexes were sequentially washed, eluted, and reverse-cross-linked. Finally, the enriched DNA fragments were purified and analyzed by quantitative PCR using primers specific to the predicted binding regions within the RAC1 promoter.

Animal experiments

Animal experiments were conducted using female nude mice aged 4–6 weeks and weighing about 18–22 g. These mice were purchased from Changzhou Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China; license number: SCXK (Su) 2021–0013). The mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility with controlled temperature (22–24 °C), humidity (50–60%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All procedures were performed in strict accordance with ethical guidelines and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (Approval number: 20240301030).

For the experiment, 7 mice per group were used. Human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells were harvested and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/200 µL. The cell suspension was injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor size and mouse body weight were measured at designated time points postinjection (Days 5, 10, 14, 19, and 26). The tumor volume was calculated via the following formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised and weighed. Tumor tissues were collected for further analysis, including Western blot and IHC staining.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 23.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Graphs were generated via GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). The normality and homogeneity of continuous variables were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test. For data meeting the assumptions of normality and homogeneity, differences between two groups were assessed via two-tailed Student’s t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Correlations were evaluated with Pearson’s coefficient. Data are presented as the mean ± SD unless otherwise noted. For data that violated normality assumptions, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis H test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used for multiple-group comparisons. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s coefficient. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups stratified by the median expression level were compared with the log-rank test. The hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from univariate Cox proportional hazards regression. A P value of < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001) was considered statistically significant.

Results

GAP43 is overexpressed and correlates with poor prognosis in LUAD

In order to identify genes associated with the occurrence and progression of LUAD, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of gene expression levels and clinical outcomes. Our analysis revealed that GAP43 is significantly overexpressed in LUAD tissues compared with normal tissues, with a log2-fold change of 2.32 (P = 3.68E-15, adjusted P value = 2.46E-14). This overexpression of GAP43 highlights it as a key target for further investigation (Fig. 1A).

To further evaluate the prognostic significance of GAP43 expression, we divided LUAD samples into high- and low-expression groups based on the median GAP43 expression level. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high GAP43 expression had significantly reduced overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Specifically, the median OS for the high-expression group was 41.36 months (95% CI, 34.29–50.33), whereas it was 66.64 months (95% CI, 49.25–NA) for the low-expression group (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.68, 95% CI, 1.25–2.27; log-rank P = 0.000556; Fig. 1B). Similarly, the median PFS for the high-expression group was 29.52 months (95% CI, 22.91–44.05), whereas it was 45.30 months (95% CI, 33.47–72.92) for the low-expression group (HR = 1.46, 95% CI, 1.10–1.92; log-rank P = 0.00768; Fig. 1C). These results strongly suggest that GAP43 overexpression is associated with poor clinical outcomes in LUAD patients.

To validate these findings at the protein level, we performed HE and IHC staining on LUAD and adjacent normal tissues from TMAs. The staining results confirmed that GAP43 protein levels were significantly higher in LUAD tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1D-F). Correlation analysis of TMAs revealed that GAP43 gene expression was significantly higher in LUAD tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (P < 0.001), and GAP43 expression showed a positive correlation with histological grade (P = 0.001) (Table S1-S3). Collectively, these findings indicate that GAP43 is a potential biomarker for poor prognosis in LUAD patients and may serve as a therapeutic target.

GAP43 expression positively correlates with the malignant phenotype of LUAD cells

We examined GAP43 expression in several cell lines, and A549 and NCI-H1299 had relatively high baseline expression levels of GAP43 and were therefore selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 2A). We established stable GAP43-knockdown cell lines and the fluorescence imaging confirmed efficient lentivirus infection (Figure S1). Subsequently, the knockdown efficiency was verified at both the mRNA and protein levels. qPCR and western blot analysis showed that both shGAP43-1 and shGAP43-3 significantly reduced the expression of GAP43 mRNA and protein, with shGAP43-1 exhibiting a more pronounced knockdown effect (Fig. 2B, C).

The effects of GAP43 knockdown on tumor cell proliferation and survival were assessed via CCK-8 assays, which revealed significantly reduced proliferation rates in both A549 and NCI-H1299 cells following GAP43 knockdown (Fig. 3A). Flow cytometry analysis revealed increased apoptosis rates in shGAP43-1 transfected cells (Fig. 3B). Additionally, colony formation assays revealed a marked reduction in the number of colonies in shGAP43-1 transfected cells (Fig. 3C). We further evaluated the impact of GAP43 knockdown on cell migration via wound healing and Transwell assays. The wound-healing assay demonstrated that, compared with the control, shGAP43-1 significantly reduced the migration rates of both A549 and NCI-H1299 cells (Fig. 3D). Similarly, Transwell assays revealed fewer migrated cells in the shGAP43-1 group than in the control group (Fig. 3E). These findings collectively indicate that GAP43 knockdown effectively suppresses the malignant phenotypes of LUAD cells, including proliferation, survival, and migration.

Next, we overexpressed GAP43 in NCI-H1299 cells to further determine its effect on the above functional experiments. Unexpectedly, overexpression of GAP43 promoted cell proliferation and migration but inhibited apoptosis compared to control group of H1299 cells (Figure S2A-G). These results suggest that GAP43 positively promotes the malignant behavior of tumor cells.

GAP43 regulates RAC1 transcription by binding to the transcription factor CREB1

To further explore the molecular mechanisms by which GAP43 influences the malignant progression of LUAD, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted on RNA-seq data from the TCGA database. The top 10 enriched pathways from both the classic pathway database and the Gene Ontology (GO) database are depicted in Figure S3A. Notably, the RAC1 pathway emerged as a key candidate, suggesting that GAP43 might modulate aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells through RAC1 [15]. Furthermore, GSEA demonstrated that the RAC1 pathway was significantly upregulated (NES = 1.9913, P value < 0.001, FDR = 0.008) (Figure S3B). To validate this finding, we analyzed the correlation between GAP43 and RAC1 expression levels across TCGA LUAD samples. The results confirmed a significant positive correlation between GAP43 and RAC1 expression (R = 0.2710; significance test P value < 0.001) (Figure S3C). These findings support the hypothesis that RAC1 is a downstream target of GAP43.

Our experiments demonstrated that GAP43 knockdown downregulated RAC1 mRNA expression, indicating its potential role in the transcriptional regulation of RAC1 (Fig. 4A). To identify potential transcription factors mediating the regulatory relationship between GAP43 and RAC1, we first retrieved the list of proteins interacting with GAP43 from the STRING database (v12.0), while also predicting potential upstream transcriptional regulators of RAC1 using the hTFtarget database. After intersecting the two sets of results, the transcription factor CREB1 exhibited the highest combined interaction score with GAP43, indicating the strongest confidence in their interaction. Therefore, CREB1 was selected for further in-depth validation (Table S4). We confirmed the interaction between GAP43 and CREB1 by Co-IP, and overexpression of GAP43 significantly promoted the nuclear translocation of CREB1 (Fig. 4B, C). Dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that CREB1 overexpression markedly enhanced RAC1 promoter activity; similarly, GAP43 overexpression also promoted this activity, and a significant synergistic enhancement was observed upon their co-expression (Fig. 4D). ChIP assays further demonstrated that CREB1 was significantly enriched in the RAC1 promoter region, and this enrichment was further increased when GAP43 was co-expressed (Fig. 4E, F). Taken together, these results indicate that GAP43 positively regulates RAC1 transcription by binding to the transcription factor CREB1.

Knockdown of GAP43 inhibited tumor progression through RAC1

To investigate whether GAP43 affects tumor cell phenotypes by regulating downstream RAC1, we performed rescue experiments in LUAD cells by knocking down GAP43 and overexpressing RAC1. The results showed that knockdown of GAP43 significantly inhibited the expression of RAC1 at both mRNA and protein levels, while overexpression of RAC1 effectively reversed this inhibitory effect (Fig. 5A-F). The CCK-8 assay demonstrated that RAC1 overexpression significantly increased the viability of A549 and H1299 cells and effectively rescued the inhibitory effect of GAP43 knockdown on cell viability (Fig. 5G, H). Moreover, RAC1 overexpression reduced the apoptosis rate in A549 and NCI-H1299 cells and was able to counteract the pro-apoptotic effect induced by shGAP43 (Fig. 5I, J). In summary, GAP43 downregulation reduced RAC1 expression, promoting tumor cell apoptosis and decreasing cell viability, while RAC1 overexpression reversed these effects, which demonstrated that GAP43 regulated tumor cell phenotypes through RAC1.

GAP43 modulates aerobic glycolysis in tumors via RAC1

To investigate the effects of the GAP43 and RAC1 genes on glycolysis in tumor cells, we assessed relevant indicators, including glucose uptake, ATP levels, and lactic acid production, following GAP43 knockdown and RAC1 overexpression. In the shGAP43 group, glucose uptake (Fig. 6A), ATP production (Fig. 6C), and lactic acid production (Fig. 6E) were significantly lower than those in the control group of A549 cells. Similar findings were observed in NCI-H1299 cells (Fig. 6B, D and F). Furthermore, within a period of 60 min, the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in the shGAP43 group was significantly greater (Fig. 6G), whereas the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was notably lower than that in the control group (Fig. 6H). Western blot analysis revealed decreased expression of RAC1 and proteins associated with glycolysis (Fig. 6I).

To validate whether these effects could be reversed by RAC1, the RAC1 gene was overexpressed following GAP43 knockdown. GAP43 knockdown led to a significant decrease in glucose uptake, ATP production, and lactate production and resulted in a marked increase in the OCR and a significant decrease in the ECAR (Fig. 7A-H). These effects could be reversed by the overexpression of RAC1, leading to a recovery of glycolytic activity, a decrease in the OCR and an increase in the ECAR within tumor cells (Fig. 7A-H). In conclusion, our findings demonstrated that GAP43 knockdown decreases glycolytic activity in tumor cells, potentially contributing to the suppression of tumor growth and invasion. Conversely, RAC1 overexpression reversed this phenomenon by restoring the glycolytic capacity of tumor cells, highlighting the GAP43/RAC1 axis as a critical regulator of tumor metabolism.

GAP43 modulates tumor cell phenotypes via the regulation of glycolysis

To further investigate the relationship between the GAP43 gene and glycolysis, we examined the effects of GAP43 overexpression on glucose uptake, ATP production, lactate production, and cell viability. Compared with the control group, the GAP43 group presented a significant increase in glucose uptake capacity in both A549 and NCI-H1299 cells. Conversely, following the inhibition of glycolysis with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), the glucose uptake capacity was reduced in the GAP43 + 2-DG group, although it remained greater than that in the control group (Fig. 8A and B). Compared with the control group, the GAP43 group presented a markedly increased ATP production capacity, whereas ATP production was diminished in the GAP43 + 2-DG group (Fig. 8C and D). Furthermore, lactate production was significantly elevated following GAP43 overexpression and diminished in the GAP43 + 2-DG group (Fig. 8E and F). Cell viability assessment via a CCK-8 assay revealed that both cell lines presented significantly increased activity within the GAP43 groups compared with their respective controls, which could be diminished by 2-DG in the GAP43 + 2-DG group (Fig. 8G and H). These results suggest that the GAP43 affected the tumor cell phenotype by enhancing the glycolytic capacity of LUAD cells.

GAP43 knockdown suppresses xenograft tumor growth in vivo

To further verify the inhibitory effects of GAP43 knockdown on LUAD in vivo, we established a tumor-bearing nude mouse xenograft model via subcutaneous injection of A549 cells transfected with the shGAP43 vector or the empty vector. The results revealed that tumors derived from GAP43-knockdown cells were smaller than those derived from control cells (Fig. 9A). The body weights of all the mice showed negligible fluctuations, thus demonstrating the favorable biosafety of the intervention method (Fig. 9.B). In addition, the tumor weight was lower (P < 0.05) and the tumor volume was smaller (P < 0.005) in the shGAP43 group than in the shCtrl group (Fig. 9C, D). The results of the Western blot analysis using tumor tissues from xenografts revealed that the expression levels of GAP43, RAC1, HK2, GLUT1 and PKM2 were lower in the shGAP43 group than in the shCtrl group (Fig. 9E). Moreover, as illustrated in Fig. 9F, the lower Ki67 index in the shGAP43 group further confirmed the slower growth of tumors detected by IHC analysis, and lower expression levels of GAP43 and RAC1 were also validated. HE staining revealed more necrotic cells in the shGAP43 group than in the shCtrl group. These results corroborated the inhibitory effects of GAP43 knockdown on LUAD development in vivo.

Discussion

The significant role of GAP43 in the nervous system has been well established, and GAP43 is characterized as a “growth” or “plasticity” protein within axons and presynaptic terminals. It plays a crucial role in guiding neuronal growth and branching during neural development and axon regeneration. Altered expression of GAP43 has been demonstrated to have protective effects on neurite growth [16, 17]. In addition to its involvement in neuronal growth and regeneration, GAP43 also regulates cell signal transduction and migration. As research progresses, the pivotal role of GAP43 in cancer has gradually come to light. For instance, D.C.C. Watson et al. [12]. reported that GAP43 facilitates network connections between glioblastoma cells and astrocytes, leading to mitochondrial transfer that promotes glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) progression. Similarly, C. Zheng et al. [9]. reported significant expression levels of GAP43 in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), noting that knockdown of GAP43 in PTC cells markedly reduced cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion capabilities and induced apoptosis. Furthermore, GAP43 was found to regulate proteins associated with epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby influencing cellular invasion and migration. Studies related to lung cancer have shown that GAP43 serves as an independent predictor of brain metastasis survival (BMS) in NSCLC patients. It may promote metastasis through modulation of the Rac1/F-actin pathway [10]. In the current study, we revealed high expression levels of GAP43 in patients with LUAD, which correlated significantly with poor clinical outcomes. This finding underscores the potential of GAP43 as an independent prognostic marker for LUAD. Our experimental data further revealed that GAP43 knockdown substantially inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasive capabilities of LUAD cells, highlighting its therapeutic potential as a drug target for LUAD treatment.

RAC1 is a cytoskeletal regulatory protein that primarily modulates cell adhesion and motility by promoting actin cytoskeletal remodeling. RAC1 plays a crucial role in transmitting migration signals to the cytoskeleton [18], indicating that RAC1 has the capacity to influence tumor cell migration and invasion. L. Liu et al. [19]. reported that the activation of RAC1 enhances the migratory ability of H1299 NSCLC cells, whereas the inhibition of RAC1 diminishes this ability; additionally, p53 can activate RAC1 activity, thereby further increasing the migratory potential of H1299 cells. Depletion of TIAM1 or inhibition of RAC1 reduces both viability and tumorigenicity in SCLC cells by promoting apoptosis associated with the transformation of BCL2 from a prosurvival function to a proapoptotic function through BH3 domain exposure [20]. M. Chen et al. [21]. demonstrated the pivotal role of RAC1 in the metastasis of LUAD. High expression levels of RAC1 in tumor tissues are critical for processes involving adhesion, the extracellular matrix (ECM), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling pathways; conversely, inhibiting RAC1 leads to decreased proliferation, invasiveness, and migratory capabilities in lung cancer cells. As a member of the RHO family of GTPases, RAC1 also influences cellular energy metabolism. The initiation of sustained macrophage exocytosis mediated by MCTRs relies on alterations in Rac1 signaling as well as glycolytic metabolism. Inhibition of Rac1 abolishes the ability of MCTRs to increase glucose uptake and exocytosis in vitro; furthermore, suppression of glycolysis restricts MCTR-mediated endocytosis and tissue repair processes [22]. In addition, Rac1 activates aldolase A and the ERK signaling pathway, thereby upregulating glycolysis, particularly the nonoxidative pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) [23]. In this study, we identified RAC1 as a potential key downstream target of GAP43 that promotes glycolysis in LUAD cells. Further, we explored the mechanism by which GAP43 regulates RAC1 expression. The transcription factor CREB1 was found to be a key factor. GAP43 was able to promote CREB1 nuclear translocation, bind and activate the RAC1 promoter, and promote transcriptional up-regulation of RAC1 to regulate glycolytic flux.

After a cell transforms into a tumor cell, it undergoes metabolic alterations that enable it to produce more energy than normal cells do, thereby fulfilling its demands for rapid growth, proliferation, and migration. To satisfy these energy requirements, even in an aerobic environment, tumor cells exhibit a preference for glucose uptake and lactate secretion, a phenomenon known as aerobic glycolysis, which serves as the cornerstone of cancer cell metabolism. Cancer cells have developed various mechanisms to enhance this process [24]. The glycolysis process in lung cancer is characterized by a highly intricate system that is regulated by a multitude of proteins and enzymes. Glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) facilitates extracellular glucose uptake and is highly expressed in 99% of LUSC patients and 50% of adenocarcinoma patients. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), which is present in two subtypes, LDHA and LDHB, is responsible for the interconversion between pyruvate and lactic acid. A study conducted by H. Li and colleagues demonstrated that circular RNA can bind to LDHA, positively regulating both its activity and expression at the transcriptional level. This promotes glycolysis and the progression of NSCLC [25]. Pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), which is also highly expressed in NSCLC, functions alongside GLUT1 as a nuclear factor within the metabolic cycle, and Hexokinase-2 has been identified as an important metabolic marker indicative of NSCLC prognosis [26–28]. In this study, the levels of GLUT1, LDHA, PKM2, and HK2 were significantly reduced following the downregulation of GAP43. These findings indicate that low expression levels of GAP43 effectively inhibit tumor glycolysis. Conversely, overexpression of RAC1 resulted in elevated levels of these four glycolytic-related proteins, further substantiating the influence of GAP43 and RAC1 on glycolytic processes.

The role of RAC1 in LUAD and various other cancers has been well established [29, 30]. Consequently, targeting RAC1 may represent an effective therapeutic strategy, leading to the development of several RAC1 inhibitors for preliminary research. For example, the compound NSC23766, identified through structure-based virtual screening of compounds that fit Rac1 surface grooves, effectively inhibits Rac1 binding and activation via the RAC-specific guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) Trio and Tiam1 in a dose-dependent manner. This compound effectively blocks serum- or platelet-derived growth factor-induced Rac1 activation and lamellipodia formation. As a small molecule inhibitor specific to RAC, NSC23766 can be utilized to investigate the role of Rac in various cellular functions and has been implicated in human endothelial dysfunction [31, 32]. Through virtual library screening, researchers identified a novel Rac1 inhibitor, ZINC69391, which disrupts the interaction between Rac1 and GEF Tiam1 while preventing EGF-induced Rac1 activation. Additionally, it inhibits cell proliferation, migration, and cell cycle progression in highly aggressive breast cancer cell lines [33]. EHT1864 has high specificity for inhibiting RAC1 and directly affects the activation of its effectors p21-activated kinase (PAK) 1 and PAK2 [34]. Targeting the RAC1 protein itself poses challenges owing to its nanomolar affinity for GTP binding and lack of significant small-molecule docking pockets; thus, most inhibitors focus on upstream or downstream proteins for their effects. In this study, we found that GAP43 serves as an upstream regulator of RAC1 expression. Therefore, designing RAC1 inhibitors that target GAP43 may provide promising solutions.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the in vivo findings are based on a single animal model using female nude mice bearing xenografts of the human A549 lung adenocarcinoma cell line. The generalizability of these results to other models or contexts requires further validation. Second, while we demonstrated the roles of GAP43 and RAC1 in regulating glycolysis in vitro, the study did not include direct validation of their impact on glycolytic metabolism within the animal models. Addressing these aspects in future research will strengthen the translational relevance of our findings.

Conclusion

In this study, GAP43, a critical target gene in LUAD, was screened, and GAP43-knockdown cell lines were successfully established. Furthermore, RAC1, the downstream target of GAP43, was also identified. By assessing cellular glucose uptake, ATP levels, lactic acid production, the OCR and the ECAR, the GAP43 gene was shown to influence glycolysis through the regulation of RAC1 expression. Mechanistically, GAP43 regulated RAC1 transcription by binding to CREB1. This mechanism may reveal a novel therapeutic target and contribute to an improved prognosis for patients with LUAD.
