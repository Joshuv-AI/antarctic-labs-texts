Pharmaceutical design of mRNA vaccines for endemic infectious diseases: integrating antigen discovery with platform engineering
Authors: Shuaibu Abdullahi Hudu, Abdulgafar Olayiwola Jimoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endemic infections and enteric pathogens, among others, persist largely because of their untreatable nature outside the protection afforded by prior exposure, and, to a greater extent, denote control rather than elimination. New developments in antigen identification, structural vaccinology, and systems immunology make it now possible to rationally identify and optimize protective epitopes with a level of confidence that was not possible before. Meanwhile, technological advances in mRNA platform engineering have also promoted the development of scalable, programmable vaccines that can be rapidly modified, cost-effective, and expanded across diverse pathogen landscapes. Here, we provide an overview of progress at the intersection of antigen design and pharmaceutical formulation, exemplifying how sequence engineering, codon optimization, nucleoside modification, and optimized lipid nanoparticle delivery systems contribute to defining the potency, safety, and thermostability of mRNA vaccines. New leading candidates for regionally endemic pathogens are discussed with a focus on matching the immunogen to population-level genetic diversity of hosts, variable antigenicity, and epidemiological patterns. These advancements demonstrate that combining precision antigen identification with mRNA platform technologies may enable rapid development of vaccines appropriate for diseases endemic in relevant settings. Ongoing advancement depends on aligning scientific novelty with manufacturability, access for all, and region-specific public health priorities.

INTRODUCTION

Endemic infectious diseases remain a significant and disproportionate burden on global health, particularly in Africa, Asia, and Latin America, where ecological, socioeconomic, and health system factors maintain endemic transmission cycles [1]. Malaria, dengue, chikungunya, Lassa fever, and Middle East respiratory syndrome, as well as tuberculosis (TB) and enteric bacterial infections in the millions of cases per year, are causes of illness and death every year despite decades of control efforts [234567]. Despite being the most successful public-health intervention for infectious diseases, vaccine development has not been uniform. Vaccines against pathogens such as measles or polio have fundamentally altered disease landscapes, yet many regionally endemic pathogens still lack effective, scalable, or durable vaccine solutions [8]. The inability of many classic vaccine strategies to achieve adequate efficacy in endemic settings, where the diversity of pathogens, reinfection pressure, and host variables makes immunity more complex, highlights a pressing need to reconsider traditional dogmas of vaccinology. The discipline of vaccine microbiology has seen dramatic growth over the last 2 decades, driven by genomics, systems biology, immunology, and molecular engineering. Conventional vaccines were generated empirically based on attenuation, inactivation, or recombinant adsorption of immunodominant proteins, without detailed knowledge of pathogen biology and host immune correlates of protection [9]. This empirical approach is inadequate for complex microbes such as parasites, viruses with many antigens, or intracellular bacteria that require more nuanced immune responses. Modern vaccine science is moving towards an antigen-directed, rational discovery of microbial correlates of protective immunity, in which genomic determinants of the host response to antigens can be analytically resolved using integrated computational and experimental assays [10]. This paradigm of discovery, exemplified by reverse vaccinology, structural vaccinology, and multi-omics–driven antigen discovery, has now empowered the systematic prediction, mapping, and engineering of immunogens with a precision never before possible.

Genomic sequencing has created a vast field of potential microbial antigens, and it is now possible to quickly scan pathogen genomes for surface-exposed, conserved proteins essential for function, even in uncultivable organisms [1112]. This approach has led to licensed vaccines, such as the Neisseria meningitidis serogroup B vaccine, which validate genome-guided antigen prediction [13]. Proteomic and immunoproteomic approaches narrow down the list of antigens by revealing antigens to which the local host immune response is directed in natural infection. At the same time, structural vaccinology facilitates stabilization of important epitopes in their most immunogenic states-an invaluable gain in cases where functionally relevant surface proteins may be conformationally labile [1415]. Antigen discovery increasingly relies on systems immunology, which extrapolates transcriptomic, metabolomic, and cellular immune signatures to characterize correlates of protection and to predict favorable immunogen compositions for complex pathogens [16]. These technologies have collectively revolutionized the discovery, characterization, and design of microbial antigens as candidate vaccine targets. Concurrently with new methods for antigen discovery, we have seen the emergence of a nucleic acid-based vaccine platform that has fundamentally changed the pace at which vaccines can be designed and deployed in outbreak settings. Of these platforms, mRNA vaccines have been considered the most revolutionary technology of the past decade. The idea of using mRNA vaccines has existed for over 30 years, and the process has accelerated significantly in recent years with advances in RNA stability, codon optimization, nucleoside modification, and lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations [17]. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) mRNA vaccines have shown unprecedented efficacy, rapid adaptation to a changing virus, and, although temporarily strained by an increasing number of vaccinated people, are easily scalable for mass vaccination [1819]. The success of these vaccines has led to an enormous investment in this technology, bringing mRNA platforms to applications against a wide variety of pathogens of regional and endemic importance that have historically had low commercial appeal.

mRNA-based platforms have several benefits in vaccine development for endemic diseases. First, they enable fast ‘design-to-vial’ timescales: a representative mRNA construct can be produced in days after the identification of an antigen, providing agility to respond to outbreaks or new variants [20]. Secondly, mRNA expression of complex or hard-to-produce antigens, such as membrane proteins, multi-epitope constructs, or antigens requiring sophisticated PTM, has previously made subunit vaccine development for parasites and intracellular bacteria challenging [21]. Third, it is possible to design mRNA vaccines as multivalent formulations or to express them in a self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) format, which could increase their immunogenicity and reduce the amount that needs to be administered, a feature of particular relevance for optimization during large-scale deployment in low-resource settings [22]. The built-in versatility of the platform provides unparalleled opportunities for antigenically diverse pathogens, such as dengue, malaria, and arenaviruses, by encoding either consensus or mosaic sequences that can provide broad immune coverage [23]. For endemic pathogens, the combination of robust antigen-discovery pipelines with mRNA vaccine platforms is a transformative moment for science and public health. For some regionally important pathogens, it is now possible to identify well-characterised antigenic targets from either genomics or structural studies, including pre-fusion stabilised envelope proteins for flaviviruses, glycoproteins for Lassa virus, and essential secretory or surface antigens for malaria and TB [1724]. These antigens can be efficiently encoded in optimized mRNA constructs, facilitating iterative, data-driven updates guided by immunogenicity and real-world surveillance. This meeting of precision immunogen design and programmable vaccine platforms could open the door to successful vaccination against pathogens that have evaded classical vaccinology for generations.

While a new, improved seed is an essential part of the solution, significant challenges persist. Endemic areas frequently have complex epidemiological profiles, including co-circulating pathogens, diverse human leukocyte antigen (HLA) backgrounds, malnutrition, chronic coinfections, and environmental pressures, which may also dampen or bias vaccine responses [2526]. Cold-chain logistics requirements, manufacturing disparities, and regulatory obstacles pose further challenges to the deployment of mRNA vaccines in resource-poor regions [27]. Furthermore, correlates of protection remain ill-defined for many endemic pathogens, while antigenic diversity poses challenges for pathogens such as dengue virus (DENV), Plasmodium falciparum, and several bacteria [28]. Ethical issues, community involvement, and building trust are essential when introducing a new technology, especially in areas that have historically seen fewer global-led vaccine efforts [29]. This manuscript aims to review recent developments in microbial antigen identification and mRNA vaccine design, with an emphasis on locally prevalent pathogens. It reviews the science, enabling technologies, translation considerations, and delivery windows for next-generation vaccines for diseases of continued regional relevance. By combining lessons learned from genomics, structural biology, immunology, and molecular engineering, this review demonstrates how the fusion of antigen discovery and mRNA platform technologies is reshaping vaccine microbiology and guiding a future of equitable, responsive, and efficacious protection against endemic infectious diseases.

METHODOLOGY

This narrative review compiles evidence on antigen discovery, mRNA vaccines, and pharmaceutical formulation strategies for endemic infectious diseases, with the aid of a layout. The literature search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar for articles published from January 2010 to December 2025. The search terms used in the databases were along the lines of “mRNA vaccine” and combinations thereof with “self-amplifying RNA,” “lipid nanoparticles,” “antigen discovery,” “structural vaccinology,” “systems immunology,” “endemic diseases,” “thermostability,” “vaccine formulation,” “vaccine delivery systems.” Other sources included World Health Organization situation reports, regulatory guidelines, and relevant preprints from bioRxiv and medRxiv when peer-reviewed data were not available. Relevant literature included peer-reviewed articles, original research, clinical studies, reviews, regulatory resources, and technology reports in the field of mRNA platform engineering or antigen discovery against endemic pathogens. Publications in languages other than English that were unclear regarding methods or otherwise irrelevant to vaccine design and pharmaceutical science were excluded. The reference lists of key articles were also reviewed to supplement relevant studies. In light of the breadth and emerging nature of mRNA vaccine technology, this review focuses on recent evidence, mechanistic studies, and platform advances most directly relevant to vaccine design, stability, and deployment in endemic settings. Since this is a narrative review, there was no formal quality scoring or meta-analysis.

MICROBIAL ANTIGEN DISCOVERY IN THE GENOMICS ERA

Over the past 2 decades, there has been a revolution in microbial antigen discovery, underpinned by recent developments in genomics, proteomics, structural biology, and systems immunology. Conventional antigen discovery was based mainly on culture-dependent approaches, serology, and empirical selection of immunodominant proteins, but these methods were intrinsically limited for genetically diverse, non-cultivable, or immunologically complex pathogens [3031]. Now, in an era of whole-genome sequencing and high-throughput bioinformatics, antigen discovery has shifted from empirical screening to a rational, data-driven selection of immunogens with a high probability of inducing protective immunity [30].

Reverse vaccinology is the key strategic innovation in this shift. By studying whole-pathogen genomes, essential proteins conserved across viruses, bacteria, and parasites that can be expressed without culture have been discovered at an accelerated pace [32]. This approach is the basis of the first registered genome-derived vaccine, 4CMenB, against Neisseria meningitidis serogroup B, demonstrating its capacity to generate orthogonal multicomponent vaccines that traditional methods did not produce [33]. And reverse vaccinology 2.0, which capitalizes on human immunology-derived data sets, such as epitope repertoires and antibody ontogenies, has further refined the process through antigen prioritization, enabling the design of antigens that more closely mimic protective immune signatures [34].

Proteomic and immunoproteomic techniques parallel genomic discovery by identifying pathogen proteins with immunogenic potential, either in natural infection or in an experimental challenge model. Surface-shaving proteomics, secretome mapping, and convalescent-sera profile analysis can help identify antigens that the immune system is effectively responding to, at least for parasites with complex life cycles, such as malaria, leishmaniasis, and TB [353637]. They are good tools, as they connect predicted antigens to host-pathogen interactions and enhance their biological relevance.

More recently, structural vaccinology has added another dimension by providing information on the 3-dimensional conformation of microbial antigens and enabling the rational design of epitope-based vaccines [38]. Stabilization of an antigen in its pre-fusion conformation, as for flaviviral envelope antigens or coronavirus spike proteins, preserves neutralization-susceptible epitopes and increases its immunogenicity [3940]. Structure-guided design can also be used to build mosaic, consensus, or chimeric antigens to increase coverage of antigenically diverse pathogens such as dengue, influenza, and P. falciparum [4041].

The cross-product of multi-omics and systems immunology is the marriage of genomic, transcriptomic, metabolomic, and single-cell immune datasets to identify correlates of protection, inform antigen choice, and predict vaccine response [42]. System signatures arising from human challenge models or naturally infected cohorts can identify immunological pathways associated with protection, guiding the selection of antigens that induce functional, durable immunity in endemic populations. They are beneficial for pathogens for which the correlates of protection are not well established, including TB, Lassa virus, and many enteric bacteria. The integration of these technologies results in a new antigen identification approach that is systematic, scalable, and amenable to pathogens of regional significance. IMMUNOLOGICAL PRINCIPLES GUIDING ANTIGEN DESIGN

The development of efficacious vaccine antigens depends on a thorough understanding of the mechanisms by which the innate and adaptive immune systems recognize, process, and respond to microbial components. Immunological concepts direct efforts in antigen selection, epitope conservation, structural stabilization, and platform engineering when it comes to endemic pathogens, in which the quality -not only quantity of- the immune response dictates protective efficacy. These principles have been essential for integrating rational antigen design into next-generation tools, such as mRNA vaccines [5051]. One of the main determinants of vaccine efficacy resides in the early activation of the innate immune system, which, in turn, conditions subsequent adaptive responses. Pattern recognition receptors, such as Toll-like receptors, RIG-I–like receptors, and cytoplasmic RNA sensors, are essential for bridging antigen encounter with dendritic cell maturation, antigen processing, and T-cell priming [5253]. For mRNA vaccines, both the RNA itself and the delivery system can act as adjuvants by activating these pathways. Still, excessive triggering can result in either overt reactogenicity or impaired translation of the encoded antigen. Progresses, such as nucleoside modification or fine-tuning of LNPs, minimize the risk of innate activation, providing a reasonable compromise between immune stimulation and tolerability [5455]. These principles may be especially important for endemic pathogens that depend on robust cellular immunity, such as TB and Lassa virus; however, they may also help prevent excessive inflammation that can increase disease risk or reduce vaccine uptake [5657].

The adaptive immune response, which is both humoral and cellular, has a central role in antigen design. For many viruses, including flaviviruses [24], coronaviruses, and some respiratory viruses, neutralizing antibodies are the primary correlate of protection [58]. For these pathogens, antigen design is guided by the need to maintain epitope conformation, particularly those that are accessible in pre-fusion forms and can induce strong neutralization responses. Structural vaccinology has shown that stabilizing antigens in conformation-dependent forms is more beneficial for boosting both the quality and breadth of an antibody response, for example, for the pre-fusion respiratory syncytial virus F protein, coronavirus spike proteins [59], and engineered flavivirus envelope proteins [60] for antigenically variable pathogens, such as dengue and influenza. Consensus or mosaic antigen construction increases cross-reactivity by representing conserved epitopes across serotypes or strains [61]. In contrast, pathogens that establish intracellular persistence, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Plasmodium spp., Leishmania, and some arenaviruses, depend on strong T-cell immunity for optimal protection. It has been shown that CD4+ Th1 responses support macrophage activation and intracellular killing.

In comparison, CD8+ T cell-mediated responses are involved in the clearance of infected cells and in controlling viremia in acute infections such as Lassa fever [626364]. Indeed, antigen design for these pathogens should include epitopes that are successfully processed and presented through the major histocompatibility complex class I and class II pathways. mRNA platforms are ideally suited to eliciting these immune responses because intracellular expression of the antigens directly activates the cross-presentation pathway, resulting in powerful cytotoxic T lymphocyte priming [6566]. Systems immunology analyses also help prioritize antigens by showing immune signatures associated with controlled infection or natural immunity and by guiding the choice of antigens that may drive long-lasting, high-quality T cell responses in endemic populations [67]. The variation in antigens also poses a massive obstacle in endemic settings. High levels of mutation, recombination, or serotype diversity can all interfere with vaccine-elicited immunity. Pathogens such as the DENV, P. falciparum, and rapidly evolving RNA viruses provide insight into the complexity of designing antigens that span the breadth of deletomes [6869]. Rational design strategies, therefore, focus on targeting conserved functional epitopes, producing multivalent constructs, and or co-expressing more than one antigen across life-cycle stages or serotypes. Genomic surveillance information, increasingly available in endemic areas, now permits real-time tracking of circulating variants and, with it, the potential to update the antigen for pr mosaic antigen redesign, analogous to seasonal influenza vaccine optimization [7071]. mRNA vaccines provide the additional advantage of rapid iteration; with the identification of new antigenic targets, updated constructs can be produced within days, providing unparalleled agility for controlling endemic disease [72].

An important yet often independently unappreciated variable shaping antigen design is population-level immunogenetics. HLA diversity affects epitope presentation, T cell recognition, and, in general, vaccine responsiveness. The high diversity of certain regions may lead to different protective responses against the same antigen, making it challenging to design a universal vaccine [73]. Methods for computational epitope mapping already enable predictions of antigen coverage across the HLA haplotypes that are most common in particular parts of the world, and these can be used, together with peptides selected to include polymorphisms present within them, to make regionally optimized immunogens. These considerations are more important in Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia, where HLA diversity is high and further amplified by the presence of co-infections, particularly human immunodeficiency virus or chronic helminthiasis, which may affect immune responses [7475]. Collectively, these immunological principles highlight that antigen design is not a matter of picking what we think are good immunodominant proteins, but a complex process of manipulating quality versus quantity, potential, and specificity and breadth. For endemic diseases where pathogen diversity, immune modulation, and population-level heterogeneity are formidable barriers, such precision is necessary. The intersection of structural biology, systems immunology, and nucleic acid platforms has ushered in a new era in which antigens are not designed solely to promote immunity but rather to command protective, durable, and population-specific immune responses. Such an immunologically driven antigen design approach is the basis for the development of next-generation vaccines, notably mRNA-based platforms, for indigenous pathogens that remain a threat to global health.

mRNA VACCINE PLATFORMS FROM CONCEPT TO CLINICAL REALITY

This technological shift has been enabled by decades of work in RNA chemistry, sequence design, and nanoparticle engineering [76]. Initial hurdles such as RNA instability, poor translational potential, and excessive innate immune stimulation have been overcome using nucleoside modifications, codon optimization, lenient untranslated regions (UTRs), and LNP formulations, which improve cytosolic delivery [77]. These scientific achievements led to the remarkable success of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, which demonstrated high efficacy and global deployability, a level never before seen for any new vaccine platform [78]. There are 2 chief architectures on which current mRNA technologies are based: conventional non-replicating mRNA and saRNA. Traditional mRNA contains only the antigen of interest and, in contrast to saRNA, lacks the complete replicase machinery needed to replicate the RNA template within host cells, generating more transcripts than are initially present from a small input dose [17]. Engineering advancements have also broadened the applicability of mRNA platforms. Nucleoside-modified RNA decreases innate RNA-sensor TLR7 and RIG-I recognition, facilitating protein translation and reducing excessive reactogenicity [80]. Innovations such as circular RNAs, sequence-controlled UTRs, and trans-amplifying RNA designs aim to prolong expression and improve the stability of the expressed antigen to enable longer-lasting immunity from lower doses [81]. These characteristics are beneficial for vaccines targeting intracellular pathogens, such as TB, Lassa virus, and Plasmodium spp., which require strong Th1-biased/CD8+ T-cell responses [8283]. LNPs remain key to efficient mRNA delivery. The ability to shield RNA from degradation, mediate cell internalization, and release the RNA contents into the cytoplasm is responsible for both the immunogenicity and the safety profile of the platform [84]. Rational optimization of ionizable lipids, helper phospholipids, and PEG-lipids has generated LNP formulations with increased tolerability, improved antigen expression, and potential attributes for mucosal or intradermal use, particularly pertinent to endemic respiratory or vector-borne threats where barrier-site immunity is required [85]. Work is underway to develop thermostable or lyophilized formulations of LNP to mitigate a deployment concern in low-resource settings.

The success of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines provided real-world validation of the platform’s promise. High titers of neutralizing antibodies, robust CD4+ and/or CD8+ T-cell responses, and swift post-vaccination protection revealed the flexibility and efficacy of mRNA vaccine immunogens [86]. As importantly, the platform allowed for near-real-time antigenic updates in response to viral evolution, a capability with profound implications for endemic pathogens with high antigenic variation, such as DENV, Chikungunya virus, Lassa fever virus, or P. falciparum [87]. The fusion of genomic surveillance and mRNA design forms a new mode of adaptable “variant-responsive” vaccinology that may ultimately overcome the long-entrenched barriers posed by rapidly evolving regional pathogens.

Comparative analyses highlight several benefits of mRNA vaccines compared to conventional platforms. In contrast to viruses, there are no anti-vector immunity issues with mRNA, and it does not integrate into the host genome. Unlike protein subunit vaccines, mRNA allows antigens to be encoded endogenously and expressed with folding, multimerization, or complex post-translational modifications. These features are prerequisites for many viral and parasitic immunogens [88]. Furthermore, the platform readily accommodates multivalent and multi-antigen co-expression, which could help tailor immune responses directed towards complex infections, such as those caused by Plasmodium species and dengue. However, several hurdles must be overcome before mRNA vaccines can realize their full potential for use in endemic diseases. Cold-chain logistics remain a challenge in remote areas, but RNA formulations are being developed rapidly [89]. Endemic pathogens often lack clearly defined correlates of protection, which makes the selection of antigens, the definition of endpoints, and the design of clinical trials difficult [90]. Host factors such as malnutrition, chronic helminth infections, high pathogen exposure, and diverse HLA backgrounds may influence vaccine responses and require country-specific consideration in immunogenicity assessment [2691].

mRNA VACCINES FOR REGIONALLY ENDEMIC PATHOGENS

The application of mRNA vaccine technology to regionally endemic pathogens has accelerated since the post-COVID-19 era, fueled by platform flexibility, speed in antigen design, and the ability to express structurally optimized immunogens. Hundreds of viral, parasitic, and bacterial pathogens that preferentially affect people living in poverty are being targeted by preclinical or early clinical mRNA vaccines, a far cry from the state of innovation pre-COVID-19. A summary of these candidates is presented in Table 3, which demonstrates the variety of pathogens and antigenic targets for which studies are now underway. Some endemic viral pathogens are ideal targets for mRNA-based vaccines. DENV, which has 4 antigenically distinct serotypes, has long been a challenge for traditional vaccine methods due to the risk of antibody-dependent enhancement. mRNA platforms facilitate the expression of structurally stabilized or mosaic envelope proteins to generate serotype-wide immunity [92]. Chikungunya virus has gone one step further with envelope glycoprotein mRNA vaccines producing robust neutralizing antibody responses in early-phase trials, suggesting they could be used in parts of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia prone to outbreaks [93]. Zika virus mRNA vaccines have also advanced to phase I trials based on high-resolution flavivirus structural biology, leading to the production of high-fidelity pre-fusion antigens [94]. Among the viral hemorrhagic fevers, Lassa virus would seem to be an ideal candidate for mRNA technology. Its glycoprotein complex and nucleoprotein are well-defined via antigen-discovery pipelines [57] and are therefore excellent candidates for both conventional and Self-Amplifying mRNA constructs. With Lassa virus lineages circulating across West Africa in a regionally variable manner, the rapid update functionality of mRNA vaccines is also advantageous for protecting lineage-specific variants.

As far as parasitic diseases are concerned, malaria has a significant global health burden, and mRNA vaccines have unique potential. The intricate life cycle of the parasite Plasmodium demands multi-stage and multi-epitope immune targeting—a strategy most adapted to mRNA’s assembly-line structure. In early experiments, mRNA vaccine constructs encoding circumsporozoite protein, thrombospondin-related adhesion protein, and liver- or blood-stage antigens have been reported to be immunogenic [102]. The capacity of mRNA vaccines to induce potent CD8+ T cells is significant for malaria, where cytotoxic T-cell function adds to the elimination of liver-stage parasites. Progress is earlier in the case of bacterial pathogens common to endemic areas, but conceptually sound. Multiple candidate TB mRNA vaccines express antigens, including ESAT-6, Ag85B, and PPE proteins, targeting the intracellular antigen-processing pathway to induce strong CD8+ and Th1 responses [98]. As TB remains a significant infectious threat in Africa and South Asia, mRNA vaccines are being considered either as boosters for BCG or as standalone constructs. Pilot studies are also in progress for enteric pathogens, based on OM proteins or secreted virulence factors identified through the reverse vaccinology approach [103].

The growing collection of endemic-pathogen mRNA vaccines illustrates the potent combination of antigen-discovery approaches and nucleic acid delivery. Structural vaccinology can inform the design of pre-fusion antigens for flaviviruses; whilst reverse vaccinology identifies conserved outer-membrane targets for enteric bacterial systems, immunology will contribute to antigen selection for intracellular pathogens such as malaria and TB [104]. The translational platform for these rationally designed immunogens is mRNA. Nonetheless, a successful application in endemic settings needs careful consideration of the immunogenetics, comorbidities, and environmental factors. High HLA diversity, the large number of coinfections, malnutrition, and variable exposure intensity may affect vaccine responsiveness; therefore, immunogenicity studies should be tailored [74]. Logistical barriers, such as the need for a cold chain and constrained regional manufacturing capacity, persist but are expected to be addressed through advances in thermostable formulations and nascent African and Middle Eastern mRNA-manufacturing hubs [105].

TAILORING ANTIGEN DESIGN AND mRNA PLATFORMS TO ENDEMIC SETTINGS

For mRNA vaccines to be effective against regionally endemic diseases, more than just antigen selection will be needed. Endemic regions present specific methodological challenges, including diverse population-level immunity, heterogeneity across populations within an area, and limited to no health infrastructure. Developing effective vaccines in these settings requires a delicate balance between immunological finesse and practical programmatic considerations. Vaccines must not only induce durable, protective immune responses but also be manufacturable, storable, and deliverable under real-world limitations. An essential factor in a vaccine's efficacy in an endemic area is population immunogenetics, in which HLA haplotype diversity plays a key role. HLA molecules present pathogen-derived peptides to T cells, and population-level differences, especially in Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, and Latin America, affect not only which epitopes are recognized but also the magnitude of immune responses [106]. For pathogens that require potent cellular immunity, such as M. tuberculosis, Plasmodium spp., and Lassa virus, epitope constructs must contain epitopes that can be immunoprecipitously presented on major regional HLA types. Computational tools for epitope prediction enable the design of multi-epitope or mosaic mRNA constructs tailored to population-level coverage [107]. This accuracy is crucial when considering areas where genetic diversity, co-infections, and environmental pressures could otherwise reduce vaccine efficacy.

In addition to genetics, the host’s immune status is a key determinant of vaccine response in an endemic setting. Infections with chronic pathogens, repeated exposure to malaria, and micronutrient deficiencies can skew immune polarization, cause exhaustion, or suppress adaptive immunity [108]. This may require personalized dosing, the addition of saRNA to augment antigen expression, and the inclusion of immune-modulatory sequences to support a durable Th1/CD8+ T-cell response [65]. Hence, vaccine candidates under development are increasingly informed by system immunology studies in endemic populations that identify immune signatures of natural protection or vaccine nonresponse [42]. Spatial aggregation, seasonal fluctuations, and lineage-biased evolution are characteristics of endemic pathogens. Genomic surveillance has also demonstrated geographical diversification of Lassa virus, dengue genotypes, P. falciparum variants, and bacterial lineages, which directly impact antigenic repertoires and potential vaccine escape [70]. The versatility of mRNA platforms enables nimble antigen updates to circulating variants, similar in concept to seasonal influenza vaccine strategies, albeit with a more rapid pace of revision. For vector-borne diseases, vaccination may be optimally administered at the time of peak transmission, and multivalent constructs can simultaneously address co-circulating serotypes or strains. Existing mRNA-LNP vaccines require ultracold-chain storage, which is difficult in underserved or remote areas. Technological advances in thermostable lyophilized vaccine formulations, alternative delivery routes (intradermal, mucosal), and the adoption of decentralized approaches are essential for global coverage. Regional mRNA production centres, as is happening in Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, will enable the incorporation of local antigens based on real-time monitoring while minimizing reliance on the supply chain [109].

REGULATORY, ETHICAL, AND IMPLEMENTATION CHALLENGES

These regulatory hurdles span licensure pathways, harmonization of regional guidelines, and post-approval pharmacovigilance. Though the expedited COVID-19 mRNA vaccine approvals exemplified the flexibility of regulatory practice in a global emergency, endemic diseases typically do not enjoy the same political urgency, financial investment, and coordinated infrastructure. Success in these efforts will rely on aligned governance, community-focused ethics, and implementation strategies to deliver practical interventions in resource-constrained settings. However, regulation is still fragmented for mRNA vaccines against endemic pathogens, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In contrast to the emergency pathways invoked during the COVID-19 pandemic, standard accelerated approval procedures do not apply to many endemic diseases, such as Lassa fever, dengue, and malaria [110]. Several realities preclude LMICs from having overarching guidance on genetic vaccine platforms, thereby hindering the rapid approval of such platforms and discouraging investment in clinical trials. Regional regulatory capacity, along with efforts towards harmonized review processes, will be key in this regard, facilitating assessments and approvals by reducing duplicative work and accelerating access [111].

Use of mRNA vaccines in endemic settings must be built on an ethical foundation of prefatory community engagement and fair benefit sharing. A history of exploitative research or insufficient post-trial benefits has undermined trust among some communities and may limit vaccine adoption [112]. Ethical development will also require open, culturally sensitive informed consent mechanisms, genuine community representation in vaccine trial design and implementation, assurance that successful vaccines will be available post-trial to participants, and responsible care of genomic data used to design antigens, with respect for privacy and data sovereignty. Forming lasting partnerships with local researchers, health workers, and community leaders is crucial for building trust and making vaccines seem credible, safe, and relevant to local people. Existing mRNA-LNP vaccines require stable ultra-cold storage, which is difficult to achieve in areas with unreliable electricity and poor cold-chain capacity [113]. Thermostable lyophilized formulations, new lipid compositions, and alternative delivery methods are under active development to reduce reliance on the cold chain and facilitate administration in remote areas.

Centralized production on a global scale may not be effective, as we have seen during the COVID-19 crisis. Localised manufacturing of mRNA vaccines, for example, in emerging regional hubs in Africa, Latin America, and Southeast Asia, would promote supply independence, allowing rapid local updates in response to antigenic drift and removing logistics bottlenecks [114]. This will require workforce development, technology transfer, and sustainable financial models that will enable such hubs. Misinformation, or historical fears and more recent anxieties about genetic technologies, may lead to vaccine hesitancy in some areas where the disease is endemic. Adopting targeted communication approaches, working with community leaders, and incorporating vaccination into established health services may enhance acceptance and coverage [115]. Tackling these challenges requires an interconnected strategy of regulatory convergence, ethical governance, and practical delivery mechanisms. Through the early integration of implementation into vaccine development, facilitated by partnerships in endemic regions, such as operationalizable and ethically acceptable mRNA-based vaccines for the world’s most neglected diseases, there is a promise of scientific robustness.

EXPERT OPINION AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Precision antigen discovery and mRNA vaccine technology have come together in a breakthrough in vaccine microbiology. Still, the efficient translation of these advances into sustained protection from endemic diseases will depend on how systems for science, policy, and public health interact over the next decade. The scientific basis for next-generation antigen design has taken a great leap forward, as has the flexibility of mRNA delivery platforms; however, their impact will be informed by how these technologies can be tailored to the biological, epidemiological, and socio-structural contexts in which infectious diseases are burdensome. And in that sense, the miracle of mRNA vaccines transcends science at the molecular level; it is the promise of a reimagined global vaccine development ecosystem that will be fairer, faster, and as local as possible. A guiding revelation over the past several years was that mRNA vaccines excel in conjunction with high-resolution antigen discovery. Technologies like reverse vaccinology, structural vaccinology, and systems immunology are now yielding antigens that are less widely generalizable to the population but more relevant based on biological and immunological mimicry. The ability to encode pre-fusion-stabilized viral proteins, multi-epitope constructs, lineage-specific immunogens, or multi-stage parasite antigens enables mRNA vaccines to engage immune mechanisms optimized for each pathogen's biology. This synergy is especially potent for diseases such as dengue, Lassa fever, malaria, and TB, for which longstanding challenges have hindered successful vaccine development. With the continued maturation of virus-neutralizing epitope structural mapping and advances in artificial intelligence-guided antigen prediction, vaccine microbiology may move towards iterative cycles of targeted antigen refinement driven by genomic surveillance and population immunology, a far cry from the static vaccine paradigms observed over the past century.

However, even as this science gains momentum, the road to real-world impact is uneven. Endemic areas display unique immunological profiles characterized by persistent infections, malnutrition, high pathogen load, and high HLA polymorphism. These factors can affect vaccine responsiveness, and preclinical models and human immunogenicity studies should be conducted in regions to confirm performance. RNA vaccines for such endemic pathogens are likely to require differentiated vaccination strategies, together with modifications to LNP formulation composition and structure, or to be made based on saRNA per se, which critically depend on the balance between dose and immunogenicity in a single vaccine format across different scenarios. Critical to the realization of systems immunology across African, Asian, and Latin American settings will be identifying correlates of protection that align with regional physiology rather than relying on assumptions derived from high-income countries.

Duration of immunity remains a critical question for endemic pathogens. With this mRNA vaccine, the strong response occurs early, and the duration of antibody persistence and the maintenance of memory T cells depend on both the antigen and its population. For diseases with year-round transmission or high reinfection pressure, such durabilities need to approach an induction threshold that reduces incidence sufficiently without frequent boosting, a hurdle that could lead to greater interest in saRNA and circular RNA, the emergence of unconventional adjuvanting strategies, and so forth. The incorporation of real-time genomic-surveillance-based antigen-update systems, somewhat akin to the seasonal influenza structure but much more agile, is likely to be a hallmark of future endemic-disease vaccination.

Decentralization of vaccine production is a pressing need for the future. The COVID-19 epidemic revealed deep inequities in access to global vaccines, bringing home the vulnerability of regions that are dependent on imported products. Organizing “distributed vaccinology,” in which regional mRNA manufacturing sites produce vaccines for locally circulating pathogens, is a paradigm shift that could address long-standing access inequities. In Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, such hubs could quickly translate genomic surveillance data into region-specific mRNA immunogens that traditional vaccines cannot match in their nimbleness. Implementation of this vision would, however, necessitate ongoing investment in workforce development, harmonization of regulations, and equitable technology transfer. How ethics and equity could make or break the next phase of mRNA vaccine adoption, as much as scientific progress. In numerous endemic areas, resistance to vaccination is not based on questioning immunization itself but stems from historical suspicion, misinformation, or prior encounters with foreign health interventions. For mRNA vaccines to be seen as credible, safe, and locally advantageous, they will require open communication, culturally oriented forums of engagement, and ongoing working relationships with indigenous researchers, clinicians, and community leaders. Insofar as genomic-empowered antigen design becomes integral to vaccinology, stewardship of pathogen genomic information, including questions of ownership, sovereignty, and benefit sharing, must be considered within the context of just governance mechanisms.

CONCLUSION

The intersection of precision antigen discovery and mRNA platform optimization presents a robust path to creating vaccines against endemic diseases. Decades of structural and systems vaccinology now enable guided epitope selection, mRNA sequence optimization, LNP formulation changes, and increased thermostability to drive improvements in potency, safety, and deployability in resource-limited settings. Distinguishing these pharmaceutical properties according to regional immunogenetic diversity, transmission dynamics, and health systems bottlenecks is necessary to elaborate context-specific vaccines. Sustainable progress will require reinforcement of regulatory harmonization, development of regional manufacturing capacity, and implementation of adaptive clinical-evaluation systems. Taken together, these new developments position mRNA vaccines as a sufficiently flexible platform for deployment against sustained endemic threats, assuming that fair implementation strategies at the local and regional scales support scientific advances.
