What factors contribute to delays in first versus non-first case operating room starts for patients undergoing elective anterior cervical discectomy and fusion?
Authors: Sean Inzerillo, Pemla Jagtiani, Salazar Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a common spine procedure with standardized steps, making it well-suited for examining inefficiencies in the operating room (OR). This study evaluated the causes and durations of surgical delays in elective ACDFs at a single academic center, focusing on differences between first and non-first case starts.Methods: We retrospectively reviewed 385 elective ACDF procedures performed from 2021 to 2022. Surgical delays were defined as cases that started more than 10 min late. Delay causes were extracted from OR records and categorized into operational themes. First versus non-first case starts were compared using descriptive statistics, Chi-square testing, and Welch’s t-tests.Results: Surgical delays occurred in 48.8% of cases, most commonly due to prior case delays (46.3%, mean delay 121.9 min) and preoperative team coordination issues (25.5%, mean delay 43.1 min). First case starts experienced significantly fewer delays (24.2%) than non-first case starts (85.7%, P < 0.001). Among delayed cases, non-first starts had significantly longer average delay durations than first starts (103.6 vs. 32.2 min; P < 0.001) and were associated with greater staffing levels, including more registered nurse (RN) staff (5.45 vs. 4.84; P = 0.005) and anesthesia providers (2.70 vs. 1.89; P < 0.001). First case delays reflected preoperative coordination inefficiencies, while later delays were driven by upstream overruns.Conclusions: This study underscores a clear first case advantage in elective ACDFs, with earlier starts experiencing fewer and shorter delays. Targeted measures including preoperative planning, turnover optimization, and surgeon engagement may enhance OR efficiency.

INTRODUCTION

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is one of the most common spinal surgical procedures.[6] Because it involves fluoroscopy, neurophysiologic monitoring, and spinal instrumentation, ACDF serves as an ideal model for evaluating surgical, operational, and institutional inefficiencies.[4] Recent studies have shown that first case start times, case delays, and the day of the week significantly influence operating room (OR) efficiency, and critically influence patient outcomes, and resource utilization.[4,7] In academic centers, OR efficiency also directly impacts trainee education and compliance with duty hour restrictions. Here, we examined the frequency, causes, and duration of surgical case delays for elective ACDF procedures performed at a single academic medical center, focusing on differences between first case and non-first case starts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Demographics

This retrospective, IRB-approved study analyzed 385 elective ACDF procedures (current procedural terminology [CPT] 22551) performed by 14 surgeons at a single, large academic medical institution from 2021 to 2022. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in Table 1. The most common procedures were two-level (43.1%) and single-level ACDF (40.0%). The average surgical duration was 250.0 min. The most procedures were performed by orthopedic surgeons alone (44.7%), followed by neurosurgeons (39.7%), and combined orthopedic/neurosurgery services (15.6%).

For each case, chart review provided data on duration, day of the week, surgical team specialty, number of registered nurse (RN) staff members, number of anesthesia providers, presence of delay, and delay reason, and whether the procedure was performed as a first case start. Case duration was defined from induction of general anesthesia to patient extubation. Surgical delays were defined as cases starting more than 10 min after the scheduled time. Delay reasons were documented by OR staff and grouped into six categories: upstream surgical delays, preoperative team coordination, administrative or patient-related factors, OR preparation delays, staffing constraints, and unspecified causes.

Statistical evaluation

Chi-square and Welch’s t-tests were used for group comparisons [Table 2]. P < 0.05 was considered the threshold for statistical significance.

RESULTS

Overall surgical delays

Surgical case delays were observed in 48.8% (188/385) of cases, with an average delay of 82.3 min [Table 3 and Figure 1]. The most common cause was upstream delays from earlier cases (46.3%, mean 121.9 min). Other causes included preoperative team coordination (i.e., preoperative assessment, surgeon availability, or anesthesiologist presence) (25.5%, mean 43.1 min), administrative or patient-related factors (i.e., late patient arrival, lab clearance issues, or case order changes) (14.9%, mean 60.3 min), OR preparation issues (10.6%, mean 40.6 min), staffing constraints (1.6%, mean 90.2 min), and unspecified reasons (1.1%, mean 12.0 min). Delayed cases were associated with slightly higher average staffing ratios (RN staff [5.27 vs. 4.81; P = 0.002] and anesthesia providers [2.46 vs. 1.96; P < 0.001]). Delay rates did not vary significantly by weekday (P = 0.184).

First case starts

Of the 231 cases (60.0%) that were first case starts, only 24.2% experienced delays compared with 85.7% of non-first case starts (P < 0.001) [Table 4 and Figure 2]. The most common causes of delay for first case delays were preoperative team coordination issues (51.8%), administrative or patient-related factors (28.6%), OR preparation delays (12.5%), and staffing constraints (5.4%).

Non-first case starts

Delays among non-first case starts were primarily due to upstream case overruns (65.9%), followed by preoperative coordination issues (14.4%), OR preparation delays (9.8%), and administrative or patient-related factors (9.1%). These delays were significantly longer than for first case starts (103.6 vs. 32.2 min; P < 0.001) and were accompanied by higher average staffing levels, including more RN staff (5.45 vs. 4.84; P = 0.005) and anesthesia providers (2.70 vs. 1.89; P < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

This study examined the frequency, causes, and durations of surgical case delays in elective ACDF procedures at a single academic center. Nearly half of all cases (48.8%) experienced a surgical delay, with first case starts showing a clear efficiency advantage: Only 24.2% were delayed compared to 85.7% of non-first case starts. First start delays were most often linked to preoperative team coordination, while non-first case delays were dominated by upstream case overruns and lasted more than 3 times longer despite higher RN and anesthesia staffing levels. Importance of first case starts

Although first case delays were substantially less frequent and shorter than delays for later cases, these delays appear to disrupt the remainder of the OR schedule, leading to suboptimal resource use, prolonged patient wait times, and increased institutional costs.[8,10] Preoperative team coordination, including anesthesia, nursing, surgical, and neuromonitoring staff, was the leading cause of first case delays, aligning with prior studies that identified surgeon lateness and anesthesia delays as key drivers.[2,3,10] For example, Firde et al., reported that delayed surgeon arrival increased the odds of a late start more than tenfold.[3] Interventions such as structured assessments, surgical team incentives, staggered case planning, and digital tools like electronic medical record notifications have been shown to improve on-time starts and even generate significant institutional cost savings.[8]

Non-first case inefficiencies

Non-first case delays were largely driven by cascading overruns from earlier cases. Turnover and transport inefficiencies between cases amplified these effects. Evidence shows that lean-based intervention, which eliminate non-value-added steps and overlap tasks, can reduce median turnover time by 60%, while a standardized postoperative debriefings have been linked to a 9% annual reduction in delays.[1,9] Quality improvement frameworks further emphasize turnover targets, granular data collection, and automated time-stamping to identify site-specific inefficiencies.[5]

CONCLUSION

This study highlights a clear first case advantage in elective ACDFs, with earlier starts experiencing fewer and shorter delays, and identifies targeted measures such as preoperative planning, turnover optimization, and surgeon engagement to enhance OR efficiency.
