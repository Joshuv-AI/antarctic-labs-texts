Reduced DJ-1/Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant signalling is associated with type 2 diabetes-related aggravation of neurodegeneration in a mouse model of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Honghao Xu, Mengjia Li, Yujia Li, Zhuochi Zou, Zichen Wang, Haroona Bashir, Shu Zhu, Qian Yang, Xianwei Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are increasingly recognized as comorbid conditions. Epidemiological evidence suggests that T2DM is associated with faster PD progression, but the biological processes that may connect these disorders remain incompletely understood. Oxidative stress is a shared pathological feature of both diseases, and the DJ-1/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway is an important antioxidant defence system.ObjectiveTo determine whether T2DM is associated with more severe PD-like neurodegeneration and whether these changes are accompanied by reduced DJ-1/Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant signalling in a mouse model of PD-T2DM comorbidity.MethodsMale C57BL/6 mice were assigned to control, T2DM, PD, or PD + T2DM groups. T2DM was induced by high-fat diet feeding combined with streptozotocin injection; PD was induced by unilateral intrastriatal 6-hydroxydopamine injection. Behavioural performance was assessed using rotarod and apomorphine-induced rotation tests. Nigrostriatal pathology was examined by haematoxylin-eosin, Nissl, and tyrosine hydroxylase immunohistochemistry. Oxidative stress markers (SOD, MDA, GSH) and DJ-1/Nrf2/HO-1 protein expression were measured in substantia nigra-striatum and serum. Final analyses included 10 control, 9 T2DM, 9 PD, and 9 PD + T2DM mice, with assay-specific sample sizes indicated in the Methods and figure legends.ResultsCompared with PD mice, PD + T2DM mice exhibited shorter rotarod latency, greater dopaminergic neuron loss, and stronger nigrostriatal oxidative stress (P < 0.05). DJ-1, Nrf2, and HO-1 expression was reduced in the substantia nigra-striatum of disease groups relative to controls, with Nrf2 and HO-1 significantly lower in PD + T2DM than in PD mice. Protein expression correlated positively with rotarod latency and antioxidant indices, and negatively with MDA content and fasting glucose. Serum oxidative stress markers showed similar directional trends, but most between-group differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionIn this combined toxin- and metabolism-based mouse model, T2DM was associated with more severe PD-like motor impairment and dopaminergic neuron loss, accompanied by reduced DJ-1/Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant signalling and increased nigrostriatal oxidative stress. These findings are consistent with the possibility that impaired antioxidant defence contributes to PD-T2DM comorbidity, while causal pathway involvement requires confirmation by future gain- or loss-of-function studies.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, DJ-1, Nrf2, HO-1, Oxidative stress, Neurodegeneration, Comorbidity

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease, affecting approximately 1–2% of individuals (de Lau and Breteler, 2006, Pringsheim et al., 2014) over the age of 60 and up to 4% of those above 80 years old in China and other aging populations worldwide. Clinically, PD is characterized primarily by motor symptoms such as resting tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability, although non-motor symptoms—including cognitive impairment, sleep disturbances, and autonomic dysfunction—are increasingly recognized as integral to disease burden. Pathologically, PD is defined by the progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) and the abnormal aggregation of α-synuclein into Lewy bodies. The etiology of PD is multifactorial, involving complex interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures, with oxidative stress emerging as a central pathogenic mechanism.

In recent years, growing epidemiological evidence has identified type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) as an important risk factor for PD. Multiple cohort and case–control studies have demonstrated that T2DM not only increases the risk of developing PD but also accelerates its clinical progression. A 2021 meta-analysis confirmed that diabetes is associated (Yue et al., 2016, Cereda et al., 2012) with a higher incidence of PD, and long-term T2DM (disease duration >10 years) is linked to a significantly increased risk of PD onset. Furthermore, clinical observations indicate that poor glycemic control in patients with PD is an independent risk factor for more rapid motor decline, as assessed by Hoehn–Yahr staging and Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) scores. These findings underscore the potential bidirectional influence of metabolic dysfunction and neurodegeneration.

The biological basis of the T2DM–PD relationship is incompletely understood, but converging evidence points to shared pathogenic processes. Both diseases are associated with chronic oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, insulin resistance, protein homeostasis imbalance, neuroinflammation, and the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) (Brownlee, 2005, Li et al., 2012). Oxidative stress—resulting from an imbalance between the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the capacity of antioxidant defenses—is considered a central hub linking these mechanisms. Dopaminergic neurons are especially vulnerable to oxidative stress due to their high metabolic activity, abundant mitochondrial content, and dopamine metabolism, which inherently generates ROS. In the diabetic milieu, chronic hyperglycemia exacerbates ROS production through multiple pathways, including the polyol pathway, protein kinase C activation, and increased AGE formation, thereby inducing cumulative cellular damage. Experimental studies have shown that hyperglycemia can trigger glycation-mediated oxidative injury and apoptosis in dopaminergic neuron-like cells, providing mechanistic support for clinical observations.

Among the body’s antioxidant defense systems, the DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 signaling axis occupies a pivotal position in mitigating oxidative injury. DJ−1 (also known as PARK7) (Bonifati et al., 2003, Taira et al., 2004) is a multifunctional protein with antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and chaperone-like properties. Loss-of-function mutations in DJ−1 are a known cause of autosomal recessive early-onset PD, and reduced DJ−1 activity has been implicated in sporadic PD. Mechanistically, DJ−1 stabilizes and activates the transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) (Johnson et al., 2008, Kensler et al., 2007), which orchestrates the expression of a battery of antioxidant and cytoprotective genes, including heme oxygenase−1 (HO−1) (Ryter et al., 2006, Schipper, 2004). Activation of the DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 axis enhances cellular resilience to oxidative stress, preserves mitochondrial integrity, and modulates inflammatory responses. In PD models, DJ−1 upregulation mitigates dopaminergic neuron loss, whereas DJ−1 deficiency exacerbates oxidative injury. Similarly, in T2DM, activation of the Nrf2/HO−1 pathway improves insulin sensitivity, protects pancreatic β-cell function, and attenuates systemic oxidative stress.

Despite these advances, the role of DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 signaling in the context of PD–T2DM comorbidity remains poorly defined. Most existing studies have examined this pathway in isolated PD or T2DM models, neglecting the potential synergistic effects of metabolic and neurodegenerative stressors on antioxidant defense mechanisms. Given the high prevalence of T2DM in the aging population and its demonstrated impact on PD progression, elucidating the molecular interactions between these diseases is of both biological and clinical significance. Understanding whether impairment of the DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 axis contributes to the exacerbation of PD pathology in the setting of T2DM could inform novel therapeutic strategies aimed at bolstering endogenous antioxidant defenses.

In the present study, we established and characterized a comorbid PD–T2DM mouse model to investigate the hypothesis that T2DM aggravates PD pathology through downregulation of the DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 antioxidant pathway. We systematically compared four groups: PD alone, T2DM alone, PD + T2DM, and healthy controls. PD was induced by unilateral stereotaxic injection of 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) into the SNpc, while T2DM was induced by a high-fat diet combined with streptozotocin (STZ) injection. Behavioral assessments—including the rotarod test and apomorphine-induced rotation—were performed to evaluate motor function. Histopathological analyses (hematoxylin–eosin, Nissl, and tyrosine hydroxylase staining) quantified neuronal integrity in the nigrostriatal pathway. Western blotting measured DJ−1, Nrf2, and HO−1 protein levels, while spectrophotometric assays quantified oxidative stress markers: superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), and malondialdehyde (MDA). Correlation and cluster analyses were conducted to identify relationships between molecular, behavioral, and metabolic parameters.

Our results show that the PD + T2DM group exhibited the most severe motor impairment, neuronal degeneration, and nigrostriatal oxidative stress, accompanied by reduced expression of DJ−1, Nrf2, and HO−1 in the substantia nigra-striatum. These findings support an association between metabolic stress, impaired antioxidant signalling, and aggravated PD-like pathology. Because the pathway was not directly manipulated, the present data should be interpreted as mechanistically suggestive rather than causally definitive.

Materials and methods

Experimental design overview

This study aimed to investigate the role of the DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 antioxidant signaling pathway in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) in the presence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). To this end, a comorbidity mouse model was established by combining 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)-induced PD with high-fat diet and streptozotocin (STZ)-induced T2DM. Four experimental groups were generated: (1) normal control, (2) PD alone, (3) T2DM alone, and (4) PD + T2DM comorbidity. Behavioral, histopathological, biochemical, and molecular assays were conducted to evaluate disease phenotypes and to quantify oxidative stress–related molecular changes. All experimental procedures complied with the institutional guidelines for animal care and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Chengdu Medical College (Approval No.: 20230315040).

Animals

A total of 64 specific-pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6 mice, aged 3 weeks and weighing 9.5 ± 1.5 g, were purchased from *** Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 25 ± 1 °C; humidity: 50–70%) with a 12-h light/dark cycle, in quiet and clean rooms. Standard bedding material was replaced daily, and mice had free access to food and water. Animals were acclimatized for one week prior to experimentation.

Experimental groups

Following acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned by computer-generated randomization into four groups:

Control group (n = 10): standard diet, no surgical or chemical intervention.

T2DM group (n = 10): high-fat diet combined with STZ injection.

PD group (n = 20): unilateral nigrostriatal lesion induced by 6-OHDA.

PD + T2DM group (n = 24): sequential T2DM induction followed by PD modeling.

Randomization and group assignment were blinded to the investigators performing subsequent behavioural, histological, biochemical, and western blot analyses. The final numbers of animals included in the main analyses were control, n = 10; T2DM, n = 9; PD, n = 9; and PD + T2DM, n = 9. Histological quantification was performed in three mice per group, whereas biochemical assays and western blotting were performed in six mice per group unless otherwise stated.

Induction of T2DM

T2DM was induced by feeding mice a high-fat diet (HFD; 60% kcal from fat) for 4 weeks, followed by intraperitoneal injection of STZ (50 mg/kg body weight, freshly prepared in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer, pH 4.2–4.5) for three consecutive days. To confirm diabetes onset, fasting blood glucose was measured from the tail vein using a Roche glucometer at 6 h fasting intervals on three consecutive days, two weeks post-STZ injection. Mice with fasting blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L on all three measurements were considered diabetic and included in the study.

Induction of PD

PD was induced using unilateral intrastriatal injection of 6-OHDA via a stereotaxic apparatus (Shenzhen Reward Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (0.15% solution, 0.2 mL/10 g, intraperitoneally), and placed in a stereotaxic frame ensuring the bregma and lambda were aligned on a horizontal plane. After scalp incision and exposure of the skull, coordinates relative to the bregma were located for two injection sites:

Site 1: anterior–posterior (AP) + 1.0 mm, mediolateral (ML) + 2.1 mm, dorsoventral (DV) −2.9 mm.

Site 2: AP + 0.3 mm, ML + 2.3 mm, DV −2.9 mm.

A freshly prepared 6-OHDA solution (2 µg/µL in 0.02% ascorbic acid–saline) was loaded into a microsyringe and delivered at a rate of 1 µL/min, 2 µL per site. The needle was left in place for 5 min post-injection before being slowly withdrawn (1 mm/min). The incision was closed with sterile sutures, and mice received intraperitoneal penicillin (40,000 U/day) for 7 days post-surgery.

PD model validation was performed using the apomorphine (Apo)-induced rotation test (0.5 mg/mL Apo in saline; intraperitoneal). Mice displaying > 7 contralateral turns per minute (≥ 210 rotations/30 min) on both day 14 and day 21 post-surgery were considered successful PD models.

Induction of PD + T2DM comorbidity

For the comorbidity model, T2DM was induced as described above. At 6 weeks after diabetes induction, successful diabetic mice underwent unilateral 6-OHDA injection to generate PD lesions. This sequential modeling produced the PD + T2DM group. Model validation used the same glycemic and rotational behavior criteria as in the individual disease models.

Behavioral assessments

Rotarod test

Motor coordination and balance were evaluated using a HR420 rotarod apparatus. Mice underwent 3 days of pre-training to acclimate to the apparatus. On the test day, the rod accelerated from 4 to 40 rpm over 5 min. Each mouse underwent three trials with 1 h rest between trials. The latency to fall was recorded, and the mean of three trials was used for statistical analysis.

Apomorphine-induced rotation

PD and PD + T2DM mice were assessed for asymmetric rotational behavior following Apo injection as described above. Rotation counts were measured for 30 min, and the average turns per minute were calculated.

Tissue collection

At designated endpoints, mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (5 mg/100 g, intraperitoneally).

Perfusion fixation: For histological analysis, three mice per group underwent transcardial perfusion with saline (50 mL) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (100 mL). Brains were post-fixed in paraformaldehyde for 24 h, cryoprotected, and processed for paraffin embedding.

Fresh tissue collection: For biochemical assays, substantia nigra–striatum tissues from six mice per group were dissected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Serum: Blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus, allowed to clot at room temperature for 4 h, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Serum aliquots were stored at −80 °C.

Histological analysis

Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) staining

Paraffin-embedded brain sections (3 µm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin, differentiated, blued, and counterstained with eosin. Sections were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped. Morphological changes in the substantia nigra–striatum were examined under a 3DHISTECH digital slide scanner. Tissue injury was scored semi-quantitatively (0–4) based on neuronal pyknosis and degeneration.

Nissl staining

Following deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were stained with toluidine blue, dehydrated, and mounted. The number of Nissl-positive neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta was counted in three non-overlapping fields at 400 × magnification.

Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunohistochemistry

Antigen retrieval was performed by microwave heating in citrate buffer. Sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-TH primary antibody (Proteintech, 1:1000), followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Servicebio, 1:8000). Detection was performed using DAB substrate, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. The percentage of TH-positive area was quantified using Halo imaging software.

Western blot analysis

Substantia nigra–striatum tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk for 2 h, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against DJ−1 (Affinity, 1:1000), Nrf2 (Affinity, 1:1000), HO−1 (Huabio, 1:2000), and β-actin (Abclonal, 1:5000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Affinity, 1:8000) for 2 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and analyzed using Gel-Pro Analyzer software.

Oxidative stress biomarker assays

Oxidative stress parameters were measured in both substantia nigra–striatum homogenates and serum using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to manufacturer instructions:

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity: Hydroxylamine method; expressed as U/mg protein.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content: Thiobarbituric acid method; expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Reduced glutathione (GSH) content: Microplate method; expressed as μmol/g protein.

Protein concentrations were normalized to total protein content determined by the BCA assay.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism version 9.5. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Normally distributed data with homogeneous variance were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by LSD post hoc testing. When variance homogeneity was not satisfied, Welch ANOVA with Tamhane’s T2 post hoc testing was used. Non-normally distributed variables were analysed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn-type pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Pearson correlation was used for normally distributed continuous variables, and Spearman correlation was used for non-normally distributed variables. Correlation heatmaps and hierarchical clustering analyses were generated to visualize interrelationships among variables. Results are presented as mean + /- SD unless otherwise specified, and individual data points are shown in revised key graphs where feasible. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05; exact P values are reported where available, with threshold values retained for comparisons for which only the original statistical output was available.

To further improve transparency, we re-analysed the available raw data and prepared a statistical reanalysis workbook (Supplementary Table S1). This file reports the raw long-format data, group-wise mean + /- SD, omnibus P values, and pairwise Welch t-test P values for behavioural, histological, biochemical, and western blot endpoints. For key pairwise outcomes, the PD + T2DM versus PD comparison yielded P = 0.004 for rotarod latency, P = 0.036 for TH-positive area, P < 0.001 for nigrostriatal SOD, P = 0.011 for nigrostriatal MDA, P = 0.002 for nigrostriatal GSH, P = 0.060 for DJ−1, P = 0.013 for Nrf2, and P = 0.013 for HO−1. The apomorphine-induced rotation difference between PD and PD + T2DM remained non-significant (P = 0.481).

Results

Model establishment and success rates

All animals in the control group survived throughout the study and were included in the final analysis (10/10). In the T2DM group, no deaths occurred during high-fat diet feeding or after STZ injection; one mouse failed to meet the hyperglycaemia criterion, yielding a final T2DM sample size of 9/10. In the PD group, 9 of 20 mice met the apomorphine rotation criterion (>7 turns/min on both test days), corresponding to a 45% modelling success rate and a final PD sample size of nine mice. In the PD + T2DM group, 9 of 24 mice achieved both hyperglycaemia and rotational behaviour criteria, resulting in a 37.5% modelling success rate and a final sample size of nine mice. These attrition rates are reported transparently because the low success rates, particularly in the lesion-based PD and combined groups, may introduce selection bias and reduce statistical power (Table 1). No formal a priori power calculation was performed; the sample size was based on prior 6-OHDA and HFD/STZ mouse studies and local feasibility. Therefore, statistically non-significant comparisons, especially serum biomarkers and PD versus PD + T2DM apomorphine rotation, should be interpreted cautiously.

Changes in body weight and blood glucose

Body weight

During the pre-modeling period, body weights increased steadily in all groups. Following T2DM induction, both the T2DM and PD + T2DM groups displayed accelerated weight gain compared with control and PD groups (P < 0.01). After 6-OHDA injection, PD and PD + T2DM mice showed weight loss or slower weight gain, with the most pronounced decline in the PD + T2DM group. This effect persisted until the study endpoint (Fig. 1A–C).

Changes in body weight (A–C) and blood glucose (D–E) in each group over the modeling period.

Blood glucose

In the T2DM and PD + T2DM groups, fasting blood glucose exceeded 11.1 mmol/L by day 42, confirming successful diabetes induction. PD mice maintained normoglycemia throughout. After PD modeling (week 8), PD + T2DM mice showed a transient decrease in blood glucose, followed by gradual re-elevation; however, glucose remained consistently higher than in the T2DM-only group during the final two weeks. Control and PD groups showed no significant glycemic changes (Fig. 1D–E).

Behavioral assessments

Apomorphine-induced rotation

Control and T2DM mice showed no apomorphine-induced contralateral rotation meeting the PD validation criterion. PD and PD + T2DM mice displayed marked contralateral rotations. Although the PD + T2DM group showed a slightly higher mean rotation rate than the PD group, this difference was not statistically significant and was not interpreted as evidence of a more severe 6-OHDA lesion.

Rotarod test

Compared with controls, PD, T2DM, and PD + T2DM mice had reduced latency to fall, indicating impaired performance on this coordination and balance task. The PD + T2DM group exhibited the greatest deficit, with significantly shorter latencies than the PD group (P < 0.01). The mild but significant reduction in the T2DM group compared with controls (P < 0.05) may reflect diabetic peripheral neuropathy, muscle weakness, altered motivation, or central effects of metabolic stress. Because rotarod testing was the only broad motor coordination assay used, these behavioural data should be interpreted as evidence of impaired motor performance rather than a complete characterization of parkinsonian motor signs (Fig. 2).

Motor function assessment. Latency to fall in rotarod test showing impaired coordination in PD, T2DM, and PD + T2DM mice, with the greatest deficit observed in PD + T2DM.

Histopathological changes in the nigrostriatal pathway

Hematoxylin–Eosin staining

In control mice, the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) displayed abundant neurons with large, round nuclei and minimal gliosis. PD and T2DM groups showed varying degrees of neuronal shrinkage, cytoplasmic vacuolation, and glial cell infiltration. These changes were most severe in PD + T2DM mice, which demonstrated extensive neuronal loss and dense gliosis (P < 0.05 vs. PD; Fig. 3A).

Histological changes in the substantia nigra–striatum. Representative HE staining images demonstrating neuronal degeneration, most severe in PD + T2DM mice.

Nissl staining

Nissl bodies were densely distributed in control neurons. In PD and T2DM mice, the number of Nissl-positive neurons was reduced, particularly on the lesioned side. The PD + T2DM group exhibited the lowest Nissl-positive neuron counts, significantly lower than PD alone (P < 0.01; Fig. 3B).

Tyrosine hydroxylase immunohistochemistry

TH-positive dopaminergic neurons were abundant in control SNpc. PD and T2DM groups had fewer TH-positive neurons, with the PD + T2DM group showing the most marked reduction (P < 0.01 vs. PD). This result indicates that the combined PD + T2DM condition was associated with greater dopaminergic neuron loss than the PD-only condition in this model (Fig. 3C).

Expression of DJ−1, Nrf2, and HO−1 proteins

Western blot analysis revealed significantly decreased expression of DJ−1, Nrf2, and HO−1 in the substantia nigra–striatum of PD, T2DM, and PD + T2DM mice compared with controls (P < 0.01). Among these, PD + T2DM mice exhibited the lowest levels of Nrf2 and HO−1, which were significantly reduced compared with PD alone (P < 0.05). DJ−1 expression was also lowest in PD + T2DM mice, although the difference from PD was not statistically significant (Fig. 4).

Nissl staining of substantia nigra pars compacta showing reduced Nissl-positive neurons in PD, T2DM, and PD + T2DM groups, most pronounced in PD + T2DM.

Oxidative Stress Markers

In the Nigrostriatal Region

SOD and GSH levels were significantly decreased, and MDA levels were significantly increased in PD, T2DM, and PD + T2DM mice compared with controls (P < 0.01). PD + T2DM mice showed the lowest SOD and GSH levels and the highest MDA levels among the disease groups, with significant differences from PD alone (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A–C).

Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunohistochemistry. Quantitative reduction of TH-positive neurons in PD, T2DM, and PD + T2DM, with the lowest levels in PD + T2DM mice.

In Serum

SOD and GSH levels were reduced, and MDA levels were increased in PD, T2DM, and PD + T2DM mice relative to controls, but these differences did not reach statistical significance. Thus, serum markers showed only non-significant directional trends and were not considered sufficient evidence of systemic oxidative imbalance (Fig. 5D-F).

Correlation and Cluster Analyses

Because the study was not powered for causal mediation analysis, correlation and clustering results were used as exploratory tools to identify relationships among behavioural, metabolic, oxidative-stress, and pathway variables.

Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses demonstrated that DJ−1, Nrf2, and HO−1 expression levels in the substantia nigra–striatum were positively correlated with rotarod latency and SOD content (P < 0.01) and negatively correlated with MDA content and fasting blood glucose at week 12 (P < 0.01). Among all variables, DJ−1 expression and MDA content showed the largest intergroup differences, suggesting their potential as key biomarkers for PD–T2DM comorbidity (Fig. 6).

Western blot analysis of DJ−1, Nrf2, and HO−1 expression in substantia nigra–striatum. All were significantly downregulated in PD + T2DM compared with PD alone.

Hierarchical clustering further revealed that molecular markers of oxidative stress clustered closely with behavioural and glycaemic parameters, supporting an association among metabolic dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neurodegeneration in this model (Fig. 7).

Oxidative stress markers (SOD, MDA, GSH) in substantia nigra–striatum. PD + T2DM mice showed the lowest antioxidant levels and highest lipid peroxidation.

Discussion

The present study shows that type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is associated with aggravated neuropathological and behavioural deficits in a mouse model of Parkinson’s disease (PD), accompanied by reduced DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 antioxidant signalling. By employing a combined 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) lesion and high-fat diet/streptozotocin (STZ) induction approach, we established a comorbidity model that captures key dopaminergic and metabolic features relevant to the two diseases. Behavioural, histopathological, biochemical, and molecular analyses collectively indicate that the PD + T2DM group exhibits more severe neurodegeneration, motor dysfunction, and nigrostriatal oxidative stress than PD alone. These findings provide descriptive and correlative evidence for molecular interplay between metabolic stress and neurodegeneration and suggest that antioxidant defence pathways merit further functional testing in PD-T2DM comorbidity (Fig. 8).

T2DM as a Risk Factor for PD Progression

The translational interpretation of the model requires caution. The 6-OHDA lesion captures dopaminergic vulnerability and motor impairment but not the full progressive and alpha-synuclein-driven biology of idiopathic PD. Similarly, the HFD/STZ protocol captures hyperglycaemia and metabolic stress, but it does not isolate insulin resistance as the sole driver of T2DM pathology (Fig. 9).

Correlation heatmap showing relationships among behavioral parameters, oxidative stress markers, and DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 expression.

Epidemiological data increasingly support an association between T2DM and an elevated risk of PD onset and accelerated progression. Meta-analyses have reported pooled relative risks of 1.21–1.38 for developing PD in diabetic populations compared with non-diabetic controls, with stronger associations observed in individuals with poor glycemic control or long disease duration. In addition to epidemiologic correlations, experimental models have shown that hyperglycemia exacerbates dopaminergic neuron loss and promotes α-synuclein aggregation. Our findings align with these observations, as PD + T2DM mice displayed significantly worse motor performance on the rotarod test and more profound dopaminergic neuron loss on histological assessment than PD-only mice.

One possible explanation is that hyperglycemia and insulin resistance increase neuronal vulnerability to oxidative damage. In the diabetic milieu, glucose autoxidation, the polyol pathway, and advanced glycation end-product (AGE) formation contribute to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. These processes may act synergistically with PD-related oxidative stress from dopamine metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction, overwhelming neuronal antioxidant defenses and accelerating degeneration.

Oxidative stress as a common pathogenic mechanism

Oxidative stress has long been recognized as a central pathological feature of PD, as evidenced by increased lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage in the substantia nigra of patients. In T2DM, chronic hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia similarly promote oxidative stress, leading to vascular damage, impaired insulin signaling, and β-cell apoptosis. The convergence of these stressors in PD + T2DM likely creates a “double hit” scenario, in which both systemic and local ROS burdens contribute to nigrostriatal degeneration.

Our biochemical results support this concept in the nigrostriatal region. PD + T2DM mice had significantly lower superoxide dismutase (SOD) and reduced glutathione (GSH) levels and higher malondialdehyde (MDA) content in the substantia nigra-striatum compared with PD alone. In contrast, serum markers showed only non-significant trends in the same direction, and therefore the present data do not establish a systemic oxidative imbalance.

Role of the DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 pathway

The DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 axis plays a critical role in cellular defense against oxidative damage. DJ−1 functions as a redox-sensitive chaperone and transcriptional regulator that stabilizes nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) under oxidative conditions. Activated Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus, binding antioxidant response elements (AREs) and promoting the transcription of phase II detoxification and antioxidant enzymes, including heme oxygenase−1 (HO−1) (Ryter et al., 2006, Schipper, 2004).

In PD, loss-of-function mutations in DJ−1 cause early-onset familial forms of the disease, and reduced DJ−1 levels have been reported in sporadic cases. Similarly, Nrf2 activity declines with aging and neurodegeneration, impairing antioxidant responses. In diabetic contexts, downregulation of Nrf2/HO−1 contributes to insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and neuropathy.

Our study shows that PD + T2DM mice have the lowest expression of DJ−1, Nrf2, and HO−1 in the substantia nigra-striatum among all groups, with statistically significant differences for Nrf2 and HO−1 compared with PD alone. Correlation analyses revealed that these protein levels were positively associated with motor coordination and antioxidant capacity (SOD, GSH) and negatively associated with lipid peroxidation (MDA) and hyperglycaemia. These findings are consistent with the possibility that impairment of the DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 axis contributes to the enhanced oxidative damage observed in PD-T2DM comorbidity, but they do not establish causality.

Several mechanisms may explain this association. Hyperglycaemia could promote glycation or oxidative modification of DJ−1, potentially reducing its capacity to stabilize Nrf2. Insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammatory signalling may also attenuate Nrf2 activation and downstream HO−1 induction. These possibilities remain hypothetical in the present study because DJ−1 oxidation/glycation, mitochondrial function, insulin signalling, and inflammatory markers were not directly measured.

Clinical implications

The exacerbation of PD pathology by T2DM has important therapeutic implications. First, it suggests that optimal glycemic control might slow PD progression in diabetic patients. Second, pharmacological activation of the Nrf2 pathway—through agents such as dimethyl fumarate, sulforaphane, or bardoxolone methyl (Scannevin et al., 2012; Pergola et al., 2011)—could offer neuroprotective benefits in the context of comorbidity. Third, enhancing DJ−1 activity, either via gene therapy or small-molecule stabilizers, may represent an upstream approach to restoring antioxidant defense.

Indeed, several preclinical studies have demonstrated that Nrf2 activators can ameliorate both diabetic complications and neurodegenerative changes. For example, sulforaphane treatment improved motor performance (Tarozzi et al., 2013, Morroni et al., 2013) and dopaminergic neuron survival in MPTP-induced PD models while also enhancing insulin sensitivity in diabetic animals. Our data provide mechanistic justification for such dual-target interventions.

Strengths of the study

A key strength of our work is the use of a validated comorbidity model that integrates both metabolic and neurodegenerative insults, reflecting an important clinical scenario of PD patients with T2DM. The comprehensive assessment, encompassing behavioural, histological, biochemical, and molecular analyses, allowed us to identify associations between hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress, and DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 signalling impairment.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has several limitations. First, although the 6-OHDA lesion model is well established and useful for detecting dopaminergic vulnerability, it is an acute toxin-based model and does not fully recapitulate progressive, alpha-synuclein-driven PD. Future studies should employ chronic PD models, such as alpha-synuclein overexpression or preformed fibril seeding, to test whether the same antioxidant-signalling changes occur during progressive pathology. Second, the HFD/STZ protocol models a hyperglycaemic diabetic state with metabolic stress but cannot fully represent pure insulin resistance-type T2DM; fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and lipid profiles were not measured. Third, we did not directly manipulate DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 activity, so the data support an association rather than a proven causal pathway. Pharmacological activation/inhibition or genetic gain- and loss-of-function studies are warranted. Fourth, the modelling success rates were modest in the PD and PD + T2DM groups, which may introduce selection bias and limits statistical power. Fifth, serum oxidative stress markers showed non-significant trends, and we did not assess inflammatory cytokines, mitochondrial function, DJ−1 glycation/oxidation, or insulin-signalling markers. These unmeasured mechanisms should be addressed in future work.

Future work should test whether pharmacological or genetic modulation of DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 signalling can modify metabolic and neurological deficits in PD-T2DM models. Moreover, longitudinal studies in human PD patients with and without T2DM could assess whether peripheral biomarkers of this pathway correlate with disease progression, paving the way for personalized therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, T2DM was associated with more severe motor impairment, dopaminergic neuron loss, and nigrostriatal oxidative stress in a 6-OHDA mouse model of PD. These changes were accompanied by reduced DJ−1/Nrf2/HO−1 antioxidant signalling. The findings support oxidative stress and impaired antioxidant defence as candidate contributors to PD-T2DM comorbidity, while future functional studies are required to determine whether restoring this pathway can causally modify disease severity.
