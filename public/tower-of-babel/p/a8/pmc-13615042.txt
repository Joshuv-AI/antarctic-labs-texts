Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke: A Methodological Systematic Review of Health Economic Decision Modeling Studies
Authors: Andreas Beisel, Hans-Helmut König, Judith Dams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveIschemic strokes are one of the main causes for death and disability worldwide and pose a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems. Currently, endovascular treatment is considered one of the most effective therapeutic options for the acute treatment of ischemic strokes. Furthermore, endovascular treatment has increasingly become the focus of health economic evaluations, in which decision modeling is used for the estimation of long-term costs and effects of endovascular treatment. This systematic review aims to assess the methodological quality regarding decision modeling-based health economic evaluations of endovascular treatment for acute ischemic strokes.MethodsA systematic search was conducted from January 2008 to December 2024 in PubMed, Econ-Lit, CDSR (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), DARE (Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effectiveness), and NHS EED/HTA (UK National Health Service Economic Evaluation Database/Health Technology Assessment) in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (search date: January 2025). The review was registered at PROSPERO (international prospective register of systematic reviews, identifier: CRD420251148065). Articles were eligible if they included a cost-effectiveness analysis of endovascular treatment compared to standard care for acute ischemic strokes using discrete event simulation or Markov modeling, and if they were considered original research and were written in English or German. General study characteristics, information on model design, parameter inputs, uncertainty, and validation were extracted and synthesized in tables. Methodological quality assessment was supported using a framework by Ramos et al.ResultsForty-nine articles were finally included. Health states were defined using a stroke disability rating scale (modified Rankin scale) in all articles, although individual modified Rankin scale levels were merged into broader categories in 15 articles. All articles used a short-term decision tree for treatment effectiveness up to 90 days after stroke, of which one article subsequently employed discrete event simulation and one article employed Markov microsimulation to model lifetime outcomes. The remaining 47 articles employed Markov cohort models capturing health state stability and deterioration for long-term results, of which 13 articles additionally modeled an intermediate recovery phase (up to 1 year after stroke), in which health state improvement was possible. Recurrences were modeled in 40 articles. While transition probabilities were primarily taken from high-quality evidence studies (e.g., randomized controlled trials for treatment effectiveness in 44 articles), costs and/or utilities were not country specific in 32 articles. Sensitivity analyses including probabilistic or deterministic/scenario analyses were conducted in almost all articles (45 and 47 articles, respectively). Some form of validation was evident in all articles, although internal validation was reported in only ten articles.ConclusionsFuture modelers should incorporate recovery up to the first year after the stroke, consider recurrences in the models, and define health states by using individual modified Rankin scale levels. Furthermore, costs and utilities plugged into the models should be country specific, probabilistic and deterministic sensitivity and scenario analyses should be assessed for costs, utilities, and transition probabilities, and the model should be internally validated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40273-026-01640-1.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Since the last comprehensive review in 2009, 49 health economic decision modeling studies assessing endovascular therapy for acute ischemic stroke were published. One used discrete event simulation and one used Markov microsimulation, the remaining used Markov cohort modeling for long-term outcomes.Future models should include the possibility for improvements in health states up to 1 year after the stroke and consider recurrences. Country-specific costs and utilities are recommended to be used.Probabilistic and deterministic sensitivity and scenario analyses should be assessed for costs, utilities, and transition probabilities. Internal validation should be provided and reported in detail.

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and a leading cause of disability worldwide, despite significant treatment progress in the last few years [1, 2]. Furthermore, the economic burden of stroke on the healthcare systems is increasing [3]: a conservative estimation projects the global costs of stroke to exceed one trillion USD$ by the year 2030 [4]. The economic burden is mainly driven by ischemic strokes (IS), making up 83% of all strokes [5].

Ischemic strokes occur when a vessel transporting blood to the brain is obstructed [6]. For the acute treatment of these obstructions, endovascular treatment (EVT) is currently one of the most effective methods [4], reducing both mortality and disability rates [7]. The procedure involves recanalizing occluded vessels to restore reperfusion of the affected brain areas [8]. Endovascular treatment includes treatments such as mechanical thrombectomy with stents, thrombo-aspiration techniques, balloon angioplasty, or the intra-arterial application of pharmacological thrombolysis [9].

Because of the high economic burden on healthcare systems, EVT is becoming increasingly the subject of health economic evaluations [10]. In such evaluations, new treatments are compared with established treatments in terms of their incremental costs and effectiveness [11], where effectiveness is often measured with the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). The mRS is a seven-point rating scale to assess the degree of disability after a stroke and ranges from 0 = “No symptoms at all” to 5 = “Severe disability: bedridden, incontinent, and requiring constant nursing care and attention,” with 6 = “Dead” often added to the scale [12, 13].

These mRS levels are translated to utility scores that reflect preference-based valuations of health-related quality of life. Utility scores range from 0 = “Dead” to 1 = “Perfect health,” with negative scores being also possible, and are used to calculate quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) [11]. Quality-adjusted life-years are a standard measure of effects in health economic evaluations with one QALY corresponding to 1 year lived in perfect health or, for example, 2 years lived with a utility score of 0.5 [11]. The most common instrument used to measure health-related quality of life and derive utilities is the EuroQol-5 Dimensions (EQ-5D), which incorporates the dimensions “Mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression” [14].

Health economic evaluations are often conducted alongside clinical trials, so that clinical outcomes as well as data on resource consumption or costs are collected throughout the trial. As a consequence, these health economic evaluations benefit from measures commonly employed in clinical trials to reduce bias, such as randomization or blinding. However, clinical trials usually encompass short-term time horizons, which may be meaningful from a clinical, but not from a health economic perspective. Decision modeling is used to overcome this limitation, which combines data from clinical trials with other sources, thereby extrapolating results beyond the follow-up and the scope of the initial trial [11, 15].

Markov cohort modeling is one of the most frequently used modeling methods employed in health conomic evaluations of stroke treatments [16]. These models involve hypothetical patient cohorts that may reside in several distinct health states, transition between these health states, or die during a finite number of discrete model cycles (e.g., length of 1 year). The typical Markovian “memorylessness” assumption is that the transitions of the hypothetical patients depend only on the previous health state they were in before the transition. Quality-adjusted life-years and costs are assigned to each health state and totaled for each patient cohort over the time horizon of the model (i.e., a certain number of cycles) [17]. By contrast, in Markov microsimulation models, patients are modeled one at a time, allowing the individual patient history to be accounted for, thereby undermining the Markovian “memorylessness” assumption [18].

Similar to Markov microsimulation models, discrete event simulation (DES) also models hypothetical patients one at a time, with each patient history stored individually. One advantage over Markov models is that events (e.g., adverse events or recurrences) can happen at any point in a patients’ history, which makes discrete cycles redundant and allows for a more realistic representation of patient pathways. Additionally, DES allows modeling interactions between patients and access to limited resources. However, DES and Markov microsimulation models are more complex to develop and, in most cases, need more elaborate input data than Markov cohort models [18].

In order to draw correct conclusions from health economic evaluations of EVT using these models, it is necessary to critically assess the methodological quality of these studies [11, 19]. In 2009, Earnshaw et al. [20] published a review of decision modeling studies analyzing the cost-effectiveness of acute stroke treatments up to 2007. Since then, numerous studies regarding the cost-effectiveness of EVT for IS were conducted and many of these were assessed in other reviews focusing on the synthesis of results, or the quality of cost types or utilities [10, 16, 21–25]. However, no evidence-based recommendations to guide model structuring, input data selection or the methodological conduct exist to improve the methodological quality of future decision modeling studies in this specific research field. Therefore, our systematic review updates the previous review by Earnshaw et al. [20]. It provides an in-depth assessment of the methodological quality of cost-effectiveness studies using decision modeling, taking into account the published literature regarding EVT for acute IS from 2008 onwards.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [26] and was registered at the international systematic review registry (PROSPERO no. CRD420251148065 [27]). A systematic literature search was performed in January 2025 in PubMed [28], EconLit [29] via EBSCOhost [30], CDSR (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews) [31], DARE (Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effectiveness) [32], NHS EED (UK National Health Service Economic Evaluation Database) [32], and HTA (Health Technology Assessment) of the UK National Health Service Centre for Review and Dissemination [32]. The search time frame ranged from 01/01/2008 (end date of literature search in Earnshaw et al. [20]) to 31/12/2024. Search terms included “Stroke,” “Economic evaluation,” and “Decision model” as well as publication date and language (see Table S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). Additionally, a hand search using reference lists of the included articles was conducted.

Initially, the research was carried out adopting a broader perspective, so included articles had to (1) deal with acute stroke, (2) aim at a health economic evaluation of a new treatment, and (3) include some type of decision modeling approach. Articles were excluded if they were (1) defined as not original (i.e., reviews) or (2) written in a language other than English or German. After the completion of the initial full-text screening, the inclusion criteria were narrowed down, so included articles had to (1) deal with acute IS, (2) comprise cost-effectiveness analyses by comparing EVT with standard care (SC), and (3) employ a Markov or DES modeling approach. The restriction of inclusion criteria was performed as methodological limitations to these common modeling types were especially evident in this specific research field and comparability across studies was improved. Duplicate removal was conducted in the reference manager software “EndNote” (Version 20.2.1) [33]. The screening process included title/abstract screening and a full-text screening for the remaining articles. Screening was completed independently by AB and JD. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion and consensus by the two authors [11].

Extracted data of the finally included articles comprised general study information, model design, uncertainty, and validation. In addition, the sources for parameter inputs were extracted. Data extraction and review structure were based on a previous methodological review [11]. Quality assessment was supported by using a framework by Ramos et al. [34]. They performed a systematic review of best practice guidelines for decision modeling approaches and synthesized their evidence into a comprehensive checklist including the following five dimensions: (1) problem concept; (2) model concept; (3) synthesis of evidence; (4) analysis of uncertainty; and (5) model transparency and validation, with 48 questions in total [34]. Questions were answered as 0 (criterion not fulfilled), 1 (criterion fulfilled), and NA (criterion not applicable) [34]. For a more accurate assessment, it was decided to include 0.5 (criterion partially fulfilled) as well [11]. Data extraction and quality assessment was completed by AB and cross-checked by JD. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion and consensus by the two authors [11].

Results

Initially, the database search resulted in 2673 articles. In addition, 13 hand-searched articles were identified in the reference lists of single economic evaluation articles. After the removal of duplicates, 2035 articles were eligible for title/abstract screening, of which 1951 articles were excluded. After a full-text-screening of the resulting 84 articles, 49 articles [8, 35–82] were finally included (Fig. 1). Publication dates ranged from 2009 to 2024. All included articles were cost-utility analyses and except for an Irish health technology assessment [45], all articles were published in peer-reviewed journals.

Setting

In the majority of articles, a US or Canadian setting was considered [8, 44, 48–53, 56, 59–61, 64, 65, 70, 71, 74, 78, 80]. Regarding South America, one article each was set in Argentina [55] and one in Brazil [75]. Furthermore, three articles were conducted for Australia [37, 42, 82], and one article combined a US, Australian, and Spanish setting [41]. Moreover, various European countries were taken into account in two articles [39, 57], while individual European countries were considered for the UK [40, 46, 54, 67], Sweden [36, 76], France [38, 47], Italy [69], the Netherlands [66], Belgium [58], Ireland [45], Spain [35], and Germany [81]. In terms of Asian countries, a Chinese [62, 63, 77, 79] and Thai [68] setting were considered. In three articles, the country was not explicitly mentioned, but based on the cost data, a US [72, 73] and Australian [43] setting could be assumed. Consequently, all included articles were published in high- and middle-income countries [83].

Treatment and Comparator

All articles employed EVT as treatment. In 35 articles, intravenous thrombolysis was added to EVT as treatment [35–40, 42, 44–60, 62, 67–71, 74, 76, 79, 80, 82]. The comparator was defined as SC without further definition in 11 articles [41, 43, 61, 63, 66, 72, 73, 75, 77, 78, 81]. In all other articles, the comparator encompassed intravenous thrombolysis.

Funding

Funding was declared in all but 16 articles, where explicitly no funding was stated [38, 42, 43, 47–52, 56, 70, 72, 73, 76, 77, 79]. One article did not mention funding, although conflicts of interest were stated [78].

Model Design

Base-Case Model Type

Markov cohort-level models were used in 25 articles [8, 39–41, 45, 47–51, 53–55, 59, 62, 63, 65–70, 76, 78, 81]. Eight of these Markov cohort-level models used a deterministic approach [39, 40, 48–50, 65, 67, 76], while one used a probabilistic approach for the base case [45]. For the remaining Markov cohort-level models, the approach was not explicitly described, but a deterministic approach could be assumed.

Twenty-two articles did not explicitly state whether they used cohort- or individual-level models [35–38, 42, 44, 46, 52, 56–58, 60, 61, 64, 71–75, 77, 79, 80], although Markov cohort-level models could be assumed. Only in one of these articles a deterministic approach was explicitly mentioned for the base case [64], while for the others this approach could be assumed. In one article an individual-level Markov model (microsimulation) [43] was used and in another article an individual-level DES model was used [82]. The choice of model type was discussed in only three articles [43, 81, 82].

Starting Age

Starting age for the models ranged from 55 years to 76 years. In seven articles, the starting age was unclear [39, 40, 46, 47, 67, 71, 74].

Short-Term Phase

All models included a decision tree at the beginning, considering patients with acute IS receiving either EVT or SC for a period of 90 days after stroke. Patients were then allocated to one of three health states with an mRS level of 0–2, 3–5, or death [8, 38, 40, 44–47, 51, 62–64, 77, 79, 80], or one of seven health states with an mRS level of 0–5 or death [35–37, 39, 41–43, 48–50, 52–61, 65–76, 78, 82] (except for one article [81], where mRS 0–1 was merged into one health state).

Intermediate Recovery Phase

In 13 articles, hypothetical patients entered an intermediate phase up to the end of the first year after the stroke in the base case, where they could keep their current health state, deteriorate, die, or recover by transitioning to a lower mRS health state in every cycle [37, 38, 40, 44–47, 58, 66–68, 77, 80]. In seven of these articles, recurrence was also possible within the intermediate phase, thus patients could keep their current health state, deteriorate, or die following a recurrent stroke [37, 40, 67], although in some articles it was not mentioned if keeping the health state was possible after recurrence [46, 47, 68, 77].

Long-Term Phase

Following the initial phase(s), hypothetical patients entered the long-term phase of the model. In three articles, they could only keep their health state or die [39, 74, 75]. In six articles, patients could keep their current health state, deteriorate, or die, without using recurrences as an explicit trigger for these transitions [38, 44, 45, 58, 66, 80]. By contrast, recurrence was modeled in all remaining articles. After recurrence, patients could either deteriorate or die [50, 53, 57, 61, 64, 65, 70, 72, 73, 76], or keep their health state, deteriorate, or die [35–37, 40–43, 48, 49, 52, 54–56, 59, 60, 62, 63, 67, 69, 71, 79, 81, 82]. Tunnel states were applied in two Markov cohort models to adjust transition probabilities (TPs) for time since the last stroke [36, 57]. In the article applying DES, time to recurrence and myocardial infarction was modeled [82]. For seven articles not all transitions were clear in the long-term phase [8, 46, 47, 51, 68, 77, 78].

Parameter Inputs

Short-Term Transition

The short-term efficacy of treatment up to 90 days after the initial stroke was based on randomized controlled trial (RCT) data in all but five articles, in which prospective cohorts, single-arm trials, or case-control-studies were used [8, 50, 51, 64, 82]. In a majority of articles based on RCT data, the five popular RCTs ESCAPE [84], SWIFT PRIME [85], MR CLEAN [86], REVASCAT [87], or EXTEND IA [88], or the HERMES meta-analysis by Goyal et al. [7] who pooled these RCTs, was used [35–40, 42–49, 52–55, 58, 60–62, 68, 69, 71, 74, 76, 78, 80].

Intermediate and Long-Term Transition

Different sources, such as prospective cohort studies, registries, or meta-analyses, were used to model the recurrence of stroke or time to recurrence. In particular, in 19 articles [36, 41–43, 48–50, 52, 56, 59–63, 70–73, 78] a Swedish cohort study by Pennlert et al. [89] was used to determine the long-term risk of recurrence.

The intermediate and long-term TPs after stroke recurrence were assumed to be the same as in the short-term phase excluding improvement in mRS health states in five articles [36, 54, 55, 65, 69]. This assumption was likely adopted but not explicitly stated in six other articles [48, 50, 53, 64, 70, 76]. Alternatively, these TPs were assumed to be the same as in the pooled control group of the HERMES meta-analysis [7] in nine articles [24, 52, 56, 59–61, 71–73]. The intermediate/long-term TPs in studies not modeling recurrence [38, 39, 44, 45, 58, 66, 80] were taken from the population-based Oxford vascular (OXVASC) study [90]. Non-stroke-related mortality was modeled using official national sources such as life tables in the majority of articles [35, 37, 38, 48–50, 52–57, 59–63, 65, 68–73, 75–79, 81, 82]. However, the reporting of sources for long-term TPs was partly inconsistent in 12 articles [8, 37, 40–43, 49, 64, 67, 68, 74, 79].

Utilities

In the majority of articles [42, 43, 48–50, 52, 56, 59–61, 71–73, 75, 78, 82], utility values from a study by Chaisinanunkul et al. [91] were used, who combined early published UK utilities by Rivero-Arias et al. [92] with the inverse disability weights obtained by international clinical experts [93]. These EQ-5D index-based utilities were assigned to mRS health states. In 11 articles, utilities from the study by Rivero-Arias et al. [92] were used without further adjustment [35, 38, 39, 41, 45, 47, 54, 55, 57, 69, 76]. Utility decrements due to recurrences were applied in 13 articles [40, 42, 43, 46, 47, 62, 63, 65, 67, 68, 77, 79, 82], whereas decrements due to adverse events were considered in two articles [62, 63]. However, tariffs were often not country specific [35, 38, 39, 41–45, 47–50, 52, 55–57, 59–61, 63, 64, 69, 71–76, 78, 80, 82], or sources were not entirely clear or not available [40, 42, 63, 68].

Costs

Direct costs such as hospitalization, medication, or staff costs were included in all articles, and most articles adopted a (public) payer’s perspective [35–37, 40, 45–47, 52, 54, 55, 57, 58, 62, 63, 65, 67, 69, 70, 72, 73, 75, 77, 79–82]. In addition, a societal perspective was claimed to be adopted in the remaining articles, although in two articles indirect costs were not incorporated [53, 64].

Indirect costs included productivity losses [38, 39, 44, 48, 50, 56, 59–61, 66, 71, 74, 76, 78] and informal care costs [38, 39, 41–44, 56, 59, 60, 66, 68, 71, 74]. However, some cost sources were not country specific [37–39, 42, 48–50, 56, 57, 59–61, 71, 78], or sources were inconsistently reported or partly not available [35, 42, 61, 68, 71, 78, 79].

Uncertainty

Deterministic sensitivity analyses (DSAs) or scenario analyses were applied in all but two articles [47, 60]. However in some cases, costs [59, 61, 71, 74], utilities [8, 56, 59, 61, 71–74], or TPs [67, 68] were not included. Regarding TPs, many articles varied short-term effectiveness input, but not long-term TPs [8, 44–46, 48–50, 56, 59, 61, 66, 71–74, 80]. Other parameters included in DSAs/scenario analyses were starting age [35, 36, 42, 44, 45, 50, 51, 55–58, 64, 68–70, 73, 76, 78, 80–82], time horizon [35–38, 42, 44, 58, 62, 63, 69, 76, 79, 80, 82], or discount rate [35, 36, 41–45, 54, 56–58, 62, 63, 68, 69, 75–77, 79–82]. However, parameter ranges differed greatly, for example, ± 20%, ± 30%, or ± 50% of the base case. Furthermore, sources used to justify these ranges were often not reported [8, 35, 37, 39, 41, 43, 46, 49, 51–53, 55–57, 62–64, 68, 72, 73, 76, 77, 81].

Probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSAs) were conducted in all but four articles [37, 58, 64, 76]. However, some articles did not include TPs [72–74, 82], costs [42, 69, 82], or utilities [38, 41, 42, 48–50, 69, 82]. Furthermore, the parameters included were unclear in three articles [8, 45, 55].

Parameter correlation was claimed to be assessed in one article, although the method used was not further specified [45]. In the remaining articles, parameter correlation was not mentioned, or parameters were assumed to be uncorrelated [41, 42, 82]. Structural uncertainty was assessed by testing assumptions in scenario analyses, for example, the ability to recover to a lower mRS health state or to maintain the same health state after a recurrent stroke [37, 54, 55].

Validation

Face validation was defined as a subjective overall evaluation of the model’s structure, sources, and results [34, 94] and was given in all but one study [45] by being published in peer-reviewed journals. Furthermore, ten articles internally validated the models (e.g., by extreme value analysis) [39, 41, 43, 45, 48, 54, 68, 70, 74, 81]. In all but three articles, results were compared with other modeling studies (cross-validation) [45, 48, 60], whereas results were compared to actual event data in 14 articles (external validation) [36–38, 40–43, 48, 51, 60, 68, 76, 78, 82]. Comparing the simulation of a pre-published study design with the actual study results (predictive validation) was not reported in any article.

For example, all but five articles used high-quality evidence such as RCTs and cohorts for TPs (item 18) [8, 50, 51, 64, 82]. However, many articles used country-unspecific sources for costs or utilities [35, 37–39, 41–45, 47–50, 52, 55–57, 59–61, 63, 64, 69, 71–76, 78, 80, 82], and sources were not consistently reported for these parameters [35, 40, 42, 61, 63, 68, 71, 78, 79] (affecting items 37 and 27). Furthermore, assessment of patient heterogeneity was often lacking, as variations in population characteristics such as age or clinical aspects were insufficient (item 29) [8, 35, 38, 40, 43, 45–48, 51–58, 60–64, 66–70, 72, 74–79, 81, 82].

Although the model uncertainty dimension was reflected in all articles as DSAs, scenario analyses, or PSAs were applied, only a few authors considered correlations among parameters [41, 42, 45, 82] or structural uncertainty [37, 54, 55] (items 34 and 36). Furthermore, all studies were validated to some extent, whereas a small portion conducted internal validation [39, 41, 43, 46, 48, 54, 68, 70, 74, 81] or external validation [36–38, 40–43, 48, 51, 60, 68, 76, 78, 82] (items 45 and 47).

Discussion

This systematic review assessed the methodological quality regarding health economic evaluations of EVT for acute IS using decision modeling in 49 articles. Although many reviews in this research field exist [10, 16, 21–25], none had evaluated methodological approaches in detail. Using a checklist by Ramos et al. [34], we identified several methodological limitations relating to model design, parameter inputs, uncertainty, and validation. Table 3 provides relevant modeling recommendations for future studies.

Model Design

The health states were based on mRS levels in all articles, which is justified given that this disability scale is often used as an outcome measure in stroke trials owing to its good psychometric properties [95]. However, some authors merged multiple mRS levels into single health states [8, 38, 40, 44–47, 51, 62–64, 77, 79–81], resulting in information loss [20]. Furthermore, a short-term phase capturing outcomes 90 days after the stroke was employed in all articles, as evidence shows that long-term health outcomes strongly depend on the outcomes 90 days post-stroke [20, 96]. Some authors also included an intermediate recovery phase of up to 1 year after the stroke, during which patients could transition to lower mRS health states [37, 38, 40, 44–47, 58, 66–68, 77, 80]. This is justified given the results of the OXVASC study, in which 23.1% of patients recovered from an mRS level of 3 to 1 or 2 within the first year and recovery beyond the first year was unlikely [96, 97]. Moreover, deteriorations were triggered by recurrences in most models, although deterioration following a recurrent event was sometimes assumed to be mandatory, without clinical justification for this assumption [50, 53, 57, 61, 65, 70, 72, 73, 76].

We therefore recommend using each mRS level individually as health states, as well as justifying and testing assumptions in scenario analyses. These assumptions relate to recovery in the first year, and to recurrences followed by health state deterioration/stability or by deterioration only.

Parameter Inputs

Endovascular treatment was compared with SC including intravenous thrombolysis in all but 11 articles, in which some authors failed to clearly define the comparator [41, 43, 61, 63, 66, 72, 73, 75, 77, 78, 81]. High-quality evidence was predominantly used, as short-term efficacy was derived from HERMES collaboration RCT data [7] [35–40, 42–49, 52–55, 58, 60–62, 68, 69, 71, 74, 76, 78, 80], and long-term recurrence risk was modeled using a Swedish population-based cohort study [89] [36, 41–43, 48–50, 52, 56, 59–63, 70–73, 78]. It should be noted that this particular cohort study included relatively young patients, and recurrence risk may have decreased in recent years because of improved secondary prevention [98]. In addition, some authors did not clearly describe TPs [8, 46, 47, 51, 68, 77, 78] and their corresponding primary sources [8, 37, 40–43, 49, 64, 67, 68, 74, 79].

Although all articles modeled age-dependent TPs (e.g., mortality), a minority systematically varied age, thus limiting generalizability [35, 36, 42, 44, 45, 50, 51, 55–58, 64, 68–70, 73, 76, 78, 80–82]. Furthermore, modeling recurrence risks was challenging, as these depend on the time since the last stroke [89]. The typical Markovian “memorylessness” assumption commonly prevents time dependency, thus some authors used microsimulation [43] or tunnel states [36, 57], while another study opted for a DES [82]. However, the implementation of DES and microsimulation is more complex than Markov cohort modeling [18, 99], and tunnel states often involve large numbers of health states [100].

Cost inputs were derived from numerous sources that were mostly country specific, whereas utilities were predominantly sourced from two UK studies [91, 92], which are considered the gold standard translation of mRS levels into EQ-5D index values [16]. However, as many included studies were not UK based, 63% of the articles lacked country-specific utility values [35, 38, 39, 41–45, 47–50, 52, 55–57, 59–61, 63, 64, 69, 71–76, 78, 80, 82], despite the fact that health state valuation differs greatly between countries [101]. Furthermore, only a few of the included articles assigned utility decrements for recurrences [40, 42, 43, 46, 47, 62, 63, 65, 67, 68, 77, 79, 82].

Overall, the sources and methods used for parameters should be described in detail and provided clearly in tables and text. Authors should aim to identify country-specific costs and utilities and assign utility decrements for recurrences. In addition, authors should incorporate age and time dependency and reflect on their choice of model type (e.g., Markov cohort or DES), as it was done in three articles [43, 81, 82].

Uncertainty

Promisingly, all but six articles employed both PSA and DSA/scenario analyses to assess univariate and multivariate parameter uncertainty [37, 47, 58, 60, 64, 76]. However, some articles did not include key parameters such as costs, utilities, or TPs [8, 38, 41, 42, 48–50, 56, 59, 61, 67–69, 71–74, 82]. In addition, many articles only used short-term effectiveness inputs but not long-term TPs in DSA/scenario analyses to identify parameter thresholds that could change the adopted decision [8, 44–46, 48–50, 56, 59, 61, 66, 71–74, 80]. Furthermore, deterministically varying one or more parameters in DSA/scenario analyses of Markov cohort models while holding the others at their mean value may be imprecise because of the nonlinear nature of these models, which also applies to the base case, where the mean of input parameters does not necessarily generate the mean in outcomes [102]. In order to address this issue, recent recommendations aim at using probabilistic simulations both in the base case and in DSAs [103, 104]. Only one article using a Markov cohort model took these considerations into account [45]. Additionally, uncertainty assessment may be biased by correlated parameters, for example, sampling utilities from independent distributions could lead to implausible cases, where the assigned utility for mRS level 5 is higher than for mRS level 4 [104, 105]. This issue was mentioned in only four articles [41, 42, 45, 82].

Taken together, future studies should include key parameters such as costs, utilities, and TPs in both DSA/scenario analyses and PSAs, applying values from the literature [106]. Ideally, probabilistic simulations should be included throughout the analyses including the base-case and DSA/scenario analyses [103, 104]. Correlations between parameters must be discussed, and preferably considered by including methods such as coupling of marginal distributions using correlation matrices [105].

Validation

Face validation was given at least to a certain extent by the peer-review process in all but one article [45]. However, external validation was performed only in a fraction of articles, thereby limiting the assessment of model generalizability to real-world scenarios [36–38, 40–43, 48, 51, 60, 68, 76, 78, 82]. Additionally, only ten articles internally validated their results [39, 41, 43, 45, 48, 54, 68, 70, 74, 81], although mathematical models are prone to errors in their equations and programming code [34, 94]. Consequently, the results should be compared to real-world data. Moreover, internal validation should be mandatory, for example in the form of extreme value analysis, and its implementation should be described in detail [34, 94].

Strengths and Limitations

One strength of our study was the in-depth analysis of methodological limitations of the articles included, for which we used a comprehensive framework by Ramos et al. [34]. This framework synthesizes several previous guidelines for assessing the methodological quality of decision modeling studies into one questionnaire. Furthermore, specific recommendations were compiled in a concise table for the convenience of future modelers.

However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, several other reviews regarding the cost-effectiveness of EVT have already been published in recent years, focusing on synthesizing results or analyzing input studies [10, 16, 21–25]. Nevertheless, our review contributed to the existing literature by focusing on methodological aspects. Second, our review only relied on information reported in the articles. Notwithstanding, failing to report information compromises transparency and reproducibility, potentially constituting a methodological limitation on itself. However, failure to report may also be due to limited publication space and thus does not necessarily indicate that important steps have been omitted [107]. Third, the inclusion criteria restricted the included articles to cost-effectiveness analyses comparing EVT with SC. Hence, other comparators (e.g., different EVT devices) and economic study types (e.g., cost analyses) were excluded. Nonetheless, this step seemed justified, as these were the most common analysis types, and it improved methodological comparability across studies.

Conclusions

Based on this review, health economic decision models for EVT indicate room for improvement. This could be achieved by incorporating recovery within the first year after stroke and using each mRS level individually as health states, as well as considering recurrences. It would also be beneficial to use country-specific costs and utilities. Furthermore, DSAs/scenario analyses and PSAs should be conducted, incorporating all key parameters (utilities, costs, and TPs), and the models should undergo internal validation.
