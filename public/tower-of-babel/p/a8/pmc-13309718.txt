Recurrent Pericarditis Evolving to Constrictive Pericarditis and Pericardiectomy Over 18 Years
Authors: Muhammad Ehsan, Jibran Ikram, Michael Villalonga, Joseph El Roumi, Saad Ehsan, Alveena Batool Syed, Mohammad Alqahtani, Carmela D. Tan, Allan L. Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrent pericarditis (RP) is a clinical condition managed with anti-inflammatory medications, with a subset of patients developing overt constriction requiring pericardiectomy.Case SummaryA 38-year-old woman with idiopathic RP presented with dyspnea, lower extremity edema, and chest pain after years of relapsing disease not adequately controlled by nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, colchicine, and corticosteroids. Rilonacept was initiated late in the disease course and could not halt progression. Multimodality imaging and right heart catheterization confirmed constrictive pericarditis, and she ultimately underwent radical pericardiectomy followed by gradual recovery on tapering immunosuppression. Pathological analysis demonstrated organizing fibrous pericarditis.DiscussionThis case highlights the importance of early recognition of high-risk RP, illustrates the role of multimodality imaging in guiding decision-making after pericardiectomy, and raises questions regarding the potential role and optimal timing of IL-1 inhibitors in modifying disease progression before constrictive physiology sets in.Take-Home MessageThe role and timing of biologic therapy in RP requires further research, while pericardiectomy remains definitive once fibrosis predominates.

History of Presentation

A 38-year-old woman presented to the Pericardial Diseases Center at Cleveland Clinic with worsening dyspnea, orthopnea, lower extremity edema, pleuritic chest pain, and palpitations. She had a long-standing history of recurrent pericarditis (RP) that had progressed to clinical features of constrictive pericarditis (CP) despite medical therapy. Her dyspnea had become functionally limiting. On physical examination, she was normotensive with a heart rate of 97 beats/min. She had leg swelling, jugular venous distension to the angle of the jaw, and a positive Kussmaul's sign. Cardiac auscultation revealed normal heart sounds with no murmur or rub; however, a pericardial knock was appreciated.Take-Home Messages•Whether earlier initiation of IL-1 inhibition in selected high-risk patients with recurrent pericarditis can alter progression toward irreversible constrictive disease remains an important unanswered question.•There is a need for prognostic markers that can signal the transition of recurrent pericarditis to constriction.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of idiopathic RP. She began experiencing pleuritic chest pain in 2006, which remained undiagnosed until 2011, when her clinical symptoms were corroborated by elevated inflammatory markers (erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR]: 54 mm/h) and echocardiographic evidence of a pericardial effusion. She was started on high-dose nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). From 2011 to 2022, she experienced multiple recurrences that were responsive to NSAIDs and colchicine. In March 2023, she was admitted to an outside hospital with a significant pericardial effusion that was subsequently drained (750 mL of fluid). She was initiated on a 3-month taper of prednisone in addition to colchicine. Over the following year, she had 3 recurrences during steroid tapering, with symptoms of fluid overload in addition to the classic chest pain. Rilonacept was initiated in March 2024 but stopped within a month owing to a lack of response. She exhibited symptoms of fluid overload, such as worsening edema and orthopnea, with normal inflammatory markers (ESR: 14 mm/h, C-reactive protein [CRP]: <2.9 mg/dL) and echocardiographic findings (thickened pericardium, septal bounce, and ventricular interdependence) suggesting the development of constriction. Right heart catheterization demonstrated elevated biventricular pressures. She was started on high-dose corticosteroids.

The patient ultimately presented to the Pericardial Diseases Center in June 2024 with classic manifestations of CP, such as pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, orthopnea, leg swelling, abdominal distension, elevated jugular venous pressure, presence of pericardial knock, positive Kussmaul sign, and elevated inflammatory markers (ESR: 23, ultrasensitive CRP: 12.2 mg/L). The complete timeline of her pericarditis episodes is depicted in the Visual Summary.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included idiopathic restrictive cardiomyopathy and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.

Investigations

At her initial evaluation at the Cleveland Clinic (June 2024), transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a small pericardial effusion near the left ventricle. The patient exhibited echocardiographic signs of CP, including a thickened pericardium, septal bounce, respirophasic septal shift, annulus reversus (septal e′: 17 cm/s, lateral e′: 15 cm/s), respiratory variation in peak mitral E-wave velocity of 38% (expected cutoff: <25%), and reversal of hepatic vein end-expiratory diastolic flow. Mitral inflow Doppler demonstrated an E/A ratio of 1.8 and a low E/e’ ratio of 4.7, which is suggestive of annulus paradoxus. The inferior vena cava (IVC) was dilated (2.3 cm) and noncollapsible. Management

Given the chronic and debilitating course of RP with evidence of heart failure, the patient was treated with aggressive anti-inflammatory medications, including prednisone 30 mg, colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily, and rilonacept 160 mg subcutaneously once a week. She was counseled on exercise restriction and was started on metoprolol 25 mg and furosemide 60 mg. Her chest pain improved along with the normalization of inflammatory markers in 1 month. However, symptoms of fluid overload persisted. She was admitted in September 2024 for preoperative work-up. MRI showed reduced inflammation with mild residual LGE (Figure 3). Noncontrast computed tomography demonstrated severe circumferential pericardial thickening (7.5 mm on the lateral left ventricle). Right heart catheterization confirmed moderate equalization of diastolic pressures. Radical pericardiectomy was performed, with pathology confirming a markedly fibrotic pericardium with organized and organized pericarditis with mild neovascularization (Figure 4). Outcome and Follow-Up

By October 2024 (1-month postoperatively), her symptoms had improved, although inflammatory markers remained elevated. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed persistent septal bounce and dilated IVC. She continued on rilonacept and a tapering steroid regimen. By November 2024, there was a significant improvement in both pericardial and pleural effusions. By January 2025, she was asymptomatic with no evidence of constriction. MRI demonstrated mild LGE of the pericardial space, likely consistent with postoperative changes. She had successfully tapered off steroids and was advised to stop rilonacept and taper torsemide gradually. As of October 2025, the patient is doing well without any medications.

Discussion

Despite advances in the understanding of pericardial diseases, RP remains a challenging clinical condition with several potential sequelae, including progression to CP.1 CP represents the advanced stage of pericardial inflammation, characterized by the loss of elasticity, impaired diastolic filling, elevated biventricular filling pressures, and clinical manifestations of right-sided heart failure. Preventing this transition is the goal of therapy, with pericardiectomy reserved for medically refractory or chronic fibrotic disease.2 Our case exemplifies this transition: recurrent episodes of pericarditis before eventually transitioning to chronic constriction requiring pericardiectomy.

Most patients with recurrent pericarditis respond to anti-inflammatory medications such as NSAIDs and colchicine, with corticosteroids reserved for refractory cases.3 However, steroid dependence is associated with recurrent flares and a myriad of adverse events. IL-1 inhibitors, such as rilonacept and anakinra, have revolutionized the landscape,4 demonstrating a rapid reduction in recurrence with significantly fewer adverse events than steroids, and they have recently been recommended as second-line in autoinflammatory cases ahead of steroids.5 Yet, the potential of IL-1 blockers in reversing or preventing the progression to constriction remains uncertain. In our patient, rilonacept was initiated only after constrictive physiology was well established. Although symptoms improved, the therapy could not stop the transition to constriction. This case highlights an important question: whether earlier initiation of biologics—particularly in patients with high-risk phenotypes—could be disease modifying by interrupting the inflammatory-to-fibrotic cascade. The optimal timing, duration of therapy, and patient selection for biologic therapy in preventing constriction warrants further investigation.

The identification of patients with RP who are primed to progress to CP is essential, so that such patients can be managed aggressively.6 Traditional monitoring with inflammatory markers such as ESR and CRP lacks sensitivity, as normalization of biomarkers may obscure ongoing remodeling. Imaging modalities such as echocardiography, MRI, and computed tomography are now essential for detecting early constrictive features. Invasive hemodynamic assessment remains the gold standard for confirming fixed constriction.1 In this patient, MRI and catheterization findings confirmed irreversible disease. However, no established guidelines exist for risk prediction, highlighting the need for further research to identify reliable prognostic markers.

Once chronic fibrotic constriction sets in, pericardiectomy remains the definitive therapy.7 Determining the timing for surgical intervention is critical: Premature surgery in inflammatory transient constrictive cases may pose unnecessary risk to the patients, while delayed surgery increases perioperative morbidity and mortality. In this patient, persistent fluid overload, elevated right-sided pressures, and imaging evidence of severe constriction favored irreversible disease, necessitating pericardiectomy. Pathology confirmed organizing fibrous pericarditis.

Postpericardiectomy management posed challenges related to medication duration and discontinuation. Although formal guidelines are lacking, pharmacologic therapy is often continued to reduce inflammation and prevent recurrence. Our patient had persistently elevated inflammatory markers, presence of LGE in pericardial space on cardiac MRI, and some echocardiographic evidence of constriction (septal bounce and dilated IVC), leading to the continuation of prednisone and rilonacept, followed by gradual tapering. The presence of elevated inflammatory markers and residual LGE guide the duration of anti-inflammatory therapy after pericardiectomy.5,8 Incomplete resolution of clinical and biochemical disease occurs because of several mechanisms including incomplete pericardial resection, presence of concomitant myocardial disease, presence of pleuropericarditis, epicardial involvement, presence of adhesions, and radiation-related heart disease.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 In our case, the presence of elevated inflammatory markers and mild LGE largely reflects postsurgical residual inflammatory changes that were ultimately controlled by medications. It is also important to note that the presence of severe LGE at baseline is a reflection of severe inflammatory disease and is a bad prognostic factor.6 There is no consensus on whether biologics should be abruptly discontinued or slowly tapered after surgery; current practice is individualized, balancing recurrence risk against drug burden.

Conclusions

Recurrent pericarditis can transition to chronic constrictive disease, requiring intensified management. Although IL-1 inhibitors can be used in high-risk cases, pericardiectomy remains the standard of care when fibrosis is well established. Further research should prioritize the identification of prognostic markers and determine whether biologic therapy can modify the clinical course of recurrent pericarditis.
