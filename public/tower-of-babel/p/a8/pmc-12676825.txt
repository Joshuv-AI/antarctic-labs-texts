Relationship between diet quality and inflammatory bowel disease: a systematic review of observational studies
Authors: Parisa Karimi, Elmira Ramezani, Shakila Dehghani, Nastaran Mahmoudi Shirkoohi, Ariyo Movahedi, Mohammad Masoumvand, Ali Jafarzadeh Esfehani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a prevalent digestive disorder, influenced by various factors, particularly dietary choices and overall diet quality. Various indices assess diet quality, yet their correlation with IBD severity and symptoms remains uncertain. This systematic review examines the relationship between IBD and diet quality, as measured by the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), Healthy Eating Index (HEI), and Diet Quality Index (DQI).MethodsObservational studies investigating the association of DII, HEI, and DQI with IBD risk, severity, symptoms, quality of life, body composition, serum calprotectin, and inflammatory markers were selected without date or geographic restrictions. A systematic search of PubMed and Google Scholar was conducted on February 2024, following PRISMA guidelines.ResultsThe search yielded 6,995 articles, of which 14 observational studies (comprising 129,465 adults living with IBD) met the inclusion criteria. Seven studies examined DII, five assessed HEI, one analyzed both DII and HEI, and one investigated DQI in people with IBD.ConclusionFindings suggest that a lower dietary inflammatory load (as indicated by DII) is associated with reduced IBD risk, severity, and symptoms, alongside improved quality of life. However, further studies are needed to clarify the role of DII, HEI, and DQI in managing IBD management.Keywords: Diet quality, Inflammatory bowel disease, DII, HEI, DQI

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a common chronic disease that affects the digestive system [1]. Two main types of IBD include ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD) [1]. The prevalence of this disease is increasing rapidly in the world [2], In 2019, there were an estimated 4.9 million cases of IBD worldwide, compared to 3.32 million in 1990, representing a 47% increase [3]. In Iran, the prevalence of IBD is also increasing rapidly, so it is predicted that by 2035, 69 thousand new cases will be added to the population of people with IBD [4].

The exact cause of IBD is not yet understood. Still, it appears that a combination of genetic and environmental factors, including unhealthy lifestyle, smoking, stress, and poor nutrition and dietary choices, can impact the development of chronic inflammation and the immunological processes associated with IBD [5, 6]. The typical signs and symptoms of IBD consist of abdominal discomfort, loss of weight, changes in bowel movements, and often presence of blood in the stool [1]. While IBD can be mild for some, it can be severely debilitating for others, impacting physical, social, and psychological well-being and potentially leading to life-threatening complications if not managed effectively. Currently, treatment plans focus on minimizing the frequency of the symptoms, with common approaches including medication such as corticosteroids, antibiotics, amino salicylates, Immunomodulators and biologics [7, 8].

Diet and its quality are crucial environmental factors that impact the onset and progression of IBD through inflammatory response and immune system regulation [9, 10]. Previous studies have demonstrated that inappropriate nutrition can weaken the immune system and worsen inflammation [11]. Consuming high saturated fat and high simple carbohydrate diets, like the Western diet, has been linked to worsening symptoms and severity of IBD [12, 13]. Conversely, reports are suggesting that the risk of developing IBD decreases as the consumption of plant-based foods such as fruits and vegetables increases [14]. The Mediterranean diet, for example, has been shown to have a protective effect against IBD and was associated with a lower risk of developing CD and UC [15]. A higher diet quality has been associated with better clinical outcomes, reduced disease activity, and improved quality of life for individuals with IBD [16].

Several indicators have been used in previous studies to assess diet quality, including the healthy eating index (HEI) and the dietary inflammatory index (DII) [17, 18]. Another determinant of diet quality is the Diet Quality Index (DQI), which evaluates the adequacy, moderation, variety, and balance of a diet [19, 20]. However, existing studies have only examined the separate association of each of the HEI, DII, and DQI indices with disease severity, symptoms, quality of life, inflammatory markers, and body composition in IBD patients [21]. This systematic review is the first to investigate and compare the relationship between various indicators of diet quality and disease severity, symptoms, quality of life, inflammatory markers, and body composition in patients with IBD.

The HEI is a tool created to assess the overall nutritional quality of an individual’s diet according to recommendations such as the Dietary Guidelines for Americans and USDA Food Patterns. Over time, the HEI has been revised to align with updated guidelines. For instance, the HEI-2015 update incorporates the most recent recommendations, while the HEI-2010 version was developed to coincide with the 2010 Dietary Guidelines for Americans [22].

This systematic review explores the relationship between diet quality, as measured by the HEI, DII, and DQI, and disease severity, symptoms, quality of life, inflammatory indices, and body composition in patients with IBD.

Methods

The current systematic review was carried out using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyzes (PRISMA) checklist. To identify studies for this systematic review, searches were conducted in the PubMed and Google Scholar databases without any limitations on time or location, up to February 17, 2024. Flow diagram of the literature search process

Keywords used for the search included inflammatory bowel disease, IBD, ulcerative colitis or UC, Crohn’s disease or CD, diet quality, diet quality index, dietary quality index, healthy eating index, dietary inflammatory index, DQI, DII, HEI, HEI-2015, Dutch Healthy Diet Index 2015, DHD-2015, Adapted Dietary Inflammatory Index, ADII which were individually or combined in PubMed and Google Scholar databases, and articles were searched.

The inclusion criteria were observational studies (including cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies) published in English and with full-text availability, focusing on adult patients with IBD and evaluating the relationship between diet quality indicators (such as DQI, DII, HEI) and disease severity, symptoms, quality of life, calprotectin levels, inflammatory indices, and body composition. The patients included in the observational studies of this systematic review must meet the following criteria: be over 18 years of age, not pregnant or breastfeeding, have no history of other gastrointestinal diseases, gastrointestinal surgeries, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, cancer, inflammatory and infectious disorders, or autoimmune diseases, and must have confirmed diagnosis of IBD by a specialist within the last 6 months.

Two researchers independently searched and selected studies based on the inclusion criteria. If the researchers did not agree with the selection of the articles and did not meet all the inclusion criteria, they were excluded from the systematic review. The review included studies from various countries, types of studies, sample sizes, age ranges of patients, nutritional assessment tools, dietary quality indices, and findings related to the correlation between diet quality indicators and disease severity, symptoms, quality of life, inflammatory indices, calprotectin, and body composition in patients with IBD (such as UC and CD).

The quality and risk of bias of the observational studies included in this systematic review were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). This scale evaluates each study based on three main perspectives: (1) How the study groups were selected, (2) The comparability of the groups, and (3) The exposure or outcome of interest for case-control or cohort studies. The tool’s rating system uses a “star system” to rate each study based on three main perspectives. Each section is awarded a star or point, resulting in scores ranging from 0 to 9. Studies whose final score is less than 6 are of low quality and studies whose final score is 6 or more are of high quality [23, 24].

Results

A total of 6995 studies were initially found in the PubMed and Google Scholar databases. After reviewing the studies, 2204 duplicated studies were excluded. In addition, 4778 studies were excluded based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, this systematic review included 14 observational studies, 7 of which were related to DII [25–30], 5 related to HEI [16, 21, 31–33], 1 related to DQI [34], and 1 related to the simultaneous relationship of DII and HEI with IBD [35] (Fig. 1).

Based on the findings of the studies outlined in Table 2, this systematic review included 7 cross-sectional studies, 5 cohort studies, and 2 case-control studies. Most of the studies were conducted in the Netherlands (5 studies), followed by Iran (4 studies), Brazil (2 studies), and USA, China, and Canada (one study from each country). The articles were published between 2016 and 2024. 10 studies were conducted after 2020 and 4 studies were conducted in 2020 and before. This systematic review included a total of 129,465 patients with IBD. The findings showed that 753 people were diagnosed with CD, 1019 people were diagnosed with UC, and the rest of the patients did not have a clear classification of IBD type. Out of the 14 studies, 12 utilized validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [37, 38] to assess the nutritional status of patients, while the remaining 2 studies used the 24-hour food recall questionnaire.

Dietary quality index (DII) and IBD

Research examining the relationship between DII (or EDII) and IBD patients has demonstrated a significant positive relationship between DII and disease severity. This suggests that an increased inflammatory diet is linked to increased disease severity in these patients [25, 26, 36]. However, these findings were not supported by Mirmiran.et al.l’s study [30]. Additionally, Lamers et al. [29] reported this relationship only in cases of Crohn’s disease, with no direct correlation observed in ulcerative colitis.

The study by Rocha et al. (2023) [26] and Vagianos et al. (2021) [28] reported a direct and statistically significant correlation between DII and calprotectin level. However, in the study of de Graaf MCG et al. (2022), this relationship was not observed and the increase in DII was not related to the increase in fecal calprotectin [35].

In addition, in a cohort study by DuBois et al. (2024) higher DII was found to be associated with an increase in fatigue, depression, anxiety, and sleep disorders in patients with IBD. Furthermore, an increase in this index was also linked to a significant reduction in quality of life and social satisfaction among these patients [25].

The findings of the study conducted by de Graaf et al. (2022) indicated a significant correlation between DII and abdominal pain in patients with IBD. However, no relationship was observed with other symptoms of the disease or digestive issues such as constipation, diarrhea, and reflux [35].

In a cross-sectional study by Rocha et al., no significant relationship was reported between DII and CRP, as an inflammatory index [26]. The results of Bian et al.‘s study showed that increased dietary inflammation is associated with increased muscle wasting and sarcopenia in CD patients [27].

Healthy eating index (HEI) and IBD

The results from research on the relationship between HEI (or DHD-2015) and the likelihood of developing IBD, as well as its severity and symptoms, did not show a significant link between HEI and the risk of IBD [21, 32]. However, Khalili et al. (2023) [31] found a significant inverse relationship between HEI-2015 and the risk of developing CD, and Rahmani et al. (2019) reported a significant inverse relationship between HEI-2015 and the risk of developing UC [33].

Most studies did not find a strong correlation between HEI and IBD symptoms [21, 35]. However, Lamers et al. (2022) demonstrated that an increase in Dutch Healthy Diet Index 2015 (DHD-2015) was linked to reduced fatigue among patients [16]. Additionally, de Graaf et al. (2022) found a relationship between HEI and decrease in fecal calprotectin levels [35].

In the included studies, only body mass index (BMI) was investigated as one of the anthropometric indicators, which in the study of Lamers et al. (2022) found no significant relationship between DHD-2015 and BMI in their study [21].

Dietary quality index (DQI) and IBD

In this systematic review, only one study examined the DQI in patients with IBD. The cross-sectional study by Cruz et al. (2021) found a direct and significant relationship between these two indices [34].

Discussion

Based on the findings of the present study, despite the limited number of observational studies examining the relationship between DII, HEI, and particularly DQI indicators in IBD patients, findings suggested diet quality as an influencing factor on the risk, severity, and symptoms of IBD. By reducing the consumption of inflammatory foods and ensuring the appropriateness of the variety, quality, and quantity of diet, the risk, symptoms, and severity of IBD can potentially be reduced, leading to improved quality of life for these patients.

Considering the inflammatory nature of IBD, the DII seems to have a more obvious correlation with the outcomes, symptoms and severity of IBD compared to other indicators of diet quality. One reason for this stronger correlation may be that the DII directly measures dietary inflammatory factors. A higher DII score, which suggests a pro-inflammatory diet, has been linked to heightened disease activity and less favorable clinical results in individuals with IBD. Conversely, a lower DII score, indicating an anti-inflammatory diet, has been linked to improved clinical outcomes [25, 26]. This systematic review revealed that among the evaluated indices, the DII index had the strongest relationship with IBD risk and symptom severity. The findings show that consuming more inflammatory diets is associated with an increased risk of IBD, greater disease severity, and decreased quality of life in IBD patients. Dietary changes may offer some symptom relief, thus increasing quality of life people with IBD. Dietary changes may offer some symptom relief, thus increasing quality of life [39]. Research has indicated that certain dietary patterns that may promote inflammation are linked to a higher risk of developing CD [40]. For instance, switching from a high fiber diet to a high protein diet (such as animal protein, ultra-processed foods, fried foods and emulsifiers) could alter the balance of bacteria in the gut, reducing the breakdown of fiber by bacteria such as Prevotella spp. An imbalance in gut microflora, known as dysbiosis, is associated with the progression of IBD [41]. Urban populations exposed to a Western diet have been found to have lower levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in their stool, which are known to have anti-inflammatory effects in IBD [42]. Hence, even though we are only beginning to clarify the connection between the inflammatory properties of diet and the risk and severity of IBD, it appears reasonable to acknowledge that diets with a high proinflammatory index are linked to IBD.

The HEI score can range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a higher quality diet [43]. Higher scores on the HEI are reported to be strongly associated with lower risk of major chronic diseases including diabetes, heart failure, colorectal and breast cancer [44, 45]. Unfortunately, to date, only a few studies have explored the relationship between HEI and the severity and symptoms of IBD, and the findings have been inconsistent. A systematic review revealed that HEI is linked to a decreased risk of UC, but due to the limited number of studies in this area, the results were inconclusive regarding the significant relationship between HEI and IBD severity [24]. Additionally, another study found a negative relationship between HEI and the risk of CD, but this relationship was not observed in relation to the risk of UC [31].

The DQI is a tool utilized for assessing the overall quality of a person’s diet using specific standards [46]. DQI consists of four main dietary components, which include variety, adequacy, moderation, and overall balance. 20 points are given to the first two cases, 30 points to moderation and 10 points to the overall balance [47]. Based on the total score calculated from these 4 sections, if the final score is 40 or less, it is classified as an inadequate diet, 41 to 64 as a diet in need of modification, and 65 or more as a healthy diet [34]. Research has demonstrated connections between DQI scores and different health results [48, 49]. For instance, a higher DQI-I score has been associated with improved lipid profiles, particularly reduced levels of total cholesterol and increased levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [46]. Our systematic review identified only one study that examined the DQI in patients with IBD. The findings of the study revealed a significant positive relationship between DQI and the BMI of patients with CD. None of the patients had a diet categorized as healthy, highlighting the importance of addressing the dietary status of CD patients and enhancing the quality of their diets [34].

The diet quality of people with IBD can be improved in ways other than traditional in-person care models. Remote or virtual monitoring by a nutrition consultant improves the quality of the diet of people with IBD by providing continuous, personalized dietary advice and close follow-up that traditional in-person care models often cannot achieve. This approach, often called tele nutrition, leverages electronic communication tools to offer quick, accessible, and cost-effective nutrition therapy tailored to the complex and fluctuating nature of IBD. It allows for ongoing nutritional assessment, education, and behavioral monitoring to adjust dietary recommendations based on symptom changes and nutritional status, which helps manage symptoms, prevent deficiencies, and support long-term disease management [50].

The current systematic review is the first study that examines the relationship between diet quality based on three nutritional indices DII, HEI and DQI with the severity and symptoms of the disease and the quality of life of people with IBD, which is one of the strengths of the present study. Another advantage of this study was the comprehensive review of related literature, without being restricted by the publication date or location. However, like other systematic reviews, this study has limitations such as the use of different questionnaires to assess the diet of patients, which depends on memory and may lead to inaccuracy in reporting data and determining the quality of diet. In addition to the recall bias, FFQ questionnaires may not have the same number of questions and some food items are not included in the indicators in the questionnaire or in the population’s diet. Furthermore, a number of studies did not consider the influence of confounding variables on the relationship between diet quality indices and IBD. Another limitation was the scarcity of studies on HEI and DQI in people with IBD, which prevents reaching a definitive conclusion about the relationship between these indicators and the severity and symptoms of IBD. It is also important to note that AI-driven diagnostic tools surpass traditional clinical assessments, suggesting that other advanced biomarkers or approaches are more accurate predictors for clinical outcomes. This highlights that DII, HEI, and DQI may have limited accuracy in predicting clinical outcomes in comparison to newer diagnostic methods, with some differences between UC and CD due to the pathophysiological and diagnostic complexities involved. Also Articles reporting the outcomes on populations that followed special diets such as vegan and Atkins were excluded, as they may have biased the study results.

Conclusion

In general, it can be said that the quality of the diet is one of the most important pillars of the IBD disease prevention and treatment protocol that should be taken into consideration. Most of the studies indicated the relationship between diet quality indicators and the increase in the severity or symptoms of IBD. According to the reviewed studies, DII had a stronger relationship with the severity of IBD and its symptoms than other indicators of diet quality. However, to confirm this relationship, more studies are needed in the future.
