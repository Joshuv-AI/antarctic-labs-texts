Novel expression and localization of hypoxia inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1α) and selected angiogenesis-related factors in the oviduct of laying and non-laying hens
Authors: Marta H. Machura, Karolina Frydrych, Andrzej Sechman, Anna Hrabia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Egg formation and oviposition in hens depend on a network of blood vessels that supply the oviduct with all the essential molecules. This network develops during angiogenesis, a process involving many regulators, including hypoxia inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1α), the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) system, and the angiopoietin (ANGPT) system. Given the lack of information on HIF1α and angiogenesis in the avian oviduct, this study examined the expression of HIF1α, VEGFA and its receptors, ANGPT1 and ANGPT2 and their receptor mRNA transcript and protein abundances, as well as protein localization in the oviduct of laying and non-laying hens. A pause in laying was induced by fasting for five consecutive days. On day 6 of the experiment, tissues were harvested from control hens and hens that had been fasted. Quantitative RT-PCR and western blot analyses revealed changes in the expression of the examined factors between the distinct segments of the oviduct. In laying hens, the mRNA expression of genes related to angiogenesis was highest in the infundibulum, shell gland, and vagina and lowest in the magnum. In fasted hens, partial oviductal regression was accompanied by a decrease in mass, thinner epithelium, and degenerating tubular glands. Fasting also affected gene expression in oviductal parts, with the most pronounced upregulation occurring in the isthmus and vagina. The presence of both the unmodified and modified forms of HIF1α was detected, with higher abundances of the modified form observed in fasted hens. Immunoreactivity for HIF1α and VEGFA proteins was observed in all tissues of the oviductal wall, with strong immunopositive signals for HIF1α in the muscles and for VEGFA in the luminal epithelium and blood vessels. In the oviduct of fasted hens, immunoreactivity for HIF1α and VEGFA proteins was relatively stronger than in the oviduct of control hens. The results indicated that HIF1α and other factors associated with angiogenesis might play a role in regulating oviductal vascularization in hens, under both physiological conditions and fasting-induced regression. Furthermore, our findings provide novel insights into the involvement of HIF1α and angiogenesis-related factors in the function and remodeling of the avian oviduct.

Introduction

The avian oviduct plays a crucial role in egg formation and egg-laying performance. This organ consists of five anatomically and functionally distinct segments. Each segment is responsible for specific processes involved in egg formation and transport, including ovum fertilization in the infundibulum, egg albumen secretion in the magnum, formation of shell membranes in the isthmus, and eggshell calcification in the shell gland. Finally, the vagina helps with oviposition and stores spermatozoa in the sperm storage tubules, located in a narrow region at the utero-vaginal junction (Sah and Mishra, 2018; Hrabia, 2022).

The oviduct is highly vascularized, with a dense network of arteries, veins, and capillaries that supply both the mucosal folds and the muscular layers. Egg formation necessitates the synchronized delivery of oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and calcium to the oviductal tissues. This phenomenon is particularly evident in the shell gland, where eggshell calcification occurs over approximately 18 to 20 h. This process is associated with the deposition of 5 to 6 g of calcified eggshell, concomitant with a significant increase in metabolic activity in this part of the oviduct (Hincke et al., 2012; Nys et al., 2022). Thus, disruption of the blood supply or metabolic imbalance may result in improper egg formation (Machura and Hrabia, 2024). Despite the physiological importance of vascularization for oviductal function, the mechanisms regulating vascularization and angiogenic signaling in this organ remain poorly understood in birds.

Angiogenesis, defined as the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing vasculature, is a tightly regulated process involving endothelial cell activation, proliferation, migration, and vessel stabilization (Risau, 1997; Folkman, 2006). In the context of reproductive tissues, the process of angiogenesis serves as a fundamental mechanism that facilitates cyclic remodeling and functional adaptation (Reynolds et al., 2002).

A central regulator of angiogenic signaling is hypoxia inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1α), a transcription factor stabilized under conditions of reduced oxygen availability (Semenza, 2000; Wenger et al., 2005). The formation of a heterodimer between HIF1α and HIF1β is a prerequisite for the transcriptional activation of numerous genes involved in angiogenesis, glucose metabolism, and cell survival (Bakleh and Al Haj Zen, 2025; Carmichael et al., 2025). It is acknowledged that one of the most significant downstream targets of HIF1α is vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), a stimulator of endothelial cell activation and proliferation, vascular permeability, and vessel formation (Ferrara et al., 2003; Olsson et al., 2006). VEGFA is known to exert its biological effects primarily through its receptors: VEGFR1 (also known as fms related receptor tyrosine kinase 1 [FLT1]) and VEGFR2 (also called kinase insert domain receptor [KDR]).

In addition to the VEGFA system, the angiopoietin family has been demonstrated to play a crucial role in vascular remodeling. Angiopoietin 1 (ANGPT1) and angiopoietin 2 (ANGPT2) have been determined to interact with the tyrosine kinase receptor TIE2, thereby regulating vessel maturation and destabilization (Maisonpierre et al., 1997; Augustin et al., 2009). The binding of ANGPT1 to TIE2 promotes endothelial stabilization and the recruitment of supporting cells. Conversely, ANGPT2 acts as a context-dependent antagonist, facilitating vascular remodeling and angiogenic sprouting (Saharinen et al., 2017). Collectively, these signaling pathways form a complex regulatory network that governs vascular adaptation.

Although angiogenic mechanisms within the ovary and uterus have been thoroughly characterized in mammals (Gargett and Rogers, 2001; Rizov et al., 2017; Dompe et al., 2021; Guzmán et al., 2023; Middelkoop et al., 2023), data concerning the regulation of angiogenesis in the avian reproductive system remain limited. Previous studies in hens have demonstrated the expression of HIF1α (Machura and Hrabia, 2026) and other angiogenesis-related factors in ovarian follicles (Kim et al., 2016; Machura and Hrabia, 2026), and have suggested a role for VEGFA in ovarian pathology (Urick et al., 2008). However, there is currently no information regarding the role of HIF1α and other angiogenesis-related factors in the oviduct of birds, although they have a high potential to be implicated in the orchestration of oviduct function. Considering the high metabolic and secretory activity of the hen oviduct, we hypothesized that the expression of key angiogenic regulators is segment-specific and sensitive to metabolic stress, such as fasting (an environmental physical stressor). Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the mRNA expression and protein abundance of major angiogenic factors (HIF1α, VEGFA, ANGPT1, and ANGPT2), as well as the expression of VEGFA receptors (VEGFR1 and VEGFR2) and ANGPT receptor (TIE2), in the oviduct of laying (peak of lay) hens and hens that had been subjected to a fasting-induced pause in egg laying. Furthermore, immunofluorescent localization of HIF1α and VEGFA proteins was conducted to assess their cellular distribution within oviductal tissues.

Material and methods

Experimental birds and tissue sampling

The Hy-Line Brown hens were obtained from a commercial farm and housed in individual cages under a photoperiod of 14L:10D, with unlimited access to commercial feed and water. The time of oviposition was recorded daily for each hen, allowing the determination of individual cycles. In accordance with previously described protocols (Leśniak-Walentyn and Hrabia, 2016; Socha et al., 2017; Socha and Hrabia, 2019; Hrabia et al., 2020; Frydrych et al., 2025; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025), 32-week-old hens (peak of lay) were randomly divided into two groups, with 8 hens in each: control (C; fed ad libitum) and experimental (F), which was subjected to an induced pause in egg laying by complete feed withdrawal for five days, i.e., until a pause in laying occurred in all fasted hens. Hens of both groups were humanely euthanized on the sixth day of the experiment. The control (laying) hens were euthanized 2–2.5 h after oviposition, when the egg was located in the oviductal magnum, as confirmed by autopsy. At this stage of the ovulatory cycle plasma concentrations of ovarian steroids are relatively low. Throughout the experiment, all procedures were performed in accordance with ethical guidelines. The hens’ health and well-being were regularly monitored to minimize stress and discomfort. The entire oviduct was quickly harvested, and each segment was collected: infundibulum, magnum, isthmus, shell gland, and vagina. Tissue samples (entire wall) collected from the middle part of each segment were stored in RNAlater (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) for future RNA isolation, or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent western blot analysis. Additional tissue fragments were fixed in 10% buffered formalin (phosphate buffer, pH 7.6) and embedded in paraffin wax for further histological and immunohistochemical analyses. The animal experiment was conducted in accordance with the research protocol approved (approval No. 218/2015) by the Local Animal Ethics Committee in Krakow, Poland.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

Oviductal tissues collected from the oviductal segments of 5 randomly chosen hens of each group were used for total RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and qRT-PCR, performed as recently described (Hrabia et al., 2020; Frydrych et al., 2025; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025; Machura and Hrabia, 2026). In brief, total RNA was isolated from tissues using TRI-reagent (Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, 2 μg of total RNA from each sample was reverse-transcribed with a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), containing random primers, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Subsequent to 10x dilution, the resulting cDNA was utilized for qRT-PCR with chicken-specific TaqMan Gene Expression Assays for HIF1α, VEGFA, VEGFR1 (FLT1), VEGFR2 (KDR), ANGPT1, ANGPT2, and TIE2 (Table 1), using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The PCR conditions were as follows: 50°C for 2 min, followed by 95°C for 10 min, then 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 60 s. The duplex qRT-PCR for the examined gene and the reference gene (18S rRNA) was performed in a 10 μL volume containing: 5 μL of TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.5 μL of TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.5 μL of Eukaryotic 18S rRNA Endogenous Control (established as an appropriate control for oviductal tissues and fasting treatment; Applied Biosystems, by Thermo Fisher Scientific), 3 μL of water, and 1 μL of cDNA. Each sample was subjected to analysis in duplicate. To ensure the reliability of the results, a no-template control was included in each run. The relative quantity (RQ) of each gene mRNA was calculated after normalizing against the 18S rRNA transcript. Protein extraction and Western blotting

To examine the expression of angiogenic factors at the protein level, oviductal tissue fragments collected from 8 hens of the control and 8 hens of the experimental group were crushed in liquid nitrogen and dissolved in cold lysis buffer (BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA). The mixture was sonicated and then centrifuged at 4°C for 20 min at 10000 × g (Frydrych et al., 2025; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025; Machura and Hrabia, 2026). The protein concentration in the resulting supernatant was determined using the Bradford protein assay with Pierce Detergent Compatible Bradford Assay Reagent (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA), with bovine serum albumin serving as the standard.

Subsequent Western blot analyses were performed according to protocols previously described by Hrabia et al. (2022), Frydrych et al. (2024, 2025), Machura and Hrabia (2026). Tissue samples containing 60 μg of total protein were mixed with loading buffer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), denatured at 99°C for 7 min, and loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel (12% for VEGFA, and 10% for HIF1α). The proteins were separated by electrophoresis under reducing conditions using a Mini-Protean Tetra Cell apparatus (Bio-Rad). The resolved proteins were then transferred to a PVDF membrane using a semi-dry blotter in FLASHBlot transfer buffer (Advansta, San Jose, CA, USA) at a constant voltage of 25 V for 7 min. The membranes were then blocked for 1 h in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (TBST, pH 7.6) and 5% non-fat milk. The membranes were then washed and incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary rabbit antibody for either HIF1α (dilution 1:500; #20960-1-AP; Proteintech, Germany) or VEGFA (dilution 1:1000; #BS-4572R; Bioss, USA; or #PA1-16948; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The specificity of the primary antibodies was previously confirmed in avian tissues (Brennan et al., 2020; Yap et al., 2025; Machura and Hrabia, 2026). After thorough washing, the membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000; #R-05072-500; Advansta) for 1 h. Detection of HIF1α and VEGFA proteins was achieved using enhanced chemiluminescence (WesternBrightTM ECL; Advansta), and images were captured using a ChemiDoc-It 410 imaging system with VisionWorks Life Science software (Ultraviolet Products Ltd., Cambridge, UK). The membranes were then stripped and reprobed with HRP-conjugated mouse anti-β-actin (1:5000; 1 h; #A2228; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The HIF1α and VEGFA protein bands were quantified using ImageJ v. 1.8.0 (developed by the National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to their respective β-actin bands.

Immunohistochemistry

According to a standardized protocol (Hrabia et al., 2022; Frydrych et al., 2024, 2025; Machura and Hrabia, 2026), paraffin-embedded oviductal tissues were sectioned at 6 μm, mounted onto microscope slides, and used for the immunofluorescent localization of HIF1α and VEGFA proteins. Firstly, the tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol. The sections were then washed in water and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwaving them in a citrate buffer solution (pH 6.0) for three min. To prevent non-specific binding of the secondary antibody, the sections were incubated in 5% normal goat serum (v/v) in TBST at room temperature for ∼20 min. Following blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with polyclonal rabbit antibodies against HIF1α or VEGFA (the same as used for Western blot) at final dilutions of 1:75 for HIF1α and 1:50 for VEGFA. After washing in TBS, the sections were incubated for 90 min with DyLight 594-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (#DI-1594-1.5; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Then, they were washed again in TBS and mounted using VECTASHIELD® Vibrance™ Antifade Mounting Medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Non-specific staining was controlled by replacing the primary antibody with normal rabbit serum or TBST. Oviductal tissue sections (three–four sections/ oviductal segment/ three hens/ group) were analyzed using an Axio Scope.A1 fluorescence microscope, an Axiocam 503 color camera, and Zen 2.3 Pro software to capture images (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Immunoreactivity intensity was categorized subjectively as strong, moderate, weak, or very weak. The micrographs show a merge of blue fluorescence, indicating DAPI-stained cell nuclei, and red fluorescence, indicating an immunopositive reaction specific to HIF1α and VEGFA.

Masson’s trichrome staining

To outline the microarchitecture and visualize the various morphological features of the oviductal wall, the Masson’s trichrome staining (MTS) was performed using a special staining kit (DiaPath, Martinengo, Italy) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. In summary, deparaffinized and rehydrated oviductal tissue slides were stained with Weigert’s ferric haematoxylin for 10 min. The slides were then subjected to an alcoholic solution of picric acid for 4 min, followed by a brief rinse in distilled water. Thereafter, the slides were stained with a solution of Biebrich’s Scarlet-Fuchsin acid for 4 min and rinsed in distilled water. The differentiation was carried out in phosphomolybdic acid for 10 min and then stained with aniline blue for 4 min. Finally, the slides were dehydrated and mounted in DPX (Sigma-Aldrich). Slides were examined using an Axio Scope A1 light microscope fitted with an Axiocam 503 color camera and ZEN 2.3 Pro software (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Using this method of staining, collagen fibers and mucus are stained blue, muscle fibers, keratin, and cytoplasm red, erythrocytes yellow, and cell nuclei black.

Statistical analysis

The variables were examined for normality and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro-Wilk and Brown-Forsythe tests, respectively. Since the data of relative gene expression in the oviduct of control hens (Fig. 2, Fig. 3) did not pass normality (P < 0.05) and/or homogeneity of variance (P < 0.05), they were analyzed statistically using a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA on ranks, followed by Dunn’s or Student-Newman-Keuls tests. To compare the means of two groups (fasted versus control; Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6), a Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used. Differences in values were considered significant at P < 0.05. All calculations were performed using SigmaPlot_V13 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Changes in the oviduct weight and histoarchitecture during a pause in laying

The hens, which had been subjected to an induced pause in egg laying, ceased egg laying primarily on the fourth day of the experiment (3.75 ± 0.31). The oviduct weights of fasted hens were found to be 62.3% lower than those of control hens (Tables 1S and 2S; Hrabia et al., 2020; Frydrych et al., 2025).

As demonstrated in Fig. 1, alterations in the composition of the oviductal wall structure are evident upon examination with MTS staining. This histological analysis revealed degeneration and a reduction in the height of the luminal epithelium in all oviductal segments. Additionally, involution of the tubular glands and their disorganization in the magnum, isthmus, and shell gland were observed in the fasted hens. Furthermore, MTS staining revealed alterations in the arrangement of elastic fibers within the oviductal wall. In control hens, an organized network of elastic fibers was observed within the connective tissue layers and around blood vessels. The highest mRNA expression of HIF1α was observed in the infundibulum, and the lowest was in the magnum. HIF1α mRNA expression was approximately 96.5% lower (P < 0.001) in the magnum than in the infundibulum. In the isthmus, shell gland, and vagina, HIF1α mRNA expression was lower by approximately 43%–74%, respectively, relative to the infundibulum (P < 0.001; Fig. 2A).

VEGFA mRNA demonstrated a distinct expression profile, with the most abundant mRNA transcript observed in the shell gland. In contrast, VEGFA expression was lower by approximately 76% in the magnum, and by 44% in the isthmus, compared with the infundibulum (P < 0.001; Fig. 2B).

The relative expression profiles of ANGPT1 and ANGPT2 mRNA exhibited a high degree of similarity along the oviduct. For ANGPT1, the highest transcript levels were observed in the infundibulum and vagina. Conversely, ANGPT1 mRNA levels were lower by approximately 99% in the magnum and 77% in the isthmus, relative to the infundibulum (P < 0.001; Fig. 2C). ANGPT2 mRNA expression was the highest in the infundibulum, shell gland, and vagina. In the magnum, ANGPT2 mRNA transcript abundance was lower by approximately 96%, whereas in the isthmus, it was about 78% lower than in the infundibulum (P < 0.001; Fig. 2D).

The expression patterns of VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 mRNA were comparable along the oviduct (Fig. 3A, B). The lowest levels of VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 expression were observed in the magnum, where transcript levels were lower by approximately 92%–94% than in the infundibulum (P < 0.001). In the isthmus, VEGFR2 mRNA expression was lower by 77% in comparison with the infundibulum (P < 0.001).

The profile of TIE2 mRNA transcript abundance exhibited a comparable pattern to that observed for VEGFR1 and VEGFR2. The highest expression level of TIE2 was observed in the infundibulum, shell gland, and vagina. In contrast, TIE2 mRNA transcript abundance in the magnum was approximately 95% lower than in the infundibulum (P < 0.001; Fig. 3C).

Fasting caused a decrease in HIF1α mRNA abundance by 64% (P = 0.003) in the infundibulum, and an increase by 110% (P = 0.002) in the vagina, compared to control hens. No significant changes were observed in the magnum, isthmus, and shell gland in hens after fasting (Fig. 4A).

In fasted hens, VEGFA expression in the isthmus and vagina was significantly higher by 110% (P = 0.002) and 73% (P = 0.007), respectively. No significant differences were observed in the infundibulum, magnum, or shell gland after fasting (Fig. 4B).

The profiles of ANGPT1 and ANGPT2 mRNA expression exhibited a similar response to fasting. In the isthmus, fasting resulted in an increase in the transcript abundance of ANGPT1 and ANGPT2 by 297% (P < 0.001) and 187% (P = 0.038), respectively, when compared to the controls. A comparable pattern was observed in the vagina, where ANGPT1 and ANGPT2 mRNA transcript abundances were found to be elevated by 90% (P = 0.05) and 111% (P = 0.02), respectively. Statistically significant changes in ANGPT1 or ANGPT2 expression were not detected in the infundibulum, magnum, and shell gland (Fig. 4C, D).

In the infundibulum, VEGFR1 transcript abundance was reduced (P = 0.029) by 49% compared to control hens, whereas VEGFR2 expression did not change (P > 0.05) in this oviductal segment. In the vagina, VEGFR2 mRNA level was increased (P = 0.05) by 74% in fasted hens. No significant differences in VEGFR1 or VEGFR2 expression were detected in the magnum, isthmus, or shell gland following feed deprivation (Fig. 5A, B).

The abundance of TIE2 mRNA transcript was found to be significantly increased in the magnum, isthmus, and vagina by 636% (P = 0.003), 122% (P = 0.003), and 195% (P = 0.014), respectively, in fasted hens, compared to controls. In contrast, TIE2 expression was reduced by 59% (P = 0.029) in the shell gland. No significant change was observed in the infundibulum (Fig. 5C).

In control hens, strong positive immunoreactivity for the VEGFA protein was localized in the luminal epithelium and in the vascular endothelium of all segments of the oviduct. Moderate or weak staining was observed in the tubular glands of the magnum, isthmus, and shell gland. Weak or very weak immunoreactive signals were noted in stromal muscle cells (Fig. 9A–E). In the oviduct of fasted hens, the VEGFA immunofluorescent signals were relatively stronger than in the control ones, particularly in the luminal epithelium and vascular endothelium of the infundibulum and vagina, as well as in the tubular glands of the shell gland (Fig. 10).

Discussion

This study presents a thorough investigation into the expression of HIF1α, along with several angiogenesis-related factors (VEGFA, ANGPT1, ANGPT2), and their receptors (VEGFR1, VEGFR2, and TIE2), in various segments of the oviduct of laying (peak of lay) and fasted (non-laying) hens. Importantly, this is the first research to investigate the expression and immunolocalization of HIF1α and angiogenesis-related factors in the chicken oviduct under physiological conditions, as well as in the regressing oviduct of the hen during a pause in laying induced by fasting. The results may indicate that the regulation of vascularization in the oviduct is a complex and dynamic process that depends on the functional specificity of distinct segments of the oviduct and on physiological changes occurring during the regression.

HIF1α plays a key role in regulating the processes of angiogenesis, cellular metabolism, and cell survival (Srivastava et al., 2023), as it is the primary transcription factor responsible for the cellular response to hypoxia. This factor exerts a regulatory influence over the expression of numerous genes associated with angiogenesis, including the gene encoding VEGFA (Semenza, 2012; Pugh and Ratcliffe, 2017). The observed variation in HIF1α expression levels across distinct segments of the oviduct may suggest that local metabolic conditions and oxygen demand differ between the segments. This phenomenon can be attributed to the unique physiological functions exhibited by individual segments of the oviduct. These functions are related to the capture of the ovum, egg transport, the secretion of egg white components, and the process of shell calcification. Consequently, local shifts in the balance between oxygen demand and availability may lead to periodic stabilization of HIF1α and activation of genes responsible for metabolic adaptation and regulation of the local blood vessel network.

Notably, the presence of HIF1α protein within the muscles of the hen oviduct, as observed for the first time in this study, suggests a potential for this factor to play a key role in the metabolic adaptation of the oviduct, beyond its function in regulating vascular remodeling. It should be stressed, that the role of HIF1α in the muscles might be related to participation of this factor in the local energy generation (Srivastava et al., 2023), required for myometrial contraction, essential for egg moving along the oviduct. This can at least in part explain the higher expression of HIF1α in the infundibulum, which is characterized by an enhanced contractility around the time of ovulation (Elhamouly et al., 2017). The transportation of the ovum through the segments of the oviduct necessitates the concerted action of the muscles, resulting in a substantial demand for energy, in the form of ATP. Such robust smooth muscle contractions invariably result in mechanical compression of the local microcirculation, inducing transient states of physiological tissue hypoxia (Semenza, 2012). In response to these fluctuations in oxygen supply, HIF1α stabilization acts as a primary metabolic switch. This process can stimulate glycolysis by inducing the expression of glucose transporters and key glycolytic enzymes (Iyer et al., 1998; Kierans and Taylor, 2021). This rapid molecular adaptation enables smooth muscle cells and highly active secretory epithelial cells to sustain continuous ATP production under hypoxic conditions. This mechanism ensures optimal oviductal contractility and the efficiency of secretory processes, which are necessary for the formation of eggs and the maintenance of the reproductive cycle (Pugh and Ratcliffe, 2017).

A significant aspect of HIF1α regulation is its complexity, involving multilevel mechanisms, including post-transcriptional and post-translational processes. The stability of HIF1α mRNA and the efficiency of its translation can be modulated, among others, by microRNAs and RNA-binding proteins, enabling the rapid regulation of protein levels independent of transcription (Pugh and Ratcliffe, 2017; Bakleh and Al Haj Zen, 2025). This regulatory mechanism may assume particular significance in tissues undergoing rapid functional transitions, where precise modulation of the cellular response is imperative (Yang et al., 2019).

Post-translational modifications have been demonstrated to exert a more direct effect on HIF1α activity (Albanese et al., 2021). In conditions of normoxia, the protein undergoes hydroxylation at the instance of enzymes belonging to the family of prolyl hydroxylase domain proteins (PHDs). This process subsequently results in the protein's degradation through the action of the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) complex (Ivan et al., 2001; Jaakkola et al., 2001). The process is inhibited by restricted oxygen availability, allowing HIF1α to stabilize and activate its transcriptional function. It is noteworthy that subsequent modifications, including asparagine hydroxylation by factor inhibiting HIF (FIH), phosphorylation, or acetylation, have been demonstrated to modulate the post-translational activity and cellular localization of this protein (Lando et al., 2002; Kaelin and Ratcliffe, 2008; Albanese et al., 2021). The presence of two forms of HIF1α, as observed in this study and recently in the ovarian atretic follicles of hens (Machura and Hrabia, 2026), may be indicative of varying degrees of these modifications, thus suggesting the existence of complex regulation of its activity in the hen oviduct.

Protein level analyses provide direct evidence that, under conditions of fasting-induced oviductal partial regression, post-translational mechanisms may play a more prominent role than transcriptional regulation. A marked discrepancy was observed between gene expression and protein abundance, particularly evident in the infundibulum. Despite a significant decrease in HIF1α mRNA transcript abundance, the levels of both forms of this protein were markedly elevated. The observed discrepancy may indicate the inhibition of intracellular HIF1α degradation and its intense stabilization, which is characteristic of an acute response to oxidative or metabolic stress (Semenza, 2012; Kuiper et al., 2014).

Furthermore, the detection of two forms of HIF1α protein (unmodified ∼93 kDa, and modified ∼110 kDa) provides deeper insight into the dynamics of activation. While fasting elevated the levels of both HIF1α protein forms, it was the modified form that exhibited a pronounced, several-fold increase in oviductal segments such as the infundibulum, shell gland, and vagina. The presence of this heavier fraction is indicative of advanced post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation or acetylation, which are crucial not only for protecting the protein from ubiquitination but, above all, for its full post-translational activation (Ke and Costa, 2006; Dimova et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2021). The marked prevalence of the modified form indicates that chicken oviductal cells do not merely passively accumulate HIF1α; rather, they actively amplify its signaling potential in response to feed deprivation (Pugh and Ratcliffe, 2017). The types of HIF1α modifications in the hen oviduct during its fasting-induced partial regression remain to be elucidated.

The functional significance of HIF1α in oviductal tissues can be attributed primarily to its ability to regulate angiogenesis. VEGFA is one of the key effectors of this pathway. Its expression is directly activated by HIF1α via hypoxia-responsive elements in the gene's promoter region (Forsythe et al., 1996; Ahluwalia and Tarnawski, 2012). An increase in VEGFA levels may result in enhanced proliferation and migration of endothelial cells, as well as increased vascular permeability (Ferrara et al., 2003). In cannot be ruled out that in the avian oviduct, this mechanism might be responsible for a local increase in blood flow in regions of high secretory activity, which is essential for ensuring adequate transport of all necessary molecules. To establish the precise roles of different forms of HIF1α and their importance in the hen oviduct function and remodeling, further experiments are necessary. In particular the use of techniques for functional knockout of genes and their products appears to be valuable for elucidating the role of each specific gene in the egg constituent synthesis in the distinct segment of the oviduct.

Angiogenesis encompasses not only the formation of new blood vessels but also their remodeling and stabilization. The angiopoietin system has been identified as a regulatory mechanism that governs these processes. ANGPT1 has been shown to promote vascular stabilization by activating the TIE2 receptor, while ANGPT2 functions as its functional antagonist, thereby enabling vascular wall destabilization and remodeling (Maisonpierre et al., 1997; Augustin et al., 2009). The simultaneous presence of these factors may indicate that there is a constant balance between the stability and flexibility of the vascular network in the oviduct, enabling it to rapidly adapt to changing physiological conditions. Of note herein, the observed differences in the mRNA transcript abundances of ANGPTs, as well as other examined genes, between the oviductal parts, with the lowest levels in the magnum, may be attributable to the cellular composition of tissues and secretory function of the given segment. The last may be associated with the domination of egg white or egg shell protein gene expression over the expression of genes related to cell metabolism. This assumption may also explain the very low or undetectable level of β-actin protein in the magnum and isthmus, in contrast to its constant expression in the infundibulum, shell gland, and vagina. Moreover, the expression levels of HIF1α and other angiogenic factors may be closely related to the location of the egg in the oviduct. Therefore, it seems necessary to undertake further studies to determine the gene expression of these factors at both the mRNA and protein levels in the hen oviduct at several stages of the egg formation cycle, which is accompanied by changes in ovarian steroid concentrations in blood plasma, the crucial regulators of oviductal activity (Hrabia, 2022). Furthermore, the influence of sex steroids on HIF1α and other angiogenic factor gene expression and regulation in the avian oviduct must be clarified.

It is imperative to acknowledge that the regulation of angiogenesis in the hen oviduct is not confined to physiological conditions; it is also susceptible to substantial alterations in response to environmental and physiological changes, including variations in nutrient availability. Research has demonstrated that, during natural or a fasting-induced pause in laying, the regression of the ovary and oviduct is accompanied by a prominent decrease in circulating gonadal steroid concentrations (Paczoska-Eliasiewicz et al., 2003; Proszkowiec-Węglarz et al., 2005; Socha et al., 2017; Socha and Hrabia, 2019). In this particular context, our in vitro results reflect more complex fasting-induced changes in the oviductal microenvironment, with fasting serving as a useful model for analyzing the mechanisms that regulate angiogenesis under conditions of altered tissue metabolic activity, especially in response to limitation in sex steroid action. In our study, we observed, in most cases, increased expression of HIF1α and selected angiogenesis-related factors at the mRNA and/or protein levels in the oviductal segments (Table 2). This may suggest that the oviductal response to restricted nutrient availability is not limited to the inhibition of angiogenesis, but rather may involve the activation of compensatory mechanisms. One potential explanation for this phenomenon may be the induction of local hypoxic conditions resulting from reduced tissue perfusion and metabolic changes. The enhanced expression of proangiogenic factors during the regression process may reflect a physiological effort to preserve minimal tissue perfusion and prevent complex degeneration. In this context, the activation of the HIF1α-VEGFA-ANGPTs axis could serve a protective function, supporting cell survival by improving the delivery of oxygen and nutrients under conditions of limited availability (Zimna and Kurpisz, 2015; Pugh and Ratcliffe, 2017). Adaptive mechanisms of this kind have been documented in other tissues, where a transient increase in angiogenic signaling accompanies states of metabolic stress (Hellsten and Hoier, 2014; Kim et al., 2017). It should also be noted that the response of a given segment to fasting might be attributable to the physiological state and egg position in the oviduct of hens served as the control.

The hypothesis concerning the protective function of the HIF1α-VEGFA axis may be substantiated by protein localization. The enhanced immunoreactivity of HIF1α and VEGFA, as observed following a period of fasting, was predominantly localized to the muscles, vascular endothelium, and/or luminal epithelium. This finding suggests a targeted tissue response of precise nature. The luminal epithelium, a region characterized by elevated secretory activity and substantial energy requirements, exhibits a remarkable sensitivity to metabolic stress induced by fasting (Ariyadi et al., 2013; Leśniak-Walentyn and Hrabia, 2016; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025). The robust expression of VEGFA in this compartment, in conjunction with its marked presence in endothelial cells, suggests the existence of intense paracrine signaling. VEGFA, expressed by hypoxic epithelial cells, exerts a direct effect on the vascular endothelium, aiming to counteract its degeneration (Ferrara et al., 2003; Rey and Semenza, 2010). This phenomenon may represent a distinct lifeline mechanism, wherein local expression of proangiogenic factors aims to maintain minimal microvascular permeability and density. This is crucial for preserving the progenitor cell pool, which, subsequent to the regression period, will be responsible for the renewal of function by the oviduct (Rafii et al., 2016).

Nevertheless, the possibility cannot be discounted that the observed increase in the expression of angiogenic factors also may reflect an imbalance between vascular stabilization and remodeling processes. Specifically, alterations in the expression of ANGPTs may signify heightened vascular wall plasticity and augmented microcirculatory remodeling. In conditions of tissue remodeling, this process can lead to both the formation of new vessels and their destabilization, depending on the local signaling context (Maisonpierre et al., 1997; Augustin et al., 2009).

Consequently, it can be inferred that the angiogenic response observed in the regressing oviduct is not homogeneous, but rather the result of the coexistence of adaptive and degenerative processes. On the one hand, HIF1α activation and increased VEGFA expression may support cell survival; on the other hand, however, disturbances in vascular organization and function may limit the effectiveness of this response. The observed discrepancy between the activation of molecular signaling and the subsequent functional outcome may represent a pivotal component of the underlying mechanism responsible for oviductal regression. More work is required to determine whether the expression of genes related to angiogenesis depends on the factor that causes a pause in laying in hens, including photorefractoriness, aging, and molting.

Conclusions

Our results demonstrate that the expression of HIF1α and angiogenic factors, including VEGFA and ANGPTs, in the oviduct of hens depends on the specific segment of the oviduct and may be associated with tissue regression induced by fasting, at least in part (Table 2). Immunoreactivity for HIF1α and VEGFA protein depends on the cell type and physiological state of the oviduct wall. Thus, our findings provide novel insights into the potential involvement of the HIF1α-VEGFA-ANGPTs axis in orchestrating the vascularization, blood supply, and metabolism in the hen oviduct. Further studies are necessary to better understand the specific contributions of angiogenesis-related factors to the mechanisms underlying the functioning and remodeling of the hen oviduct under different physiological states.
