A Multicenter, Open‐Label, Phase 2 Trial Comparing Crizanlizumab Combined With Standard Therapy to Standard Therapy Alone on Renal Function in Patients With Sickle Cell Nephropathy (STEADFAST)
Authors: Kenneth I Ataga, Pablo Bartolucci, Santosh L Saraf, Vimal K Derebail, Adlette Inati, Jeffrey D Lebensburger, Laurie DeBonnett, Rodolfo D Cancado, Claire C Sharpe
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Sickle cell nephropathy (SCN) represents one of the most serious chronic complications of sickle cell disease (SCD), contributing substantially to morbidity and premature mortality [1, 2]. SCN manifests as hyperfiltration, albuminuria, and progressive loss of kidney function [3], with an estimated 12% of adults with SCD progressing to end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) over 10 years [4, 5, 6]. Albuminuria, especially urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (ACR) ≥ 100 mg/g, is among the strongest predictors of rapid estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline and future kidney failure [7, 8, 9, 10]. Despite this burden, no therapies are approved specifically for SCN. The recommended standard of care (SoC) includes use of angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin‐receptor blockers (ARBs) and/or hydroxyurea/hydroxycarbamide (HU/HC), although the supporting evidence is modest, largely from small or short‐term studies not powered for renal outcomes [11, 12, 13]. This underscores the need to evaluate new therapeutic strategies that target the mechanisms driving SCN progression.

P‐selectin mediates vaso‐occlusion by promoting interactions among sickled erythrocytes, leukocytes, and activated endothelium [14]. In preclinical studies, P‐selectin is upregulated after kidney ischemia–reperfusion injury, and blockade reduces neutrophil infiltration and glomerular injury [14, 15, 16]. Crizanlizumab, a humanized anti‐P‐selectin monoclonal antibody, reduced annualized vaso‐occlusive crises (VOCs) in the Phase 2 SUSTAIN trial and was initially approved to reduce VOC frequency in SCD [17, 18]. However, its role in mitigating kidney injury due to SCD has not been evaluated in a dedicated clinical trial.

We report STEADFAST, a multicenter, open‐label, randomized Phase 2 trial (NCT04053764) evaluating crizanlizumab plus SoC versus SoC alone for kidney outcomes in patients with SCN at moderate‐to‐very high risk of CKD progression.

Patients aged ≥ 16 years with confirmed HbSS or HbSβ0 thalassemia (six HbSβ+ genotype patients were enrolled in protocol deviations) were eligible if they had: eGFR 45–140 mL/min/1.73 m2 and ACR ≥ 100 and < 2000 mg/g, on stable SoC (ACE inhibitor and/or ARB ≥ 3 months and/or HU/HC ≥ 6 months). Patients with recent acute kidney injury, kidney replacement therapy, uncontrolled hypertension, or recent voxelotor use were excluded. Patients were stratified by CKD risk category (moderate vs. high/very high, based on ACR and eGFR Table S1) and HU/HC use, and randomized 1:1 to crizanlizumab 5 mg/kg intravenously (Weeks 1 and 3, then every 4 weeks for 12 months) plus SoC, or SoC alone (Figure 1).

The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients achieving ≥ 30% reduction in urine ACR at Month 12. Secondary endpoints included changes in ACR and eGFR over time, protein‐to‐creatinine ratio (PCR) response, CKD progression (defined by ≥ 25% eGFR decline and ACR increase), and annualized rates of VOC‐related emergency room (ER) visits and hospitalizations. Exploratory endpoints assessed changes in biomarker levels and improvements in health‐related quality of life (HRQoL). Safety was assessed using CTCAE v5.0.

The trial planned to enroll 170 patients but ended early due to recruitment challenges and a high screen‐failure rate, limiting power to detect between‐arm differences. The Full Analysis Set (FAS) comprised all patients to whom study treatment was assigned by randomization (ITT). The Safety Set included all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment.

Of 121 screened patients, 58 were randomized to crizanlizumab plus SoC (n = 30) or SoC alone (n = 28). The most frequent reasons for screen failure were ACR or eGFR outside the protocol‐specified ranges. Overall, 57 patients received study treatment and 45 completed the 12‐month treatment period (Figure S1).

The mean age of study participants was 41.5 years and 63.8% were female (Table S1). Most patients had severe genotypes (HbSS or HbSβ0 in 82.7%) and had experienced 0–2 VOCs in the prior year; 77.6% were on HU/HC at baseline. Baseline mean ACR was 528.3 mg/g and mean eGFR was 104.3 mL/min/1.73 m2. Based on the risk of CKD progression, moderate‐risk patients were 36.7% in the crizanlizumab arm and 53.6% in the SoC arm, and high/very high‐risk patients were 50.0% and 32.1%, respectively. The mean ACR was higher in the crizanlizumab arm compared with the SoC arm (565.9 vs. 489.4 mg/g), while the mean eGFR was similar between arms.

At Month 12, a ≥ 30% reduction in ACR from baseline was achieved by 10/30 (33.3%) patients in the crizanlizumab arm and 6/28 (21.4%) in the SoC arm (odds ratio [OR] 1.94; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.53–7.14). The absolute difference in proportions was 12% (95% CI, −11% to 35%). Given the small sample size and wide CI, this result should be interpreted as a favorable trend rather than evidence of a definitive treatment effect (Table 1).

Changes in ACR and eGFR (Figure S2a,b) showed favorable trends for the crizanlizumab plus SoC arm, with numerically greater reductions in ACR and a slower decline in kidney function than in the SoC alone arm. PCR improvement or stability at Month 12 was broadly similar between arms. Fewer patients in the crizanlizumab arm met criteria for CKD progression at 12 months (4/30 [13.3%] vs. 9/28 [32.1%]; OR 0.32; 95% CI, 0.09–1.21), suggesting, but not proving, a reno‐protective effect (Table 1). While the total number of VOC‐related ER visits was similar between groups, the annualized rate of hospitalizations and ER visits was lower in the crizanlizumab arm (Table S2).

HRQoL, measured using 36‐item Short Form Health Survey version 2 scores, improved numerically across all domains with crizanlizumab, with > 10‐point differences in general health, physical component score, and physical role compared with SoC alone, but the differences were not statistically significant (data not shown).

As expected, free P‐selectin rapidly fell to near the lower limit of quantification and remained low with crizanlizumab (Figure S3a,b), confirming target engagement, while increasing over time with SoC. Complement marker sC5b‐9 decreased more with crizanlizumab; other biomarkers (blood: NT‐proBNP, C3a, C5a, cystatin C, IFN‐γ, MCP‐1, tryptase, TNF‐α; urine: NGAL/lipocalin‐2, KIM‐1, NAG, nephrin) were similar between arms (Figure S3a–j).

Crizanlizumab plus SoC was generally well tolerated, with a safety profile consistent with prior SCD experience. Overall, AEs were more common with crizanlizumab than SoC (86.2% vs. 71.4%), but grade ≥ 3 AEs were less frequent (13.8% vs. 21.4%), with no grade 4/5 events (Table S3). Common AEs with crizanlizumab were nausea (13.8%) and arthralgia, fatigue, and pruritus (each 6.9%; Table 2). One patient (3.4%) had a grade 3 infusion‐related reaction. Serious AEs were infrequent and similar between arms (one grade 3 SAE each: sickle cell anemia with crisis with crizanlizumab; hyperthyroidism with SoC). One participant discontinued crizanlizumab due to a treatment‐related AE (sickle cell anemia with crisis). Infections (an adverse event of special interest [AESI]) occurred in 72.4% versus 39.3%; hematologic abnormalities were similar (Data S1 for detailed AESI).

Due to the exploratory nature of the analyses and wide CI, this Phase 2 STEADFAST study does not provide definitive evidence that crizanlizumab has reno‐protective effects but offers important insights into kidney outcomes in adults with SCN. Crizanlizumab treatment showed favorable trends in mean change in ACR from baseline, eGFR and ACR decline rates, proportion of patients with CKD progression and the mean annualized rate of hospitalization or ER visits due to VOCs. ACR also decreased in the placebo group, which may reflect improved adherence to standard‐of‐care medications, including RAAS inhibitors, during trial participation or regression to the mean with the known variability of urinary ACR in SCD. Crizanlizumab showed a favorable safety profile; although overall AEs including infections were more frequent in the crizanlizumab arm, they were mild with no grade 4 or 5 AEs. Unlike prior SCD trials where CKD status was not a primary focus [17, 19], STEADFAST specifically enrolled patients who have clinically significant ACR and heightened risk of CKD progression. By enrolling this high‐risk population, the study aimed to better evaluate crizanlizumab for SCD kidney manifestations and provide insights into its role in SCN management. However, inconsistencies in clinical practice for assessing CKD in SCD patients contributed to a high screen‐failure rate, recruitment challenges, and subsequent early termination.

Currently, no prospective randomized clinical trials have demonstrated long‐term benefits of any treatments for SCN, and no treatments have been approved for this indication in patients with SCD. The observed trends in reducing ACR, eGFR, and CKD progression with crizanlizumab align with the hypothesized impact of P‐selectin inhibition on microvascular vaso‐occlusion and inflammation in the kidney [15, 16]. Although prior [17, 19] and ongoing [20] trials in crizanlizumab were designed primarily to assess VOC‐related outcomes, their collective findings support the biological plausibility that a reduction in vaso‐occlusion through inhibition of P‐selectin‐mediated adhesion may also play a role in mitigating acute microvascular injury to the kidney or reducing the need for nephrotoxic analgesic medications.

In conclusion, while the study was underpowered to establish a reno‐protective effect of crizanlizumab in patients SCN, the observed trends suggest a potential benefit in reducing ACR. Larger, adequately powered randomized trials with longer follow‐up are required to determine whether P‐selectin inhibition can meaningfully modify the natural history of SCN and improve clinically relevant kidney outcomes.
