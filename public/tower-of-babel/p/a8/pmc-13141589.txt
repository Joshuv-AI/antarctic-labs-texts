Introduction

Hepatitis B virus infection remains a major global health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to screening and treatment is limited [1–3]. According to the World Health Organization, an estimated 254 million people were living with chronic HBV infection in 2022, resulting in about 1.1 million deaths annually, primarily due to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [4]. Despite the availability of effective vaccines and antiviral therapies, HBV continues to impose a heavy public health burden worldwide. The chronic nature of the infection, its often asymptomatic progression, and the long latency period before severe liver complications arise all contribute to its persistence and underdiagnosis. These challenges underscore the urgent need for improved understanding of HBV dynamics, both at the population and intra-host levels, to design more effective therapeutic interventions and optimize treatment protocols. At the intra-host level, HBV virus primarily targets hepatocyte, the main functional cells of the liver by binding to specific receptors that facilitate viral entry [5, 6]. Once inside the cell, the virus hijacks the host’s replication machinery to produce new viral particles, which are then released to infect surrounding healthy cells. This replication cycle leads to a progressive spread of the infection within the hepatic tissue. However, the spatial distribution of infected and healthy cells is not homogeneous [7, 8]. Local variations in immune response, tissue architecture, and viral diffusion may create clusters or reservoirs of infection. These spatial heterogeneities are further complicated by the dynamics of the immune system, particularly the recruitment and activation of immune cells such as lymphocytes, which attempt to control viral spread. Moreover, antiviral drugs must diffuse through various compartments including blood, liver tissue, and intracellular spaces to reach their targets effectively, making pharmacokinetic considerations essential for treatment success [9, 10]. Over the past two decades, mathematical modeling has emerged as a powerful tool to investigate hepatitis B virus dynamics and optimize antiviral strategies [11–15]. Several types of models have been developed to capture various aspects of the infection process, ranging from simple compartmental systems to more sophisticated spatiotemporal frameworks.

The earliest intra-host models of HBV infection were formulated using ordinary differential equations (ODEs), describing the temporal interactions among healthy hepatocytes, infected cells and free viral particles while neglecting spatial effects [16–19]. These compartmental models have provided valuable insights into viral replication mechanisms, treatment response and the estimation of key epidemiological parameters such as the basic reproduction number () [20, 21]. Nevertheless, the analysis do not take into account the tissue heterogeneity and local variations in drug concentration which significantly influence infection progression and therapeutic outcomes. More recently, partial differential equation (PDE) models have been developed to incorporate spatial diffusion and local interactions within tissues [22, 23]. The related reaction-diffusion (RD) models describe how viral particles, hepatocytes and immune components spread through spatial domains, enabling the investigation of pattern formation, wave propagation and spatial heterogeneity during infection [24–26]. In the context of HBV modeling, several RD models have assumed that hepatocytes remain spatially immobile under normal physiological conditions, while free viral particles diffuse within liver tissue [27, 28]. For instance, Wang and Wang [27], Xu and Ma [29] and Hattaf and Yousfi [28] considered that liver cells do not move, whereas viral particles follow Fickian diffusion. This assumption can reflect the biological structure of liver tissue, where hepatocytes are densely packed and essentially immobile over the time scale of viral dynamics, while viruses propagate through the extracellular space. Following this established modeling framework, spatial dependence is often restricted to the viral compartment, allowing the analysis of viral spatial propagation while preserving analytical tractability.

Despite their biological relevance, spatially explicit HBV models remain scarce. To account for biological time lags, delay differential equation (DDE) models were subsequently introduced [30–32]. These models capture the delays arising from viral incubation, the time between infection and the onset of immune responses, or the pharmacological latency of antiviral agents [33, 34]. This could explain oscillatory and persistent behaviors observed in clinical data. Despite substantial progress, several critical aspects of HBV intra-host dynamics remain insufficiently explored. Most existing models still rely on deterministic, fixed-delay assumptions and overlook the variability inherent in biological processes. In reality, delays associated with viral replication, immune activation, or drug pharmacodynamics exhibit random fluctuations due to inter-patient variability, immune system heterogeneity and variations in drug absorption or metabolism. The use of stochastic delay structures provides a more realistic framework to capture these uncertainties and to better understand the emergence of oscillations, persistence, or treatment failure observed in clinical practice. In addition, antiviral treatment profoundly affects infection dynamics by modulating viral replication, reducing infectivity and altering hepatocyte turnover. However, the combined influence of treatment efficacy, spatial diffusion and stochastic time delays on HBV progression has not been extensively characterized. To address these limitations, the present study introduces a novel reaction-diffusion model incorporating both stochastic delay dynamics and treatment effects. This framework integrates the spatial diffusion of free viral particles, while accounting for the variability in the time required for viral infection and therapeutic response. The rest of this paper is structured as follows. Section “Methods” presents the model formulation and analytical results. Section “Numerical results” reports the numerical results with emphasis on the effects of key parameters such as the stochastic delay and treatment efficacy on infection dynamics. Finally, Section “Discussion and conclusion” discusses the biological implications of the findings and outlines potential directions for future research.

To proceed with the analysis, we assume that the stochastic delay between the interaction of healthy hepatocytes and viral particles is relatively short. Discussion and conclusion

The proposed RD model with stochastic delay and antiviral treatment parameters provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the spatiotemporal behavior of hepatitis B virus infection. By extending previous deterministic models with a constant intracellular delay, the present work captures the random variability inherent to biological processes such as viral replication timing, infection latency and drug response.

The global sensitivity analysis (Fig. 3) provides important insights into the key parameters governing the steady-state viral load of HBV. The partial rank correlation coefficients reveal that the viral production rate exerts the strongest positive influence, indicating that viral replication is a major driver of infection persistence. Conversely, viral clearance shows a pronounced negative correlation, confirming its central role in viral elimination. Therapeutic efficacies (on infectivity) and (on production) have strong negative impact, shows the greatest effect, highlighting the crucial contribution of treatments that inhibit viral production. Infectivity has a moderate positive influence, while the natural death rate of healthy cells and the viral diffusion coefficient have weaker negative effects. These results show that efficient therapy should combine enhanced viral clearance with dual inhibition of production and infectivity to achieve a substantial reduction in viral burden.

The influence of stochastic delay intensity on infection dynamics was also explored (Fig. 4). The boxplots in Fig. 4a show that increasing amplifies both the median and variability of the peak infected-cell population. This suggests that stochastic fluctuations in intracellular delay which represent random variations in viral replication or infection timing can increase the intensity and unpredictability of infection outbreaks. Meanwhile, the mean viral load (Fig. 4b) remains relatively stable across values, although variability increases for stronger stochasticity. This indicates that the virus population remains globally regulated despite local perturbations, possibly due to compensatory effects between infection, clearance, and diffusion processes. Overall, the stochastic delay effect mainly destabilizes the infected-cell compartment while leaving the mean viral concentration more resilient.

A similar trend is observed with the transmission rate (Fig. 5). As increases, both the peak of infected cells (Fig. 5a) and the mean viral load (Fig. 5b) rise sharply, with broader variability ranges. Biologically, this indicates that higher infectivity promotes faster and more severe infection spread.

The model extends previous deterministic frameworks such as those proposed in [50, 51], which assumed a constant intracellular delay. This model provides a more realistic description of intra host HBV dynamics under treatment, accounting for the intrinsic variability observed in biological systems and clinical outcomes. The spatial component of the model further captures the diffusion and local propagation of viral particles, enabling the analysis of spatial heterogeneities that can emerge during infection. An additional observation emerging from the numerical simulations is the apparent increase in spatial heterogeneity under strong antiviral treatment (Fig. 13). It is important to clarify that this heterogeneity does not reflect an absolute increase in spatial variability, but rather an enhanced contrast relative to the significantly reduced mean viral load. As treatment efficacy increases (from , in Fig. 13a to , in Fig. 13c ), the average viral concentration across the domain drops by several orders of magnitude. However, residual stochastic fluctuations arising from the delayed infection process and initial spatial variability persist locally. As the global viral background level decreases, these residual localized variations become more pronounced relative to the mean concentration. Moreover, while viral diffusion contributes to spatial smoothing, its magnitude may not be sufficient to fully homogenize remaining infection clusters within the simulation period of days. This results in an enhanced spatial contrast characterized by regions of very low viral concentration interspersed with isolated hotspots of slightly higher concentration creating an illusion of increased heterogeneity. From a biological perspective, this phenomenon may reflect the persistence of localized viral reservoirs under antiviral therapy, a behavior consistent with clinical observations in chronic hepatitis B where residual viral replication can persist in niche microenvironments despite effective systemic suppression [52, 53]. The interaction between reduced viral production, stochastic delay fluctuations, and incomplete spatial smoothing thus provides a plausible mechanism for the emergence of such patterns.

From a biological standpoint, the results emphasize the complementary roles of treatment efficacy and stochasticity in shaping infection dynamics. High stochastic intensity may generate transient localized surges in infected cells, even under effective therapy, which could explain variable patient responses observed clinically [54]. However, the overall viral load remains largely governed by antiviral potency and clearance mechanisms. These findings suggest that strong suppression of both production and infectivity, combined with strategies enhancing clearance are crucial to stabilize infection and to prevent viral rebound.

In conclusion, the proposed stochastic RD model captures both the deterministic and random aspects of HBV infection dynamics. The sensitivity analysis identifies key therapeutic targets, while the stochastic simulations reveal how randomness in intracellular processes can modulate infection variability. This framework offers valuable perspectives for optimizing treatment strategies and for understanding the interplay between therapy, stochasticity and spatial heterogeneity in chronic viral infections such as hepatitis B. Future work may include fitting the model to patient data and extending it to account for immune responses or combination therapies.
