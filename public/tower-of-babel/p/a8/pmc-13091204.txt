NF-κB signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma: Mechanisms of tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance
Authors: Ping Yang, Xiaoli Li, Lin Yang, Na Xiao, Lili Ren, Mengmeng Ji, Hua Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•NF-κB signaling in HCC is pathologically stabilized rather than transiently activated, functioning as a central adaptive transcriptional program.•Sustained NF-κB activity integrates tumor progression, immune evasion, metabolic rewiring, and resistance to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and ferroptosis.•Targeting NF-κB-dependent adaptive modules offers a more precise therapeutic strategy than global NF-κB inhibition in hepatocellular carcinoma.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, NF-κB signaling, Tumor microenvironment, Metabolic reprogramming, Therapeutic resistance

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) arises almost invariably in the setting of chronic liver injury and persistent inflammation. Major etiological factors—including chronic hepatitis B or C virus infection, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and alcohol-associated liver disease—converge on a shared pathological trajectory characterized by long-standing immune activation, hepatocyte death–regeneration cycles, and progressive fibrotic remodeling[[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Within this inflammatory milieu, hepatocytes are continuously exposed to cytokines, oxidative stress, metabolic perturbations, and viral or damage-associated molecular patterns. These conditions create a permissive environment for malignant transformation that extends beyond the accumulation of genetic mutations, instead favoring transcriptional programs that enable cells to adapt, survive, and expand under chronic stress.

Accordingly, the conceptual framework of HCC pathogenesis has shifted from a mutation-centric model toward one that emphasizes inflammation-driven adaptive transcriptional reprogramming[[6], [7], [8]]. While recurrent genetic alterations undoubtedly contribute to tumor initiation and clonal selection, they alone cannot fully explain the profound intratumoral heterogeneity, phenotypic plasticity, and rapid acquisition of therapy resistance observed in HCC[[9], [10], [11]]. Increasing evidence suggests that non-genetic mechanisms—particularly those governed by stress-responsive transcription factors—play a dominant role in sustaining malignant phenotypes under fluctuating environmental and therapeutic pressures. Among these, nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) has emerged as a central integrator of inflammatory cues and cellular adaptation in the liver.

The NF-κB signaling pathway is classically activated through the canonical IKK–IκB axis, in which upstream inflammatory or stress signals trigger IκB kinase–mediated phosphorylation and degradation of IκB proteins, thereby releasing p65/p50 heterodimers for nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation[12,13]. Although a non-canonical NF-κB pathway exists and contributes to specific immunological contexts, current evidence suggests that its contribution to HCC is less well defined and appears less prominent than that of canonical NF-κB signaling, which has been more consistently linked to inflammatory transcription, tumor cell survival, and microenvironmental remodeling in this disease[14,15]. Importantly, NF-κB signaling should not be viewed as a simple on–off cascade. Rather, it represents a dynamic transcriptional program whose amplitude, duration, and target gene spectrum are finely tuned by upstream signaling networks, post-translational modifications, and chromatin context[16,17]. A defining feature of HCC is the recurrent and sustained activation of NF-κB signaling despite extensive genetic diversity and heterogeneous microenvironmental conditions. This raises a fundamental question: why does NF-κB remain persistently engaged in a tumor type subjected to intense immune surveillance and therapeutic stress? Emerging studies indicate that NF-κB activity in HCC is not merely a residual consequence of chronic inflammation but is actively stabilized by multiple, partially redundant mechanisms. These include dysregulated protein homeostasis, aberrant kinase signaling, metabolic rewiring, and loss of endogenous inhibitory circuits, all of which collectively lock NF-κB into a constitutively permissive state.

Increasing evidence suggests that non-genetic mechanisms—particularly those governed by stress-responsive transcription factors—play a major role in sustaining malignant phenotypes under fluctuating environmental and therapeutic pressures[18,19]. Among these, nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) has emerged as an important integrative node linking inflammatory cues to cellular adaptation in the liver. However, NF-κB should not be viewed as a universally dominant regulator of hepatocarcinogenesis. HCC is driven by extensive crosstalk among multiple oncogenic pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, p53, TGF-β, and PI3K/AKT signaling[[20], [21], [22], [23]]. These pathways contribute to distinct but overlapping aspects of tumor biology, such as lineage plasticity, genomic surveillance, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, metabolic fitness, and therapy resistance. Within this broader network, NF-κB appears to be particularly relevant to inflammation-associated transcriptional adaptation, microenvironmental remodeling, and survival under chronic stress. In this review, we synthesize current evidence supporting NF-κB as a major and context-dependent mediator of HCC progression, with particular emphasis on its roles in tumor aggressiveness, immune evasion, and resistance to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and regulated cell death. We also discuss how NF-κB interacts with other major oncogenic pathways and why selective targeting of NF-κB-dependent adaptive outputs may be more feasible than global pathway inhibition[24,25].

Persistent activation of NF-κB signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma

In contrast to acute inflammatory responses—where NF-κB signaling is rapidly induced and subsequently terminated—HCC exhibits a chronic, self-reinforcing NF-κB transcriptional state. This sustained activity enables tumor cells to continuously integrate inflammatory stress, metabolic demands, and therapeutic pressure into adaptive gene expression programs. Elucidating the mechanisms that maintain NF-κB in an “always-on” configuration is therefore critical for understanding its central role in HCC biology.

Chronic inflammatory and viral cues

The liver’s unique immunological environment predisposes it to inflammation-driven oncogenesis, and chronic inflammatory cues represent the earliest triggers of NF-κB activation in hepatocellular carcinoma[[26], [27], [28]]. As a frontline organ constantly exposed to gut-derived antigens, metabolic by-products, and microbial signals, the liver maintains a finely tuned balance between immune tolerance and immune activation. Persistent liver injury—arising from viral infection, metabolic stress, or toxic insults—disrupts this balance and establishes a chronic inflammatory milieu in which NF-κB signaling is repeatedly engaged. Sustained exposure to pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-1β, creates a baseline level of NF-κB activity in chronically damaged liver tissue[[29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. While these cytokines are not oncogenic per se, their prolonged presence reshapes transcriptional programs in hepatocytes, favoring cell survival, stress tolerance, and compensatory proliferation. Importantly, chronic cytokine signaling alters not only the intensity but also the temporal dynamics of NF-κB activation, shifting the pathway from transient, tightly regulated pulses toward a more sustained and permissive transcriptional state. This altered signaling behavior provides a selective advantage to hepatocytes capable of tolerating inflammatory stress, thereby setting the stage for malignant transformation.

In hepatitis B virus–associated HCC, viral factors further amplify and stabilize NF-κB signaling. The viral oncoprotein HBx has been shown to directly engage NF-κB pathways by promoting nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of p65-containing complexes. Unlike cytokine-driven activation, which fluctuates with inflammatory stimuli, HBx-mediated NF-κB activation is persistent and cell-intrinsic[[35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]]. By continuously reinforcing NF-κB signaling, HBx links viral replication, immune evasion, and hepatocyte survival into a unified transcriptional program that operates independently of external inflammatory cues[[40], [41], [42]]. Over time, this virus-driven NF-κB activation becomes progressively uncoupled from its original inflammatory and viral triggers. As hepatocytes undergo malignant transformation, tumor-intrinsic alterations—including changes in proteostasis, kinase signaling, and regulatory feedback loops—take over the maintenance of NF-κB activity. Thus, chronic inflammation and viral infection function primarily as initiating forces that prime NF-κB signaling, while subsequent tumor evolution consolidates this pathway into a stable, self-sustaining oncogenic program.

Proteostasis- and kinase-driven stabilization of NF-κB signaling

Beyond inflammatory initiation, accumulating evidence suggests that persistent NF-κB signaling in established HCC may be reinforced through a convergent network involving proteostasis disruption, kinase-mediated signal amplification, and ubiquitin-/trafficking-associated regulatory mechanisms[43,44]. Rather than representing isolated upstream events, these processes appear to act at complementary levels to lower the threshold for NF-κB activation, prolong its transcriptional output, and couple pathway activity to broader programs of stress adaptation, proliferation, and immune remodeling. One major layer of this model involves proteostasis-associated reinforcement of NF-κB signaling. A representative example is the UFL1/LRP1 regulatory axis, which links endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis and UFMylation to NF-κB activity. Loss of LRP1 has been reported to disrupt cellular proteostasis and promote UFL1-dependent modifications associated with enhanced NF-κB signaling[45]. Similarly, GRP78-mediated induction of the ubiquitin-like modifier FAT10 provides another route by which unfolded protein stress may reinforce NF-κB activity[45]. Taken together, these findings suggest that disturbances in protein quality-control systems do not merely reflect passive cellular stress, but may actively support a more stably engaged NF-κB state in HCC. A second layer is kinase-mediated amplification, which may strengthen and sustain NF-κB transcriptional output after pathway engagement. Casein kinase 2 (CSNK2A2) and the serine/threonine kinase PIM2 have both been implicated in supporting NF-κB signaling in HCC[46,47]. Rather than functioning solely as transient upstream triggers, these kinases may act as signal amplifiers that enhance downstream transcriptional activity and attenuate inhibitory feedback. In this way, kinase networks may help convert stress-responsive NF-κB signaling into a more durable pro-survival and pro-growth program.

A third layer involves trafficking- and ubiquitin-associated coordination of NF-κB signaling with malignant adaptation. GOLPH3, a Golgi-associated protein, promotes NF-κB activation by linking vesicular trafficking and oncogenic signaling, thereby integrating secretory stress with inflammatory transcription[48]. Similarly, GRP78-mediated induction of the ubiquitin-like modifier FAT10 provides a proteostatic route to NF-κB reinforcement, coupling unfolded protein responses to sustained pro-survival signaling[49]. Together, these mechanisms underscore the intimate connection between intracellular stress adaptation and NF-κB persistence in HCC. E3 ligase–associated regulation further expands this network. DCAF13 has been shown to activate NF-κB signaling while simultaneously promoting tumor proliferation and immune escape[50]. This dual functionality illustrates how ubiquitin-related machinery can synchronize NF-κB–dependent oncogenic transcription with microenvironmental modulation, reinforcing malignant fitness on multiple fronts.

Collectively, these observations support a more integrated framework in which NF-κB stabilization in HCC is not driven by any single regulator, but instead emerges from the convergence of proteostasis imbalance, kinase reinforcement, and ubiquitin-/trafficking-associated modulation. Although these mechanisms arise from distinct cellular processes, they appear to converge on a shared functional outcome: maintaining NF-κB in a more permissive and prolonged signaling state that supports oncogenic transcriptional programs. However, it should be noted that current evidence for this model remains derived predominantly from preclinical studies, and its relative importance across molecularly diverse HCC subsets requires further clarification.

Loss of endogenous brakes on NF-κB signaling

In parallel with the reinforcement of activating mechanisms, hepatocellular carcinoma is marked by a progressive erosion of endogenous inhibitory circuits that normally restrain NF-κB signaling. Under physiological conditions, NF-κB activation is intrinsically self-limiting: nuclear repressors, ubiquitin-dependent degradation machinery, and transcriptional feedback loops ensure timely termination of NF-κB–dependent gene expression once inflammatory or stress signals subside[51,52]. This tight temporal control is essential to prevent chronic inflammation and aberrant cell survival. In HCC, however, this regulatory balance is profoundly disrupted. PDLIM2 represents a prototypical nuclear brake on NF-κB signaling. By facilitating ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of activated NF-κB subunits within the nucleus, PDLIM2 limits both the amplitude and duration of NF-κB transcriptional responses[53,54]. Loss or functional attenuation of PDLIM2 abolishes this nuclear checkpoint, allowing p65-containing complexes to persist on chromatin and sustain oncogenic transcription[55]. Rather than merely increasing NF-κB activation frequency, PDLIM2 deficiency fundamentally alters NF-κB signaling kinetics, shifting the pathway from a pulsatile, self-resolving mode to a prolonged, quasi-constitutive transcriptional state. Additional tumor suppressors reinforce this braking system through complementary mechanisms. ING4 suppresses NF-κB–driven transcription by coordinating microRNA networks and downstream tumor-suppressive programs, thereby coupling transcriptional repression with broader regulatory circuitry[56]. EYA4 similarly constrains NF-κB–dependent gene activation involved in invasion and growth, acting at the level of transcriptional output rather than upstream signal initiation[[57], [58], [59]]. Importantly, these suppressors do not function redundantly; instead, they operate at distinct regulatory layers—nuclear stability, transcriptional complex activity, and target gene selection—collectively forming a multilayered negative feedback architecture. The functional loss of these endogenous brakes has consequences that extend beyond simple pathway hyperactivation. By dismantling NF-κB termination mechanisms, HCC cells become permissive to low-level or stochastic upstream signals that would otherwise be insufficient to sustain transcription. In this context, NF-κB signaling no longer requires strong inflammatory or genotoxic input to remain active; instead, it becomes autonomously maintained, effectively uncoupled from its original physiological triggers. Crucially, the erosion of endogenous inhibitory circuits also renders NF-κB signaling highly susceptible to secondary modes of regulation[[60], [61], [62], [63]]. In the absence of robust protein-based brakes, NF-κB activity becomes increasingly shaped by non-coding RNAs, epigenetic modifiers, and RNA–protein interaction networks. This regulatory shift sets the stage for the multilayered post-transcriptional and epitranscriptional control mechanisms discussed in later sections, and helps explain why NF-κB signaling in HCC is not only persistent but also remarkably plastic and context responsive.

NF-κB-Driven tumor progression in hepatocellular carcinoma

NF-κB coordinates multiple downstream functional modules, including proliferation and survival, metabolic reprogramming, and invasive plasticity. These programs operate in parallel and often reinforce one another, enabling HCC cells to thrive in a hostile and fluctuating microenvironment.

Proliferation and survival signaling

One of the most fundamental oncogenic functions of NF-κB in hepatocellular carcinoma is the promotion of tumor cell proliferation and survival. NF-κB directly regulates the transcription of anti-apoptotic genes and cell cycle–associated factors, thereby shielding malignant hepatocytes from both intrinsic stress signals and extrinsic cytotoxic insults[[64], [65], [66], [67], [68]]. Through sustained transcriptional activity, NF-κB suppresses apoptotic checkpoints while preserving proliferative competence, even under conditions of metabolic constraint, oxidative stress, or therapeutic pressure. Importantly, NF-κB–mediated survival signaling in HCC extends beyond acute protection from cell death. Persistent NF-κB activation establishes a transcriptional state that favors long-term cellular fitness, enabling tumor cells to withstand chronic stress without triggering irreversible growth arrest or apoptosis. This sustained survival advantage allows hepatocytes harboring genomic instability to persist and expand, thereby facilitating clonal evolution and tumor progression. In this context, NF-κB functions less as an emergency response factor and more as a constitutive regulator of tumor maintenance.

Multiple tumor-intrinsic regulators reinforce this proliferative NF-κB program. Kinase-driven pathways, including those mediated by PIM2 and CSNK2A2, amplify NF-κB transcriptional output and stabilize downstream survival signaling[69,70]. These kinases act as signal integrators, coupling growth factor inputs, metabolic stress, and inflammatory cues to sustained NF-κB activity. By buffering NF-κB signaling against negative feedback, kinase networks create a permissive intracellular environment for continuous cell cycle progression. E3 ligase–associated regulation further links NF-κB signaling to proliferative control[17,71]. DCAF13-dependent activation of NF-κB has been reported to promote not only tumor cell growth but also immune evasion, suggesting a functional link between survival signaling and microenvironmental adaptation[72,73]. These findings support the possibility that NF-κB-driven proliferative programs may be coordinated with mechanisms that reduce immune-mediated elimination, which in turn may contribute to tumor persistence. Crucially, NF-κB–mediated survival signaling enables HCC cells to tolerate conditions that would normally impose strong selective pressure against proliferation. By sustaining expression of anti-apoptotic and stress-adaptive genes, NF-κB allows tumor cells to survive in the face of genomic instability, redox imbalance, and fluctuating nutrient availability—hallmarks of advanced liver tumors(Fig. 1). This capacity for stress tolerance not only supports ongoing proliferation but also primes tumor cells for resistance to therapeutic interventions, linking proliferative signaling to later stages of disease progression.

Metabolic reprogramming and redox adaptation

Beyond its classical roles in inflammation and survival, NF-κB has emerged as a central regulator of metabolic reprogramming in HCC[[74], [75], [76], [77]]. Tumor cells exploit NF-κB signaling to reshape energy metabolism and redox balance, thereby aligning metabolic flux with proliferative and survival demands. In hepatitis B virus–associated HCC, NF-κB signaling has been shown to cooperate with viral factors to enhance aerobic glycolysis. Activation of the HBx–NF-κB–HK2 axis drives increased glucose uptake and glycolytic flux, supporting rapid biomass accumulation and ATP production[78]. This metabolic shift not only fuels proliferation but also generates intermediates required for nucleotide and lipid synthesis, reinforcing malignant growth. NF-κB also plays a pivotal role in maintaining redox homeostasis and suppressing oxidative cell death[55,79]. By transcriptionally activating SLC7A11, a key component of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system, NF-κB promotes glutathione synthesis and antioxidant defense[[80], [81], [82]]. This mechanism protects HCC cells from lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent oxidative damage. In this context, NF-κB signaling increases the ferroptosis threshold by enhancing antioxidant defense, thereby enabling tumor cells to better withstand oxidative stress and therapies that rely on redox imbalance. These findings highlight a critical concept: metabolic regulation is not a peripheral consequence of NF-κB activation but a core component of its oncogenic function. By synchronizing glycolytic reprogramming with antioxidant defense, NF-κB ensures that HCC cells possess both the energetic resources and redox resilience required for sustained survival(Fig. 2).

Epithelial–mesenchymal transition, invasion, and metastasis

In addition to supporting growth and survival, NF-κB critically contributes to the invasive and metastatic potential of hepatocellular carcinoma. Through direct transcriptional control of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) programs, NF-κB enables tumor cells to acquire migratory capacity, resist anoikis, and dynamically remodel interactions with the extracellular matrix[[83], [84], [85], [86]]. This EMT-associated transcriptional output represents a key mechanism by which HCC cells escape spatial constraints imposed by the primary tumor and initiate dissemination.

NF-κB–dependent EMT has been well illustrated in several oncogenic signaling contexts. In RANKL-mediated activation, NF-κB drives coordinated transcriptional changes that promote loss of epithelial features and acquisition of mesenchymal traits, thereby enhancing migratory and invasive behavior[87]. Similarly, HAX-1 has been shown to facilitate HCC cell migration and invasion through NF-κB–dependent induction of EMT-associated gene expression[88]. These signaling axes converge on cytoskeletal reorganization, altered cell–cell adhesion, and enhanced matrix interaction, collectively enabling tumor cells to traverse tissue barriers and invade surrounding stroma. Importantly, NF-κB does not function as a binary EMT switch but rather as a plasticity-associated transcriptional regulator. Sustained NF-κB activity allows tumor cells to dynamically tune EMT-related programs in response to microenvironmental cues such as hypoxia, inflammatory signals, and metabolic stress[[89], [90], [91], [92], [93]]. This dynamic regulation favors partial or hybrid EMT states in which epithelial and mesenchymal features coexist. Such intermediate phenotypes confer maximal adaptive advantage by preserving proliferative capacity while enabling motility and stress resistance, features increasingly recognized as critical determinants of metastatic efficiency.

NF-κB–driven EMT is also tightly coupled to tumor cell survival and metabolic adaptation. EMT-associated transcriptional programs often intersect with NF-κB–regulated survival pathways, enabling migrating tumor cells to withstand detachment-induced apoptosis and oxidative stress encountered during dissemination[[94], [95], [96], [97]]. This coupling ensures that invasive behavior is not achieved at the expense of cell viability, but rather integrated into a broader adaptive framework that supports metastatic competence. At later stages of disease progression, NF-κB–mediated plasticity facilitates metastatic colonization and outgrowth. By maintaining transcriptional flexibility, NF-κB enables disseminated tumor cells to reversibly modulate EMT programs, allowing mesenchymal-like cells to reacquire epithelial characteristics upon arrival at secondary sites. This capacity for phenotypic reversibility further underscores the role of NF-κB as a regulator of metastatic adaptability rather than a driver of a single fixed cell state. Together, these observations illustrate how NF-κB integrates proliferative, metabolic, and invasive signaling into a unified oncogenic framework. By coordinating EMT-associated plasticity with survival and stress-adaptive programs, NF-κB endows HCC cells with the capacity not only to grow but also to dynamically adapt, invade, and ultimately colonize distant organs(Fig. 3).

Crosstalk between NF-κB and other major oncogenic pathways in HCC

HCC progression is shaped by the coordinated activity of multiple major pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, p53, TGF-β, and PI3K/AKT signaling, each of which contributes to distinct but partially overlapping dimensions of tumor biology[20,23,98,99]. Wnt/β-catenin signaling is strongly linked to hepatocyte lineage identity, tumor initiation, stemness maintenance, and immune exclusion. By contrast, p53 primarily governs genomic integrity, cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and senescence, thereby functioning as a major barrier to malignant progression[100]. TGF-β signaling exhibits marked context dependence, contributing to fibrogenesis and tumor suppression in early stages, but promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition, invasion, and immune suppression during tumor progression[23,101]. PI3K/AKT signaling, in turn, supports anabolic metabolism, growth, survival, and treatment tolerance[102]. Within this broader framework, NF-κB appears to be particularly relevant to inflammation-associated transcriptional adaptation, tumor–microenvironment remodeling, and stress tolerance under chronic injury and therapeutic pressure. This comparative perspective is important because the oncogenic significance of NF-κB in HCC lies less in universal dominance than in its ability to connect chronic inflammatory signaling with adaptive malignant programs[103,104]. In the setting of persistent liver injury, NF-κB integrates cytokine-driven inputs, oxidative stress, metabolic perturbations, and therapy-induced damage into transcriptional outputs that promote survival, redox homeostasis, immune evasion, and phenotypic plasticity. These functions distinguish NF-κB from pathways that are more directly associated with lineage determination, genomic surveillance, or canonical growth signaling. Thus, NF-κB may be best viewed as a context-dependent adaptive signaling node whose impact is amplified in inflammation-rich, stress-laden tumor ecosystems.

Among these interactions, the interplay between NF-κB and p53 is of particular relevance in HCC. In principle, p53-mediated programs oppose malignant progression by promoting cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, senescence, and DNA damage surveillance, whereas sustained NF-κB activation tends to favor survival, inflammatory adaptation, and stress tolerance[105,106]. Accordingly, p53 loss or dysfunction may shift the cellular balance toward NF-κB-dominant transcriptional outputs, thereby facilitating tumor cell persistence under genotoxic, metabolic, and inflammatory stress. Conversely, chronic NF-κB activation may attenuate the effectiveness of p53-dependent tumor-suppressive responses by reinforcing anti-apoptotic and pro-survival signaling states. This antagonistic and context-dependent relationship has important implications for tumorigenesis and treatment response, as tumors with impaired p53 function may be more permissive to NF-κB-driven survival programs, chemoresistance, and immune escape.

NF-κB also functionally intersects with other major signaling pathways in ways that may further reinforce malignant progression. Crosstalk with Wnt/β-catenin may contribute to immune exclusion and stem-like phenotypes, while convergence with TGF-β may facilitate epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity, fibrotic remodeling, and suppressive microenvironmental signaling[107,108]. Similarly, parallel activation of PI3K/AKT and NF-κB may enhance metabolic fitness and resistance to therapeutic stress by jointly sustaining pro-survival and anabolic programs[78,109]. Although the relative contribution of each pathway likely varies across tumor contexts, these observations underscore that NF-κB is most meaningfully understood as part of an integrated signaling architecture rather than as an isolated oncogenic driver. From a translational perspective, this also suggests that therapeutic strategies aimed at NF-κB may be most effective when aligned with pathway context, particularly in tumors characterized by inflammatory activation, p53 dysfunction, and adaptive resistance phenotypes.

NF-κB signaling in immune evasion and tumor microenvironment remodeling

The tumor microenvironment of hepatocellular carcinoma is profoundly immunosuppressive, despite the liver’s intrinsic immune surveillance capacity. Increasing evidence indicates that sustained NF-κB signaling plays a central role in shaping this paradoxical immune landscape. Beyond its tumor-intrinsic functions, NF-κB acts as a key coordinator of immune evasion and microenvironmental remodeling, enabling HCC cells to escape immune destruction while actively reprogramming surrounding immune populations.

Tumor cell-intrinsic immune escape programs

Within tumor cells, NF-κB directly drives transcriptional programs that attenuate anti-tumor immune responses and promote immune evasion[24,[110], [111], [112], [113]]. In HCC, NF-κB activation has been linked to the induction of immunoregulatory cytokines and mediators, including IL-6, IL-1β, CSF1, and PD-L1, which can interfere with effective cytotoxic lymphocyte function and reshape inflammatory signaling toward immune tolerance and dysfunction[33,93,114,115]. Rather than eliciting productive anti-tumor immunity, NF-κB-dependent signaling may therefore bias tumor-associated inflammatory outputs toward cytotoxic T-cell impairment, exhaustion-like phenotypes, and recruitment or support of suppressive myeloid programs.

A defining feature of NF-κB–driven immune escape in HCC is its cell-autonomous nature. Sustained NF-κB activation within tumor cells establishes an intrinsic immunosuppressive transcriptional state that operates independently of external immune selection pressure. This state not only dampens immune recognition but also alters the qualitative nature of tumor-derived inflammatory signals, converting them from immune-activating cues into signals that favor immune suppression and tolerance[[116], [117], [118], [119], [120], [121]]. In this context, inflammatory cues are not simply amplified, but are reoriented toward outputs that favor immune suppression rather than effective tumor clearance. As a result, tumor-associated inflammation may become permissive, rather than antagonistic, to malignant growth. Several tumor-intrinsic regulators directly connect NF-κB activation with immune escape phenotypes. PIGU promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression through activation of NF-κB signaling while also enhancing immune evasion, illustrating that oncogenic growth and immune suppression can be mechanistically linked[122]. Similarly, DCAF13 activates NF-κB signaling in HCC cells and contributes to both proliferative fitness and immune escape. These observations suggest that NF-κB may coordinate tumor cell survival programs with adaptive changes in the surrounding immune milieu, rather than regulating proliferation and immune modulation as entirely separate processes (Fig. 4).

Importantly, NF-κB-associated immune escape should not be viewed solely as a late consequence of tumor expansion. In at least some settings, tumor cells may acquire NF-κB-dependent immunosuppressive features relatively early during disease evolution, thereby limiting effective immune surveillance and facilitating clonal persistence. In addition, tumor-derived NF-κB-regulated factors can shape downstream immune cell behavior, providing a mechanistic link between tumor-intrinsic signaling and broader microenvironmental remodeling. In this sense, NF-κB signaling in tumor cells may serve as an upstream driver of both intrinsic immune escape and extrinsic immunosuppressive tissue organization, with potential implications for resistance to immune-based therapies.

NF-κB-dependent crosstalk with tumor-associated macrophages

Among immune populations, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) constitute a major and highly plastic compartment that critically shapes hepatocellular carcinoma progression. Increasing evidence indicates that NF-κB-dependent crosstalk between tumor cells and macrophages promotes the accumulation of immunosuppressive and tumor-supportive TAM states. Importantly, macrophage phenotypes in HCC should not be viewed through a strictly binary M1/M2 framework, but rather as dynamic and spectrum-like populations shaped by inflammatory signaling, metabolic stress, hypoxia, and tumor-derived factors. NF-κB signaling contributes to TAM reprogramming through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Disruption of autophagic processes in HCC has been reported to alter NF-κB signaling dynamics and generate inflammatory outputs that favor macrophage polarization toward M2-like or otherwise immunoregulatory phenotypes[123]. Rather than simply suppressing inflammation, this altered NF-κB activity appears to reshape the qualitative nature of tumor-derived signals, biasing macrophage differentiation toward states associated with tissue remodeling, angiogenesis, and immune suppression. These immunosuppressive TAM programs support tumor progression through multiple complementary mechanisms. Such macrophage states can suppress cytotoxic T-cell activation and effector function, secrete pro-angiogenic mediators that enhance vascular remodeling, and facilitate extracellular matrix degradation and reorganization, thereby promoting invasion and metastatic dissemination[[124], [125], [126], [127], [128], [129]]. Importantly, TAM-mediated immune suppression is tightly coupled to their trophic and remodeling functions, collectively creating a microenvironment that is permissive for malignant expansion rather than hostile to tumor cells[[130], [131], [132]]. Crucially, this macrophage reprogramming does not occur in isolation but reflects the extension of tumor-intrinsic NF-κB-dependent signaling into the surrounding microenvironment. NF-κB-regulated cytokines, chemokines, and stress-associated mediators released by tumor cells can collectively influence macrophage recruitment, phenotypic polarization, and functional specialization. Through this process, NF-κB signaling in tumor cells may shape immune cell behavior beyond the malignant compartment itself, thereby broadening its impact on tumor progression. In this context, the tumor immune microenvironment may be understood, at least in part, as a downstream consequence of NF-κB-associated inflammatory and immunoregulatory signaling rather than as a passive bystander of tumor growth. Thus, NF-κB-dependent macrophage crosstalk provides a mechanistic basis for immune suppression in HCC and may contribute to reduced responsiveness to immunotherapeutic interventions (Fig. 5).

Implications for immunotherapy responsiveness

Although immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown clinical benefit in a subset of patients, overall response rates remain limited, highlighting the presence of intrinsic and adaptive resistance mechanisms. One emerging hypothesis is that HCCs characterized by high basal NF-κB activity may be intrinsically less responsive to immunotherapy. In such tumors, NF-κB–driven immune escape programs and macrophage-mediated immunosuppression create a hostile environment for effective T cell–mediated anti-tumor immunity[[133], [134], [135], [136]]. This immune-resistant state may persist even in the presence of checkpoint blockade, limiting therapeutic efficacy. These observations suggest that NF-κB signaling status could serve as a functional determinant of immunotherapy responsiveness and a potential stratification biomarker. More importantly, they provide a rationale for combination strategies in which modulation of NF-κB–dependent pathways is integrated with immunotherapeutic approaches. Targeting NF-κB–regulated immune escape modules—rather than globally inhibiting the pathway—may help recondition the tumor microenvironment and enhance sensitivity to immune-based therapies.

NF-κB–Mediated therapeutic resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma

Therapeutic resistance remains a major obstacle in the clinical management of hepatocellular carcinoma, limiting the durability of responses to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and emerging targeted approaches. Accumulating evidence indicates that sustained NF-κB signaling constitutes a central adaptive mechanism by which HCC cells survive therapeutic stress. Rather than conferring resistance to a single modality, NF-κB orchestrates a broad stress-response program that enables tumor cells to tolerate DNA damage, metabolic disruption, and oxidative stress. This shared survival logic positions NF-κB at the core of therapy resistance in HCC.

Resistance to radiotherapy

Radiotherapy exerts its antitumor effects primarily through the induction of DNA damage and subsequent activation of cell death pathways. However, hepatocellular carcinoma frequently displays intrinsic or acquired radioresistance, which substantially limits therapeutic efficacy. Accumulating evidence indicates that NF-κB functions as a central mediator of this resistance by acting as a DNA damage–responsive survival program rather than a passive inflammatory effector. Activation of Aurora-A kinase has been shown to promote radioresistance in HCC through NF-κB signaling[137]. Upon irradiation-induced DNA damage, Aurora-A–dependent signaling engages NF-κB, leading to enhanced nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of p65-containing complexes[[138], [139], [140]]. This activation drives the expression of pro-survival, anti-apoptotic, and stress-adaptive genes, thereby attenuating radiation-induced apoptosis and facilitating cellular recovery from genotoxic insult[[141], [142], [143], [144], [145]]. In this setting, NF-κB is directly coupled to DNA damage signaling cascades, linking genomic stress to transcriptional protection. Importantly, NF-κB activation in response to radiotherapy is not transient or self-limiting. Instead, irradiation establishes a prolonged NF-κB–dependent survival state that persists beyond the acute damage response. This sustained transcriptional program enables a subset of tumor cells to survive initial treatment, maintain viability under continued stress, and ultimately repopulate the tumor mass. Thus, NF-κB contributes not only to immediate radioresistance but also to long-term tumor recurrence.

From an evolutionary perspective, radiotherapy imposes a strong selective pressure on heterogeneous tumor cell populations. NF-κB–driven survival signaling preferentially supports the persistence and expansion of cells capable of mounting robust stress-adaptive responses. Over successive treatment cycles, this process may enrich for NF-κB-high, radioresistant clones, thereby reinforcing therapeutic failure. In this context, NF-κB serves as a molecular conduit through which radiotherapy-induced stress is converted into selective advantage. These observations underscore an important clinical implication: while radiotherapy aims to eliminate tumor cells through DNA damage, it may paradoxically activate NF-κB–dependent adaptive programs that promote survival and resistance[[145], [146], [147]]. This dual effect highlights the necessity of combinatorial strategies that disrupt NF-κB–mediated stress responses alongside radiotherapy. Targeting upstream regulators such as Aurora-A or selectively interfering with NF-κB-dependent survival modules may help prevent the establishment of radioresistant tumor states and improve long-term treatment outcomes(Fig. 6).

NF-κB–mediated resistance to radiotherapy in hepatocellular carcinoma. Radiotherapy induces DNA damage that activates Aurora-A–dependent NF-κB signaling in HCC cells. NF-κB translocates to the nucleus and drives transcription of anti-apoptotic and stress-adaptive genes, attenuating radiation-induced cell death. This sustained survival program promotes persistence and repopulation of radioresistant tumor cells, linking therapeutic stress to NF-κB–dependent adaptation and tumor recurrence.

Chemoresistance and stress adaptation

Similar principles govern NF-κB–mediated resistance to chemotherapy in hepatocellular carcinoma. Cytotoxic agents impose multiple, often simultaneous forms of cellular stress, including DNA damage, oxidative imbalance, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and metabolic perturbation. Rather than responding to each insult independently, NF-κB functions as an integrative stress-response hub that consolidates these signals into a unified transcriptional program promoting survival, repair, and adaptation.

A central signaling axis underlying this process involves transforming growth factor-β–activated kinase 1 (TAK1) and its adaptor proteins TAB3, which act upstream of NF-κB activation[[148], [149], [150], [151]]. Engagement of the TAK1/TAB3–NF-κB pathway enhances the expression of anti-apoptotic, detoxifying, and stress-response genes, thereby attenuating chemotherapy-induced cell death. Importantly, TAK1/TAB3 does not merely relay cytotoxic signals but serves as a convergence point for diverse stress inputs, including inflammatory cues and genotoxic damage[152]. This positioning allows NF-κB to translate heterogeneous chemotherapy-induced stresses into a coherent adaptive transcriptional output. Beyond immediate cytoprotection, NF-κB–driven stress adaptation promotes the stabilization of chemoresistant phenotypes. Sustained NF-κB activity supports the survival of tumor cell subpopulations capable of tolerating repeated cycles of chemotherapeutic stress. Over time, this selective pressure favors the expansion of NF-κB–high clones with enhanced stress tolerance, thereby converting transient adaptive responses into durable resistance states. In this manner, NF-κB signaling contributes not only to acute drug tolerance but also to the long-term failure of chemotherapy.

MicroRNA-mediated regulation provides critical insight into the reversibility and modularity of NF-κB–driven chemoresistance. miR-26b has been shown to suppress NF-κB signaling by directly targeting TAK1 and TAB3, effectively dismantling the upstream stress-sensing machinery required for NF-κB activation[153]. Restoration of miR-26b expression enhances chemosensitivity in HCC cells, demonstrating that NF-κB–dependent resistance is not fixed but remains amenable to regulatory intervention. This finding underscores the importance of upstream signaling nodes as therapeutic leverage points. Collectively, these observations highlight two key concepts. First, NF-κB–mediated chemoresistance reflects an adaptive stress-integration program rather than resistance to a specific cytotoxic mechanism. Second, selectively targeting NF-κB–activating modules—such as TAK1/TAB3—may offer a more precise and clinically feasible strategy than global inhibition of NF-κB itself. By disrupting the stress-adaptive circuitry that sustains tumor cell survival under chemotherapy, such approaches hold promise for overcoming resistance while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Ferroptosis resistance and therapeutic vulnerability

Beyond classical apoptosis-based resistance, NF-κB also governs resistance to ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation. Ferroptosis has attracted considerable interest as a therapeutic vulnerability in HCC due to the tumor’s reliance on metabolic and redox homeostasis. However, NF-κB signaling raises the ferroptosis threshold, thereby enabling tumor cells to escape this lethal pathway[154,155]. Mechanistically, NF-κB transcriptionally activates SLC7A11, a key component of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system that supports glutathione synthesis and antioxidant defense. By enhancing cystine uptake and redox buffering capacity, NF-κB protects HCC cells from lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death. This NF-κB–SLC7A11 axis exemplifies how inflammatory transcription factors can be repurposed to maintain metabolic resilience under therapeutic stress. In this context, aspirin provides a compelling conceptual example. Its ability to induce ferroptosis in HCC cells is not simply attributable to a nonspecific anti-inflammatory effect or drug repurposing phenomenon. Rather, aspirin disrupts NF-κB p65–dependent transcription of SLC7A11, directly dismantling the antioxidant defense system that shields tumor cells from ferroptosis[156]. This finding supports the idea that pharmacological interference with NF-κB-associated antioxidant programs may expose a therapeutically relevant metabolic vulnerability. However, the ferroptosis-inducing effects of aspirin should not be attributed solely to NF-κB–SLC7A11 modulation, as aspirin has multiple molecular targets and may influence ferroptosis through additional inflammatory, metabolic, or stress-response pathways. Accordingly, the aspirin example is best interpreted as evidence supporting the relevance of the NF-κB–SLC7A11 axis, rather than as proof that this pathway fully accounts for aspirin-mediated ferroptosis in HCC.

Together, these observations underscore a unifying principle: NF-κB mediates therapeutic resistance in HCC by coordinating a spectrum of stress-adaptive programs rather than by blocking individual cytotoxic pathways. Consequently, effective therapeutic strategies may require selective disruption of NF-κB–dependent survival modules—such as DNA damage tolerance, stress signaling, or antioxidant defense—rather than wholesale inhibition of NF-κB itself.

Non-coding RNAs and tumor suppressors as negative regulators of NF-κB

While sustained NF-κB activation underlies many oncogenic features of hepatocellular carcinoma, accumulating evidence indicates that this pathway is not intrinsically constitutive but is instead shaped by a multilayered network of negative regulators. Non-coding RNAs and tumor suppressor proteins constitute critical components of this regulatory architecture, functioning as endogenous brakes that restrain NF-κB signaling at transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational levels. Disruption of these inhibitory circuits contributes to the pathological fixation of NF-κB activity observed in HCC. circRNAs and lncRNAs restraining NF-κB activity

Circular RNAs and long non-coding RNAs have emerged as important modulators of NF-κB signaling in HCC, often acting through network-based regulatory mechanisms rather than linear signaling cascades. A representative example is circPTPN12, whose biogenesis is regulated by ESRP1[157]. circPTPN12 suppresses HCC progression by facilitating PDLIM2-mediated inhibition of NF-κB signaling. PDLIM2 serves as a nuclear negative regulator that promotes termination of NF-κB transcriptional activity, and its functional coupling with circPTPN12 highlights how circRNAs can reinforce endogenous inhibitory checkpoints within the NF-κB pathway. Long non-coding RNAs further expand the regulatory complexity of NF-κB signaling. HULC, one of the most extensively studied lncRNAs in liver cancer, has been shown to modulate NF-κB pathway activity and is closely associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Rather than acting through a single effector, HULC participates in a broader regulatory network that influences inflammatory transcriptional programs, underscoring the capacity of lncRNAs to fine-tune NF-κB signaling in a context-dependent manner[158]. Similarly, lncRNA CASC2 suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma oncogenesis through a miRNA-dependent axis converging on NF-κB signaling[159]. By functioning as a competing endogenous RNA, CASC2 indirectly attenuates NF-κB activity and downstream oncogenic outputs. Together, these findings illustrate that circRNAs and lncRNAs operate as higher-order regulators that integrate transcriptional control, RNA–RNA interactions, and protein-mediated inhibition to restrain NF-κB signaling.

MicroRNAs targeting NF-κB signaling nodes

MicroRNAs represent another critical layer of negative regulation within the NF-κB signaling network. Notably, most NF-κB–suppressive microRNAs do not directly target the p65 subunit itself but instead modulate key upstream signaling nodes, thereby achieving effective pathway attenuation with greater specificity. miR-26b exemplifies this regulatory logic by targeting TAK1 and TAB3, essential components of NF-κB activation cascades. Through suppression of these upstream kinases and adaptors, miR-26b dampens NF-κB signaling and enhances chemosensitivity in HCC cells. This mechanism highlights how microRNAs can reprogram stress-response pathways and reverse malignant phenotypes by selectively disrupting signaling bottlenecks. Additional microRNAs, including miR-595 and miR-29–3p, have also been shown to inhibit HCC progression through NF-κB–dependent mechanisms[160,161]. These microRNAs attenuate NF-κB signaling indirectly, affecting proliferation, migration, and survival pathways. Collectively, these observations support a unifying principle: microRNA-mediated control of NF-κB signaling in HCC is modular and node-centric, targeting critical regulatory junctions rather than the terminal transcription factor itself.

Tumor suppressor–mediated transcriptional repression

Beyond non-coding RNAs, classical tumor suppressor proteins provide an additional tier of NF-κB regulation by directly repressing its transcriptional activity or destabilizing its downstream programs. ING4 functions as a potent inhibitor of NF-κB–dependent oncogenic signaling through a regulatory axis involving miR-155 and FOXO3a. By constraining NF-κB–driven transcription, ING4 suppresses tumor growth and promotes apoptosis, illustrating how tumor suppressors can antagonize inflammatory signaling at multiple levels. EYA4 represents another example of tumor suppressor–mediated NF-κB repression. By inhibiting NF-κB–dependent RAP1 transactivation, EYA4 limits HCC cell growth and invasion[162]. This mechanism emphasizes that tumor suppressors can exert context-specific control over NF-κB target gene selection, thereby selectively restraining malignant phenotypes without globally abolishing NF-κB signaling. The functional loss or downregulation of these tumor suppressors dismantles critical inhibitory checkpoints, permitting unchecked NF-κB activity and reinforcing oncogenic transcriptional programs. Importantly, the convergence of non-coding RNAs and tumor suppressors on NF-κB signaling underscores the existence of an intrinsic regulatory network designed to balance inflammatory responses and cellular homeostasis.

Clinical implications and future perspectives

The clinical relevance of NF-κB signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma lies not only in its association with tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance, but also in its potential value for patient stratification, biomarker development, and rational combination therapy design. However, translating this mechanistic knowledge into effective clinical strategies requires moving beyond pathway-centric concepts toward clinically measurable, context-aware, and safety-conscious intervention frameworks. In this section, we discuss the potential of NF-κB as a biomarker and stratification tool, evaluate emerging therapeutic strategies targeting NF-κB-dependent networks, and highlight key translational issues, including how NF-κB activity might be assessed clinically, whether NF-κB-related therapeutic strategies have entered clinical evaluation in HCC, and what feasibility and safety challenges remain to be addressed.

NF-κB as a biomarker and stratification tool

One of the most immediate clinical implications of sustained NF-κB activation in HCC lies in its potential utility as a biomarker for tumor stratification. Rather than serving solely as a molecular hallmark of inflammation, NF-κB activity reflects the functional state of multiple adaptive programs within tumor cells and the surrounding microenvironment[163]. HCCs characterized by high basal NF-κB activity tend to exhibit a constellation of features, including enhanced metabolic dependency, robust antioxidant capacity, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Such tumors are often reliant on glycolytic reprogramming and redox buffering mechanisms, as exemplified by increased expression of HK2 and SLC7A11, and are enriched for immune escape phenotypes shaped by altered macrophage polarization and attenuated cytotoxic T cell activity. In this context, NF-κB signaling may serve as a composite indicator of metabolic addiction and immune suppression.

From a translational perspective, however, the clinical value of NF-κB depends on how its activity can be measured in a standardized and reproducible manner. Potential assessment strategies may include immunohistochemical detection of nuclear p65 localization, evaluation of phosphorylated NF-κB pathway components, and transcriptomic signatures enriched for NF-κB target genes. In principle, these approaches could be integrated with metabolic markers, immune profiling, and pathological parameters to generate a more functional classification of HCC beyond conventional histopathological or genetic subtypes. At present, however, the biomarker utility of NF-κB in HCC remains largely exploratory, as most supporting evidence derives from mechanistic studies, retrospective tissue-based observations, and transcriptional inference rather than standardized prospective clinical validation. Integrating NF-κB-related transcriptional signatures with metabolic and immune profiling may help identify patient subsets that are less likely to respond to standard therapies, including immune checkpoint blockade, and more likely to benefit from tailored combination strategies.

Therapeutic strategies targeting NF-κB networks

Despite its central role in HCC, direct pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB has proven challenging due to concerns regarding systemic toxicity and impairment of physiological immune responses. These limitations have prompted increasing interest in alternative strategies that target NF-κB–dependent networks rather than the core pathway itself. A key conceptual distinction lies between direct pathway inhibition and modular intervention. While direct suppression of NF-κB signaling may yield antitumor effects, it risks disrupting essential inflammatory and immune functions in normal tissues. In contrast, targeting discrete NF-κB–regulated modules offers the potential for greater selectivity and therapeutic window. Metabolic vulnerabilities represent a particularly attractive target[[164], [165], [166], [167], [168]]. In NF-κB–high HCCs, inhibition of glycolytic enzymes such as HK2 or disruption of redox defense systems mediated by SLC7A11 can selectively undermine tumor cell survival. By lowering the metabolic and antioxidant threshold, such interventions may sensitize tumors to ferroptosis and other forms of stress-induced cell death. Immune-oriented strategies constitute another promising avenue. Modulating NF-κB–dependent remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment—rather than directly inhibiting NF-κB in immune cells—may help reverse immune suppression and enhance responsiveness to immunotherapy. Approaches that reprogram tumor-associated macrophages or attenuate NF-κB–driven immune escape programs could synergize with checkpoint inhibitors. Targeting stress adaptation pathways also holds translational potential. Inhibitors of Aurora-A or upstream stress signaling nodes such as TAK1 can disrupt NF-κB–mediated survival responses following radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Such strategies aim to prevent the activation of adaptive transcriptional programs that enable tumor persistence after treatment.

From a clinical standpoint, however, it is important to distinguish between NF-κB-related therapeutic concepts and agents that have been formally evaluated in HCC patients. At present, direct NF-κB inhibitors have not become established therapeutic agents for HCC, and much of the current rationale remains preclinical. Some upstream or functionally connected targets, such as TAK1, Aurora-A, metabolic regulators, or redox-associated pathways, are being explored primarily as pathway-interfering strategies rather than as clinically validated NF-κB-directed therapies in HCC[137,169]. Therefore, the translational relevance of these approaches currently lies more in mechanism-guided combination design than in the existence of an approved or clearly validated NF-κB-targeted drug class for HCC. Collectively, these approaches reflect a paradigm shift: effective targeting of NF-κB in HCC is likely to depend on dismantling its adaptive outputs rather than suppressing the pathway in its entirety.

Challenges and unresolved questions

Despite significant progress, several challenges continue to complicate the clinical translation of NF-κB–targeted strategies in HCC. Systemic toxicity remains a major concern, as NF-κB plays essential roles in immune defense, tissue homeostasis, and stress adaptation in the liver and other tissues[170,171]. Importantly, the biological effects of NF-κB are highly context-dependent and cannot be regarded as uniformly oncogenic. In non-malignant hepatic settings, NF-κB may contribute to tissue protection, injury limitation, and physiological stress responses, whereas in HCC-associated chronic inflammatory environments it is more often co-opted to support malignant progression, immune remodeling, and therapeutic resistance[103,104]. Achieving tumor-selective modulation of NF-κB–dependent programs without disrupting these physiological functions therefore remains a major unresolved challenge. Cell type–specific effects further add to this complexity. NF-κB signaling exerts distinct and sometimes opposing functions in tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal components of the liver[13,31,115]. Interventions that inhibit NF-κB in one cellular compartment may have unintended consequences in another, potentially undermining therapeutic efficacy or safety. A deeper understanding of cell type–specific NF-κB signaling dynamics is therefore essential for the development of more precise therapeutic strategies. Finally, pathway redundancy and compensatory activation pose formidable obstacles. NF-κB operates within an extensive signaling network characterized by feedback loops and crosstalk with other major stress-responsive and oncogenic pathways. Suppression of one NF-κB-dependent module may trigger compensatory mechanisms that restore survival signaling or activate parallel pathways. Addressing this challenge will likely require rational combination strategies informed by comprehensive molecular profiling, pathway-context stratification, and dynamic monitoring of signaling activity.

Conclusions

Accumulating evidence indicates that NF-κB signaling is an important and context-dependent component of the adaptive signaling landscape in hepatocellular carcinoma, linking chronic inflammatory stress with metabolic reprogramming, immune remodeling, and therapeutic resistance. Rather than functioning as an isolated or universally dominant driver, NF-κB operates within a broader oncogenic network involving Wnt/β-catenin, p53, TGF-β, and PI3K/AKT signaling, with a particularly prominent role in inflammation-associated transcriptional adaptation, tumor–microenvironment crosstalk, and stress tolerance under therapeutic pressure. Taken together, the findings discussed in this review support a forward-looking framework in which the future clinical relevance of NF-κB in HCC depends on three major priorities. First, at the biological level, NF-κB should be interpreted in a context-, stage-, and cell type-specific manner rather than as a uniformly oncogenic pathway, given its diverse functions in tumor cells, immune cells, and non-malignant hepatic tissues. Second, at the translational level, progress will require more reliable approaches to measuring NF-κB activity in clinically meaningful settings, including tissue-based markers, pathway-associated transcriptional signatures, and integrated metabolic and immune profiling strategies. Third, at the therapeutic level, effective intervention is likely to depend less on indiscriminate pathway blockade than on selective disruption of NF-κB-dependent adaptive modules, such as metabolic dependencies, redox buffering systems, immune-suppressive circuits, and stress-response programs. Accordingly, the next stage of progress in this field will require biomarker-guided patient stratification, rational combination therapies, and cell type-aware intervention strategies that align NF-κB-related vulnerabilities with tumor context. A more precise understanding of when NF-κB is functionally dominant, how it interacts with other oncogenic pathways, and which downstream outputs are most targetable may ultimately determine whether NF-κB can be translated from a mechanistic hallmark into a clinically actionable framework in hepatocellular carcinoma.
