Pulmonary Aspergillus Nodule in an Immunocompetent Host: A Case Report
Authors: Ahmad Alikhani, Pooria Sobhanian, Mahla Hosseinzade, Shadi Shayesteh Azar, Farshad Naghshvar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAspergillus is a common fungal pathogen that typically causes severe infections in patients with compromised immunity. This report explores a rare case of a pulmonary Aspergillus nodule in an immunocompetent person without underlying conditions. A 52‐year‐old man initially presented for evaluation of his son's allergic rhinitis, during which a chronic cough accompanied by brown sputum was incidentally discovered. The cough started 4 months previously and then gradually worsened. He had a smoking history of 24 pack‐years, without any other symptoms or underlying diseases. A chest X‐ray and spiral lung CT scan revealed round haziness in the left lower lobe compatible with a pulmonary mass, leading to the diagnosis of a pulmonary Aspergillus nodule, which was confirmed by needle aspiration staining and culture. Treatment with voriconazole resulted in significant improvement and reduction in the size of the mass during the 3‐month follow‐up period. This case highlights the potential for pulmonary Aspergillus nodules to develop in immunocompetent individuals.Keywords: fungi, immunocompetence, pulmonary aspergillosis, solitary pulmonary nodule

Introduction

Aspergillus is the most common fungal pathogen in developed countries and includes many species, especially Aspergillus fumigatus [1]. Many Aspergillus spores enter the human body through inhalation daily [2]. The decreased size of these organisms facilitates their passage through the upper respiratory mucosa, enabling colonization in the lower respiratory tract. This colonization is generally inconsequential in individuals with healthy immune systems, as the combined efforts of the respiratory epithelium, resident macrophages, neutrophils, and monocytes effectively prevent significant impacts [3].

Pulmonary aspergillosis severity is predominantly influenced by the host's immune status. The condition manifests in various forms, including allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), invasive bronchial aspergillosis, chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA), and invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) [4]. CPA involves gradual deterioration of the lung parenchyma, manifesting as cavities, nodules, infiltrates, or fibrosis [5]. This condition primarily affects individuals with mild or non‐systemic immunosuppression or compromised pulmonary integrity due to underlying diseases [6]. An Aspergillus nodule is a rare subtype of CPA that can occur even in immunocompetent individuals [7]. We present a case of pulmonary Aspergillus nodule in an immunocompetent individual without any associated comorbidities or notable symptoms.

Case Presentation

Case History and Examination

A 52‐year‐old man presented to our clinic primarily for the evaluation of his son's allergic rhinitis. During the consultation, we incidentally noted that the father himself had a chronic cough. Upon further inquiry, it was revealed that the cough had begun approximately 4 months prior, with recent worsening in both severity and frequency. The patient disclosed that the cough was accompanied by expectoration of brownish sputum. He did not receive medical attention to this issue and disregarded the cough altogether. He did not present any additional symptoms, such as fever, chills, dyspnea, or odynophagia. He worked as a farmer and had no known occupational exposure to industrial dusts or chemical fumes.

The patient's medical history included a smoking history of 24 pack‐years. But we did not find significant signs of chronic bronchitis. Moreover, no other notable medical conditions were reported, and the patient denied the use of any medications, including angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors.

We did not find any remarkable abnormalities on examination, including lymph node enlargement, skin abnormalities, chest malformations, or restricted chest expansion. Auscultation of the lungs revealed decreased sound intensity at the lower left lung base, whereas percussion was normal. On initial presentation, his vital signs were within normal limits, with a temperature of 36°C, blood pressure 115/75 mmHg, heart rate 74 beats per minute, respiratory rate 12 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Routine laboratory tests, including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, C‐reactive protein, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate, did not reveal any abnormal findings. Serologic testing for Toxoplasma gondii (IgM and IgG anti‐Toxoplasma antibodies) was negative. Viral marker tests, including hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), anti‐hepatitis C virus antibody (anti‐HCV), and anti‐HIV antibody, were also negative.

Chest X‐ray (CXR) revealed a round patchy density in the left lower lobe at the pleural base, measuring approximately 6 × 4 cm. The costo‐phrenic angle was sharp. The other part of the CXR was unremarkable (Figure 1).

Round patchy density of the left lower lobe on CXR.

A spiral chest computed tomography (CT) scan showed a subpleural, round homogeneous consolidation in the middle zone of the left lung, measuring approximately 3 × 5 cm in diameter (Figure 2).

Spiral chest CT scan (Round homogenous consolidation in the left lung).

Serum galactomannan and beta‐D‐glucan assays were not performed because these tests were not readily available in our center at the time of evaluation. Diagnosis relied on histopathological examination and culture of the CT‐guided needle biopsy specimen.

Because the lesion was peripheral and pleural‐based, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was not performed. Instead, a CT‐guided percutaneous needle biopsy was undertaken. Histologic examination demonstrated a high burden of Aspergillus, and fungal culture of the biopsy specimen yielded Aspergillus species, confirming the diagnosis.

In the immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, no signs of malignancy were detected.

Fine‐needle percutaneous CT‐guided aspiration was performed, and a histologic study revealed a high burden of Aspergillus (Figure 3). Histopathologic diagnosis revealed the presence of a fungus compatible with Aspergillus fungi and chronic lympho‐plasmocytic infiltration.

Histopathological diagnosis showing a fungus ball consistent with Aspergillus species, accompanied by chronic lymphoplasmacytic infiltration.

Although the patient was immunocompetent and had no known structural lung disease, the presence of a solitary, pathologically confirmed Aspergillus nodule, chronic respiratory symptoms (including chronic cough with brownish sputum and suspected mild hemoptysis), and typical radiological findings led us to classify this case within the spectrum of CPA. Aspergillus nodules are recognized as a subtype of CPA, and current international guidelines recommend azole therapy for symptomatic patients and to prevent disease progression. Accordingly, we initiated voriconazole as first‐line antifungal therapy. Voriconazole was administered as oral antifungal therapy and continued for a total of 6 months. This treatment duration was chosen within the recommended 6–12‐month range for chronic pulmonary aspergillosis, taking into account the absence of major immunodeficiency and the favorable early response. During therapy, liver and renal function tests were monitored regularly, and the patient was assessed for potential drug‐related adverse effects. Clinical follow‐up and serial lung CT scans were used to document progressive reduction in lesion size and to exclude recurrence.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Subsequent evaluation during the 3‐month follow‐up after treatment initiation revealed a marked improvement in the patient's condition, characterized by a notable reduction in both sputum production and coughing, as well as a reduction in the mass size from approximately 5 cm to 3 cm on lung CT. Voriconazole was continued for a total of 6 months. During this period, the patient remained clinically stable, with complete resolution of respiratory symptoms, and serial clinical assessments and follow‐up lung CT imaging were used to monitor further regression and to exclude recurrence.

Discussion

Pulmonary Aspergillus nodules are characterized by opacities surrounded by aerated lung tissue on radiological images [8]. These nodes, although rare, can occur in individuals with intact immune systems [9], but they are more commonly associated with those who have compromised immunity or chronic lung diseases [10, 11]. Therefore, recognizing the potential for Aspergillus nodules in all individuals, regardless of immune status, is crucial for timely intervention and management.

Among these studies, the demographic patterns of patients with pulmonary nodules caused by Aspergillus were highly diverse. A study conducted by Eavan et al. [7] analyzed 33 patients and reported that the mean age of those affected was 58 years (ranging from 27 to 80 years), with a relative prevalence in males (54.5%). This finding aligns with the presented case (a 52‐year‐old man), who falls within the common age range. In contrast, Shuangxia et al. [9] reported that among five patients, the ages ranged between 44 and 73 years, with a predominance of females (60%), suggesting potential differences in sex distribution across the studied populations.

Patients diagnosed with CPA commonly exhibit a range of clinical manifestations, including constitutional symptoms, chronic productive cough, breathlessness, chest discomfort, and occasional hemoptysis [12]. For example, the presence of brownish sputum aligns with these typical features. Additional symptoms frequently reported in the literature include dyspnea, hemoptysis, and weight loss [7, 9]. However, some individuals remain asymptomatic until the advanced stages of the disease. Kang et al. reported that 23% of patients were asymptomatic at the time of diagnosis [13]. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and conducting comprehensive diagnostic evaluations in patients presenting with persistent or chronic respiratory symptoms.

The suspicion of CPA, including Aspergillus nodules, typically arises from radiological findings observed on chest radiographs or CT scans [14]. In CPA, radiological findings typically present as one or more cavities with irregular or thick walls, which may expand over time and form pericavitary infiltrates [15, 16]. Diagnosing pulmonary aspergillus nodules in immunocompetent individuals can be challenging because of their radiological similarity with other conditions, such as malignancies [17]. Imaging modalities are essential for accurately identifying the location and extent of these lesions, although definitive diagnosis often requires histopathological examination. Moreover, in the study of Kosmidis et al., half of the patients tested negative for Aspergillus fumigatus IgG, indicating that a negative result of this test does not exclude a diagnosis [15, 18].

There are no clear guidelines for antifungal therapy for Aspergillus nodules, particularly after surgical resection [19]. Treatment may be individualized on the basis of clinical symptoms and outcomes, and further studies are needed to establish optimal strategies [13].

While some studies, such as that by Shuangxia et al., have shown successful outcomes with surgical resection alone without postoperative antifungal therapy [9], other patients have benefited from antifungal treatment, such as voriconazole, which can lead to improvement without surgery. Azoles, including itraconazole and voriconazole, are widely recognized as first‐line treatments for chronic CPA, supported by strong recommendations from guidelines published by the ESCMID (European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases), ERS (European Respiratory Society), ECMM (European Confederation of Medical Mycology), and IDSA (Infectious Diseases Society of America) [20, 21, 22]. Surgical intervention, as noted by Farid et al., can yield good outcomes with acceptable morbidity, although recurrence remains a challenge [23]. Therefore, a combination of surgical and antifungal approaches may be necessary for optimal management, emphasizing the need for further research to establish optimal treatment strategies.

In conclusion, our study emphasizes the importance of recognizing pulmonary Aspergillus nodules in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients, as evidenced by our case report. These findings suggest that clinicians should consider this condition in the differential diagnosis of respiratory symptoms, even in the absence of traditional risk factors. Considering that there are still diverse perspectives on the management of pulmonary Aspergillus nodules, it is essential to conduct clinical trials that are randomized and multicenter in nature to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of this issue.
