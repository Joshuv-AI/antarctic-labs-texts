Residual Wrist Pain and Cyst Recurrence Following Removal of Dorsal Ganglion With and Without Posterior Interosseous Nerve Neurectomy
Authors: Charles T. Furlong, Henry L. Stanton, Riujie Yin, Michael W. Kessler, Curtis M. Henn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePain from dorsal wrist ganglion cysts may result from irritation of the terminal branch of the posterior interosseous nerve. Excision of this branch is often used to treat wrist pain from various etiologies, including recurrent dorsal wrist cysts. This study examines the effect of performing concurrent posterior interosseous nerve neurectomy (PINN) during primary cyst excision. We hypothesize that concurrent PINN reduces postoperative pain and lowers the need for repeat cyst excision compared with excision alone.MethodsA retrospective chart review identified 173 patients who underwent open primary dorsal wrist ganglion excision from 2015 to 2024. Phone interviews were completed with 73 patients, 37 with PINN and 36 without, at mean follow-ups of 4.4 and 6.3 years after surgery, respectively, to collect Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand scores, persistent pain intensity and duration, cyst recurrence, reexcision, and patient satisfaction.ResultsThere were no statistically significant differences between the PINN and non-PINN groups’ Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand scores regarding patient satisfaction, level of pain, cyst recurrence, and improvement of pain following surgery (P = .7). There was a nonsignificant decrease in pain scores in the PINN group compared with the non-PINN group (P = .26). Among patients with recurrence, there was a nonsignificant increase in pain scores in the PINN group compared with the non-PINN group (P = .83). The rate of cyst recurrence was 13.5% (5/37) in the PINN group and 27.8% (10/36) in the non-PINN group, which was nonsignificant (P = .16). None of the patients with cyst recurrence in the PINN group sought reintervention, whereas 4/10 patients who did not undergo concurrent PINN sought reintervention.ConclusionsThis study failed to show that PINN during dorsal wrist ganglion excision significantly affects clinical outcomes. Further research is needed to evaluate whether concurrent PINN influences cyst recurrence rates and the need for reintervention after recurrence.Type of study/level of evidencePrognostic III

Ganglions are the most common soft tissue masses of the upper extremity, and the dorsal wrist is the most common location in which they occur.1 Although dorsal wrist ganglion cysts are often asymptomatic, patients may report pain, tenderness, weakness, and cosmetic concerns and may elect to undergo aspiration or surgical excision to alleviate these symptoms. The exact pathogenesis of such cysts, as well as the etiology of associated symptoms, is unknown.1 Dellon and Seif2 explain that most, if not all, dorsal wrist ganglions arise from the scapholunate ligament. Therefore, he suggests 1 potential cause of pain associated with dorsal wrist ganglion cysts is compression of the distal branch of the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN), which provides sensory innervation to the dorsal wrist capsule.

When considering the relief of associated pain, surgical intervention produces similar alleviation of pain as nonsurgical treatment but is more likely to prevent recurrence and symptoms associated with recurrence.3 Removing a wrist ganglion is often performed via an open technique to allow for debridement of extracapsular pathology, including mucinous degeneration and scar tissue.4 As such, concurrent excision of the terminal sensory branch of the PIN has been proposed as a treatment for persistent wrist pain and for pain associated with ganglion recurrence that does not respond to conservative management.5, 6, 7 Some surgeons routinely perform PIN neurectomy (PINN) at the time of primary dorsal wrist cyst excision, whereas other surgeons do not include PINN in primary dorsal wrist ganglion excision.

The purpose of this study is to retrospectively compare an open surgical technique that removes the terminal branch of the PIN with an open technique that does not excise the PIN. We hypothesize that patients who undergo neurectomy of the PIN at the time of ganglion excision will have less pain after surgery, especially in the event of cyst recurrence, and therefore be less likely to undergo revision dorsal wrist cyst excision.

Materials and Methods

We (C.T.F. and H.L.S.) conducted a retrospective review evaluating the outcomes of primary dorsal wrist ganglion excision with and without concomitant PINN as performed by 2 experienced hand surgeons at our institution. After institutional review board approval, patients were identified using current procedural terminology codes corresponding to wrist ganglion excision. Inclusion criteria consisted of any adult or adolescent patient diagnosed with a dorsal wrist ganglion who underwent open surgical excision of the cyst with at least 1 subsequent postoperative office visit between 2015 and 2024. Patients were excluded if they did not follow-up with the surgeon for a postoperative visit at any point or if the first follow-up visit was greater than 3 months after the procedure, if they underwent wrist cyst excision in any other location aside from dorsal wrist, or if the cyst excision coincided with additional unrelated wrist pathology addressed operatively at the time of cyst excision.

Pre-, intra-, and postoperative notes were reviewed for pre- and postoperative symptoms, duration of follow-up, episodes and timing of recurrence, and reoperation. Relevant demographic data such as sex, age, and body mass index were also collected.

Within the study period, a total of 173 eligible patients underwent open dorsal ganglion excision at our institution, 69 of whom underwent a PINN at the time of excision and 104 who did not. The decision whether to undergo PINN or not was based on the surgeon and patient preference, after a thorough discussion between the patient and surgeon about their options.

Following the chart review, all eligible patients were contacted by phone for follow-up discussion. Interviews consisted of a short questionnaire on current level of function and activity; involvement in sport or other physical activity; the intensity and duration of any persistent pain, weakness, or stiffness; cyst recurrence; and overall satisfaction with the procedure and outcome. Each metric was scored by the participant on a scale of 0 to 10. Function was assessed using the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) score.8 Other assessed values were graded on a scale from 1 to 10 or via qualitative response. These interviews were conducted at a mean of 4.4 and 6.3 years after initial operation for the PINN group and non-PINN group, respectively. Patients were not known by the authors prior to conducting the phone interviews.

After adjusting for covariates, QuickDASH scores, pain trajectories, cyst recurrence rates, and overall satisfaction were each compared between the 2 groups. Nonparametric continuous outcomes (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand scores, satisfaction scores, pain scores) were compared between groups using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical outcomes (persistent pain, recurrence rates) were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher exact tests, whereas linear mixed-effects models with subject-level random intercepts adjusted for demographic and clinical covariates were used in pain score analysis. Patients were not known to the authors performing statistical analysis.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to determine the effect of routinely removing the PIN at the time of open dorsal cyst excision on patient outcomes. This study failed to support the hypothesis that PINN at the time of dorsal wrist ganglion excision affects clinical outcomes, satisfaction, level and rate of persistent pain, or level of pain in the event of cyst recurrence. Further study is needed to investigate the potential effect of concurrent PINN on the rate of dorsal wrist cyst recurrence and its effect on rates of reintervention in the event of a cyst recurrence.

As previously mentioned, Dellon and Seif2 described the pain associated with dorsal ganglion cysts as originating from compression of the distal branch of the PIN. Furthermore, the posterior interosseous nerve has been suggested to be responsible for persistent pain following dorsal wrist ganglion excision.5 Therefore, a PINN at the time of dorsal wrist excision may theoretically reduce postoperative pain and potentially reduce the need for reexcision in the event of recurrence. Lee et al5 compared an open technique with PINN to an arthroscopic technique without PINN that showed both techniques significantly reduce average pain, but some patients undergoing an arthroscopic procedure without PINN experienced persistent or worse pain, while all open excision patients improved. Additionally, the open technique with PINN led to higher patient satisfaction and fewer reoperations with similar recurrence rates. It should also be noted that, as mentioned in Elahi et al,4 an open technique allows for debridement of extracapsular pathology that may also be responsible for persistent pain described in Lee et al.5 The current study showed that, for an open technique, both the PINN and non-PINN groups experienced reduced pain scores at final follow-up appointments. Additional research is needed to confirm whether there is a difference between PINN and non-PINN groups in an open technique.

Our findings of patient satisfaction of 8.8/10 in the PINN group versus 9.1/10 in the non-PINN group are difficult to compare with the existing literature for both isolated PINN procedures as well as for ganglion excision. Weinstein and Berger9 reported an 85% satisfaction rate with partial wrist denervation with anterior and PINN, whereas Schweizer et al10 reported 69% satisfaction for patients undergoing Wilhelm’s wrist denervation technique. Although Lee et al5 compared a PINN in an open technique to an arthroscopic technique without PINN, the results similarly cannot be directly compared with this study, which compared 2 open techniques. Thus, current literature suggests there may be numerous factors contributing to relatively high patient satisfaction following ganglion excision, with results ranging from 74% to 89% after surgery.11,12 Additional research is needed to determine which factors, including a PINN at the time of cyst excision, may contribute to patient satisfaction.

Lastly, the rate of cyst recurrence, as reported by the patients on the phone interviews, 13.5% in the PINN group and 27.8% in the non-PINN group, aligns with current literature, which suggests that recurrence can range from 4% to 40%. Cyst recurrence had been shown to be associated with women, age under 24 years, and most importantly, the incomplete excision of the cyst stalk.13 The current study suggests the PINN group demonstrated a lower overall patient-reported recurrence rate than the non-PINN. The reasons for this finding are not clear, but it is plausible that patients who underwent concurrent PINN were less likely to notice or seek treatment for recurrent ganglion cysts. This theory is supported by the fact that no patients who reported cyst recurrence in the PINN group sought reintervention, whereas 40% of the patients who reported cyst recurrence in the non-PINN group sought additional intervention. Additional investigation is required to further examine this gap. Differences in surgical technique required to remove the PIN may also account for the lower recurrence rate. A wider excision necessary to remove the PIN may lead to a lower recurrence rate.

This study has several limitations including its reliance on retrospective review. Patients without significant postoperative pain or cyst recurrence may have been lost to follow-up and subsequently excluded from the study. Similarly, responses from phone interviews were incomplete; this may have minimized any potential differences in QuickDASH scores or introduced biases. The patients who agreed to a phone interview may be inherently different than those who did not. Furthermore, dissatisfied patients may have been less likely to return to the clinic or respond to phone inquiries, thereby limiting any difference between the 2 groups.

Dorsal wrist ganglion cysts are the most common soft tissue mass of the upper extremity. Although surgical excision is considered the definitive treatment for such cysts once conservative management has failed, some patients continue to experience postoperative pain and cyst recurrence, necessitating further intervention. To this end, we examined the effects of concomitant PINN on pain, recurrence, and patient satisfaction and found no significant difference from the standard intervention. Further study is needed to evaluate the effects of concurrent PINN on the recurrence rate and the need for reintervention in the event of cyst recurrence.
