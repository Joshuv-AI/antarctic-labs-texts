Statin Use and Recurrent Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Events in Patients With Coronary Artery Intervention: A Retrospective Analysis From a Large Health Care Network
Authors: Omair Ahmed, Jianhui Zhu, Floyd Thoma, Agnes Koczo, Oscar C. Marroquin, Suresh R. Mulukutla, Salim S. Virani, Anum Saeed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) poses a significant health challenge worldwide. While statins effectively reduce low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and lower cardiovascular risk, patients with coronary artery disease who undergo revascularization remain vulnerable to recurrent ASCVD events. This study examined the link between statin intensity, LDL cholesterol levels, and recurrent ASCVD events in patients undergoing coronary interventions within a large health care system.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis using the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center database, including patients aged ≥18 with coronary artery disease confirmed by revascularization (coronary artery bypass graft or percutaneous coronary intervention) since January 2010. Patients were categorized by statin intensity: guideline‐directed statin intensity (GDSI), less than GDSI (<GDSI), or no statin therapy. Outcomes included recurrent myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and all‐cause mortality over a median 6‐year follow‐up.ResultsOf 45 949 patients (69% men), 65% were on GDSI, 25% on <GDSI, and 10% were not on statins. GDSI patients compared with those on <GDSI or no statins had lower rates of myocardial infarction (21.6 versus 34.8 versus 65.3), myocardial infarction/revascularization (38.7 versus 57.5 versus 93.9), and ischemic stroke/transient ischemic attack (10.7 versus 17.7 versus 24.6) (P<0.001 for all). LDL cholesterol levels ≤70 mg/dL correlated with fewer adverse events, multivariable analysis indicated that GDSI significantly lowered recurrent ASCVD events and mortality.ConclusionsGDSI reduces recurrent ASCVD events and mortality more effectively than less intensive regimens or no statins. Optimizing statin use and LDL cholesterol monitoring could improve ASCVD management and outcomes.

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVD) leads to significant morbidity and morbidity worldwide. 1 ASCVD—comprising acute coronary syndrome, coronary revascularization, ischemic stroke, or transient ischemic attack (TIA) and peripheral arterial disease—is estimated to affect ~500 million people globally accounting for close to 19 million deaths. 2 In the United States alone, 2 million hospitalizations and 400 000 deaths are directly attributable to ASCVD, annually. 3 Effective management of ASCVD is multifaceted, focusing on a combination of lifestyle and pharmacological interventions. Among the various interventions, statin therapy has emerged as a cornerstone in the management of ASCVD. Through their cholesterol‐lowering effects, statins have been demonstrated to be efficacious in lowering cholesterol levels, mitigating atherosclerotic progression, stabilizing plaques, and preventing adverse cardiovascular outcomes. 4 Current recommendations by the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association as well as its European counterpart, the European Society of Cardiology recommends the use of high‐intensity statins in patients with ASCVD to target a 50% reduction in low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), underscoring their pivotal role in the management of ASCVD. 5 , 6

Guidelines consistently recognize that patients with acute coronary syndrome remain at an elevated risk for future ASCVD events. 5 , 6 An analysis of 8168 patients showed that approximately half of the patients had not filled a statin prescription within 90 days and that post index ASCVD, a statin user had a lower risk of major adverse cardiac events compared with those who did not fill a statin prescription. 7 In the same vein, LDL‐C reduction with the addition of nonstatin medications to background statin therapy has been shown to reduce the risk of of major adverse cardiac events. 8 , 9 Despite the unequivocal benefits of statins, patients with ASCVD on statins can experience recurrent ASCVD, the reasons for which are multifaceted. 10 , 11 We have previously shown that approximately one third of high‐risk patients without known ASCVD are not adequately treated in a large contemporary health care system. 12 , 13 Considering persistently higher risk of recurrent ASCVD in patients who have established ASCVD particularly coronary artery disease requiring intervention, this research aims to elucidate the rates of adverse outcomes in patients based on their adherence to goal‐directed statin intensity (GDSI) as well as the association of adverse events with LDL‐C levels.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

Data are available upon reasonable request by emailing the corresponding author.

Study Design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted on a cohort of secondary prevention established using data from the extensive network of the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center. This study was approved by the Quality Improvement and Institutional Review Board committees. This study was restricted to secondary data analysis, and thus, the requirement for informed consent from participants was waived. The deidentified data that support the findings of this study may be available from the research team upon reasonable request.

Participants

We included adult patients ≥18 years who had at least 2 interactions in the electronic medical record (EMR) system and a cholesterol panel result within 180 days of their initial encounter. The 180‐day cutoff was used to capture lipid panel data around the index event as well as the recurrent event at study censure given the infrequency to lipid testing in clinical practice. Inclusion criteria required patients to have established coronary artery disease confirmed by revascularization (coronary artery bypass graft or percutaneous coronary intervention) on or after January 1, 2010. Exclusion criteria included no prior ASCVD event, history of rhabdomyolysis, or residence in a skilled nursing facility or hospice. The baseline for data extraction was the encounter at the time of the index coronary event. Patients were followed until a recurrent ASCVD event or until October 2022, with a median follow‐up of 6 years. Data collection used the Epic EMR databases across the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center system. Given that our EMR has limited historic data on self‐identified gender, in this article we used female sex at birth as women and male sex at birth as men. A total of 45 949 patients met the inclusion criteria and were evaluated within the study period. Figure 1 shows the study selection flow chart.

Procedures

All data collection was performed from EMR extraction. Once eligibility was met, patients' baseline characteristics were recorded at time of enrolment after their index cardiac event. Statin regimen (GDSI, less than GDSI [<GDSI], and no statin) and LDL‐C levels were collected at time of enrolment of the patient, as well as near the time of their recurrent ASCVD event. LDL‐C levels were assessed at at least 2 time points: at enrollment (index event) and during follow‐up. For all outcome analyses, follow‐up LDL‐C was defined as the most recent LDL‐C measurement obtained before the occurrence of the specific outcome event (myocardial infarction [MI]/revascularization, ischemic stroke/TIA, or all‐cause death). For patients who did not experience a given outcome, follow‐up LDL‐C was defined as the most recent measurement before censoring. LDL‐C values measured after an outcome were not used as exposures in the primary analyses. Baseline LDL‐C values were used for descriptive analyses (Table 1). For subgroup and outcome analyses, follow‐up LDL‐C levels were used to reflect achieved lipid control after treatment initiation or adjustment, providing a more accurate representation of posttreatment exposure status.

Baseline Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

GDSI indicates guideline‐directed statin intensity; and PCSK9i, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitor.

Baseline characteristics included sociodemographic data (age, sex, and race or ethnicity), Elixhauser comorbidity score (a set of 30 comorbidity indicators), LDL‐C, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, current smoking status, and diabetes status. Categorical variables included sex, race, smoking status, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, current statin use, current ezetimibe use, current PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) inhibitor use, history of kidney disease, history of liver disease, history of rheumatic disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, HIV, and depression. Continuous variables included age, body mass index, blood pressure, cholesterol, LDL‐C, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and hemoglobin A1c. Diabetes was defined as the use of diabetes medication before the baseline date plus either 2 International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) diagnosis codes. LDL‐C values were obtained from routine laboratory tests documented in the electronic health record across our health care system. The majority of these values were calculated using the Friedewald equation. In cases where direct measurement was ordered, direct LDL‐C values were used.

GDSI was based on the 2018 American College of Cardiology/ American Heart Association multisociety guidelines on the management of blood cholesterol, which define GDSI as a high‐intensity statin after an ASCVD event. Specifically, GDSI consisted of atorvastatin 40 or 80 mg or rosuvastatin 20 or 40 mg. <GDSI was defined as any statin use other than GDSI that is, moderate intensity (atorvastatin 10 and 20 mg, rosuvastatin 5 and 10 mg, simvastatin 20 and 40 mg, lovastatin 40 and 80 mg) or low intensity (simvastatin 10 mg, lovastatin 20 mg, fluvastatin 20 and 40 mg) use. Patients on pravastatin represented <1% of the cohort and were excluded from the study.

Patients were also stratified by different LDL‐C level groups with LDL‐C <55 mg/dL, LDL‐C 55 to 70 mg/dL, LDL‐C 71 to 100 mg/dL, LDL‐C 101 to 130 mg/dL, and LDL‐C >130 mg/dL at the time of their recurrent event.

The primary outcomes included repeat MI, ischemic stroke/TIA events, a composite of MI and revascularization and all‐cause mortality. The primary outcomes were reported per statin category as well as LDL‐C levels. Time to start a new statin after index time in the entire cohort was also calculated. Patients were followed from the time of their index event until the first occurrence of a primary outcome, which included repeat MI, revascularization, stroke, TIA, or death. Each clinical end point (MI, MI/revascularization, ischemic stroke/TIA, and all‐cause death) was analyzed independently using time‐to‐event methods. Patients were followed from baseline until the occurrence of the specific event of interest, death, loss to follow‐up, or administrative censoring—whichever occurred first. Thus, outcomes were not treated as mutually exclusive, ensuring that events such as MI followed by death were captured in both respective analyses.

Mortality was assessed using the US Social Security Death Index. Our health care system is exempt from the 3‐year delay period by the Social Security Administration.

All medical records were reviewed for combination of ICD codes from the Center for Medicare & Medicaid Services data sources and documentation review.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographics were initially examined using descriptive statistical methods to detect any outlier of missing values. Missing data were observed across several variables, ranging from 2.8% to 16.7% (Table S1). For continuous variables with <5% missingness, mean imputation was applied, consistent with standard practice when data are missing completely at random or missing at random. Variables with greater proportions of missing values were initially categorized, and missing values were treated as a separate category in regression models to retain sample size and minimize potential bias.

To further assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a multiple imputation sensitivity analysis. Missing values were imputed using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method under the assumption of multivariate normality. Ten imputed data sets were generated using SAS PROC MI, and the final parameter estimates were combined using PROC MIANALYZE, which appropriately accounts for both within‐ and between‐imputation variability. The results from the multiple imputation analyses were consistent with those of the primary analyses and the complete‐case analyses (Tables S2 and S3), supporting the stability and robustness of our conclusions with respect to the handling of missing data.

Descriptive characteristics are presented as n (%) for categorical variables. Continuous variables following normal distribution were presented as mean±SD, and continuous variables not following normal distribution were presented as the interquartile range. Chi‐square method was used to test statistical significance for categorical variables and ANOVA was used to compare differences between groups for continuous variables. The linearity assumption for continuous covariates was evaluated using restricted cubic spline models. For variables demonstrating evidence of nonlinearity, categorical groupings were derived based on inflection points observed in the spline curves, supplemented by clinical relevance.

The primary and composite outcome incident rates (IRs) and mortality were calculated as event rates per 1000 person‐years across risk categories stratified by LDL‐C levels and GDSI. The 95% CIs were estimated using a generalized linear model with the Poisson distribution. Incidence rates and Cox regression hazard ratios (HR) of ASCVD outcomes and all‐cause mortality were calculated across statin intensities (GDSI, <GDSI, or no statin) as well as different LDL‐C groups (LDL‐C >130 mg/dL, LDL‐C 101–130 mg/dL, LDL‐C 71–100 mg/dL, LDL‐C 55–70 mg/dL, LDL‐C <55 mg/dL). All regression models were adjusted for pooled cohort equation variables including age, sex, race, diabetes status, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, aspirin use, and smoking status. Proportional hazards assumption was assessed by Schoenfeld residuals. The GDSI variable was treated as time invariant, and classification of GDSI was based on the statin status before each outcome. Time to GDSI across ASCVD risk groups was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Fine–Gray test was used to assess statistical significance when calculating cumulative incidence function of primary outcomes in patients on different statin intensities. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS (version 9.4, Cary, NC). Statistical significance was set at α=0.05. All tests of statistical significance were 2 ‐tailed.

All analyses were completed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute).

RESULTS

Baseline Demographics

Baseline characteristics are noted in Table 1. Our sample included 45 949 patients who met inclusion criteria (69% men and 31% women) of whom 4711 (10%) patients were not on a statin, 11 609 (25%) were on <GDSI, and 29 629 (65%) were on GDSI at time of their recurrent ASCVD event. White patients were the predominant racial group in the study population (91%) followed by Black patients (5%). Patients on GDSI had higher prevalence of hypertension (52% versus 50% versus 24%), obesity (50% versus 44% versus 23%), and current tobacco use (18% versus 11% versus 8%) compared with those on <GDSI or no statin respectively. There were marginal differences in rates of chronic kidney disease between groups with highest prevalence in <GDSI (5.7%) followed by GDSI (4.35%) and no statin (3.35%). Rates of liver disease were similar between groups.

Outcomes Across Different Statin Groups

The was a graded decrease in incidence rates in patients on GDSI compared with those on no statin and this held true across all adverse outcomes including MI (21.6 versus 65.3, P<0.001), MI/revascularization (38.7 versus 93.9, P<0.001), ischemic stroke/TIA (10.7 versus 24.6, P<0.001), and death (27.9 versus 71.3, P<0.001) (Table 2). Cumulative incidence function analysis (Figure 2) showed a similar trend in the incidence of adverse events across different statin level groups.

In an exploratory multivariable adjusted hazard ratio model comparing LDL‐C levels using LDL‐C 55 to 70 as a reference group (Table S1), similar increases in MI were seen for patients with LDL‐C <55 mg/dL compared with those with LDL‐C 55 to 70 mg/dL (HR,1.23 [95% CI, 1.11–1.37]; P<0.01). Similarly, we saw a marginal increase in mortality in patients with LDL‐C <55 compared with LDL‐C 55 to 70 mg/dL (HR, 1.41 [95% CI, 1.29–1.54]; P<0.01). Similarly for patients on <GDSI, when LDL‐C 55 to 70 mg/dL was used as a reference group, patients with LDL‐C <55 mg/dL had similar to modest increases in MI (HR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.02–1.33]; P<0.05) and mortality (HR, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.17–1.43]; P<0.01).

DISCUSSION

Using an electronic health registry, we examined statin use and its associated CVD event burden across a spectrum of LDL‐C levels in a contemporary health care network (encompassing >200 practice sites). We present 3 key findings: (1) GDSI (ie, high‐intensity statin) use remains suboptimal across this secondary prevention population spanning academic, semiprivate, and private practices; (2) GDSI use, wherever adhered with, was associated with lower rates of recurrent ASCVD events and all‐cause mortality across all LDL‐C levels compared with <GDSI and no statins use; and (3) although rates of adverse events generally decreased with lower LDL‐C levels, there were minimal differences in MI and mortality in patients with LDL‐C <55 mg/dL compared with those with LDL‐C between 55 and 70 mg/dL.

Our results align with existing literature emphasizing the superiority of high‐intensity statins over other statin regimens in reducing cardiovascular risk. 14 Current guidelines recommend the use of a high‐intensity statin to target a >50% reduction in LDL‐C in patient with coronary disease. 5 , 6 Hong et al. reported that among patients with coronary artery disease, targeting an LDL‐C of 50 to 70 mg/dL with moderate‐intensity statin therapy, and escalating to high‐intensity statin if target was not met, was noninferior to patients on high‐intensity statin therapy in reducing major adverse cardiac events. 15 Notably in their cohort of patients being treated to target LDL‐C, statin therapy was escalated to high intensity in only 378 patients (17%), downtitrated in 208 patients (9%), and maintained at moderate intensity in 1614 patients (73%). Our results show that GDSI use compared with no statin was associated with statistically significant reductions in adverse events across all LDL‐C levels, even in those with higher LDL‐C levels beyond the recommended range. Similarly, the benefit of statins was also demonstrated in patients on <GDSI compared with no statins across all LDL‐C levels. These results indicate that although the effect of statin is generally dependent on LDL‐C with the greatest benefit derived from GDSI use and lower LDL‐C levels, significant ASCVD protection can also be achieved with <GDSI. The multifaceted role of statins in reducing ASCVD events has been postulated to extend beyond their LDL‐C lowering effects. 16 Statins have anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties that stabilize coronary plaque and slow atherosclerotic progression. 17 , 18 They also improve endothelial function by increasing nitric oxide production leading to coronary vasodilation as well as acting as a platelet inhibitor to reduce the risk of thrombus formation. 19 , 20

Despite the clear benefits of statins, a considerable number of patients experience recurrent ASCVD events while on this medication. The underlying factors contributing to these events remain complex.

Not all patients achieve optimal LDL‐C reductions despite being on statins, which can lead to residual cardiovascular risk. The IMPROVE‐IT (Improved Reduction of Outcomes: Vytorin Efficacy International Trial) Trial and FOURIER (Further Cardiovascular Outcomes Research With PCSK9 Inhibition in Subjects With Elevated Risk) trial highlight that additional LDL‐C reduction with ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors further reduces the risk of adverse cardiovascular events. 8 , 9 Additionally, there are genetic variations among patients that lead to alterations in statin metabolism resulting in either inadequate LDL‐C reduction or increased risk of statin induced myopathy with subsequent discontinuation or suboptimal dosing, ultimately leading to increased adverse cardiac events. 21 , 22 , 23

Statin nonadherence is a significant cause of recurrent events. Rosenson and colleagues examined the trends in high intensity statin therapy after MI and reported that whereas the use of guideline‐directed high‐intensity statins after MI generally increased from 2011 to 2014, up to 28% to 40% of patients did not fill a prescription for high‐intensity statins. 24 A real‐world analysis focusing on statin use for primary prevention of ASCVD noted that 30% of high‐risk patients who were eligible for statins never received any statins over a 6‐year follow‐up period. 12 The authors also found a higher risk of ASCVD and mortality in the high‐risk group who were not using statins compared with those who were on the GDSI. 12

LDL‐C Levels and ASCVD Events

Whereas the European Society of Cardiology guideline recommends aiming for an LDL‐C <55 mg/dL for patients classified as very high risk, the 2018 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology multisociety cholesterol guideline suggested the consideration of nonstatin lipid‐lowering therapy in this high‐risk group when LDL‐C is >70 mg/dL. 5 , 6 Subsequently, the 2022 American College of Cardiology Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on the role of nonstatin therapies for LDL‐C lowering recommended a target LDL‐C <55 mg/dL in the high‐risk group. 25 High‐risk patients are defined as those having a history of multiple major ASCVD events OR 1 major ASCVD event AND ≥2 high‐risk conditions. Major ASCVD events include recent acute coronary syndrome within the past 12 months, history of MI, history of ischemic stroke, and symptomatic peripheral arterial disease. High‐risk conditions include age ≥65, familial hypercholesterolemia, history of prior coronary artery bypass graft or percutaneous coronary intervention, diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate 15–59 mL/min per 1.73 m), current tobacco smoking, persistently elevated LDL‐C ≥100 mg/dL despite maximally tolerated statin therapy and ezetimibe, and history of congestive heart failure.

Our analysis revealed that although rates of adverse events generally decreased with lower LDL‐C levels, an unexpected pattern emerged in the exploratory analysis among patients with LDL‐C <55 mg/dL. Although overall our findings support using statins to lower LDL‐C levels prevent future risk of ASCVD, we do acknowledge the results in the “lowest” LDL‐C categories. A recent meta‐analysis of 60 randomized controlled tirals did not show a relationship between LDL‐C lowering and all‐cause mortality. 26 States of cachexia secondary to other causes particularly in the setting of malignancy can lead to altered lipid metabolism and increases as well as variable changes in LDL‐C levels but further work is required to elucidate these relationships. 27 Additionally, LDL‐C is also a negative acute phase reactant and levels are known to fall in the immediate period following an acute coronary syndrome event, so the low levels of LDL‐C may be an indicator of the severity of an ischemic coronary event as opposed to a causative factor. 28 However, the sensitivity analysis adjusting for competing risks did not find any alter of our findings.

LIMITATIONS

Several limitations should be considered. As with all large‐scale EMR data, inaccuracies in data entry, coding errors, misclassification of statin exposure, and nonadjudicated end points are possible. Although ASCVD events were not adjudicated, all‐cause mortality was verified using a validated Social Security Index measure. The secondary prevention cohort was limited to individuals with coronary atherosclerosis and does not capture the full spectrum of systemic ASCVD; adults younger than 40 years were not included, although this group was small.

Use of ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors increased from 3566 (7%) patients at enrollment to 14 236 (31%) at censoring, which may have contributed to LDL‐C lowering. Because statin intensity was assessed only at the time of recurrent events, interim dose changes or discontinuation related to tolerability could not be accounted for.

Many patients initiated statin therapy before study entry (defined as the time of coronary revascularization); thus, baseline LDL‐C often reflects the steady‐state effects of long‐term treatment rather than postinitiation change. Under these conditions, LDL‐C functions as a posttreatment risk marker or potential effect modifier rather than a causal mediator. Although multiple LDL‐C measurements were available, limited follow‐up and outcome data restricted to first recurrent events precluded use of a formal time‐dependent Cox model. Accordingly, despite preserving temporality by defining achieved LDL‐C as the most recent value before event or censoring, the findings should be interpreted as associational rather than causal. Finally, although Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were used to account for death as a competing risk and models adjusted for major comorbidities such as cancer, residual confounding and survivorship bias cannot be fully excluded.

CONCLUSIONS

High‐intensity statin use in patients with coronary artery disease was found to be suboptimal within a large modern health care system. The implementation of GDSI contributed to a reduction in recurrent events beyond just lowering LDL‐C levels. Systemwide efforts to promote consistent statin use and regular LDL‐C monitoring could significantly reduce ASCVD risk across the health care system.
