HIV screening and associated factors among adolescent girls and young women in Tanzania: analysis of the 2022 demographic and health survey data
Authors: Elihuruma Eliufoo Stephano, Linus Paul Rweyemamu, Tian Yusheng, Li Yamin, Theresia Ambrose Ottaru, Christopher Hariri Mbotwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection remains a significant global challenge, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to healthcare services is constrained. Despite substantial efforts to combat HIV, the uptake of screening among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) remains sub-optimal. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of HIV screening and identify the factors associated with screening uptake among AGYW in Tanzania.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional analysis of the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (2022 TDHS). The study population included AGYW aged 15–24 years. The outcome variable was HIV screening. Modified Poisson regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with HIV screening among AGYW.ResultsA total of 5,852 AGYW with a mean age of 19.3 ± 2.8 years were included in the analysis. The prevalence of ever “HIV screening” was 60.2% (95% CI: 58.4–61.9). Factors associated with a higher prevalence of HIV screening included older age 20–24 years (adjusted Prevalence Ratio, aPR 1.09, 95% CI 1.04–1.15), currently or previously in marital union (aPR 1.21, 95% CI 1.15–1.27), history of sexual intercourse (aPR 2.32, 95% CI 2.04–2.63), pregnancy history (aPR 1.43, 95% CI 1.35–1.52), awareness of sexually transmitted infections (aPR 1.16, 95% CI 1.10–1.22), awareness of HIV testing kits (aPR 1.08, 95% CI 1.03–1.13), condom use (aPR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.12), ownership of telephone (aPR 1.14, 95% CI 1.09–1.19 for non-smartphone; aPR 1.25, 95% CI 1.13–1.37 for smartphone), and visiting a healthcare facility in the previous 12 months (aPR 1.18, 95% CI 1.13–1.24). Geographical differences showed that women in the Northern and Central zones (aPR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.96) had a lower prevalence of HIV screening compared to those in the Eastern zone.ConclusionThe study revealed a sub-optimal prevalence of ever HIV screening among AGYW in Tanzania and was associated with different individual and interpersonal factors. Targeted interventions that address individual and interpersonal factors to improve HIV screening rates among AGYW should be implemented.Keywords: HIV testing, HIV screening, Adolescent girls, Young women, Tanzania

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection continues to pose a significant global public health challenge, particularly in resource-limited settings like sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where access to healthcare services is restricted [1, 2]. As of 2023, an estimated 39.9 million people were living with HIV worldwide, with SSA accounting for a substantial portion, approximately 25.9 million individuals [3]. In the same year, SSA saw 640,000 new infections and around 390,000 AIDS-related deaths [3]. Encouragingly, 86% of people living with HIV globally are aware of their status, and 77% are receiving treatment [3].

Among the most affected populations are women, including adolescent girls and young women (AGYW), who accounted for 44% of all new HIV infections globally in 2023 [3]. Approximately 62% of these new infections occurred in SSA [3, 4]. AGYW in this region face more than triple the risk of contracting HIV compared to their male peers, with approximately 3,100 new infections occurring among AGYW each week [5]. Despite the high HIV burden in this demographic, prevention programs remain limited and often underfunded [5, 6].

In Tanzania, significant challenges persist in the fight against HIV as the country strives to meet Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.3, which aims to end HIV/AIDS by 2030 [7, 8]. The 2022–2023 Tanzania HIV Impact Survey reported an HIV prevalence of 1.3% among AGYW aged 15 to 24 years, more than double the 0.6% prevalence among males of the same age group [9]. The annual incidence among AGYW was 3.3 per 1,000, compared to 0.0 per 1,000 for males, indicating a growing disparity since 2018, when the incidence was 2.2 per 1,000 [9].

The disproportionate burden of HIV among AGYW underscores the need for targeted interventions that address their specific vulnerabilities despite an overall decline in HIV incidence in the general population [9, 10]. Contributing factors to the high rates of HIV among AGYW in Tanzania include cultural norms, poverty, limited access to education and healthcare, inadequate HIV screening and counselling services, and persistent gender inequality [11, 12].

HIV screening is a cornerstone of prevention and control strategies, as outlined in the Global Health Sector Strategies on Fighting AIDS [6]. Early detection through screening facilitates timely treatment and prevents mother-to-child transmission [13]. However, uptake of HIV screening among AGYW remains suboptimal, particularly in resource-constrained countries like Tanzania, where only 30% of AGYW reported being tested for HIV in the past 12 months [9].

AGYW are especially vulnerable to HIV infection both vertically, through mother-to-child transmission, and horizontally, during adolescence, a period marked by sexual behaviours that increase infection risk [14]. Several factors influence HIV screening among AGYW, including demographic characteristics (age, education, wealth), engagement in high-risk behaviours such as multiple sexual partners, and psychosocial influences like HIV awareness and societal stigma [15–17]. While research on HIV screening among highly vulnerable groups in Tanzania is limited, existing studies highlight several persistent barriers to screening, including cultural norms, stigma, and lack of healthcare access and education [11, 18–20]. Educational attainment and marital status have also been identified as factors that increase the likelihood of HIV screening [11, 18].

Given the disproportionately high number of new infections among AGYW, understanding the factors that influence HIV screening uptake is crucial for developing targeted interventions in Tanzania and similar resource-constrained settings. This study leverages nationally representative datasets to provide updated insights into the prevalence and factors associated with HIV screening among AGYW, with the goal of improving screening services across the country.

Methods

Study design

The present cross-sectional study used secondary data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Indicator Survey (2022 TDHS) [21]. The 2022 TDHS is a nationally representative survey conducted between February and July 2022, aiming to provide current and reliable information on the Tanzanian population and health-related issues.

Study population and sample design

This study included AGYW aged 15–24 years. The sample design for the 2022 TDHS is detailed in the final report [21]. In brief, the 2022 TDHS utilized a two-stage sampling design, covering both urban and rural areas in mainland Tanzania and Zanzibar. In the first stage, 629 enumeration areas were selected using a probability proportion to size method, with 211 from urban areas and 418 from rural areas. In the second stage, 26 households were systematically selected from each cluster (which were delineated based on the 2012 Tanzania Population and Housing Census). All women aged 15–49 years who were either usual residents or non-usual residents to the household on the night before the survey interview were eligible to participate in the 2022 TDHS, resulting in a targeted sample size of 16,354. A total of 15,254 women aged 15–49 years were interviewed. The analysis of this paper is based on a subsample of 5,852 AGYW aged 15–24 years at the time of the interview.

Study variables

The outcome variable for this article was “HIV screening,” a self-reported measure of HIV testing. A respondent was defined as having been screened for HIV if she responded “YES” to the question “Have you ever been tested for HIV?“. The independent variables were identified based on different literature. These included age, educational level (no formal education, primary, secondary or higher), marital status (never in marital union, currently or previously in marital union), age at first sexual debut, occupation (not working, professional occupation, sales, agricultural, skilled manual, unskilled manual, others), exposure to mass media (radio, newspapers/magazines, and television), ever heard of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), use of condoms during sexual intercourse, awareness of the use of HIV testing kits, having multiple sexual partners, pregnancy history, sex of the household head, geographical zone (Western, Northern, Central, Southern highlands, Southern, Southwest highlands, Lake, Eastern, Zanzibar), place of residence (rural, urban), household wealth index (poor, middle, rich) computed based on household assets using principal components procedures [22], mobile phone ownership (no telephone, non-smartphone, smartphone), internet access in the last 12 months, possession of health insurance, visiting a health facility in the previous 12 months, distance to the health facility (big problem, not a big problem), and history of using contraceptives.

Statistical analyses

Data management and analyses were conducted using STATA version 18. Prior to analysis, data were weighted and adjusted for clustering and stratification to account for complex survey design. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Subsequently, we applied a modified Poisson regression model to assess the factors associated with HIV screening among AGYW. Both bivariate and multivariable modified Poisson regression analyses were performed. In the bivariate analyses, we examined the association of each independent variable with ever HIV screening. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable analysis to determine the independent factors associated with HIV screening. To allow comparison across all zones, the variable selection criteria were ignored for the categories of geographical zones. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with no significant multicollinearity detected, as all VIF values were below 5. We presented crude and adjusted prevalence ratios and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

A total of 5,852 AGYW, with a mean age of 19.3 ± 2.8 years, were included in this study. The majority of participants were aged 15–19 years (53.7%), had attained a secondary or higher educational level (50.3%), had never been in a marital union (59.9%), had no occupation (54.6%), had heard of sexually transmitted infections (70.3%), and always used condoms during sexual intercourse (63.0%). Furthermore, a significant proportion of participants were not aware of HIV self-testing kits (88.5%), had not had multiple sexual partners (97.0%), and were not covered by any health insurance scheme (95.8%). The prevalence of ever HIV screening among AGYW in Tanzania

The weighted prevalence of ever having been screened for HIV among AGYW in Tanzania was 60.2% (95% CI: 58.4%−61.9%). This prevalence varied significantly across different socio-demographic characteristics. The highest rates were observed among AGYW with pregnancy history (95.2%), those with a history of marital union (90.1%), those aged 20–24 years (85.5%), those aware of HIV self-testing kits (83.9%), and those reporting sexual activity (82.6%) (Table 2). In contrast, the lowest prevalence of ever having been screened for HIV was found among those aged 15–19 years (37.8%), and those who had never been in a marital union (37.9%) (Table 2).

Factors associated with ever HIV screening among AGYW in Tanzania

Table 2 presents the bivariable and multivariable analyses of the factors associated with ever HIV screening. Our bivariable analyses revealed that age, educational level, marital status, history of sexual intercourse, occupation, exposure to mass media, awareness of sexually transmitted infections, history of using condoms during sexual intercourse, pregnancy history, awareness of HIV testing kits, history of having multiple sexual partners, geographical zone, ownership of a mobile phone, internet access in the last 12 months, visiting a health facility in the previous 12 months, and history of using contraceptives were associated with HIV screening.

In the multivariable analysis, older age, current or previously marital union, history of sexual intercourse, pregnancy history, awareness of sexually transmitted infections, history of using condoms during sexual intercourse, awareness of HIV testing kits, ownership of a mobile phone, visiting a health facility in the last 12 months, and residing in the Northern, Central, and Southern highlands zones were independently associated with HIV screening. Compared to women aged 15–19 years, those aged 20–24 years had a greater prevalence of HIV screening (adjusted prevalence ratio (aPR) 1.09, 95% CI 1.04–1.15). Women who were currently or previously in a marital union had a greater prevalence of HIV screening (aPR 1.21, 95% CI 1.15–1.27) than those who had never been in a marital union. Women with a history of sexual intercourse had over two times higher prevalence of HIV screening (aPR 2.32, 95% CI 2.04–2.63) than those who reported never having had sexual intercourse. Furthermore, women with a history of pregnancy had a 1.4 times higher prevalence of HIV screening compared to women with no pregnancy history (aPR 1.43, 95% CI 1.35–1.52).

The prevalence of HIV screening was greater among women who were aware of sexually transmitted infections (aPR 1.16, 95% CI 1.10–1.22) and HIV testing kits (aPR 1.08, 95% CI 1.03–1.13) than among those who were not aware. Women who reported using condoms during sexual intercourse (aPR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.12) than their counterparts. Women who owned either a non-smartphone (aPR 1.14, 95% CI 1.09–1.19) or a smartphone (aPR 1.25, 95% CI 1.13–1.37) had a greater prevalence of HIV screening than those who did not own a mobile phone. Furthermore, women who had visited a health facility in the last 12 months (aPR 1.18, 95% CI 1.13–1.24) had a greater prevalence of HIV screening than those who had not. When comparing women residing in other geographical zones, those in the Northern and Central (aPR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.96) zones had a lower prevalence of HIV screening compared to those in the Eastern zone.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence of self-reported HIV screening and associated factors among AGYW aged 15–24 years in Tanzania, utilising nationally representative data from the 2022 TDHS. We found that 60.2% of AGYW reported having been screened for HIV. This rate is comparable to the pooled HIV screening prevalence of SSA countries, estimated at 64.4% [23]. Our findings show an increase from the 30% prevalence reported in 2017 among AGYW in Tanzania, indicating improvements in the utilization of HIV screening services in this high-risk population [11]. This improvement may be due to greater awareness of HIV among AGYW and the implementation of the 2019 HIV policy, which allows individuals aged 15 years and older to consent to HIV testing. Another contributing factor could be the expanded availability of self-testing services, broadening access to HIV testing for AGYW and the general population in Tanzania [24].

Despite these improvements, HIV screening among high-risk AGYW in Tanzania remains suboptimal and lags behind neighbouring country Rwanda, where the prevalence of screening is 97.4% [25]. Tanzania, however, has made significant strides toward achieving the joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) 95-95-95 goals, which aim for 95% of people living with HIV to be diagnosed, 95% of those diagnosed to receive antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 95% of those on ART to achieve viral suppression. Tanzania’s progress from 61-94-87 in 2016/2017 to 83-98-94 in 2022/2023 is commendable [9]. To close the gap further, especially among AGYW, innovative strategies must be developed and scaled up. One promising example is the Malkia Klabu (“Queen Club”) initiative, implemented in the Shinyanga region of Tanzania. This program has successfully improved the uptake of HIV self-testing among AGYW by providing easy access to HIV self-test kits through accredited drug dispensing outlets (ADDOs) [26]. Expanding such initiatives could accelerate progress toward achieving the 95-95-95 targets.

HIV testing is crucial not only for identifying HIV-positive individuals but also for raising awareness of their status. Knowing one’s HIV status is vital for accessing appropriate care, initiating ART, and achieving viral suppression, which are key elements in reducing HIV transmission [11, 27, 28]. ​This study highlights the importance of meeting testing targets and ensuring that AGYW who test positive fully understand their diagnosis and receive necessary follow-up care.​ This comprehensive approach is essential for maximizing the effectiveness of HIV testing in preventing further spread of the virus [28].

Sociodemographic factors, including age, marital status, sexual activity, awareness of HIV testing kits, usage of condoms during intercourse, ownership of a mobile phone, visiting healthcare facilities, and geographical disparities, were identified as significant factors associated with HIV screening among AGYW in this study. There was a greater prevalence of HIV screening among women aged 20–24 years than among their younger counterparts aged 15–19 years. This may be due to the perceived low risk among women aged 15–19 years, which is driven by their relatively low sexual activity and lack of awareness of potential HIV-related risks [19]. Another reason could be that women aged 15–19 years are facing barriers such as stigma, inadequate reproductive health education, and poor access to testing services compared to those aged 20–24 years [29]. Furthermore, there is growing evidence suggesting that women in the younger subgroup may face parental or partner opposition in HIV testing, fear of confidentiality breaches and that their results are known, and diminished autonomy in decision-making, all of which can lead to poor HIV testing uptake [30]. Our finding is also supported by other studies, which revealed an increase in the HIV screening rate with age [30, 31]. Taken together, these findings indicate an age-related gap that needs to be addressed by tackling all essential age-specific barriers to HIV screening among AGYW.

This study revealed that the prevalence of HIV screening is associated with marital status. Specifically, women in marital unions or previously in marital unions had a greater prevalence of HIV screening than those who had never been in a marital union. Women in marital unions or previously in unions might have more frequent interactions with the healthcare system due to antenatal care, family planning, and other reproductive health services, leading to more opportunities for HIV screening. Additionally, as evidenced by previous studies, these women may perceive a greater risk of HIV due to potential exposure from their partners [25], prompting them to be tested more frequently. In some cases, these women may receive encouragement or pressure from their partners or healthcare providers to comply with Tanzanian healthcare policies, which require every woman receiving antenatal care to test for sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV. Our findings align with results from Rwanda [25], suggesting that marital status may play a vital role in healthcare-seeking behaviours, particularly in the context of HIV screening.

Our findings show that AGYW who have ever had sex and those who always used condoms during sexual intercourse had a greater rate of HIV screening than their counterparts. This was anticipated because HIV transmission typically occurs through unprotected sexual intercourse, and using condoms is a widely known method to prevent transmission. Thus, sexually active individuals, such as AGYW, particularly those who always use condoms, are more likely to perceive themselves to be at greater risk for STIs, including HIV [32] This heightened awareness and sense of responsibility towards their sexual health motivated them to consider regular HIV testing a proactive approach to maintaining their health. Our findings are consistent with other nationally representative population-based studies from Ghana [33] and Rwanda [25], highlighting the importance of enhancing sexual health education and HIV awareness programs that focus on the role of condom use and its association with proactive health behaviours.

This study revealed that AGYW, who were aware of the use of HIV self-testing kits, had a greater prevalence of HIV screening than those who were not aware of the use of HIV testing kits. This finding was consistent with other literature, which showed that awareness and availability of rapid HIV testing kits influenced the increased uptake of HIV screening [34]. Additionally, adequate HIV/AIDS awareness contributes significantly to the adoption of HIV testing among AGYW [35]. These findings emphasise the role of awareness campaigns and health education in promoting HIV testing. However, some conflicting findings have shown that fear of results and a lack of perceived risk are barriers hindering screening despite awareness of HIV testing kits [36]. Another study indicated that the HIV screening rate was suboptimal despite participants being aware of the structural barriers [37]. Intervening these barriers will increase HIV screening rates among AGYW.

The AGYW who owned a mobile phone had a greater prevalence of HIV screening than those who did not. Mobile phones are vital in disseminating health-related information and have been associated with increased access to healthcare information and services, such as HIV testing. Our findings are supported by a recent study that demonstrated the effectiveness of a smartphone-based mHealth application in increasing the use of HIV prevention services among women at high risk of HIV infection [38]. Similarly, a Ugandan study reported that mobile phone ownership among AGYW was associated with higher HIV screening rates [39], mirroring the findings of this study. The improvement in healthcare-seeking behaviours was also linked to the ownership of mobile phones among AGYW in South Africa [40]. To increase awareness of how to effectively utilize mobile phones for enhancing health-related activities, including HIV screening, tailored interventions are recommended. These include developing and implementing mobile health (mHealth) programs and providing subsidies or financial support to help AGYW purchase smartphones and data plans. Owning a smartphone can significantly enhance HIV screening among AGYW by offering access to vital health information, personalized reminders for testing, and telehealth services, thereby fostering greater engagement with healthcare resources and encouraging proactive health management. For those without access to smartphone or basic mobile phone, health education and outreach campaigns can serve as alternative approaches to raise awareness about HIV screening services.

Access to healthcare services, including routine check-ups and preventive care, has been associated with an increase in HIV screening uptake [41]. In this study, AGYW who visited health facilities in the previous 12 months had a greater prevalence of HIV screening than did their counterparts. These findings were supported by a study showing that the availability of and access to healthcare facilities were associated with increased HIV screening among AGYW in SSA [42]. Regular interactions with healthcare providers provide the opportunity for HIV education, counselling and screening, thereby increasing screening rates. Integrating HIV screening services with other health services could improve the use of HIV testing among AGYW.

A history of pregnancy was associated with a higher prevalence of HIV screening among AGYW. In Tanzania, HIV screening is integrated into reproductive health services, and as part of the first antenatal care visit, women are required to undergo HIV testing. Therefore, AGYW with a history of pregnancy are more likely to have been screened for HIV during antenatal care visits. Promoting the utilization of antenatal and other maternal health services, including breast and cervical cancer screening, could further enhance HIV screening uptake of among AGYW. This finding aligns with existing literature, which highlights that reproductive health events often serve as critical touchpoints for healthcare engagement, particularly in initiating HIV testing [43–45].

The literature shows that access to HIV screening services varies across geographical locations due to differences in healthcare infrastructure, service availability, and awareness of HIV/AIDS [33, 46, 47]. This aligns with our findings, which highlighted geographical disparities in HIV screening. Our results indicate that AGYW in the Northern and Central zones had a lower prevalence of HIV screening, while AGYW in the Southern highlands zone had a greater prevalence. Geographical barriers, including distance to the health facility and transportation costs, also hinder access to HIV screening [48] and may contribute to these inequalities, especially in rural areas in the Central zone. Targeted interventions to ensure equitable access to HIV testing and related health services in the Northern and Central zones are recommended.

The primary strength of this study lies in its use of a nationally representative dataset collected through standardized methods to examine the prevalence of HIV screening and associated factors among AGYW. Additionally, the use of weighting and adjustment for clustering and stratification ensured the robustness of the estimates. However, the study has several limitations. First, the study’s cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Second, the HIV screening variable was self-reported, which may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Participants might have over-reported their engagement in healthcare services, including HIV screening, due to societal expectations or to preserve their social image. Conversely, some findings may be underreported due to the stigma associated with HIV/AIDS. Furthermore, the measurement of outcome variable “ever tested for HIV” may not reflect the current prevalence of HIV screening among AGYW in Tanzania. Some participants may have undergone HIV testing a long time ago, potentially leading to an overestimation of the current prevalence. Nonetheless, the estimates from this study suggest that the issue may be even more significant than reported. Future studies should focus on examining the current HIV testing behaviours among AGYW in Tanzania.

Conclusion

Despite the significant improvement in HIV screening rates among AGYW in Tanzania, the rates remain below the margin recommended by the UNAIDS. This study identified several factors associated with the use of HIV screening such as the age of participants, marital status, engagement in sexual activity, awareness of the use of HIV testing kits, condom use, mobile phone ownership, healthcare facility visits, and geographical location. Targeted interventions should be developed to address these factors with a particular focus on younger girls. The government and other nongovernmental organisations should consider harnessing digital platform such as mHealth in promoting HIV screening among AGYW thereby contributing to the achievements of national and global goals.
