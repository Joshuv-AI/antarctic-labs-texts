Omics insights into the impact of slightly acidic electrolyzed water with sodium benzoate on food and oral associated microbes
Authors: Xiuqin Chen, Momna Rubab, Ramachandran Chelliah, Deog-Hwan Oh, Lingyue Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFood intake strongly shapes the human oral microbiota, as microorganisms can be introduced through fermented foods and sustained by dietary nutrients. In this study, oral microbial samples from 20 caries-free individuals were cultured on hydroxyapatite disks, and sucrose addition shifted the community toward a cariogenic state. Slightly acidic electrolyzed water (SAEW) combined with sodium benzoate (NaB), a food preservative, was applied to control these biofilms. Several food-related bacteria were identified, including Limosilactobacillus mucosae and Lactobacillus sakei with cariogenic potential, and Pseudomonas paralactis and Streptococcus periodonticum with competitive effects against cariogenic strains. SAEW with NaB reduced cariogenic pathogens and suppressed key metabolic pathways, including starch and sucrose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and N-glycan biosynthesis. These findings indicate that SAEW with NaB slows micro environmental acidification and highlights the ecological links between food-associated and oral microorganisms, offering new perspectives for the development of food-derived anti-cariogenic strategies.Keywords: Food microorganism, Oral microbiota, Dental caries, Slightly acidic electrolyzed water, Sodium benzoate

Introduction

There are numerous bacteria in the mouth (Pitts et al., 2017). These bacteria can form an organized community by self-producing extracellular polymers (called biofilms) (Sim et al., 2016). Dental biofilms are made up of microbial cells and extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), and they exist in a highly dynamic and structured matrix (Sánchez et al., 2022). The acidic end products such as lactic acid produced by specific microorganisms accelerate biofilm pathogenesis. These biofilms contribute to the persistence of bacteria within the oral cavity. Such biofilms consequently lead to a low efficacy of antimicrobial agents and a high prevalence of antibiotic resistance (Takahashi and Nyvad, 2008). Moreover, they are involved in synergistic and antagonistic interactions among individual bacterial species, between themselves and the environment, as well as with the host. Hydroxyapatite is the main component of dental enamel, and the hard enamel is the outermost layer of the tooth, which is a rigid surface that is not easily attached. However, the occlusal surface of the tooth and the space between the two teeth are prone to bacterial accumulation to form biofilms (supragingival plaque). These biofilms maintain a stable microbial composition in different niches without significant fluctuations over time, and interactions between species, biofilm, and host are reflected in co-existence with the host in homeostasis during demineralization and re-mineralization. As the most common oral health problem, dental caries accounting for an estimated $27.23 billion in global productivity loss each year (Listl et al., 2015). In addition to genetic factors, excessive free sugars (all monosaccharides and disaccharides added to foods) and dietary carbohydrates intake is now widely accepted as a cause (Ha et al., 2021; Pitts et al., 2017). Consumption of dietary carbohydrates (especially, sucrose) alters the composition of oral microflora towards a disease state, causing acidification of the oral microenvironment, and long-term exposure to an acidic environment may cause enamel demineralization, and form the cavities on the teeth's surface.

Targeting the acidic microenvironment and the structure of cariogenic biofilms is more efficient to prevent and treat dental caries. SAEW, generated by electrolyzing dilute NaCl (sometimes KCl or MgCl2), has broad-spectrum antibacterial properties (Feng et al., 2021). The potent antimicrobial properties of SAEW were attributed by the available chlorine content (ACC) (Farah and Al-Haj Ali, 2021). SAEW is considered an eco-sustainable anti-microbial detergent, one advantage is that SAEW may be quickly reduced to water, reducing the influence of residual chlorine on human health and safety. It is reported that SAEW showed synergetic anti-microbial effects with organic acid (Mansur et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2021), and the reactive oxygen species(ROS) production of SAEW at pH 5 was 48% higher than that produced at pH 7, this suggests that SAEW has greater antibacterial activity in the acid-rich cariogenic niche than in non-acidic environments (Son et al., 2022). SAEW completely killing activity within 1 min with the tested oral Candida spp (Wu el al., 2025). NaB is used as a food preservative and approved by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as generally recognized as safe (GRAS). The antibacterial activity of NaB is particularly effective in acidic environments. HClO applied in human oral has been reported by many researchers (Cantero Giraldo and Endo Cabrera, 2022; Deng et al., 2010). SAEW and NaB widely used to kill pathogens in foods such as fruits, vegetables and meat (Du et al., 2020). Few people look at the effect of the combination of SAEW and NaB on oral microbiota and ecological competitiveness of oral commensal bacteria.

Identification of dental caries-related bacteria and oral symbiotic bacteria is critical for the prevention of dental caries. Currently, Streptococcus mutans is a recognized pathogen associated with dental caries, there are no other widely recognized pathogens directly related to caries process. S. mutans can catalyze fermentable dietary carbohydrates into organic acids, which is a major etiologic agent of dental caries. When exposed to sub-lethal low pH, the competitive advantage of S. mutans conferred the acid-adaptive response known as ATR (acid tolerance response). Once S. mutans is dominant, large amounts of the toxin (PrrC, reutericyclin, mutanocyclin) are released to kill nearby commensal colonization (Bacusmo et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2020). Interestingly, S. mutans and some pathogenic bacteria have synergistic cariogenic effects, for example, S. mutans gtfs coding for glucan synthesis, and also bind to other oral microbes, even those that do not naturally express gtfs, thereby converting them into de facto glucan producers (Lemos et al., 2019). Several opportunistic cariogenic microorganisms in dental plaque, primarily streptococcus and lactic acid bacteria, metabolize carbohydrates (particularly sucrose) to produce organic acids such as lactic acid, resulting in local tooth demineralization. Dietary carbohydrate consumption, dysfunction of saliva flow and composition, and poor oral hygiene, which can lead to plaque accumulation, can disrupt this balance and be directly linked to dysregulation of bacterial biofilms, which can lead to the onset and progression of dental tissue damage (Pyati et al., 2018). In our previous study, Streptococcus salivarius, Streptococcus anginosus, and L. sakei were determined to be the most cariogenic pathogens (Chen et al., 2020). In this study, we developed a multi-species biofilm though co-cultural S. mutans with the oral isolates (S. salivarius, S. anginosus, and L. sakei). The cariogenic biofilm was discovered to be capable of demineralizing dental materials, making it one of the best models for studying dental caries.

The objective of the present study was to develop a cariogenic biofilm model and validate its demineralization capabilities, as well as to characterize the anti-caries effect of the combination of SAEW and NaB against the cariogenic biofilm. This therapy was also applied on a S.mutans mediated caries related model consisting of the S. mutans, Streptococcus salivarius, Streptococcus anginosus, and L. sakei to further verify its anti-caries function.

Material and method

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Kangwon National University, Chuncheon, South Korea (KWNUIRB-2022-02-008-002).

Samples collection and preparation

A dental examination was carried out by one experienced dentist, and all enrolled volunteers (n = 20, aged 25-35) had no clinical symptoms of systematic diseases. The International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) criteria were used to detect dental caries. Healthy 25-35 years old adults who were caries-free and had no history of dental caries (DMFT = 0) were included in the study. Local oral medicine use or treatment within 7 days, irregular eating within 3 months, and pregnant or lactating women were also excluded. Subjects were asked to fast for 2 h before donating supragingival plaque samples. Before the sample collection, participants were instructed not to brush their teeth for 24 h and were asked to rinse their teeth with water. The sample collection was based on an earlier study (Du et al., 2020). Operation procedure: Separate the area to be sampled with a cotton roll and blow dry with a gentle airflow from an air-water syringe. All supragingival plaque was scraped from the mesial surface of the occlusal part of the inside of the mouth using a sterile cotton swab. Dip the tip of a cotton swab into the buffer in the test tube for 4-5 s. Repeatedly rub the surface of the cotton swab against the edge of the collecting tube. Put the lid on the tube and shake for 4-5 s to try to maximize the dispersion of the specimen in the liquid. Record the sample number and sampling time on the label and place the tube in a Ziploc bag on ice. All the tubes were transferred to the clinical laboratory within 4 h and were centrifuged at 5000 g for 5 min. The supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were stored at - 80 °C.

Cariogenic biofilm model

Using hydroxyapatite (HA) disks (Biotek, Santa Clara, CA, United State) as the growth surface, HA disks were dipped into artificial saliva (Biochemazone, ST NW, Edmonton, Canada) for 5 min to form an acquired pellicle, followed by being transferred to a sterile 24-well plate, each well was filled with 3 mL of sheep horse (SHI) medium, since analysis showed that SHI medium was capable of supporting a more diversified community with a microbial profile closer to that of the original oral microbiota (Tian et al., 2010). The SHI medium has the following composition: proteose peptone (Difco) 10 g; trypticase peptone (Difco) 5.0 g; yeast extract (Difco) 5.0 g; KCl 2.5 g; sucrose 5g; hemin 5 mg; vitamin K1 mg; urea 0.06 g, arginine 0.174 g; mucin (type III, porcine, gastric; Sigma Chemical Co., StLouis, MO) 2.5 g; sheep blood (Colorado Serum Co., Denver, CO) 5 % and NAM 10 mg. After 24 h biofilm formation, the HA disks were carefully taken out from the growth medium and transferred into new 24-well plates containing two types of medium: 1) artificial saliva, 2) artificial saliva supplement with 1% sucrose.

SAEW and NaB solution preparation and treatment procedure

The preparation of SAEW solution followed the method described by Feng, H. et al. (2021) with slightly modification (Feng et al., 2021). The SAEW was created using the SLB-120 HOCl production system (SeoulinMedicare Co., Ltd. Gyeonggi-do, Korea). A composite electrolyte consisting of dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl, 3-5%, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) and sodium chloride (NaCl, 2-5 M, Sigma, USA) was passed through the system at 2.5-3 V, 6000 mA to produce SAEW. By adjusting the concentration of the electrolyte, the SAEW with different concentrations of the ACC was obtained (18 ppm-22 ppm). The pH values of all produced SAEWs were determined using a dual-scale pH meter (Accumet model 15; Fisher Scientific Co., Hampton, VA, United States). The ACC of all SAEW was measured by a colorimetric method with a digital chlorine test reagent (20J3A; Kasahara Chemical Instruments Corp., Japan). The NaB (Junsei Chemical, Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was dissolved in sterile distilled water and mixed with SAEW(ACC:18 ppm-22 ppm) to obtain the final treatment solution with the working concentration of 15 ppm SAEW and 0.1% NaB. The treatment procedure was as following: The HA disks that dipped in the artificial saliva supplement with 1% sucrose were subjected to intermittent exposure to treatment solution (15 ppm SAEW + 0.1% NaB) for 1 min at 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, and 60 h of biofilm culture. The treatment solution was applied by complete immersion (3 mL per well, gentle shaking for uniform contact). After each 1 min exposure, the disks were immediately rinsed twice with sterile 0.1% BPW (1 min per rinse) and blotted dry with sterile filter paper to remove residual solution. A parallel control group was set with HA disks exposed to 0.1% BPW using the same immersion, rinsing and culture procedures. Fresh medium was replaced for all groups immediately after each exposure and rinsing step. After 60 h growth and treatment, the disks were transferred into tubes content PBW, and biofilm were removed from disk surface by ultrasonic treatment (50 kHz, 25 w) for 5 min and collected for further metagenomics analysis. Meanwhile, the remaining HA disk was transferred into dry oven to remove moisture, the changes in dry weight were calculated by initial weight minus the final weight.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

DNA was extracted from plaque samples and amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with 16S rRNA gene primers 341 F and 806 R targeting the V3-V4 region. Following AMPure bead cleaning of the PCR amplicons, 100 ng from each library was pooled, run on an agarose gel, and gel purified. A bioanalyzer was used to determine the DNA concentration of the gel-purified amplicons, which was followed by surrogate quantification using real-time qPCR. The library mixtures were then spiked with 20% sequencing control (PhiX Control, Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and run on a next-generation sequencer for 12 pM. (MiSeq, Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The analyses were purged of bad reads and chimeric sequences.

Metabolite annotation and pathway analysis

Sample preparation and UHPLC-ESI-QTOF-MS/MS metabolites detection

After the last round of exposure, biofilm samples were collected from the attached surface, and 100 mg biofilm was mixed with 300 mg cold methanol (stored at −20 °C), followed by thoroughly vortexed and subjected to ultrasonication (50 kHz, 25 w) for 10 min. Samples were then stored at −20 °C for 2 h, after which they were spun for 15 min at 25,000 g at 4 °C, 200 μL of the combined supernatant was taken and filtered through a 0.22 μm hydrophilic polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane prior to metabolomics analysis. An ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (SCIEX ExionLC AD system, Framingham, MA, USA) operated with an Acquity UPLC CSH C18 column (100 mm × 3 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Columns were maintained at 50 °C with a 0.4 mL/min flow rate and a mobile phase composed of water and 0.1% formic acid (A) and methanol and 0.1% formic acid (B). Gradient elution conditions were as follows: 0-2 min, 100% A; 2-11 min, 0% B–100% B; 11-13 min, 100% B; 13-15 min, 0% A-100% A. The ESI-QTOF-MS/MS was operated under electrospray ionization in positive and negative modes (ESI±) with a mass range of m/z 50–1500; the collision energy was ramped at 10–40 eV (collision gas: high-purity nitrogen), capillary voltage was 3.5 kV (positive) and 3.0 kV (negative), and desolvation temperature was 500 °C. Agilent Mass Hunter qualitative analysis software (B.10.0) was used for MS control, data acquisition, and data analysis. By comparing retention time (RT) and UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS2 data with spectral literature evidence. The spectral library XCMS online (Metlin) (https://metlin.scripps.edu) and metabolomics workbench (https://www.metabolomicsworkbench.org) cross-check and determine the compounds.

Metabolite annotation and pathway analysis

The identified metabolites from different biofilm samples were mapped onto Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) reference in compound ID, the pathways enrichment analysis for these metabolites was identified through MetaboAnalyst5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).

S. mutans mediated caries related model development

Strains culture conditions

S. mutans (KCTC 3065) was purchased from Korean Collection for Type Cultures (Daejeon, Korea). S. salivarius, S. anginosus, and L. sakei were originally isolated from a carious dentin by our previous work (Chen et al., 2020), and the nucleotide sequence of three strains have been submitted to the NCBI (MW014348, MW014349, MW014351). The strains were frozen at −80 °C, and activated in growth media at 37 °C for 18-24 h before the experiment. All the microbes were grown in Brain Heart Infusion medium (BHI, BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) (Tian et al., 2010).

Biofilm formation

Before the establishment of a multispecies biofilm model, all the cariogenic strain cultures used in this study were first standardized. S. mutans, S. salivarius, S. anginosus, and L. sakei were grown overnight in BHI broth at 37 °C. Similarly using a previously published study, all strains were washed three times with 0.1 % buffered peptone water (BPW), and then standardized to a final concentration of 3 log CFU/mL in BHI medium (Liu et al., 2018). The sterilized HA disks were dipped into artificial saliva (Pickering laboratories, INC, CA, United State) for 5 min to form acquired pellicle, and then placed into the 24 well plates that filled with 2 mL BHI medium. Strains were mixed in equilibrium and a total volume of 20 μL was inoculated into 24 well plates. Cultures were incubated statically and horizontally for 12 h at 37 °C for the initial attachment, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h after the attachment phase change new medium. The single-species and multi-species biofilms formed on the surface of HA disks were examined using a crystal violet (CV) assay combined with image analysis as described before (Sen et al., 2020). To improve the mechanistic reliability and translational potential of this study, two complementary biofilm models were employed: a saliva-derived multispecies cariogenic biofilm model to simulate the complex oral ecological environment, and a S. mutans-mediated caries-related model to clarify the core antibacterial and antibiofilm mechanisms under defined conditions.

Assessment of caries related models

CV assay

The experiment was performed according to a previously described procedure with slight modifications (Agarwalla et al., 2019). Briefly, the HA disks coated by biofilms were washed with 0.1% BPW three times to remove unattached cells. The washed disks were transferred into 24-well plates filled with 2 mL of 0.1% CV solution staining for 30 min. The stained biofilms were rinsed three times with 0.1% BPW before being transferred to a new 24-well plate filled with 3 mL of 70 % ethanol. After 45 min washout, the amount of biofilm biomass was determined by measuring absorbance at 595 nm with the SpectraMax i3 plate reader (Molecular Devices Korea, LLC). The assays were carried out in three independent experiments.

AFM image processing and roughness quantification analyses

The architecture of biofilm coated on the surface of HA disks was observed by atomic force microscopy (AFM). The biofilm was dried in a laminar flow safety cabinet, and the AFM (Dimension Fastscan, Bruker Inc., Germany) image analyses were done using a Bruker Corporation Nanoscope V Multimode 8 scanning probe microscope. All studies were conducted under ambient conditions (25 °C, 25% relative humidity) using the same AFM probe. After biofilm AFM measurement, the biofilms were removed by keeping the HA disks under ultrasonic treatment (50 kHz, 25w) for 2 min. SEM image processing analyses

The surface topography of the biofilm was inspected using a scanning electron microscope (SEM), and the SEM (ThermoFisher) measurement according to the protocol described in a previous published study (Wijesinghe et al., 2019). Briefly, the biofilms were treated with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in water (Sigma-Aldrich Corp; St Louis, MO, USA) fixation (2 h, 4 °C) followed by continuous ethanol dehydration (from 50% to 100%; 10 min/step). All samples were gold-coated by a sputter coater before the observation.

Rheological parameters

The rheological parameters of the biofilm before and after treatment were measured using a Discovery Hybrid Rheometer HR3 (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) with a cone and plate geometry (cone-diameter 40 mm, cone angle 2). The biofilm was placed in a 2 mm gap between the cone and the plate geometry at 25 °C. The complex viscosity analysis was done at a constant angular frequency (0.2 rad/s) at a strain value of 0.04%.

Statistical analyses

Biofilm biomass, reduction of dry weight, and surface roughness data were analyzed using Graphpad Prism 8.0. Normality and homogeneity of variance were verified prior to analysis. Differences among groups were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For metabolomics and microbiome data, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. All results were presented in triplicate.

Results

Sucrose saliva-derived biofilm was cariogenic

Sucrose is unique among dietary carbohydrates and primarily recognized as a cariogenic substrate (facilitating S. mutans biofilm formation via glucosyltransferase) for both fermentation (acid production) and the synthesis of insoluble EPS. Its role in regulating secondary metabolism, including antibiotic biosynthesis is well-documented (Paes Leme et al., 2006). The addition of sucrose to the culture medium significantly increased the biofilm formation of oral microbiota. However, subsequent treatment with SAEW + NaB substantially reduced biofilm volume, indicating an effective inhibitory effect on biofilm development (Fig. 1a). Oral biofilms are highly structured microbial communities embedded in the extracellular matrix, a multifunctional scaffold required for oral microbes to live and fully express their virulence. The extracellular matrix has physical and biological properties that improve biofilm adhesion and cohesion, as well as creating a diffusible regulatory environment that protects microorganisms and allows for the formation of local acidic pH niches. This study compared the acid production properties of various biofilms, and the results showed that cariogenic biofilm could result in rapid acidification of the growing environment, when compared to the commensal biofilm (Fig. 1b). When SAEW and NaB was applied during the cariogenic biofilm formation, it not only slowed the rate of acid production but also increased the environment's final pH.

The production of acids of the biofilm is the key virulence factor constitutes dental caries. We measured the damage to HA disks caused by various oral biofilms. The results showed that cariogenic biofilm reduces the dry weight of HA disks nearly 8 times more than the control (HA disks were dipped in the medium without inoculation). The use of SAEW + NaB reduced the damage caused by cariogenic biofilm on HA disks (Fig. 1c). It is worth noting that commensal biofilm also caused weight loss in HA disks (28.0 mg), much higher than the control (5.5 mg). To further elucidate the relationship between biofilm biomass and HA disk demineralization, Spearman's rank correlation(ρ) analysis was performed on the experimental data of CC, CAC and TC groups to quantify the monotonic correlation between biofilm biomass (OD590) and HA disk dry weight reduction. Results showed a positive monotonic correlation between biofilm biomass and HA disk dry weight reduction.

SAEW and NaB modulated bacterial compositions of oral biofilm

SAEW and NaB tended to restore the dysbiotic biofilm community toward a commensal-like profile as supported by the bacterial composition patterns in Fig. 2. Biofilm formation is a complex process that is affected by many factors such as growth environment, nutrition, bacterial vitality, and surface characteristics. Sucrose has long been considered one of the most cariogenic carbohydrates (Cai et al., 2016; Du et al., 2020). In the present study, the saliva derived biofilm model was established to test whether sucrose can introduce microecology imbalance. The mean relative abundance of phylum, genus, and species of biofilm were increased after SAEW and NaB treatment. SAEW and NaB have regulate the biofilm taxonomic dysbiosis toward a commensal phenotype.

As can be seen from Fig. 2b, the unique microbiome composition of commensal biofilm and cariogenic biofilm is evident in the species level. The cariogenic biofilms showed a significant increase of S. salivarius, Limosilactobacillus mucosae, Staphylococcus caprae, S. mutans and Corynebacterium vitaeruminis. Meanwhile, we detected that the composition of Serratia liquefaciens, Streptococcus periodonticum, Pseudomonas paralactis, and Porphyomonas pasteri was significant high in the commensal biofilm. Fig. 2b showed that in the treatment biofilm, the composition of S. salivarius, Limosi Limosilactobacillus, and Staphylococcus caprae deceased, while the content of Pseudomonas paralactis, Rothia mucilaginosa, and Lactiplantibacillus garii increased. Fig. 2b shows that some strains have a significant presence in the cariogenic biofilm; these bacteria are thought to be potential caries-causing bacteria. We investigated the effects of SAEW + NaB on these potential pathogens. The results showed that the treatment inhibited the amount of those bacteria; however, the composition of some oral residents in the biofilm increased after being treated with SAEW + NaB (Fig. 2d).

Bacteria that are abundant in the cariogenic biofilm, implying that they were enhanced by sucrose, are regarded as potential cariogenic bacteria (caries-causing bacteria). In contrast, some bacteria showed a significant decrease in abundance after sucrose addition due to competition from bacteria that were heavy sucrose users. Fig. 2c shows that caries-causing bacteria and oral health-related resident bacteria are negatively correlated, indicating that they are competitive, except for L. sakei, which demonstrated a symbiotic relationship with Porphyromonas pasteri and Rothia mucilaginosa. Among them, S. salivarius, Limosilactobacillus mucosae, Staphylococcus caprae, and S. mutans were significantly negatively correlated with Serratia liquefaciens, Pseudomonas paralactis, Streptococcus periodonticum, and Haemophilus parainfluenzae (p < 0.05). The most prominent microbial activity in the oral microbial ecosystem associated with dental caries is the fermentation of carbohydrates or host-derived components, particularly the conversion of sucrose to lactic acid.

The metabolites from the biofilms developed under healthy, cariogenic and treatment environments were detected using UHPLC/Q-TOF-MS. 120 compounds were identified from all the metabolomics features observed. Microorganisms present in oral biofilms live together through intermicrobial communication, including co-adhesion/aggregation, metabolic interactions, bacteriocin release, quorum sensing, gene exchange, etc. Metabolic activity is the ultimate output of the biological hierarchy, the end product of oral biofilm is acid production, which is directly related to the formation of dental caries. In addition, other products of microbial biofilm metabolism may also be indirect contributing factors to dental caries. Overall, supplementing the medium with sucrose enhanced several bacterial metabolic activities. These included starch and sucrose metabolism, as well as N - glucan biosynthesis.

S. mutans mediated caries related model development

To establish and validate a S. mutans-mediated caries-related model for subsequent mechanistic studies. To rule out the ability of biofilm formation of different combinations, we allowed strains to grow in the SHI medium for a total of 60 h, and then quantified attached cells and their metabolites by reading under 590 nm wavelength. Oral-isolated bacteria benefited from co-culturing with S. mutans by demonstrating increased biofilm mass, according to the OD value (Fig. 3a). We discovered that oral-isolated strains may produce multi-species biofilm, but only at low biofilm volumes. However, S. mutans monospecies biofilm reached greater biofilm volumes. It is worth noting that S. mutans increased the ability of oral-isolated strains to form biofilms significantly, multispecies biofilm showed a dramatic increase in the presence of S. mutans. That could be explained by the viscous metabolites of S. mutans, the metabolites bind to bacterial cells nonspecifically, which may allow adhesion and aggregation of other pathogens. Li et al. discovered S. mutans produces mutanofactin-697, which increases bacterial hydrophobicity and promotes bacterial adhesion and subsequent biofilm formation (Li et al., 2020).

The distribution of microbes in the biofilm is not random, and one microorganism may influence the colonization of another. Patricia et al. reported the same trend, they found that C. albicans promoted the ability of Streptococcus oralis, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Streptococcus gordonii to form biofilm (Khan et al., 2021). The presence of S. mutans caused major structural changes in the mixed biofilm (Paula et al., 2020). Besides, other researchers also reported that S. mutans-derived multi-species biofilm is more biomass-rich than non-mutans biofilm. Wang et al. found that Streptococcus oralis, Actinomyces naeslundii, and S. mutans were grown together to stimulate gene expressions in bacteria upon adhesion to a surface, and they conclude that multi-species oral biofilm influence adhesion associated gene expression by S. mutans (Wang et al., 2020). Auto-inducers, on the other hand, maybe engaged in quorum sensing exclusively with the cariogenic bacteria, non-mutans Streptococci and Lactobacillus may interfere with the adhesion force sensing with S. mutans. The transformation of oral biofilm colonization from health to disease is based on the concept of microbial “complex”, in vitro biofilm model provides a useful system for oral microbiology research. Caries is now believed to be caused by unbalanced microecology and pathogenic bacteria rather than mono-species biofilms formed by a single species (Bowen and Koo, 2011). The specific plaque idea was developed by Loesche [8], he proposed that S. mutans, together with other streptococcus and lactic acid bacteria, are the pathogens that cause tooth caries (LOESCHE, 1976). There is no appropriate oral biofilm model, and accurate microbe-microbe or microbe-host interactions cannot be replicated in vitro. However, understanding how some organisms interact in biofilms and are impervious to their hosts can aid in our knowledge of microbial dynamics.

The ultrastructure of S. mutans mediated caries related model was evaluated using AFM and SEM. The thickness of the naturally dried biofilms was showed in Fig. 3b, S. mutans co-cultural with isolated cariogenic bacteria formed the thickest biofilm (557.8 nm), followed by S. mutans single biofilm (396.4 nm), and finally isolated bacteria (269.1 nm), SEM micrographs showed that mixed biofilm (S. mutans co-cultural with isolated cariogenic bacteria) demonstrated a complex and dense biofilm structure (Fig. 3c). Such a compact and well-organized structure is known to contribute to the pathogenicity of cariogenic biofilms, including enhanced acid tolerance, drug resistance, and persistence in the oral environment. Many pathogenic mechanisms of microbial disease are now commonly viewed as crossover and multiple microbes found in nature, especially in the oral cavity, in addition to S. mutans, Candida albicans (C. albicans) also has been implementing different oral infections as potential “active accomplice” (Delaney et al., 2019), it is reported that hydrolases produced by C. albicans promote symbiotic and pathogenic properties (Wibawa, 2016). Although the fungal load was estimated to be several orders of magnitude lower in healthy people, their synergistic interactions with bacteria, indicating that these microorganisms play an essential role in the formation of dental plaque (Koo et al., 2018). Hyun Koo et al. reported that C. albicans, in particular, collaborate with Streptococcus gordonii, Streptococcus oralis, and Streptococcus sanguinis to promote bacterial colonization and biofilm formation (Koo et al., 2018). Our findings indicated that when S. mutans was included in the oral biofilm, the biomass and acid production of the biofilm increased, and the structure of the biofilm changed. It means that in a biofilm mode of growth, oral inhabitants often possess new, emergent properties.

In vitro biofilm culture systems using specific media have become useful for oral biofilm studies and shed light on microbial adhesion, species interactions, and anti-biofilm therapy. It is of great interest to investigate the link between multi-species oral biofilms and dental caries' etiology. A three-oral-Streptococcal-species model, including S. mutans, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Streptococcus gordonii was developed by Lyu et al., they present a novel idea for inhibiting key cariogenic agents in mixed biofilms (Lyu et al., 2021). The multi-species biofilm model developed in the present study was representative and destructive, and the biofilm coated on the surface of HA disks mimics the cariogenic biofilm on human teeth.

Effect of S. mutans mediated caries related model on the demineralization of HA disks

S. mutans-derived multi-species biofilm leads to a large degree of demineralization (Fig. 4). HA disks were allocated into three groups (n = 6) and immersed in a microbial culture medium. Microbial metabolites, particularly organic acids, can cause the demineralization of HA disks, as a result, reduce their dry weight. Both metabolic activity and biomass levels of biofilm increased in the presence of S. mutans. By examining the polynomial correlation, we found that dry weight reduction of HA disks was correlated with the biomass levels of biofilm (Fig. 4b). It is worth noting that, S. mutans biofilm caused more demineralization of HA disks than isolated strains biofilm. However, there is no significant difference in biofilm biomass between those two groups. This indicates that biofilm biomass is not the only factor influencing tooth demineralization, biofilm structure, and virulence properties are also important. As shown in Fig. 4b, the surface of the HA disks is broken after being coated with biofilm, indicating calcium and phosphorus loss, which explains the drop in dry weight. The interkingdom biofilms presented here are physically robust, S. mutans impacted the biofilm virulence profiles.

Artificial HA disks have previously been used as a model system or equivalents for natural enamel due to their thickness, homogeneity, and composition (Elliott et al., 2005). HA disks covered by cariogenic biofilm exhibited a rough surface morphology (Fig. 4c). Surface roughness is one of the important indexes to measure tooth demineralization, S. mutans-derived oral multispecies biofilm covered on the surface of HA disks for 60 h can promote a increase in the surface roughness (Fig. 4c). Acid corrosion is responsible for the increase of HA disk's surface roughness, there is a prior report of HA disks immersed in 0.1 mol/L acetic acid, which leads to Ca2+ progressively being released from HA disks into the solution, and the release of Ca2+ was essentially linear with time (Huang et al., 2022; Shipman et al., 2020).

When the SEM method is used to monitor the demineralization of HA disks caused by oral biofilm, we discovered fissures on the disks' surface, which could indicate mineral loss (Fig. 4b). Caries progress due to many cycles of demineralization and re-mineralization of enamel, HA disks resemble a teeth surface (Pathan et al., 2021). We attempted to explore the relationship between tooth demineralization and the surface covering biofilm, the biofilm amount was polynomial fit to the dry weight of HA disks, and this indicates that demineralization is not only related to the biomass of biofilm covered on the surface but also may be related to the structure and acid production of biofilm. Previous literature reported that enamel mineral loss has a logarithmic relationship with proton concentration (Harper et al., 2021). Besides, the prismatic structure in significantly demineralized enamel may be vulnerable to chewing force. Our findings demonstrated that one of the key virulence characteristics of S. mutans is their ability to promote cariogenic biofilm formation. S. mutans exist in subgingival plaque and have regulatory effects on other species.

S. mutans is a significant opportunistic pathogen seen in dental biofilms associated with severe pediatric caries (Ellepola et al., 2019). Our work proves that S. mutans promote cariogenesis in the multi-species biofilm by interacting with other oral cariogenic bacteria and increasing the biomass of the biofilm. In the early stages of demineralization, it is possible to develop effective therapeutic viable treatment strategies for remineralization.

Effect of SAEW + NaB on S. mutans mediated caries related model

Anti-biofilm materials prevent biofilm formation by impeding biomass accumulation, bacterial population growth, and exopolysaccharide matrix deposition. Anti-biofilm effect of SAEW + NaB against S. mutans mediated caries related biofilm is shown in Fig. 5a. Biofilm volumes of S. mutans mediated biofilm were significantly reduced after treatment with SAEW + NaB (72.8% of inhibition compared to the control). In our knowledge, the mechanisms of SAEW's disinfection efficacy are including: 1) HOCl would destroy cell membrane and disorder intracellular metabolism; 2) The high ORP characteristics of SAEW can alter the electron flow in microbial cells (Ding et al., 2015). The mechanism of NaB's disinfection efficacy is to reduce the bacterial intracellular pH, forcing the bacteria to pump extra protons out of the membrane, which consumes ATP and leads to damage to the bacteria cell (Chen and Zhong, 2018). It is well known that the biomass of a biofilm depends on the number of microorganisms and extracellular matrix secreted. In addition to disinfection efficacy, we have investigated the effect of the treatment used in the present study on the biofilm extracellular matrix. As can be seen from the SEM image (Fig. 5a), the image of the treated biofilm showed distinguishing signs of density reduction and morphological abnormalities. There is currently no sufficient information explains the mechanism which led to this conversion. A theoretical possibility is that SAEW + NaB can disrupt biofilm viscoelastic metabolites synthesis and structural architecture. The rheological properties of biofilm have not been fully studied. They can, however, be seen as viscoelastic material in which the cells are analogous to colloids and the encapsulating extracellular polymeric mucus (EPS) operates as a cross-linked polymeric gel. Erin S et al. propose that viscoelasticity contributes to the virulence of biofilms during infection, contributing to the persistence and tenacity of these communities (Gloag et al., 2020). Vinogradov et al. proposed that S. mutans biofilms represent the behavior of rheological fluids (Vinogradov et al., 2004). Therefore, we have developed a multi-species cariogenic biofilm model induced by S. mutans, and taking the cariogenic biofilms as an example, the effect of SAEW + NaB on the viscoelasticity of cariogenic biofilms was tested. As shown in Fig. 5a, for the treatment group that produces less exopolysaccharide, the biofilms behave as viscosity reduction.

Aside from the microbial communities, there is still a scarcity of “what is really happening” on the surface of teeth. Because dental caries is caused by local cariogenic bacteria and their carbohydrate metabolism, there has been increased interest in learning about the microecology of teeth biofilm (Wang et al., 2022). The polysaccharide component of the extracellular matrix is essential for the viscoelasticity of oral biofilm. Furthermore, it is reported that the viscous component was generated by the sucrose metabolism of bacteria in the biofilm envelope, whereas the viscoelastic properties represent the liquid-like behavior of the biofilm (Araújo et al., 2019). For example, glucan, which is a polysaccharide made up of many glucose molecules with varying chain lengths and generated by extracellular glucosyltransferases on sucrose, is the most well-studied biofilm matrix polymer in dental plaque. It is also thought to be a key virulence factor in the etiology of dental caries (Jakubovics et al., 2021). In this regard, we have measured the metabolite content in the biofilm. Then, to find the relevant metabolic pathways, we did an enrichment analysis of the differential metabolites in the KEGG pathways (Fig. 5b). The results that starch and sucrose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and N-glycan biosynthesis of biofilm were enriched in the cariogenic biofilm. Xiao et al. explored pathogens’ function association with caries from a virulence factors view, which suggested N-glycan biosynthesis was positively correlated with the caries enrichment (Xiao et al., 2021). Meanwhile, the enrichment of arginine biosynthesis was detected in the treated biofilm, and the arginine biosynthesis pathways were previously known to be dental health-promoting (Baker et al., 2021). We concluded that SAEW + NaB could alter biofilm structure by influencing bacterial metabolic pathways. The disruption in metabolism causes a loss of structural integrity and stability in biofilm, interfering with colonized cells' ability to aggregate and hindering the formation of structured microcolonies. Similarly, Amauri et al. determined that the presence of dextranase resulted in the displacement of bacterial clusters on the surface of tooth material (Paula et al., 2020). Given the wide variety of polysaccharides contributing to the biofilm matrix, the mechanism of biofilm matrix degradation remains to be determined. In particular, those metabolic pathways related to acid and extracellular polysaccharide production (Ellepola et al., 2019).

Discussion

Several bacterial species identified in this study, such as Limosilactobacillus mucosae, L. sakei, and Pseudomonas paralactis, are frequently associated with foods including fermented meat, dairy products, and vegetables. Their detection in oral biofilms suggests a close ecological link between food-associated microorganisms and the human oral microbiota. This overlap highlights the potential of dietary intake not only to introduce beneficial microbes but also to alter oral microbial balance in ways that may affect oral and general health. Limosilactobacillus mucosae is a core lactic acid-producing bacterium in fermented dairy products and fermented soybean products (Morais et al., 2022), while L. sakei is the dominant spoilage and functional bacterium in fermented meat products such as kimchi (Kim et al., 2020). Their direct detection in oral biofilms confirms the oral-food microbial transmission axis and reveals a close ecological link between food-associated microorganisms and the human oral microbiota: dietary intake acts as a direct microbial colonization pathway, through which food-borne bacteria enter the oral cavity with food consumption, adhere to the hydroxyapatite surface of tooth enamel via their surface adhesion proteins, and participate in the construction of oral biofilm communities.

NaB is widely applied as a food preservative, and SAEW has been explored as an eco-friendly sanitizer in the food industry. The combination tested here showed strong inhibitory effects on sucrose-driven cariogenic biofilms. These findings suggest potential applications beyond oral health, including the control of spoilage or pathogenic microorganisms in food systems. SAEW and NaB exhibited a synergistic anti-cariogenic effect. SAEW could disrupt bacterial cell membranes and cause intracellular pH disturbance, which may promote the entry of NaB and enhance its inhibitory activity against bacterial metabolism and biofilm development. On the other hand, NaB might stabilize the microenvironment and help maintain the antibacterial activity of SAEW. The combination of SAEW and NaB thus exerted enhanced effects against cariogenic biofilms compared with individual treatment.

The differences in microbiota between patients and non-patients necessitates a very large sample size; on the other hand, there are large differences in the specificity of individual hosts (Rupf et al., 2018). Therefore, environmentally induced pathogenic transformation of the oral microbiota in caries-free individuals is a simple and compelling way to investigate caries-related microbiota.

Attempts to link specific biological species to human caries have investigated in this study, the dominant strains of cariogenic biofilm may be potential pathogens that promote dental caries progression. With the advent of microbiome analysis, researchers have begun to uncover the relative importance of various bacterial species in community structure. Furthermore, a significant research effort is required to understand the mechanisms underlying the development of mixed-species communities containing S. mutans and define their roles. Indeed, our assay results revealed that S. mutans accounted for only 0.02% to 0.15% of the disease bacterial community in dental caries biofilms, demonstrating the disease's polymicrobial nature. This lends credence to the eco-plaque hypothesis of dental caries, which holds that the entire acidproducing/aciduria flora, not just S. mutans, contributes to the dental caries process. Recent glycolysis ecotype characterization in orally healthy individuals suggests that our own functional microbiome may play a role in determining our susceptibility to dental caries.

We have developed two caries biofilm models in this study that is able to recapitulate the above concept, that is, the biodiversity associated with dental caries. The isolates in S. mutans mediated caries related model are not consistent with the caries associated pathogens identified by omics, simply because the growth medium we used for pathogen isolation may have led to the loss of some species because it did not fully meet their nutrient requirements (Chen et al., 2020). To facilitate further understanding of caries mechanisms, models that can elucidate the biological processes of dental caries are needed. Current models have limited biodiversity and lack representation of some species and genera, it does not fully represent competitive bacteria that may be present in cariogenic states. It is scientifically relevant to understand clinical isolates and test how it can be transformed in simple single species and novel multimicrobial biofilm systems.

The distribution of microorganisms in the biofilm is non-random, one organism may have an impact on the colonization of another. Interspecific interactions are also expected to be a key factor in determining the microenvironment within biofilms, which in turn determines disease relevance. Liu et al. (2018), for example, found that four co-isolated bacteria from soil have spatial positioning dynamics that correlate with an increased bio-volume of the community. Similarly, interactions between species inside dental biofilms create a niche for dental caries promotion or health maintenance (Khoury et al., 2020).

Notably, the in vitro S. mutans-mediated multi-species biofilm model established in this study, which consisted of S. mutans, S. salivarius, S. anginosus and L. sakei, cannot fully recapitulate the structural and functional complexity of in vivo oral biofilms. The native oral microbial ecosystem harbors over 700 bacterial species along with fungi, archaea and viruses, with intricate interspecific and interkingdom interactions, and is continuously regulated by host factors such as saliva flow, immune responses and mechanical friction (Pathak et al., 2021).

Collectively, the present study demonstrates that SAEW combined with NaB can effectively suppress cariogenic biofilms by targeting pathogenic bacteria and disrupting key cariogenic metabolic pathways. In line with current trends in synergistic disinfection strategies, this combined approach achieves enhanced antimicrobial effects through complementary mechanisms, where SAEW disrupts bacterial integrity and NaB potentiates metabolic inhibition, thereby avoiding the limitations of single-agent applications. Furthermore, by reshaping the oral microbial community rather than simply eliminating pathogens. These findings provide a novel, food-related antimicrobial strategy with promising translational potential for future clinical and oral healthcare applications.
