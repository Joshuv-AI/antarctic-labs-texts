Cloud-based collaborative CNC manufacturing framework integrating tool wear monitoring and scheduling support
Authors: Imran, Mourad Nouioua, Samir Mekid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a cloud-native cyber-physical CNC manufacturing framework that integrates collaborative CNC operation with a tool wear condition monitoring (TWCM) case study for supervisory decision support. The framework combines edge–cloud connectivity, secure data exchange, and data-driven analytics to enable condition-informed scheduling and coordination across distributed manufacturing resources. As a representative analytics service, a vibration-based TWCM pipeline is implemented for CNC turning, where laser Doppler vibrometry signals are decomposed using variational mode decomposition (VMD) and processed through an AutoML-based regression workflow to estimate flank wear. Tool-health outputs are published to the cloud and linked to supervisory scheduling logic, supporting actions such as tool-change recommendations and job-queue updates. The system is demonstrated on CNC turning of AISI 1045 steel using carbide inserts in a multi-node edge–cloud testbed. The results show accurate wear estimation and illustrate how tool-health intelligence can be integrated into a collaborative cloud environment for decision support. The proposed framework does not implement autonomous machine-level closed-loop control, but provides an extensible pathway toward condition-aware CNC collaboration aligned with Industry 4.0 principles.Keywords: Industry 4.0, Tool wear condition monitoring, Cloud manufacturing, Cyber-physical systems, Machine learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Manufacturing is changing rapidly as cyber physical systems (CPS), the industrial Internet of Things (IIoT), cloud and edge computing, digital twins, and artificial intelligence are increasingly combined under the industry 4.0 vision1–3. These technologies support connected production systems that can monitor processes, analyze data, and support timely decision-making. However, successful adoption requires more than technology alone. Small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs) often face practical barriers related to maturity, compatibility, and scaling, and therefore need clear and realistic integration strategies4–6.

CNC machining remains essential in sectors such as aerospace, automotive, and energy, but many CNC systems still operate as isolated units with limited interoperability and rigid control architectures7–9. This limits data sharing across machines and reduces the use of advanced data-driven services. Cloud manufacturing addresses this by virtualizing manufacturing resources and offering them as services, enabling shared analytics and collaboration across distributed sites10,11. Recent work has also explored cyber physical integration using digital twins and service-oriented manufacturing frameworks to support coordination and system-level visibility12,13. In addition, STEP-NC enabled edge–cloud systems have shown that distributed CNC machining can be coordinated through standardized process information exchange14,15.

Despite these advances, cloud enabled CNC collaboration still faces major challenges. Key issues include real-time data acquisition, communication delay and reliability, secure connectivity, and the integration of legacy equipment that lacks standard interfaces16–18. For collaborative cloud CNC to be effective, accurate and timely information about machine and process health is required. Tool wear is a major driver of unexpected downtime, reduced quality, and increased production cost, and it also influences sustainability through its impact on energy use, surface quality, and waste19–21.

Tool wear condition monitoring (TWCM) is therefore an important enabler of predictive maintenance in Industry 4.0 settings20,22,23. Vibration based monitoring is widely used because it is non-intrusive and sensitive to changes in tool–workpiece interaction. Advanced signal decomposition methods, such as variational mode decomposition (VMD), can help isolate wear-related components in complex and noisy signals and have been applied successfully in tool wear prediction studies24–26,29. In parallel, machine learning approaches are commonly used to model the nonlinear relationship between sensor features and tool wear progression20,29.

Automated machine learning (AutoML) has also been used to reduce manual model selection and hyperparameter tuning, which can support more practical deployment of data-driven monitoring solutions32–34. However, many studies still treat cloud-based CNC collaboration and TWCM as separate topics. In many cases, wear prediction results remain at the level of monitoring dashboards and are not connected to higher-level coordination and supervisory decision workflows such as scheduling support and workload allocation27,28,36. Recent edge–cloud monitoring studies and wireless tool monitoring systems represent important progress, but comprehensive integration of tool-health outputs into collaborative CNC decision support remains limited31,38.

Motivated by these gaps, this study proposes a cloud native CPS CNC manufacturing framework that integrates collaborative operation with a TWCM case study. The goal is to publish tool-health outputs as a service and connect them to a cloud-based scheduling and decision-support environment, so that wear information can inform supervisory actions such as tool-change recommendations and job-queue adjustments. The key contributions of this work are:

• A cloud native CPS CNC manufacturing framework that enables distributed CNC machines and other manufacturing resources to share data and coordinate through cloud services, consistent with Industry 4.0 principles.

• A TWCM case study based on vibration signals processed using VMD to identify wear-sensitive components during turning operations.

• An AutoML based modeling pipeline adopted from our previously published TWCM study to support VMD mode ranking and regression model selection for predicting flank wear (VB).

• Integration of TWCM health events into a supervisory scheduling workflow for condition-informed job allocation and tool-change recommendations, demonstrated in a CNC turning case study on AISI 1045 steel with carbide inserts. The current prototype does not implement autonomous machine-level closed-loop control.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on cloud-based CNC manufacturing, CPS, and TWCM. Section 3 describes the proposed cloud enabled CNC architecture, sensing setup, and TWCM methodology. Section 4 presents the experimental results and discusses the model performance and system-level integration. Finally, Sect. 5 concludes the paper and outlines directions for future research.

Related work

Cloud manufacturing has evolved manufacturing systems from machine-centric automation toward service-oriented and networked production environments10,11. Early cloud-based CNC frameworks mainly focused on migrating monitoring and management functions to centralized platforms and enabling resource virtualization for on-demand services27. More recent studies introduced cyber physical integration concepts, including digital twins, to support shop-floor visibility, simulation, and decision support12,13.

Edge–cloud collaborative systems have been proposed to combine scalable cloud resources with faster local response by distributing computation between local gateways and centralized services15,33. STEP-NC based approaches further improve interoperability by using standardized process information, which enables workstep-tagged data exchange and coordinated task delivery across heterogeneous CNC machines14. However, practical deployment still faces barriers related to legacy equipment connectivity, communication reliability, and cybersecurity requirements16–18. Tool wear condition monitoring is an important component of predictive maintenance in machining. Reviews report a wide range of sensing modalities, including cutting forces, vibration, acoustic emission, temperature, and motor current19,20. Vibration-based monitoring remains attractive because it is non-intrusive and cost-effective for existing machine tools20. Advanced signal processing methods are used to improve wear-sensitive feature extraction. Variational mode decomposition has gained attention because it reduces mode mixing and provides meaningful frequency-band separation in noisy machining signals, and it has been applied in tool wear prediction studies25,29. These features are commonly combined with machine learning models to estimate flank wear and related quality outcomes29,35.

AutoML frameworks automate model selection and tuning and are increasingly used in predictive maintenance applications to reduce manual effort and support repeatable deployment33,34. At the same time, recent studies have begun to integrate tool condition information in distributed or connected monitoring settings. For example, Li et al. (2024) propose an edge–cloud collaborative monitoring architecture that integrates tool condition information into surface quality monitoring through distributed recognition and edge–cloud deployment38. Chen et al. (2025) propose an intelligent wireless tool condition monitoring system based on an instrumented monitoring ring and a deep learning model, supported by monitoring software for acquisition and visualization31.

Despite these advances, the integration of TWCM outputs into higher-level CNC collaboration and scheduling workflows remains limited. Many works focus on isolated diagnostics or single-machine monitoring and do not explicitly connect tool-health predictions to supervisory coordination actions such as job rescheduling or tool-change planning27,28,39. This work addresses this gap by presenting a cloud-native integration pathway in which tool-health outputs are published as a service and consumed by supervisory scheduling and decision-support logic within a collaborative CNC environment. Materials and methods

In this work, vibration signals were captured using a scanning laser Doppler vibrometer, as shown in Fig. 2, which targeted the surface of the tool holder during operation.

Illustrative example of a CNC machine sensor setup.

The system detected motion-induced Doppler shifts in the reflected laser beam, enabling non-intrusive and high-resolution characterization of the tool holder’s dynamic response in both amplitude and frequency domains. Laser Doppler vibrometry (LDV) was selected to avoid physical contact with the tool–holder or machine structure, thereby eliminating mass loading effects and mounting variability that can influence measurements obtained with contact-based accelerometers. This approach allows subtle vibration changes associated with tool wear to be captured without altering the machining dynamics. The scanning trajectory was synchronized with the feed rate to ensure accurate tracking of the tool path.

A TNMG-style carbide insert was used as the cutting tool in all machining trials. Surface roughness (Ra) was measured at the end of each machining pass, while flank wear (VB) was quantified using an Optika B-500 optical microscope.

Each machining test involved the acquisition of high-resolution vibration signals sampled at 51,200 Hz. This sampling configuration ensured that detailed dynamic behavior was captured throughout the tool wear progression and across relevant frequency ranges. Although LDV was used in this study to establish a high-fidelity reference dataset, the proposed TWCM framework is sensor-agnostic and can be adapted to industrial accelerometer-based measurements for deployment.

Variational mode decomposition (VMD) was applied to preprocess the vibration signals and reduce noise and drift effects. VMD decomposes the raw signal into a finite set of intrinsic mode functions, each associated with a specific frequency band. This decomposition facilitates the separation of wear-related vibration components from irrelevant high-frequency noise and baseline drift, improving feature extraction quality without requiring additional filtering or denoising steps.

Cyber-physical integration for intelligent machining

Research demonstrates how traditional CNC machining systems achieve intelligence through the implementation of CPS architecture, which combines edge-Layer intelligence with explainable decision-making13. The CPS system distributes intelligence across machines in the network through its cloud-based learning mechanism. Through distributed coordination, real-time adjustments to tool degradation and process disturbances, along with workload variations, become possible, boosting production agility14,15. Accurate tool degradation predictions minimize scrap production while extending tool lifespan, supporting sustainable lean operations.

Automated decision support reduces manual intervention while maintaining operator supervision to ensure reliable manufacturing quality and consistent production outcomes. Implementing tool-condition-aware scheduling helps prevent unnecessary tool replacements and overuse, aligning operational performance with environmental sustainability goals and energy efficiency requirements. The implementation holds strategic value for the South Asian and Middle Eastern manufacturing sectors, which prioritize maximizing cost-effectiveness alongside productivity. The implementation of these systems faces ongoing obstacles.

The AutoML pipeline presented in Fig. 4 extracts frequency-domain features, including power spectral density (PSD) and peak amplitude from each intrinsic mode that VMD produced. The training of regression models for VB estimation occurred independently for each mode while their performance was evaluated through RMSE and R² metrics. The AutoML system ranked the modes according to these evaluation criteria to determine which features provided the most wear information. The ensemble model received its best candidates after multiple learners were compared for performance evaluation. The AutoML engine performed hyperparameter tuning and cross-validation operations to achieve optimal generalization and stability.

The main obstacle to successful integration arises when attempting to connect legacy CNC systems that do not support standardized protocols or contemporary interfaces. The process of retrofitting these machines demands investments in IoT hardware devices and secure networking solutions. Cloud connectivity introduces security risks that require the implementation of secure data transfer channels together with access restrictions and data integrity standards to safeguard machine data while it is remotely analyzed. Cloud connectivity provides advantages through remote analytics and scalability yet creates security risks for data integrity and failover reliability. This study specifies a baseline security architecture; full security implementation and validation are outside scope.

Security architecture and data protection

The proposed cloud enabled CNC framework defines a security baseline requirement to protect manufacturing data and limit unauthorized access. Communication between edge devices and the cloud is intended to use encrypted channels with mutual authentication, for example TLS 1.3 with X.509 certificates, to support confidentiality and endpoint verification. Access to data streams and services can be restricted using role-based access control at the broker and API layers, for example using token-based authorization (such as OAuth 2.0) and topic level permissions for different user roles (operator, engineer, administrator). Data integrity can be supported through authenticated transport, and optional application layer signing or hashing can be applied when storing or forwarding messages across services.

In addition, edge gateways should apply local firewall policies and continuous monitoring of abnormal traffic patterns. While these measures define a practical baseline, a complete industrial cybersecurity program also requires physical security of edge devices, routine patch management, security audits, incident response planning, and compliance processes aligned with industrial standards such as IEC 62,443. The current work focuses on the architecture and its functional integration and does not provide a full security implementation, security testing, or certification.

Within this cyber physical architecture, tool condition awareness supports intelligent and adaptive machining operations at the supervisory level. Real time monitoring outputs are integrated into the decision workflow to support live performance evaluation, intelligent task allocation, and decision support for actions such as tool change recommendations and job queue adjustments.

The integrated TWCM pipeline (VMD-Based AutoML regression) is used as a representative condition-monitoring microservice to illustrate how edge analytics can publish health indicators to the cloud scheduler. Other machine types and analytics (e.g., AM process monitoring, energy monitoring, quality inspection) can be integrated via the same interfaces.

Results and discussion

Integrated TWCM

A complete experimental assessment of the proposed cloud-based CNC manufacturing framework integrated with the TWCM system was conducted after deployment to evaluate predictive performance, operational impact, and demonstrator level responsiveness. The demo factory setup enabled a multi node test in which the cloud platform managed tool condition data exchange among CNC machines operating at the same time. The centralized scheduler used real time tool health indicators to support distributed coordination and task assignment.

Scalability and communication performance were evaluated in this controlled demonstrator with multiple CNC nodes and a cloud broker. The end-to-end communication delay remained below 250 ms, which is acceptable for many CNC monitoring and supervisory functions. However, industrial operating conditions such as network jitter, bandwidth contention, and heterogeneous controller interfaces were not assessed. Therefore, the reported results demonstrate feasibility at demo scale and show that the framework can be extended, but they do not represent industrial scale validation.

The TWCM system was evaluated under a controlled machining setup using a TNMG style carbide insert and AISI 1045 steel. Fixed baseline settings were selected to track how vibration features evolve with flank wear (VB). As a result, the current model is specific to this tool and material pair, and extension to other tool geometries, coatings, materials, or broader operating regimes will require additional data and model adaptation. For deployment on other tool material combinations, additional training data and model updating or retraining are required.

In this work, TWCM is presented as an example edge analytics service integrated into the cloud framework, rather than a universal model covering all tools, materials, and processes. TWCM evaluation focused on predicting VB from vibration features extracted using VMD followed by PSD based feature computation.

In the present prototype, the scheduler issues recommendations and priority updates based on VB severity events; autonomous execution of tool change commands at the CNC controller level is not implemented (Table 2).

The detailed investigation showed that the second intrinsic mode function exhibited a higher relationship to wear than the other bands as shown in Fig. 5.

Frequency spectra (FFT) of the second VMD mode for signals S2 and S21.

The second VMD mode function corresponds to a mid-frequency resonance band which may include tool–workpiece–holder system dynamics. This mode is highly sensitive to VB progression because it captures changes in contact stiffness, damping, and vibration coupling induced by wear. As shown in Fig. 5, the FFT of VMD Mode 2 reveals stronger spectral peaks and slight frequency shifts between early-stage (S2) and worn-state (S21) signals. These changes reflect the altered dynamics caused by tool degradation and justify strong correlation of VB with second VMD mode. This physical interpretation supports the selection of the second mode as a dominant wear-indicative component. The identified bands matched existing mechanical resonance frequencies from cutting tool vibrations, which supported the physical meaning of extracted features. The Spectral features extracted from these bands demonstrated predictable changes when tool wear increased, which allowed the system to monitor tool degradation in real time.

The system demonstrated good quality control by helping to maintain stable surface finish. The Ra measurements showed that increasing flank wear (VB) led to a clear increase in surface roughness during the tests. In particular, Ra exceeded 2.5 μm when VB was greater than 0.25 mm, which indicates unacceptable surface quality. By integrating wear predictions into the collaborative scheduling engine, the system can support early actions such as rescheduling jobs or recommending tool changes to help preserve surface quality.

Although the TWCM module predicts VB progression using vibration features, it functions within this framework as a decision-support component rather than an autonomous controller. The predicted wear values are mapped to severity levels and displayed in the user interface, allowing operators or scheduling logic to initiate actions such as tool changes or job queue adjustments. No autonomous tool change actuation or fully automated closed loop rescheduling was executed in this study; the loop is demonstrated at the supervisory decision level.

The TWCM ensemble model was evaluated using an 80/20 train-test split in the original study40. While the reported R² of 0.98 demonstrates strong predictive capability on the test set, we acknowledge that single-split evaluation does not fully characterize model variance across different data partitions. The reported RMSE and MAE values should be interpreted as point estimates specific to this particular train-test configuration rather than population statistics with confidence intervals.

Future validation studies will employ k-fold cross-validation or bootstrap resampling to provide more robust statistical characterization of model performance variability. Additionally, these metrics reflect performance within the controlled experimental dataset (TNMG carbide insert on AISI 1045 steel under fixed cutting parameters detailed in40) and may not generalize directly to different machining conditions, tool geometries, or workpiece materials without model recalibration or transfer learning approaches.

While the proposed framework demonstrates robust TWCM performance in the experimental setup, practical deployment requires acknowledgment of industrial implementation challenges. The current validation was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with stable network connectivity (latency < 50 ms), standardized MTConnect protocols, and homogeneous machine configurations.

Limitations

This study presents a demonstrator-level cloud-enabled CNC framework with an integrated TWCM case study, and several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the multi-node connectivity and communication performance were evaluated in a controlled demonstrator environment. Industrial network conditions such as variable latency, packet loss, bandwidth contention, and large-scale heterogeneity of machines were not systematically assessed, and therefore industrial-scale validation remains future work.

Second, the TWCM results are based on a specific tool–material combination, namely a TNMG carbide insert machining AISI 1045 steel under controlled conditions. Although the selected methodology is general, the trained models are tool- and material-specific, and extension to other tool geometries, coatings, materials, or broader operating ranges will require additional data collection and model adaptation or retraining.

Third, the proposed framework currently operates as a supervisory decision-support system. While tool-health outputs are integrated into scheduling logic and operator interfaces, autonomous machine-level closed-loop control, such as automatic tool changes or direct controller actuation, was not implemented or validated in this work.

Finally, security considerations are addressed at the architectural level through a baseline description of encrypted communication, authentication, and access control. A complete industrial cybersecurity implementation, including intrusion detection or prevention systems, formal threat modeling, and compliance validation with standards such as IEC 62,443, was not within the scope of this study and remains an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

This study proposed a cloud-native cyber-physical CNC manufacturing framework that integrates tool-health intelligence into a collaborative edge–cloud environment for supervisory decision support. The main contribution lies in the system-level integration: tool wear estimates generated by a TWCM case study are published as structured health events and connected to cloud-based scheduling and coordination logic, enabling condition-informed job allocation and tool-change recommendations across distributed manufacturing resources.

A vibration-based TWCM pipeline using variational mode decomposition and an AutoML-guided regression workflow was adopted from prior work and integrated as an example analytics service. The demonstrator results on CNC turning of AISI 1045 steel show that accurate wear estimation can be achieved and effectively incorporated into a cloud-based decision workflow. However, the validation was conducted at demonstrator scale, the TWCM model is specific to the selected tool–material combination, and system operation remains at the supervisory decision-support level without autonomous machine-level control.

Overall, the results demonstrate the feasibility of integrating tool-health monitoring into cloud-enabled CNC collaboration and provide a practical architectural foundation for future condition-aware manufacturing systems aligned with Industry 4.0.

Future work will focus on extending the framework along several directions. First, large-scale validation under industrial network conditions will be conducted, including variable latency, bandwidth contention, and heterogeneous legacy controllers. Second, the TWCM module will be extended to additional tool geometries, materials, and machining processes through adaptive or transfer-learning strategies and multi sensor data fusion. Finaly, tighter integration with scheduling and control systems will be explored, including semi-autonomous and fully autonomous decision execution under defined safety constraints.
