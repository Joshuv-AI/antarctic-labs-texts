Aging, HIV infection, and alcohol exert synergist effects on regional thalamic volumes resulting in functional impairment
Authors: Adolf Pfefferbaum, Natalie M Zahr, Stephanie A Sassoon, Rosemary Fama, Manojkumar Saranathan, Kilian M Pohl, Edith V Sullivan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Brain, Age, Alcohol use disorder, HIV, Cognition, Motor, Thalamus

Introduction

Antiretroviral treatment (ART) has afforded people living with HIV infection extended longevity. As of 2021, more than 50 % of HIV-infected individuals in the U.S. are over 50, 25 % are over 60, and the trend is growing (Control, 2024). Given that people with HIV are living into later adulthood, age-disease interactions involving brain structural volumes may be expressed, consistent with accelerated aging and functional decline including HIV-related dementia (Hyle et al., 2024). Longevity also presents opportunities for engagement in unhealthy behavior including heavy alcohol drinking (NIAAA, 2020). Numerous neuroimaging and neuropsychological studies report brain regions and cognitive and motor abilities typically compromised with HIV infection (e.g., Milanini and Valcour, 2017), but only recently have reports emerged using longitudinal paradigms to track trajectories of aging with HIV infection. The few extant controlled longitudinal studies have produced conflicting results. Specifically, a 2-year follow-up study found regional volume deficits in their HIV group relative to controls but no interaction with age (Sanford et al., 2018). Similarly, a longitudinal study of cognitive performance in cohorts of younger (<60 years at first study) and older (>60 years) people with HIV identified deficits at initial testing with age-related declines 12 years later but without age interactions (Heaton et al., 2022). By contrast, other controlled studies report HIV-age-related accelerated brain volume declines in selective regions of the parcellated frontal cortex (Pfefferbaum et al., 2014, Pfefferbaum et al., 2018) and whole thalamus (Pfefferbaum et al., 2014) in HIV participants examined upwards of 5 times over 1 to 9 years.

Several lines of evidence indicate the thalamus as a target of HIV pathology potentially accelerated by aging. Firstly, HIV-1 proviral reservoirs discovered postmortem in selective brain regions and lymphoid tissue comparison sites are hypothesized to have infiltrated the brain at initial infection and before initiation of ART (Cochrane et al., 2022). Additional postmortem study reported differential susceptibility of brain regions and body tissue to proviral reservoirs despite viral suppression and note the thalamus (Sun et al., 2023) in addition to frontal and periventricular white matter as reservoir sites (Gabuzda et al., 2023, Oliveira et al., 2023, Sun et al., 2023). Secondly, numerous cross-sectional in vivo studies using structural MRI to survey the whole brain commonly find a pattern of volume deficits that involve nodes of Papez circuit plus frontolimbic (Lew et al., 2021) and frontothalamic sites (Wade et al., 2015). Our cross-sectional study that surveyed multiple structures found a selective volume deficit in the thalamus of an HIV group that was even greater in an HIV group comorbid for alcohol use disorder (AUD) (Pfefferbaum et al., 2012). Thirdly, consideration of neuropsychological test performance in relation to regional brain volumes indicated that HIV-Associated Neurocognitive Disorder (HAND) status determined the greatest volume deficits in thalamus and hippocampus (Lew et al., 2021).

Here, we focus on the thalamus, a multi-nucleated structure that interacts with many cortical and brainstem networks subserving myriad sensory, motor, cognitive, and emotional functional domains (Aggleton et al., 2010, Antonucci et al., 2021, Brandt and Dieterich, 2019, Fama and Sullivan, 2015, Hwang et al., 2021, Schmahmann and Pandya, 1990, Sherman and Guillery, 2011). Traditionally, the thalamus has been measured as a whole because of challenges in delineating its nuclei with conventional neuroimaging protocols, thereby limiting tests of potential regional vulnerabilities to aging and disease. Using a high-resolution structural neuroimaging protocol in conjunction with a specialized thalamus atlas—THalamus Optimized Multi-Atlas Segmentation (THOMAS)–enabling regional parcellation of the thalamus (Saranathan et al., 2021), another study examined participants with HIV and others with AUD; both groups were observed to have volume deficits relative to controls in the ventral lateral posterior nuclei, which was predictive of lower CD4 nadir in the HIV group and of greater chronic back pain and poorer deep tendon ankle reflexes in the AUD group (Zahr et al., 2020).

The thalamic atlas approach was subsequently adapted for standard structural MRI in 238 healthy men and women, ages 20 to 86 years (Pfefferbaum et al., 2023a). That cross-sectional study revealed age-related volume declines in most thalamic nuclei of men and women with correction for supratentorial intracranial volume (sICV) and a selective relation between pulvinar volumes and a test of speeded eye-hand coordinated movement. A further refinement of the volumetric method (Vidal et al., 2024) examined regional and nuclear volumes cross-sectionally in HIV, AUD, and HIV comorbid for AUD and identified deficits relative to controls in most volumes, with the largest deficits in the comorbid group (Fama et al., 2024).

The current mixed cross-sectional/longitudinal analysis aimed to track and model normal aging trajectories of regional thalamic nuclei and then to test whether people living with HIV with and without AUD comorbidity show differential or accelerated aging in thalamic regions relative to the uninfected comparison group. Thus, we tested whether 1) regional thalamic volume declines would mark normal aging previously observed cross-sectionally; 2) individuals with HIV or HIV + AUD comorbidity would exhibit faster age-related thalamic volume declines than those measured in control participants; and 3) performance declines related to age and regional thalamic volumes would be steeper in the HIV groups than in controls.

Methods and materials

Participants

Data from 854 MRI sessions of 359 participants collected from 11 April 2003 to 8 March 2022 were analyzed. Supplemental Table 1 presents the demographic data of the three study groups: 216 control, 69 HIV, and 74 HIV + AUD. Supplemental Table 2 presents number of MRI sessions per participant. Cross-sectional results of some control (Pfefferbaum et al., 2023a) and HIV participants (Fama et al., 2024) were previously reported.

This work, approved by the Institutional Review Boards of SRI International and Stanford University School of Medicine, was conducted in accordance with The Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). Participants were recruited from local HIV support centers, postcard mailings, recruitment flyers, and word of mouth. After providing written informed consent for study participation, all volunteers underwent Structured Clinical Interviews for DSM-IV-TR (First, 2000) to determine diagnosis for alcohol dependence/abuse and DSM-5 diagnosis for AUD (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Of the 143 HIV participants, 74 met DSM-5 criteria for AUD and included one man with HIV who met AUD criteria between his second and third visits. Interviews included structured health questionnaires and a semi-structured timeline follow-back interview to quantify lifetime alcohol consumption (Skinner, 1982). Principal exclusion criteria at study entry were history of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and neurological disorders other than AUD. Substance dependence/abuse (current or in remission) for licit and illicit drugs was common and involved cocaine, amphetamines, opiates, cannabis, and nicotine (see Supplemental Table 9 for sample sizes). All participants also completed metal screening to ensure MRI safety. Starting each study day, participants underwent alcohol breathalyzer testing. Participants whose breathalyzer reading exceeded 0.0 were deferred for testing after refraining from drinking.

Neuroimaging acquisition and thalamic parcellation

Protocols and parameters. Scanning used ASSET for parallel and accelerated imaging on an 8-channel head coil and was conducted at SRI International on a GE 3 Tesla systems (Waukesha, WI, U.S.A.): from April 11, 2003 to August 16, 2013, 546 MRIs were acquired on a GE Signa 3 T scanner; from September 11, 2013 to March 8, 2022, 329 MRIs were acquired on a GE 750 3 T scanner; see Supplemental text for comparisons by scanner type. Standard T1-weighted (w) fast spoiled gradient echo (SPGR) images were acquired with the following parameters: axial TR/TE/TI = 6.008/1.952/300, FOV 24 cm, 1.25 mm thickness, 120 slices; or sagittal TR/TE/TI = 5.928/1.94/400, FOV 24 cm, 1.2 mm thickness, 146 slices.

MRI structural analysis. Using the longitudinal-consistent SIBIS processing pipeline (Adeli et al., 2019), T1-w and T2-w MRI data were corrected for noise (Coupe et al., 2008) and field inhomogeneity via N4ITK (Tustison et al., 2010) and aligned via CMTK (Rohlfing and Maurer, 2003). For the baseline, the SRI24 atlas (Rohlfing et al., 2010) was non-rigidly aligned to T1-w images via ANTS (Avants et al., 2008), and the SRI24 mask was used to refine inhomogeneity correction of both modalities. Next, an improved brain mask was created by majority voting (Rohlfing et al., 2004) across maps extracted by FSL BET (Smith, 2002), AFNI 3dSkullStrip (Cox, 1996), FreeSurfer mri_gcut (Sadananthan et al., 2010), and the Robust Brain Extraction method (Iglesias et al., 2011). A voxel was labelled as inside the brain if a majority of masks agreed; otherwise, the voxel was labelled as outside the brain. Brain mask and atlas alignment of follow-up scans were generated by first matching their intensity profiles with the baseline and then rigidly registering them to the baseline. Based on the aligned brain mask, the follow-up scans were inhomogeneity corrected again and skull stripped. For all time points, Atropos generated brain tissue segmentations (gray matter, white matter, and CSF) (Avants et al., 2011). The resulting probabilistic label maps of each time point were overlaid with the SRI24 atlas to define cortical and subcortical regions in each brain lobe. From these data, sICV was determined.

Thalamic parcellation. THOMAS is a multi-atlas segmentation method (Vidal et al., 2024) that produced separate unilateral estimates of total bilateral thalamic volume and 10 bilateral nuclei (20 left/right, unilateral volumes), which were then aggregated into four, functionally relevant regions: Anterior = Anterior Ventral nucleus (AV); Ventral = Ventral Lateral anterior (VLa) + Ventral Lateral posterior (VLp) + Ventral anterior (VA) + Ventral Posterior lateral (VPl); Posterior = Pulvinar (Pul) + Lateral Geniculate Nucleus (LGN) + Medial Geniculate Nucleus (MGN); and Medial = Centromedian (CM) + Mediodorsal (MD) (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

The recently proposed histogram-based polynomial synthesis (HIPS) algorithm was used that first synthesizes WMn-like images from standard T1 images prior to THOMAS segmentation (Vidal et al., 2024). HIPS-THOMAS showed significant improvement in accuracy compared to direct T1 THOMAS segmentation. This HIPS-THOMAS method was used in a cross-sectional study using standard T1-weighted images to examine regional and nuclear volume in groups with HIV, AUD, and HIV + AUD and identified deficits relative to controls in most regional and nuclear volumes with the largest deficits in the comorbid group (Fama et al., 2024).

Parcellation, whether for regions or nuclei, included about 70 % of the total thalamic volume. Tests of the study hypotheses used the four regional and total bilateral thalamic volumes; exploratory analyses used the 10 bilateral nuclear volumes. Because thalamic volume is highly correlated with brain size, all thalamic measures were expressed as brain-size-regressed data based on the thalamus versus sICV regression function determined on the control baseline measures and applied to all participants at all times. The mean size of the control volume for each measure was added to the observed minus predicted values to preserve relative anatomical size.

Neuropsychological testing

Cognitive and motor testing was typically conducted within six months of MRI acquisition (control mean ± SD = 30.1 ± 91.6 days, HIV mean ± SD = 16.0 ± 62.4 days, HIV + AUD mean ± SD = 33.1 ± 101.2 days). Scores were available for 106 control, 69 HIV, and 71 HIV + ALC participants. Mean composite scores, adjusted and standardized for age and education in a laboratory control group, were derived from tests assessing five functional domains: attention/working memory, executive functioning, verbal/visual immediate and delayed episodic memory, and motor skills. The mean score of the control group used for standardization was 0 ± 1 standard deviation for each domain and the score of the composite mean. The tests and standardization method appear in supplemental material (also see Fama et al., 2024, Zahr et al., 2020).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with R 3.5.1 (R Core Team, 2019). The primary statistic was a linear mixed effects model (lmer) enabling inclusion of multiple observations for individuals for testing target relations between each thalamic region (Anterior, Medial, Posterior, Ventral) and Total volumes and age by group (control, HIV, HIV + AUD) while allowing for model expansion to account for potentially contributory factors, such as sex. The models were conducted with linear (age) and again with quadratic (age + age2) functions, enabling determination of which model better described the data and whether there was acceleration (i.e., non-linear) aging in later years. Interaction of diagnosis-by-age or diagnosis-by-age2 allowed determination of whether the HIV groups showed different rates of decline from controls. A second set of analyses restricted age to 50 + years to quantify accelerated late life volume decline; for all four regions, the late-age volume decline was best fit as a linear process. Each model produced t and p values. Family-wise Bonferroni correction (α=0.05, 2-tailed) for 4 thalamic regions required p ≤ 0.0125 and for 10 nuclear regions required p ≤ 0.005.

The first set of regression analyses focused on relations between regional thalamic volumes and age and their functions (i.e., linear or quadratic). Follow-up analyses controlled for sex in each volume model. The second set of analyses focused on the HIV groups and entered disease-related variables in the regression models: sex, years with HIV infection, log viral load, and CD4 nadir. The third set of regressions examined relations between thalamic volumes and neuropsychological test composite scores within each group. Secondary analysis examined relations between each of 10 parcellated thalamic volumes and age and then with composite test scores by group.

Comparison of groups on demographic variables

The three groups did not differ significantly in BMI or systolic blood pressure; however, the control group was younger, had more years of education, higher socioeconomic status and global assessment of functioning, lower depressive symptom scores, and lower mean neuropsychological test composite scores than the HIV groups (Supplemental Table 1). Compared with the HIV only group, the HIV + AUD group was younger; had lower SES, GAF, and mean composite test scores; and had higher diastolic blood pressure. The HIV + AUD group had drunk more alcohol than the control or HIV groups. The only HIV-related measure that differed between the HIV groups was viral suppression, which was less prevalent in the comorbid group.

Group differences in relations between regional thalamic volumes and age

A series of lmer models testing differences across the three groups in regional thalamic volumes revealed that both the HIV and HIV + AUD groups had smaller volumes in all four regions and total thalamus than controls even when controlling for sex and age (Table 1; Supplemental Fig. 1). Comparison of volume-by-age models indicated that the quadratic fit, revealing accelerated volume loss in later years, was significantly better than the linear fit for three regions and the total; the exception was the Posterior region. Extended analyses examining the individual thalamic nuclei presented in the bottom half of Table 1 support quadratic over linear fits for 8 of the 10 nuclei.

Tests of interactions between regional volume by age or age2 across the three groups across the entire 50-year age range indicated that the age-related decline was steeper in the Anterior and Ventral regions of the HIV versus control comparison. For the comorbid versus control comparison, the age-related decline was significant only for the Anterior region. Testing these relations using age2 resulted in the same pattern of decline as with linear age (Supplemental Table 3; Fig. 1). By contrast, the analysis restricted to 50–75 years old produced linear fits showing faster volume decline with age (volume-by-age interaction) compared to controls for both HIV and HIV + AUD (t < -7.7, p < 10-12). On average, the whole thalamic volume declined about 0.60 %/year (−.61 % for controls, −.57 % for HIV, −.56 % for HIV + AUD); the Anterior nucleus declined more rapidly: −5.1 mm3 = -2.41 %/year for HIV, −5.5 mm3 = -2.84 %/year for HIV + AUD compared to −4.0 mm3 = 1.77 %/year for controls (Supplemental Table 4). Extended analyses examining the individual thalamic nuclei (bottom half of Supplemental Table 3) showed only modestly stronger interactions for two or the four ventral nuclei (Fig. 2).

Effect of HIV variables on regional thalamic volumes/age relations

The Anterior and Ventral volumes were significantly smaller in the HIV + AUD than HIV only group when controlling for age. The relations were better fit with quadratic than linear models (Supplemental Table 5). Despite the volume differences, the group-by-age or age2 interactions were not significant, indicating that the two HIV groups did not differ from each other in rates of age-related declines. Analyses examining individual thalamic nuclei (bottom half of Supplemental Table 5) reflect the age patterns observed in the regional analysis.

The greater regional Anterior and Posterior volume deficits of the HIV + AUD group relative to the HIV group endured after controlling for sex, CD4 nadir, years with HIV, viral load, and age (as a linear or quadratic function). Although the HIV versus HIV + AUD group volume differences endured (Table 2), age and sex were both significant factors in these models, where regional volumes declined with age either linearly or quadratically, and the volume deficits were greater in men than women but did not show a group-by-sex interaction (Table 2). Extended analyses examining the individual thalamic nuclei (Table 2 bottom) indicate the same pattern as observed with the regional analysis.

Contributions of age and regional thalamic volumes to performance scores

First, lmer across all observations tested relations between each neuropsychological domain score and age within each group. Most relations were in the expected direction, that is, declining with older age (Supplemental Table 6; Supplemental Fig. 2). The greatest number of significant correlations meeting Bonferroni correction for 6 comparisons (one-tailed, P ≤ 0.0167) occurred in the HIV + AUD group and were for Attention/Working Memory, Verbal/Visual Learning, Motor Skills, and Mean composite scores. These four performance/age declines were significantly steeper in the HIV + AUD than control group (Supplemental Table 6; Supplemental Fig. 2). Further, the Attention/Working Memory and Motor Skills/age decline were marginally steeper in the HIV than control group but did not meet criteria for multiple comparison correction.

Next examined were relations between thalamic regional volumes and neuropsychological test domain scores across all observations. All three groups showed several relations involving Ventral and Posterior volumes and Attention/Working Memory scores; the most consistently observed relations occurred in the HIV + AUD group (Fig. 3; Supplemental Table 7). The pattern where lower performance scores were related to smaller regional volumes was prominent throughout the relations tested and was significantly stronger in the HIV + AUD group than the control group in 16 of the 30 comparisons (Supplemental Table 7).

Discussion

Overall, the results of this mixed cross-sectional/longitudinal analysis of thalamic regions and nuclei in the two HIV and control groups supported the study hypotheses.

1) Regional thalamic volumes declined with normal aging; 2) individuals with HIV with and without AUD comorbidity exhibited faster age-related volume declines than controls in certain regions; and 3) performance declines related to age and regional thalamic volumes were steeper in HIV + AUD group than controls.

Normal aging of thalamic regions

Modeling normal aging is an essential step to determining aberrations related to disease. Our mixed cross-sectional/longitudinal design showing quadratic volume declines in Anterior and Ventral regions and nuclei may have been more sensitive to age modeling than simple cross-sectional findings, which were linear in these regions (Pfefferbaum et al., 2023a). Indeed, the earlier cross-sectional results identified quadratic declines in the three smallest nuclei (LGN, MGN, and CM), which present measurement challenges. The linear declines in Posterior and Medial regions of the thalamus in the control group were consistent with earlier observations made cross-sectionally, noted next.

A cross-sectional study by Choi et al. (Choi et al., 2022) used a version of the thalamic atlas protocol and combined data acquired at 3 T and 7 T. The whole thalamus of their healthy sample of 86 men and 112 women declined linearly in volume at a rate of 0.45 % per year. That cross-sectional rate was similar to our longitudinal rate of 0.51 % for the whole thalamus. The thalamic regional grouping of Choi and colleagues (Choi et al., 2022) showed the greatest “annualized atrophy rates” of 0.53 % to 0.62 %, whereas the Ventral and Medial groupings showed slower rates, 0.32 % to 0.37 %. Analogous linear volume declines in our control group were 0.37 % for the Ventral, 0.45 % for the Medial, 0.57 % for the Posterior, and 1.19 % for the Anterior regions, recognizing that the thalamic groupings used in each study were not identical. One region closely matched in the two studies was the anterior nucleus in rates of decline, where Choi et al. (Choi et al., 2022) reported annual declines of 1.19 % for the left and 1.08 % for the right anterior nuclei, and we observed 1.19 % decline for the combined left + right anterior nucleus. Another cross-sectional analysis using HIPS-THOMAS on MRI data from 1095 male and 1279 female healthy participants, ages 5.6 to 100 years, aggregated from multiple publicly-available data sets, reported age-related declines in all nuclei with similar age functions in both sexes (Young et al., 2024). As observed in the current longitudinal study, the pulvinar showed linear declines, whereas the anterior nucleus decline was linear cross-sectionally (Young et al., 2024) and quadratic longitudinally (herein).

Other studies have successfully used THOMAS in myriad conditions. One compared the robustness of THOMAS with other thalamic parcellation approaches in differentiating normal aging from Mild Cognitive Impairment and Alzheimer's disease (Williams et al., 2024). THOMAS was also used to target probes for deep brain stimulation (Schiff et al., 2023). Another study used THOMAS to localize and quantify thalamic volume deviations from controls, enabling identification of functional ramifications of the deviations in the mediodorsal and pulvinar nuclei in schizophrenia (Huang et al., 2024).

Potential factors contributing to HIV-accelerated, regional thalamic decline with age

In the context of the age-related declines in participants, age 50 years and older, the Anterior thalamic volume of each HIV group declined significantly faster (HIV = -2.4 %/year; HIV + AUD = -2.8 %/year) than that of the controls (−1.8 %/year). Further, the Anterior thalamic volumes had faster declines with aging in the older comorbid than the HIV only group. This difference in decline rate held for the Anterior volumes even after controlling for sex, CD4 nadir, years with HIV infection, viral load, and age. This pattern extended to the pulvinar nucleus, suggesting that the effect observed in the Posterior region was largely due to pulvinar shrinkage.

HIV-related factors examined with respect to brain structural integrity found to interact with aging in HIV infection are history of AIDS-defining events (Cohen et al., 2010, O'Connor et al., 2021, Pfefferbaum et al., 2012, Thompson et al., 2005), effectiveness of ART for viral suppression (Cooley et al., 2021), HIV-related health indices such as CD4 nadir (Pfefferbaum et al., 2012), length of infection (Bolzenius et al., 2023), biological sex in relation to HIV viral reservoirs (Oliveira et al., 2023), and substance use comorbidities, notably AUD (Pfefferbaum et al., 2012, Pfefferbaum et al., 2018, Pfefferbaum et al., 2023b). Several of these factors have also been observed to contribute to brain structural decline in longitudinal study (Pfefferbaum et al., 2014, Pfefferbaum et al., 2018, Pfefferbaum et al., 2023b), although some reports found volume deficits without accelerated aging (O'Connor et al., 2021, Sanford et al., 2018). Controlling for these variables did not override the age-HIV interactions in Anterior and Posterior regional thalamic age-related declines, suggesting that other variables were contributing to accelerated aging of these regions in people with HIV, despite viral suppression. Although highly speculative, this pattern is consistent with the possibility of a viable HIV-1 viral reservoir based in the brain and exerting an untoward effect on aging CNS tissue and potentially selective to the thalamus (Sun et al., 2023). Convergence on this speculation derives from our earlier observation of accelerated aging effects in people living with HIV further acceleration in those with AUD of periventricular white matter hyperintensity volumes (Pfefferbaum et al., 2023b), another HIV-1 viral reservoir noted postmortem (Sun et al., 2023).

Functional ramifications of thalamic aging, HIV, and AUD comorbidity

Performance on the five functional domains was examined. Considering aging, controls showed significant age-related declines only on the Attention/Working Memory domain. The HIV group showed trends toward decline on Attention/Working Memory and Motor Skills, whereas the comorbid group showed significant age-related declines in those two domains and Verbal/Visual Learning. Overall group performance levels followed a graded pattern, where relative to the controls, HIV performance showed a 0.77 SD deficit, and comorbid group performance showed a 1.12 SD deficit. The untoward effect of HIV + AUD comorbidity in compounding the HIV effect was similarly evident in the regional thalamic profile deficits, which were greatest for the Anterior and Posterior volumes. Given these contemporaneous patterns of age-related deficits, we speculate that aging, HIV infection, and AUD history, which exerted their strongest compounded effects on the Anterior and Posterior thalamic regions, consequently disturbed cognitive and motor processes reliant on the integrity of those regions and associated networks. This brain structure/function scenario is consistent with our previous cross-sectional findings describing relations between smaller whole thalamic volumes and poorer scores on verbal and visual memory tests in an HIV group with AUD comorbidity (Fama et al., 2014, Fama et al., 2024). This expanded analysis incorporating longitudinal data extends our earlier work to include age-disease interactions as enhancing or even accelerating the relations.

The average age of the HIV groups was over 50 years, a time of heightened risk for dementia. A study of aviremic HIV infected people over age 60 years compared carriers of the ApoE e4 allele, a genetic risk for Alzheimer's disease, with HIV non-carriers, and found greater brain volume deficits in the posterior corpus callosum, thalamus, and brain stem of carriers than non-carriers (Wendelken et al., 2016). Using epigenetic estimates of age as a biomarker of age acceleration, thalamic and hippocampal gray matter volumes together with cortical gray matter volumes differentiated HIV groups with versus without cognitive impairment (Lew et al., 2021).

Whether aging with HIV infection accelerates functional declines remains controversial. Indeed, age acceleration of functional decline was not supported by a longitudinal study, which followed groups of younger and older people with HIV for 12 years (Heaton et al., 2022). Despite observing cognitive and motor deficits in both groups for their ages, their respective performance levels did not diverge by age, that is, the older HIV group did not show accelerated aging decline unless accompanied by comorbid conditions.

Limitations

Despite the power of a mixed cross-sectional/longitudinal design spanning most of the adult age range, a true longitudinal paradigm initiated before disease onset is currently unavailable. Thus, we can only model differences between normal aging and disease-related aging in cohorts contemporaneously examined. Expanding this shortcoming to HIV infection, we did not examine our HIV participants before their initiating ART, a time when the brain is particularly vulnerable to HIV1 infection through breaches in the blood brain barrier (de Almeida et al., 2016, Kaul and Lipton, 2006, Valcour et al., 2011). Although examination of substance use comorbidity, prevalent in the HIV groups, did not yield robust non-alcohol drug-related effects (Supplemental Table 9), more detailed information from participants about drug use parameters may reveal their influence on thalamic volumes. Lack of baseline measurement is also relevant to cognitive and motor testing performance that would have provided a pre-infection anchor to measure disease-related effects. Genetic testing for the ApoE allele, unavailable for our cohort, may also have served to distinguish older people who exhibited accelerated declines in cognitive or thalamic volume (Wendelken et al., 2016).

Conclusions

For decades, human lesion studies and animal models have provided evidence for functionally dissociable nuclei of the thalamus (Aggleton et al., 2010, Hwang et al., 2021, Schmahmann and Pandya, 1990). Parcellation of this anatomically and functionally complex structure enabled detecting differential rates of aging local to regions and nuclei that are diagnostically and functionally relevant (cf., Choi et al., 2022, Fama et al., 2024, Pfefferbaum et al., 2023a, Saranathan et al., 2021, Williams et al., 2024, Young et al., 2024). The current results add to the growing literature on the relevance of thalamic parcellation to enable identification of the interaction of age-related regional thalamic decline now in HIV infection with exacerbation by comorbid alcohol use disorder as contributing to functional deficits.
