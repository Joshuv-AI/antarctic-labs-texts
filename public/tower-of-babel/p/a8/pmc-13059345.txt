Controversy and redefinition: morphological subphenotypes and therapeutic insights in pancreatitis-associated ARDS
Authors: Xiaolin Li, Wei Liu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor:

The study by Pensier et al.., “Morphological subphenotypes of acute pancreatitis-related acute respiratory distress syndrome [1],” presents a significant contribution to the evolving paradigm of precision medicine in critical care. By identifying two distinct CT-based morphotypes (Focal and Non-Focal ARDS) in a specific etiological cohort and demonstrating their differential response to prone positioning and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), the authors provide a compelling rationale for phenotype-guided therapy. However, the findings also unveil several profound controversies that merit vigorous discussion, challenging existing dogmas and pointing to necessary future research directions.

The most striking and potentially paradigm-shifting finding is the lack of a significant difference in 90-day mortality between Focal and Non-Focal ARDS (aHR 1.01). This stands in direct contrast to a substantial body of literature in heterogeneous ARDS populations, where the Focal phenotype is consistently associated with better outcomes. The authors appropriately hypothesize that the unique pathophysiology of severe acute pancreatitis, particularly local upper abdominal inflammation and frequent intra-abdominal hypertension, may cause a more severe form of Focal injury. This raises a critical ontological question: are we observing true morphological subphenotypes, or are these patterns merely epiphenomena of a dominant, etiology-specific pathophysiological driver? The prognosis in AP-ARDS may be governed more by the burden of pancreatic necrosis, systemic inflammatory response, or abdominal compartment syndrome than by lung morphology per se. A meta-analysis on SAP patients lists abdominal compartment syndrome as an independent correlate of ARDS, underscoring this point [2]. Therefore, the prognostic validity of a purely morphological classification may be etiology-context dependent, limiting its generalizability.

The treatment interaction effects are equally provocative. The benefit of prone positioning exclusively in Focal ARDS and of higher PEEP only in Non-Focal ARDS is clinically enticing but mechanistically and practically complex. For prone positioning in AP-ARDS, the ever-present concern of exacerbating intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) cannot be overlooked. A pertinent case report successfully used prone ventilation with meticulous abdominal offloading techniques in a patient with concurrent ARDS and ACS [3], suggesting that the feasibility and benefit of proning may depend less on lung morphology and more on specific techniques to manage IAP. This indicates a crucial gap: future studies must integrate abdominal compliance and IAP metrics into the phenotypic model. Regarding PEEP, the finding that a relatively modest “high” PEEP (~ 11 cm H2O) benefits Non-Focal ARDS appears to contradict physiological principles from other studies. Research indicates that high PEEP improves ventilation-perfusion matching only in patients with high lung recruitability, and may be harmful in patients with low recruitability, especially in the prone position [4]. The critical missing link in the present study is the measurement of lung recruitability. It is uncertain if “Non-Focal ARDS” in AP is synonymous with “high recruitability.” Without this data, implementing a fixed higher PEEP strategy based solely on morphology could inadvertently cause harm in a subset of patients.

Finally, the study’s framework relies on a static, early classification. Clinical management, however, is dynamic. Preliminary data suggests that optimal PEEP can decrease over extended prone positioning sessions, necessitating real-time adjustments [5]. This highlights a potential disconnect: the observed “treatment response” may be partly confounded by the lack of personalized, dynamic titration of ventilator settings throughout the clinical course. A morphology-based initial strategy should perhaps be viewed as a starting point for a physiology-guided, continuous optimization process, rather than a fixed prescription.

In conclusion, this important study successfully demonstrates treatment-response heterogeneity within AP-ARDS, moving the field beyond a one-size-fits-all approach. To translate this into improved outcomes, the next step must be to evolve from a unimodal (morphology) to a multimodal stratification system. This system should integrate etiology-specific factors (IAP, inflammatory biomarkers), dynamic physiological data (recruitability, transpulmonary pressure), and the morphological phenotype [6]. Prospective trials testing such an integrated precision strategy are the necessary and logical sequel to this foundational work.
