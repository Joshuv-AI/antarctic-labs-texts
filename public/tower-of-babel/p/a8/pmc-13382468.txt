Paracentral acute middle maculopathy following physical exertion: A case report
Authors: Nicolas Owlya, Tim Beltraminelli, Andrea Montesel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo report a case of paracentral acute middle maculopathy (PAMM) after physical exertion and to explore possible underlying microvascular mechanisms.ObservationsA 42-year-old Caucasian man presented with a persistent, unilateral, painless paracentral scotoma that appeared after an intense ski session seven days earlier. His medical history included an 11-year course of migraine with aura and recurrent transient scotomas in either eye, lasting from minutes to days, often triggered by fatigue, exertion, or altitude. At presentation, best-corrected visual acuity was 1.6 Snellen equivalent bilaterally (20/12), with unremarkable slit lamp and fundoscopy examination. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed a focal hyperreflective band in the inner nuclear layer of the right eye, consistent with PAMM. Fundus autofluorescence revealed a hyperautofluorescent spot in the same area. Microperimetry confirmed a localized scotoma in the corresponding area. At follow-up, clinical examination before and after moderate exertion (90 minutes of cycling) showed no new scotomas or clinical abnormalities. However, en face OCT angiography (OCTA) of the deep capillary plexus revealed multifocal variations in capillary density, with some areas demonstrating reduced perfusion after exercise.Conclusions and importancePAMM is an OCT finding of focal retinal ischemia associated with various vascular disorders. Migraine has also been reported as a risk factor. In this case, physical exertion may have contributed to transient hemodynamic changes, potentially unmasking an underlying retinal microvascular vulnerability. The multifocal post-exercise decrease in perfusion on OCTA despite stable symptoms emphasizes the dynamic, subclinical nature of microvascular injury.Multimodal imaging combining structural and functional assessment is critical for the early detection of ischemic retinal events, even in patients without classic vascular risk factors.

Introduction

Paracentral acute middle maculopathy (PAMM) is an optical coherence tomography (OCT) finding characterized by hyperreflectivity within the inner nuclear layer (INL), reflecting ischemia of the retinal deep capillary plexus (DCP).1 It has been associated with a broad spectrum of retinal and systemic vascular disorders, including retinal artery and vein occlusions, diabetic retinopathy, hypertensive retinopathy, Purtscher-like retinopathy, and systemic hypoperfusion states.2 The condition is thought to arise from transient or localized ischemia within the metabolically demanding middle retinal layers, which depend on the DCP for oxygenation and are particularly sensitive to fluctuations in perfusion pressure.3

Migraine with aura affects approximately 15% of migraine patients and has been linked to systemic and retinal vascular dysregulation.4 Although transient visual disturbances are frequent during migraine attacks, accumulating imaging evidence suggests that migraineurs may exhibit subclinical alterations in retinal and choroidal perfusion.5 However, structural ischemic lesions such as PAMM remain uncommon, with only a few cases reported6, 7, 8, 9.

During physical exercise, systemic hemodynamic shifts and autoregulatory adjustments may transiently alter ocular blood flow10, 11, 12, 13. In predisposed individuals with impaired vascular reactivity, these physiological adjustments may exacerbate local perfusion deficits.14 Exercise-induced PAMM, however, remains exceedingly rare, with only one case previously reported.15

Here, we report a case of PAMM occurring shortly after exercise in a patient with a history of migraine with aura. This case illustrates how the combination of migraine-related endothelial vulnerability and exertion-induced hemodynamic stress can converge to provoke focal ischemia in the deep retinal circulation, supporting the concept of a “two-hit” pathophysiologic model.

Case rReport

A 42-year-old Caucasian man presented with a persistent painless paracentral scotoma in his right eye, that had developed shortly after an intense skiing session seven days earlier. He had an 11-year history of migraine with aura. The first episode prompted evaluation with cerebral magnetic resonance imaging, cardiac assessment including electrocardiogram and echocardiography, and routine blood tests, all of which were unremarkable. Since then, he had experienced recurrent transient paracentral scotomas affecting either eye, described as small black spots “like dead pixels” in his visual field. These episodes varied in duration from a few minutes to several days and were often triggered by fatigue, physical exertion or altitude changes, with most resolving spontaneously.

Nine years prior to the current presentation, he was evaluated for an inferior scintillating scotoma in the context of fatigue. Slit-lamp microscopy, dilated fundoscopy, color vision assessment, and pupillary examination were all unremarkable, as were OCT, blue fundus autofluorescence (FAF), fluorescein angiography (FA), and 30° computerized visual field testing. Two years later, he reported the onset of an additional transient scotoma, with persistence of the previously noted defect. A comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation at that time again demonstrated no abnormal findings.

Clinical review revealed no cardiovascular risk factors other than a family history of myocardial infarction (both grandfathers, one deceased in his 50s) and maternal hypertension. The patient was otherwise healthy, engaged in regular physical activity, and reported moderate caffeine intake (approximately two cups daily, range 0-3). He had no history of clotting disorders, Raynaud phenomenon, or hematologic disease, intake of systemic medications or vasoactive supplements, and denied recreational drug use. The patient lived at approximately 650 m above sea level; the skiing activity took place in Switzerland where slopes commonly range between 2500 and 3900 m.

At the current presentation, best-corrected visual acuity was 1.6 Snellen equivalent (20/12) in both eyes. Slit-lamp and dilated fundus examinations were normal. On the Amsler grid, the patient delineated two scotomas in the right eye: one temporal to fixation along the horizontal meridian and another inferiorly on the central vertical axis (Fig. 1). As part of the diagnostic workup following this episode, repeat cardiologic evaluation identified a small patent foramen ovale (PFO), not detected on initial assessment 11 years earlier. The PFO was not hemodynamically significant, demonstrating permeability only after Valsalva maneuver with no spontaneous shunt. Subsequent cerebral magnetic resonance angiography showed no abnormalities or embolic sequelae. The PFO was considered an incidental finding unrelated to the retinal presentation, and no closure was recommended.

At 3-month follow-up, SD-OCT demonstrated evolution of the PAMM lesion into a retinal ischemic perivascular lesion (RIPL), while the previously noted interdigitation zone defect had completely resolved (Fig. 2). At this visit, multimodal imaging was performed before and immediately after moderate physical exertion (90 minutes of cycling). The patient reported no new symptoms, and clinical examination remained unchanged. At rest, en face structural OCT of the deep capillary plexus (DCP) showed a hyporeflective focus corresponding to the RIPL, together with additional hyporeflective areas that co-localized with regions of reduced capillary density on OCT angiography (OCTA). Following exercise, multifocal variations in DCP perfusion were observed: some areas demonstrated increased attenuation, while others showed relatively increased perfusion signal (Fig. 5). OCTA image quality was high in both examinations, with signal strengths of 40 dB (pre-exercise) and 35 dB (post-exercise). All scans were acquired using the same device and identical settings (20° × 20° scan, 512 B-scans, High Speed mode), and segmentation required no manual adjustment. Post-exercise OCTA was performed approximately 5 minutes after completion of exertion.

Review of prior OCT scans did not demonstrate any PAMM or RIPL corresponding to the inferior scotoma. No additional areas of INL thinning were identified elsewhere in the macula of either eye on the scans at baseline and at 3-month follow-up.

Discussion

PAMM represents an OCT finding characterized by a hyperreflective band at the outer plexiform layer (OPL) and INL junction, reflecting ischemia within the retinal capillary plexuses.1 Over time, PAMM lesions may resolve or evolve into chronic structural changes such as RIPL, characterized by thinning of the INL associated with OPL elevation.16 This sequence, captured through longitudinal imaging in the present case, highlights the transition from acute ischemic injury to chronic retinal remodeling characteristic of PAMM.

The middle retinal layers are particularly vulnerable to ischemia due to their high metabolic demand and location within a watershed zone between retinal and choroidal circulations. Indeed, perfusion of this retinal layer relies on the DCP, which lacks a direct arterial supply and depends on terminal arterioles from the intermediate capillary plexus.17 This indirect and limited flow, combined with the intense oxygen consumption due to horizontal cells activity, makes the middle retina highly susceptible to transient hypoperfusion and ischemic manifestations such as PAMM.2

Migraine, particularly migraine with aura, has been increasingly recognized as a systemic microvascular disorder affecting both the retinal and choroidal circulations.4 Recurrent vasospastic episodes are thought to induce transient hypoperfusion, which over time may lead to endothelial dysfunction and impaired neurovascular coupling.18 Evidence from recent meta-analyses of OCT and OCTA studies demonstrates reduced vessel density in the superficial and deep capillary plexus, enlargement of the foveal avascular zone, thinning of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer, and decreased subfoveal choroidal thickness, even in patients without visual symptoms.4,5 Collectively, these findings support widespread microvascular compromise in migraine, which may lower the ischemic threshold of retinal and choroidal tissues and predispose affected individuals to events such as PAMM.

A small number of cases reports have described PAMM occurring in migraine patients. Most cases highlight a temporal relationship between migraine attacks and the onset of paracentral scotomas, supporting a vasospastic mechanism6, 7, 8, 9. However, PAMM lesions have also been reported independent of acute migraine episodes, suggesting an intrinsic susceptibility of the retinal microcirculation in migraineurs.19 Limitations of the existing literature include small sample sizes and the absence of longitudinal vascular assessments, which restrict the ability to fully characterize the natural history of microvascular compromise in migraine. In our case, PAMM developed outside a typical migraine attack, suggesting that exercise-induced hemodynamic stress may have acted as a secondary trigger in an already vulnerable vascular environment.

Moreover, physiological studies have shown that physical exercise generally enhances retinal blood flow, tissue perfusion, and capillary function.20 Conversely, several studies have also demonstrated a transient decrease in retinal vessel density during exercise, even in healthy individuals10, 11, 12, 13. This phenomenon has been attributed to a redistribution of systemic blood flow toward active muscles, exercise-induced vasoconstriction to regulate increased flow velocity, or autoregulatory adjustments aimed at preventing overflow. In predisposed eyes with impaired neurovascular coupling or defective vascular autoregulation, this normal balance of retinal blood flow regulation may be disrupted.14 However, generalization of these findings remains challenging due to heterogeneity in exercise protocols (type, duration, and intensity), OCTA devices, perfusion metrics, and timing of post-exercise image acquisition across studies.

Exercise-related PAMM is an exceptionally rare entity in the literature. To date, only one case has described the onset of bilateral scotomas occurring minutes after a high-intensity interval training session, with subsequent OCT findings consistent with PAMM.15 A subsequent letter to the editor, however, questioned the causality of this association, noting several limitations - including the absence of prior ophthalmic evaluations, incomplete characterization of the exercise regimen, and potential confounding vascular risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, or migraine.21 In contrast, the present case provides novel longitudinal evidence linking physical exertion to retinal hypoperfusion in a patient with a predisposing factor of endothelial vulnerability (i.e. migraine with aura). The initial episode of PAMM occurred shortly after physical effort, indicating an exertion-triggered ischemic insult. Furthermore, en face OCTA imaging performed after moderate exercise revealed multifocal variations in capillary density within the DCP compared to pre-exercise imaging. While some areas demonstrated increased attenuation of the perfusion signal, others showed relatively increased perfusion. This heterogeneous response may reflect patchy retinal autoregulation in a migraineur, where hemodynamic stress unmasks regional differences in microvascular reactivity. Areas of increased perfusion after exercise included the RIPL region and a superior macular area without structural abnormalities on B-scans, potentially corresponding to the scotoma reported 9 years prior; these may represent sequelae of prior ischemic events with partially normalized flow. Conversely, areas with reduced perfusion showed no structural damage on B-scans and may indicate zones of latent microvascular vulnerability apparent only under increased metabolic demand. These findings support the hypothesis that physical activity can alter retinal microvascular perfusion in predisposed individuals, unmasking a latent susceptibility to ischemia.

The case presented here supports a multifactorial model in which migraine-related microvascular fragility and exercise-induced hemodynamic stress act as distinct yet converging triggers for retinal ischemia. According to the “two-hit” hypothesis, migraine-induced endothelial dysfunction represents the first hit, while exercise-related blood flow redistribution serves as the second, jointly precipitating ischemic injury within the DCP. Alternatively, exercise may act as a migraine trigger, suggesting a dependent rather than additive mechanism. Previous studies have reported that up to one-fifth of migraineurs experience exercise as a precipitating factor, although confounding factors such as dehydration, heat exposure, fatigue, sleep deprivation, or hypoglycemia may challenge the notion of a direct causal relationship.13,22 In this case, altitude-related hypoxia may represent an additional contributing factor. Hypoxia is known to trigger headache syndromes and has been associated with migraine, with experimental evidence suggesting it may precipitate attacks.23 It is also implicated in high-altitude retinopathy, a condition typically occurring at higher elevations and driven by hypoxia-induced vasodilation, leading to retinal overperfusion and capillary leakage24 - mechanisms distinct from the ischemic changes seen in PAMM. Given that the patient had experienced similar scotomas at lower altitude, hypoxia is unlikely to be the primary driver, although it may have contributed by reducing oxygen delivery in a retina with impaired vascular regulation.25

An additional element to consider is the detection of a small PFO. A well-recognized epidemiological association exists between PFO and migraine with aura, with prevalence rates of approximately 40–50% in this population compared with about 25% in the general population,26,27 and paradoxical embolism has occasionally been implicated in retinal arteriolar occlusions and isolated PAMM lesions in young patients.28,29 However, several elements argue against a primary causative role. The shunt was not hemodynamically significant and had escaped detection 11 years earlier. Cerebral magnetic resonance angiography showed no embolic sequelae. Symptoms were consistently triggered by exertion, fatigue, and altitude rather than Valsalva-type events. The multifocal, bidirectional DCP perfusion variations on OCTA are inconsistent with discrete microembolic events, which would produce focal, territorial defects rather than patchy, dynamic changes. The PFO is therefore interpreted as an incidental finding.

The interdigitation zone alteration may be partly attributable to shadowing from the overlying PAMM lesion. However, OCT B-scan demonstrates signal attenuation extending beyond the lesion margins along with focal photoreceptor thinning - findings that argue against a pure shadowing artifact. Furthermore, the focal hyperautofluorescence on FAF is inconsistent with simple masking, which typically manifests as hypoautofluorescence. Together, these findings raise the possibility of limited outer retinal involvement, a feature associated with acute macular neuroretinopathy (AMN). Recent evidence by Iovino et al.30 supports the concept of a pathophysiologic continuum between PAMM and AMN, in which ischemia within the DCP may extend toward the outer retina through Müller cell dysfunction. These glial cells, which provide structural and metabolic support to both the DCP and photoreceptor axons within the Henle fiber layer, appear particularly vulnerable to hypoxic stress.

While the clinical history with longitudinal follow-up and the supporting comprehensive multimodal imaging represent the strengths of this case report, limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of quantitative vascular density metrics and the lack of direct ocular blood flow or perfusion measurements prevent confirmation of true hypoperfusion. Additionally, inherent variability in OCTA acquisition - including signal strength, segmentation accuracy, and motion artifacts - may contribute to apparent changes in capillary density between examinations. The absence of repeated measurements and a control group further limits the interpretation of these findings. Indeed, normative OCTA data characterizing DCP responses to physical exertion in healthy individuals are lacking, limiting the ability to distinguish pathologic from physiologic vascular responses in this case. The qualitative DCP response to exercise observed here should thus be considered an exploratory analysis. Additional factors such as systemic blood pressure fluctuations, hydration status, caffeine intake, or altitude exposure may influence retinal perfusion and cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, the proposed pathophysiologic interpretations remain speculative and inferred from a single case. Larger prospective studies with pre- and post-exertion multimodal imaging are warranted to test the proposed two-hit hypothesis.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the interaction between migraine-related microvascular vulnerability and exercise-induced hemodynamic stress as potential triggers for paracentral acute middle maculopathy. The findings are consistent with a “two-hit” model linking systemic and local vascular factors. Longitudinal OCTA monitoring proved crucial for revealing subclinical capillary changes, underscoring the importance of multimodal imaging in detecting early or transient retinal ischemia in at-risk individuals.
