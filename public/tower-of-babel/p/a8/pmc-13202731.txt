Spatial transcriptomics from pancreas and local draining lymph node tissue reveals a lymphotoxin-β signature in human type 1 diabetes
Authors: Miguel A Medina-Serpas, Maigan Brusko, Gregory J Golden, Martha Campbell-Thompson, Trevor Rogers, Shay Reardon, Amanda L Posgai, Rhonda Bacher, Eline T Luning Prak, Chengyang Liu, Klaus H Kaestner, Ali Naji, Michael R Betts, Lauren M McIntyre, Mark A Atkinson, Todd M Brusko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThis study explores the inflammatory response observed in the pancreas and pancreatic lymph nodes (pLNs) during the natural history of type 1 diabetes (T1D). Using multicell-resolution spatial transcriptomics (ST), we profile individuals without diabetes (ND), at-risk autoantibody-positive (AAb+) individuals, and T1D donors. In the T1D pancreas, we observed global upregulation of inflammation-associated transcripts, including REG family genes, C3, SOD2, and OLFM4. In the T1D pLN, LTB was significantly upregulated within the lymphoid follicles. Using an orthogonal subcellular-resolution ST platform on an independent donor set, we identified follicular B cells as the primary source of LTB in the pLN and observed increased LTB expression in lymphocytes in insulitic lesions proximal to CCL19/CCL21-expressing endothelium. Collectively, these findings highlight lymphotoxin-β and downstream chemokine signatures in the pancreatic lymphatics as well as within the insulitic lesion, which can inform future therapeutic interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Recent analyses of the human pancreas and pancreatic lymph nodes (pLNs) acquired from type 1 diabetic organ donors have addressed a series of major knowledge voids regarding the cellular frequencies, phenotypes, and autoreactive lymphocyte receptor repertoire signatures associated with type 1 diabetes (T1D) pathogenesis.1–5 More specifically, consortium-based efforts, including the Network of Pancreatic Organ donors with Diabetes (nPOD)6 and the Human Pancreas Analysis Program (HPAP),7 have allowed for vital and unique access to these target tissues from organ donors with T1D, individuals without diabetes, and those at elevated risk for developing the disease (i.e., those with detectable islet autoantibodies). Such efforts have dramatically improved our ability to address a series of questions regarding the tissue-specific autoimmunity that occurs during the natural history of T1D.8 However, there remains a critical need to identify therapeutically targetable pathways in individuals with failed peripheral tolerance to enable future disease prevention efforts.

High-throughput single-cell profiling represents a powerful method for assessing cellular heterogeneity within tissues by identifying cell-type-specific molecular profiles suspected to contribute to the histopathological events observed in T1D or other disorders.3,9–11 Despite their utility, one notable limitation of these techniques involves the potential introduction of confounders inherent to the generation of single-cell suspensions, including destruction of the tissue architecture, loss of stromal and rare cell types, and possible alterations in cellular phenotypes.12–14 Sequencing-based spatial transcriptomic (ST) techniques afford the ability to address these challenges by allowing for gene expression quantitation in whole-tissue sections at variable scales (i.e., multicell vs. single cell vs. subcellular) and gene detection sensitivities (unbiased whole transcriptome vs. a pre-defined gene panel).15 Furthermore, ST methods utilizing transcript-specific probe sets for gene detection exhibit increased sensitivity by overcoming stochastic dropout observed in single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq)-based approaches.16 These techniques are also compatible with analysis of fixed tissues that inherently preserve the underlying tissue microenvironment, enabling simultaneous consideration of individual cellular positions, cellular neighborhoods, cell-cell contacts, and gene expression.17

Herein, we performed multicell-resolution ST profiling on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) pancreata and donor-matched pLN sections corresponding to the same pancreatic anatomical region (i.e., head, body, or tail) acquired from human organ donors in the HPAP collection.7 This cross-sectional cohort represents donors across the natural clinical history of T1D, including islet-autoantibody-negative (AAb−) controls with no diabetes (ND), single-islet-AAb+ with ND (sAAb+), multiple (≥2)-islet-AAb+ with ND (mAAb+), and T1D donors. Furthermore, we leveraged annotated pancreas18 and pLN3 scRNA-seq datasets for integration and cell deconvolution, enabling the assessment of relative changes in cellular frequencies across both tissues. From this analysis, we identified significant immune cell enrichment in the pancreas of at-risk donors and alterations in immune cell subset enrichment in the pLN across T1D disease states. The data reported herein provide evidence for dysregulated gene profiles in distinct pancreatic regions as well as innate and adaptive inflammatory signatures in the pLN, along with candidate biomarkers to guide future interventional therapies for T1D.

RESULTS

Interrogation of matched pancreas and pLN by ST

To gain insights into the tissue-specific inflammatory events resident in the human pancreas and pLN across the natural history of T1D, we performed ST profiling at 55 μM capture-spot resolution on FFPE pancreatic tissue sections from 20 human organ donors, including ND control donors (n = 5), sAAb+ donors without diabetes (n = 3), mAAb+ donors without diabetes (n = 4), and donors with a clinical history of T1D (n = 8; mean disease duration 4.19 ± 2.85 years). For these studies, pLN sections were acquired from the same pancreatic donors when available (16/20). Per-donor tissue availability, islet-AAb status, and T1D-associated HLA genetic risk data are summarized in Figure 1A, with detailed donor characteristics provided in Table S1.

For each tissue, capture spots were integrated into a latent space via anchor-based canonical correlation analysis (CCA),19 yielding two separate pancreas and pLN datasets containing 113,356 capture spots for pancreas and 25,584 total capture spots for pLN. To address inter-donor variability of unnormalized count distributions following integration (Figures S1A and S1B), we applied an upper quartile normalization strategy20 across each dataset (Figure 1B). We quantitatively (Figures S1C and S1D) and qualitatively (Figures S1E and S1F) assessed the integration quality and observed comparable mean embedding values across donors and disease groups.

We next annotated capture spots by their tissue compartment localization for each respective tissue using histopathological features (Tables S2 and S3) identified in the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections prepared during spatial expression profiling (Data S1). Expression of canonical marker genes corresponding to known cell types also aided in capture-spot annotation (Figures 1C, S1E, and S1F). At 55 μm diameter, capture spots reflect expression profiles derived from multiple cells and/or cell types. We thus applied in silico cell deconvolution21 to calculate the predicted proportion of constituent cell types within capture spots using single-cell gene expression reference data3,22 (isolated pancreatic islets—scRNA-seq [https://hpap.pmacs.upenn.edu18]; pLN—CITE-seq [cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing; GEO: GSE221787]3) (Figures 1D, S1G, and S1H). Donor overlap between our ST cohort and the respective pancreas and pLN single-cell reference is outlined in Table S1. ST images and associated metadata are accessible as fully interactive visualizations at the UFDI Spatial Data Portal (https://ufdi.rc.ufl.edu/).

Differentially expressed genes across defined compartments of the pancreas

Marker gene analysis of annotated pancreas compartments identified enrichment of canonical endocrine (GCG, α cell; INS, β cell; and SST, δ cell), exocrine (CELA2A, CELA3B, and PRRS3), and stromal (FN1, ACTA2, and MYH11) genes within the expected annotated compartments (Figure 2A). Corresponding whole-slide scanned H&E-stained pancreas sections (Data S1) were examined to ensure that the tissue gene expression signature matched the underlying histology (Figure 2B; Data S1; UFDI Spatial Data Portal, https://ufdi.rc.ufl.edu/). We then performed within-compartment differential expression testing between AAb+ (sAAb+ and mAAb+ considered together for increased statistical power), T1D, and ND individuals (Tables S4, S5, and S6). Twenty-eight differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed evidence of compartment-specific expression across all comparisons, with the majority restricted to the endocrine compartment (17/28, 61%) (Figures 2C and 2D). Similarly, the endocrine compartment accounted for the greatest number of DEGs overall (n = 60), followed by the exocrine (n = 50) and stromal compartments (n = 28) (Figure 2D).

Endocrine-compartment-specific DEGs indicate reduced β cell function, increased inflammation, and tissue injury in AAb+ and T1D pancreas

Within the endocrine compartment, we observed downregulation of INS, GCG, TTR, CUZD1, and SERPINA3 among AAb+ donors relative to ND (Figures 2C and S2A). Reduced INS expression may indicate declining β cell mass or function prior to T1D onset, which is consistent with prior reports.23,24 Similarly, reduced GCG expression may also point toward α cell dysfunction, which has been reported in individuals with a single GAD AAb, where it was linked to altered expression of oxidative phosphorylation genes.25 TTR (transthyretin) encodes a transport protein highly expressed in α cells but that also functions in insulin release and protection of β cells from apoptosis.26 CUZD1 (CUB and zona pellucida-like domain-containing protein 1) is reported as reduced in pancreatitis and is an autoantibody target in Crohn’s disease.27 SERPINA3 (α-1-antichymotrypsin) is an acute-phase protein with purported roles in diabetic nephropathy and heart disease.28,29 Collectively, these gene expression differences point toward aberrations in β cell function and, potentially, inflammation within endocrine areas in AAb+ individuals.

The endocrine compartment of T1D subjects exhibited reduced expression of β-cell-enriched transcripts INS, IAPP (islet amyloid polypeptide), and G6PC2 (glucose-6-phosphatase catalytic subunit 2), as expected relative to ND controls. PPY (pancreatic polypeptide) expression was also increased in T1D, possibly due to a minor imbalance of pancreatic head region sections (Table S1), as cells producing this hormone are found primarily in the ventral lobe of the pancreatic head.30,31 Although direct detection of HLA transcripts was not possible with the Visium CytAssist assay due to their allelic hypervariability, we observed upregulation of the class I HLA subunit B2M (β2 microglobulin) in T1D subjects relative to ND (Figures 2C and S2B), as reported by others.32–34 We also observed upregulation of the cell adhesion and migration gene CD44 in the T1D endocrine compartment relative to AAb+ donors (Figures 2C and S2C), potentially indicative of tissue injury.35

Exocrine compartment changes support chronic pancreatic inflammation in T1D

Though no significant exocrine-compartment-specific differences were observed in AAb+ vs. ND (Figures 2C and S2D), we detected upregulation of MMP7 (matrix metalloproteinase 7), HSBP1 (heat shock factor binding protein 1), and REG3G (regenerating islet-derived protein 3γ) in T1D sections relative to ND controls (Figures 2C and S2E). These genes have been implicated in models of acute pancreatitis: MMP736 and REG3G37 are thought to contribute to inflammation, whereas HSBP138 has been shown to have protective qualities. Furthermore, we also detected upregulation of TACSTD2, DEPP1, CEBPD, IL32, and B2M in the T1D exocrine compartment when compared with AAb+ donors (Figures 2C and S2F). Exocrine-specific upregulation of B2M is supported by immunohistochemical staining, which revealed expression of class I HLA protein on the surface of acinar cells in recent-onset T1D cases but not in non-diabetic controls.39 Last, upregulation of TACSTD2,40 DEPP1,41 CEBPD,42 and IL3243 in the exocrine pancreas has been associated with pancreatic malignancies; however, in this context, they may suggest a chronic inflammatory environment in the T1D pancreas.

DEGs shared across compartments indicate a global inflammatory signature in T1D pancreas

Among T1D sections, we observed global upregulation of additional REG gene family members (REG1B and REG3A) as well as MT1X (metallothionein 1X), ALDOB (aldolase B), SERPINB1 (leukocyte elastase inhibitor), OLFM4 (olfactomedin 4), C3 (complement component 3), and SOD2 (superoxide dismutase 2) (Figure 2E). Relative to ND donors, REG genes were significantly downregulated in AAb+ donors yet upregulated in T1D, with consistent patterns observed across individuals (Figures 2E and S2A–S2I). This would suggest that dynamic regulation of REG expression occurs across disease groups in response to inflammation. MT1X, which was upregulated in T1D vs. AAb+ and ND across all tissue compartments, excluding the stroma (T1D vs. ND) (Figures S2B, S2C, S2E, S2F, S2H, and S2I), is a negative regulator of insulin secretion, primarily expressed by β cells and associated with β cell failure in type 2 diabetes (T2D) islets.44 Similarly, ALDOB was elevated in T1D endocrine and exocrine compartments compared with ND and AAb+ donor groups (Figures S2B, S2C, S2E, and S2F), and its induction in β cells negatively correlates with insulin secretion.45 Together, these changes likely reflect stress in residual β cells after T1D onset.

Functionally, SERPINB1, OLFM4, C3, and SOD2 participate in both innate and adaptive immunity.46–48 To identify the immune cell types potentially contributing to this pancreatic signature, we assessed their expression in the pLN CITE-seq reference dataset (GEO: GSE221787).3 Interestingly, we observed SOD2 and C3 expression enriched only among myeloid subsets (Figure S2J), whereas SERPINB1 was expressed in both B cells and myeloid subsets (Figure S2J). OLFM4 was largely restricted to lymphoid lineage cells; however, CD8+ T cell subsets exhibited low level expression. Collectively, these data indicate that pancreatic innate immune activity is heightened and sustained in T1D, even in the absence of observable insulitis by H&E analysis (Data S1 and Table S3).

Immune enrichment in mAAb+ pancreas prior to T1D onset

Next, in order to characterize inflamed regions of the pancreas at the cellular level, we used an independently prepared scRNA-seq reference prepared from isolated human pancreatic islets18 to deconvolute immune-enriched capture spots. Prior to cell deconvolution, we removed all cells acquired from T2D subjects in the scRNA-seq reference. The reference includes annotations for major endocrine (α, β, and δ cells) and exocrine (acinar and ductal) cell types, with immune cells represented as a singular heterogeneous population. Spatial mapping revealed localization of acinar, endocrine, ductal, and epithelial cell types consistent with the compartmental annotation (Figures S3A and S3B). T1D samples exhibited minimal immune cell enrichment in their endocrine compartment, consistent with their histopathological examination of paired H&E images (Data S1). Instead, immune signatures were enriched on average in the stroma (Figure S3A). Accordingly, localized mononuclear cell infiltration was observed along interlobular and fascial connective tissues in donors classified as chronically inflamed by histopathological assessment (Table S3). As confirmation, we spatially mapped immune-enriched capture spots across the AAb+ (HPAP016) and T1D (HPAP123) samples with the highest chronic inflammation score (Table S3). Immune-enriched regions were identified in both samples, and histopathology confirmed colocalization of mononuclear cells within these areas (Figure 3A). We next compared the overall immune enrichment across disease groups, and after excluding capture spots with no predicted immune presence, the mAAb+ samples in this cohort were significantly more immune enriched relative to all other disease groups (Figure 3B).

Next, we measured the expression of canonical immune-cell-type marker genes within immune-cell-containing capture spots to determine their cellular composition and compared across donor groups. We directed our focus on positions considered immune enriched. This was defined as capture spots possessing an immune proportion ≥2 standard deviations (σ) above the ND mean, which is representative of heightened immune activity. We then visualized the expression levels of known marker genes expressed across a variety of immune cell types (PTPRC and CD74) and those specific or enriched in T cells (CD3E, CD2, TRAC, CD4, and CD8A), B cells (MS4A1, CR2, CD79A, and PAX5), natural killer (NK) cells (NCAM1, CD160, CD244, and KLRC3), monocytes/macrophages (MARCO, CD14, and CD68), classical dendritic cells (DCs; ZBTB46, XCR1, and MRC1), and plasma cells (JCHAIN, IGHA1, and IGHG3) across donor groups within immune-enriched capture spots (Figures 3C and S3C).

Consistent with the overall greater predicted immune enrichment in mAAb+ donors (Figure 3B), the highest relative expression levels of all measured immune marker genes were detected in this group (Figures 3C and S3C). Except for the enrichment of some NK cell marker genes, the immune composition of sAAb+ donors was comparable to that of ND. T1D donors were enriched for B-cell-associated transcripts rather than T cell genes.

Finally, to assess the expression of inflammatory genes previously implicated in the pathogenesis of T1D,32,49–51 expression of interferon (IFNG, IFNA1, IFNA2, IFNAR1, and IFNGR1), tumor necrosis family (TNF, LTA, LTB, TNFRSF1A, TNFRSF1B, and LTBR), and antigen presentation genes (TAP1, TAP2, TABP, ERAP1, ERAP2, PDIA3, B2M, CIITA, and CD74) was compared across disease states. We also included additional genes directly involved in regulating class II HLA expression (NFYA and RFX1)52,53 as surrogates. Mirroring our assessment of immune markers, the expression of nearly all inflammation-associated genes was most elevated in mAAb+ donors (Figures 3D and S3D). Interestingly, we also detected upregulation of LTA, IFNG, IFNA1, and IFNA2 in sAAb+ donors (Figure 3D and S3D). Collectively, these results indicate disease-associated inflammation in the target organ prior to T1D onset and highlight the heterogeneous cellular contribution to the immune signature among mAAb+ donors.

Transcriptional characterization of major histological compartments of pLN

Next, we characterized the transcriptional landscape of the draining pLN by first defining gene expression signatures corresponding to five distinct regions based on tissue architecture identified in H&E images (Data S1): the medulla (CLEC4G, MARCO, and STAB2), stroma (DES, MYH11, and CNN1), lymphoid follicles (CXCL13, PAX5, and CD19), T cell zone (TCZ) (CD3G, CD3E, and TCF7), and a region of mixed cell types (FOXD4L5 and PEX11A) (Figure 4A). The spatial gene expression pattern of regional marker genes supported these annotations (Figure 4B), with the exception of the mixed cell region, which had lower overall gene counts (Figure 4C). Last, we examined the capture spot frequency across each annotated region at the individual donor level and across each disease group to assess for regional imbalances (Figures 4D and 4E). Variations in capture-spot numbers and regional coverage reflect differences in tissue specimen size and orientation at embedding and sectioning. Aside from the mixed region (p = 0.016), all compartments showed comparable representation across disease groups. The mixed region was not consistently represented, further supporting its exclusion from downstream analysis (Tables S2 and S3).

Spatial mapping of immune cell heterogeneity in pLN at distinct stages of T1D

As in the pancreas, we performed cell deconvolution of capture spots across all pLN regions utilizing an existing annotated CITE-seq reference (GEO: GSE221787) generated from 34 human pLNs isolated at distinct stages of T1D from HPAP.3 This reference contains 30 distinct immune subsets represented across five major cell types (CD4+ T cell, CD8+ T cell, B cell, NK cells/innate lymphoid cells [ILCs], myeloid cells) (Figure S1G). The TCZ and lymphoid follicles were respectively predicted to be highly enriched for T and B cells, while the medulla was particularly enriched for myeloid antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in addition to NK cells and ILCs (Figure 5A). Spatial cell-type mapping of B cell and CD4+ T cell proportions displayed the expected spatial organization characteristic of both the lymphoid follicles and the TCZ54 (representative sections, Figure 5B; Data S1).

Next, we examined the relative predicted immune cell composition of each pLN capture spot at the expanded 30 immune subset resolution for individual donors and across donor groups (Figure S4A). To assess overall enrichment patterns across the natural history of T1D progression, cell subset enrichment scores for all capture spots were scaled, compared across disease status, and organized into four distinct modules of peak relative enrichment (modules: ND enriched, AAb+ enriched, T1D enriched, and mAAb+/T1D enriched) (Figures 5C–5E and S4B). Among ND donors, we observed peak enrichment of naive B cell subsets, myeloid cells, and NK cells relative to all other groups (Figure 5C). Interestingly, both naive and memory/effector T cell subsets were predicted to be most enriched in AAb+ donors (Figure 5D), with naive T cell subset enrichment in sAAb+ donors and memory/effector subsets enriched in mAAb+ donors. In the pLN of mAAb+/T1D donors, however, expression programs associated with distinct memory B cell subsets were heightened (Figure 5E).

Evaluation of the mAAb+/T1D enriched module, comprising cell types enriched before and after disease onset, revealed distinct class-switched memory (CSM) B cell subsets and an IgM+ memory subset (Figure 5E). At the per-donor level, 2/2 (100%) mAAb+ and 3/6 (50%) T1D pLNs showed global enrichment for the B CSM #2 signature (Figure S4A). However, spatial analysis identified focal enrichment within an annotated lymphoid follicle in only one T1D donor (HPAP102) (Figure 5F). Neither ND nor sAAb+ donors showed follicular colocalization of this signature despite global enrichment (Figure S4D). To characterize the functional identity of the B CSM #2 subset, we measured surface protein expression of memory (CD27), class-switch (IGHM), and costimulatory markers (CD40, FAS, and CD86), together with their corresponding transcripts, using the pLN CITE-seq reference data3 (Figures S4E and S4F). We also examined BCL6, which encodes the master regulator of germinal center (GC) B cell responses55 and assessed immunoglobulin gene expression to determine class-switched isotypes (Figures S4F and S4G). We noted that B CSM #2 cells exhibited the highest surface expression of costimulatory proteins, low levels of IgM and IgD (suggestive of class switching), and high expression of BCL6, suggestive of a GC-like phenotype. To validate this, we stained a serial section of HPAP102 pLN for three-color immunofluorescence imaging against CD3E (T cell), CD20 (B-cell), and Ki67 (proliferation) and observed extensive CD20+ Ki67+ B cells colocalized with CD3E+ cells within the same follicle (Figure 5G). Collectively, these findings highlight follicular activation in pLN of mAAb+ and T1D donors when compared with ND.

A lymphotoxin-β signature is observed in the T1D lymphoid follicles

To assess alterations in gene expression across disease groups, we implemented differential expression testing of the four pLN tissue compartments—the lymphoid follicles, TCZ, stroma, and medulla. Approximately 70% (59/85) of all reported DEGs were localized to the lymphoid follicles (33/85; 38.8%) and the medulla (26/85; 30.6%) across all pairwise comparisons, whereas the fewest were associated with the TCZ (9/85; 10.5%) (Figure S5A). We further noted that the majority of compartment-specific DEGs were localized to the T1D lymphoid follicles relative to ND and/or AAb+ donors, suggesting altered follicular activity as a feature of disease (Figure S5B). All significant DEGs across tested pLN regions are reported in Figures S5C–S5K and Tables S7, S8, S9, and S10.

Within AAb+ lymphoid follicles, we observed reduced expression of the immunoregulatory gene CD5256 and the immunoregulatory purinergic receptor P2RX557 relative to ND controls (Figure 6A). We also observed reduced expression of immunoglobulin genes IGHA1 and JCHAIN alongside the oxidative stress gene TXNDC558 in AAb+ vs. ND lymphoid follicles. Within T1D follicles, LTB, a member of the TNFSF family, was the most upregulated gene relative to ND/AAb+ donors (Figures 6B and 6C). Notably, T1D follicles also show elevated expression of multiple B-cell-associated marker genes (e.g., CD79A, BANK1, etc.), indicating a broader enrichment of follicular B cell activity as a whole relative to AAb+ follicles.

LTB is abundantly expressed by T cells and B cells,59–61 and accordingly, the highest regional expression of LTB localized to the lymphoid follicles and TCZ (Figure 6D). LTB expression in the pLN displayed the expected spatial expression pattern in ND sections, which is characterized by higher expression in the lymphoid follicles followed by moderate expression in the adjacent TCZ (Figure 6E). LTB expression was not significantly different in the follicles of AAb+ donors nor in the TCZ of AAb+ and T1D donors relative to ND (Figures 6A and S6A; Tables S4 and S5). In support of our spatial findings, analysis of the pLN CITE-seq dataset (GEO: GSE221787)3 demonstrated significant upregulation of LTB among naive B cell subsets from T1D donors when compared with ND (Figure S6B). Interestingly, this analysis revealed that LTB was significantly upregulated among naive and activated effector CD4+ T cell subsets and the CD8+ naive #2 T cell subset in AAb+ donors relative to ND (Figure S6B). Furthermore, in addition to LTB, CLU (clusterin), FDCSP (follicular dendritic cell secreted protein), NIBAN3 (niban apoptosis regulator 3), and FCER2 (Fcε receptor 2) were among the top upregulated genes in T1D follicles relative to ND (Figure 6B), a pattern that was consistent across individual T1D donors (Figure S6C). Collectively, CLU, FDCSP, and FCER2 have been reported as enriched in mature human follicular DCs (FDCs),62 a specialized stromal-derived APC that regulates follicular B cell activity and relies on lymphotoxin signaling for development.63,64 FCER2, along with NIBAN3, is also highly expressed in B cells65; therefore, we cannot exclude that this observation may instead reflect enhanced B cell activity. Overall, these findings suggest that increased LTB expression within follicles is associated with heightened activity in follicular cell types.

LTB encodes the TNF superfamily ligand lymphotoxin-β (LTβ), which forms a membrane-bound heterotrimer with LTα (LTα1β2) and represents one of two known ligands for the LTβ receptor (LTβR). LTβR is expressed by stromal and myeloid cells, absent in lymphoid cells, and activated by direct cell-to-cell contact.66 Notably, LTβR engagement in lymphoid stromal cells, such as fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs) and FDCs, triggers non-canonical NF-κB activation, which regulates the production of genes essential for lymphoid tissue organization.66–68 To assess whether the increase in LTB expression is associated with the coordinated activation of the non-canonical NF-κB pathway within lymphoid follicles, we performed gene set enrichment analysis using a curated gene module representing this pathway sourced from the REACTOME database (R-HAS-5668541). Here, we observed module enrichment in lymphoid follicles in T1D as compared with AAb+ and ND controls, indicating increased pathway activation (Figure 6F).

To determine the relative contributions of follicular B cells, follicular T cells (Tfh cells), or T cells in the adjacent TCZ toward enhanced LTB expression observed in T1D follicles, we performed imaging-based, subcellular-resolution ST on FFPE pLN sections from two age-matched (no. 6374, ND, 14 years of age, and no. 6579, T1D, 13.91 years of age; Table S1) human organ donors, one without diabetes and one with short-duration T1D (1 year), using the Xenium platform. Within these pLN sections, we defined lymphoid follicles by the expression of CXCL13 (Figure 6G), a well-characterized marker for follicular homing,69 which was previously identified as a top lymphoid follicle marker gene in our Visium dataset (Figure 4A). The spatial expression of LTB was highest in regions that overlapped with CXCL13, whereas moderate expression was observed in interfollicular regions, presumably representing the TCZ (Figure 6G). T cell and follicular B cells were readily identified by unsupervised clustering (Figure S6D), and upon projecting these cells back into their respective spatial coordinates, we confirmed the TCZ as a secondary source of LTB expression in the pLN (Figure S6E). Finally, we evaluated LTB expression within individual T1D follicles at single-cell resolution and found that LTB was robustly expressed by follicular B cells but largely absent in follicular T cells. Although expression was low in T cells within and adjacent to follicles, it remained detectable across the TCZ (Figures 6G, 6H, and 6E). Collectively, these findings reveal a previously unreported increase in LTB expression localized to T1D lymphoid follicles, originating from follicular B cells, and driving non-canonical NF-κB activation in lymphoid stromal cells.

Colocalization of LTB and downstream inducible chemokines is observed in the T1D pancreas

LTβ regulates immune-cell trafficking and organization of the lymphoid microenvironment by regulating the production of lymphoid homing chemokines, such as the CCR7 ligands CCL19 and CCL21.70 Having identified robust LTB expression within T1D lymphoid follicles, we next asked whether this signature was sustained among infiltrating lymphocytes and whether such expression could aberrantly promote lymphoid-associated chemokine production in the inflamed pancreas. To address this, we performed Xenium on FFPE pancreas sections from the same two donors evaluated in the pLN Xenium dataset.

We confirmed cross-platform consistency by recapitulating compartment level annotations defined in the previous Visium data (Figure S7). Unsupervised clustering resolved major cell types across the exocrine (acinar and ductal), endocrine, and stromal (endothelium, stellate cells, and fibroblasts) compartments, along with distinct T cell, B cell, and myeloid populations (Figures S7A and S7B). Assessment of compartmental marker genes (defined in Figure 2A) showed regional expression consistent with Visium for the 3 of 20 exocrine, 8 of 20 endocrine, and 4 of 20 stromal marker genes detectable with the Xenium 5k Pan Human Tissue panel (Figure S7C). Spatial analysis of annotated cell types identified regions of immune cell aggregation in the T1D pancreas that were absent in the ND control, as expected71 (Figure 7A), comprising a mixture of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, B cells, monocytes, and macrophages (Figure S8A). Among these cells, LTB expression was enriched in lymphocytes, notably among B cells and CD4+ T cells (Figure S8A). Examination of LTBR, CCL19, CCL21, and TNFSF14 (encoding LIGHT—the only other known LTβR ligand66) revealed that LTBR expression was highest in acinar and ductal cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts (Figure S8B). TNFSF14 was enriched in acinar, ductal, and monocyte populations, while CCL19 and CCL21 were enriched in fibroblasts and endothelial cells (Figure S8B).

Further examination of immune aggregates revealed the colocalized distribution of lymphocytes, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts (Figure 7B). We observed strong colocalization of LTB, CCL19, and CCL21, but not TNFSF14, within these inflamed lesions (Figures S8C and S8D). No such colocalization was observed in the ND pancreas (Figures S8C and S8D). Interestingly, CCL19 and CCL21 expression revealed a distinct population of CCL21high endothelium bordering the insulitic lesions (Figures 7B and S8B). Despite the presence of LTB+ lymphocytes in both the ND and the T1D pancreas, LTB expression was greater in those from the T1D donor, notably among B cells and CD4+ cells (Figure 7C). Furthermore, as expected, the total frequency of T and B cells was elevated in the insulitic T1D pancreas relative to the ND control with very few B cells (n = 23 in ND vs. n = 12,368 in T1D) and proximal T/B cell populations (n = 6 in ND vs. n = 5,480 in T1D), suggesting minimal T and B cell interaction in the normal pancreas (Figure 7D).

We again performed gene set enrichment analysis using the non-canonical NF-κB module sourced from the REACTOME database (R-HAS-5668541). This module was distinctly enriched in the CCL21high endothelium in the T1D pancreas, suggesting the activation of LTβR in endothelial cells observed proximal to the insulitic lesion (Figure 7E). Similarly, neighboring fibroblasts from the T1D pancreas exhibited elevated expression of both CCL19 and CCL21 (Figure 7F). Notably, TNFSF14 was not elevated in T1D, highlighting LTB as the likely driver of these observed genes in tissue stroma (Figure 7F). Taken together, these findings reveal a shared signature of elevated lymphocyte-derived LTB in both the pLN and the pancreas, with non-canonical NF-κB pathway activation and chemokine expression in tissue stroma as key features of insulitis progression.

DISCUSSION

Herein, we report an in situ transcriptome-wide reference dataset from matched pancreas and pLN sections from human organ donors at distinct stages of T1D. By annotating capture spots using the underlying histological and canonical marker gene expression in each respective tissue, we avoided reliance on inferred cell identities and were able to define gene expression signatures characteristic of functional tissue compartments in the pancreas and pLN. Intra-compartment differential expression testing revealed that some DEGs were compartment restricted, while others were globally altered. Collectively, our results highlight distinct inflammation-associated signatures in the T1D pancreas and enhanced follicular activity in the T1D pLN.

Notably, we observed increased LTB expression in pLN lymphoid follicles of T1D donors relative to ND and AAb+ donors. LTβ has been implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diabetes in the murine model. Soluble LTβR-Ig treatment delays T1D onset and prevents pancreatic tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice.72 Similarly, gestational LTβR-Ig treatment conferred long-term protection in offspring, and adoptive transfer of bulk splenocytes from gestationally treated NOD mice failed to transfer disease in 12/13 NOD.scid recipients.73 Together with our human tissue data, these findings implicate the LTβ signaling pathway as a key candidate therapeutic target. Our data suggest a potential benefit from agents capable of blocking both LTβ and TNF (e.g., etanercept, a blocking soluble receptor-Ig fusion protein)74 vs. agents that singularly block TNF or its receptor, which also have demonstrated some efficacy in stage 3 T1D.75

Our ST data did not detect LTB changes in pre-onset AAb+ pLN; however, reanalysis of published pLN CITE-seq data3 revealed significant upregulation of LTB among distinct naive CD4+ and activated CD4+ T cell subsets and a single subset of naive CD8+ T cells in AAb+ donors (Figure S6B). Furthermore, with this CITE-seq analysis, we noted a significant increase in LTB among naive B cell subsets only in T1D donors (Figure S6B), corroborating the ST findings. By employing an orthogonal subcellular-resolution ST in an independent donor pair, we confirmed that follicular B cells, not T cells, predominantly express LTB in the T1D pLN (Figure 6H). Importantly, despite originally defining this LTB signature in T1D lymphoid follicles, LTB expression was also enriched in T1D insulitic lymphocytes relative to ND control (Figure 7C). Strikingly, expression of LTB, CCL19, and CCL21 highly colocalized within inflamed lesions in the T1D pancreas (Figures S8C and S8D). We demonstrated that fibroblasts and a distinct subpopulation of CCL21high endothelial cells predominantly contributed toward CCL19 and CCL21 expression within inflamed lesions of the T1D pancreas (Figure 7B). Gene set enrichment analysis further supported LTB-driven non-canonical NF-κB pathway activation in T1D.

Although further studies are required to corroborate and validate this LTB signature and its downstream effects during the pathogenesis of T1D, we propose two distinct mechanisms for its respective impacts on the pancreas and pLN. Within the pLN, increased LTβ in the lymphoid follicles likely represents continued follicular activation in ongoing autoimmunity. The observed increased LTβ expression on the surface of pancreas-infiltrating lymphocytes may promote further immune infiltration or the organization of TLSs by inducing chemokine production derived from pancreatic endothelial cells or fibroblasts, as we observed in this single T1D donor (Figures 7B, 7E, and 7F) and has been noted by other efforts.76,77

Analysis of pancreas sections revealed significant changes in REG family expression before and after disease onset. The REG gene family, initially identified in regenerating pancreatic islets in rats,78 consists of five members in humans (REG1A, REG1B, REG3A, REG3G, and REG4), each of which encodes a small, secreted C-type lectin.78 REG expression is linked to inflammation, with multiple studies reporting increased REG1 and REG3 ortholog expression in murine acute pancreatitis.79,80 Overexpression of REG genes has also been reported in pancreata from NOD mice and new-onset human T1D.81,82 Consistent with these findings, we observed significantly higher REG1B and REG3A expression in human T1D across all pancreatic compartments. REG3G was also upregulated in T1D but specifically within the exocrine compartment. The global elevation of REG1B and REG3A in T1D likely reflects chronic autoimmune-mediated inflammation, supported by its consistency across a broad range of T1D durations (0.01–8.0 years; median: 4.19 years). Notably, however, AAb+ donors showed a significant reduction in tissue-wide REG expression relative to T1D and ND donor groups, suggesting some involvement before disease onset. Because REG proteins have trophic and protective qualities in islet cells,83,84 including REG3B-mediated protection from streptozotocin-induced β cell death,85–87 reduced REG expression in the AAb+ pancreas may indicate loss of protective factors, whereas post-onset upregulation may reflect generalized inflammation or survival bias.

The endocrine compartment contained the most pancreas-compartment-specific DEGs. INS and GCG expression was downregulated in non-diabetic AAb+ donors, implying pre-onset endocrine dysfunction, a finding that is consistent with prior reports.23,25,88 This is further evidenced by reduced expression of TTR, which is known to participate in glucagon89 and insulin26 secretion. Endocrine-specific decreases in CUZD1 and SERPINA3 were also observed in AAb+ donors. CUZD1 encodes a zymogen granule-associated transmembrane protein highly expressed in the acinar pancreas with implications in pancreatitis.90 Due to the expected multicell composition of individual capture spots, it is likely that endocrine compartment-specific downregulation of CUZD1 may reflect gene expression changes specifically within islet-adjacent acinar cells in AAb+ donors, since CUZD1 expression is unchanged in the acinar compartment. SERPINA3 encodes a serine protease inhibitor that has been shown previously to promote insulin production in response to a high-fat diet by negatively regulating the JNK pathway.91

The multicell-resolution ST cohort of T1D cases with variable disease duration was limited by a paucity of insulitic lesions as confirmed by expert histopathological assessment (Table S3). Insulitis presents with a heterogeneous pattern of inflammation between lobules of the same pancreas and across samples and is most prevalent within the first year following T1D onset.92 Despite this, cell deconvolution identified capture spots exhibiting immune-associated transcript enrichment in order to focus on areas of the pancreas suspected to be involved in some inflammation response. Using this approach, we detected the most immune-enriched capture spots in mAAb+ pancreas relative to the ND, sAAb+, and T1D groups in this cohort. We observed the most abundant enrichment of general T cell markers in mAAb+ donors; T1D donors were predominantly enriched with general B cell markers. The latter finding is consistent with the higher frequency of proliferating B cells in the T1D pancreas as observed by imaging mass cytometry.34

In the pLN, naive lymphocytes, myeloid APCs, and ILCs were predominantly enriched in ND donors but relatively infrequent in T1D donors. Activated, effector, and central memory T cells peaked in mAAb+ donors but were infrequent in T1D donor pLN. Interestingly, several memory B cell phenotypes were uniquely enriched among T1D donors, indicating that persistent humoral activity may be a feature of the T1D follicles. These findings match our observations in donor-matched pancreas sections, indicating a possible common B cell signature across both tissues. Contrary to these observations in tissues, peripheral blood studies have reported unchanged or reduced memory B cell frequencies in T1D samples compared with controls.93–96 This points toward differences between systemic circulation and tissue-specific accumulation.

By leveraging spatially resolved gene expression imparted by ST, we identified global and compartment-specific transcriptional changes affecting immunomodulatory and functional pancreatic genes. Complementary in silico cell deconvolution further revealed shifts in immune phenotypes and cellular frequencies across tissues, which can inform potential cellular targets and inflammatory pathways in the development of preventative strategies. Last, these data constitute a community resource, offering tissue-specific inflammation signatures that can be integrated with additional molecular modalities to create a comprehensive multi-omic description of inflammation and pancreatic dysfunction across the pathogenic stages of T1D.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations to this study that should be acknowledged. First, our findings are based on a cross-sectional cohort design. While we aimed to include representative donors across the natural progression of T1D, our characterizations are limited to a single time point. Additionally, the Visium CytAssist platform utilizes 55-μm-diameter multicell-resolution capture spots, which inherently reflect an aggregate transcriptome of multiple cells. Although cell deconvolution via single-cell transcriptome data integration facilitated the interpretation of these data at the cellular level, our findings require additional validation with single-cell- or subcellular-resolution ST or the incorporation of orthogonal validation studies for further support. Indeed, although we began to address this need by performing subcellular-resolution ST using an orthogonal platform (Xenium), we were able to process only a single pair of donor-matched pLN and pancreas; therefore, additional validation is required in a larger cohort. Furthermore, as our analyses include a single tissue section from each donor pancreas and pLN in our cohort, these findings are subject to potential sampling bias, which precludes our ability to capture the full cellular heterogeneity represented throughout the entire organ or tissue. Access to shared biobank tissues is limited to preserve availability for other investigators; however, by leveraging multimodal datasets generated by other groups from larger representative tissue areas from the same donors, we can jointly assess the consistency of these results, as demonstrated in our evaluation of LTB expression in donor pLN using previously published CITEseq data (Figure S6B).

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Human organ donor tissues

Pancreas and pLN FFPE sections were obtained from the HPAP consortium (RRID:SCR_016202; https://hpap.pmacs.upenn.edu), part of the Human Islet Research Network (https://hirnetwork.org/), and the Network of Pancreatic Organ Donors with Diabetes (RRID:SCR_014641; https://npod.org/) with approval from the University of Florida Institutional Review Board (IRB # 201600029) and the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS). A legal representative for each donor provided informed consent prior to organ retrieval. Donor summary characteristics are outlined in Table S1.

METHOD DETAILS

Spatial transcriptomics

Visium CytAssist spatial transcriptomics

ST of pancreatic and pLN tissues from the HPAP donor cohort were performed using the Visium CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression for FFPE kit (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, FFPE tissue blocks from pancreas and pLN tissue for each donor were cut to a thickness of 5μm and mounted onto positively charged microscope slides. Tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated according to Visium CytAssist Tissue Preparation Guide Tissue sections were H&E stained and imaged using either a BZ-X700 (Keyence) or PhenoImager Slide Scanner (Akoya Biosciences) for histological assessment and gene expression underlay. Whole transcriptome probe hybridization (v2 Whole Transcriptome Panel; 10x Genomics) was performed for approximately 18 hours at 50°C for both tissue types. Probe hybridized tissue sections were transferred onto a 6.5mm × 6.5mm (pLN) or 11mm × 11mm (pancreas) Visium CytAssist Gene Expression slide using the Visium CytAssist instrument with transfer conditions set to 37°C for 30 minutes for both tissues. Spatially barcoded cDNA libraries were analyzed by Agilent High Sensitivity DNA kit on the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent) to estimate library concentration and quality. Libraries were pooled to equimolar concentrations and sequenced on an S4 Illumina flow cell using the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) with a target depth of 25,000 reads per tissue-covered capture spot. Arrangement of donor sections on the Visium CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression array is provided in Table S1. FASTQ and image files are deposited at the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository under accession number GSE296626.

Xenium Prime 5k in situ gene expression

Imaging-based ST of pancreatic and pLN tissue from two nPOD donors were performed using the Xenium Prime in situ Gene Expression platform (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, FFPE pancreas and pLN tissue blocks from each donor were cut to a thickness of 5μm and both were mounted directly onto their own donor-respective Xenium slide. Samples were deparaffinized, decrosslinked, and processed according to the Xenium Prime in Situ Gene Expression Guide. Probe-based transcript detection was facilitated using the Xenium Prime 5K Human Pan Tissue and Pathways Panel which provides coverage against 5,001 genes. Additionally, the base 5k Human Pan Tissue gene panel was supplemented with a custom 100 add-on gene panel to provide additional coverage against immune and disease-relevant genes not represented in the base panel (Table S11). In order to improve cell segmentation accuracy, we performed the optional cell segmentation staining step outlined in the Xenium Prime in Situ Gene Expression Guide using the Cell Segmentation Staining kit (10x Genomics). The Xenium Multi-Tissue Stain Mix consists of four distinct labeling methods used for the detection of cell membrane (anti-ATP1A1/CD45/E-Cadherin), cell interior (anti-αSMA/Vimentin and 18s rRNA), and the nucleus (DAPI) which are later used as inputs for the cell segmentation algorithm implemented within the Xenium in Situ software suite onboard the Xenium Analyzer instrument. Upon completing sample preparation, Xenium slides were loaded onto the Xenium Analyzer for imaging and transcript detection. Raw output data was automatically processed using the Xenium Onboard Analysis software (v3.2), which handles image processing (multi-focus image fusion97, tile stitching, and background subtraction), cell segmentation, transcript amplicon image registration, and transcript decoding, which yields the final output data for user analysis.

Histopathology review

Paraffin sections were stained by H&E and whole slide scans reviewed for pathological findings. pLN was evaluated for adequacy of tissue compartments (follicles, medulla, cortex) and the presence of fat, fibrosis or other significant findings (Table S2). Pancreas sections were evaluated for pancreatitis using a semi-quantitative scoring system (0= none or absent, 1= few or mild, 2= several or moderate, 3= many or severe), alongside evaluation of other relevant histopathological features including islet numbers, fibrosis, lipid infiltration, neoplasia, and amyloid formation, as previously reported98 (Table S3).

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed according to the PhenoCycler-Fusion user guide (Akoya Biosciences). Deparaffinization and rehydration of FFPE tissue was performed as described with exception for our choice of solvent, xylene was used instead of HistoChoice Clearing Agent. Antigen retrieval was performed by immersing sections in 1x AR9 antigen retrieval buffer (Akoya Biosciences, PN#AR6001K) and incubating for 20 minutes under high pressure in a pressure cooker. Primary antibody staining using oligonucleotide conjugated anti-human CD20 (AKYP0049-BX064), anti-human CD3 (AKYP0027-BX015), and anti-human Ki67 (AKYP0052-BX047) was performed for 3 hours at room temperature. A complementary oligonucleotide conjugated fluorescence reporter plate corresponding to barcodes BX064, BX015, and BX047 was prepared for protein detection. Automated detection and imaging were performed at 40x magnification using the PhenoCycler-Fusion System (Akoya Biosciences).

QC, data integration, and normalization

Visium CytAssist spatial transcriptomics

Initial data quality was assessed on a per-tissue and per-donor basis prior to data integration and downstream analysis. Summary count information on each donor and tissue is outlined in Table S1. We evaluated the total number of reads, detected genes, and percentage of reads mapping to mitochondrial genes per sample and per capture spot. Capture spots with mitochondrial content ≥ 10% were removed. Individual donor datasets were integrated by respective tissue (pancreas n=20; pLN n=16) by anchor based canonical correlation analysis (CCA) integration procedure implemented in Seurat to create a unified data set. After integration, raw count data for each donor were normalized by upper quartile normalization to account for technical interdonor variability in sequencing depth and total library size20. Expression values for each gene were standardized across all capture spots (z-score transformation) by implementing the ScaleData function in Seurat. Prior to dimensionality reduction, a total of 1000 highly variable genes detected across all donors were identified for downstream analysis using the FindVariableFeatures function in Seurat using the vst method (selection.method = ‘vst’). We then performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the top 1000 highly variable genes using the RunPCA function to visualize the grouping of each annotated compartment along the top 3 dimensions for each respective tissue. Lastly, cells were clustered and projected in 2-dimensions by uniform manifold approximation projection (UMAP) using the RunUMAP function to qualitatively assess the performance of our integration strategy (Figures S1E and S1F).

Xenium Prime 5k in situ gene expression

Initial data quality was assessed on a per-tissue and per-donor basis prior to data integration and downstream analysis. Summary count information for each donor and tissue is outlined in Table S1. On a per-sample basis, we applied an extended version of Tukey’s fences to total gene counts, removing individual cells with count values below Q1−(3*IQR) or above Q3+(3*IQR) as outliers. Individual donor datasets were integrated by respective tissue (pancreas n=2; pLN n=2) by anchor based canonical correlation analysis (CCA) integration procedure implemented in Seurat to create a unified data set. After integration, raw count data for each donor were normalized by upper quartile normalization to account for technical interdonor variability in sequencing depth and total library size20. Following normalization, we performed dimensionality reduction by PCA and generated a shared nearest-neighbor (SNN) map to enable unsupervised Louvain clustering in order to partition cells into transcriptionally distinct clusters. Cell type identities were subsequently assigned based on canonical marker gene expression. Lastly, expression values for each gene were standardized across cells (z-score transformation) by implementing the ScaleData function in Seurat. Following data processing, Xenium data were visualized using the Xenium Explorer 4 software (v4.0.0; 10x Genomics).

Tissue compartment annotations

Tissue compartment annotations were guided by histology using H&E stained tissue sections generated during the Visium assay. Additionally, these histologically defined regions were validated and refined based on canonical marker gene expression corresponding to the predominant cell types in each compartment of the pancreas and pLN. For the pancreas, we identified 3 major tissue compartments: the exocrine (AMY1A), endocrine (CHGA), and stromal (ACTA2). For the pLN, we identified 5 major tissue compartments: lymphoid follicles (MS4A1), T-cell zone (TCZ; CD3E), medulla (MARCO), stroma (ACTA2), and mixed region. Following compartment level annotation of capture spots, we used FindAllMarkers (test.use = ‘wilcox’, min.pct = 0.25) to calculate the top 20 marker genes for each compartment in the pancreas and pLN. Compartment-level marker genes were then assessed and shown to accurately represent the major cell types localized to their respective tissue compartments (e.g., INS in the endocrine compartment, ZAP70 in the TCZ.) (Figures 2A and 4A).

Differential expression (DE) testing

We performed three pairwise comparisons - AAb+ vs. ND, T1D vs. ND, & T1D vs. AAb+ - for intercompartmental differential expression testing. Genes considered for differential expression testing were detected in ≥ 25% of capture spots per annotated compartment across all donors. Within each tissue compartment, gene counts were aggregated across capture spots per donor and normalized by capture spot frequency to account for sampling bias and the autoimmune mediated loss of endocrine tissue observed in T1D. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the likelihood ratio test on a generalized linear model using the FindMarker function in Seurat (test.use = poisson). Nominal p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for multiple test correction99. Genes were reported as significantly differentially expressed if the absolute log2FC difference is ≥ 0.25 and has a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Differential expression analysis results are visualized using EnhancedVolcano100.

Capture spot cell deconvolutionS

Cell deconvolution of multi-cell resolution ST in the Visium gene expression assay enables inference of constituent cell type proportions contained within individual capture spots101. For this approach, we integrated our individual spatial gene expression assays from pancreas and pLN with a single-cell transcriptomic reference data. The isolated islet pancreas scRNA-seq data were obtained from the the PancDB database18. Only samples from T1D individuals were included. The pLN CITE-seq data were acquired from HPAP (GSE221787)3. These single-cell reference data contain 40% and 50% donor overlap with our pancreas and pLN spatial gene expression cohort, respectively. Donor representation in each respective single cell reference is outlined in Table S1. Deconvolution was implemented using the anchor-based canonical correlation analysis (CCA) method in the FindTransferAnchors function in Seurat with parameters set to default. The predicted cell-type proportion at each capture spot position was calculated for each cell type annotation represented in the reference dataset.

University of Florida Diabetes institute Spatial Data Portal

Visualizations of the processed data, histological images, and project-specific annotations are made available for interactive exploration using the WebAtlas pipeline and application (CITE)102,103. The interactive environment enables gene-level queries and selectable annotation layers for detailed exploration of spatial gene expression in human T1D. The app is hosted on the University of Florida’s HiPerGator Computing System, ensuring stable, high-performance access. The app can be accessed by visiting https://ufdi.rc.ufl.edu. Raw and processed data files are publicly accessible at the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository [GSE296626].

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL analysisS

No statistical test was performed to predetermine cohort size. We performed three pairwise comparisons during differential gene expression tests: AAb+ vs ND, T1D vs ND, & T1D vs AAb+. We employed the likelihood ratio test within Seurat for differential expression testing. Genes with an absolute log2FC > 0.25 and FDR < 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg test correction were reported as significant (Tables S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, and S10). We used the unpaired students T-test to calculate significance for deconvolution-assisted immune enrichment scores and gene expression data from CITE-seq data across disease groups. A one-way ANOVA was used to calculate global significance of pLN compartment representation across disease groups. Significance values are denoted as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.005, ***p < 0.0005, and ****p < 0.00005. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3. Seurat version 4.3.0 was utilized for these analyses.
