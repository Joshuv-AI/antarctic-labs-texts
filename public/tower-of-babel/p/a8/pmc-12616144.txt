Prevention, Detection, and Management of Post-Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography Pancreatitis
Authors: Huapyong Kang, Chang Hwan Park, Kihyun Ryu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is a widely used diagnostic and therapeutic procedure for pancreaticobiliary diseases. However, its relatively invasive nature necessitates a thorough understanding of potential adverse events and appropriate preventive strategies. Post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP), the most common ERCP-related adverse event, occurs in approximately 10% of cases. While often mild, severe cases can rapidly progress and lead to clinical deterioration and mortality. The pathogenesis of PEP involves direct tissue injury, impaired ductal drainage, inflammatory mediator release, and individual susceptibility. These insights have informed the currently employed prevention and management strategies. PEP risk factors include both patient- and procedure-related variables, underscoring the need for precise risk stratification and individualized procedural planning. Evidence-based preventive strategies—such as rectal nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, prophylactic pancreatic stent placement, aggressive intravenous hydration, guidewire-assisted cannulation, and other pharmacologic agents—have demonstrated efficacy in reducing PEP incidence. Future developments, including optimal combination strategies and machine learning–based risk prediction models, may further improve outcomes. Significantly reducing the burden of PEP requires integrating mechanistic insight and risk stratification with timely, evidence-based prevention and management.

INTRODUCTION

1. Definition of PEP

Post-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) pancreatitis, hereafter referred to as post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP), is defined as a new or worsened abdominal pain occurring after ERCP, accompanied by an elevation of serum pancreatic enzymes—amylase or lipase—to more than three times the upper limit of normal (ULN) measured at least 24 hours post-procedure, and necessitating unplanned hospital admission or prolongation of an already planned hospital stay.1,2 This definition, initially proposed in the 1991 consensus by Cotton et al.,1 has been widely adopted in clinical research and society guidelines. It captures both the clinical presentation and biochemical markers of pancreatic injury, while also emphasizing the healthcare utilization impact as part of the diagnostic criteria. Over time, the definition has been slightly refined—for instance, by the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) in 2020—to clarify that the abdominal pain must be “new or worsened,” and that enzyme levels must be obtained after a minimum of 24 hours following ERCP to avoid misclassification due to transient post-procedural hyperamylasemia.

2. Classification and severity grading of PEP

The severity of PEP is commonly assessed using either the traditional consensus criteria or the revised Atlanta classification. Both systems are widely used in clinical practice and research, but differ significantly in methodology and prognostic precision.3,4

The consensus criteria, first introduced by Cotton et al. in 1991,1 classify PEP severity based primarily on the duration of hospitalization and the need for clinical interventions. In this framework, mild PEP is defined as requiring hospitalization for 2 to 3 days, moderate PEP as 4 to 10 days, and severe PEP as hospitalization for more than 10 days or requiring percutaneous or surgical intervention due to complications such as necrosis or pseudocyst formation.4

In contrast, the revised Atlanta classification (2012) offers a pathophysiological and organ dysfunction–based grading system that is applicable to all forms of acute pancreatitis, including PEP. This system classifies mild acute pancreatitis as the absence of organ failure or local/systemic complications, moderate as transient organ failure (≤48 hours) or local complications, and severe as persistent organ failure (>48 hours), which may involve one or more organs.3

Several comparative studies suggest that the revised Atlanta classification may provide better predictive value for clinical outcomes. For example, Smeets et al.4 reported that the Revised Atlanta System demonstrated higher sensitivity (100% vs 55%) and specificity (98% vs 72%) for mortality prediction compared to the consensus criteria. Moreover, reliance on hospital stay in the consensus model introduces variability due to differences in institutional practices, admission thresholds, and comorbidities.

Given these differences, the revised Atlanta classification is increasingly preferred for research purposes and in tertiary care settings where comprehensive organ function monitoring is feasible. However, the consensus criteria remain practical and widely used, especially in procedural documentation and early post-ERCP assessment. Ideally, both systems can be complementary: one for standardized reporting, the other for stratifying clinical risk and guiding resource allocation (Table 1).1,3

EPIDEMIOLOGY

PEP is the most frequent and clinically significant adverse event following ERCP, with reported incidence rates ranging from 3.5% to over 15%, depending on patient risk profile, procedural complexity, and prophylactic strategies employed.5

In a large meta-analysis of 145 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the pooled incidence of PEP was estimated at 10.2% (95% confidence interval [CI], 9.3% to 11.3%), with the rate remaining stable across decades despite improvements in ERCP techniques and preventive measures.6 Notably, the incidence is substantially higher in high-risk populations—up to 40% in those with multiple risk factors such as suspected sphincter of Oddi dysfunction (SOD), prior PEP, or difficult cannulation.7,8

Geographical variation in PEP incidence has also been observed. A systematic review reported higher rates in North America (13%) compared to Asia (9.9%) and Europe (8.4%), potentially reflecting differences in patient selection, procedural techniques, prophylactic practices, and reporting standards.9

In terms of severity, most PEP cases are mild to moderate; however, approximately 0.5% to 1% progress to severe pancreatitis, characterized by persistent organ failure or local complications such as necrosis or fluid collections. The overall mortality of PEP remains low (0.2% to 0.9%) but is significantly higher in patients with severe disease.6,10,11

The healthcare burden of PEP is non-negligible. In the United States alone, the annual cost attributed to PEP has been estimated at over 200 million USD, considering both direct hospitalization costs and indirect consequences such as intensive care unit admission, interventional radiology, and prolonged recovery.9,12 Given the substantial healthcare burden associated with PEP, the importance of risk stratification and evidence-based prophylaxis becomes evident—particularly as ERCP is increasingly restricted to therapeutic indications and performed in more complex patient populations.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

The pathophysiology of PEP is multifactorial, involving a cascade of mechanical, chemical, thermal, microbiological, and immunological insults to the pancreatic ductal system. While the precise mechanism remains incompletely elucidated, current evidence supports a model in which procedural trauma leads to premature activation of pancreatic enzymes, triggering an inflammatory response within the pancreatic parenchyma.13,14

Mechanical injury is considered a primary initiator of PEP. Repeated attempts at cannulation, deep passage of guidewires into the pancreatic duct (PD), or injection of contrast under pressure can damage the ductal epithelium and disrupt acinar integrity.15-20 This promotes intrapancreatic activation of trypsinogen to trypsin, thereby initiating autodigestion and inflammation.21-23 Anatomical features of the ampulla of Vater, such as mucosal reduplications and deep crypts, may predispose certain individuals to trauma and edema during instrumentation.17,18

Hydrostatic injury from excessive contrast injection or overdistension of the PD can also contribute to epithelial disruption. Studies have shown that high intraductal pressure increases permeability of tight junctions and promotes pro-inflammatory cytokine release, including interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor-α, potentially leading to systemic inflammation.24-30

Chemical irritation is another mechanism. Contrast media, particularly hyperosmolar or ionic agents, can directly damage ductal epithelial cells. Although guidewire-assisted cannulation is now the standard, chemical-induced PEP remains a risk in centers using older techniques.31-33

Thermal and electrical injury may occur during precut or biliary sphincterotomy. Electrocautery can induce local tissue necrosis and papillary edema, contributing to SOD and impaired pancreatic drainage. Interestingly, use of blended current (cut+coagulation) has been associated with lower rates of PEP compared to pure cut current.34,35

Infectious and microbiological mechanisms have also been proposed. Instrumentation of the ampulla may introduce gut flora into the pancreaticobiliary system, facilitating bacterial translocation and inflammation. However, the exact role of microbiota in PEP remains to be fully defined.36-39

Genetic predisposition is increasingly recognized as a modifying factor. Single nucleotide polymorphisms in genes such as GGT1 and IRF2BP1 have been associated with heightened inflammatory responses and increased susceptibility to PEP via IL-6 amplifier and STAT3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) activation pathways.39-41

Finally, neurogenic inflammation may contribute to symptom severity and disease propagation. Experimental models have implicated sensory neurons, including those expressing transient receptor potential vanilloid 1, in mediating pain and promoting substance P-driven edema and leukocyte recruitment. Low pH and tissue acidosis, common during early pancreatitis, may further amplify these signals.39,42

Taken together, PEP results from a convergence of direct tissue injury, impaired ductal drainage, inflammatory mediator release, and host susceptibility. This mechanistic understanding continues to inform targeted strategies for PEP prevention and management.

RISK FACTORS

1. Patient-related risk factors

Female sex and younger age are consistently associated with increased risk of PEP. Women are nearly twice as likely as men to develop PEP, possibly due to hormonal factors, ductal anatomy differences, or the higher prevalence of SOD in females. Patients, younger than 60 years, especially those under 40, also show greater susceptibility. This may reflect preserved acinar cell integrity and a more vigorous inflammatory response. In contrast, older individuals or those with pancreatic fibrosis may have diminished enzymatic activation, offering relative protection.14,28,43,44

SOD is a well-established risk factor for PEP. It is commonly diagnosed in younger women with recurrent biliary-type pain but no structural abnormalities. ERCP in these patients often involves papillary manipulation, such as sphincterotomy or manometry, which increases the risk of ductal trauma and subsequent pancreatitis. This also helps explain the demographic overlap in PEP risk related to age and sex.12,45

Prior history of acute pancreatitis, especially PEP, significantly increases the risk of recurrence. These patients exhibit heightened ductal sensitivity, and even minor instrumentation can trigger enzyme activation and inflammation. Despite use of prophylactic measures, the incidence of PEP remains high in this group.5,28

Patients undergoing ERCP for asymptomatic choledocholithiasis are at an elevated risk. In these cases, the lack of biliary dilation or biochemical abnormalities can make cannulation more difficult and increase the likelihood of unnecessary PD access. Similarly, normal serum bilirubin is associated with higher PEP risk, often reflecting the absence of obstructive pathology and suggesting a low-yield indication for ERCP.28,43,44

The absence of chronic pancreatitis has also been linked to increased PEP risk. Fibrosis and acinar atrophy in chronic pancreatitis may attenuate inflammatory responses to mechanical or chemical injury. In contrast, a healthy pancreatic parenchyma reacts more robustly to ductal trauma, enhancing the risk of clinically significant pancreatitis.14,28

2. Procedure-related risk factors

Repeated or prolonged cannulation attempts are consistently associated with an increased risk of PEP. This includes procedures with more than five cannulation attempts, cannulation times exceeding 10 minutes, or unintentional contrast injection into the PD. Mechanical irritation and hydrostatic injury from such manipulation may result in early enzyme activation and acinar damage.12,14,28,43

Precut sphincterotomy, especially after failed conventional cannulation, is a high-risk technique. It increases mucosal trauma and inflammatory response, particularly when performed by less experienced operators. Similarly, pancreatic sphincterotomy, often used in patients with suspected SOD, disrupts ductal integrity and leads to increased susceptibility to pancreatitis.12,43,45

PD instrumentation—including guidewire insertion, brush cytology, and placement of pancreatic stents (PSs)—have been identified as a procedural risk. Even when performed for prophylaxis, repeated instrumentation may provoke mechanical stress and ductal hypertension.13,28 Likewise, contrast injection into the PD, particularly with high pressure or large volumes, increases the risk of acinar injury and ductal obstruction.13,43

Endoscopic balloon dilation of the sphincter of Oddi, especially without prior sphincterotomy, increases the risk of PEP. The dilation can cause compression of the pancreatic orifice and edema, impairing pancreatic juice outflow.12,45 Though used as an alternative for large stone removal, this technique should be approached cautiously.

Operator experience and procedure volume significantly influence the risk. Low-volume centers or endoscopists with limited experience in complex biliary procedures have been shown to have higher PEP incidence. Inadequate technique, prolonged procedures, and failure to apply prophylactic measures contribute to this outcome.28,43

3. Definition of patient at high risk for PEP

Identifying patients at high risk for PEP is critical for guiding both procedural decisions and the application of prophylactic measures. Current guidelines by the ESGE and the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) provide structured criteria to define high-risk patients based on combinations of patient-related and procedure-related risk factors.2,12

According to ESGE (2020), a patient is considered at high risk of developing PEP if at least one “definite” risk factor is present or two or more “likely” risk factors are identified (Table 2).2 The classification is based on the strength of association derived from multiple observational studies and meta-analyses.2,4,28,46

This classification has been endorsed for clinical use, especially when selecting patients for prophylactic interventions such as rectal nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and PS placement. In particular, the presence of a single definite risk factor, such as SOD or a history of PEP, is associated with a markedly elevated risk of pancreatitis—ranging from 15% to over 20% in some cohorts.28 Meanwhile, multiple likely risk factors may similarly confer substantial risk, especially when occurring in combination—for example, a young female with normal bilirubin undergoing her first ERCP.

Although ASGE (2017) does not adopt the same “definite/likely” terminology, it identifies similar factors and recommends prophylactic measures for patients at increased risk.12 In both guidelines, emphasis is placed on the cumulative nature of risk: the more risk factors present, the greater the likelihood of PEP.

Therefore, structured pre-procedural risk assessment using validated criteria is essential. Accurate stratification not only improves the safety profile of ERCP but also informs decisions such as referral to expert centers and the pre-emptive use of protective techniques and pharmacological agents.

PREVENTION STRATEGIES

1. Rectal NSAIDs

Rectal administration of NSAIDs, particularly indomethacin or diclofenac, is a cornerstone in the prevention of PEP. A single 100 mg dose administered via the rectal route—either immediately before or after the procedure—has demonstrated consistent benefit across multiple RCTs and meta-analyses, particularly in patients at elevated risk.47,48

This route of administration is favored for both pharmacokinetic and practical reasons. Rectal delivery ensures rapid systemic absorption, bypassing the variability of gastrointestinal conditions and avoiding first-pass hepatic metabolism, which can limit bioavailability. Conversely, oral formulations have repeatedly failed to demonstrate efficacy in PEP prophylaxis and are therefore not recommended for this indication.2,12 Accordingly, expert consensus strongly discourages substitution with oral NSAIDs. Mechanistically, NSAIDs interrupt the early inflammatory cascade in pancreatitis by inhibiting phospholipase A₂ and cyclooxygenase enzymes. This suppresses the synthesis of pro-inflammatory lipid mediators such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes, thereby limiting local vascular injury, leukocyte infiltration, and pancreatic tissue damage.49 Experimental studies and clinical data support this mode of action, underscoring its biologic plausibility and therapeutic value.

According to the International Association of Pancreatology (IAP) Revised Guidelines on Acute Pancreatitis 2025, prophylactic rectal NSAIDs are recommended for both high-risk and average-risk patients undergoing ERCP, preferably within 30 minutes before the procedure.50 In some countries, however, rectal NSAIDs formulations are not commercially available due to lack of production and regulatory approval, which remains a barrier to their widespread adoption.

2. Aggressive IV hydration

Aggressive intravenous (IV) hydration has emerged as an important strategy for reducing both the incidence and severity of PEP, supported by an increasing body of evidence. The physiological rationale is based on the observation that early acute pancreatitis is often associated with impaired pancreatic microcirculation. Adequate intravascular volume expansion through aggressive hydration is thought to restore pancreatic microperfusion, thereby preventing necrotizing pancreatitis and subsequent multiorgan failure.51

The most commonly used aggressive hydration protocol involves the administration of isotonic crystalloid solutions—typically lactated Ringer’s solution (LRS)—at a rate of 3 mL/kg/hr during the procedure, followed by a 20 mL/kg bolus immediately after ERCP and continued at 3 mL/kg/hr for the next 8 hours. This regimen contrasts with standard hydration, which consists of 1.5 mL/kg/hr during and after the procedure for 8 hours.52 Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses have demonstrated that aggressive hydration significantly reduces the risk of PEP compared to standard hydration, with relative risk (RR) reductions ranging from 54% to 71%.52-59 Additionally, aggressive hydration has been associated with lower rates of severe pancreatitis, shorter hospital stays, and reduced post-procedural abdominal pain.

LRS is generally preferred over normal saline due to its lower risk of inducing metabolic acidosis and its potential anti-inflammatory effects.52,54,60 However, careful patient selection is essential. Factors such as age, cardiac function, and renal function must be considered to avoid fluid overload. Frequent assessment of volume status may be necessary to mitigate risk. A recent multicenter RCT employed a tailored hydration protocol, in which IV fluids were discontinued 4 to 6 hours after ERCP if serum amylase levels remained within normal limits and the patient had no abdominal pain. This individualized approach still favored the aggressive hydration group in reducing PEP (3.5% vs 9.4%; p=0.03).61 Notably, no PEP occurred in patients from either group who met the early cessation criteria, and total fluid volume was significantly reduced. These findings suggest that individualized aggressive hydration may optimize both efficacy and safety by limiting unnecessary fluid exposure.

3. Other pharmacologic agents

Beyond rectal NSAIDs, various pharmacologic agents have been investigated for their potential to prevent PEP. However, current clinical trials and meta-analyses indicate that only a few agents demonstrate meaningful efficacy as monotherapy. Nitrates such as glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) are hypothesized to reduce PEP by relaxing the sphincter of Oddi, thereby facilitating pancreatic drainage and reducing papillary edema.62 A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs reported that GTN use was associated with a 33% reduction in PEP incidence compared with placebo.63 GTN can be administered via IV, sublingual, or transdermal routes; however, two meta-analyses found that only the sublingual route significantly reduced the risk of PEP (by 53% to 63%), whereas other routes showed no benefit or yielded inconsistent results.63,64 ESGE recommends a single dose of 5 mg sublingual GTN before ERCP in patients with contraindications to NSAIDs or aggressive hydration.2 Nonetheless, this recommendation is based on limited evidence from only two RCTs, highlighting the need for further validation in larger trials.65,66

Hormonal agents such as somatostatin and octreotide, which inhibit pancreatic exocrine secretion, have also been evaluated for PEP prophylaxis. In a large RCT involving 908 patients, bolus somatostatin significantly reduced PEP incidence compared with no treatment (4.04% vs 7.47%; p=0.0276).67 Meta-analyses suggest that bolus administration is more effective than continuous infusion, with benefits mainly observed in high-risk patients.68,69 However, due to heterogeneity and inconsistent findings across studies, current guidelines do not support routine somatostatin use for PEP prevention.

Other agents—including protease inhibitors (gabexate, ulinastatin, and nafamostat), topical epinephrine, proton pump inhibitors, antibiotics, and allopurinol—have also been studied. While some trials reported modest benefit, the overall evidence remains limited and inconsistent.70,71

4. PS placement

PS placement, along with rectal indomethacin, is one of the most well-established strategies for preventing PEP. A well-recognized mechanism of PEP is impaired PD drainage secondary to papillary edema and/or sphincter of Oddi spasm after ERCP. PS helps maintain ductal drainage by bypassing this obstruction.72 Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses have demonstrated that PS placement significantly reduces the risk of PEP, particularly among high-risk patients, with a RR reduction of approximately 60% to 80% and a pooled number needed to treat of 7.68,72-79 Accordingly, the ASGE, ESGE, and IAP guidelines recommend selective prophylactic PS placement in high-risk patients, such as those with unintentional repeated pancreatic cannulation, PD injection, difficult biliary cannulation, endoscopic papillectomy, precut sphincterotomy, or a history of PEP.2,50,80 In contrast, for average-risk patients, rectal indomethacin remains the preferred method due to its cost-effectiveness, procedural simplicity, and lower complication risk.81 However, recent meta-analyses suggest that PS placement may also provide preventive benefits in average-risk populations.68,79,82 Thus, in regions where rectal NSAIDs are unavailable—such as South Korea—or in patients with contraindications to NSAIDs use, prophylactic PS may be considered a viable alternative even for average-risk patients.

Currently, there is insufficient high-level evidence to definitively guide optimal stent selection. A 5-F stent is generally preferred over a 3-F stent due to greater efficacy in preventing PEP and easier placement, with similar rates of spontaneous dislodgement.83,84 Stents without internal flange, 3–7 cm in length, are typically used to facilitate spontaneous dislodgement. In cases of difficult deep cannulation, shorter stents (2–3 cm) may be considered.80 A recent post hoc subgroup analysis of an RCT examined five technical variables related to prophylactic PS placement—(1) whether PD access was achieved solely for stenting or incidentally, (2) level of technical effort, (3) stent length, (4) stent caliber, and (5) guidewire size—and found that none significantly influenced the preventive efficacy of PS among high-risk patients. These findings suggest that PS offers consistent benefits across various technical approaches, though further research is warranted to optimize procedural strategies.85

PS retained for prolonged periods can induce ductal fibrosis and should be endoscopically removed if not spontaneously passed.86 In one study using 5-F stents, spontaneous dislodgement occurred in 68.4% of patients within 2 weeks.83 However, the safe duration for expectant management varies across guidelines, ranging from 10 days to 4 weeks, and further research is needed to clarify the optimal window for stent retention.2,80

5. Guidewire-assisted cannulation

As previously discussed, key procedural risk factors for PEP include difficult biliary cannulation, unintentional PD cannulation, pancreatic contrast injection, and precut sphincterotomy. To prevent PEP, endoscopic techniques should aim to achieve timely and safe biliary cannulation while minimizing mechanical and chemical trauma to the major papilla and PD. In this context, guidewire-assisted cannulation is recommended over contrast-guided cannulation by both ESGE and ASGE, as it improves the success of biliary access and reduces the risk of PEP.2,80 This technique, now widely adopted by most endoscopists, involves advancing a guidewire prior to contrast injection to confirm biliary access, thereby minimizing pancreatic irritation from contrast media.87,88

Two technical variants of guidewire-assisted cannulation have been described. In the first method, the sphincterotome or catheter is first inserted into the papilla, followed by advancement of the guidewire to confirm adequate ductal entry. In the second method, the guidewire is protruded 1 to 2 mm from the catheter tip and the entire unit is advanced into the papilla together.88 To date, there have been no head-to-head studies comparing these two methods. A meta-analysis of 15 RCTs reported that guidewire-assisted cannulation reduces PEP risk by approximately 50% compared with contrast-guided cannulation.33 Notably, subgroup analyses showed that trials restricting prophylactic PS use demonstrated a significant reduction in PEP risk, whereas trials allowing PS placement did not. Moreover, trials employing the catheter-then-wire technique showed a 70% reduction in PEP risk compared with contrast-assisted approaches (RR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.18 to 0.49; I2 = 0), while trials using the wire-then-catheter method did not show significant benefit (RR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.39 to 1.13; I2=43%). Accordingly, the ASGE recommends a “cannulate-then-advance-wire” approach.80 There is currently no established association between guidewire caliber or tip configuration and the risk of PEP.85,89,90

6. Combinational approaches of various PEP prevention methods

Rectal administration of NSAIDs is the most well-established and cost-effective strategy for preventing PEP, and is currently the most widely adopted prophylactic strategy.81,91 To improve efficacy, various combination approaches have been explored using rectal NSAIDs as a backbone method. However, current ASGE and ESGE do not recommend routine combination prophylaxis in all patients, citing insufficient evidence to support generalized adoption.2,12,80 Nevertheless, accumulating data from recent large-scale RCTs and network meta-analyses have demonstrated potential benefit in combining rectal NSAIDs with other strategies.58,79,82,92-103

Among these, combining rectal NSAIDs with IV hydration is the most extensively studied. In a four-arm RCT including high-risk patients showed that pre-ERCP hydration with 1 L of LRS plus rectal indomethacin significantly reduced PEP incidence compared to normal saline plus placebo.92 Subsequent network meta-analyses consistently ranked this combination above other single agents and combinations.58,82,94,95,103 In contrast, the FLUYT trial, a multicenter RCT, compared aggressive IV hydration with LRS to standard IV hydration with normal saline alongside rectal NSAIDs. The study found no statistically significant difference in PEP incidence between the two groups (RR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.53 to 1.33; p=0.53).96 These findings suggest that in patients already receiving rectal NSAIDs, the routine use of aggressive hydration may not provide additional preventive benefit. This challenges previous assumptions about the role of aggressive hydration in PEP prevention. Given the potential risks of fluid overload and the lack of added efficacy, a more individualized approach—based on patient comorbidities and early post-procedural markers—may be more appropriate. Reflecting these results, the recently revised IAP guidelines recommend moderate IV hydration with LRS during the periprocedural period, in addition to rectal NSAIDs, to prevent PEP.50 Taken together, these insights underscore a shift away from routine aggressive hydration in patients receiving NSAIDs prophylaxis, reinforcing the importance of individualized strategies rather than uniform protocols.

In another pivotal RCT published in 2024 involving 1,950 high-risk patients, indomethacin alone failed to demonstrate non-inferiority compared with the combination of rectal indomethacin and prophylactic PS placement, with PEP occurring in 14.9% and 11.3% of patients, respectively (risk difference, 3.6%; 95% CI, 0.6 to 6.6; p=0.18 for non-inferiority).100 A post hoc analysis indicated that indomethacin alone was inferior to the combination strategy (p=0.011). Consistent with these findings, the recently revised IAP guidelines recommend that, in high-risk patients, prophylactic pancreatic stenting should be considered in addition to rectal NSAIDs, particularly when inadvertent multiple PD cannulations or contrast injections occur.50 These results underscore the need for dynamic risk reassessment during ERCP: even when rectal NSAIDs are administered pre-procedure, PS placement should be strongly considered if additional risk factors arise during the procedure.

Other pharmacologic combinations with rectal NSAIDs—such as sublingual GTN,93,97 IV somatostatin99 and others. While some studies have reported potential benefit, the evidence remains inconclusive, and further trials are needed to clarify their role in clinical practice.

7. Emerging ML approaches for PEP risk prediction

Several prediction models for PEP have been introduced, and a systematic review of 19 models reported area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.66 to 0.97.104 Among these, six studies employed machine learning (ML) techniques. Emerging ML-based approaches are reshaping PEP prophylaxis by enabling individualized risk prediction.105-108 These models not only identify novel relationships among known variables—such as patient demographics, clinical indications, and procedural factors—but also incorporate complex data, including radiomic features from endoscopic or fluoroscopic imaging, which are difficult for clinicians to interpret. By enabling more accurate risk stratification, ML models may help guide timely preventive interventions and ultimately improve clinical outcomes following ERCP.

One study by Chen et al.105 incorporated radiomic features of the duodenal papilla into a prediction model alongside clinical and procedural variables. They developed an R-score by quantitatively analyzing papillary features from endoscopic images and combined it with variables such as age, white blood cell count, small papilla, number of cannulation attempts, and use of intraductal ultrasound. This radiomics‐augmented model achieved AUC of 0.83 in training cohort and 0.86 in external validation cohort, suggesting the additive predictive value of subtle radiomic features.

In another study, Xu et al.107 established a multimodal prediction model incorporating clinical variables spanning the pre-, intra-, and peri-ERCP phases, including baseline characteristics, diagnostic factors, procedural techniques, and prophylactic measures. They also included imaging features of the major duodenal papilla. In their model including baseline, diagnostic, and technical factors, the top five predictors were difficult cannulation, prior pancreatitis, SOD, and needle-knife sphincterotomy. When imaging features were added, the model achieved high AUCs of 0.91 (0.86 to 0.96) for internal and 0.93 (0.90 to 0.96) for external validation. When prevention strategy variables were integrated, it increased the AUCs to 0.96 (0.94 to 0.98) and 0.98 (0.96 to 1.00), demonstrating excellent predictive performance.

Real-world application of these models may enable real-time assessment and precise risk stratification across all procedural phases, supporting better counseling, tailored prophylaxis, efficient resource allocation, and improved patient outcomes. Despite these promising results, most ML-based prediction models are not validated outside their study. These studies suggest the potential of ML-driven prediction for PEP and warrant prospective trials to assess their real-world clinical impact.104

DETECTION AND DIAGNOSIS

Diagnosis of PEP is made according to the criteria previously described, which include new or worsened abdominal pain, serum amylase or lipase levels greater than three times the ULN at more than 24 hours after ERCP, and the requirement for admission or prolongation of a planned admission. Therefore, post-procedural clinical assessment and laboratory testing at 24 hours are essential for definitive diagnosis. However, in routine clinical practice, serum amylase and lipase levels are often measured earlier, typically 2 to 6 hours after ERCP. When the enzyme levels at this time point remain less than 1.5 and 4 times the ULN, the negative predictive value for PEP exceeds 90%.43 Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis reported that amylase or lipase levels exceeding three times the ULN at 2 to 4 hours post-ERCP had pooled sensitivity and specificity values of 85.8% and 85.3%, respectively, for predicting PEP.109 Thus, serum amylase or lipase values measured 2 to 6 hours after ERCP contribute to early risk stratification, helping to identify patients who may require close monitoring or preemptive treatment for PEP.110 Conversely, they can reliably support discharge decisions in patients with a low predicted risk of PEP.

MANAGEMENT OF PEP

Initial management of PEP is similar to that of acute pancreatitis. Once PEP is diagnosed, patient should be managed according to the recent clinical practice guidelines for acute pancreatitis which is appropriate to the local and institutional setting.110 Disease severity should be assessed using the revised Atlanta classification and re-evaluated after 48 hours. Adequate fluid resuscitation remains the cornerstone of treatment, with LRS preferred over other IV fluids. A recent well-designed RCT demonstrated that moderate fluid resuscitation (1.5 mL/kg/hr with an optional 10 mL/kg bolus if hypovolemic) was as effective as an aggressive protocol (3 mL/kg/hr with 20 mL/kg bolus), while resulting in fewer cases of fluid overload.111 Therefore, early initiation of moderate resuscitation is recommended. However, fluid administration must be individualized based on patient age, comorbidities, and dynamic volume status. Close monitoring of vital signs, urine output, hematocrit, and blood urea nitrogen levels is important to appropriately adjust fluid resuscitation.112 For nutrition, early oral feeding should be initiated as soon as tolerated. If oral intake is not feasible, enteral nutrition via a nasogastric tube is preferred over parenteral nutrition, as it is associated with lower infection and mortality rates.113 Regarding analgesia, both opioid and non-opioid agents are considered effective, with no significant difference in adverse events; therefore, a step-up approach starting with non-opioids is recommended.114 Lastly, prophylactic antibiotics should not be routinely administered in either mild or severe PEP unless there is clear evidence of bacterial infection, as no clinical benefit has been demonstrated.112

CONCLUSIONS

Despite advances in technique and preventive strategies over the past five decades, PEP remains a significant challenge for endoscopists and continues to impose a non-negligible healthcare burden. However, a growing understanding of PEP pathophysiology and risk factors has enabled the emergence of evidence-based preventive measures—including rectal NSAIDs, PS placement, aggressive IV hydration, and guidewire-assisted cannulation—which offer a promising outlook for reducing PEP incidence. Optimal protocols for each of these interventions still require further investigation, and the potential synergistic effects and cost-effectiveness of combination strategies must be clarified. Effective prevention hinges on accurate risk stratification, with identification of high-risk patients prior to ERCP and ongoing reassessment during and after the procedure. In this regard, ML and artificial intelligence–based prediction models may play an important role in supporting clinical decision-making and integrating risk prediction into real-time workflows. Importantly, because PEP is influenced not only by patient-related factors but also by procedure-dependent factors, structured education on prevention, early detection, and management of PEP should be emphasized throughout endoscopic training programs. In this context, we propose an integrated algorithm that summarizes preventive, diagnostic, and management strategies to support both clinical practice and training (Fig. 1). Multidisciplinary approaches between endoscopists, radiologists, and pancreatology teams will also be essential for optimizing outcomes.
