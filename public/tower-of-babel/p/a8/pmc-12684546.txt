Contemporary Practice Patterns of Vasoactive Use in Cardiogenic Shock in the American Heart Association Cardiogenic Shock Registry
Authors: Siddharth M Patel, David D Berg, Erin A Bohula, Michael G Palazzolo, Wayne B Batchelor, Stavros G Drakos, Arman Kilic, Robert L Kormos, Marc D Samsky, Mariell Jessup, Mitchell W Krucoff, David A Morrow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVasoactives are the predominant first line of therapy for management of cardiogenic shock (CS). Contemporary practice patterns regarding vasoactive agent selection in CS have not been well characterized.MethodsThe American Heart Association (AHA) CS Registry captures consecutive CS admissions across participating hospitals in the United States. Admissions treated with vasoactive agents within 6 hours of CS onset were included. Agents were categorized as inopressors (norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine), inodilators (dobutamine, milrinone), and pure vasopressors (vasopressin, phenylephrine).ResultsFrom 2022 to 2024, among 6847 CS admissions across 84 sites, 3387 (49.5%) were treated with a single vasoactive agent and 3460 (50.5%) were treated with ≥2 agents. Inopressors were used most commonly (73.7% of CS cases), with inodilators and pure vasopressors used in 48.3% and 27.5% of cases respectively. Norepinephrine was used most frequently (64.7%), followed by dobutamine (31.6%) and epinephrine (28.9%). Pure vasopressor use consisted primarily of vasopressin (83.5% of use). Use patterns differed by CS etiology with greater inopressor use in acute myocardial infarction‐CS and greater inodilator use in acute‐on‐chronic heart failure‐CS. Use of inopressors and pure vasopressors was greater and inodilator use lower in those with cardiac arrest, supported by mechanical circulatory support, or at higher Society of Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions shock stage.ConclusionsIn a broad population with CS in contemporary practice, inopressors are the most commonly used vasoactive category, with norepinephrine being the most frequently used agent. Several factors including CS etiology and severity are associated with differential practice patterns for vasoactive selection. These data depict the contemporary landscape and may help inform future evidence generation around optimal vasoactive selection for patients with CS.Keywords: acute heart failure, cardiogenic shock, inotropes, vasopressorsSubject Categories: Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiac Care, Heart Failure

Cardiogenic shock (CS) is a syndrome characterized by insufficient cardiac output resulting in end‐organ hypoperfusion. Vasoactive agents, typically vasopressors or inotropes, have traditionally been the first line of therapy to restore and maintain systemic perfusion in CS. However, no adequately sized randomized trials have rigorously demonstrated the beneficial effects of one agent over another with respect to major clinical outcomes in this population. 1 Thus, decision making regarding vasoactive agent selection is based on clinician judgment, in general guided by the anticipated physiologic effects of each agent and the specific clinical context (eg, underlying etiology of CS, early escalation to concomitant mechanical circulatory support [MCS], or presence of pulmonary hypertension). 2 , 3 A better understanding of practice patterns and the current landscape of vasoactive agent use in CS may help to inform future evidence development to guide optimal selection of vasoactive agents. The American Heart Association (AHA) CS Registry is a novel data set that offers a unique opportunity to characterize contemporary practice patterns of vasoactive use in CS across a broad range of sites and clinicians caring for patients with CS in the United States.

METHODS

Data Source and Study Population

The design and methods of the AHA CS Registry have been described. 4 Briefly, participation is open to all hospitals in the United States caring for patients with CS. Sites participating in the AHA CS Registry collect data on all consecutive hospitalized patients with CS. The AHA CS Registry protocol and waiver of informed consent is approved by a central institutional review board (Advarra) and the institutional review board at each of the participating centers, where required. The diagnosis of CS is investigator‐determined consistent with the Shock Academic Research Consortium definition of CS. 4 , 5 Admissions captured within the AHA CS Registry from 2022 to 2024 across 84 sites were used for this analysis. Patients included in this analysis were those presenting with CS and treated with ≥1 vasoactive agents within the first 6 hours of CS onset. We encourage parties interested in collaboration and data sharing to contact the AHA CS Registry directly for further discussions. 4

Vasoactive Agent Capture and Categorization

Vasoactive agents used within the first 6 hours of CS onset were captured prospectively using the electronic case record form and included the following: norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, dobutamine, milrinone, vasopressin and phenylephrine. Pure vasodilators (eg, nitroprusside) were not included in this analysis. Each agent was analyzed individually and by vasoactive category: inopressor, inodilator, or pure vasopressor. For this analysis, norepinephrine, epinephrine, and dopamine were categorized as inopressors; dobutamine and milrinone as inodilators; and vasopressin and phenylephrine as pure vasopressors. 1 , 2 , 3 Data capture reflects all agents used collectively within the first 6‐hour period from CS onset, inclusive of cross‐titration from one agent to another or combination use during this time frame. Serial assessment of vasoactive medications at 24 hours was captured in a subset of patients.

Statistical Analysis

Use of vasoactive agents is reported as the proportion of CS cases using an individual vasoactive agent or vasoactive category. Subgroups of interest for this analysis included patients with preceding cardiac arrest, Shock Academic Research Consortium CS subtypes (acute myocardial infarction [AMI]‐CS, de novo heart failure [HF]‐CS, acute‐on‐chronic HF‐CS), 5 CS severity as assessed by the Society of Cardiovascular Angiography and Intervention (SCAI) shock stage, 6 concomitant MCS use, and presence of severe pulmonary hypertension at baseline. To allow for stability of vasoactive selection around MCS insertion and removal, the concomitant MCS subgroup was defined as patients with MCS insertion at the time of or within 1 hour of CS onset and remaining in place for the full 6 hour duration from CS onset. A supplementary analysis was performed in the subset of patients with serial assessment at 24 hours, with the concomitant MCS subgroup at 24 hours being defined as those having MCS insertion at any time from 0 to 23 hours after CS onset and remaining in place at the 24 hours assessment. Sensitivity analyses were performed excluding patients with cardiac arrest. All analyses in this study are descriptive, with risk ratios (RRs) and corresponding 95% CIs reported to describe relative differences in vasoactive use across subgroups. All analyses were performed using SAS system v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Overall Vasoactive Use Patterns

A total of 6847 cases presenting with CS were treated with ≥1 vasoactive agents within the first 6 hours of CS onset (Table S1). Among these admissions, 3387 (49.5%) were treated with a single vasoactive agent and 3460 (50.5%) were treated with ≥2 agents.

Inopressors were the most commonly used category of vasoactive agents, used in 73.7% (n=5048) of admissions with CS (Figure 1). Among inopressors, norepinephrine was used most frequently (64.7%; n=4431) followed by epinephrine (28.9%; n=1982), whereas dopamine use was infrequent (6.4%; n=438). Inodilators were used in approximately half of CS cases (48.3%; n=3312), with dobutamine being more commonly used (31.6%; n=2167) than milrinone (20.1%; n=1379) (Figure 1); 23.4% (n=1603) of all CS cases or 48.4% of those treated with an inodilator were treated solely with an inodilator regimen. Use of pure vasopressors (27.5%; n=1882) consisted primarily of vasopressin (83.5% of pure vasopressor use), which was almost always in combination with other agents (97.8% of vasopressin used with other agents) (Figure 1); phenylephrine use was rare (7.3%).

Examined as a proportion of all CS cases, the most common combinations used within the first 6 hours were norepinephrine‐epinephrine (22.3%; n=1529), norepinephrine‐vasopressin (20.0%; n=1368), and norepinephrine‐dobutamine (15.3%; n=1049) (Figure 2).

Vasoactive Use Patterns Across Key Subgroups

Use of the individual agents and categories of agents differed when stratified by preceding cardiac arrest, as well as CS etiology and severity. After cardiac arrest, there was greater use of inopressors, particularly epinephrine, and pure vasopressors than among cases without preceding cardiac arrest (Figure S1 and Table S2).

Compared with AMI‐CS, cases with acute‐on‐chronic HF‐CS had greater use of inodilators (70.8% versus 34.1%; RR, 2.08 [95% CI, 1.94–2.22]), with lower use of inopressors (52.2% versus 85.7%; RR, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.58–0.64]) and pure vasopressors (17.9% versus 29.2%; RR, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.55–0.68]) (Figure 3A and Table S3). These patterns were consistent after excluding cases with cardiac arrest (Figure S2A and Table S4). Examining the individual vasoactive agents, norepinephrine was used most frequently across all 3 CS subtypes; however, dobutamine was the second most frequently used in acute‐on‐chronic HF‐CS (43.7% of cases) compared with epinephrine in those with AMI‐CS and de novo HF‐CS (32.2% and 33.9%, respectively) (Figure 4). The most common combination of vasoactives was norepinephrine‐epinephrine in both AMI‐CS and de novo HF‐CS (25.2% and 27.5%, respectively), and norepinephrine‐dobutamine (15.7%) in acute‐on‐chronic HF‐CS (Figure S3). Among those with AMI‐CS, 11.3% were treated solely with an inodilator regimen, compared with 45.7% and 17.3% of admissions presenting with acute‐on‐chronic and de novo HF‐CS, respectively.

With higher SCAI CS stage, there was greater use of inopressors (up to 96.7% from 69.8%; RR, 1.26 [95% CI, 1.23–1.30] for SCAI stage D/E versus stage C) and pure vasopressors (51.4% from 21.0%; RR, 2.13 [95% CI, 1.95–2.32]) with lower use of inodilators (26.6% from 50.6%; RR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.77–0.88]) (Figure 3B). After excluding cases with cardiac arrest, these patterns were generally consistent with greater inopressor and pure vasopressor use at higher SCAI stages (RR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.19–1.29] and 2.07 [95% CI, 1.85–2.30] for stage D/E versus C, respectively); however, inodilator use was more comparable across SCAI stages in cases without arrest (RR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.89–1.02]) (Figure S2B).

Among cases receiving concomitant MCS, there was greater use of inopressors (88.1% versus 70.8%; RR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.20–1.29]) and pure vasopressors (40.1% versus 25.2%; RR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.42–1.78]) with lower use of inodilators (40.1% versus 50.3%; RR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.72–0.89]) compared with those without concomitant MCS (Figure 3C). Compared with cases without pulmonary hypertension, cases with pulmonary hypertension had greater inodilator use (62.5% versus 47.1%; RR, 1.33 [95% CI, 1.24–1.42]) and lower inopressor use (61.5% versus 74.8%; RR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.77–0.88]), without clear differences in use of pure vasopressors (24.5% versus 27.7%; RR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.76–1.03]) (Figure 3D).

Serial Assessment at 24 Hours

A total of 1769 CS cases were treated with vasoactives within the first 6 hours of CS onset with serial assessment performed at 24 hours. A greater proportion of cases were supported with inodilators and pure vasopressors (predominantly vasopressin) at 24 hours compared with the initial 6 hours period (inodilator: 61.2% versus 52.2%; pure vasopressor: 30.0% versus 24.3% with vasopressin 28.5% versus 20.9%), with 50.9% being supported with a sole vasoactive agent and 49.1% being supported by a combination of agents (Figure S4). Stratifying by concomitant MCS status at 24 hours, inopressor and pure vasopressor use remained more common among those with concomitant MCS (RR, 1.35 [95% CI, 1.27–1.45] and 1.78 [95% CI, 1.53–2.06]) with lower inodilator use (RR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.80–0.99]) compared with those without (Figure S5).

DISCUSSION

In this analysis leveraging the newly created AHA CS Registry, we examined contemporary patterns of vasoactive agent selection in CS across 84 sites and >6800 cases with CS, reflecting a broad range of institutions and health care providers caring for patients with CS in the United States. Inopressors were the most frequently used category of vasoactives, with norepinephrine being the most used agent in CS. Importantly, however, selection of vasoactive agents differed in the context of several patient‐ and shock‐related phenotypes. Specifically, we observed greater use of inodilator‐based regimens in HF‐CS, particularly among cases with acute‐on‐chronic HF‐CS, whereas there was markedly greater use of inopressors in cases with AMI‐CS. We further observed differential vasoactive selection by SCAI CS stage, use of concomitant MCS, presence of pulmonary hypertension, and in those with cardiac arrest complicating the CS presentation. Collectively, these data characterize the contemporary landscape of vasoactive use in CS and may help to inform future research directed at processes of care and randomized trials of these therapies in CS.

Prior Data on Vasoactive Use in CS

Although there exist studies describing the prognostic associations of different vasoactive agents or of dosing intensity in CS, 7 , 8 , 9 contemporary practice patterns regarding vasoactive agent selection in CS have not been well described. In a single‐center study of consecutive patients admitted to the Mayo Clinic cardiac intensive care unit from 2007 to 2015, dopamine was the most commonly used vasoactive agent, used in approximately half of all patients, followed by norepinephrine and epinephrine in just over a quarter of all patients each; notably, however, only 1 in 4 patients had a diagnosis of CS as assessed by discharge diagnostic codes without further data reported for this subgroup. 10 More recent descriptions are limited to randomized trials of MCS in CS. In the ECMO‐CS (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation in the Therapy of Cardiogenic Shock) trial of 122 patients with refractory or worsening CS from 2014 to 2022 enrolled in 4 centers in the Czech Republic, norepinephrine was the most commonly used vasoactive agent in >80% of patients irrespective of treatment arm, with dobutamine used in just over half of all patients. 11 In the DanGer Shock (Danish–German Cardiogenic Shock) trial of 360 patients with AMI‐CS from 2013 to 2023 predominantly from 2 countries in Europe, norepinephrine was used in >80% of all patients, followed by epinephrine, dobutamine and milrinone used in approximately a third of patients each. 12 Expanding upon these smaller institution‐, trial‐, or CS subtype‐ specific analyses, this study from the AHA CS Registry characterizes the contemporary epidemiology of vasoactive selection in CS across a wide array of patients presenting with CS irrespective of baseline medical history, CS etiology, shock severity, or other concomitant CS‐directed therapy. Moreover, our findings reflect a broad range of institutions and clinicians providing care for patients with CS in day‐to‐day practice within the United States, encompassing sites with wide variability in both the availability of temporary MCS devices as well as advanced therapies.

Contextualizing Findings Based on Current Evidence for Vasoactive Therapies in CS

To date, no large, adequately powered, randomized trials have been conducted to examine the beneficial effects of one vasoactive agent over another in CS. 13 , 14 , 15 In an analysis of the SOAP‐II (Sepsis Occurrence in Acutely Ill Patients II) trial comparing dopamine to norepinephrine in patients with shock irrespective of subtype among the small subgroup with CS (n=280), there was suggestion of a possible mortality benefit and fewer atrial arrhythmias with norepinephrine, though the overall trial was neutral and there was no evidence of statistically significant effect modification by shock subtype. 13 With respect to inodilator therapy, the DOREMI (Dobutamine Compared with Milrinone) trial randomized 192 patients with CS to milrinone or dobutamine, finding no significant difference for a broad primary composite outcome that included all‐cause mortality, resuscitated cardiac arrest, escalation to MCS or heart transplantation, renal replacement therapy, myocardial infarction, or stroke/transient ischemic attack. 15

In the absence of robust randomized data, current professional society recommendations for vasoactive management in CS vary, with some guidelines not favoring a specific agent and others endorsing the use of norepinephrine or dobutamine as the first‐line vasoactive medication in CS. 3 For example, the AHA scientific statement for CS management suggests use of norepinephrine or dopamine, 2 whereas the 2022 American College of Cardiology/AHA HF guidelines note a class IB recommendation for intravenous inotropic support in CS, with the specific agent to be decided by the clinician based on blood pressure, concurrent arrhythmias, and drug availability. 16 Despite the lack of a clear recommendation for any single agent across society documents, we found that dopamine use was quite rare—a finding that, despite the extremely sparse outcomes data, may reflect the lack of perceived equipoise after SOAP‐II.

Notably, despite the experimental evidence for adverse effects of catecholamines, we observed that inopressors are the most frequently used category of continuous intravenous vasoactive therapies, particularly among patients with AMI‐CS, a cohort in whom increased adrenergic stimulation and afterload may be especially disadvantageous. 3 Moreover, we observed greater use of inopressors or pure vasopressors in patients with greater shock severity and in those with a preceding arrest, a finding that may be driven by the presence of not only depressed cardiac output but also concomitant vasoplegia in these subgroups. 17 , 18 , 19 By the same token, in patients with concomitant MCS, there was greater use of inopressors or pure vasopressors, perhaps indicative of residual vasoplegia despite device‐related augmentation or even normalization of cardiac output.

Collectively, these findings underscore the need for further investigation into the relative efficacy and safety of different vasoactive agents among these phenotypically distinct CS subtypes, ideally with prospective randomized trials.

Examining the most frequently used combinations of vasoactives, we found that norepinephrine was common among all combinations, with the top 3 being norepinephrine‐epinephrine, norepinephrine‐vasopressin, and norepinephrine‐dobutamine. These findings suggest that clinicians commonly use norepinephrine as a first‐line agent, with variability in selection of the secondary vasoactive type (inopressor, pure vasopressor, inodilator) driven by the manifesting shock physiology. To this end, we observed greater use of norepinephrine‐dobutamine in those presenting with acute‐on‐chronic HF‐CS, a phenotype that may more commonly present with depressed CO but with preserved vascular tone, compared with greater use of norepinephrine‐epinephrine in those with AMI‐CS or de novo HF‐CS, both subtypes in which acute end‐organ insults without protective adaptations may be more likely to result in a concomitant loss of vascular tone and ensuing hypotension. 17 , 18

Limitations

Assessment of vasoactive agents in this analysis was limited to the first 6 hours of CS onset; thus, assessment of vasoactive agents over other durations or at serial time points may be informative. Given that vasoactive assessment is reflective of all agents used within the first 6 hours of CS onset, we are unable to assess sequencing and whether specific agents or combinations of agents were used in a mutually exclusive fashion over this duration. The observational design of this registry analysis does not support comparative evaluation of efficacy and thus the observations describe the landscape of practice but not necessarily optimal therapy. The AHA CS Registry is limited to sites within the United States; practice patterns in other regions of the world may differ.

CONCLUSIONS

In a broad population with CS in the AHA CS Registry, inopressors are the most frequently used category of vasoactive agents, with norepinephrine being the most commonly used agent. Multiple factors, including CS etiology and shock severity, are associated with differential use of vasoactive agents in this population. These data may help to inform future research examining optimal vasoactive selection in CS.
