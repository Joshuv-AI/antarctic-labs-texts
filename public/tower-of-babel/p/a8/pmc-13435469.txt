Associations between oral migraine preventive regimens and CGRP monoclonal antibody initiation: real-world evidence from a resource-limited setting
Authors: Sekh Thanprasertsuk, Akarin Hiransuthikul, Sirawit Roongrojwittayakul, Thanakit Pongpitakmetha, Wanakorn Rattanawong, Prakit Anukoolwittaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCalcitonin gene–related peptide monoclonal antibodies (CGRP mAbs) are effective migraine-specific preventive therapies; however, access remains limited in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In such settings, oral migraine preventive medications (OMPMs) continue to serve as the foundation of preventive care. The association between baseline preventive regimens and real-world escalation to CGRP mAbs has not been well characterized.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of adults with migraine attending a university-based headache clinic in Thailand between January 2021 and December 2023. Participants with at least one baseline OMPM, no prior CGRP mAb exposure, and at least 6 months of follow-up were included. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, adjusted for age, sex, and migraine subtype, were used to examine associations between baseline OMPM classes and CGRP mAb initiation. A secondary analysis evaluated factors associated with discontinuation of baseline OMPMs.ResultsAmong 80 participants, 16 (20.0%) initiated CGRP mAbs over 11,554 person-days of follow-up, with a median time to initiation of 44.5 days. Baseline use of beta-blockers (BBs) or tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) was independently associated with a lower likelihood of CGRP mAb initiation (BB: adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 0.17, 95% CI 0.04–0.66; TCA: aHR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.57). In secondary analyses, baseline BB use was also associated with greater persistence with preventive therapy (aHR for discontinuation 0.24, 95% CI 0.09–0.64).ConclusionsBaseline oral preventive regimens were associated with differing likelihoods of switching to CGRP mAbs in this LMIC setting. Optimizing oral preventive treatment strategies remains essential in settings where access to CGRP-targeted therapies is limited, either due to unavailability or lack of reimbursement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05030-0.

Background

Migraine is among the leading causes of disability worldwide and disproportionately affects working-aged adults [1, 2]. In Thailand and other Asian Countries, migraine imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden through reduced productivity, frequent healthcare utilization, and impaired quality of life [3]. Preventive treatment is therefore an important measure of migraine management for individuals with frequent or disabling attacks, as it can reduce headache frequency, severity, and reliance on acute medications [4].

In real-world practice, especially in resource-limited settings, oral migraine preventive medications (OMPMs) remain the foundation of preventive care. These include several drug classes, such as angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), antiseizure medications (ASMs), beta-blockers (BBs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) [5–8]. Although not originally developed for migraine, they are widely prescribed because of their accessibility, affordability, and reimbursability within public healthcare systems or private insurance. For many individuals with financial constraints, OMPMs represent the only feasible long-term preventive option, making optimization of these regimens clinically and economically critical. However, their use is often limited by suboptimal efficacy, tolerability issues, and poor long-term persistence [9, 10].

Over the past decade, therapies targeting the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) pathway, particularly CGRP monoclonal antibodies (CGRP mAbs), have revolutionized migraine prevention by offering mechanism-based efficacy with favorable safety profiles in both episodic and chronic migraine [11]. Despite strong clinical evidence, access to CGRP mAbs in Thailand remains highly restricted. These agents are not reimbursed by the national healthcare systems and are rarely covered by private insurance, leaving most patients to pay entirely out-of-pocket. In addition, CGRP mAbs are not available in many countries, particularly low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [12].

Treatment decisions for migraine prevention often follow a stepwise escalation strategy, with clinicians and patients aiming to maximize benefit from OMPMs before considering CGRP-targeted therapy. Previous research from our group has characterized treatment patterns and discontinuation rates of OMPMs in Thailand, highlighting challenges related to medication underuse and poor persistence in real-world practice [10]. However, factors influencing whether, and how quickly, patients transition from OMPMs to CGRP mAbs remain poorly understood, particularly in LMIC settings where cost and reimbursement barriers are prominent. Understanding these factors could help identify regimen characteristics associated with delayed or reduced access to effective migraine-specific therapies. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between baseline OMPM use and subsequent CGRP mAb initiation at a tertiary headache clinic in Thailand. Specifically, we sought to identify baseline OMPM regimens associated with the likelihood and timing of CGRP mAb initiation. Secondly, we examined whether specific OMPM classes were associated with persistence of preventive therapy, as reflected by discontinuation of baseline oral medications during follow-up.

Methods

Study design and participants

A single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted on the database of individuals living with migraine who attended the Comprehensive Headache and Orofacial Pain (CHOP) clinic at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital (KCMH), The Thai Red Cross Society, Bangkok, Thailand. Briefly, the clinic is overseen by headache specialists, or have a special interest in headache, and all headache-related information at each clinic visit is documented using a structured, pre-planned system to ensure consistent and systematic data collection. Notably, this tertiary referral clinic is enriched for patients with more severe and complex migraine disorders, including chronic migraine and medication overuse headache (MOH).

In accordance with the primary objective of evaluating initiation of CGRP mAbs, we included data from individuals who attended the clinic between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023. This period was selected because erenumab, the first CGRP mAb, became available at the study site in late 2019, shortly after its approval by the Thai Food and Drug Administration. The prescription of CGRP mAbs was prescribed following the 2022 Thai Migraine Guideline, which recommends CGRP mAbs for patients with migraine who have at least 8 monthly headache days (MHDs) and show insufficient effectiveness or intolerance to at least two classes of conventional OMPMs for at least 6 weeks, including TCAs, ASMs, BBs, or CCBs [5]. Eligible participants were adults (aged ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with migraine according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) criteria [13], who were receiving at least one OMPM at baseline, had never used a CGRP mAb prior to cohort entry, and had a minimum follow-up duration of 6 months after their initial visit to the clinic. Those who lost to follow-up for more than 3 consecutive months or having less than 3 follow-up visits in the 6 months period were excluded.

Standard protocol approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand (Certificate of Approval No. 1156/2024). The approval of retrieving retrospective data was made and informed consent was waived by the IRB. All methods were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study definitions and outcomes

At baseline, we extracted data on demographic characteristics, comorbidities, migraine subtype (episodic or chronic), headache features, number of abortive medication uses, history of MOH, and concurrent use of OMPM(s). OMPMs were categorized into the following classes: ARBs (candesartan); ASMs (lamotrigine, sodium valproate, topiramate, zonisamide); BBs (metoprolol, propranolol); CCBs (flunarizine); SNRIs (desvenlafaxine, duloxetine, venlafaxine); and TCAs (amitriptyline, nortriptyline). At each follow-up visit, we recorded MHDs, number of abortive medication uses, changes in preventive medications including CGRP mAb initiation, and discontinuation of baseline preventive therapy.

The primary outcomes were: (1) the cumulative incidence of CGRP mAb initiation, defined as the first prescription of any CGRP mAb during follow-up; and (2) the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of baseline OMPM regimens associated with CGRP mAb initiation. These outcomes were used to assess both the timing and likelihood of transitioning from OMPMs to CGRP mAbs within the first six months after entry into specialized headache care. Secondary outcomes included the cumulative incidence of participants discontinuing at least one baseline OMPM during follow-up, as well as the association between baseline OMPM regimens and the probability of OMPM discontinuation.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Comparisons between participants who eventually initiated CGRP mAbs and those who did not, as well as between those who discontinued at least one OMPM and those who did not, were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate the cumulative incidence of CGRP mAb initiation over time. Because many participants were receiving multiple preventive medication classes concurrently, exposure groups were not mutually exclusive. Therefore, the comparisons between OMPM classes reflected the presence or absence of a given OMPM class in the overlapping preventive regimens rather than comparisons between distinct monotherapy treatment groups. The log-rank test was used to compare initiation rates across groups defined by baseline OMPM class (each class vs. all others). Two separate multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted to evaluate the association between baseline OMPM regimens and (1) CGRP mAb initiation and (2) discontinuation of any OMPM, with both models adjusted for age, sex, and migraine subtype. Given the limited number of outcome events, the multivariable models were intentionally restricted to a small number of clinically relevant covariates to reduce the risk of model overfitting. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and no significant violations were identified for the primary exposure variables. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using Stata/SE version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 80 eligible participants, the median age was 40.0 years (IQR 28.4–51.9), and 71 of them (88.8%) were female. Nearly half of the participants had chronic migraine (47.5%) and over half had MOH (54.4%). Median baseline headache frequency was 12 days/month (IQR 8.0–16.0). The most commonly prescribed OMPM classes were BBs (46.2%), TCAs (35.0%), and ASMs (22.5%). No participants were using ARBs at baseline. Twenty-five participants (31.2%) were receiving more than two preventive medications at their first clinic visit (Table 1).

Incidence and association of baseline OMPM on CGRP mAbs initiation

Over a total follow-up of 11,554 person-days, 16 participants (20.0%) initiated CGRP mAbs, at a median of 44.5 days (IQR 23.5–109.0). Baseline characteristics were comparable between participants who initiated CGRP mAbs and those who did not, except for the significantly higher proportion of those receiving SNRIs among those who eventually initiated CGRP mAbs (37.5% vs. 9.4%, p = 0.005).

The rates of CGRP mAbs initiation were significantly lower among those receiving BBs compared with those not receiving BBs (2.7% vs. 11.6% by month 1, 5.3% vs. 16.5% by month 3, and 5.3% vs. 34.3% by month 6; p = 0.01). Similarly, participants receiving TCAs had significantly lower CGRP mAbs initiation rates compared with those not receiving TCAs (0% vs. 12.5% by month 1, 3.0% vs. 16.8% by month 3, and 6.5% vs. 28.8% by month 6; p = 0.02). In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, having BBs or TCAs as part of the baseline OMPM regimen was independently associated with a lower probability of initiating CGRP mAbs during follow-up (BBs: aHR 0.17, 95% CI 0.04–0.66, p = 0.01; TCAs: aHR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.57, p = 0.01) (Figs. 1 and 2). However, these estimates were derived from only 16 initiation events and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Incidence and association of baseline OMPM with discontinuation of any baseline OMPM

During the study period, 26 participants (32.5%) discontinued at least one baseline OMPM, at a median of 51.5 days (IQR 28.0–103.0). Compared with participants who did not discontinue any baseline OMPM, those who discontinued at least one baseline OMPM were significantly more likely to be receiving SNRIs (34.6% vs. 5.6%, p < 0.001) and to be using more than two preventive medications at baseline (61.5% vs. 16.7%, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S1).

Participants receiving BBs had significantly lower rates of discontinuation of any baseline OMPM compared with those not receiving beta blockers (10.9% vs. 11.7% by month 1, 13.4% vs. 30.8% by month 3, and 19.4% vs. 45.6% by month 6; p = 0.02). In multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, having BBs as part of the baseline OMPM regimen was associated with a lower probability of discontinuation of any baseline OMPM during follow-up (aHR 0.24, 95% CI 0.09–0.64, p = 0.004) (Fig. 3). These estimates were based on 26 discontinuation events and should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

In this real-world cohort from a tertiary headache clinic in Thailand, baseline OMPM choice at the first specialist visit was associated with subsequent treatment trajectories, including escalation to CGRP mAbs and persistence with preventive therapy. There were two principal findings from the analyses. First, the use of BBs or TCAs at baseline was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of CGRP mAb initiation within the first six months of follow-up. Second, BB use was associated with greater persistence with preventive medications, as reflected by a lower risk of discontinuation of any baseline OMPM.

The observed association between BB or TCA use and reduced CGRP mAb initiation likely reflects a combination of clinical, contextual, and system-level factors. In Thailand and many other LMIC settings, BBs and TCAs are inexpensive, widely available, and long embedded in routine migraine care [14]. Although formal studies examining clinician prescribing familiarity in Thailand are lacking, this pattern likely reflects long-standing clinical experience and comfort with these agents, as observed by the authors in routine practice. Such familiarity may encourage clinicians to optimize dosing and duration of these medications before considering escalation to newer, costly therapies such as CGRP mAbs.

In addition, BBs and TCAs are frequently selected in patients with relevant comorbidities, including insomnia, anxiety, palpitations, or tachycardia [15–17]. Importantly, these conditions are not only common in people with migraine, but they may also aggravate migraine [18–20]. TCAs can help improve sleep and mood problems [21], while BBs may mitigate sympathetic autonomic symptoms such as tachycardia, palpitations, and anxiety-related physiological responses [22]. Thus, these agents may confer a dual benefit: a direct preventive effect on migraine and an indirect benefit through attenuation of aggravating factors. This multidimensional clinical impact may contribute to greater perceived overall treatment benefit in some patients and potentially reduce the perceived need for escalation to CGRP-targeted therapies.

Although BBs and TCAs were associated with a lower likelihood of CGRP mAb initiation, a non-negligible proportion of patients in these groups still escalated to CGRP-targeted therapy. This finding likely reflects the clinical reality of a tertiary headache clinic, where a substantial proportion of patients have more severe or treatment-refractory disease. Thus, even when initial preventive regimens provide partial clinical benefit or acceptable tolerability in routine practice, escalation may still occur in patients with persistent disability or unmet treatment goals.

Another important finding of the study was that BB use was associated with greater persistence with preventive medications during follow-up. This finding aligns with our prior work showing that certain preventive agents are associated with lower discontinuation rates in a period of six months, while others, particularly SNRIs and CCBs, are more frequently discontinued due to tolerability issues [10]. In real-world practice, treatment persistence is often d by a balance between perceived benefit and side effects, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously as a surrogate marker rather than a direct measure of treatment efficacy or tolerability. Because detailed measures of treatment efficacy and adverse events were not systematically captured in this study, we were unable to distinguish whether persistence with specific medication classes was driven primarily by effectiveness, tolerability, or both. Although TCAs and BBs belong to different pharmacologic classes, the consistency lies in the broader concept that medications with long-standing clinical experience, widely used in various medical conditions, and generally acceptable tolerability profiles reported in prior studies may be associated with better short-term adherence in real-world practice [23, 24].

From a clinical perspective, these findings support the pragmatic message that, when clinically indicated, clinicians should not hesitate to initiate BBs or TCAs when they are suitable for the individual patient. In resource-limited settings where access to CGRP mAbs is constrained, thoughtful selection and optimization of OMPM(s) remain essential components of high-quality migraine care. Importantly, this approach should not be interpreted as discouraging the use of CGRP mAbs when indicated. On the contrary, prior real-world studies from our country and our region have demonstrated that CGRP mAbs are effective and clinically meaningful but remain underutilized due to access barriers [25–27]. Within this context, optimizing oral preventive regimens represents a necessary and pragmatic step in migraine management, rather than a competing strategy to CGRP-targeted therapy.

From another viewpoint, our results also raise the possibility that treatment sequencing and prescribing patterns may contribute to the observed associations with CGRP mAb initiation. Based on clinical practice patterns in Thailand, BBs and TCAs are often prescribed earlier in the preventive pathway, whereas ASMs or SNRIs may be introduced after failure of one or more prior therapies. Based on our results, SNRI use was more common among participants who subsequently initiated CGRP mAbs, while the use of SNRI and the use of more than two preventive medications at baseline was more frequent among those who discontinued at least one baseline OMPM during follow-up. Together, these patterns may reflect a subgroup of patients with more treatment-refractory disease [10], and therefore have a higher likelihood of subsequent CGRP mAb initiation. While this interpretation cannot be definitively established in a retrospective design, it underscores the importance of considering prior treatment history and clinical context when interpreting real-world escalation patterns.

In addition, the decision to initiate CGRP mAbs in this study was based on routine clinical judgment in a tertiary headache clinic, rather than predefined protocolized criteria. In general, escalation to CGRP-targeted therapy in our setting reflects a combination of factors, including inadequate response to prior preventive medications, persistent disability, patient preference, and financial feasibility. The absence of standardized switching criteria is consistent with real-world practice but may introduce variability in treatment pathways.

Access considerations further contextualize our findings. We have a considerable unique context in Thailand as multiple CGRP-targeted therapies, including all CGRP mAbs and one gepant, are available but generally not reimbursable. Consequently, escalation to CGRP mAbs often occurs only after multiple OMPM failures or in patients with sufficient financial support. Although access barriers likely contribute to treatment decisions in Thailand, baseline preventive regimens were also associated with both the likelihood and timing of escalation to CGRP mAbs.

Some limitations should be considered. First, there is potential selection bias that may limit generalizability, as all participants were recruited from a specialist tertiary referral headache clinic enriched for individuals with more severe and complex migraine, as evidenced by the fact that nearly half had chronic migraine and more than half experienced MOH. In addition, the requirement for at least three visits within a six-month period likely enriched patients with higher healthcare engagement and greater treatment complexity. Second, follow-up was limited to a minimum of six months, precluding assessment of longer-term treatment trajectories. Importantly, this study was not designed to evaluate the efficacy of individual preventive medications, and the relatively short follow-up period may not be sufficient to capture the full therapeutic effect of certain OMPM(s), which often require gradual dose titration and longer observation periods. As such, whether BBs or TCAs confer sustained long-term efficacy beyond the early treatment phase could not be determined. Lastly, the relatively small sample size, together with the retrospective data collection, may have limited our ability to perform additional analyses, particularly those examining treatment effectiveness and adverse events, as well as potential reduced statistical power to detect modest associations for certain medication classes. In addition, the low number of outcome events led the multivariable models to be restricted to a limited number of covariates to reduce the risk of overfitting. Consequently, residual confounding by factors such as comorbidities, MOH, number of prior preventive failures, and treatment sequencing remains possible and should temper causal interpretation. Furthermore, ARBs were not represented in the baseline cohort, likely reflecting their more recent incorporation into migraine prevention guidelines and the retrospective nature of data collection spanning earlier years. Despite these limitations, we achieved our primary goal of determining the association of baseline OMPM regimens with the escalation to CGRP mAbs therapy.

Conclusions

This real-world study suggests that baseline preventive medication regimens are associated with differences in observed clinical pathways toward CGRP mAb initiation in Thailand. Use of BBs and TCAs was associated with lower short-term escalation to CGRP mAb, while BB use was also associated with greater persistence with preventive therapy. These findings highlight the continued importance of optimizing oral preventive strategies in LMIC settings and provide insights that may inform clinical decision-making and treatment sequencing.
