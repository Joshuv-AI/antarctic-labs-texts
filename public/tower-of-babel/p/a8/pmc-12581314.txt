A comparative neuropsychological assessment of anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder and healthy control: a cross-sectional study in female university students
Authors: Öznur Özge Özcan, Burcu Çevreli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEating disorder (ED) symptoms are particularly prevalent among young adults. This study aimed to examine the distinct roles of memory function, mental health, and sleep disturbances in university students with different ED diagnostic categories.MethodologyIn this cross-sectional study, 403 female students were allocated to anorexia nervosa (AN, n = 90), bulimia nervosa (BN, n = 53), binge-eating disorder (BED, n = 116), or healthy controls (HCG, n = 144) based on self-reported physician diagnoses consistent with DSM-5 categories. Participants completed the Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (EDE-Q), Memory Functioning Questionnaire (MFQ), Bergen Insomnia Scale (BIS), and Depression Anxiety Stress Scales – 21 (DASS-21) in a single time-point assessment (January and May 2025).ResultsParticipants in the AN and BN groups showed significantly higher levels of depression, anxiety, stress, insomnia, and ED pathology, along with memory dysfunction, compared to the BED and HCG groups (p < 0.001). Depression scores were markedly elevated in AN (mean = 13.8, 95% CI [6.5–8.8]) and BN (mean = 19.2, 95% CI [12.1–14.1]) relative to HCG (mean = 6.1) and BED (mean = 9.7). Similarly, insomnia scores were higher in BN (mean = 35.2, 95% CI [25.1–27.7]) and AN (mean = 26.6, 95% CI [15.5–19.9]) compared to BED (mean = 11.3) and HCG (mean = 8.9). Within-group analyses further revealed that in AN, anxiety, stress, forgetfulness, and weight/appearance concerns were strongly interrelated (p < 0.05), while in BN, insomnia, affective symptoms, and memory dysfunction were tightly linked with overall ED severity (p < 0.05). In the BED group, binge eating behaviors showed significant associations with insomnia, affective symptoms, and cognitive difficulties (p < 0.05).ConclusionED groups—particularly BN and AN—are characterized by co-occurring insomnia, affective symptoms, and poorer perceived memory functioning. Findings support integrated assessments that include sleep and cognition alongside ED psychopathology and may inform individualized interventions. Results should be interpreted cautiously given reliance on self-reported diagnoses and a single-site, female-only sample.Keywords: Anorexia Nervosa, Binge Eating, Anxiety, Bulimia Nervosa, Depression, Eating Disorders, Insomnia, Memory function, Stress

Introduction

The two primary diagnostic frameworks for Eating disorders (EDs) are the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) [1], which is in its 10th edition, and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition [DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association [2]. The DSM-5 is more commonly utilized in research, including epidemiological studies. It identifies five primary EDs: Anorexia Nervosa (AN), Bulimia Nervosa (BN), Binge Eating Disorder (BED), Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID), and Other Specified Feeding and Eating Disorders (OSFED) [3]. Eating disorders (EDs) are serious psychiatric conditions marked by persistent disruptions in eating behavior, body image disturbances, and maladaptive cognitions about food and weight. EDs disproportionately affect young women, with prevalence estimates ranging from 5.5% to 17.9% compared to 0.6% to 2.4% in men, and recent evidence suggests rising rates, particularly among adolescents and young adults [3–6]. Large-scale surveys confirm that ED risk is especially high in university populations [7], Hasan et al., 2023), with symptoms often emerging between ages 18 and 21 and including restrictive eating, binge eating, purging, and weight-related concerns [8, 9]. Untreated EDs are associated with a twofold increase in mortality risk and substantial psychiatric and medical comorbidities [10].

Psychiatric comorbidity is a defining feature of EDs. Depression, anxiety, and stress frequently co-occur, exacerbating both functional impairment and illness severity [5, 11, 12]. Personality traits such as self-control and emotional dysregulation further shape symptom expression and treatment outcomes [13], Sander et al., 2021). Insomnia is particularly common in AN and BN, and accumulating evidence indicates that poor sleep not only worsens affective symptoms but also interacts with maladaptive eating behaviors [14–16].

Beyond affective and behavioral features, recent research has expanded toward neurocognitive mechanisms in EDs. Studies highlight attentional biases, impaired emotion regulation, and decision-making difficulties as core features of AN and BN. However, memory functioning remains understudied, despite being closely tied to these processes. Findings suggest that memory impairment and executive dysfunction may contribute to the maintenance of disordered eating and comorbid psychopathology, yet systematic investigation across ED diagnostic categories remains limited [17].

Recent research has rapidly expanded in understanding the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying the cognitive (e.g., attentional biases), emotional (e.g., difficulties in emotion regulation), and behavioral (e.g., decision-making) characteristics of EDs. While other fields recognize that these processes are closely tied to memory, studies exploring memory functions in EDs remain relatively scarce, and research linking different aspects of memory in EDs is particularly limited. To advance this area of study, we propose an operational framework for organizing memory-related research, provide a brief overview of current findings within this framework, and suggest strategies to address critical gaps in the literature [17]. Despite the extensive literature on the prevalence and diagnostic classification of eating disorders, less is known about how core cognitive and affective processes interact within specific ED diagnostic categories. Emerging evidence suggests that memory dysfunction, insomnia, and affective disturbances such as depression, anxiety, and stress are frequent comorbidities in EDs, yet studies rarely evaluate these dimensions simultaneously across different diagnostic groups. This gap is particularly important in young adult populations, where ED symptoms are highly prevalent and may critically impair academic performance and psychosocial functioning.

Therefore, the present study aims to (1) comparatively evaluate memory functioning, sleep disturbances, and psychological distress in female university students diagnosed with anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), and binge eating disorder (BED), relative to healthy controls (HCG); and (2) explore the interrelationships among these domains within each each ED diagnostic group. Specifically, we address the following research questions:

Do levels of memory dysfunction, insomnia, and affective symptoms differ significantly between ED diagnostic categories and healthy controls?

Are cognitive, sleep, and emotional impairments interrelated within AN, BN, and BED groups?

By framing these questions, our study seeks to provide an integrated understanding of the cognitive–affective–behavioral interplay in EDs, thereby informing more targeted approaches to assessment and intervention.

Methods

Study design and population

The study consists of students in associate degree programs studying at Üsküdar University between January and May 2025 (N = 403). The study included female students who self-reported having been previously diagnosed with an eating disorder (ED) by a physician within the last six months. Participants who answered yes to this question were asked to specify which ED diagnosis they had received. All participants who met the criteria of being at least 18 years old and having a self-reported physician ED diagnosis were eligible for this study.

Importantly, no structured clinical diagnostic interviews [e.g., Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders (SCID-5), Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI)] or validated screening instruments were administered. Thus, the classification of participants into anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), and binge-eating disorder (BED) groups was based solely on self-reported diagnostic history. While this approach is pragmatic and commonly used in large-scale university-based surveys, it introduces a risk of diagnostic misclassification. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the results.

Participants who met the criteria for one of the study groups were included in the current analysis: AN (n = 90), BN (n = 53), BED (n = 116), and healthy control group (HCG, n = 144). Exclusion criteria included any neurological or psychiatric disease and medication use for this reason, being an exchange student, vegetarian, physical or mental disability, foreigners, or women in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Demographic data included age, gender, BMI, marital status, alcohol use, and smoking status. All participants in the study were cisgender female students enrolled at a single university in Turkey. Therefore, the sample is homogeneous in terms of gender identity and educational context, which enhances internal validity but may limit generalizability to broader populations, including males, non-binary individuals, and students from different sociocultural or academic backgrounds. Additionally, potential confounding factors such as socioeconomic status, academic stress, and substance use were not assessed in the current study. These variables may independently influence psychological well-being, memory functioning, and sleep patterns, and should be considered in future research.

All participants gave written informed consent prior to the study. Data were collected in face-to-face sessions using the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q), Memory Functioning Questionnaire (MFQ), Bergen Insomnia Scale (BIS), and Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21). The adequacy of the sample size was calculated at a confidence level of 95% using the “G. Power-3.1.9.2” program. As a result of the analysis, the power value was found to be 0.95 at the level of α = 0.05, indicating that the sample size was sufficient for statistical analysis.

Eating disorder examination scale (EDE-Q, version 6.0)

The Eating Disorder Examination Scale (EDE-Q) is the self-reported version of the Eating Disorder Examination Interview (EDI) developed according to DSM-IV [18]. This scale, consisting of twenty-eight items, is graded from 0 to 6, and scores of four and above are considered clinically significant. The EDE-Q consists of 5 subscales: Restriction (items 1,2,3,4,5), Eating Concerns (items 7,9,19,20,21), Appearance Concerns (items 6,8,10,11,23,26,27,28), Weight Concerns (items 8,12,22,24,25), and Binge Eating (items 13–18). Subscale scores and overall scores indicate the severity of the eating disorder. To obtain subscale scores, related items are added and divided by the total number of items forming the subscale. The total score is obtained by adding the subscale scores of Restriction, Concerns About Eating, Concerns About Appearance, and Concerns About Weight and dividing by the number of subscales (four). The binge eating score is obtained by adding the answers to questions 13–18 and is not included in the total scale score. Its validity and reliability in Turkey was conducted by Yücel et al. in 2011 [19].

Memory functioning questionnaire (MFQ, 64-item version)

Memory Functioning Questionnaire (MFQ) was developed by Gilewski et al. in 1990. Turkish validity and reliability were made by Sahin et al. in 2013 [20]. It is a Likert-type scale consisting of 64 items in total to determine whether we remember what we read and our old information, including simple daily forgetfulness. Each item is scored and the scores obtained provide descriptive information about memory functionality and designed to assess daily memory functions. This self-report questionnaire consists of subscales: general forgetfulness frequency, retrospective functions, severity of forgetting and use of reminder techniques. Each item is rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 7. Higher scores across all subscales indicate that the individual perceives themselves as having good memory functionality.

Bergen insomnia scale (BIS, 6-item version)

The Bergen Insomnia Scale was developed by Pallesen and colleagues in 2008. The scale is based on official and clinical diagnostic criteria for insomnia. The Bergen Insomnia Scale is based on the DSM-4 diagnostic criteria but also complies with the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. The scale consists of six questions measuring different symptoms of insomnia. Participants indicate the number of days per week during the past month when they experienced sleep problems in various aspects on an 8-point scale between 0 and 7. The lowest score that can be obtained from the scale is 0, and the highest score is 42. Pallesen et al. applied the scale to three different sample groups. These were student, community, and patient samples. A two-factor structure was found in the student and patient samples, and a single-factor structure was found in the community sample. In the original study, the internal consistency of the scale was found to be α = 0.87 and the test–retest reliability was found to be r = 0.77 in the community sample including adult individuals [21]. The validity and reliability of the Turkish form of the Bergen Insomnia Scale in adults was conducted by Bay and Ergün [22], and the Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient was found to be 0.85 in the daytime symptoms sub-dimension, 0.80 in the nighttime symptoms sub-dimension, and 0.85 for the total BIS [22].

Depression, anxiety, stress scale 21 (DASS-21, short form)

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale 21 (DASS-21), originally created by Lovibond and Lovibond [23], was initially composed of 42 items. A condensed 21-item version (DASS-21) was later introduced to evaluate the emotional states of depression, anxiety, and stress in both clinical and non-clinical groups. This shorter version includes three subscales—each containing 7 items—designed to measure one of the emotional states. Responses are recorded using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“does not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“applies to me very much or most of the time”). The depression subscale includes items 3, 5, 10, 13, 16, 17, and 21,the anxiety subscale comprises items 2, 4, 7, 9, 15, 19, and 20; while the stress subscale consists of items 1, 6, 8, 11, 12, 14, and 18. Each subscale is scored separately, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores reflect more severe symptoms in the respective emotional domain. In the original study validating the tool, Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.94 for depression, 0.87 for anxiety, and 0.91 for stress, indicating strong internal consistency. The Turkish version of the DASS-21 was validated by Sarıçam, who reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.72 for depression, 0.71 for anxiety, and 0.70 for stress [24].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 package program was used for data analysis in the study. Descriptive data regarding the sociodemographic information of the participants were given in the form of frequency tables. The data of the study were evaluated with the Kolmogorv-Smirnov test in terms of normality assumptions and p > 0.05 was determined. Therefore, Pearson correlation analysis was performed from parametric tests to determine the relationship between the scale and subscale scores. In addition, ANOVA test from parametric tests was applied to determine whether there was a significant difference between the scale and subscales and the groups. In case of a significant difference between the groups, Bonferroni test from post-Hoc tests was used to determine between which groups the significance was. Pearson Chi Square test and Fisher's Exact test were used to compare categorical variables. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Body mass index differed as expected: AN and BN had lower BMI and BED had higher BMI than healthy controls (all p < 0.001). All participants were female. Smoking and alcohol use were more common in BED than in the other groups (both p < 0.001; Table 1).

Insomnia (BIS)

BN showed the highest insomnia severity, followed by AN, then BED, all greater than healthy controls (all p < 0.001). Pairwise contrasts indicated BN > AN and AN, BN > BED (examples: BN vs HCG 95% CI [25.06, 27.68]; AN vs HCG 95% CI [15.50, 19.89]).

Affective symptoms (DASS-21)

BN had the most severe depression, anxiety, and stress, with all clinical groups exceeding healthy controls (all p < 0.001). BN also scored higher than AN and BED on each DASS-21 subscale (e.g., Depression BN vs AN 95% CI [4.53, 6.34]).

Memory functioning (MFQ)

Overall perceived memory functioning was lowest in BN and AN and highest in healthy controls; BED was intermediate but above AN and BN (all key contrasts p < 0.001; e.g., MFQ-Total HCG vs BN 95% CI [96.23, 132.07]). Subdomains (general forgetfulness, retrospective functioning, severity of forgetting, use of reminder techniques) follow the same pattern (Table 2).

Eating-disorder pathology (EDE-Q)

Total EDE-Q scores were highest in BN, followed by AN, then BED, with all clinical groups greater than healthy controls (all p < 0.001). By construct, AN and BN showed higher restraint/appearance/weight concerns, whereas BED showed the highest binge-eating scores (all pairwise p < 0.001; Table 2).

Correlation analyses in the AN group demonstrated significant associations between clinical symptoms and eating disorder severity. Anxiety and stress were highly interrelated (r = 0.85, p < 0.001) and both showed strong positive correlations with the EDE-Q global score (r = 0.72 and r = 0.75, respectively, p < 0.001). Depression also correlated positively with the EDE-Q total (r = 0.52, p < 0.001), although these associations were weaker compared to anxiety and stress. Within the MFQ, the total score was moderately associated with anxiety (r = 0.52, p < 0.001) and stress (r = 0.43, p < 0.001), as well as with the EDE-Q total (r = 0.54, p < 0.001). Subscales such as GFF and SF demonstrated robust correlations with both EDE-Q severity indices (r values > 0.60, p < 0.001) and affective symptoms. By contrast, the RF dimension exhibited weaker or nonsignificant associations. Regarding eating disorder subscales, RC, EC, AC, and WC were all strongly correlated with one another (r = 0.78–0.96, p < 0.001), indicating high internal consistency of eating pathology dimensions. Interestingly, BE displayed negative correlations with several EDE-Q subscales (e.g., RC: r = –0.32, WC: r = –0.40, all p < 0.01) and with depression (r = –0.34, p < 0.01), suggesting a distinct clinical profile within the AN sample. Overall, these results highlight that anxiety and stress are the most salient predictors of eating disorder severity, while depressive symptoms and specific cognitive-behavioral features also contribute meaningfully. Discussion

The current study provides a comprehensive analysis of the interrelationship between memory functioning, sleep quality, and psychological health in female university students diagnosed with different ED diagnostic categories; AN, BN, and BED. In doing so, it fills a critical gap in the literature by integrating cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains that are often studied in isolation.

Our findings demonstrate that participants with BN reported the most severe impairments across variables, including higher depression, anxiety, stress, sleep disturbances, and lower memory functioning scores compared to AN, BED, and HCG. These results align with prior research on the multifaceted nature of EDs and their association with broader psychopathology. Previous epidemiological studies have emphasized the rising prevalence of EDs, particularly among young female populations, with recent data showing a growing burden of undiagnosed cases in university settings [4, 6]. Lipson and Sonneville [8] similarly found that ED symptoms were significantly elevated among university students, supporting our sampling frame. EDs frequently co-occur with depression and anxiety, comorbidities strongly linked to poorer outcomes and greater cognitive dysfunction [11, 12, 25]. Our results expand on this literature by showing that BN participants had the highest depression, anxiety, and stress levels, alongside the most elevated EDE-Q scores and severe sleep impairment. This pattern supports evidence that BN is particularly associated with emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, and psychiatric comorbidity [13, 14, 16, 26]. Notably, BN was the only group where insomnia correlated strongly with both cognitive and affective parameters, consistent with the sleep-dependent memory consolidation theory [11, 12, 27, 28].

AN and BN participants reported greater memory dysfunction on the MFQ, including higher forgetfulness, reduced retrospective memory, and less use of reminder techniques. These findings are consistent with prior evidence of impaired memory and executive functions in ED populations [17, 29]) and may reflect the impact of nutritional deficits and stress on memory consolidation and retrieval [30]. While causal inference cannot be made, our results extend neurocognitive evidence by highlighting self-reported memory impairments in both AN and BN. Interestingly, BED participants showed cognitive and sleep profiles more closely aligned with healthy controls. This finding is consistent with Degasperi et al. [10], who reported in their meta-analysis that individuals with BED do not experience the same degree of sleep quality reduction as those with AN and BN. Roveda et al. [31] similarly found no significant disruptions in circadian rhythm or sleep architecture among BED patients when compared to non-clinical samples. Despite elevated scores in binge eating symptoms, our BED group did not exhibit the same level of impairment in MFQ subscales, suggesting that while BED is associated with behavioral dysregulation, its neurocognitive correlates may be less pronounced than in AN or BN. Strong associations between poor sleep quality and emotional dysregulation have been documented in both ED and non-ED populations. Our findings further support the bidirectional model in which sleep disruption contributes to negative affect, while emotional dysregulation exacerbates sleep problems [14]. Similarly, studies in female patients with AN and BN have reported high rates of insomnia, suggesting that sleep disturbances are intrinsic features of these disorders [32, 33].

The correlations observed between memory dysfunction and stress, anxiety, and depression are consistent with prior literature. Mao et al. [16] showed that chronic insomnia and anxiety are linked to dysregulation in frontoparietal and limbic networks, which are also central to memory and emotion regulation. Neurobiological evidence supports these findings: abnormalities in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus have been reported in EDs, regions essential for executive function and memory [34, 35]. fMRI studies have further demonstrated impaired hippocampal activation during memory encoding in AN, suggesting that nutritional deficits may compromise neural plasticity and long-term potentiation. Cognitive inflexibility and set-shifting difficulties, widely documented in AN and BN, also contribute to rigid thought patterns and perseverative behaviors [36–38]. Our findings of reduced use of reminder techniques and increased retrospective forgetfulness in AN and BN may reflect these executive deficits, warranting further investigation with objective cognitive and neuroimaging approaches. In addition, chrononutrition research highlights the interplay between eating patterns and circadian rhythms. Irregular eating and night eating, common in BN and BED, have been associated with disrupted sleep–wake cycles, melatonin secretion, and metabolic regulation [39]. Such chronodisruption impairs sleep-dependent memory consolidation, particularly hippocampus-dependent retention during slow-wave sleep [27, 28]. Thus, our findings of memory impairment in groups with elevated sleep disturbances align with the sleep-dependent memory consolidation theory.

Our findings suggest that as psychological distress increases, self-reported memory functioning decreases, a trend supported by significant negative correlations between DASS-21 and MFQ scores. This study adds to the growing body of literature highlighting the need for multidimensional assessments in EDs. While current diagnostic criteria focus primarily on eating behaviors and weight concerns [2], our results demonstrate that impairments in cognitive functioning and sleep are also important correlates that deserve greater clinical attention.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that sleep quality and memory assessments may provide valuable complementary markers for evaluating the severity and functional impact of EDs, especially in young adults. Given that memory impairment and poor sleep quality may negatively influence academic and social functioning, targeted interventions that address these cognitive and behavioral domains could improve overall treatment outcomes in ED populations. Integrated treatment approaches—such as cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, and emotion regulation interventions—could be particularly beneficial adjuncts to standard ED treatments, especially for BN and AN populations [40, 41].

Such multidimensional strategies may enhance treatment adherence and reduce relapse by addressing comorbid symptoms that undermine recovery. Importantly, our study emphasizes the necessity of screening for cognitive and sleep disturbances in ED patients, which are often overlooked in routine clinical assessments. Incorporating tools such as the MFQ and BIS may provide a more holistic understanding of patient functioning and guide personalized treatment planning.

Limitations

A key limitation of the present study is the reliance on self-reported physician diagnoses of eating disorders without confirmation via structured clinical interviews. Although self-report methods are commonly employed in large-scale epidemiological studies, this approach may reduce diagnostic precision and increase the risk of misclassification across groups [42]). As a result, some observed differences between diagnostic categories may have been underestimated or overestimated. Future research should incorporate validated diagnostic instruments such as the SCID-5 or MINI to strengthen diagnostic reliability and improve the validity of group comparisons. Additionally, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether the associations observed between EDs, sleep, and cognitive functioning reflect causal pathways or bidirectional processes. Another limitation is that potential confounders such as socioeconomic status, academic stress, and substance use were not assessed. These factors are known to independently influence memory, mood, and sleep, and their omission may partially account for the observed associations. Future studies should incorporate these variables into multivariate models to provide a more precise estimation of effect sizes. Also, as the study included only cisgender female students from a single university in Turkey, the findings may not generalize to males, non-binary individuals, or students from different educational and cultural contexts. Future research with more diverse and representative populations is needed to enhance external validity. AN subtypes (restrictive vs. binge-purging) were not distinguished. These subtypes are known to present distinct clinical and cognitive profiles, with prior studies reporting more pronounced deficits in executive functioning and emotion regulation among binge-purging patients compared to those with restrictive AN. The lack of this distinction may have limited the nuance of our findings, particularly in interpreting associations between memory dysfunction and emotional variables. Future research should incorporate subtype-specific analyses to provide greater diagnostic precision.

Conlusion

In conclusion, This study can contribute to the growing body of literature highlighting the multifactorial nature of EDs. By highlighting the interactions among psychological, cognitive, and behavioral dimensions, especially in BN and AN, we underscore the need for comprehensive, individualized, and integrative approaches to ED assessment and intervention. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as they rely on self-reported diagnostic histories. Future research with structured diagnostic tools will be essential to confirm these associations.
