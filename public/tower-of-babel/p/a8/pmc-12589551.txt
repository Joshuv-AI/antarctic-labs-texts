Research on the front face styling design of a ship type based on low drag coefficient
Authors: Ganshu Sheng, Yiren Ran, Jiefeng Lyu, Zihao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the continuous development of modern ship technology, the design of ships is no longer limited to meeting functional requirements, but also deepens the demand for modelling. Ship exterior modelling not only affects the aesthetic characteristics of the ship, but also influences the ship’s sailing resistance and energy consumption level. Based on the drag coefficient of the ship, this paper explores the influence of different configurations of ship exterior modelling design on the wind resistance of the ship, in order to provide design guidance for modern ship exterior modelling design. To this end, this paper takes the front face design of a certain ship type as an example. Based on the confirmation of the front face design by the client, the windward face of the ship type is divided into three characteristics: the ratio of the front face segmentation width, the inclination angle between the main elevation of the front face and the vertical plane, and the shape of the main elevation of the front face. An orthogonal experimental design for aerodynamic drag simulation is proposed. For the experimental combinations, a ship simulation model is established in SolidWorks, and fluid simulation analysis is conducted using StarCCM + to obtain the drag coefficient at each experimental level as the evaluation criterion.

Introduction

With the increasing attention to the economic, aesthetic, and environmental concerns of ships, more and more people are placing greater emphasis on the appearance design of ships and their energy consumption levels. The energy consumption of a ship is directly related to its resistance, which can be divided into wind resistance and water resistance. The wind load on a ship is closely related to the design of its windward face. For mainstream commercial vessels, water resistance accounts for a large proportion of the total resistance, thus, in long-term research, the focus of drag reduction optimization has mainly been on reducing water resistance1,2.

With the continuous advancement of green shipping research, the demand for optimizing green ship design to improve ship energy efficiency and meet energy efficiency design index requirements has been steadily increasing. The optimization of wind speed reduction for ships has also gradually attracted widespread attention in the shipping industry3–6. Currently, both domestic and international scholars and research institutions have focused on wind resistance optimization in the design of future green ships7,8. In some special cases, for cruise ships and passenger vessels with large superstructure areas, the large windward area increases the proportion of wind resistance in the total resistance, which poses significant challenges to navigation energy consumption and safety. For passenger ships with high maneuverability requirements, wind resistance is one of the primary external forces encountered during low-speed maneuvering when approaching ports9,10. Therefore, the wind load on ships, determined after the exterior styling design, is crucial not only for navigation safety but also for improving ship energy efficiency.

Regarding the research on the impact of wind load on ships, a large number of studies have focused on two main areas: ship safety and energy efficiency improvement. In terms of ship safety, both domestic and international scholars have conducted in-depth research on ship wind resistance calculation, wind-structure interaction evaluation, and the resulting effects11,12. Regarding energy efficiency improvement, current research mainly focuses on reducing wind resistance and optimizing the layout of the superstructure13,14.

As for the calculation methods for ship wind load testing, they primarily focus on three common research methods: wind tunnel testing, numerical simulation, and empirical formulas. These three methods have different technical approaches, and their advantages and disadvantages complement each other. Although empirical formulas have certain limitations, they remain an important method for obtaining accurate data. Wind tunnel testing is effective for simulating specific environmental conditions, but due to economic and time constraints, it limits the ability to verify multiple solutions. CFD methods can overcome many of the limitations of test conditions and study wind loads under different operational conditions by modifying models and adjusting settings. They also have a good correlation with wind tunnel test results, but the disadvantage is that the simplified models result in lower calculation accuracy.

Based on the research progress mentioned above regarding ship wind load calculation and drag reduction optimization, I believe that further research is needed in the following areas. Currently, there is limited research on the relationship between ship exterior styling design and wind resistance optimization. Considering the existing research progress on wind load and the outlook for the shipbuilding industry, exploring a design method that both ensures ship aesthetics and effectively reduces wind load is highly valuable. Of course, since ship exterior designs are varied, it is advisable to focus on representative design features for study. Analyzing how the front face segmentation width ratio, the inclination angle between the main elevation of the front face and the vertical plane, and the shape of the main elevation of the front face influence ship wind resistance is key. Therefore, this paper will discuss the impact of combined surface characteristic factors on ship exterior styling and wind load, calculate the ship’s longitudinal wind load coefficient based on an orthogonal experiment, and then use range analysis to identify the optimal parameter combination for wind resistance optimization. Finally, a multifactorial variance analysis will be conducted to analyze the weight of each influencing factor, and the optimal windward face profile combining aesthetics and low wind load will be identified using an aesthetic design evaluation method.

Orthogonal experimental method

In scientific research and engineering practice, many target outcomes are influenced by a combination of multiple factors. When the number of influencing factors is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m$$\end{document}, and each factor has \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:q$$\end{document} levels, if a full factorial experimental design is adopted, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{q}^{m}$$\end{document} experiments are required. Although this method allows for a comprehensive examination of the factors and their interactions, it results in an extremely large experimental workload under multi-factor and multi-level conditions, making it impractical in real-world applications.

Orthogonal experimental design is an efficient experimental method that carefully selects a limited number of representative experimental conditions, allowing reliable conclusions about the overall problem to be obtained with fewer trials. As a result, it is widely used for solving multi-factor and multi-level problems. The core of orthogonal experiments lies in selecting an appropriate orthogonal table to ensure the representativeness, rationality, and balance of the experimental design.

The choice of an orthogonal table is key to the design of the orthogonal experiment. A valid orthogonal table must have two characteristics: the first is balance, where each level appears an equal number of times in the same column; the second is matching, where combinations of different levels between any two columns appear an equal number of times. The orthogonal table is represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Ln\left({q}^{m}\right)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L$$\end{document} stands for orthogonal table, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n$$\end{document} is the number of experiments, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:q$$\end{document} is the number of levels for each factor, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m$$\end{document} is the number of factors.

After the orthogonal experiment is completed, analyzing the experimental results is an essential step. Range analysis and variance analysis are the most commonly used methods and are important tools for extracting valuable information. Range analysis calculates the mean of the experimental results at different levels for each factor to make an initial judgment about the degree of influence each factor has on the target outcome. The core of range analysis is to calculate the range of each factor, providing a visual representation of the variation between the experimental results at different levels. A larger range indicates a greater influence of the factor on the experimental result. This intuitive analysis allows for the rapid identification of the main influencing factors and the optimal level combinations for each factor, providing direct references for subsequent optimization. However, range analysis is a qualitative judgment that is susceptible to random errors and cannot accurately assess the significance of the factors or quantify their specific impact on the results. Therefore, relying solely on range analysis may lead to conclusions that are not scientifically sound.

To compensate for the shortcomings of range analysis, variance analysis is needed for further validation. Variance analysis is a rigorous statistical method that can quantitatively assess the significance of each factor and its interactions on the target outcome. By comparing the variation caused by each factor with the variation caused by random errors, variance analysis can determine whether the influence of a factor is significant and provide the significance level (such as the P-value). Furthermore, variance analysis effectively eliminates the interference of random errors on experimental results, improving the reliability of the conclusions. In multi-factor experiments, interactions may have an important impact on the target outcome, and variance analysis can quantitatively verify the significance of these interactions, providing deeper guidance for experimental optimization.

The combination of range analysis and variance analysis enables complementary qualitative and quantitative analysis. Range analysis provides optimization directions quickly through intuitive calculations and result comparisons, while variance analysis ensures the scientific and accurate nature of the conclusions through statistical validation. The combination of both not only reveals the influence patterns of the factors on the target outcome but also effectively verifies the rationality of the experimental design. Therefore, after completing the orthogonal experiment, performing both range analysis and variance analysis is a crucial step to ensure the scientific validity and reliability of the experimental results. It is an indispensable key component in the research of multi-factor and multi-level problems.

Orthogonal design of the experimental plan

Based on previous research, this experiment initially adopts a three-factor, three-level experimental plan. However, considering that the factor of the shape of the main elevation of the front face includes three types to be studied—isosceles trapezoid, rectangle, and inverted isosceles trapezoid—and that the angle range of the bottom corners in both the isosceles trapezoid and inverted isosceles trapezoid is quite broad, selecting a single angle for each type as a reference index for subsequent experimental analysis would introduce too much randomness, making the results less convincing. Therefore, this experiment sets five levels for the factor of the shape of the main elevation of the front face: upper edge 1/3, upper edge 1/2, normal, lower edge 1/3, and lower edge 1/2. For the factor of the front face segmentation width ratio, three levels are set: 1:2:1, 1:1:1, and 2:1:2. For the factor of the inclination angle between the front face windward side and the vertical plane, three levels are set: 30°, 20°, and 15°, in order to enhance the reliability of the data analysis. Thus, the final experimental plan adopts a design with two factors at three levels and one factor at five levels, requiring a total of 45 experiments in the traditional analysis approach.

Considering the efficiency of the experiment and computational resources, and ensuring the scientific validity of the results, orthogonal experiments are used for subsequent analysis. The corresponding factors and level distributions are shown in Table 1.

Governing equations

Assume that the wind field around the ship is steady, incompressible, and viscous. The simulation of the viscous flow field is solved using the RANS (Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes) equations. To close the governing equations, the k-Epsilon turbulence model is used with a two-layer full y + wall treatment17.

Computational domain and boundary conditions

A right-handed Cartesian coordinate system O − xyz is used, with the coordinate system defined as follows: the origin is placed at the intersection of the ship’s central transverse section and the waterline. The directions of the coordinate axes are defined as follows: the x-axis runs along the longitudinal axis of the ship, pointing from the stern to the bow; the z-axis is vertical, perpendicular to the waterline; the y-axis is parallel to the waterline, perpendicular to the central longitudinal section, and points from the starboard to the port. The wind direction is from the front face to the stern, with the rotation direction following the right-hand rule, where counterclockwise is positive.

Computational mesh

The computational domain for the external flow field is set as a rectangular box, with dimensions defined as follows: the length is 10 times the ship’s length, the width is 8 times the ship’s length, and the height is 2 times the ship’s length. The ship is positioned at the central bottom of the computational domain. To accommodate changes in wind direction, a velocity inlet and a pressure outlet are set in the computational domain. The remaining boundary conditions are as follows: the upper wall, lower wall, left wall, and right wall are all set as slip walls to reduce flow constraints; the ship’s surface is set as a no-slip wall to simulate the real fluid viscous effects.

The mesh is generated using a cutting-body mesh, with a base size of 2 m, 5 layers of prismatic cells, and a slow volume growth rate. Surface control and volume control are applied; in the surface control setup, the six outer faces of the fluid domain are prohibited from generating prismatic layers, and in the volume control setup, a refined mesh domain is added around the ship. The dimensions of the refined domain are: length 3 times the ship’s length, width 1.5 times the ship’s length, and height 1.5 times the ship’s length.

The final mesh generated on the ship’s surface captures the shape of the superstructure well, with a total of 4.34 million cells in the computational domain. After performing mesh quality diagnostics, the surface validity statistic is 100%, and the volume change statistic is 99.903%.

Discretization scheme and numerical solution method

In the numerical simulation of ship windward face wind resistance using StarCCM+, the finite volume method is applied to discretize the governing equations. Both the discretization scheme and iterative algorithm are designed to ensure the accuracy of the simulation results and the stability of the computational process.

To ensure computational accuracy, second-order discretization is used for both the convective and viscous terms of the governing equations. Second-order discretization has significant advantages in capturing gradient changes in fluid flow, as well as flow separation and vortex characteristics. It has lower truncation errors and can better reflect the true physical characteristics of the flow. Particularly in ship windward face wind resistance simulations, the presence of separated flows and high-gradient regions makes first-order discretization less able to meet the accuracy requirements, making the choice of second-order discretization especially important. However, due to its increased sensitivity, second-order discretization may introduce numerical oscillations. Therefore, in specific calculations, other parameter settings, such as relaxation factors and iterative algorithms, are combined to achieve stable computations.

For the iterative solution, the SIMPLE (Semi-Implicit Method for Pressure Linked Equations) algorithm is chosen. This is a classic velocity-pressure coupling algorithm suitable for handling incompressible steady-state flows. The SIMPLE algorithm iterates by predicting the velocity field, correcting the pressure field, and then updating the velocity field based on the corrected pressure field until convergence is reached. This process solves the coupling problem between velocity and pressure. The SIMPLE algorithm is computationally efficient and stable, offering significant advantages in steady-state flow simulations, such as ship windward face wind resistance.

To further control the stability of the iterative process, under-relaxation factors for velocity and pressure are set to 0.7 and 0.3, respectively. The purpose of the under-relaxation factor is to adjust the magnitude of variable updates during each iteration, preventing numerical divergence caused by rapid changes in variables. For velocity, the under-relaxation factor is set to 0.7, allowing for faster convergence while maintaining iterative stability. For pressure, due to its significant impact on velocity corrections and the presence of separated flows and high-gradient regions in the flow field, pressure fluctuations may be more pronounced. Therefore, a more conservative under-relaxation factor of 0.3 is used for pressure to enhance iterative stability. These parameter settings consider the complexity of the simulation scenario and the stability requirements of the numerical algorithm, effectively reducing the risk of numerical oscillations caused by separated flows and vortex regions.

The mesh density of the medium grid is consistent with the mesh density used in the current simulation of this study.All combinations shared the same width ratio of 1:2:1, covering three inclination angles and five main elevation shapes of the bow, thus ensuring representativeness for the analysis.

Subsequently, the drag coefficient (Cd) was monitored for each of the six bow configurations through CFD simulations. Further increasing to fine meshes resulted in minor changes (< 1%) and a convergence trend, despite slight Cd rebounds in a few cases (e.g., 0.70% for “30° Upper Edge 1/2”). Summary and outlook

Based on the above experiments and data analysis, in this model, considering the ship’s exterior streamlined design, the main influencing factors on the drag coefficient under real sea conditions are, in order of importance: the front face segmentation width ratio, the inclination angle between the windward face and the vertical plane, and the shape of the main elevation of the front face. According to the arrangement of A1B1C3, the optimal drag reduction effect can be achieved.

The simulation analysis results show that the width ratio of the ship and the inclination angle between the windward face and the vertical plane both significantly affect the drag coefficient, while the shape of the main elevation of the front face has no significant effect.

For the optimal parameter combination A1B1C3, it means the width ratio is 1:2:1, the inclination angle is 30°, and the shape of the main elevation of the front face is rectangular. From an aesthetic perspective, the rectangular shape of the main elevation of the front face is too traditional, so the experimental combination, considering both aesthetics and drag optimization, requires further analysis and discussion.

In A1B1C3, the width ratio is the widest type, and the inclination angle is the maximum value. Since two parameters in the best low wind load parameter combination reach extreme values, this raises the question of whether a wider front face and a larger inclination angle result in better drag optimization. This point can be further studied by exploring the ideal values for the interaction between the width ratio and inclination angle factors.
