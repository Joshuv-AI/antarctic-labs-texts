Methyl gallate and hyaluronic acid-nanoconjugate targeting HSP90β for osteoarthritis therapy
Authors: Anping Shen, Xuan Mo, Yan Chen, Yan Xue, Lin Wang, Qinguang Xu, Laijun Yan, Hongping Deng, Yuelong Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is a joint disease with complicated pathophysiologic process, however, the lack of effective therapeutic targets and corresponding pharmaceuticals severely limits treatment options.ResultsMethyl gallate (MG) could delay the pathological changes of OA by reducing cartilage inflammation and degeneration at both cellular and animal levels. Using drug affinity responsive target stability assay and mass spectrometry analysis, we identified HSP90β as a key target of MG in chondrocytes. The interaction between MG and heat shock protein 90β（HSP90β）was further validated by surface-plasmon resonance and cellular thermal shift assay experiments. In OA rat models, a positive correlation between HSP90β and cartilage degeneration was confirmed and upregulated expression of HSP90β and its related pathways was revealed through antibody chip monitoring and KEGG analysis. Moreover, clinical analysis of 53 OA patients showed a positive association between HSP90β expression and pain scores. Conversely, silencing HSP90β in rat chondrocytes significantly reduced the mRNA levels of matrix metalloproteinases and IL-6, and decreased the expression of PI3K/Akt pathway-related proteins. To achieve more effective OA therapy, MG was conjugated to hyaluronic acid (HA) via a reduction-responsive disulfide linker. In an OA mouse model, HA-MG exhibited enhanced therapeutic efficacy compared to free MG, leading to increased accumulation and retention of the conjugate in the joint cavity, as demonstrated by in-situ long-term fluorescence imaging.ConclusionsThis study has identified the important role of HSP90β as a therapeutic target for OA and provided a robust approach to attenuate OA progression using a polysaccharide nanoconjugate.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04239-y.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Heat shock protein 90β, Methyl gallate, Reduction-responsive nanoconjugate, Hyaluronic acid

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common chronic joint disorder, affecting over 500 million individuals globally and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden [1]. It is characterized by the progressive destruction of articular cartilage, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone remodeling [2]. The pathogenesis of OA is multifactorial, involving mechanical stress, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation within the joint microenvironment [3, 4]. These pathological processes collectively disrupt the balance between cartilage matrix synthesis and degradation, ultimately leading to irreversible joint degeneration and pain [5]. Conventional treatments, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids, and intra-articular hyaluronic acid (HA) injections, provide only transient symptomatic relief and are often accompanied by systemic side effects or limited efficacy [6]. Thus, there remains an urgent need to identify novel molecular targets and effective delivery strategies that can intervene during the early stages of OA progression.

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) constitute a conserved family of molecular chaperones that maintain protein homeostasis under various cellular stress conditions, including harmful external temperature fluctuations and inflammation [7]. Among them, heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) plays a central role in stabilizing and activating a wide range of client proteins involved in cell signaling, apoptosis, and inflammatory regulation [8]. The HSP90 family includes two major cytosolic isoforms, HSP90α and HSP90β, which exhibit distinct expression patterns and biological functions. While HSP90α is primarily inducible and associated with acute stress responses, HSP90β is constitutively expressed and critically involved in maintaining cellular proteostasis and tissue homeostasis [9, 10]. As a molecular chaperone, HSP90β regulates multiple signaling cascades implicated in OA progression, including NF-κB, MAPK, and PI3K/Akt pathways, which are essential for inflammatory amplification and cartilage matrix degradation [11]. Moreover, HSP90β stabilizes several pathogenic client proteins-such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)-that drive cartilage catabolism and oxidative injury [12–14]. Despite these insights, the direct functional significance of HSP90β in OA pathophysiology remains poorly understood, and its potential as a therapeutic target has not been fully validated. Current studies have primarily focused on HSP90 inhibitors, which often lack isoform selectivity and cause off-target cytotoxicity, limiting their clinical translation [15] (Table S1). Therefore, identifying small molecules capable of selectively modulating HSP90β activity and exploring the specific mechanisms of HSP90β in OA represents a promising yet underexplored approach for OA therapy.

Our previous studies have revealed the therapeutic effects of the traditional herbal medicine formula Guizhi decoction on OA using bioinformatics and network pharmacology methods. In particular, Methyl gallate(MG) was identified as one of its key active components [16]. MG is a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound in plants such as Galla chinensis and Paeonia anomala [17]. Some studies have shown that MG exerts anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects in various inflammatory injury models, including intestinal inflammation, rheumatoid arthritis, and nephritis, by inhibiting autophagy, oxidative stress, and regulating downstream inflammatory signaling pathways, such as nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and MAPK [18–20]. However, the effects of MG in OA and its precise molecular target remains largely undefined.

Despite its promising pharmacological profile, the clinical translation of MG faces notable challenges. Free MG suffers from poor stability, rapid clearance, and limited intra-articular retention, which severely restrict its therapeutic efficacy when administered by conventional routes. HA, a naturally occurring polysaccharide composed of repeating disaccharide units of N-acetylglucosamine and glucuronic acid, is a key structural component of synovial fluid and cartilage extracellular matrix. Owing to its excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and intrinsic joint affinity, HA has been widely utilized as a biomaterial for intra-articular therapy and as a carrier for drug delivery [21]. Recent advances have further expanded the therapeutic applications of HA through three major strategies: chemically modified HA hydrogels for enhanced joint lubrication and surface retention; HA-based platforms for controlled release of anti-inflammatory agents, growth factors, and gene therapeutics; and HA hydrogels serving as chondroinductive microenvironments for stem cell delivery and cartilage regeneration [22]. Despite these promising developments, no HA-drug nanoconjugate has yet received FDA or EMA approval, underscoring the substantial translational hurdles. By combining HA’s biological compatibility and targeting capacity with MG’s functional pharmacological effects, this platform holds great potential for precision therapy of osteoarthritis.

Based on the aforementioned considerations, we hypothesized that HSP90β may act as a critical molecular mediator of cartilage degeneration and a promising therapeutic target for osteoarthritis. We further postulated that MG may interact with and inhibit HSP90β, thereby protecting chondrocytes from stress-induced apoptosis and inflammation. To overcome the pharmacokinetic limitations of free MG and achieve targeted intra-articular delivery, we designed a hyaluronic acid-methyl HA-MG through the introduction of disulfide linkages, prolonging MG retention within the joint and enhancing its therapeutic effects. This study integrates chemical synthesis, proteomics, clinical correlation analysis, and in vivo evaluation to comprehensively elucidate the therapeutic mechanism and efficacy of MG and it’s nanoconjugate. Specifically, we (i) identified HSP90β as a key MG-binding protein through drug-affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) and mass spectrometry (MS), with validation by surface-plasmon resonance (SPR) and cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) experiments; (ii) examined the correlation between HSP90β expression and disease severity in OA patients and animal models; silencing HSP90β in rat chondrocytes resulted in decreased cartilage degradation-related proteins.; and (iii) demonstrated that HA-MG nanoconjugates achieve enhanced intra-articular retention, potent inhibition of HSP90β activity, and superior chondroprotective effects compared with free MG (Scheme 1). Collectively, this work not only establishes HSP90β as a previously unrecognized therapeutic target in osteoarthritis, but also provides a novel polysaccharide-based nanoplatform for sustained intra-articular drug delivery.

Results

MG attenuates osteoarthritis progression in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the protective effects of MG on chondrocytes and osteoarthritis progression, we performed both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Building on previous research indicating that temperature affects chondrocyte metabolism [23], we treated rat chondrocytes with serum collected from osteoarthritis (OA) rats exposed to a cold artificial climate chamber. The resulting cold-interfered serum (CIS), in combination with IL-1β, was used to co-stimulate the chondrocytes, thereby aggravating cartilage degeneration (Fig. S1). This approach replicates the metabolic environment of chondrocytes under harmful external temperature conditions while avoiding the adverse effects on the overall biological functions of the cells caused by direct exposure to low temperatures and enhancing the precision and reliability of cellular behavior studies. In primary rat chondrocytes, MG effectively attenuated inflammatory responses and matrix degradation induced by interleukin-1β (IL-1β). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis showed that IL-1β stimulation significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 (Fig. 1A, B), as well as catabolic enzymes ADAMTS5 and MMP3 (Fig. 1C, D). Treatment with MG markedly reversed these elevations, indicating its potent anti-inflammatory and anti-catabolic effects in vitro. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that MG preserved the expression of collagen type II alpha 1 (Col2α1), which was otherwise reduced in IL-1β-stimulated cells (Fig. 1E). Quantification of Col2α1 fluorescence intensity confirmed a significant restoration of cartilage matrix integrity following MG treatment (Fig. 1F). Western blotting revealed that the levels of cartilage matrix degrading proteins (iNOS, MMP13, COX-2, Acan, and SOX9) were significantly increased in the IL-1β and CIS-IL-1β groups compared with the control group (P < 0.05). However, MG treatment significantly downregulated these proteins (P < 0.05; Fig. S2A). Apoptosis-related proteins Bcl-2 levels were decreased, while Bax and Caspase-3 were elevated (P < 0.05). MG treatment reversed these changes by upregulating Bcl-2 and downregulating Bax and Caspase-3 levels (P < 0.05; Fig. S2B). Furthermore, the PI3K/Akt pathway-associated proteins (p-Pi3k, Pi3k, p-Akt, and Akt) were significantly altered in the IL-1β and CIS-IL-1β groups, with increased p-Pi3k and p-Akt levels (P < 0.05), whereas MG treatment decreased the expression of p-Pi3k and p-Akt (P < 0.05; Fig. S2C). To evaluate the therapeutic potential of MG in vivo, a destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) model was established in mice. Intra-articular injections of high (20 mg/mL), medium (4 mg/mL), and low (0.8 mg/mL) doses of MG were administered to the three treatment groups, as illustrated in the experimental timeline (Fig. 1G). Histological assessment using Safranin O-Fast Green staining demonstrated that DMM surgery induced significant cartilage erosion and proteoglycan loss in the PBS-treated group. MG treatment preserved cartilage structure and matrix content in a dose-dependent manner, with the high-dose group exhibiting the most pronounced protection (Fig. 1H). Consistently, OARSI scoring indicated that MG significantly mitigated DMM-induced cartilage degeneration compared with PBS controls (Fig. 1I). Together, these results demonstrate that MG effectively protects chondrocytes from inflammatory and catabolic insults in vitro and attenuates OA progression in vivo.

HSP90β is the major target of MG in murine chondrocytes

To identify the target of MG in murine chondrocytes, we performed a DARTS assay on the ATDC5 chondrocyte cells, both with and without MG treatment (Fig. S3). The results demonstrated that MG significantly regulated 80 differentially expressed proteins (Table S2, S3, and Fig. 2A); 46 were upregulated, 34 were downregulated (p < 0.05), and HSP90β was the most downregulated (Fig. 2B). Because proteins within organisms do not function independently but rely on complex regulatory networks, we conducted a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. Proteins with high connectivity may serve as critical nodes in signaling pathways. The PPI network analysis revealed that HSP90β occupies a central position in the MG-regulated protein interaction network (degree = 10; Fig. 2C). Molecular docking of the three-dimensional structures of MG and HSP90β indicated potential binding activity, with a binding energy of − 5.3292 kcal/mol (Fig. 2D). The theoretical binding model (Fig. 2E) revealed that the binding sites were concentrated around the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, primarily involving THR(A:179), LEU(A:102), ASP(A:88), VAL(A:145), and PHE(A:133). These interactions stabilize MG-HSP90β binding. Methyl gallate demonstrated direct, dose-dependent binding to immobilized HSP90β in SPR assays. Global fitting confirmed a 1:1 interaction with a dissociation constant (KD) of 16.0 µM, indicating a moderate micromolar affinity (Fig. 2F and G). CETSA further confirmed this interaction, showing the stability of the MG-HSP90β complex with increasing temperature (Fig. 2H and I). Western blot analysis of primary chondrocytes confirmed that both IL-1β and CIS-IL-1β upregulated HSP90β expression, whereas MG treatment suppressed the increase in HSP90β protein expression (Fig. 2K).

HSP90β is upregulated in OA rat models

To investigate the involvement of HSP90β in OA progression, we established OA models using the Hulth method and simulated cold stimulation on OA via an artificial climate chamber. We performed phosphoproteomic profiling of articular cartilage from control, Hulth, and cold-Hulth rat models. Heatmap analysis revealed distinct phosphorylation patterns among the three groups, with multiple sites exhibiting significant upregulation in OA samples (Fig. 3A). Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially phosphorylated proteins highlighted key signaling pathways related to cartilage degeneration and inflammatory responses (Fig. 3B). PPI network analysis further identified clusters of phosphorylated proteins potentially interacting with HSP90β, and 9 functional modules were identified using the Markov Cluster Algorithm (MCL), suggesting its central role in OA-associated signaling networks (Fig. 3C).

Consistent with these molecular findings, micro-CT analysis of tibial subchondral bone showed notable structural deterioration in OA rats, including trabecular thinning and loss of bone volume (Fig. 3D). Histological evaluation with H&E staining revealed disrupted cartilage architecture and chondrocyte disorganization, while Safranin O-Fast Green staining indicated decreased proteoglycan content in OA joints (Figs. 3F-G). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed significantly more pronounced inflammation (TNF-α) and cartilage degradation (MMP9) in the Hulth and cold-Hulth groups compared to the control group (Fig. S4), along with elevated HSP90 protein expression in the articular cartilage of both the Hulth and cold-Hulth groups (Fig. 3E). Quantitative assessment demonstrated significantly elevated subchondral bone mineral density (BMD) reduction and decreased trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in OA rats compared with controls (Figs. 3H-I). Correspondingly, Mankin scoring of cartilage degeneration was significantly higher in OA groups (Fig. 3J). Integrated optical density (IOD) quantification further confirmed marked upregulation of HSP90 in OA cartilage (Fig. 3K). Together, these results indicate that HSP90β is significantly upregulated at both the phosphorylation and protein levels in OA rat models, correlating with structural and histological features of cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone loss.

Clinical correlation and mechanistic analysis of HSP90β in OA

To assess the clinical relevance of HSP90β in OA, serum HSP90 levels were measured in 53 knee osteoarthritis (KOA) patients and correlated with short-term temperature fluctuations and clinical symptoms, and their characteristics are listed in Table S4. Serum HSP90 levels were negatively correlated with short-term temperature changes (r = − 0.483, P = 0.012) and daily mean temperature (r = − 0.485, P = 0.012; Figs. 4A-B). In addition, HSP90 levels showed positive correlations with Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) pain scores (r = 0.430, P = 0.032) and stiffness scores (r = 0.442, P = 0.027; Figs. 4C-D), indicating that higher HSP90 expression is associated with more severe clinical symptoms. Furthermore, partial correlation analysis revealed no significant association between HSP90 levels and short-term or daily relative humidity (r = − 0.217, P = 0.287; r = − 0.280, P = 0.167, respectively; Table S5), suggesting that humidity exerts minimal influence on HSP90 expression.

To elucidate the specific mechanism of HSP90β, a broad-spectrum signal pathway antibody array was used to compare the expression levels of signaling molecules between the control and IL-1β groups. In total, 1498 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and were primarily associated with cartilage matrix degradation, inflammatory responses, and apoptosis. Several key pathways include PI3K-Akt and NF-κB (Fig. 4E-G).

To elucidate the mechanistic role of HSP90β, neonatal rat chondrocytes were transfected with HSP90β siRNA. Silencing of HSP90β significantly reduced mRNA levels of key catabolic and inflammatory mediators, including MMP3, MMP13, IL-6, and ADAMTS5 which were upregulated in IL-1β and CIS treatments (Figs. 4H-K). Western blot analysis revealed that HSP90β knockdown also downregulated phosphorylation of PI3K and Akt, while total PI3K and Akt protein levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4L), suggesting that HSP90β modulates cartilage degradation and inflammation through the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. Furthermore, Western blot analysis also revealed that HSP90β silencing upregulated cartilage matrix-associated proteins Col2α1 and Acan, while downregulating the expression of iNOS and COX-2. In addition, HSP90β knockdown decreased the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved Caspase-3, while increasing the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 (Fig. S5A-B), indicating that HSP90β regulates cartilage degradation and apoptosis in chondrocytes. A schematic model summarizing the proposed role of HSP90β in OA pathogenesis is shown in Fig. 4M.

Collectively, these results indicate that HSP90β expression correlates with OA severity in patients and promotes chondrocyte catabolic and inflammatory responses, likely via activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway.

Synthesis and characterization of HA-MG nanoconjugates

We synthesized a hyaluronic HA-MG nanoconjugate via a disulfide linker to enable efficient delivery of MG into joint tissues (Fig. 5A). Detailed experimental conditions could be founded in HA-MG Synthesis in experimental section. Dynamic light scattering measurements revealed that HA-MG exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 12.5 nm (Fig. 5B) and a negative zeta potential of about − 10.8 mV in PBS (Fig. 5C), indicating good colloidal stability. Drug release studies demonstrated sustained release behavior in PBS over 72 h, ensuring efficient local availability of MG. Under physiological condition (10% synovial fluid), the drug release rate was increased as compared with pure PBS (Fig. 5D). The successful conjugation of MG to HA was confirmed by ¹H NMR spectroscopy in D₂O, which displayed characteristic peaks of MG and amide bonds (Fig. 5E). And drug loading of MG was determined to be 8.3% using a UV-Vis standard calibration curve (Fig. 5F). To evaluate cellular uptake, ATDC5 chondrocytes were incubated with HA-Cy5.5 for 6 h. Confocal fluorescence imaging showed robust intracellular fluorescence, confirming effective internalization of the HA-MG conjugates (Fig. 5G).These results demonstrate that the HA-MG nanoconjugate was successfully synthesized, possesses favorable physicochemical properties, and can be efficiently taken up by chondrocytes, supporting its potential as a therapeutic delivery system for OA.

Intra-articular injection of HA-MG enhances OA therapy by inhibiting HSP90β

The therapeutic efficacy of HA-MG was evaluated in a mouse model of DMM-induced OA, following weekly intra-articular injections (Fig. 6A). Comparative micro-CT analysis demonstrated that while both HA-MG and MG treatments reduced osteophyte formation around the femoral condyle and tibial plateau compared to untreated DMM mice, HA-MG treatment exhibited superior efficacy, resulting in a significantly greater reduction in osteophyte development. (Fig. 6B). Quantitative analysis of tibial subchondral bone demonstrated that HA-MG treatment significantly improved BMD and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) compared with the DMM and MG group (P < 0.05; Figs. 6C-D). Histological evaluation showed severe cartilage degeneration in DMM mice, as indicated by reduced chondrocyte numbers and cartilage matrix loss. H&E and Safranin O/Fast Green staining demonstrated that HA-MG markedly preserved cartilage architecture and proteoglycan content (Figs. 6E-F). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that HA-MG treatment maintained Col2α1 expression and reduced HSP90β levels in articular cartilage compared with MG and untreated DMM mice (Fig. 6G-H and K-L). Serum ELISA further demonstrated that HA-MG significantly lowered HSP90β levels in circulation (Fig. 6I). Correspondingly, OARSI scores indicated that HA-MG substantially attenuated cartilage degeneration compared with MG and DMM controls (P < 0.05; Fig. 6J). These results collectively indicate that HA-MG enhances the protective effects of MG, preserving subchondral bone and cartilage structure, maintaining Col2α1 expression, and reducing HSP90β levels, thereby providing superior therapeutic efficacy against OA progression.

Joint retention and biosafety of HA-MG

We prepared fluorescently labeled HA-Cy5.5 (HA-Cy5.5) and evaluated its drug retention in KOA joints compared with Cy5.5 alone using in vivo IVIS imaging and fluorescence signal quantification in the knee joint area. After a single injection, fluorescence signals in joints treated with HA-Cy5.5 remained significantly higher at all time points compared with joints treated with Cy5.5 alone (Fig. 7A, B), indicating that the HA complex slows the clearance of small-molecule drugs from the joint cavity and extends their retention time. HA-MG demonstrated high biocompatibility. In ATDC5 cells, apoptosis was reduced 48 h after the MG and HA-MG interventions (p < 0.05; Fig. 7C, D). After 7 weeks of intra-articular injection treatment, H&E staining of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues showed no differences among the HA, MG, HA-MG, and sham groups (Fig. 7E).

Discussion

In this study, we identified HSP90β as a key therapeutic target for OA. Our findings showed that HSP90β is upregulated in both human OA cartilage and animal models, and its expression correlates with disease severity. We demonstrated that MG, by inhibiting HSP90β, effectively reduces cartilage inflammation and degeneration. To enhance its therapeutic potential, we developed a HA-MG nanoconjugate that improved drug retention in the joint and showed superior efficacy in OA mouse models compared to free MG. These results highlight HSP90β as a critical target in OA and suggest that MG and HA-MG provide promising therapeutic strategies for the treatment of OA.

HSP90β, a member of the HSP family, is a molecular chaperone that assists in the proper folding, stability, and function of various client proteins [8, 24]. Numerous studies have shown that HSP90 can be induced under high or low-temperature stress [25, 26]. Monoclonal antibodies targeting HSP90 can effectively protect joint cartilage in OA [10, 27]. Our clinical surveys revealed that HSP90 is negatively correlated with external temperature and positively correlated with patients’ clinical symptoms of pain and stiffness. In animal experiments, we observed similar results. Rat OA models exposed to cold conditions exhibited higher HSP90 expression, more severe cartilage damage, and subchondral bone loss. Mechanistically, HSP90β may facilitate the maintenance of chondrocyte homeostasis by stabilizing client proteins that regulate inflammation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix degradation [28, 29]. In OA, overexpression of HSP90β contributes to the activation of pro-inflammatory mediators and MMPs, exacerbating cartilage breakdown [30]. This is consistent with our proteomic analysis, which identified key proteins involved in the inflammatory and catabolic processes in OA. By inhibiting HSP90β, MG was able to reduce these pathological changes, providing evidence that targeting HSP90β can attenuate OA-related cartilage degeneration.

MG is a naturally polyphenolic compound found in various plant types [31, 32], exhibiting anti-inflammatory effects in multiple experimental models. For example, Luana et al. found that MG can inhibit immune-mediated inflammatory responses in AIA-induced arthritis [33], and Zhou et al. demonstrated that MG can ameliorate immune colitis by modulating macrophage polarization and inhibiting the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway [34]. Our study demonstrated that MG exerts its therapeutic effects by directly inhibiting HSP90β, thereby reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and cartilage degradation associated enzymes in chondrocytes, both in vitro and in vivo. Through proteomic target identification and molecular docking techniques, we discovered that MG targets HSP90β and may bind to its N-terminal domain and affect the hydrolysis of ATP, consequently affect the function of HSP90β [35, 36]. Nevertheless, a significant challenge when using MG is maintaining sufficient drug concentrations around chondrocytes to induce the desired biological response. Like other small-molecule drugs, MG is rapidly cleared from the joint cavity through synovial capillaries and lymphatics. Our observations indicate that the effective concentration of MG is retained for less than one week. Consequently, the diminished long-term efficacy and the increased risk of infection associated with the need for frequent injections limit the clinical application of MG. Therefore, employing highly compatible biomaterials as carriers for the delivery of MG, thereby converting the spatial barrier of the joint cavity into a drug reservoir and prolonging the retention time of therapeutic agents, represents a highly promising strategy for treating OA [37].

In this study, we used HA to deliver MG. Compared with other biomacromolecules used in tissue-engineered constructs for OA therapy, HA presents distinct biological advantages, as a structural component of the ECM and a lubricating element in synovial fluid. Extensive studies have concentrated on the development of HA-based treatments. For instance, Rongshuai et al. developed a novel hydrogel using modified poloxamer 407 and hyaluronic acid loaded with keratinocyte growth factor 2 (KGF-2), which improved joint inflammation in a rat OA model [38]. Similar strategies were used to enhance the sustained release of extracellular vesicles derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) within the joint cavity, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy for OA [39]. These modification strategies not only preserve HA’s native lubrication function but also enable prolonged joint retention and controlled drug release, thereby reducing injection frequency and mitigating the risk of toxicity associated with burst drug release. Furthermore, such systems can actively target cartilage via electrostatic interactions, further improving localization and therapeutic precision [40]. In our experiments, MG was conjugated to HA molecules via disulfide bonds to form a conjugate, thereby addressing the challenges associated with MG delivery. This polysaccharide conjugate remains in the joint for over a week, allowing the combined anti-inflammatory effects of MG and the cartilage-protective effects of HA to contribute to OA treatment in a synergistic manner. Moreover, HA-MG exhibits excellent biocompatibility with no significant side effects on joint structures or surrounding organs. Overall, HA-MG addresses the short in vivo half-life and low cartilage penetration efficiency of free small-molecule drugs. Local delivery of HA-MG to the knee joint after OA injury effectively reduces cartilage degeneration and joint pain in mice. Despite its structural simplicity, the HA-MG conjugate effectively integrates and enhances the intrinsic chondroprotective benefits of both HA and MG. Such a concise design not only ensures biological efficacy but also offers advantages in scalability and translational potential compared with more sophisticated delivery systems.

HA-MG conjugate offers a dual-functional, synergistically therapeutic platform that is synthesized via a simple two-step aqueous process, and retains chemical versatility for modular expansion. Yet several limitations must be acknowledged. This study revealed that MG influences OA through modulation of HSP pathways. However, due to the high sequence homology among HSP90 family isoforms, the potential off-target effects of MG remain to be systematically evaluated, although intra-articular administration may partially mitigate systemic exposure. In addition, defining MG as a client-selective HSP90β inhibitor still requires further investigation, including studies on interaction affinity, binding and dissociation kinetics, and structural biology. Furthermore, while we successfully conducted gene interference experiments targeting HSP90 in an in vitro cell model and observed the effects of down expression of HSP90 on chondrocyte synthesis, degradation, apoptosis, and signaling pathways, the broad role of HSP90 suggests that in vitro models may overlook the complex regulatory networks and potential compensatory mechanisms at the organ level. Accordingly, we will pursue validation in animal models using gene-editing approaches as the next step of our research. In addition, DMM validation in the animal model was equivalent to the early stages of OA; therefore, the therapeutic effects on mid- to late-stage OA will require further validation.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings establish HSP90β as a critical molecular mediator of cartilage degeneration in OA and provide substantial evidence that targeting HSP90β with MG and enhanced HA-MG nanoconjugates represents a promising approach for mitigating OA.

Methods

Animals and grouping

Male Sprague-Dawley rats, SPF grade, 6 weeks old, and 150–170 g, were obtained from Shanghai Bikai Kexin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The animals were housed under standard conditions at 23–25 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle, and they had free access to water and standard laboratory chow. For the in vivo validation of the HSP90β target, the rats were randomly assigned to three groups: sham-operated (sham), Hulth (KOA), and cold-Hulth model. Male C57BL/6J mice, SPF grade, 8 weeks old, and 19–23 g, were purchased from Shanghai Bikai Kexin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. In the MG treatment study, the mice were randomly divided into five groups: sham-operated (sham), DMM with PBS treatment (PBS), DMM with low-dose (0.8 mg/mL) MG treatment (Low), DMM with medium-dose (4 mg/mL) MG treatment (Mid), and DMM with high-dose (20 mg/mL) MG treatment (High). MG treatment was administered via intra-articular injection of 10 µL of the specified concentration. For the HA-MG treatment study, the mice were randomly assigned to five groups: sham-operated (sham), DMM with PBS treatment (OA), DMM with MG treatment (MG), DMM with HA treatment (HA), and DMM with HA-MG treatment (HA-MG). Treatment was administered via intra-articular injection of 10 µL of PBS, MG (20 mg/mL), HA (without MG), or HA-MG (containing MG 20 mg/mL) once a week, starting 4 weeks after the DMM surgery. Mice were euthanized after the final injection (a total of 7 injections), and knee joints and other required tissues were collected.

Experimental OA and serum

In rats, OA was induced using the modified Hulth method [41]. After anesthesia, an incision was made on the medial side of the right knee joint to open the joint capsule. The subcutaneous fascia was carefully dissected to expose the medial collateral ligament, which was transected with a scalpel. The dissection continued to expose and transect the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments. In the sham-operated group, the joint capsule was opened similarly, but no additional damage was inflicted.

In mice, OA was induced using the DMM method [42]. After anesthesia, an incision was made on the medial side of the right knee joint to open the joint capsule. The medial meniscal ligament was cut to destabilize the meniscus without affecting other tissues. In the sham-operated group, the joint capsule was opened in the same manner, but no additional damage was inflicted.

An artificial climate chamber (provided by Shanghai Santeng Instrument Co., Ltd.) was used to simulate an cold environment; it was set to 95%±2% humidity, 4 °C ± 2 °C, and a wind speed of 4 m/s. After Hulth operation in KOA models, rats were housed in the artificial climate chamber for 8 h daily for 4 weeks to establish the cold- OA model (cold-Hulth).

To prepare CIS, blood was collected from the abdominal aorta following anesthesia, allowed to stand at room temperature for 2 h, and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and the serum, which had been heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min, was separated and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter to remove bacteria. The serum was then aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C.

Cartilage explant and cell viability

Primary rat chondrocytes were isolated in accordance with previously described methods [43]. Under sterile conditions, the cartilage caps from the bilateral humeral and femoral heads of neonatal rats were excised, minced, and digested in 0.2% type II collagenase (Solarbio, 17101015) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator until fully dissociated. The resulting cell suspension was filtered, centrifuged, and the chondrocytes were collected by discarding the supernatant. These cells were then cultured in a growth medium and passaged when they reached 70%-80% confluency. Second-passage chondrocytes were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 24 h before subsequent treatments.

The ATDC5 mouse chondrocyte cell line was obtained from Shanghai Qingqi Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. and cultured according to the method described by Mandy et al. [44]. Cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, under standard conditions of 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, and the media was changed every 2 days. Upon reaching 70%-80% confluency, the growth medium was replaced with induction medium containing 1% ITS liquid culture supplement (Sigma, I3146), and the cells were cultured for an additional 7 days. In this study, the concentration of IL-1β used to induce an inflammatory state in chondrocytes was set at 10 ng/mL, and cells were exposed to IL-1β for 48 h.

Chondrocyte viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To determine the concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, chondrocytes were treated with MG at various concentrations (0-100 µM) for 24–48 h. Optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured to assess cell viability. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of MG was calculated using nonlinear regression analysis based on the relationship between the percentage of cell proliferation and the concentration of MG.

KOA patients, WOMAC score for clinical symptoms and meteorological data

Clinical data and blood samples were collected from the FHS cohort, which included patients with KOA who visited the Department of Orthopedics at Shuguang Hospital between June 2017 and January 2018 [45]. The inclusion criteria were a K/L score of 2 on knee X-rays and a visual analogue scale (VAS) score for pain of 1 or greater. The Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) [46] was used to assess KOA clinical symptoms. This index measures knee pain (5 items), knee stiffness (2 items), and knee function (17 items) over the past 48 h. The traditional 24-item WOMAC scale utilizes a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from “none” to “severe,” with a total score of 0 to 240; higher scores indicate worse clinical symptoms of the knee.

Temperature, expressed in degrees Celsius (°C) and relative humidity, expressed in percentage (%) were defined as their mean values for the Shanghai region over 3 days: the day of the inclusion visit and the two previous days, using data obtained from the Shanghai Pudong New Area Meteorological Station website (http://www.smb.gov.cn/index.html).

HA-MG synthesis

MG (48 mg, 260 µmol, MCE, HY-N2010) was dissolved in a mixture of 15 mL dry dichloromethane (DCM) and 10 mL dry tetrahydrofuran (THF) in a round-bottom flask. Then, 4-nitrophenyl chloroformate (52 mg, 260 µmol, 1 eq) was added and the reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for 15 min. Following this, DMAP (28.6 mg, 235 µmol, 0.9 eq) was introduced, and the reaction was continued for an additional 3 h at room temperature. After completion, cysteamine dihydrochloride (59.2 mg, 260 µmol, 1 eq) was added to the reaction mixture, and triethylamine (TEA, 72 µL, 260 µmol) was further added to neutralize the hydrochloric acid. The mixture was left to react overnight at room temperature. The next day, solvents were evaporated from the MG reaction mixture, and the residue was dissolved in 1 mL of dry DMSO.

In a 50 mL reaction flask, HA (100 mg, 10 µmol, RHNWA, R034049) was dissolved in 10 mL deionized water. EDC hydrochloride (260 mg, 1.35 mmol) and HOBt (190 mg, 1.4 mmol) were added to this solution, and the reaction was stored at room temperature for 1 h while stirring. The DMSO solution of the MG mixture was then added, followed by the addition of TEA (42 µL). The mixture was then stirred at room temperature for 24 h.

After completion, the mixture was dialyzed using a 1 kDa dialysis membrane for 48 h, followed by centrifugation at 2,500 g for 15 min to obtain the upper liquid. The product was finally obtained after lyophilization. The drug loading capacity of the final product was subsequently determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer with a standard curve at 275 nm.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from chondrocytes using an RNA purification kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of the purified RNA was measured with a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000) and adjusted accordingly. Reverse transcription was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions (Accurate, No. AG11721), and the resulting cDNA was stored at − 20 °C. The target primers, deionized water, and 2X SYBR® Green Pro Taq HS Premix were pre-mixed and then added to a PCR reaction plate for amplification using a PCR machine according to the parameters specified by the reagent instructions. Each sample underwent at least three independent RT-qPCR amplifications. The PCR primers used in this study were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., and their sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Western blotting

Chondrocytes, either treated or untreated, were lysed in cold RIPA buffer containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein from each group were separated on 7.5%-12.5% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Germany). The membranes were blocked for 60 min and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Col2α1 (1:1500, Beyotime), Aggrecan (1:1500, Beyotime), MMP13 (1:1500, Beyotime), and BCL2 (1:1500, Beyotime). HRP-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam) was used as the secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). GAPDH (1:1500, Beyotime) served as the loading control. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell immunofluorescence assays

After fixing the cells, they were blocked with QuickBlock™ Immunostaining Blocking Solution for 30 min. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-Col2α1 antibody (#34712, Abcam, United States) at a dilution of 1:200. The next day, the cells were incubated at room temperature for 1 h in the dark with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (SA00007-2, Proteintech, United States). Finally, DAPI was added to stain the nuclei, and the cells were incubated in the dark for 5 min before imaging under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell apoptosis assay

According to the standard protocol of the Annexin V-FITC Kit (LinkBio, Beijing, China, AP101), ATDC5 chondrocytes were collected using a cell scraper and washed three times with pre-cooled PBS. The cells were then resuspended in 500 µl of Binding Buffer. Each tube was added with 5 µl of Annexin V-FITC and 10 µl of PI. After gently vortexing, the cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5 min. Apoptosis was detected using flow cytometry, with Annexin V-FITC measured in the FITC detection channel and PI measured in the PI detection channel.

Constructing siRNA for silenced HSP90β

To further investigate the role of HSP90β in cartilage, we constructed three pairs of small interfering RNA (siRNA) to silence HSP90β (Fig. S6). We observed the changes in synthetic and degradative metabolism in vitro cartilage cells. We designed three siRNA pairs through RiboBio Company (Table S7) and performed in vitro transfections. After transfection, we collected total RNA and protein from the cells to assess transfection efficiency. RT-PCR results showed that all three siRNA pairs achieved silencing effects (P < 0.05), with siRNA 3 demonstrating the most substantial silencing effect (Figure S7A). This finding was further validated at the protein level using Western blot analysis, which revealed that siRNA 3 maintained comparable silencing efficacy at 48, 72, and 96 h (P < 0.05; Figure S7B). Consequently, we selected siRNA 3 for subsequent experiments, using a 48-h transfection period.

Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay, mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, and bioinformatics analysis

To prepare the DARTS samples, we collected ATDC5 cells and extracted total proteins from the lysate, which contained protease inhibitors. Protein quantification was performed using a BCA assay kit, and protein concentrations were adjusted accordingly. Total proteins were aliquoted into two tubes, which were then incubated with equal volumes of DMSO and MG (50 µM) at room temperature for 1 h. After digestion with diluted pronase solution for 30 min, the digestion was terminated. The samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis, and differential bands were identified using Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. Gel bands were cut out and sent to the Chinese Medicine Chemical Biology Research Center at Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine for subsequent mass spectrometry analysis [47]. The selected proteins were subjected to Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org) to identify key genes.

Target prediction and molecular docking

The three-dimensional structure of the core active component of methyl gallate (MG) was retrieved from the TCMSP database (https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php), and the three-dimensional structure of HSP90 was obtained from the PDB database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Using Discover Studio software, the core active component was solvated and designated as the ligand. In parallel, water molecules were removed from the key target protein’s crystal structure, polar hydrogen atoms were added, and the original ligand was removed from the active site to prepare the receptor. Docking simulations were then conducted to explore the interaction between the ligand and receptor.

ELISA

TGF-β1 and HSP90 levels in mouse peripheral blood serum were determined using the Mouse TGF-β1 ELISA Kit (YEPCOME, BY-EM220862) and Mouse HSP90β ELISA Kit (YEPCOME, BY-EM220579), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. OD values were recorded at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Histological analysis

Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Beijing, China) and decalcified with EDTA. They were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 7 μm slices. The sections were stained using Hematoxylin-Eosin (Beyotime), Orange G/Green staining (Solarbio), and Masson’s trichrome staining kits (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Each specimen was evaluated by three orthopedic surgeons according to the modified Mankin’s score and OARSI guidelines [48, 49].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, then subjected to antigen retrieval in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Non-specific binding sites were blocked by incubating the sections with a 5% goat serum solution. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies, including HSP90β, at 4 °C overnight. After washing, sections were incubated with an appropriate secondary antibody for 1 h. Freshly prepared DAB chromogen was added, and color development was monitored under a microscope until positive signals appeared brownish-yellow. The reaction was stopped by washing with distilled water. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min to stain the cell nuclei, then mounted with neutral gum for observation under a light microscope.

Signal pathway antibody microarray (CSP100_PLUS) analysis

Proteins were extracted using lysis buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors from rat chondrocytes, followed by centrifugation at 13,200 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and stored at -80 °C. For protein labeling, biotin reagent was added to the protein samples, incubated with labeling buffer, and mixed with protein coupling solution. The labeled samples were incubated on the chip for 2 h at room temperature. After washing, the chip was incubated with Cy3-Streptavidin solution, followed by another wash. Finally, the chip was dried and scanned using an Agilent SureScan Dx Microarray Scanner at 532 nm. Data were analyzed using Z-score normalization, with a |Fold change| ≥1.2 considered statistically significant.

HA-MG characterizations test

1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker ASCEND spectrometer using a 600 MHz instrument with D2O as the solvent at 298 K. Chemical shifts (δ) were measured in parts per million (ppm) and the residual peak of deuterated solvents (D2O: 4.79 ppm) were used as the reference to all chemical shifts. The UV-Vis absorption spectra were measured at room temperature using a PerkinElmer Lambda 20 UV-Vis spectrometer. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) tests were performed using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Panalytical) with a 633-nm laser. Measurements were acquired for 15 s and averaged over 11 runs, with hydrodynamic diameter calculated from a number-based distribution. Zeta potentiometry was conducted with a disposable capillary cell (DTS1070, Malvern) via a Zetasizer (Malvern Panalytical) at sample concentrations of 0.1–0.2 mg/mL.

Drug release study

HA-MG was dissolved in PBS or PBS with 10% synovial fluid to prepare a 3 mg/mL solution. A 0.75 mL aliquot was placed into a dialysis bag, which was then submerged in a 30 mL bottle containing 20 mL of PBS (pH 7.4). The bottle was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. At specific time points (0 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h), 1 mL of the solution was withdrawn for analysis, and an equal volume of fresh PBS was added back into the bottle to keep constant buffer volume. The released drug was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 275 nm using UV-Vis spectrophotometry or HPLC analysis.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

Cells were incubated with or without HA-Cy5.5 for 6 h, then harvested and resuspended in PBS. Each sample was subjected to heat treatment for 3 min at varying temperatures (no heat, 37 °C, 42 °C, 47 °C, 52 °C, 57 °C, 62 °C, 65 °C, and 69 °C), followed by cooling on ice. To inhibit protease activity, 1% protease inhibitor was added, and the samples were then processed through three freeze-thaw cycles: freezing in liquid nitrogen for 3 min and thawing in a 37 °C water bath for 3 min to facilitate cell lysis. After centrifugation at 15,000 rpm, 4 °C for 20 min to remove cellular debris, the supernatant was collected and mixed with loading buffer. Protein thermal stability was assessed by western blotting to analyze the stability of target proteins.

Surface-plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR experiments were performed on a Biacore 8 K system (Cytiva) using CM5 sensor chips at 25 °C. The sensor surface was activated with a 1:1 mixture of 400 mmol/L EDC and 100 mmol/L NHS at 10 µL/min for 420 s. HSP90β protein (20 µg/mL in 10 mmol/L sodium acetate, pH 4.5) was immobilized onto the sample channel (Fc2) at 10 µL/min, reaching ~ 12,600 RU, followed by blocking with 1 mol/L ethanolamine hydrochloride (pH 8.5) at 10 µL/min for 420 s. For kinetic analysis, MG was serially diluted in running buffer (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.4) to seven concentrations (0.31–20.00 µmol/L). Samples were injected at 20 µL/min with 100 s association and 180 s dissociation. The chip was regenerated after each cycle with 10 mmol/L glycine-HCl (pH 2.0) at 20 µL/min for 30 s. All sensorgrams were processed using Biacore Insight Evaluation Software and globally fitted to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to obtain ka, kd, and KD.

Statistical analysis

For clinical studies, the effects of baseline age, sex, BMI, and duration of KOA were considered. Partial correlation analysis was used to examine the relationship between HSP90 and WOMAC indicators. A two-tailed test with a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 17.0, IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA). For in vivo and in vitro studies, statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All graphs were plotted, and statistical and quantitative assessments were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. Each experiment was conducted in at least three independent repetitions, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.
