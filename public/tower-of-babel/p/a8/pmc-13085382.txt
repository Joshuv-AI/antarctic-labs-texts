Rupture and dissection of aortic autograft following the ross procedure: a systematic review
Authors: Ria Ramkaran, Jessica Briscoe, Duke E. Cameron, Hanghang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Ross procedure offers physiologic hemodynamics and freedom from anticoagulation for young patients needing aortic valve replacement, yet rare catastrophic complications such as autograft (neoaortic) dissection and rupture can occur years later.MethodsPRISMA-guided search (inception–August 2025) identified peer-reviewed reports with patient-level data on presentation, imaging, management, and outcomes.ResultsEleven cases were included. Median age was 39 years (range 20–56) and 55% were male. Median interval from Ross to event was 10 years (range 6–18). Native bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) morphology was documented in 9 out of 11. Median autograft root diameter was 55 mm (range 50–90), with 60% occurring at ≤ 55 mm. Presentations were symptomatic in 7/11, incidental in 3/11, and fatal preoperative rupture in 1/11. Echocardiography was the most common initial test; diagnosis was confirmed by CT (6/11) or MRI (3/11). Disease was confined to the autograft root in 9/11. Ten patients underwent operative intervention with 100% survival to discharge (composite root replacement 6/10; valve-sparing or root remodeling 4/10).ConclusionsAutograft dissection or rupture after Ross is rare but life-threatening, concentrated among patients with native BAV, and can occur below conventional diameter thresholds. Vigilant lifelong surveillance and consideration of earlier elective intervention are warranted.

Background

The Ross procedure, first described by Dr. Donald Ross in 1967, replaces the diseased aortic valve with the patient’s pulmonary valve and originally reconstructs the right ventricular outflow tract using a homograft. Two principal techniques, subcoronary implantation and full-root replacement, aim to replicate native aortic valve anatomy and function while preserving pulmonary autograft growth potential, an advantage particularly relevant in pediatric and young adult populations [1].

Early enthusiasm for the Ross procedure reflected its physiological and clinical benefits: excellent hemodynamics, avoidance of lifelong anticoagulation, and favorable quality of life compared with mechanical prostheses [1]. Over subsequent decades, however, concerns regarding long-term durability, particularly autograft dilatation and regurgitation, led to a decline in its utilization during the late 20th century [2, 3]. Influential critiques subsequently characterized the operation as a potential “Trojan horse”, warning that late autograft failure could emerge years after initial success [4].

More recently, refinements in patient selection, operative technique, and postoperative surveillance have driven a renewed interest in the operation [5], with contemporary series reporting durable outcomes in appropriately selected adults and adolescents [6]. Despite improved outcomes, rare but catastrophic complications, such as autograft rupture and dissection, remain significant concerns. These events typically occur years after the index operation and carry substantial morbidity and mortality when not recognized promptly.

This review summarizes published cases of autograft rupture or dissection after the Ross procedure worldwide, focusing on presentation, diagnosis, management, and early outcomes to inform risk stratification and surveillance.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Fig. 1). Sources queried from database inception through August 2025 included MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Google Scholar. Search terms combined keywords and phrases relevant to the Ross procedure and autograft pathology: “Ross procedure,” “pulmonary autograft,” “autograft dissection,” “autograft rupture,” and “autograft complications,” with Boolean operators and truncations as appropriate. Reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were hand-searched to identify additional studies.

Eligibility criteria

We included peer-reviewed case reports or cohort studies that (i) described pulmonary autograft dissection or rupture after the Ross procedure and (ii) provided patient-level data on demographics, presentation, imaging/diagnosis, operative management, and outcomes. We excluded conference abstracts without full text, preprints, reviews without original patient data, and studies lacking extractable individual data.

Study selection and deduplication

Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and then full texts. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. When multiple publications appeared to report the same patient, we retained the most complete account and did not double-count cases. Duplicate records were removed prior to full-text review.

Data extraction

Using a standardized form, we abstracted age, sex, native aortic valve phenotype (e.g., bicuspid), Ross implant technique (subcoronary vs. full-root), interval from Ross to event, aortic root/ascending dimensions at presentation, clinical presentation, imaging modality, anatomic extent (root-confined vs. extension), operative strategy, in-hospital outcome, and follow-up findings when available. Details regarding autograft stabilization or reinforcement techniques at the index Ross procedure were inconsistently reported across studies and therefore could not be systematically extracted or analyzed.

Quality appraisal

Case reports were assessed with the CARE checklist; cohort studies were appraised with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Given the rarity and heterogeneity of reports, we performed descriptive synthesis only without meta-analysis.

Synthesis and statistics

Continuous variables are summarized as medians with ranges and categorical variables as counts and percentages.

Results

Demographics and risk factors

The systematic review identified 11 cases of autograft rupture or dissection following the Ross procedure [7–17] (Table 1). Among these, the Ross procedure was performed for predominant aortic regurgitation in 8 patients, isolated aortic stenosis in 1 patient, and mixed aortic stenosis and regurgitation in 2 patients. Preoperative aortic root dilatation at the time of the index Ross operation was reported in 2 cases. The median age at presentation was 39 years (range, 20–56), and 6 of 11 (54.5%) were male. The median interval from Ross to the adverse event was 10 years (range, 6–18). Native bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) morphology was documented in at least 9 of 11 cases; valve phenotype was not specified in two reports. An autograft root diameter was available in 10 patients, with a median of 55 mm (range, 50–90 mm); 6 of 10 (60%) events occurred at ≤ 55 mm.

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

Eight of 11 (73%) patients presented symptomatically, most often chest pain/tightness, dyspnea, or back pain; three (27%) were detected incidentally on surveillance imaging. There were two ruptures: one fatal preoperative autograft root rupture [7] and one contained sinus rupture with tamponade treated successfully [16]. Transthoracic echocardiography was the initial test in 5 of 11 (45%), typically prompting confirmatory cross-sectional imaging. Computed tomography was used in 6 of 11 (55%) and magnetic resonance imaging in 3 of 11 (27%).

In 9 of 11 (82%) cases, dissection/rupture was confined to the autograft root. Two (18%) showed extension into the native ascending aorta (one with arch involvement).

Management strategies and outcomes

Ten of 11 (91%) patients underwent operative intervention; one patient [7] died before intervention. Among the operated patients, procedures included mechanical Bentall in 4/10 (40%), bio-Bentall in 1/10 (10%), valve-sparing root replacement (David/Yacoub) in 2/10 (20%), modified root remodeling in 2/10 (20%), and replacement with a stentless porcine root in 1/10 (10%). In-hospital survival among operated patients was 100%. Limited follow-up was reported in four patients: two had mild residual aortic regurgitation (at 3 months and 3 years), and two had stable valve/root status (4 and 8 months).

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes worldwide evidence on autograft rupture and dissection following the Ross procedure and highlights three consistent observations: these events occur late postoperatively, they are frequently associated with native bicuspid aortic valve morphology, and they may arise at diameters below conventional surgical thresholds.

BAV as a frequently observed substrate

Most reported cases occurred in patients with non-tricuspid native aortic valve morphology, most commonly BAV. Where reported, the index indication for Ross was predominantly aortic regurgitation, with fewer cases performed for isolated stenosis or mixed disease. Incomplete reporting of operative details, particularly autograft stabilization strategies, limits the ability to determine whether valve phenotype independently contributes to late autograft failure. In addition, historical variability in valve classification raises the possibility that some valves labeled as bicuspid in earlier reports would today be categorized as unicuspid, a phenotype increasingly recognized to confer a more aggressive aortopathy [18].

Nonetheless, the recurrent presence of non-tricuspid valve morphology across cases supports biological plausibility. Prior studies have demonstrated intrinsic abnormalities in extracellular matrix composition, wall mechanics, and flow-related stress in patients with BAV or related phenotypes, which may persist at the neo-aortic root after Ross and predispose to maladaptive remodeling under systemic pressure [19]. Contemporary studies report up to 85% freedom from reoperation at 20 years in carefully selected BAV patients with pure aortic regurgitation [20, 21]. Accordingly, native valve morphology should be viewed as a potential risk marker that may warrant heightened long-term surveillance.

Surgical technique considerations

Interpretation of these cases must be framed by the era in which most index Ross procedures were performed. The majority originated from the late 1990s and early 2000s, before autograft dilatation was widely recognized and prior to routine adoption of stabilization strategies. Consequently, details regarding autograft reinforcement or external support were inconsistently reported and could not be systematically analyzed, and most cases likely involved unsupported autografts.

While the present case-based literature cannot adjudicate the comparative merits of free-root versus inclusion/stabilized techniques, contemporary series suggest that stress-shielding the autograft (e.g., inclusion cylinder, external support, or prophylactic management of a concomitantly enlarged ascending aorta) may mitigate late dilatation in predisposed patients [22]. Prospective, technique-specific registries are needed to determine whether such strategies reduce dissection/rupture risk in BAV-predominant cohorts.

Size thresholds and timing of reintervention

Event diameters clustered around the sizes that typically trigger elective surgery for native aortopathy, but a notable proportion occurred at or below 55 mm. Among patients with reported measurements, 6/10 events (60%) occurred at ≤ 55 mm (range 50–90 mm overall), underscoring that waiting for larger diameters may be unsafe in selected Ross patients [23]. Pregnancy-associated hemodynamic and hormonal stress was present in one case [9] and coincided with a large (65 mm) autograft root at presentation, reinforcing the concept that physiological stressors can accelerate destabilization in aortopathy-prone substrates. Taken together, the data argue for earlier cross-sectional imaging and consideration of elective reoperation in Ross patients with BAV, rapid autograft enlargement, or pregnancy planning, even when diameters are in the “borderline” 50–55 mm range.

Failure phenotype and operative strategy

Pathology was confined to the autograft root in most patients; a minority showed contiguous propagation into the native ascending aorta. This pattern may reflect stress localization at the autograft–native aorta interface, intrinsic differences in tissue properties between the pulmonary autograft and native aortic wall, or vulnerability at suture lines created during the index operation. Operative management reflected this spectrum. Root replacement (composite conduit or stentless root) was used in 60% of operated patients, while valve-sparing/root-remodeling strategies were feasible in about 40%, generally when pathology was localized and leaflet quality preserved. These experiences suggest two practical considerations: expeditious surgery is mandatory once dissection or rupture is suspected, and valve-sparing reintervention remains an option for carefully selected patients, preserving autograft hemodynamics and avoiding anticoagulation.

Outcomes and surveillance implications

Operative survival in reported cases was excellent, but one patient died of preoperative autograft root rupture. Given the late timing of most events (often around a decade post-Ross), lifelong surveillance is warranted: annual echocardiography, early transition to CT/MR when the root approaches 45–50 mm or growth exceeds ~ 3 mm/year, and lower thresholds for imaging frequency and elective intervention in higher-risk profiles. In parallel, strict blood pressure control to minimize aortic wall stress should be considered a core component of long-term management, particularly in patients with root dilatation, rapid growth, or additional risk modifiers such as pregnancy.

Limitations

This synthesis is constrained by small numbers, publication bias toward dramatic presentations, incomplete reporting (e.g., valve phenotype or imaging details), and heterogeneous follow-up. In addition, longitudinal pre-event imaging was inconsistently reported, limiting evaluation of autograft growth patterns preceding dissection or rupture. As a result, formal stratification by baseline valve geometry or hemodynamic phenotype was not feasible. Blood pressure status and antihypertensive management were also variably documented and could not be systematically analyzed. Incidence cannot be inferred, and causal statements regarding valve morphology or operative technique must remain tentative.

Conclusions

Autograft dissection or rupture after the Ross procedure is rare but clinically important and has been reported primarily in patients with non-tricuspid native valve morphology and autograft dilatation. Events may occur at diameters at or below conventional thresholds, supporting vigilant lifelong surveillance and timely cross-sectional imaging. In experienced hands, both composite root replacement and valve-sparing strategies yield excellent immediate outcomes, provided the diagnosis is made before catastrophic rupture.
