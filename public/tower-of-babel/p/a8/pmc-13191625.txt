Association of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist use with patient-reported outcomes after lumbar decompression or fusion
Authors: Jack Parker, Jane Brennan, Andrea Johnson, Matthew Peterman, Chad Patton, Justin Turcotte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are increasingly prescribed for diabetes and obesity, conditions that are common among patients undergoing lumbar surgery. Prior studies suggest comparable or improved complication rates among GLP-1 users; however, the association between GLP-1 use and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following lumbar surgery remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients undergoing lumbar decompression or instrumented posterolateral fusion from 2020 to 2025 at a single institution with 2 surgeons. Included patients completed Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Physical Function (PF) surveys preoperatively and at a minimum of 6 months postoperatively. Patients using GLP-1 agonists at surgery were propensity score matched 1:2 to nonusers based on age, sex, body mass index, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), diabetes status, procedure type, and number of operative levels. Primary outcomes included PROMIS-PF scores and achievement of minimal clinically important difference (MCID). Secondary outcomes included PROMIS Global Mental Health (MH), length of stay, discharge disposition, 30-day emergency department visits, and readmissions. Weighted linear and logistic regression models were used to evaluate outcomes, incorporating inverse probability of censoring weights to account for differential 1-year follow-up.ResultsThe matched cohorts included 80 GLP-1 users and 160 nonusers. GLP-1 users had a slightly lower CCI (3.2 ± 1.5 vs. 3.8 ± 2.1; p = .011). PROMIS-PF scores were similar at all time points, and rates of MCID achievement were similar between groups (56.2% vs. 51.9%, p = .615). PROMIS-MH scores were similar at all time points, though GLP-1 users demonstrated higher MH MCID achievement (53.8% vs. 37.0%, p = .040). These findings remained consistent in the weighted multivariate models. Subgroup analyses by procedure type showed no differences in PROMs at any time point. No differences were observed in perioperative or clinical outcomes.ConclusionsIn this single-institution cohort of patients undergoing lumbar decompression or fusion, GLP-1 agonist use was not associated with differences in postoperative functional recovery or complication rates. While a higher proportion of GLP-1 users achieved clinically meaningful improvement in MH, only modest improvements were observed on average. Overall, these results suggest that GLP-1 agonist use does not meaningfully alter PROMs following lumbar spine surgery.Keywords: GLP-1 receptor agonists, Lumbar decompression, Lumbar fusion, Patient-reported outcomes, PROMIS, Mental health outcomes, Obesity

Introduction

The prevalence of obesity and Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) continues to rise [1], with parallel increases observed among patients undergoing spine surgery [2]. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 agonists) are increasingly prescribed for the management of T2DM [3] and obesity [4]. Given that both elevated body mass index (BMI) [5] and glycemic control [[6], [7], [8], [9]] influence outcomes following spine surgery, GLP-1 agonists have become increasingly relevant in this population. Prior studies examining patients with T2DM undergoing lumbar decompression and/or fusion have demonstrated comparable or improved perioperative outcomes among GLP-1 users, including shorter length of stay, lower infection rates, and reduced rates of pseudoarthrosis [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. However, concerns regarding potential adverse effects of GLP-1 agonists, such as sarcopenia and nutritional status [[18], [19], [20], [21]], raise questions about their impact on postoperative recovery following spine surgery.

Despite growing interest in GLP-1 agonist use in spine surgery, data evaluating patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) in this context remain limited [13,14,16]. These measures play a central role in spine outcomes research, as spinal pathology is closely linked to functional limitation and diminished quality of life [22,23]. Accordingly, assessment of postoperative improvement using metrics such as minimal clinically important difference (MCID) and recovery trajectory is critical for capturing clinically meaningful change beyond absolute score differences [22,24,25].

Understanding whether preoperative GLP-1 agonist use influences PROMs has important implications for shared decision-making, risk stratification, and interpretation of outcomes research in lumbar spine surgery. While existing studies have largely focused on perioperative complications and weight-related outcomes, the relationship between GLP-1 agonist use and patient-reported recovery following lumbar decompression and/or fusion remains unclear. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the association between preoperative GLP-1 agonist use and postoperative PROMs in patients undergoing lumbar decompression and/or fusion. Secondary aims included assessment of hospital length of stay, discharge disposition, and 30-day emergency department return and readmission rates. We hypothesized that patients using GLP-1 agonists preoperatively would demonstrate similar improvements in PROMs and comparable clinical outcomes relative to nonusers.

Methods

This study was deemed exempt by the institutional review board (WCG IRB), and a waiver of informed consent was granted.

Study population

A retrospective observational study was conducted, including 1,162 patients who underwent lumbar decompression or lumbar fusion between March 2020 and June 2025. All procedures were performed by 2 fellowship-trained orthopedic spine surgeons at a single institution. Included patients completed the Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Physical Function (PF) v2.0 short form 10a survey at preoperative clinic visits and at a minimum of 6 months postoperatively. Patients were grouped based on preoperative GLP-1 receptor agonist use. GLP-1 receptor agonist use was identified by review of electronic medical records for a documented active prescription of semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, exenatide, or tirzepatide at the time of surgery. Propensity score matching was performed in a 2:1 ratio of GLP-1 agonist nonusers: GLP-1 agonist users on age, sex, BMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), diabetes status, surgical procedure, and number of operative levels.

Independent variables

In addition to the independent variables used for matching, rates of insulin-dependent diabetes, diabetes with end-organ damage, and last preoperative HbA1c (for diabetic patients only) were assessed. Patient body weights at 12 months preoperatively, day of surgery, and 12 month preoperative change in body weight were recorded. For GLP-1 agonist users, the duration of use preoperatively was evaluated.

Patient-reported outcome measures

The PROMIS PF v2.0 short form 10a and PROMIS Global Health v1.2 surveys were administered at all clinic visits during the study period [26]. Scores were recorded at baseline, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and >1 year postoperatively. The primary outcomes of interest were PROMIS-PF t-scores at last follow-up and achievement of the MCID for PROMIS-PF, defined as a 5-point improvement in t-scores from baseline. This threshold was selected as it represents a ½ standard deviation improvement using the anchor-based method applied to the PROMIS population normative t-scores. The secondary outcomes of interest were PROMIS-Mental Health (MH) t-scores at last follow-up and achievement of MCID for PROMIS-MH, as measured by the PROMIS Global Health v1.2 Mental Health component.

Clinical outcome measures

The secondary clinical outcomes of interest included length of stay (LOS), discharge to skilled nursing facility (SNF), 30-day emergency department (ED) return, and 30-day readmission.

Statistical analysis

An a priori power analysis was performed to detect a clinically meaningful 5-point difference in PROMIS scores. Assuming a standard deviation of 10, a mean of 50, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, and 80% power, 64 patients per group (total N = 128) were required. Differences in patient demographics, comorbidities, surgical characteristics, clinical outcomes, and patient-reported outcomes between matched groups were assessed using chi-square tests and two-sample independent t tests, as appropriate. Differences in PROMs by surgical procedure between matched groups were similarly assessed. Fisher’s exact test was used when assumptions for chi-square testing were not met.

To account for potential bias from incomplete 1-year follow-up, inverse probability of censoring weights was applied for each patient-reported outcome. Follow-up status was defined separately for PROMIS-PF and PROMIS-MH. Patients with either a recorded 1-year PROMIS score or a score collected beyond 1 year were considered to have the opportunity for 1-year follow-up. Weights were estimated using logistic regression models predicting follow-up, including age, sex, BMI, CCI score, diabetes status, type of surgery, and baseline PROMIS scores.

Weighted models were then used to evaluate predictors of PROMIS scores at the last follow-up and MCID achievement. Continuous PROMIS scores at the last follow-up were analyzed using weighted linear regression, while achievement of MCID was analyzed using weighted logistic regression. In each model, the main predictor of interest was GLP-1 use, and covariates included age, sex, BMI, CCI score, diabetes, type of surgery, baseline PROMIS score, and 12-month preoperative weight. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (Version 1.4.1717; © 2009–2025 RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA), and statistical significance was defined as p < .05.

Results

After propensity score matching, the final matched cohorts consisted of 80 GLP-1 agonist users and 160 nonusers, and were well balanced. Among the variables used for matching, age, sex, BMI, diabetes status, type of surgical procedure, and number of operative levels were similar between groups, with only comorbidity burden, as measured by CCI scores, being slightly lower in GLP-1 agonist users (3.2 ± 1.5 vs. 3.8 ± 2.1; p = .011). Across both groups, 82% of patients were diabetic, and 57% of patients underwent lumbar fusion; on average, surgeries involved 2 operative levels. Moreover, similar rates of insulin-dependent diabetes (GLP-1 users: 26.2 vs. Nonusers: 15.0%, p = .054) and diabetes with end-organ damage (GLP-1 users: 8.8 vs. Nonusers: 11.2%, p = .709) were observed between groups. In comparison to nonusers, GLP-1 agonist users presented with higher average body weight at 12 months preoperatively (220.9 ± 50.5 vs. 203.7 ± 46.5 lbs., p = .012), but reached similar weights by the day of surgery (214.9 ± 49.9 vs. 202.9 ± 48.5 lbs., p = .080), reflecting greater preoperative weight loss on average (−6.0 ± 15.0 vs. −0.8 ± 14.2 lbs., p = .012). Prior to surgery, GLP-1 agonist users used these medications for an average of 14.2 ± 14.3 months, demonstrating significant variability in duration of use (Table 1).

At all time points, PROMIS-PF scores were similar between GLP-1 agonist users and nonusers (Table 3; Fig. 1). The average change in PROMIS-PF score from baseline to last follow-up was also similar between groups (GLP-1 users: 4.9 ± 7.3 vs. nonusers: 4.7 ± 7.9, p = .851), although GLP-1 agonist users had slightly shorter follow-up duration (20.7 ± 13.6 vs. 25.4 ± 16.2 mos., p = .018). Similarly, PROMIS-MH scores were similar between GLP-1 agonist users and nonusers at all time points (Table 3; Fig. 2). Average change in PROMIS-MH scores from baseline to last follow-up was similar between groups (GLP-1 users: 1.4 ± 9.3 vs. 1.0 ± 9.0, p = .796), as was time to last follow-up (GLP-1 users: 17.7 ± 10.6 vs. nonusers: 20.7 ± 13.2 mos., p = .104). However, a higher proportion of GLP-1 users achieved MCID on the PROMIS-MH instrument compared with nonusers (53.8% vs. 37.0%, p = .040) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, preoperative use of GLP-1 receptor agonists was not associated with differences in functional recovery following lumbar decompression and/or fusion. Postoperative complications and healthcare utilization outcomes were also comparable between GLP-1 users and nonusers. Trends in PROMS between GLP-1 agonist users and nonusers were consistent across both decompression and fusion procedures. These results provide reassurance that GLP-1 agonist use does not adversely affect short- to midterm recovery following common lumbar spine procedures, but also suggest their use does not lead to improved early PF. As one of the first studies to evaluate the effect of GLP-1 agonist use on PROMs among lumbar spine surgery patients, these results suggest that GLP-1 agonist users may be counseled to expect recovery trajectories similar to those of patients with comparable risk profiles undergoing lumbar procedures.

Prior studies evaluating perioperative GLP-1 agonist use in spine surgery have reported mixed associations with clinical outcomes. Several retrospective analyses suggest use of GLP-1 agonists may be associated with lower pseudoarthrosis rates following fusion procedures [11,[14], [15], [16]], while systematic reviews highlight heterogeneity across study designs, procedures, and outcome definitions [13,14]. Other studies have suggested potential perioperative benefits, including earlier return of bowel function and shorter hospital LOS [10,12]. However, pooled analyses emphasize inconsistent effects across perioperative complications and clinical outcomes [13,14]. In this context, our findings contribute to the growing literature by demonstrating no difference in early postoperative outcomes among GLP-1 agonist users, supporting the safety of GLP-1 agonist use in this population.

While the relationship between GLP-1 agonist use and perioperative complications in spine surgery has been increasingly described, relatively few studies have evaluated PROMs in this setting. In a prior single-institutional study of 117 patients with T2DM undergoing lumbar fusion, Fano et al. reported that no differences were observed in PROMs [10]. The PROMs included in this study included the Oswestry Disability Index, Visual Analog Scale, and the Short Form-12 Mental Component and Physical Component Scores, all of which were unaffected in the GLP-1 agonist cohort [10]. In alignment with these results, the present study found comparable PROMIS-PF outcomes between groups, suggesting that GLP-1 agonist use does not meaningfully alter perceived functional recovery following lumbar decompression and/or fusion. However, our results deviated from those of Fano et al. with regard to MH changes. Interestingly, a greater proportion of GLP-1 users achieved MCID for MH compared with nonusers. MH comorbidities are common among patients with obesity and T2DM [27,28], and preoperative MH comorbidities have been associated with worse baseline disability and may predict poorer absolute postoperative PROMs [29,30]. Emerging evidence suggests that GLP-1 agonists may improve quality of life and MH outcomes in select populations [27,28]. However, in the present study, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it was not consistently observed in procedure-stratified analyses and was not accompanied by large absolute changes in PROMIS-MH scores. As such, additional research into the potential effects of GLP-1 agonists on MH in spine surgery patients is warranted.

Concerns have been raised regarding the potential adverse effects of GLP-1 agonists, particularly related to sarcopenia and nutritional status [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Sarcopenia is a well-established risk factor for poorer outcomes following lumbar spine surgery and has been associated with decreased improvements in PF, pain, and quality of life [31]. Prior studies have reported that lean muscle mass loss may account for a substantial proportion of total weight loss associated with GLP-1 agonist therapy [32]. In a recent study comparing obese patients who lost ≥5% of body weight within 2 years prior to cervical or lumbar fusion to those who maintained stable weight, Ibrahim et al. found that recent weight loss did not confer the same benefits as stable lower preoperative BMI, and may increase risk of some complications, potentially due to loss of muscle mass and nutritional deficiencies [33]. However, to our knowledge, direct studies of changes in muscle mass specifically among GLP-1 agonist users undergoing orthopedic surgical procedures have not been performed. Despite these concerns, GLP-1 agonist use in the present cohort was not associated with worse PF outcomes, suggesting that any potential muscle loss did not translate into clinically meaningful functional impairment during the follow-up period. Notably, GLP-1 users in the present study only lost 6 pounds, or 2.7% of body weight in the year prior to surgery on average. This is potentially due to the high variability in duration of GLP-1 use preoperatively, which displayed a standard deviation of over 14 months. While it is possible that the metabolic and weight-related benefits of GLP-1 agonists offset theoretical risks associated with muscle loss and nutritional deficiencies, larger studies with longer follow-up are needed to further evaluate this trade-off.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces the potential for unmeasured confounding, and the single-institution cohort limits generalizability. Although propensity score matching was performed, remaining differences in comorbidity burden or unmeasured patient characteristics may have influenced the observed results. Further, the indication for GLP-1 agonist use was not stratified, and the duration of therapy could not be reliably assessed. Notably, the majority of GLP-1 agonist users in the study (85%) were diabetic, limiting the generalizability of the findings in nondiabetic patients using GLP-1 agonists for weight loss. Follow-up was limited to the short- and midterm postoperative period, which may not capture longer-term differences in outcomes. Finally, the relatively small sample size may limit the detection of discrete but clinically meaningful differences. While a priori power analysis indicated the primary analysis was adequately powered, the subgroup analyses of decompression and fusion procedures were underpowered; the negative results of these analyses may be due to type II error.

Despite these limitations, the present study has several important strengths. Propensity score matching was used to balance key demographic, comorbidity, and surgical factors between groups, improving internal validity. Outcomes were assessed using validated PROMIS instruments with MCID-based interpretation, allowing evaluation of clinically meaningful recovery. Additionally, analyses included both decompression and fusion procedures, enhancing applicability across common lumbar surgeries.

Conclusion

In this single-institution cohort of patients undergoing lumbar decompression and/or fusion, preoperative GLP-1 agonist use was not associated with differences in postoperative functional recovery or complication rates. While a higher proportion of GLP-1 users achieved clinically meaningful improvement in MH, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory. Overall, these results suggest that GLP-1 agonist use does not meaningfully alter patient-reported recovery following lumbar spine surgery and should not independently change patient expectations.
