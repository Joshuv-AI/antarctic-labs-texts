Improvement of handling occupational health records and clinical records of employees at an in-house clinic
Authors: Kimiyo Mori, Chikara Kadoya, Kotaro Ishizuka, Chikage Nagano, Seichi Horie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder Japanese law, employers are required to manage health information related to occupational health. This report describes improvements in health information management at an in-house clinic that provides both general medical care and occupational health services. Initially, the workplace lacked formal regulations for handling health information, and the scope of data and responsible personnel were unclear. Health records were categorized by type and fiscal year, making it difficult for occupational health physicians to assess individual workers’ health status. Moreover, medical and occupational health information were mixed, raising concerns about unintended disclosure of personal medical data to the employer. To solve these issues, a health information management regulation was established. Records were reorganized to link data to individual employees, and medical information was clearly separated from occupational health data. These changes enabled more secure and appropriate occupational health services and supported the employer’s legal duty to ensure worker safety. This case highlights the importance of structured health information management in workplaces where clinical and occupational health services coexist. Clarifying data handling responsibilities and separating sensitive information are essential for protecting employee privacy and improving the effectiveness of occupational health practices.

Background

Under Japanese law, employers are required to manage health information related to occupational health. As an international standard for managing workplace health information, employers in many countries outside Japan typically do not retain employees’ health records. Guidelines set by the International Labour Organization and the International Commission on Occupational Health generally prohibit companies from collecting personal health information about workers1,2,3).

In Japan, the Industrial Safety and Health Act (ISHA) requires companies to conduct mandatory medical examinations for workers. Employers must obtain and retain the results, consult occupational health physicians (OHPs) for interpretation and assessment, and manage employee health accordingly4). Under the Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI), health data is classified as sensitive personal information, necessitating appropriate management to prevent unfair treatment of employees5). To ensure proper handling of such data, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) issued guidelines in 2018 titled “Measures for Companies to Properly Handle Information on Workers’ Physical and Mental Conditions”6). These guidelines allow companies to collect health information without employee consent under the ISHA. In workplace settings, it is recommended to establish and implement protocols for handling health information that balance effective utilization with the protection of employee privacy.

According to the guidelines, OHPs should be responsible for converting medical information into non-medical formats that are easy for employers to handle while ensuring employee privacy, for appropriate handling and management. These include:

∙ Results of statutory medical examinations

∙ Results of follow-up medical examinations (same statutory items)

∙ Outcomes of face-to-face consultations for employees with long working hours

∙ Psychological burden assessment results

∙ Outcomes of face-to-face consultations for employees identified as experiencing high psychological burden

Non-statutory health information must not be handled by employers directly; it should be processed only by qualified occupational health (OH) professionals, and only with the prior consent of the individual. However, in practice, OHPs are not involved in converting this information, and companies themselves handle medical data directly. In Japan, most small- and medium-sized workplaces with fewer than 1,000 employees are not required to appoint a full-time OHP. This is especially true for small-scale workplaces with fewer than 50 employees, which account for 96% of businesses in Japan7). In small- and medium-sized workplaces, especially those without OHPs, there is a significant likelihood that no personnel have expertise in personal information management, making it uncertain whether the required internal regulations are being properly implemented.

In Japan, particularly among large-scale employers, some companies establish workplace clinics to support employee welfare and provide medical care. According to a 2021 survey on medical institutions, there are 1,714 general medical clinics operated by companies8). According to a study by Nishio et al., an analysis of patient visits at a company clinic revealed that circulatory system diseases were the most common, accounting for 36.7% of visits. This was followed by respiratory system diseases (20.8%), urinary and genital system diseases (9.6%), digestive system diseases (9.0%), and endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases (7.5%)9). In company clinics, physicians may serve both as OHPs and medical practitioners, though some may not hold occupational health roles. Additionally, medical professionals — including OH nursing staff — may work either concurrently in multiple roles or separately in specialized positions. In company clinics, health information collected by medical institutions for treatment purposes must be handled separately from the information gathered by companies to manage workers’ health. If health information is not essential for ensuring workers’ safety, there is a risk that it may be shared with the company and repurposed in unintended ways, potentially leading to disadvantages for employees.

In such workplaces, where no full-time OHP is appointed, contracted OHPs are often assigned, and they are typically clinicians specializing in clinical medicine. In such cases, workers may visit medical institutions where OHPs practice for examinations and treatment of health check abnormalities or physical and mental illnesses. In some instances, the OHP may also serve as the worker’s attending physician. It remains unclear whether physicians can effectively distinguish between workplace health management data and medical treatment information. As a result, even at an individual level, the handling of personal health information by physicians raises concerns.

Although OH staff are identified as those responsible for handling health information within companies, some OH staff in Japan are also involved in the treatment of employees. Therefore, the possibility that they may handle both clinical information and health data collected by the company has not been sufficiently considered in guidelines. Furthermore, guidelines and manuals10) only state that “medical professionals” and “occupational health personnel” are involved in processing such data. In reality, some roles — such as public health nurses from health insurance associations or psychological counselors — are permitted to access only limited types of information. Additionally, human resources staff may hold a health supervisor qualification and thus be classified as occupational health personnel. Therefore, it is not practical to simply group all roles under “medical professionals” or “occupational health personnel.” This case illustrates a good practice tailored to workplace realities, clarifying differences in information handling based on job category, position, and level of involvement.

Issue and viewpoint on improvement

We report on a case on enhancing health information management at an in-house clinic that provides both occupational health services for workers and general medical care.

This report focuses on a non-ferrous metal manufacturing factory with approximately 890 employees, 500 of whom are engaged in hazardous work. The occupational health team responsible for employee health management consists of two OHPs (one full time and one part time), one nurse (health supervisor), one pharmacist, one OH nurse from the health insurance association, and an external counselor.

The information related to employee health management at the workplace includes results from statutory medical examinations (conducted at hiring, annually, for specific work tasks, overseas assignments, and special health checkups), as well as results from non-statutory examinations (eg, uric acid level tests and cancer screenings conducted in addition to the annual checkup). Results from follow-up and repeat examinations are also maintained; employees required to undergo further testing must visit a medical institution and submit the results. Additional records include psychological stress assessment results, face-to-face consultations with physicians, medical certificates submitted for sick leave procedures, medical information provided by attending physicians and obtained by OHPs, and records of health consultations. The company collects and retains additional medical data based solely on the employee’s implied consent; explicit consent has not been obtained.

All records are preserved in both electronic format within the health management system and paper format. At this workplace, health information collected by the company was stored by category (eg, medical examination results, OHP opinions, and medical certificates submitted by employees) and by fiscal year but was not organized into individual employee files. Although guidelines recommend organizing health information by statutory and non-statutory categories6), in practice, linking data to individual workers facilitates understanding of longitudinal changes and past health management efforts. In this case, it was difficult for the OHP to assess the health status of individual employees. Additionally, some information was not recorded in either the health management system or paper files, and details from interviews were retained only in the memory of the responsible staff. To help OH staff better understand each worker’s health status, managing health information individually was necessary.

Furthermore, various personnel were involved in occupational health roles, including OHPs, nurses serving as health supervisors, pharmacists, OH nurses from the health insurance association, and external counselors, as well as other health supervisors, managers, and human resources personnel. However, in the absence of formal regulation governing the handling of health information, it was unclear which personnel were authorized to access and manage specific types of sensitive data. Therefore, it was necessary to establish a regulation that clearly defines the roles and responsibilities of staff members authorized to handle sensitive personal health information, along with the appropriate methods for its management.

In this case, since medical professionals were employed at the clinic, general insurance-based medical services were also provided as part of employee benefits. Physicians, a nurse, and a pharmacist — all of whom also served as OH staff — were involved in delivering medical care. The clinic provided treatment for common conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hyperuricemia, colds, and allergies, and recorded a total of 896 patient visits in 2021. Medical services were limited to employees working on-site, and services were not extended to local residents.

The information handled by medical professionals in the course of clinical practice includes medical records, results of clinical tests, medication details, and billing information, which are comparable to the data managed by general medical clinics. These records are maintained in paper format for medical charts, while billing data is stored electronically on the reception computer system.

Medical information, such as medical records, constitutes sensitive personal data and must be clearly separated from health information collected by the employer. However, at this workplace and clinic, no clear distinction was made between employer-managed health information and clinical medical data. For example, occupational health interview records were included in medical charts, and documents related to leave and return-to-work procedures were attached to medical records. Additionally, an agreement was in place between the employer and the health insurance association regarding the scope of health information accessible to the public health nurse affiliated with the association. This access was limited to medical examination results, follow-up actions based on those results, and outcomes of health guidance.

Previously, no concerns were raised about mixing employee health information with medical records, nor was the data organized by individuals. Medical information with high clinical specificity — such as diagnoses, treatment details, and prognoses — can easily lead to bias or misunderstanding. Even with the same diagnosis, severity and level of control may vary significantly. If such information is shared with employers, there is a risk that it may influence decisions not to continue employment, regardless of actual work capacity. Therefore, separating health and medical information and improving their management became necessary.

Implementation

To resolve this issue, health data collected for occupational purposes must be clearly separated from clinical medical information. In clinical medicine, electronic health records are considered to enhance the efficiency of healthcare delivery, improve the quality of care, and strengthen collaboration among medical professionals11). Similarly, systematically managing health information collected by companies — by storing it as electronic data in dedicated health management systems and organizing paper records in individual folders — can not only improve the efficiency and coordination of OH staff, but also contribute to providing high-quality occupational health services to workers. As these records contain sensitive personal information, and in light of recent international developments and revisions to the APPI, it was necessary to clarify and formalize the scope of information that each category of personnel is permitted to handle5).

From 2022, health information obtained through occupational health activities was systematically entered into the electronic health management system, while paper-based documents that could not be digitized or required original retention were filed and managed individually in personal folders (Figure 1). We securely separated health data from clinical information and presented a privacy policy to obtain implied consent for using health check results in treatment, as the OHP also provides clinical care. Conversely, we explicitly stated that medical information obtained during treatment would not be shared with the employer without the individual’s express consent. This policy was based on the understanding that health check results generally contain less detailed information than clinical medical records, and that such results may be beneficial for treatment when shared with the attending physician.

Effect, impact, and outcome

Health information is now organized and managed on an individual basis, enabling OH staff to more easily access and interpret each worker’s health status. This improvement is considered to be instrumental in supporting the employer’s legal obligation to ensure workplace safety. Furthermore, by clarifying the roles and responsibilities of staff members authorized to handle health information, employees are now able to provide their information with greater confidence, and employers can more effectively implement health management measures.

We were able to clearly differentiate between health information and medical information. In cases where both types of data were recorded on the same medium — such as medical charts — there was a risk that medical information could be accessed by the employer, potentially resulting in disadvantageous consequences for the employee. However, this risk has now been significantly reduced. Although the OHP also provides clinical care, and health check data may support treatment, medical information from clinical care was not shared with the employer without explicit consent. No complaints or consent withdrawals were reported.

In addition, OHPs and nursing staff gained a deeper understanding of the importance of managing sensitive personal information through the implementation of structured data management practices and the clear separation of health information from medical records. Furthermore, they came to recognize the distinct purposes and professional roles associated with occupational health activities versus clinical medical services and were thereby able to carry out their responsibilities with greater clarity and appropriateness.

Implication

We reported a case of enhancing health information management at an in-house clinic that provides both occupational health services and general medical care. Specifically, we (1) established internal regulations for managing health information, (2) organized health data into individual employee files, and (3) separated health information from clinical medical records. We believe this report holds significance for the following four key reasons.

First, we established formal regulations governing the handling of health information, including health check results and medical certificates. These regulations clarified the roles and responsibilities of personnel authorized to manage sensitive personal health data, as well as the appropriate procedures for its handling. This initiative is believed to have reduced the risk of improper data management that could conflict with employee intentions and helped prevent disadvantageous outcomes, such as unfair treatment during promotion or job transfer. As a result, employees are now able to share health-related information with greater confidence, and it has become easier for them to consult with employers or OH staff when job-related accommodations are needed.

Second, we improved the organization and storage of health information, such as health check results and medical certificates, by linking records to individual employees rather than categorizing them by type or fiscal year. This approach enabled OH staff to manage employee health more accurately and efficiently, thereby contributing to both the maintenance of worker health and the employer’s legal duty to ensure workplace safety. To support treatment and work-life balance, access to health data is essential — even if not explicitly required by ISHA. As health data increases, managing it individually becomes increasingly important. The method of filing health records per employee, as described in this report, is considered both practical and effective, even for small- to medium-sized enterprises that may not have access to a comprehensive health management system.

Third, this case highlights the importance of enhancing personal information management practices at in-house clinics. By clearly distinguishing between medical information obtained through clinical treatment and health information managed by the employer, the risk of unintentional disclosure of medical data to the employer — which may not be necessary for occupational health purposes — has been significantly reduced. As a result, employees are less likely to experience personnel disadvantages, such as unjustified dismissal, contract termination, or work restrictions; prejudice or bias; and psychological distress caused by concerns over potential information leakage. In this initiative, because OHPs also serve as treating physicians, posters were displayed to obtain implied consent for the use of health check results and similar data in clinical care. Although individual consent is generally recommended for handling sensitive personal information, health check results typically contain less detailed data than clinical records and were therefore considered useful for informing treatment decisions. Conversely, explicit consent was obtained to ensure that medical information acquired during treatment would not be shared with the employer without the individual’s express permission. No complaints or requests for withdrawal of consent were received, and it was concluded that this approach did not result in any disadvantages for clinic users in this case.

Finally, this case underscores the importance of raising awareness among OH staff regarding the handling of sensitive personal information. As a result of the implemented improvements, staff members became more aware that the health information they manage constitutes sensitive personal data, and they are now able to maintain appropriate data management practices with greater diligence. In this case, a diverse group of OH staff — including OHPs, a workplace nurse, a pharmacist, a public health nurse from the health insurance association, and an external counselor — were involved in occupational health activities. By clarifying the scope of information each staff member is authorized to handle, it is believed that they were able to share health information securely and collaborate effectively across disciplines. In particular, OHPs, the nurse, and the pharmacist, who also provide clinical care, began to consciously distinguish between occupational health duties and medical treatment responsibilities, and performed their roles with a clear understanding of the differences in purpose and professional boundaries.

Managing health information on an individual basis and clearly defining the roles of personnel responsible for handling such data within in-house clinics is considered essential for providing employees with a sense of security and access to appropriate occupational health services. Moreover, this approach supports employers in fulfilling their legal and ethical obligation to ensure the safety and well-being of their workforce.

Currently in Japan, there exists a notable discrepancy in the handling of sensitive personal information, as defined by the APPI, between health information specified under the ISHA and other types of health-related data. Since many companies manage both categories of information, it is essential to establish clear and distinct regulations that differentiate between them. Such regulations should aim to promote the appropriate handling of health information in a manner that respects the autonomy and privacy of individual workers to the greatest extent possible.
