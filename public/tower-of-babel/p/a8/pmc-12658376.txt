NtCML19 Is Recruited by Tobacco to Interact With the Deacetylase Protein RsDN3377 of Rhizoctonia solani AG3‐TB, Inhibiting Fungal Infection
Authors: Xinchun Li, Yan Li, He Liu, Mengnan An, Zihao Xia, Chong Zhang, Wenfu Chen, Yuanhua Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe necrotrophic fungal pathogen Rhizoctonia solani anastomosis group 3 (AG3‐TB) is a major cause of global tobacco crop yield losses. Secreted proteins produced by filamentous fungi, as important virulence factors, play a core role in the interaction between plants and pathogens. In this study, we identified a secretory protein, RsDN3377, which localised to the intercellular space and induced cell death in Nicotiana benthamiana. Heterologous expression in Escherichia coli coupled with matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionisation time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry analysis confirmed RsDN3377 possessed deacetylase activity. In addition, RsDN3377 was an essential pathogenicity factor for mycelial development by double‐stranded RNA‐mediated gene silencing. Through yeast two‐hybrid and bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays, we demonstrated that RsDN3377 interacted with the calcium‐binding protein NtCML19. In addition, transgenic Yunyan 87 overexpressing NtCML19 exhibited enhanced resistance to R. solani AG3‐TB infection. Microscale thermophoresis analysis verified the calcium‐binding activity of NtCML19. These lines of evidence indicate that the deacetylase RsDN3377 is secreted by R. solani AG3‐TB, and this protein, critical for promoting fungal mycelial development and pathogenicity, was disrupted by its resistance‐related interaction with NtCML19.Keywords: calcium‐binding protein, deacetylase, Rhizoctonia solani AG3‐TB, tobacco

Introduction

Rhizoctonia solani is a basidiomycete species with a broad host range, including rice, maize, beet and tobacco (Taheri and Tarighi 2011). This pathogen induces necrosis in aboveground plant tissues, thereby posing a threat to crop yield and quality (Singh et al. 2019). R. solani can be classified into at least 14 distinct anastomosis groups, AG‐1 to AG‐13 and a bridging isolate AG‐BI (Taheri and Tarighi 2011). R. solani AG1‐IA causes rice sheath blight, characterised by cloud‐like lesions on leaf sheath tissue, whereas R. solani AG3‐TB induces tobacco target spot disease, which manifests as leaf perforations that reduce yield and quality (Li et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2012). Over the past decade, although multiple virulence factors have been identified in R. solani AG1‐IA (Zheng et al. 2013), the molecular mechanisms by which key proteins secreted by R. solani AG3 directly participate in modulating plant defence responses remain unclear.

Most fungal pathogens actively secrete proteins to regulate plant immune responses and maintain the integrity and development of the hyphae (Asai and Shirasu 2015; Thines and Kamoun 2010). For example, chitinase is a virulence factor that reshapes cell walls to support hyphal growth and generates chitin oligomers through deacetylation, thereby evading host recognition (Tian et al. 2022). Verticillium dahliae has been reported to secrete serine protease 1 (SSEP1), which hydrolyses cotton chitinase 28 (Chi28). However, this process can be interrupted by cysteine‐rich repeat protein 1 (CRR1), which stabilises the production of chitin oligomers (Han et al. 2019). Plants have evolved a sophisticated immune system capable of recognising pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as fungal cell wall‐derived chitin, through plasma membrane‐localised pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), thereby initiating defence responses against pathogen invasion. As a functional chitinase, Magnaporthe oryzae MoChia1 suppresses plant immunity by binding to free chitin, while the rice OsTPR1 protein competitively binds with MoChia1 to reestablish immune responses (Yang et al. 2019). A secreted‐aspartic protease TiAP1 generated by Thinopyrum intermedium inactivates the function of chitin deacetylase (BgtCDA1) and enhances host resistance (Yang, Gu, et al. 2022; Yang, Fan, et al. 2022).

Effectors play pivotal roles in host–pathogen interactions and are recognised by host resistance (R) proteins, triggering defence responses (Dou and Zhou 2012; Selin et al. 2016). For instance, the effector protein MoErs1 secreted by M. oryzae interacts with the rice papain‐like cysteine protease OsRD21 to modulate rice immune responses (Liu et al. 2024). Ustilago maydis effector Jasmonate/Ethylene signalling inducer 1 (Jsi1) activates ethylene response factors (ERFs) via the interaction of EAR motif and TPL/TPR proteins (Darino et al. 2020). In Cladosporium fulvum and M. oryzae , effectors like Avr4 and Mg3LysM bind with long‐chain and short‐chain homopolymers to avoid plant chitinase (van den Burg et al. 2006; Marshall et al. 2011). In R. solani , proteinaceous exudates such as effectors and enzymes are required for the colonisation and infection (Zheng et al. 2013; Wibberg et al. 2014, 2016; Anderson et al. 2017). Most effectors in R. solani AG1‐IA, including RsIA CtaG/Cox11 (AG1IA_05310), RsIA_peptidase_S8 (AG1IA_07795) and RsIA_GT (AG1IA_09161), have been experimentally verified to induce necrosis symptoms on leaves by infiltrating recombinant proteins produced in Escherichia coli (Zhang et al. 2023). Although a total of 807 secretory proteins, including 124 apoplastic effectors, 236 cytoplasmic effectors and 78 small cysteine‐rich proteins (SCR) have been identified in R. solani AG3‐TB (Li et al. 2022), their functions remain uncharacterised, particularly in immunity activation or chitin signalling suppression.

Based on previous studies, we selected 10 highly expressed genes, RsDN3377, RsDN21085, RsDN10085, RsDN12825, RsDN20094, RsDN22565, RsDN22904, RsDN22462, RsDN22450 and RsDN21125, to validate their functions, using reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (RPKM) and read values as criteria (RPKM ≥ 200; read counts ≥ 100). Transient expression of RsDN3377 in Nicotiana benthamiana triggered cell death and a reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst. Biochemical characterisation via heterologous expression in E. coli coupled with matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionisation time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF MS) analysis confirmed that RsDN3377 functions as a chitin deacetylase, whose deacetylase activity is closely associated with motifs I and II of RsDN3377. Following functional studies using double‐stranded RNA‐mediated gene silencing demonstrated that RsDN3377 plays a critical role in fungal hyphal development. In addition, transient expression of RsDN3377 with mutations in motifs I and II still induced cell necrosis and the ROS burst. Yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay, bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) analysis showed that RsDN3377 interacts with the calcium‐binding protein NtCML19 of the host plant. Transgenic tobacco Yunyan 87 overexpressing NtCML19 confirmed that NtCML19 is a resistance gene against R. solani AG3 infection. These findings demonstrate that RsDN3377, a chitin deacetylase critical for regulating mycelial growth in R. solani AG3‐TB, could be recognised by the host calcium‐binding protein NtCML19, thereby inducing cell death and enhancing host immunity.

Results

RsDN3377 Induces a Hypersensitive Response in Plants

In our previous investigation (Li et al. 2022; Figure S1), transcriptomic profiling across distinct infection stages of R. solani AG3‐TB yielded 807 putative secreted proteins. Among these, 10 genes (RsDN3377, RsDN21085, RsDN10085, RsDN12825, RsDN20094, RsDN22565, RsDN22904, RsDN22462, RsDN22450 and RsDN21125) were selected based on stringent expression thresholds: ≥ 100 reads and ≥ 200 RPKM. The relative expression levels of the 10 genes were validated by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR). The results indicated that these genes were significantly induced at 12, 48 and 72 h post‐infection (hpi) (Figure 1A).

To identify potential virulence genes, we employed an agroinfiltration approach in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves to examine the cell necrosis‐inducing activity of the 10 genes. While no cell death symptoms were observed in the GFP‐negative control group, leaves expressing RsDN3377 exhibited clear cell death symptoms similar to the positive control (Bax protein; Figure 1B). Furthermore, we evaluated the accumulation of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the area surrounding the necrotic‐like lesion induced by agroinfiltration with RsDN3377 using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining. Compared to the negative control, RsDN3377 induced the accumulation of H2O2 5 days after infiltration (Figure 1C). These findings collectively demonstrated that RsDN3377 triggered a hypersensitive response (HR) in plants, indicating its important function during R. solani AG3‐TB infection.

RsDN3377 Is Associated With Mycelium Development and Pathogenicity of R. solani AG3‐TB

To investigate the sequence features of RsDN3377, we analysed its amino acid sequence using InterPro and UniProt. Sequence analysis revealed that RsDN3377 has a NodB homologous domain belonging to the esterase family 4 (Figure 2A). Populations of R. solani possess high genetic diversity in terms of pathogenicity and growth habits. Double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) was applied to silence the expression of the RsDN3377 gene, aiming to analyse the role of this gene during pathogen infection (Ding et al. 2025). dsRNA‐DN3377 was generated through T7 in vitro transcription, and incubated with the YC‐9 strain of R. solani AG3‐TB. Compared to the dsRNA‐GFP control the hyphae of dsRNA‐DN3377 treated YC‐9 exhibited crumpled and deformed morphology (Figure 2B). Red fluorescence was observed from the hyphae that were incubated with Cy3‐labelled dsRNA, which confirmed that dsRNA could be absorbed in the fungal hyphae (Figure 2C).

To further evaluate the impact of RsDN3377 silencing on fungal pathogenicity, we treated R. solani AG3 with 200 ng/μL dsRNA‐DN3377 on tobacco leaves. The results indicated that dsRNA‐DN3377 treatment significantly reduced lesion diameter caused by pathogen infection compared to the control at 48 and 72 hpi, resulting in lesion diameters of 0.28 and 0.40 cm, respectively (Figure 2D,E). RT‐qPCR analysis confirmed that RsDN3377 expression in R. solani was effectively reduced to 41.1% at 48 hpi and 66.1% at 72 hpi compared to the control (Figure 2D). Collectively, these results demonstrated that RsDN3377 plays a critical role in both hyphal development and pathogenicity of R. solani AG3‐TB.

RsDN3377 Is a Secreted Protein and Localises in the Extracellular Space of N. benthamiana

The putative signal peptide (SP) of RsDN3377 was predicted using SignalP v. 6.0, and its functionality was experimentally validated via a yeast signal sequence trap system (Jacobs et al. 1997). We cloned the N‐terminal 29 amino acid SP of RsDN3377 and Avr1bSP (positive control) into the pSUC2 vector and transformed it into invertase‐deficient YTK12 yeast. Successful transformations were selected on both CMD−W medium (without tryptophan) and YPRAA plates, demonstrating that the empty pSUC2 vector and recombinant constructs were transformed into the yeast strain. Notably, consistent with the positive control Avr1bSP, RsDN3377SP grew normally on YPRAA plates, indicating functional invertase secretion (Figure 3A). Further validation through 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining revealed strong invertase activity in both Avr1bSP and RsDN3377SP transformants, as evidenced by the formation of insoluble red 1,3,5‐triphenylformazan (TPF) (Figure 3A). In contrast, negative controls failed to produce this characteristic red colour (Figure 3A).

To further test the secretory function of RsDN3377 in plants, we generated GFP fusion constructs of both full‐length RsDN3377 (RsDN3377:GFP) and its SP‐deleted variant (RsDN3377ΔSP:GFP) for transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves. NaCl‐induced plasmolysis assays showed that RsDN3377:GFP was secreted into the extracellular space, while both RsDN3377ΔSP:GFP and GFP remained intracellular (Figure 3B). Additionally, western blot analysis using anti‐GFP antibodies confirmed the expression of eGFP (25 kDa), RsDN3377:GFP (53 kDa) and RsDN3377ΔSP:GFP (50 kDa) (Figure 3C).

To assess the function of SP of RsDN3377 in cell death induction, we performed Agrobacterium tumefaciens ‐mediated transient expression of full‐length RsDN3377 and its SP‐deletion mutant (RsDN3377ΔSP) in N. benthamiana leaves. The results demonstrated that transiently expressed RsDN3377 and RsDN3377ΔSP triggered cell death in the leaves, which was consistent with the positive control (Figure 3D). Furthermore, DAB staining analysis confirmed that RsDN3377 and RsDN3377ΔSP promoted H2O2 accumulation at infiltration sites (Figure 3D). Combined with our previous findings, the SP of RsDN3377 is not the key region that triggers HR.

RsDN3377 Has Deacetylase Activity and Depends on NodB Homologue in the Motif I and II

Comparison of chitin deacetylases (CDA) and polysaccharide deacetylases (PDA) produced by Colletotrichum lindemuthianum (AY633657), Cryptococcus neoformans (AJ414580), Flammulina velutipes (AB250372), Mucor rouxii (Z19109), Saccharomyces cerevisiae (AY557948), Schizophyllum commune (AF271216) and Verticillium dahliae (NW_009276934) demonstrated that five motifs in RsDN3377 possess high similarity with these characterised deacetylases (Figure 4A). N‐acetyl‐d‐glucosamine (GlcNAc) could be deacetylated to d‐glucosamine (GlcN) by enzymes possessing deacetylase activity (Figure 4B). To test the deacetylase activity of RsDN3377, we expressed and purified the full‐length RsDN3377ΔSP fused with a His‐tag in E. coli (Figure 4C). The enzymatic activity of RsDN3377ΔSP was tested using chitosan containing six GlcNAc moieties (A6), and the reaction products were analysed by MALDI‐TOF MS. Results showed that the substrate A6 (molecular weight: 1275.526) was partially deacetylated, yielding the product A4D2 (molecular weight: 1175.539; Figure 3D–F). Moreover, to evaluate the connection between active motifs and deacetylase activity in RsDN3377, a mutant protein RsDN3377ΔSPD19G,H74G in which the critical aspartic acid (D) and histidine (H) residues in motifs I and II were substituted with glycine (G), was purified following expression in E. coli (Figure 4A, Figure S2A). MALDI‐TOF MS analysis revealed that RsDN3377ΔSPD19G,H74G protein lost its deacetylase activity, as the reaction product (molecular weight: 1259.442) showed no molecular weight shift compared to the unmodified A6 (molecular weight: 1275.526; Figure S2B), demonstrating that the active motifs in RsDN3377 were essential for enzymatic function.

In addition, to determine whether deacetylase activity of RsDN3377 was required for cell necrosis induction, we agroinfiltrated N. benthamiana leaves with Agrobacterium strains carrying the RsDN3377D48G,H103G and RsDN3377ΔSPD19G,H74G constructs. Both RsDN3377D48G,H103G and RsDN3377ΔSPD19G,H74G constructs triggered cell death symptoms and H2O2 accumulation at the infiltration sites, similar to that of the positive control (Figure S2C). These results indicated that while the motifs were crucial for the deacetylase activity of RsDN3377, this enzymatic function was not required for the induction of HR caused by RsDN3377.

RsDN3377 Interacts With Host Calcium‐Binding Protein NtCML19

To investigate the mechanism by which RsDN3377 induced cell death, we analysed the phosphorylation status and expression levels of resistance‐related genes in Nicotiana tabacum following treatment with the deacetylation products A6 and D6. Both treatments significantly enhanced the proteins' phosphorylation in seedlings and upregulated the expression of RLK1 and FLS2, two key genes involved in immunity‐triggering responses (Figure S3A,B). To further elucidate how RsDN3377 modulated plant resistance, we performed transcriptomic analysis to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in seedlings treated with A6 and D6. Among the 187 DEGs associated with plant–pathogen interactions, we identified several critical regulators, including calcium‐binding proteins (NtCML37, NtCML19), transcription factors (NtWRKY22, NtWRKY53, NtWRKY31), and genes implicated in pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI; NtTNT1‐94, NtRPV1, NtCML19 and NtRD19a) (Figure S3C–E). The upregulation of NtTNT1‐94, NtRPV1, NtCML19 and NtRD19a was further validated by RT‐qPCR (Figure S3F), confirming their role in the RsDN3377‐mediated resistance response.

Furthermore, the integration of RNA‐seq data with predictions from PyMOL software revealed that RsDN3377 interacted with the calcium‐binding protein NtCML19 through hydrogen bonding (Figure S4). To validate this interaction, we performed a Y2H assay that confirmed the binding between RsDN3377 and NtCML19 (Figure 5A). Subsequently, BiFC assays supported this physical interaction in planta (Figure 5B). In addition, to functionally assess the role of NtCML19 in RsDN3377‐induced cell death, we employed a virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) system to decrease NtCML19 expression in N. benthamiana. Compared to the GFP‐silenced control, NtCML19‐silenced leaves no longer exhibited cell necrosis upon transient expression of RsDN3377, for which the silencing efficiency was 56.7% confirmed by RT‐qPCR (Figure 5C,D). These results suggested that RsDN3377 interacts with NtCML19, which is required for RsDN3377‐mediated cell death.

NtCML19 Play Central Roles in Host Resistance Against R. solani AG3‐TB

To further investigate the role of NtCML19 in disease resistance, we inoculated R. solani AG3‐TB on NtCML19‐silenced leaves and measured lesion development. The results showed that NtCML19‐silenced leaves exhibited significantly larger lesion diameters at both 48 and 72 hpi than GFP‐silenced control (Figure S5A,B). Notably, RsDN3377 expression was markedly upregulated at 48 hpi but declined at 72 hpi following R. solani infection (Figure S5C). To further confirm the role of NtCML19 in resistance against R. solani infection, transgenic Yunyan 87 plants with either overexpression (NtCML19‐OE) or knockout (NtCML19‐KO) of NtCML19 were generated. The successful transformatants of NtCML19‐OE were subjected to RT‐qPCR and PCR (Figure 6C and Figure S6). Following R. solani AG3‐TB infection, NtCML19‐OE plants exhibited significantly smaller lesion diameters compared to wild‐type WT and NtCML19‐KO controls (Figure 6B,D). Given that RsDN3377 physically interacted with NtCML19 (Figures 5B and 6C) and that overexpressed NtCML19 enhanced resistance to R. solani , we determined that NtCML19 is a defence‐related gene activated against R. solani AG3‐TB infection.

To investigate the biological function of NtCML19, we analysed its sequence using InterPro and UniProt. Bioinformatic analysis revealed that NtCML19 is a calcium‐binding protein containing four EF‐hand domains and eight calcium‐ion binding sites (Figure 6A). To characterise the calcium‐binding function of NtCML19, we purified NtCML19 protein with His‐tag (Figure 6E) and assessed its calcium‐binding capacity using microscale thermophoresis (MST). The MST analysis confirmed that NtCML19 exhibited specific calcium‐binding activity, with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 2.25 ± 0.18 μM (Figure 6F). In addition, subcellular localisation studies revealed that NtCML19:GFP fusion protein localised to the plasma membrane and cytoplasm in N. benthamiana leaves (Figure 6G). Taken together, these results demonstrated that NtCML19 is a calcium sensor that plays a critical role in plant defence against R. solani AG3‐TB infection.

Discussion

Fungal pathogens secrete proteins that accumulate in the intercellular matrix, playing critical roles in host–pathogen interactions through their translocation into plant cells (Baker et al. 2011; Nguyen et al. 2018). To date, various classes of secretory proteins, including effectors and small cysteine‐rich (SCR) proteins, have been widely implicated in pathogenesis. For instance, RsMf8HN a secreted protein from R. solani AG1‐IA containing a 17 aa SP, exhibits elicitor activity in N. benthamiana and localises to the chloroplast and membrane (Niu et al. 2023). In this study, we identified RsDN3377, a secretory protein whose expression significantly increased at 24 h during R. solani AG3‐TB infection. Transient expression of RsDN3377 in N. benthamiana via Agrobacterium infiltration confirmed its elicitor activity, inducing cell death and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. RsDN3377 could secrete into the intercellular space via the classical secretory pathway, facilitated by its 29 amino acid SP. These findings are consistent with a previous report showing that, among 11 putative effectors, only RsIA_NP8 triggered cell death in N. benthamiana and plays a critical role in R. solani AG1‐IA–host interactions (Wei et al. 2020). Interestingly, our study further revealed that both full‐length RsDN3377 and its SP‐deleted variant (RsDN3377ΔSP) retained elicitor activity when overexpressed. This suggests that while the SP is essential for protein secretion, it appears dispensable for triggering plant immune responses. Collectively, our results demonstrated that RsDN3377 is a crucial fungal elicitor that activates plant immune responses, including cell death and ROS accumulation, during R. solani AG3‐TB infection.

Fungal polysaccharide/chitin deacetylases play a crucial role in modifying chitin into chitosan, thereby protecting hyphae from degradation and preventing the release of chitin oligomers when attacked by host chitinases (Mauch et al. 1988; Ride and Barber 1990; Stegrist and Kauss 1990). In this study, we identified RsDN3377 from R. solani AG3‐TB as a putative polysaccharide/chitin deacetylase, which contains five conserved motifs and a NodB homology domain characteristic of the carbohydrate esterase family 4 (CE‐4). MALDI‐TOF MS analysis confirmed its deacetylase activity, with motifs I and II being critical for enzymatic function. Intriguingly, these catalytic motifs were dispensable for the elicitor activity of RsDN3377, which was found to trigger cell death and ROS accumulation in N. benthamiana. This functional divergence suggests that deacetylases in fungal pathogens share structural similarities but exert distinct roles during plant interactions. For instance, in Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici, the closely related deacetylases Pst_13661 and Pst_13662 exhibit differential virulence roles, with Pst_13661 being a virulence factor suppressing plant immune recognition, while Pst_13662 is nonessential for infection (Xu et al. 2020). Similarly, a total of 12 protein‐encoding gene clusters in U. maydis collectively regulate virulence and infection (Kämper et al. 2006). In this study, unlike Pst_13661, which inhibits Bax‐induced cell death (Xu et al. 2020), the function of RsDN3377 acts as an elicitor that induces defence responses in N. benthamiana. Furthermore, silencing RsDN3377 via dsRNA caused shrivelling of the hyphal margin, consistent with observations where chitin deacetylase facilitates germ tube adhesion and appressorium development but is nonessential for full virulence (Geoghegan and Gurr 2016). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that RsDN3377 is a unique deacetylase: it contributes to mycelial development and pathogenicity through its deacetylase activity, and it acts as an elicitor, activating plant immune responses independently of its enzymatic function. The results highlight the evolutionary adaptation of fungal effectors in balancing metabolic roles and host manipulation during infection.

To neutralise host plant immune responses, necrotrophic phytopathogens secrete various protein molecules including effector proteins (Weiberg et al. 2013; Zhu et al. 2013; Takahara et al. 2016). In response, infected plant cells activate induced immunity as a defence mechanism against pathogen infection. In this study, the expression levels of FLS2 and RLK1 in N. tabacum seedlings treated with polysaccharide/chitin deacetylases RsDN3377 were increased. These findings contrast with previous reports showing that VdPDA1, a deacetylase secreted by V. dahliae, suppresses PAMP‐triggered immunity and downregulates the expression of defence‐related genes, including RbohB‐like, PRp27‐like and MPK6‐like (Gao et al. 2019). Based on this observation and the elicitor activity of RsDN3377, we hypothesised that a host‐recognition factor might interact with RsDN3377 to trigger a defence response. We demonstrated that RsDN3377 interacts with the calcium‐binding protein NtCML19, as evidenced by PyMOL software analysis, Y2H, BiFC and VIGS assays. NtCML19 is a calmodulin‐like protein containing EF‐hand domains that can form single or paired chiral structures. Based on MST analysis and NtCML19‐overexpressing (OE) and knockout (KO) transgenic N. tabacum , we further demonstrated that NtCML19, a calcium‐binding protein with confirmed Ca2+‐binding activity, functions as a key resistance gene against R. solani AG3‐TB infection. Many CML proteins are Ca2+ sensors in plants, detecting microbial‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) during bacterial, fungal or viral infections, initiating diverse cellular defence responses. Ca2+‐binding protein 41 (CML41) is transcriptionally upregulated and regulates the plasmodesmata closure in defence response against Pseudomonas syringae (Xu et al. 2017; Leba et al. 2012). Our findings identified NtCML19 as an important resistance gene conferring protection against R. solani infection. However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms governing host defence responses need further investigation.

Despite the promising potential of novel fungicides in controlling fungal infections, effective biocontrol strategies against R. solani remain limited due to its high genetic diversity. Advances in molecular biology approaches are therefore critical for mitigating pathogen damage. Recent studies have identified key virulence factors in R. solani AG1‐IA, such as RsCRP1 and RsIA_CtaG/Cox11 (Tzelepis et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023), while host resistance genes like NtWRKY70, OsWRKY30 and OsPAL1 have been shown to play essential roles in defence against R. solani (Li et al. 2024; Yang, Gu, et al. 2022; Yang, Fan, et al. 2022). In this study, we demonstrated that the polysaccharide/chitin deacetylase RsDN3377 is essential for mycelial development and pathogenicity during R. solani AG3‐TB infection. However, during R. solani infection, deacetylase RsDN3377 can be recognised by the calcium‐binding protein NtCML19, which induces cell death and ROS accumulation to subsequently enhance plant resistance (Figure 7). Given the complex genetic system of R. solani AG3‐TB, further research is needed to elucidate how RsDN3377 regulates fungal nutrient acquisition and growth. Our findings highlight the functional diversity of fungal deacetylases in pathogenesis and provide insights into durable disease management strategies. Moreover, this study reveals a novel immune mechanism triggered during R. solani–host interactions, advancing our understanding of plant–pathogen co‐evolution.

The model of RsDN3377 interaction with NtCML19 to promote host immunity during Rhizoctonia solani infection. With its deacetylase activity, RsDN3377 plays a key role in hyphal development and pathogenicity. However, as an elicitor, RsDN3377 can be recognised by calcium‐binding protein NtCML19, which promotes host resistance against R. solani infection by inducing cell death and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation.

Experimental Procedures

Prediction of RsDN3377

Effector protein prediction was conducted using EffectorP v. 2.0 (Sperschneider et al. 2018). Signal peptide sequences were predicted with SignalP v. 6.0 using default parameters (Petersen et al. 2011). Protein domain analysis was performed using InterPro and UniProt databases (Mulder et al. 2008). The amino acid residues constituting the active‐binding site were identified through structural visualisation and analysis using PyMOL software (Conte et al. 2023).

Plant Materials and R. solani AG3‐TB Infection

Seeds of N. benthamiana were sown in containers and maintained at 26°C for 30 days. Similarly, seeds of N. tabacum ‘Yunyan 87’ were sown and grown under the same temperature conditions for 50 days. For dsRNA inoculation assays, the fifth and sixth fully expanded leaves of N. tabacum were selected.

Nicotiana tabacum seeds (WT, NtCML19‐OE and NtCML19‐KO) were sown in containers and grown at 26°C for 50 days for inoculation and RT‐qPCR analysis. In addition, N. tabacum seeds were surface‐sterilised by soaking in a disinfectant solution (10% NaOCl in sterile water) at 37°C for 60 min, followed by rinsing with sterile water. The sterilised seeds were then sown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) solid medium supplemented with 2% sucrose, 1% agar and vitamins, and cultured at 26°C. After 25 days, seedlings were harvested for extraction of total protein and RNA.

The R. solani AG3‐TB (YC‐9 strain) was cultured on potato dextrose agar at 27°C for 48 h. For pathogenicity assays, 6‐mm diameter agar plugs from these plates containing actively growing mycelia of the YC‐9 strain were inoculated on leaves. The inoculated leaves were maintained at 27°C, and lesion diameters were measured at 24, 48 and 72 h.

Total RNA Preparation, cDNA Synthesis and RT‐qPCR

Leaves were inoculated with the YC‐9 strain, and N. tabacum plants were treated with 10 μM A6 (Hengcheng; CAS: 38854‐46‐5), 10 μM D6 (Hengcheng; CAS: 41708‐95‐6), and a combination of 10 μM A6 and 10 μM RsDN3377. The inoculated leaf tissue was cut into 1 cm pieces and flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen tissue was then homogenised using a grinding machine (Haixin; Tissuelyser‐24L) at 50 Hz for three grinding cycles. Total RNA was isolated using MagZol Reagent (Magen) following the manufacturer's protocol.

First‐strand cDNA was synthesised using a commercial cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme). The cDNA was cloned into a TA/Blunt‐Zero vector (Vazyme) and verified by sequencing.

The gene expression was performed by RT‐qPCR using Q711. The qPCR was conducted in a 20 μL volume containing 1 μL of cDNA, 10 μL of ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), 0.5 μL of each primer (10 μM) (Tables S1 and S2) and 8 μL of double‐distilled water.

Transient Expression of Genes

DNA fragments (RsDN3377, RsDN337 Δ SP, RsDN21085, RsDN10085, RsDN12825, RsDN20094, RsDN22565, RsDN22904, RsDN22462, RsDN22450 and RsDN21125) were cloned into the pGR106 vector (Table S3). Additionally, RsDN3377 and RsDN3377 Δ SP fragments were inserted into pGD‐GFP and pCold‐TF vectors (Tables S4 and S5). The recombinant plasmids were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 and cultured in Luria Bertani medium supplemented with rifampicin (Rif) and kanamycin (Kan) at 28°C for 48 h. Bacterial cells were harvested and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, 150 μM acetosyringone pH 5.6) to an OD600 of 0.6. Cell death symptoms were assessed 3–5 days post‐infiltration (Wei et al. 2020).

The H2O2 Accumulation in Leaves by DAB Staining

The H2O2 accumulation in leaves was visualised using DAB staining following established methods (Thordal‐Christensen et al. 1997). Fresh leaf samples were immersed in 1 mg/mL DAB solution (Servicebio) and incubated at 25°C for 12 h in darkness. Subsequently, stained leaves were decolorised by boiling in 95% ethanol for 10 min, followed by washing with distilled water to remove residual ethanol.

RsDN3377 Silencing and Immunoblot Analysis

The RsDN3377 fragment was amplified using Phanta Flash Master Mix (Vazyme) with specific primers (Table S6). Subsequently, dsRNA‐DN3377 was synthesised using the T7 RNAi Transcription Kit (Vazyme). A total of 50 ng ds‐DN3377 was applied to the mycelium of R. solani AG3‐TB, followed by incubation at 28°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 24 h. Mycelial morphology was examined under a microscope (Nikon; ECLIPSE 80i). In addition, ds‐DN3377 was labelled with Cy3‐UTP (Canspec). The labelled dsRNA (50 ng) was similarly introduced into R. solani AG3‐TB mycelium and incubated under the same conditions (28°C, 200 rpm, 24 h). Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Nikon; FV3000).

A total of 50 mg of leaf tissue was cracking in 1 mL of RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing 10 μM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Beyotime). The protein concentration was quantified using the Bradford protein assay kit (Beyotime). For immunoblotting, the proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Biosharp). The membrane was then probed with anti‐GFP (Beyotime) and anti‐His (Beyotime) antibodies to detect GFP‐tagged and His‐tagged proteins, respectively.

Site‐Directed Mutagenesis

The target fragments containing specific amino acid mutations in RsDN3377 were generated using overlapping PCR. The mutagenesis primers (Table S7) were designed to include the desired mutation sites, with each primer consisting of at least 20 nucleotides to ensure specificity. Two primary PCRs were conducted, with one using primers a and d and the other using primers c and b. Both reactions were carried out with the 2× SuperNova High‐Fidelity PCR Mix (Genstar). To obtain the full‐length RsDN3377 fragment containing the desired amino acid mutations, the two primary PCR products were mixed and subjected to a final PCR using outer primers (a + b, Table S7) as templates.

Plasmolysis Analysis of the RsDN3377 and RsDN3377ΔSP

The coding sequences of RsDN3377, RsDN3377 Δ SP and NtCML19 were individually cloned into the pGD‐GFP expression vector. The resulting constructs (pGD‐GFP‐RsDN3377, pGD‐GFP‐RsDN3377ΔSP and pGD‐GFP‐NtCML19) were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. For subcellular localisation assays, A. tumefaciens was harvested and resuspended in infiltration buffer to an OD600 of 0.6. The bacterial suspensions were pressure‐infiltrated into the abaxial side of leaves using a needleless syringe. At 48 hpi, subcellular localisation was analysed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon; FV3000). GFP fluorescence was excited at 488 nm. For the analysis of mass wall separation, the leaves were treated with 90 mM NaCl solution for 15 min to examine cellular responses.

Yeast Secretion System

The SP sequence of RsDN3377 was cloned into the pSUC2 vector (Table S8). The pSUC2‐RsDN3377SP plasmid was transformed into the yeast YTK12 strain. Transformed colonies were selected and inoculated onto both CMD−W and YPRAA media, followed by incubation at 28°C for 2 days. Positive colonies were identified by mixing with 5 mL of TTC solution and incubating at 37°C for 10 min (Oh et al. 2009).

Expression, Purification of RsDN3377 and NtCML19 and Deacetylase Activity Assay

The RsDN3377 Δ SP and NtCML19 were integrated into the pCold‐TF‐His vector and subsequently transformed into E. coli Rosetta (DE3) competent cells (AngYuBio). Recombinant protein expression was induced, and the target proteins were purified using Ni‐NTA affinity chromatography (Beyotime). The purified RsDN3377 and NtCML19 were quantified using a Bradford protein assay kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's protocol (Table S9).

A reaction mixture containing 2 μM RsDN3377ΔSP, 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and 1 mM A6 was incubated at 37°C for 30 min, followed by denaturation at 100°C for 10 min. The molecular weight of reaction products was analysed using MALDI‐TOF MS (Bruker Ultraflextreme) (Gao et al. 2019). A negative control, consisting of the reaction mixture without RsDN3377ΔSP, was determined using MALDI‐TOF MS.

RNA‐Seq and Data Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from N. tabacum treated with 10 μM A6, 10 μM D6 and 10 μM RsDN3377ΔSP incubated with 10 μM A6 at 37°C for 12 h. RNA quality was assessed, and sequencing libraries were prepared following standard protocols. Library quality was evaluated using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and paired‐end sequencing was performed on the HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina). Raw reads were aligned to the N. tabacum reference genome (GCF_000715135.1; available at NCBI) using HISAT2. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (v. 1.30.1) with a significance threshold of adjusted p < 0.05.

Y2H and BiFC Assays

The coding sequences of NtTNT, NtRPV1 and NtCML19 were cloned into the pGBDT7 bait vector, while RsDN3377 Δ SP was cloned into the pGADT7 vector (Table S10). The pGBDT7‐TNT, pGBDT7‐RPV1, pGBDT7‐CML19 and pGADT7‐RsDN3377ΔSP constructs were co‐transformed into the Y2H Gold yeast strain using the lithium acetate method. To assess protein–protein interactions, transformants were plated on SD/−Leu/−Trp medium to verify co‐transformation, and SD/−Leu/−Trp/−Ade/−His medium to test for interaction. Plates were incubated at 28°C for 2–3 days before evaluating growth.

The RsDN3377 Δ SP fragment was cloned into both N‐terminal (nYFP) and C‐terminal (cYFP) split‐YFP vectors, while NtCML19 was cloned into the nYFP vector (Table S10). The recombinant plasmids RsDN3377ΔSP‐cYFP and NtCML19‐nYFP were individually transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. For transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves, agrobacterial cultures were harvested, and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES pH 5.6, 150 μM acetosyringone). The bacterial suspension was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.6. Equal volumes of the two strains were mixed and co‐infiltrated into the abaxial side of leaves using a needleless syringe. After 48 h of incubation at 22°C under dark conditions, YFP fluorescence was visualised using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon; FV3000).

Functional Validation of NtCML19 by VIGS

The NtCML19 fragment was cloned into the pTRV2 vector using the ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Table S11; Vazyme). The recombinant pTRV2‐NtCML19, along with empty pTRV2‐GFP (negative control) and pTRV1 plasmid were individually transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The agrobacterium fluid was diluted using an infiltration buffer and cultured at 25°C for 2 h. The Agrobacterium mixture was infiltrated on the fourth and fifth leaves of N. benthamiana. After 12 days, the upper systemic leaves were inoculated using R. solani AG‐3.

Construction of Transformation Vector and Obtaining Transgenic Plants

The CDS of NtCML19 was cloned into pBWA(V)HS‐3 × FLAG with a 35S promoter, obtaining a recombinant pBWA(V)HS‐NtCML19‐3 × FLAG vector. This vector was introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The Yunyan 87 leaves were inoculated into Agrobacterium suspension for 15 min and induced the mature embryos of tobacco. The transformed tobacco was screened in hygromycin‐containing medium. The positive transgenic plants were tested for the target gene using the hygF/R primers (Table S12).

The NtCML19 fragment was cloned into K5‐KRSN (Table S13), obtaining a recombinant K5‐KRSN‐NtCML19 vector. This vector was introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The mature embryos of tobacco were induced. The transformed tobacco was screened in hygromycin medium.

MST

A total of 10 mM solution of NtCML19‐His proteins was labelled with NHS/NT‐647 dye at a 1:5 M ratio and incubated for 30 min at 22°C in the dark. Unbound dye was removed by buffer exchange using phosphate‐buffered saline with Tween (PBST). For binding affinity measurements, 1 mM CaCl2 was serially diluted in HEPES buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM KCl, pH 7.4) and mixed with the labelled protein solution. The mixtures were loaded into capillaries, and interactions were analysed using the Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper, NT.115). The dissociation constant (Kd) was calculated based on Signal Thermophoresis and T‐Jump data.

Statistical Analyses

Student's t tests were performed using SPSS. Two‐way ANOVA was performed using the R statistical package (http://www.R‐project.org/).
