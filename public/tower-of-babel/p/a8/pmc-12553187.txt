Methyl jasmonate enhances boron toxicity tolerance in strawberry (Fragaria × Ananassa ‘Albion’) by modulating plant growth, antioxidant defenses, and biochemical responses
Authors: Faezeh Mostafapour, Jafar Amiri, Zohreh Jabbarzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plants frequently encounter environmental stressors that impair growth, physiology, and metabolism. Among these, boron (B) toxicity is particularly damaging in arid and semi-arid regions due to excessive B accumulation that restricts productivity. Methyl jasmonate (MeJA), a plant signaling molecule, has been shown to mitigate abiotic stress; however, its role in alleviating B toxicity in strawberry remains unclear. This study investigated the effects of foliar-applied MeJA on Fragaria × ananassa ‘Albion’ grown hydroponically under four B levels (23, 81, 162, and 323 µM) and four MeJA concentrations (0, 25, 50, and 100 µM). Boron toxicity significantly reduced shoot and root biomass, fruit yield, chlorophyll content, and nutrient uptake (N, Ca²⁺, Fe²⁺, Zn²⁺), while elevating oxidative stress indicators including malondialdehyde, hydrogen peroxide, and electrolyte leakage. MeJA application—particularly at 50 µM—partially alleviated these adverse effects. Positive responses included enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities (superoxide dismutase, catalase, ascorbate peroxidase, guaiacol peroxidase), increased osmolytes, improved nutrient balance, and better fruit quality attributes. MeJA also stimulated phenylalanine ammonia-lyase activity and promoted phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins under moderate B stress. In contrast, higher MeJA concentration (100 µM) produced weaker or inconsistent effects under severe toxicity. To our knowledge, this is the first integrated study to evaluate the dose-dependent impact of MeJA on strawberry under B toxicity by simultaneously assessing physiology, biochemical responses, and fruit quality. These findings suggest MeJA as a promising strategy to enhance strawberry stress tolerance, though further field-based studies across cultivars and soil conditions are required for broader validation.

Introduction

Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa), a Rosaceae family member, is a globally important fruit crop valued for its nutrition and bioactive compounds but is highly sensitive to boron (B) toxicity [1]. Its tolerance range is narrow. Precise nutrient management is thus essential. Boron is an essential micronutrient required for normal growth and development in higher plants. It plays vital roles in cell wall formation, plasma membrane stability, sugar transport, nucleic acid synthesis, phenolic metabolism, carbohydrate regulation, and hormone activity, including modulation of indole-3-acetic acid oxidase, pollen tube development, and root elongation [2, 3]. In plants, boron mainly exists as boric acid (B(OH)₃) and borate (B(OH)₄⁻), which form stable complexes with cis-diol–containing molecules such as ribose, apiose, and glycoproteins [3, 4]. Despite its essential roles, boron exhibits a narrow range between sufficiency and toxicity, making precise nutrient management crucial. Even small deviations from optimal levels can disrupt physiological balance and impair plant performance [5]. Although boron deficiency is widespread globally, boron toxicity poses a significant problem in arid and semi-arid regions with alkaline soils, low rainfall, and boron-rich irrigation water [6–8]. Excessive boron accumulation threatens crop productivity and long-term agricultural sustainability [9]. Sensitive crops such as citrus, maize, wheat, and rice show toxicity symptoms at soil B concentrations as low as 5 mg kg⁻¹, while more tolerant species like barley and soybean tolerate up to 100 mg kg⁻¹ [10]. Leaf boron concentrations above 10 mg kg⁻¹ dry weight are generally toxic [11].

Physiological symptoms of boron toxicity include inhibited root and shoot growth, leaf chlorosis and necrosis, impaired flowering and fruit set, and reduced yield [12, 13]. These symptoms often result from boron accumulation in actively growing tissues, especially upper leaves and shoot tips, where limited exclusion promotes toxicity [14]. At the cellular level, boron toxicity disrupts cell division and elongation, damages chloroplasts, induces oxidative stress, and alters cell wall composition [15, 16]. Borate esters formed with cell wall components increase rigidity and reduce plasticity [17–19]. High boron impairs chlorophyll biosynthesis and photosynthesis, elevates lipid peroxidation, suppresses proton extrusion, and weakens antioxidant defenses, causing oxidative damage and reduced performance [20–23]. Abiotic stress exacerbates boron toxicity by increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and membrane injury [24, 25].

Excess boron not only disrupts nutrient balance and metabolism in plants but also triggers oxidative stress by promoting the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide, hydroxyl radicals, and hydrogen peroxide. These ROS attack cellular components, induce lipid peroxidation, and weaken membrane integrity, ultimately impairing plant growth and survival. Oxidative damage is commonly assessed through malondialdehyde (MDA) content, H₂O₂ levels, and electrolyte leakage. To cope with B-induced stress, plants activate antioxidant defenses such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and glutathione reductase (GR), which help restore redox balance and protect cellular functions [11, 14, 26–28].

Excess boron in kiwifruit caused tissue-specific accumulation and toxicity symptoms, reducing growth and chlorophyll content, suppressing ascorbate peroxidase and catalase, while increasing superoxide dismutase activity and MDA levels [29]. In Cucumis melo L., elevated boron reduced chlorophyll, relative water content (RWC), membrane stability, and leaf iron, while increasing leaf temperature, collectively inhibiting growth [30]. In Citrus grandis L., boron toxicity decreased fruit yield, total soluble solids (TSS), titratable acidity (TA), fruit pH, vitamin C, total phenols, nitrogen, and iron, while increasing boron, potassium, and magnesium accumulation [31]. Plants activate antioxidant systems to combat oxidative stress, but prolonged boron exposure can overwhelm these defenses, leading to irreversible damage. This highlights the importance of studying boron sensitivity in vulnerable crops, especially those with limited detoxification capacity. Plant growth regulators, especially phytohormones, play key roles in stress responses [10, 32, 33]. Methyl jasmonate (MeJA), a volatile jasmonic acid derivative, regulates plant responses to various abiotic stresses. It activates antioxidant defenses, modulates hormone signaling, and promotes biosynthesis of protective secondary metabolites like phenolics [34–36].

MeJA mediates responses to drought, salinity, chilling, heavy metals, and nutrient imbalances including boron excess [37]. Its effects vary by species and dose. For example, foliar MeJA (0.5–15 µM) improved cold tolerance in Solanum lycopersicum, with 10 µM best enhancing membrane stability and antioxidants [38]. In Vaccinium corymbosum, similar doses protected photosynthesis and increased antioxidant activity under drought [39]. Also, 0.075 mM MeJA enhanced salt tolerance in GF677 and bitter almond rootstocks [40], and 100 µM MeJA alleviated boron toxicity in grapes by boosting antioxidant enzymes [41]. Studies in Artemisia annua, strawberry, Matricaria chamomilla L., and loquat report MeJA reduces ROS and strengthens antioxidant responses under boron stress [42–45].

Based on previous studies and observed physiological disruptions caused by boron toxicity, we hypothesize that foliar application of MeJA can mitigate B-induced damage in strawberry by activating antioxidant defense mechanisms, enhancing osmotic adjustment, and preserving photosynthetic pigments and membrane stability. To address this hypothesis, we investigated the physiological, biochemical, and fruit-quality responses of Fragaria × ananassa ‘Albion’ exposed to boron toxicity and treated with varying concentrations of MeJA under controlled conditions. Specifically, this study aims to: (i) assess the effects of MeJA on plant growth and chlorophyll content under B toxicity; (ii) quantify changes in both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems as well as osmoprotectant compounds; and (iii) evaluate the influence of MeJA on fruit quality parameters and overall physiological resilience. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first integrated study to comprehensively investigate MeJA-mediated mitigation of boron toxicity in strawberry, offering novel insights into its potential as a biostimulant for stress management in sensitive horticultural crops.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The experiment was conducted in a commercial greenhouse located in Urmia, West Azerbaijan Province, Iran. A 4 × 4 factorial experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replicates, comprising four methyl jasmonate concentrations (0, 25, 50, and 100 µM) and four boron levels (23, 81, 162, and 323 µM). Each experimental unit consisted of a single strawberry plant grown in an individual plastic pot. Uniform strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa Duch. cv. Albion) plants at the two-leaf stage were procured from a commercial nursery and initially grown in seedling trays under controlled conditions prior to transplantation. Albion is generally recognized as a day-neutral strawberry cultivar, meaning it can initiate flowering regardless of photoperiod length, whether under long-day or short-day conditions [46]. To break dormancy and meet the chilling requirement, plants were subjected to a cold treatment at 4 ± 1 °C for 14 days in a growth chamber. Uniform plants approximately 10 cm tall were then transplanted into plastic pots (15 cm diameter, 13 cm height) filled with a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of perlite and peat moss, forming part of a hydroponic system. Greenhouse conditions were maintained at 22 ± 2 °C during the day and 15 ± 2 °C at night, with a 12-hour photoperiod providing approximately 25,000 lx light intensity and relative humidity of 60 ± 5%. Methyl jasmonate (Sigma-Aldrich, 95% purity) was dissolved in ethanol and diluted with distilled water to prepare final concentrations of 0, 25, 50, and 100 µM. These concentrations were selected based on previous studies [39–41]. Fresh methyl jasmonate solutions were prepared prior to each application to ensure stability and efficacy. The solution was applied foliarly using a manual pump sprayer, uniformly spraying the leaves to runoff, while avoiding contact with the growing medium.

Methyl jasmonate was applied as a foliar spray to strawberry plants in the early morning (between 8:00 and 9:00 a.m.) to ensure optimal uptake and minimize photodegradation. Each plant received 25 mL of MeJA solution per application. To prevent the solution from dripping into the potting soil and causing unintended root exposure, the soil surface was carefully covered with a plastic film during spraying, which was removed immediately after application. These procedures were consistently applied for all MeJA treatments throughout the experiment to ensure uniformity and reproducibility.

Applications were carried out six times at 10-day intervals. Boron was supplied as boric acid (H₃BO₃) obtained from Applichem, Germany. Boron stress treatments included 23 (control), 81, 162, and 323 µM, concentrations commonly used to simulate moderate to severe boron stress in plant research [11, 30, 41, 47]. Boron treatments were initiated 10 days prior to the first methyl jasmonate foliar application (August 1, 2021), starting at the early flowering stage of the strawberry plants, and continued throughout the 70-day experimental period (October 11, 2021).

A modified Hoagland nutrient solution was used to supply essential macro- and micronutrients throughout the experimental period. The pH of the solution was maintained at 6.0 by daily adjustments using 1 N hydrochloric acid (HCl), and fresh solution was prepared every three days to ensure chemical stability and nutrient availability. All plants, including controls and treatment groups, received the same nutrient solution, with the exception of added boron concentrations for B stress treatments.

Four treatment groups were established: (i) control (no methyl jasmonate or boron stress); (ii) boron stress alone (81, 162, and 323 µM); (iii) methyl jasmonate alone (25, 50, and 100 µM); and (iv) combined treatments including all possible combinations of the specified methyl jasmonate and boron concentrations. A total of 16 treatments (4 methyl jasmonate concentrations × 4 boron levels) were applied with three replicates per treatment, resulting in 48 plants in total. Treatments lasted for 70 days under greenhouse conditions. Methyl jasmonate was applied foliarly every 10 days, while boron stress was imposed through the nutrient solution. Control plants received only the nutrient solution. Seventy days after the onset of boron stress treatments, the plants’ responses to MeJA application and boron toxicity were evaluated.

At the end of the experiment, a comprehensive set of parameters was assessed, including shoot and root biomass, Fruit morphological traits (fruit weight and length), fruit quality indices (pH, total soluble solids, titratable acidity), biochemical markers (total phenolics, flavonoids, anthocyanins, ascorbic acid, antioxidant activity), photosynthetic pigments, osmolyte contents (proline, soluble sugars, and glycine betaine), oxidative stress markers (hydrogen peroxide, malondialdehyde, and electrolyte leakage), enzymatic antioxidants (CAT, GPX, APX, SOD, PAL), and leaf nutrient concentrations (N, Ca²⁺, Fe²⁺, Zn²⁺, and B).

Measurement of biomass and fruit morphology

At the end of the experiment, the fresh weight of shoots and roots was measured using a digital balance (Model PJ300, Mettler Toledo). To determine dry weight, the plant materials were oven-dried at 65 °C for 72 h, and their dry weight was then recorded using the same balance. Additionally, fruit weight and length were measured using a Sartorius balance (Model GE 412, Germany; accuracy: 0.01 g) and a digital caliper (Model No: Z 22855; accuracy: 0.01 mm), respectively.

Leaf chlorophyll index (SPAD) measurement

Three fully expanded, physiologically mature leaves were randomly selected from each pot. Chlorophyll content was non-destructively assessed using a SPAD chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502, KONICA MINOLTA, Saka, Japan), which provided a chlorophyll index for each leaf. Individual SPAD readings were recorded, and their average was used to represent the chlorophyll index per pot.

Determination of chlorophyll and carotenoid content

Chlorophyll and carotenoid contents were determined following the method of Lichtenthaler [48]. Briefly, 0.1 g of fully expanded fresh leaf tissue was finely chopped and ground in a chilled porcelain mortar with 5 mL of pure acetone under low-light conditions to prevent pigment degradation. The homogenate was centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a clean glass cuvette. Absorbance was measured at 663, 645, and 470 nm using a spectrophotometer (HALO DB-20, Dynamico Instruments), with pure acetone as the blank. Concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids (mg g−1 FW) were calculated using the following equations:

Leaf relative water content (LRWC)

Relative water content (RWC) was determined using leaf discs (1 cm²) excised from fully expanded leaves of all treatment groups, following the method of Ritchie et al., [49]. Fresh weight (FW) was immediately measured using a digital balance (Model PJ300, Mettler, Switzerland). The discs were then immersed in distilled water and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. After incubation, turgid weight (TW) was measured. The samples were subsequently oven-dried at 70 °C for 24 h to determine dry weight (DW). RWC was calculated using the following formula:

Electrolyte leakage (EL)

Electrolyte leakage (EL), an indicator of membrane integrity, was assessed following the method of Lutts et al. [50] with slight modifications. Leaf discs (10 mm diameter) were excised from fully expanded leaves and placed in test tubes containing 10 mL of distilled water. Samples were gently shaken at 25 °C for 24 h, after which the initial electrical conductivity (EC₁) was measured using a conductivity meter (Model 8306; Cond./Temp./TDS/Salt/Logger). The tubes were then incubated in a water bath at 100 °C for 15 min to completely disrupt cell membranes. After cooling to room temperature, the final conductivity (EC₂) was measured. Electrolyte leakage was calculated as follows:

Malondialdehyde (MDA)

Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, as an indicator of lipid peroxidation, were measured following the method of Cakmak and Horst [51] with slight modifications. Briefly, 0.2 g of frozen leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of 1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Then, 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 4 mL of a solution containing 20% TCA and 0.5% thiobarbituric acid (TBA). The mixture was incubated in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min and rapidly cooled in an ice bath. After cooling, samples were centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 and 600 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Model HALO DB-20, Dynamico). MDA concentration was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and expressed as nmol g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW).

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content

Hydrogen peroxide content was determined according to the method of Velikova et al. [52] with minor modifications. Approximately 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. For the assay, 0.5 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 0.5 mL of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 mL of 1 M potassium iodide (KI). The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 390 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Model HALO DB-20, Dynamico Instruments). H₂O₂ concentration was calculated based on a standard curve expressed as micromoles per gram of fresh weight (µmol g⁻¹ FW).

Proline content

Proline concentration was determined following a modified method of Irigoyen et al. [53]. Approximately 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of 95% ethanol using a chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate was centrifuged, and the residue was rinsed twice with 5 mL of 70% ethanol. All supernatants were pooled and centrifuged again at 3,500 rpm for 10 min. The resulting alcoholic extract was stored at 4 °C until analysis. For proline determination, 1 mL of extract was diluted with 10 mL of distilled water, followed by the addition of 5 mL of freshly prepared ninhydrin reagent (1.25% w/v ninhydrin in a 2:1 mixture of glacial acetic acid and 6 M phosphoric acid) and 5 mL of glacial acetic acid. The reaction mixture was vortexed and incubated in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min. After cooling, 10 mL of benzene was added and vigorously shaken. Following phase separation (30 min), 1 mL of the upper benzene phase was transferred to a cuvette, and absorbance was measured at 515 nm using a spectrophotometer. Proline content was calculated from a standard curve constructed using known concentrations of proline (0–0.1 µmol mL⁻¹) in 70% ethanol, as described by Paquin and Lechasseur [54].

Determination of soluble sugar content

Soluble sugar content was determined using a modified anthrone–sulfuric acid method as described by Irigoyen et al. [53]. Briefly, 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of 95% ethanol using a mortar and pestle. The homogenate was transferred to a test tube and diluted to a final volume of 25 mL with 95% ethanol. The remaining residue was washed with 5 mL of 70% ethanol, and the wash was combined with the initial extract to ensure complete sugar recovery. A 10 mL aliquot of the pooled extract was centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 15 min. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of the clear supernatant was mixed with 3 mL of freshly prepared anthrone reagent (0.2 g anthrone dissolved in 100 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid, 95–98%). The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min to allow color development. After cooling to room temperature, absorbance was measured at 625 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Determination of glycine betaine content

Glycine betaine content was determined following the method described by Grieve and Grattan [55]. Briefly, 0.25 g of dried leaf tissue was transferred to a test tube containing 10 mL of distilled water and gently shaken at room temperature for 24 h. The extract was then filtered through Whatman filter paper. A 0.25 mL aliquot of the filtrate was mixed with 0.25 mL of 2 N sulfuric acid and incubated in an ice bath for 1 h. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of potassium iodide–iodine (KI–I₂) reagent was added, and the mixture was refrigerated at 4 °C for 16 h. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 6,000 rpm for 25 min, resulting in the formation of two distinct phases. The upper aqueous phase was discarded, and the lower organic phase was extracted with 6 mL of dichloroethane by vigorous vortexing to ensure thorough mixing. Absorbance was then measured at 365 nm using a spectrophotometer (Model HALO DB-20, Dynamico). Glycine betaine content was quantified using a standard curve and expressed as mg g⁻¹ dry weight.

Measurement of fruit extract pH

The pH of the fruit extract was measured using a digital pH meter (Mettler Toledo SevenEasy™, Model Mi 150, Hungary) at a controlled temperature of 20 °C. Prior to measurement, 10 mL of the extract was filtered through standard filter paper to remove particulate matter and then diluted 1:1 with distilled water. The pH meter was calibrated before each use using standard buffer solutions at pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0 to ensure measurement accuracy.

Measurement of total soluble solids (TSS)

Total soluble solids (TSS) content was determined using a handheld digital refractometer (Atago Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at a controlled temperature of 20 °C. Prior to measurement, fruit juice samples were filtered to remove suspended solids that could interfere with refractive index readings. TSS values were expressed in degrees Brix, indicating the percentage of soluble solids present in the juice. Measurements were performed according to the protocol described by Roussos et al. [56].

Titratable acidity (TA) measurement

Titratable acidity (TA) of the fruit extract was determined by acid–base titration following the method of Ayala-Zavala et al. [57], with slight modifications. Briefly, 2.5 mL of the fruit extract was transferred to a clean Erlenmeyer flask and diluted with 10 mL of distilled water. The mixture was titrated with a standardized 0.1 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution until the pH reached 8.2, as measured by a calibrated digital pH meter. Titration was carried out at room temperature (20–25 °C) under constant stirring to ensure uniform mixing.

Titratable acidity was calculated using the following formula and expressed as grams of citric acid per 100 mL of fruit extract:

Determination of ascorbic acid content

Ascorbic acid content in fruit extracts was determined based on the reduction of 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol (DCPIP) dye by ascorbic acid, following Mukherjee and Choudhuri [58] with slight modifications. Briefly, 0.1 g of fresh fruit tissue was homogenized in 3 mL of 1% metaphosphoric acid. The homogenate was stored at 4 °C for 30 min to stabilize the ascorbic acid, then centrifuged at 6,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. A 100 µL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 2.5 mL of freshly prepared 0.1 mM DCPIP solution and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5 min. Absorbance was measured at 520 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Model HALO DB-20, Dynamico). A reagent blank containing all components except the extract was used for baseline correction. Ascorbic acid concentration was calculated using a standard calibration curve prepared with known concentrations (0.1–0.5 g L⁻¹) of ascorbic acid in the presence of DCPIP.

Extraction of samples for antioxidant capacity, total phenolics, and total flavonoids

Extraction of Samples for Antioxidant Capacity, Total Phenolics, and Total Flavonoids.

To prepare extracts for the analysis of antioxidant capacity, total phenolic content, and total flavonoid content, 0.5 g of frozen fruit tissue was finely ground to a powder under liquid nitrogen using a chilled mortar and pestle. Then, 5 mL of 80% methanol was added to the powdered sample. The mixture was sonicated at 20 °C for 30 min in an ultrasonic bath to enhance extraction efficiency. Following sonication, samples were centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through filter paper to remove residual solids. The clear extracts were collected and stored in sealed tubes at − 20 °C until further analysis [59]. These extracts were subsequently used for quantification of total phenolics, total flavonoids, and antioxidant capacity.

Antioxidant capacity assay using DPPH measurement

The antioxidant capacity of fruit extracts was evaluated using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging assay, following Nakajima et al. [60] with slight modifications. Briefly, 100 µL of the extract was mixed with 1,900 µL of 0.1 mM DPPH solution prepared in methanol. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Model HALO DB-20, Dynamico). A methanol blank served as the control. The percentage of DPPH radical scavenging activity was calculated using the following formula:

DPPH (%) = [(AC - AS)/AC] × 100, where AC is the absorbance of the blank and AS is the absorbance of the sample. Results were expressed as percentage inhibition of DPPH radicals.

Measurement of total phenolic content

Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method [61] with slight modifications. Briefly, 30 µL of fruit extract was mixed with 90 µL of distilled water and 600 µL of 10% (v/v) Folin–Ciocalteu reagent. After incubation at room temperature for 3–5 min, 480 µL of 7.5% (w/v) sodium carbonate solution was added. The reaction mixture was then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 60 min. Absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Model HALO DB-20, Dynamico). Total phenolic content was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of fresh weight (mg GAE g⁻¹ FW).

Measurement of total flavonoid content

Total flavonoid content was quantified using the aluminum chloride (AlCl₃·6 H₂O) colorimetric assay, following the method of Chang et al. [62]. Briefly, 500 µL of extract was mixed with 1.5 mL of 80% methanol, 100 µL of 10% (w/v) aluminum chloride solution, 100 µL of 1 M potassium acetate, and 3.8 mL of distilled water. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 40 min, after which absorbance was measured at 380 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Model HALO DB-20, Dynamico), with a reagent blank as reference. Total flavonoid content was expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents per gram of fresh weight (mg QE g⁻¹ FW).

Measurement of total anthocyanin content

Total anthocyanin content in strawberry fruits was determined using an acidified methanol extraction method. Briefly, 0.1 g of fresh fruit tissue was homogenized in 10 mL of methanol containing 1% (v/v) hydrochloric acid. The mixture was incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 24 h to ensure efficient pigment extraction. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 530 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Model HALO DB-20, Dynamico). Total anthocyanin content was expressed as milligrams of cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents per gram of fresh weight (mg C3G g⁻¹ FW) and calculated using the following equation, as described by Giusti and Wrolstad [63].

Total Anthocyanin Content (mg C3G/g FW) = (A × MW × DF × 1000)/(ε × l × W).

Where: A = absorption, MW = molecular weight of cyanidin 3-glucoside (449.2), DF = dilution factor, ε = is the molar extinction coefficient of cyanidin 3-glucoside (26,900 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹), W = is the fresh weight of the sample in grams, l = is the path length of the cuvette (1 cm).

Preparation of plant extract for enzyme activity measurement

To prepare plant extracts for enzyme assays, 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in a chilled mortar with 3 mL of 50 mM Tris-HCl extraction buffer (pH 7.5). For the assay of ascorbate peroxidase (APX), the buffer was supplemented with 0.2 mM ascorbate. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the crude enzyme extract. This extract was used to determine the activities of catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and APX, following the method of Kang and Saltveit [64].

Measurement of catalase (CAT) enzyme activity

Catalase (CAT) activity was determined according to the method of Aebi [65], with slight modifications. The reaction mixture (3.0 mL total volume) contained 2.8 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), supplemented with 2 mM EDTA, 100 µL of 300 mM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and 100 µL of crude enzyme extract. The decomposition of H₂O₂ was monitored by measuring the decline in absorbance at 240 nm for 1 min using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The reaction was terminated by placing the cuvette on ice. Measurement of guaiacol peroxidase (GPX) enzyme activity

Guaiacol peroxidase (GPX) activity was measured according to the method of Upadhyaya et al. [66], with slight modifications. The reaction mixture (total volume: 3.2 mL) contained 3.0 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 50 µL of 20 mM guaiacol, 50 µL of 20 mM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and 100 µL of crude enzyme extract. Measurement of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) enzyme activity

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was determined following the method of Nakano and Asada [67], with minor modifications. The reaction mixture (final volume: 3.0 mL) consisted of 2.6 mL of 25 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 100 µL of 7.5 mM ascorbic acid, 100 µL of 300 mM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), 2 mM EDTA, and 100 µL of crude enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by the addition of H₂O₂, and the decrease in absorbance at 290 nm, corresponding to ascorbate oxidation, was recorded over 1 min using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (HALO DB-20, Dynamico). The reaction was terminated by placing the cuvette on ice. APX activity was calculated using the molar extinction coefficient of 2.8 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ for ascorbate. Results were expressed as micromoles of ascorbate oxidized per minute per gram of fresh weight (µmol min⁻¹ g⁻¹ FW), based on the following equation:

where: ΔA290 (per min) = is the decrease in absorbance at 290 nm per minute, V = is the total volume of the reaction mixture (3.0 mL = 0.003 L), ε = is the molar extinction coefficient of ascorbate (2.8 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹), l = is the path length of the cuvette (1 cm), FW = is the fresh weight of the plant tissue used (g).

Measurement of superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzyme activity

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined by evaluating the inhibition of photochemical reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), following the method of Beauchamp and Fridovich [68], with slight modifications. The reaction mixture (total volume: 3.0 mL) consisted of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 2 µM riboflavin, and 100 µL of crude enzyme extract. The mixture was exposed to light from a 30-watt fluorescent lamp at 25 °C for 10 min to initiate the reaction. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (HALO DB-20, Dynamico Instruments). A reaction mixture lacking the enzyme extract and kept in the dark was used as the control. The photoreduction of NBT results in the formation of a blue formazan product, and SOD activity was calculated based on the percentage inhibition of absorbance compared to the control. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cause 50% inhibition of NBT reduction. Results were expressed as units per milligram of protein (U mg⁻¹ protein).

Measurement of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) enzyme activity

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL; EC 4.3.1.24) activity was determined according to the method of Zucker [69], with slight modifications. Approximately 0.1 g of frozen leaf tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 1.5 mL of extraction buffer consisting of 0.1 M borate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and 1.4 mM β-mercaptoethanol using a chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract. For the enzyme assay, 30 µL of the extract was added to a reaction mixture containing 1 mL of assay buffer (0.1 M borate buffer, pH 8.8, supplemented with 0.1% PVP and 1.4 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and 1 mL of 12 mM L-phenylalanine. The mixture was incubated in a water bath at 30 °C for 30 min. The formation of trans-cinnamic acid was measured by recording absorbance at 290 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (HALO DB-20, Dynamico Instruments). PAL activity was calculated using the Beer–Lambert law with an extinction coefficient of 9,630 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹ and expressed as micromoles of trans-cinnamic acid produced per minute per gram of fresh weight (µmol min⁻¹ g⁻¹ FW).

Mineral nutrient analysis

Leaf samples were thoroughly washed with distilled water and oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h. The dried tissues were ground into a fine powder using an electric mill. For elemental analysis, dry ashing was performed on the powdered samples. Approximately 0.5–1 g of the dried powder was placed in porcelain crucibles and incinerated in a muffle furnace at 500–550 °C until white ash was obtained (about 6 h). Boron concentration was determined colorimetrically using the Azomethine-H method. One gram of ash was extracted with 10 mL of 0.05 M sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) at room temperature for 1 h and then filtered. One milliliter of the extract was mixed with 2 mL of buffer solution and 2 mL of freshly prepared Azomethine-H reagent, incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 420 nm. A calibration curve was prepared using boric acid standards ranging from 0.5 to 3.0 mg L⁻¹ [4]. Total nitrogen content was determined by the Kjeldahl method. Samples were digested with concentrated sulfuric acid in the presence of catalysts to convert organic nitrogen into ammonium sulfate, followed by distillation and titration to quantify the released ammonia. Calcium (Ca²⁺) concentration was determined by titration with 0.01 M EDTA, as described by Pauwels et al. [70]. Iron (Fe²⁺) and zinc (Zn²⁺) concentrations were measured using atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS; Shimadzu AA-6300, Japan), following the procedures of Ohyama et al. [71]. For these analyses, the white ash was dissolved in hydrochloric acid (HCl), and the solution was brought to a known volume prior to measurement.

Experimental design and statistical analysis

The experiment followed a factorial arrangement based on a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications. It investigated the effects of four methyl jasmonate concentrations and four levels of boron stress, resulting in 16 treatment combinations. Each combination was replicated three times, totaling 48 experimental units. Statistical analyses were performed through ANOVA using SAS software (version 9.2), and treatment means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.01.

Fresh and dry weight of roots

Root growth of Fragaria × ananassa responded negatively to increasing boron levels, with untreated plants showing a dose-dependent decline in fresh weight, reflecting impaired water status and biomass accumulation (Fig. 1C). Foliar MeJA application mitigated these detrimental effects, particularly at 50 µM, which consistently promoted root growth across B stress levels. At 81 and 162 µM B, MeJA-treated plants exhibited significantly higher root fresh weight compared to untreated controls, indicating a partial restoration of root growth potential under toxic conditions. Even under severe B stress, MeJA maintained enhanced root biomass, highlighting its functional role in supporting root resilience and sustaining belowground productivity under boron-induced stress.

As shown in Figure 1D, increasing boron levels caused a sharp decline in root dry weight in control plants, with the lowest values at 323 µM B, indicating strong inhibition of root biomass accumulation. Foliar application of MeJA mitigated these negative effects, particularly at 50 and 100 µM, where root dry weight was consistently improved under moderate to high B stress. For instance, at 81 µM B, MeJA treatments increased root dry weight by more than 25% compared with untreated plants, suggesting enhanced tolerance to stress-induced growth suppression. Even under severe B stress, MeJA-treated plants sustained relatively higher root dry weight than the control, indicating that MeJA contributes to maintaining root structural integrity and dry matter production under boron toxicity.

Fruit morphology (Fruit length, fruit number, fruit weight)

Figure 1E shows that fruit length declined as boron concentration increased in plants without MeJA, with the shortest fruits observed at 323 µM B—indicating that excess boron inhibits fruit growth by disrupting cell division and expansion. However, MeJA application, particularly at 50 and 100 µM, significantly enhanced fruit length even under severe B stress, suggesting its role in maintaining cellular function and growth. The longest fruits were recorded with 50 and 100 µM MeJA combined with low boron levels (23–81 µM), demonstrating a synergistic effect. While 25 µM MeJA provided noticeable protection under mild stress, its efficacy decreased at higher boron concentrations.

Fruit number significantly decreased with increasing B, especially at 162 and 323 µM, indicating that high boron impairs reproductive development, likely by affecting flower function or fruit set (Fig. 1F). However, MeJA application—particularly at 100 µM—substantially alleviated these effects. The highest fruit number was observed with 100 µM MeJA under no B stress, suggesting MeJA can enhance fruit set even under optimal conditions. At moderate B stress (81 µM), 100 µM MeJA also maintained significantly higher fruit numbers, indicating protection against early boron damage. While 25 and 50 µM MeJA improved fruit number at low to moderate B, their effects were less pronounced than 100 µM, highlighting a concentration-dependent response. Under severe B stress (323 µM), none of the MeJA treatments fully restored fruit number, suggesting that excessive boron toxicity surpasses MeJA’s protective capacity.

Fruit weight steadily declined with increasing boron levels in plants without MeJA, reaching its lowest point at 323 µM B—indicating boron toxicity hampers fruit biomass, likely through disrupted assimilate transport and impaired cell expansion (Fig. 1G). MeJA application significantly mitigated this reduction. At 25 and 50 µM, fruit weight notably improved under 81 and 162 µM B, peaking at 81 µM, suggesting moderate MeJA doses alleviate mild boron stress by supporting hormonal balance and metabolic processes during fruit development. Although 100 µM MeJA maintained relatively high fruit weight under low to moderate B stress, a decline occurred at 323 µM, implying that severe boron toxicity may overwhelm MeJA’s protective capacity.

Discussion

Boron plays a dual role in plants, serving as an essential micronutrient at optimal levels but becoming toxic when accumulated in excess. In the present study, high boron concentrations significantly reduced shoot and root biomass, as well as fruit yield traits, highlighting its detrimental impact on plant growth (Fig. 1A-G). These reductions are primarily linked to boron-induced alterations in cell wall integrity and the inhibition of cell division and elongation, which collectively restrict organ development [19, 72]. Boron toxicity also triggered severe oxidative stress, as evidenced by the accumulation of H₂O₂ and elevated MDA, a key marker of lipid peroxidation and membrane damage [26, 73]. Consequently, membrane integrity was compromised, resulting in increased electrolyte leakage, disrupted nutrient transport, and impaired cellular homeostasis. The simultaneous decline in relative water content (RWC) further reflected cellular dehydration and reduced metabolic activity [30]. Moreover, excessive boron markedly decreased chlorophyll levels and other photosynthetic pigments, limiting assimilate production necessary for fruit development [27, 74]. Boron stress also disturbed the uptake and balance of vital nutrients such as nitrogen, calcium, iron, and zinc, which are crucial for protein synthesis, enzymatic activity, and pigment biosynthesis [30, 75]. Collectively, these combined effects of boron accumulation, oxidative stress, and impaired water relations explain the pronounced reductions in vegetative growth and reproductive traits observed in ‘Albion’ strawberry plants. Similar growth inhibition under high boron has also been reported in trifoliate orange (Poncirus trifoliata) [76], sweet cherry [77], pomelo (Citrus grandis L.) [31], and kiwifruit [29], confirming the widespread impact of boron toxicity across species.

Exogenous application of MeJA substantially alleviated the adverse effects of boron toxicity in ‘Albion’ strawberry plants. By stimulating antioxidant defense enzymes, MeJA reduced lipid peroxidation and lowered H₂O₂ and MDA accumulation under stress, thereby enhancing redox homeostasis. These improvements were further reflected in increased RWC and reduced electrolyte leakage, indicating greater membrane stability and improved water conservation [78, 79]. An optimal concentration of MeJA also promoted cell elongation during fruit development, contributing to larger fruit size and weight [36, 80]. Preharvest application of MeJA has been widely demonstrated to enhance fruit growth and yield across various species. For example, treatment with 0.5 mM MeJA significantly increased fruit size by enhancing both vertical and transverse diameters in pear [81].

Similarly, low concentrations of MeJA sprays improved overall yield in grape [82], pomegranate [83], and yellow pitahaya (Selenicereus megalanthus Haw.) [84]. In sweet cherry cv. ‘North Wonder’, spraying MeJA three weeks prior to harvest led to a notable increase in fruit mass and diameter [85]. Overall, these findings underscore MeJA’s effectiveness as a protective agent that mitigates boron-induced oxidative stress, maintains physiological integrity, and enhances both vegetative growth and fruit-related traits such as weight, number, and length in strawberry plants [43, 86].

Photosynthesis relies primarily on chlorophyll and carotenoids, which serve as its key pigments. Chloroplasts are highly susceptible to oxidative damage, and their stability is critical for maintaining pigment levels [87]. In ‘Albion’ strawberry, elevated boron concentrations caused a notable decline in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, carotenoids, and SPAD index, highlighting the vulnerability of the photosynthetic apparatus under boron stress (Fig. 2A- E). This reduction is likely driven by multiple physiological processes. Excess boron promotes the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, such as H₂O₂, which can damage thylakoid membranes and other chloroplast components, resulting in chlorophyll degradation and impaired photosystem II function. Furthermore, boron toxicity disrupts the uptake and homeostasis of essential nutrients—including nitrogen, calcium, iron, and zinc—that are vital for chlorophyll synthesis, membrane stability, and pigment-related enzymatic activities. Deficiencies in these elements further restrict pigment production, contributing to the observed decline in photosynthetic pigments [5, 21, 30, 76].

Foliar application of MeJA partially alleviated the negative effects of boron stress on photosynthetic pigments. Treatment with 50–100 µM MeJA enhanced chlorophyll a and b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid concentrations, particularly under low to moderate boron toxicity. Our findings suggest that MeJA mitigates boron-induced pigment degradation, most likely by activating antioxidant defense systems and reducing oxidative stress. By counteracting ROS-mediated damage, MeJA helps preserve chlorophyll and carotenoid integrity, sustaining the photosynthetic machinery and supporting overall plant growth and productivity. These effects are likely linked to MeJA’s ability to boost antioxidant defenses and stabilize chloroplast structure, although the precise mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated [78, 79, 88]. These effects align with previous studies showing that MeJA stabilizes photosynthetic pigments under various stress conditions [89, 90]. Similar results were reported by Karimi et al. [91], who applied 100 µM MeJA to grapevines under salt stress. A plausible mechanism for this protective effect involves limiting membrane lipid peroxidation in chloroplasts—particularly within thylakoid membranes—through the enhancement of antioxidant enzyme activities and scavenging of reactive oxygen species. Together, these actions preserve the integrity of the photosynthetic apparatus and maintain pigment stability under stress.

Under boron stress, RWC in ‘Albion’ strawberry leaves declined markedly, consistent with observations in Kiwifruit, loquat, Citrus rootstocks, and Narcissus [29, 92–94]. This reduction likely results from impaired root water uptake and compromised membrane integrity, as excess boron induces ROS overproduction, such as H₂O₂, which damages membrane lipids and proteins, increasing permeability and electrolyte leakage. Foliar application of MeJA, particularly at higher doses, alleviated this decline, suggesting its role in maintaining membrane stability and osmotic adjustment (Fig. 3A). These gains were accompanied by reductions in H₂O₂ and MDA, as well as decreased electrolyte leakage, indicating enhanced membrane stability and limited oxidative damage. Comparable protective effects of MeJA on plant water status have been reported under abiotic stress in other crops, such as purple basil and blueberry [39, 95]. Additionally, MeJA appears to support stomatal regulation through interactions with abscisic acid, further sustaining cellular water balance [91, 96].

The increase in RWC observed in MeJA-treated strawberry plants under boron stress can be attributed to improved cellular integrity and osmotic regulation, highlighting MeJA’s role in enhancing physiological resilience through maintenance of water balance. Cell membranes are crucial for maintaining cellular structure and enabling normal physiological and metabolic processes. Electrolyte leakage (EL) and MDA are widely used indicators of membrane integrity and lipid peroxidation. In this study, ‘Albion’ strawberry plants under elevated boron (B) levels exhibited clear stress symptoms, as evidenced by significant increases in EL, MDA, and H₂O₂ (Fig. 3B- D). The pronounced rise in EL, particularly at 81 µM B, indicates that even moderate B concentrations compromise membrane integrity, likely due to ROS-induced lipid peroxidation. Elevated MDA levels at 81 and 162 µM B further confirm peroxidative membrane damage, while the progressive accumulation of H₂O₂ reflects a strong oxidative burst and disrupted redox homeostasis. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that boron toxicity induces oxidative stress, which activates antioxidant defense mechanisms—including CAT, APX, and SOD—to scavenge ROS and limit cellular damage [19, 97–100].

Exogenous MeJA application mitigated boron-induced oxidative damage in ‘Albion’ strawberry. Treatment with 50–100 µM MeJA produced the greatest reductions in EL, MDA, and H₂O₂, suggesting enhanced membrane stability and improved redox homeostasis. The protective effect of MeJA can be attributed to its ability to enhance both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant defenses, reduce ROS accumulation, and stabilize membrane lipids, thereby maintaining cellular integrity and physiological function. By limiting MDA formation, MeJA slows membrane lipid oxidation and prevents damage to membrane-bound proteins and enzymes involved in ion transport, signal transduction, and metabolic processes. Comparable reductions in MDA and H₂O₂ under MeJA treatment have been observed in banana peels [100], papaya [101], blueberries [102], Cassia obtusifolia L [103]., and Iranian grape [41], supporting the generality of this mechanism. These results highlight the role of MeJA in preserving membrane integrity, enhancing antioxidant capacity, and mitigating ROS-mediated injury in ‘Albion’ strawberry under boron stress [38, 94].

Carbohydrates, stored as starch, can be mobilized to produce osmo-protective compounds under stress, linking starch metabolism closely to plant growth and environmental adaptation [104]. Methyl jasmonate has been shown to increase soluble sugars under abiotic stress, serving as photosynthetic products for long-distance transport through the phloem [105]. In this study, boron toxicity suppressed the accumulation of key osmolytes—soluble sugars, glycine betaine (GB), and proline—indicating disruption of metabolic pathways involved in osmoprotection in ‘Albion’ strawberry (Fig. 4A- C). The increase in soluble sugars under 162–323 µM B with 50 µM MeJA likely reflects improved carbohydrate metabolism, consistent with observations in Brassica napus L., loquat, and pepper [79, 96, 106]. MeJA also stimulated GB biosynthesis, especially at 25–50 µM under moderate B levels, potentially via upregulation of betaine aldehyde dehydrogenase, in line with findings in Glycine max under Ni stress and in Vicia faba and Mentha arvensis under Cd stress [107–110]. Proline content was markedly elevated with MeJA, particularly at 100 µM under 162–323 µM B, reflecting enhanced biosynthesis through activation of key metabolic enzymes, as previously reported in Abelmoschus esculentus L., Portulaca oleracea, and grapevine rootstocks [78, 111, 112]. Foliar application of 100 µM MeJA also improved proline accumulation and drought resistance in grapevine rootstocks [78].

The accumulation of these osmolytes supports osmotic adjustment, protein and membrane stabilization, and ROS detoxification. Proline not only maintains osmotic balance but also stabilizes cellular structures and scavenges ROS [113, 114], while GB contributes to osmoregulation, protection of photosynthetic proteins, and enhancement of antioxidant defenses under stress [115, 116]. The accumulation of soluble sugars further aids in maintaining membrane integrity, stabilizing proteins, and scavenging excess ROS [117]. Preharvest MeJA application has also been reported to increase total soluble solids in pear [81] and enhance sugar and organic acid contents in pomegranate [83], supporting the general role of jasmonates in carbohydrate metabolism under stress.

The interactive effects of boron toxicity and MeJA on fruit biochemical traits indicate that MeJA-mediated hormonal signaling plays a crucial role in maintaining strawberry fruit quality under stress (Fig. 5A- H). Elevated boron levels in untreated ‘Albion’ strawberry plants disrupted the acid–sugar balance, resulting in higher pH, increased total soluble solids (TSS), and reduced titratable acidity (TA). This is likely due to stress-induced metabolic shifts, including enhanced respiration that accelerates the degradation of organic acids—key intermediates of the TCA cycle [103, 118, 119].

Organic acids, as secondary metabolites derived from the citric acid cycle, are gradually consumed during ripening. Treatments like MeJA that moderate excessive respiratory activity help preserve organic acid content by limiting oxidation and conversion of sugars [58, 120]. Additionally, as ripening progresses, sucrose phosphate synthase activity rises while enzymes converting sucrose into organic acids decrease [121]. Breakdown of carbohydrates, pectic substances, glycosaccharides, and proteins further contributes to pH increase [58]. Elevated TSS under boron stress likely reflects intensified carbohydrate catabolism and osmotic adjustment, where starch and sucrose are converted into soluble sugars via amylases and invertases, acting as osmolytes to maintain cell turgor and stabilize cellular function [122, 123].

Boron-induced root damage may also limit nutrient uptake, triggering compensatory sugar redistribution. Foliar application of MeJA, particularly at moderate concentrations (25–50 µM), mitigated these boron-induced effects, maintaining a favorable balance of pH, TA, and TSS, likely through partial stabilization of respiratory metabolism [45, 117]. Higher concentrations (100 µM) appeared less effective, possibly due to metabolic feedback inhibition. MeJA treatment also significantly influenced antioxidant metabolism. Ascorbic acid (AsA), a non-enzymatic antioxidant, was reduced under boron stress due to intensified oxidative damage. Exogenous MeJA, particularly at 50 µM, enhanced AsA levels, likely via increased activity of ascorbate peroxidase (APX), which detoxifies hydrogen peroxide, and possibly by modulating enzymes in the AsA–GSH cycle such as MDHAR and DHAR [124–126]. MeJA also upregulated enzymatic antioxidants including SOD, CAT, and peroxidases, reducing ROS accumulation and protecting AsA from oxidative degradation [127]. Foliar MeJA applications have consistently been reported to elevate AsA levels, total antioxidant capacity, antioxidant enzyme activities, and organic acids in various fruits [36, 128–131].

Similarly, total phenolic content (TPC) and flavonoids increased under MeJA treatment, reflecting activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway. Phenolic and flavonoid compounds scavenge ROS, stabilize membranes, and modulate stress signaling [132–134]. Boron stress alone modestly activated phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), the gateway enzyme of phenolic biosynthesis, indicating that B toxicity alone is insufficient to trigger this pathway. In contrast, MeJA induced a dose-dependent increase in PAL activity, particularly at 50–100 µM, enhancing secondary metabolite production, supporting non-enzymatic antioxidant defenses, and stabilizing cellular structures under stress. The pronounced rise in PAL at 100 µM MeJA under moderate to high boron stress highlights strong induction of secondary metabolites [135–137].

MeJA-mediated activation of phenolic metabolism also reduces polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity, delaying phenolic oxidation [138]. The increased TPC and associated enzymatic activities align with findings in strawberry, blackberry, raspberry, persimmon, mango, pomegranate, and other fruit crops following MeJA application [36, 39, 82, 83, 139–142]. DPPH radical scavenging activity, indicative of total non-enzymatic antioxidant capacity, was enhanced by MeJA, especially at 50 µM, reflecting both increased enzymatic defense and accumulation of secondary metabolites such as phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins [41, 143, 144]. This effect is mediated via the phenylpropanoid pathway, with key enzymes including PAL, chalcone synthase (CHS), and peroxidases (POD) contributing to elevated antioxidant activity [145–147]. Preharvest MeJA applications in grapes, blood oranges, and other fruits similarly upregulated genes involved in phenylpropanoid and terpenoid metabolism, enhancing the accumulation of bioactive compounds [148, 149].

Anthocyanins, major pigments in strawberry responsible for red coloration, were only minimally affected by boron stress alone. Low-dose MeJA (25 µM) caused slight increases, while 50 µM MeJA significantly enhanced anthocyanin content under moderate B stress, likely through activation of stress-responsive pathways and flavonoid biosynthesis. MeJA upregulates PAL and other flavonoid pathway enzymes, promoting anthocyanin accumulation and stabilization [150, 151]. These findings align with reports in Fragaria × ananassa, blackberry, raspberry, apple, pear, grape, and other fruit species [81–83, 141, 142, 149, 152–154]. Overall, the results demonstrate that MeJA acts as a potent elicitor of fruit metabolic defenses under boron toxicity, modulating respiration, supporting sugar and organic acid metabolism, enhancing antioxidant systems, and maintaining fruit quality traits including flavor, nutritional composition, and pigment stability.

Under boron toxicity, the coordinated responses of key antioxidant enzymes—CAT, GPX, APX, and SOD—to MeJA reveal a dynamic, enzyme-specific regulation that maintains redox balance and oxidative stress tolerance (Fig. 6A- D). CAT activity increased markedly in a dose-dependent manner, particularly under moderate to high MeJA concentrations combined with elevated boron, highlighting its central role in H₂O₂ detoxification during stress. GPX activity was sharply induced at 50 µM MeJA under 162 µM B, reflecting a finely tuned ROS-scavenging response. In contrast, APX showed only modest, inconsistent increases across treatments, suggesting a limited or context-dependent role in MeJA-mediated oxidative stress mitigation. The modest APX response may reflect its role in maintaining steady-state redox conditions rather than dynamically responding to acute stress. In contrast, SOD activity, which catalyzes the initial dismutation of superoxide radicals, was significantly enhanced under 50–100 µM MeJA, particularly with 162 µM B, but declined at higher MeJA levels, possibly due to feedback inhibition or metabolic burden. MeJA modulates antioxidant enzymes through the activation of the jasmonic acid signaling pathway [155]. These responses are consistent with studies showing that MeJA (0.1 mM) significantly enhances APX, CAT, POD, and SOD activity in blueberry fruits [135] and that 100 µM MeJA increases CAT, POX, and APX activities during lemon fruit development [131].

Boron toxicity disrupted the homeostasis of essential macro- and micronutrients in strawberry leaves, causing progressive declines in N, Ca²⁺, Fe²⁺, and Zn²⁺ concentrations, likely due to impaired assimilation, transporter inhibition, or membrane damage under high B stress (Fig. 7A- E). Interestingly, boron accumulation in leaves increased with rising external B concentrations, reflecting dose-dependent uptake. These results align with previous findings showing that boron toxicity induces oxidative stress, alters cell wall structure, and disrupts nitrogen metabolism, as observed in trifoliate orange [156] and tomato [157]. Foliar application of MeJA, particularly at moderate-to-high concentrations (50–100 µM), effectively mitigated these nutrient losses, enhancing nitrogen retention, partially restoring Ca²⁺ levels, and improving Fe²⁺ and Zn²⁺ uptake. MeJA-treated plants retained more N and Ca²⁺, supporting growth and structural stability, and improved Fe²⁺ and Zn²⁺ levels indicate better nutrient retention under stress. These findings indicate that MeJA supports nutrient metabolism and transport, reinforcing the plant’s physiological resilience under boron stress. However, MeJA application moderated excessive B accumulation under high B stress, suggesting a protective role in preventing over-accumulation and potential toxicity. Similar effects have been reported in grapevine under drought [78], Calendula officinalis under chromium stress [158], and Triticum aestivum under cadmium toxicity [159], underscoring MeJA’s multifunctional role in supporting mineral nutrition, optimizing nitrogen metabolism, and enhancing stress adaptation across diverse plant species.

Conclusion

This research confirms that excess boron negatively influences growth, nutrient acquisition, and fruit quality in Fragaria × ananassa ‘Albion’. Foliar treatment with MeJA, especially at 50 µM, showed partial effectiveness in lessening the physiological and biochemical effects of B toxicity. This was reflected in improved antioxidant enzyme function, accumulation of compatible solutes, more stable nutrient levels, and better fruit quality indicators, including sugar–acid balance, ascorbic acid content, and the accumulation of bioactive secondary compounds. While these results point to MeJA as a potentially valuable agent for enhancing tolerance to boron-related stress, its effectiveness diminished at higher concentrations and under more severe B toxicity, underscoring the importance of identifying optimal application levels. Despite these promising findings, it is important to acknowledge several constraints. This experiment was conducted in a controlled hydroponic setting with a single strawberry cultivar, which may limit the broader applicability of the results. Therefore, field trials are essential to verify these outcomes across a range of cultivars, soil types, and environmental scenarios. Additionally, future research should explore the long-term reliability, appropriate dosing strategies, environmental persistence, and any unintended physiological effects associated with repeated MeJA applications in horticultural systems.
