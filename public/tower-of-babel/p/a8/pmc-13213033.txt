Experimental study on seismic performance of prefabricated grid composite walls with different vertical connection forms
Authors: Cheng-hua Zhang, Xiao-long Wang, Qing Qin, Liang Cheng, Zhi-gang Gao, Lei Wang, Hua Zhang, Guang-hong Xiong, Chun-lin Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis experimental study evaluates the seismic performance of two vertical connection types in a novel precast grid composite wall structure (PGCWS): a steel bar anchorage (SW-1) and a mortar cushion connection (SW-2). Quasi-static cyclic tests were conducted on 1:2-scale specimens to assess failure modes, hysteretic behavior, stiffness degradation, and energy dissipation capacity. The results indicate that SW-1 maintains structural integrity and facilitates controlled damage distribution, demonstrating ductile failure with an ultimate drift of 1/45.5, stable hysteresis loops, and high energy dissipation (20,400 kN mm). In contrast, SW-2 exhibited premature interface slip, resulting in brittle failure characterized by significant hysteresis pinching, rapid stiffness loss, and substantially lower energy dissipation (11,400 kN mm), with a limited ultimate drift of 1/66.5. Based on these findings, performance-based design recommendations are proposed. The steel bar anchorage is advised for mid- to high-rise buildings in high-seismic regions due to its robustness and energy dissipation capability. The mortar cushion connection may be suitable for low- to mid-rise structures where construction efficiency is emphasized, provided that inter-story drift is strictly controlled. This research offers essential experimental evidence to support the practical implementation of PGCWS.Keywords: Precast grid composite wall, Vertical connection joint, Quasi-static test, Steel bar anchorage connection, Seismic design recommendationsSubject terms: Engineering, Civil engineering

Introduction

Against the backdrop of escalating global climate change and tightening resource constraints, the building sector—responsible for a substantial share of worldwide energy consumption and carbon emissions—faces urgent demands for energy conservation and emission reduction. Statistics indicate that the combined carbon emissions from building operations and construction materials exceed one-third of the global total1,2. Conventional cast-in-place concrete structures have long been criticized for their high energy intensity during construction and low material efficiency. In this context, precast building systems have emerged as a pivotal direction for industrial upgrading in construction, leveraging advantages such as industrialized production and rapid on-site assembly3,4. However, conventional precast shear wall systems still encounter critical technical challenges, among which the unverified reliability of joint connections under seismic action remains a primary obstacle, directly compromising structural integrity and energy dissipation capacity.Therefore, while energy efficiency is a significant driver for prefabrication, this study focuses on addressing the seismic performance of connections as a key requirement for safe and widespread adoption.

Reliable connection details are essential to ensure structural integrity and seismic performance in precast buildings, with their design being a decisive factor for load-bearing capacity, construction efficiency, and broader market penetration5,6. Extensive research has thus been conducted on precast shear wall systems, focusing primarily on two broad categories of connections: horizontal and vertical. For horizontal connections, studies have explored various mechanical and dissipative solutions. Sun etal.7 investigated bolted steel plate connections, while Zhi etal.8 examined lap splices. To enhance seismic resilience, innovative energy-dissipating horizontal connections have been proposed and validated experimentally and numerically9–11, Fan et al.12 proposed a novel prefabricated shear wall structure with a focus on its resilience model, Furthermore, the influence of design parameters such as axial compression ratio on the performance of horizontal joints has been analyzed13,14, with comparative studies highlighting behavioral differences from cast-in-place systems15. Regarding vertical connections, grouted sleeve solutions have been widely studied for their effectiveness in stress transfer16,17. For instance, research on precast concrete shear walls with mortar–sleeve connections for longitudinal bars confirmed effective stress transfer, though it also highlighted drawbacks such as strain concentration near the splice and reduced deformation capacity compared to cast-in-place walls16. Researchers have also developed diverse alternatives to improve construct ability and performance, including keyed joints18, novel sleeve designs19, specialized lap splices20,21, and Laminated Reinforced Concrete Shear Wall with a Vertical Seam22,often evaluating the effects of axial stress and reinforcement details21.Meanwhile, Fu et al.23 proposed a novel precast shear wall with unconnected vertical distributed reinforcements. However, a critical weakness persists, as evidenced by studies on lightly reinforced precast wall systems, where panel-to-panel joints were consistently identified as the weakest link, leading to premature shear failure and limiting the system’s overall energy dissipation capacity6.

Overall, the prevailing approach has been to modify joint configurations. A growing body of evidence suggests that systems employing vertical connections offer greater structural stability and adaptability than those with horizontal ones24–27, in addition to streamlining on-site assembly28–30,This notion is strongly reinforced by the comprehensive state-of-the-art review by Kurama et al.31, However, many existing solutions prioritize structural performance, often at the expense of integrated building envelope functionality—such as thermal insulation and air-tightness—or involve complex on-site operations that offset the advantages of prefabrication.This gap underscores the need for innovative structural systems that holistically address seismic safety, thermal performance, and construction efficiency.

To bridge this gap, the present study introduces a prefabricated grid composite wall structure (PGCWS). This system employs modular, nestable wall panels with inherent thermal insulation and is designed with a damage-controlled philosophy to achieve staged energy dissipation under seismic loads.However, the efficacy of this integrated design critically depends on the performance of its vertical connections, which must coordinate deformation and force transfer among the distinct components—namely, the precast panel, the perimeter frame, and the concealed beams. Despite the system’s innovative concept, the seismic behavior, failure mechanisms, and overall effectiveness of practical vertical connection details within this specific composite system remain experimentally unverified. This lack of fundamental performance data constitutes a pivotal research gap that hinders the performance-based design and practical application of the PGCWS.

Directly responding to this need, the prefabricated grid composite wall structure (PGCWS) proposed in this study is a modular seismic-resistant system characterized by its stage-controlled energy dissipation mechanism and functionally partitioned damage zones. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the system comprises prefabricated grid composite wall panels (PGCPs), implicit frames, floor slabs, and related auxiliary members, which are assembled on-site and integrated through cast-in-place connections to form the complete structure. The PGCP, serving as the fundamental infill module, is manufactured off-site. Its core configuration, shown in Fig. 1b, consists of a reinforced concrete lattice frame—formed by interconnected gridded beams and columns—with initially embedded aerated concrete blocks. This configuration inherently provides continuous thermal insulation through the infill blocks, directly addressing the envelope performance limitation noted in conventional systems. To enhance sustainability and regional adaptability, these infill blocks can be substituted with locally available or alternative materials, such as plant-fiber raw soil-based composites, recycled EPS lightweight aggregate concrete, or cotton-straw reinforced materials32–35. As the primary lateral-force-resisting unit within the PGCWS, the assembled prefabricated grid composite wall (PGCW) integrates the PGCP with perimeter connecting columns and confined concealed beams into a nested structural design. This design strategically allocates materials and components with distinct mechanical properties into predefined damage zones, following a damage-control philosophy. The result is a tun able, multi-stage energy dissipation mechanism capable of responding to varying seismic intensity demands. Under lateral loading, all components interact through mutual restraint and coordinated force transfer, significantly enhancing the overall structural resilience and repair ability. This innovative conceptual framework aligns with the ongoing development of advanced precast wall systems3 and presents a promising approach for designing high-performance, adaptable seismic-resistant structures.

Prefabricated grid composite wall structure (PGCWS).

Nevertheless, the practical implementation and performance realization of this novel system are critically dependent on the behavior of its vertical connections, an aspect that remains insufficiently explored. This critical dependency arises because the nested design, which enables the staged energy dissipation, fundamentally relies on vertical connections to coordinate force transfer and deformation among the distinct components (i.e., the PGCP, connecting columns, and concealed beams). The efficacy of its nested design and the intended collaborative load-transfer mechanism hinge fundamentally on the reliable performance and deformation compatibility of these connections. Previous research on specific vertical connection types, such as grouted sleeve splices, has identified key factors governing deformation capacity and failure modes—issues that must be meticulously addressed in the design of connections for such innovative composite systems6. Furthermore, documented vulnerabilities in panel-to-panel joints of other precast systems25 underscore the imperative for rigorous experimental validation of any new connection detail intended for systems relying on integral composite action.

To address this research gap, the present study systematically investigates the seismic performance of two typical vertical connection configurations employed in the PGCWS: mortar bed joints and rebar anchorage connections. Quasi-static cyclic tests were conducted on two half-scale PGCW specimens to evaluate and compare their mechanical properties, failure mechanisms, and key seismic performance indicators. These indicators include load-carrying capacity, hysteretic response, ductility, stiffness degradation, and energy dissipation capacity. The findings aim to validate the effectiveness of these connection methods within the proposed structural system and to establish a solid theoretical and experimental basis for their future engineering application.

Experimental overview

Specimen design

To evaluate the seismic performance, two 1:2-scale prefabricated grid composite wall (PGCW) specimens were designed based on a six-story prototype structure. The use of a reduced-scale model is a well-established practice in seismic experimentation. The validity of the 1:2 scaling adopted herein is grounded on three key principles: (1) Material Identity: Identical concrete and steel reinforcement were used for the model and the prototype, ensuring realistic constitutive material behavior; (2) Equivalence of Key Parameters: Similitude laws (Table 1) were applied to preserve critical dimensionless indices, most importantly the drift ratio (displacement-to-height ratio), thereby ensuring that the model and prototype undergo equivalent damage states; (3) Fitness for Purpose: As the primary objective is a comparative assessment of two connection detailing methods, the scaled model provides a controlled and effective platform. While certain limitations inherent to scale modeling (e.g., perfect bond-slip similitude) are acknowledged, the design prioritizes the accurate capture of global force-transfer mechanisms and failure modes central to this investigation.

The connection methodologies for Specimens SW-1 and SW-2 were implemented as follows.

Specimen SW-1 (Rebar anchorage connection)

This connection was designed to achieve a monolithic, rigid interface. Pre-drilled holes with a diameter of 50 mm were formed in the foundation beam prior to assembly. The anchorage depth exceeded 300 mm, in compliance with the design codes GB 50010-201036 and GB 50011-201037. Following the hoisting and positioning of the precast wall panel, the longitudinal rebars projecting from the base of the lattice columns were inserted into these holes. The annulus around the rebars was then pressure-grouted with C35 fine-aggregate concrete, which had a specified flowability of 280 mm to ensure complete filling. Subsequently, the laterally protruding reinforcement from the panel sides and the vertical rebars at the panel top were aligned and anchored. Finally, reinforcement for the boundary elements was placed, formwork was assembled, and the joint zone was cast with concrete to create an integral monolithic system.

Specimen SW-2 (Mortar bed joint connection)

This connection facilitated load transfer primarily through a masonry mortar bed. The construction procedure began with placing an approximately 20-mm-thick layer of M10 mortar (minimum specified compressive strength) onto the prepared surface of the foundation beam. The precast wall panel was then lowered and seated onto the freshly placed mortar bed within a critical 15-min window to ensure optimal bond integrity. After panel placement, the exposed horizontal reinforcement on both lateral sides and the vertical rebars at the top were aligned and fixed. The reinforcement cage for the boundary elements was then installed. To complete the assembly, the joint zone was cast with concrete, forming a continuous cast-in-place structural region.

Verification and structural integrity

For both specimens, the development length of all reinforcement anchored into the boundary elements and concealed beams satisfied the stipulations of the relevant codes36,37. These wet-connection processes were designed to ensure reliable force transfer between the precast panels and the surrounding frame, resulting in a rigid composite assembly capable of resisting combined shear, moment, and axial actions.

Test loading system

To replicate seismic actions, a pseudo-static cyclic loading regime was employed following the Chinese technical specification JGJ/T 101-201542. The overall test setup is illustrated in Fig. 6. A hybrid force–displacement control strategy was implemented, with the specific loading history detailed in Fig. 7.

Vertical loading The axial load was determined by scaling the compressive stress from the bottom-story wall of the prototype structure. The design load on the prototype bearing wall was 440 kN, which corresponded to a vertical load of 110 kN on the specimen based on the similitude relationships. This load was applied uniformly through a spreader beam system and maintained constant throughout the test.

The design load on the prototype bearing wall was 440 kN, which corresponded to a vertical load of 110 kN on the specimen based on the similitude relationships. This load was applied uniformly through a spreader beam system and maintained constant throughout the test.

Horizontal loading After the vertical load stabilized, lateral loading was applied under a hybrid force–displacement control protocol. Prior to yielding, force-controlled loading was implemented in increments of 20 kN per cycle, with one cycle performed at each load level. Upon observation of initial cracking, an additional cycle was applied at the same displacement amplitude. After yielding, the control mode was SWitched to displacement control, wherein the displacement amplitude was increased stepwise with three fully reversed cycles performed at each amplitude. Loading continued until the lateral load dropped below 85% of the peak strength, and was sustained thereafter until specimen failure.

Experimental results and analysis

This section presents a comprehensive analysis of the data obtained from quasi-static cyclic tests on two half-scale Prefabricated Grid Composite Wall (PGCW) specimens. A systematic comparison of the results is conducted to quantify the influence of the wall-to-foundation connection detail—rebar anchorage (SW-1) versus a mortar bed joint (SW-2)—on key seismic performance indicators. These include damage progression, hysteretic behavior, deformation capacity, stiffness degradation, energy dissipation capacity, and internal strain distribution.

Failure modes and damage evolution

The failure mechanisms under reversed cyclic loading showed fundamentally different progression patterns, dictated by the connection type (Fig. 9).

Failure images of precast grid walls. (Crack color indicates loading direction: red—positive; blue—negative.)

The rebar-grouted connection in SW-1 ensured effective composite action, resulting in a sequential and ductile damage evolution. During the initial force-controlled phase (≤ 55 kN), the response remained predominantly linear-elastic, with only micro-cracking observed on the infill block surfaces. Upon SWitching to displacement control, major diagonal cracks initiated within the infill at approximately 68 kN and progressively propagated towards the interface with the surrounding grid frame. The rigid base connection successfully confined the core damage zone to the infill panel at this stage. As the drift increased, cracking extended into the rib beams and columns (~ 84 kN), followed by the development of horizontal flexural cracks at the base of the boundary columns, indicating a shift in the primary moment resistance mechanism. Yielding of the longitudinal reinforcement commenced at around 91 kN, which was accompanied by the formation of new shear cracks at the web-boundary interface. The specimen attained its peak lateral load of 118.7 kN, characterized by minor concrete crushing at the column base and localized spalling in the heavily cracked infill regions (Fig. 7a). The post-peak phase was marked by gradual strength degradation, culminating in severe concrete crushing and reinforcement buckling at the column base, alongside extensive spalling of the infill material. This progressive engagement of the infill, grid, and frame components demonstrates an effective redistribution of stresses and a high energy dissipation capacity.

In stark contrast, the structural behavior of SW-2 was dominated by the premature degradation of the mortar interface. Diagonal cracks emerged on the infill block surfaces at a significantly lower load of 34 kN (only ~ 50% of SW-1’s cracking load), indicating an early loss of interfacial bond and shear transfer capability. Subsequently, beyond 42 kN, cracks propagated rapidly along ~ 45° diagonals through the infill and into the rib joints, a direct consequence of the lack of mechanical interlock and the ensuing interface slip (Fig. 7b). This stage was characterized by progressive and visible slip along the bedding plane. With further loading, horizontal flexural cracks developed at the tensile side of the outer column base at approximately 81 kN. The specimen attained its comparable peak lateral load of 117.7 kN, which coincided with pronounced crack widening and incipient concrete crushing at the column base. However, the post-peak phase exhibited an abrupt strength loss, as damage intensely concentrated within the lower wall region, ultimately leading to severe disintegration of the infill blocks and a rapid deterioration of lateral resistance.

While both specimens ultimately failed in shear-dominated modes, their damage sequences and failure mechanisms were fundamentally distinct, governed by the connection detail. SW-1 exhibited a ductile, distributed damage pattern involving sequential yielding of multiple components, indicating a robust and redundant load path. Conversely, SW-2 experienced a brittle, localized failure, concentrated primarily along the weak mortar interface and within the lower wall zone, demonstrating a single, non-ductile failure mechanism. This marked divergence unequivocally highlights the decisive role of the vertical connection in controlling the seismic damage hierarchy, energy dissipation mode, and collapse mechanism of the precast grid wall system. The grouted connection promoted integrity and distributed inelasticity, whereas the mortared connection led to a vulnerable interface that triggered premature localization and brittle failure.

Hysteretic behavior

The hysteretic load–displacement responses, which are critical for evaluating seismic energy dissipation and damage tolerance, are presented in Fig. 10. Both specimens underwent a characteristic evolution from initial linear-elastic loops to developed spindle-shaped loops, and finally to significantly pinched loops, reflecting the cumulative effects of stiffness degradation, strength deterioration, and increasing nonlinear slip throughout the loading history.

Hysteretic curve.

As shown in Fig. 10a, Specimen SW-1 exhibited relatively full and stable spindle-shaped hysteresis loops after yielding. The moderate pinching observed during unloading and reloading phases can be attributed to a composite energy dissipation mechanism. This mechanism encompassed the closure of widely distributed fine cracks, controlled bond-slip along the anchored reinforcing bars, and dowel action across cracks, which collectively facilitated stable and considerable energy dissipation per cycle (quantified in “Synthesis and performance-based design implications” section). The robustness of the connection maintained the integrity of the load path, allowing for sustained hysteretic stability even at large drift levels.

In stark contrast, the hysteretic response of SW-2 was dominated by severe pinching, resulting in inverse-S-shaped loops from an early stage (Fig. 10b). This pronounced pinching is a direct manifestation of significant shear slip and intermittent “stick–slip” behavior at the mortar-bedded interface during load reversals. The absence of a well-developed spindle shape indicates that energy dissipation relied primarily on Coulomb friction along the degrading interface, with minimal contribution from distributed inelastic deformation within the concrete components. Consequently, the system exhibited limited, less stable, and rapidly deteriorating hysteretic energy dissipation capacity.

Comparative Analysis and Implications:The stark divergence in hysteretic loop morphology between the two specimens—full spindles versus pinched inversed-S shapes—provides unequivocal experimental evidence that the vertical connection detail fundamentally dictates the energy dissipation mechanism and cyclic performance of the system. The grouted anchorage in SW-1 promoted distributed inelasticity and stable hysteresis, characteristic of a ductile, composite system. Conversely, the mortared interface in SW-2 precipitated a brittle, slip-concentrated response, leading to poor energy dissipation and rapid cyclic degradation. This critical linkage between connection design and hysteretic quality forms the basis for the mechanistic discussion on seismic resilience in “Mechanistic interpretation: the connection as the governing factor in system behavior” section, where these behavioral patterns are further explained through analytical models.

Specimen SW-1 exhibited superior pre-peak performance. Its mean cracking load was 60% higher than SW-2’s (54.5 vs. 34.0 kN), indicating significantly enhanced initial stiffness. It also yielded at a 16% higher load (96.6 vs. 87.9 kN) and subsequently developed a pronounced, sustained hardening phase, reaching peak load at a larger drift. Its post-peak strength decay was gradual.

Interpretation and link to seismic design

The high cracking/yield loads and ductile skeleton of SW-1 stem from the rigid composite action of its grouted connection, which promotes distributed damage and stable energy dissipation. For SW-2, the flexible mortar interface leads to premature yielding and a brittle skeleton, despite a similar peak strength. This dichotomy directly impacts seismic design implications. Although the calculated response modification coefficient (R) values are similar (~ 4.8), the underlying performance is fundamentally different. The robust skeleton of SW-1 justifies the reliable use of a higher R factor in ductile design. For SW-2, the comparable R value is misleading; its brittle failure necessitates a strength-based design approach with stringent drift limits, as elaborated in “Synthesis and performance-based design implications” section.

Displacement ductility and deformation capacity

Critical analysis of displacement ductility Both specimens exhibited ductility factors (μ ≈ 4.8–5.1) exceeding the common seismic design threshold (μ ≥ 3–4). However, this numerical similarity masks a fundamental performance distinction. The marginally higher μ value for SW-2 (5.05) is not an indicator of superior ductility, but rather a mathematical artifact of its significantly smaller yield displacement (Δy = 4.32 mm vs. 6.66 mm for SW-1). This reduced Δy is a direct consequence of the premature stiffness degradation and interface slip in the mortar bed, leading to an early departure from linear elastic behavior. In contrast, SW-1 achieved its ductility through a substantially larger ultimate displacement (Δu = 31.87 mm, 46% greater than SW-2’s 21.80 mm), which reflects a robust and reliable deformation capacity in absolute terms.

This reduced Δy is a direct consequence of the premature stiffness degradation and interface slip in the mortar bed, leading to an early departure from linear elastic behavior. In contrast, SW-1 achieved its ductility through a substantially larger ultimate displacement (Δu = 31.87 mm, 46% greater than SW-2’s 21.80 mm), which reflects a robust and reliable deformation capacity in absolute terms.

Evaluation of ultimate deformation and collapse safety margin The ultimate drift ratio provides a more direct measure of collapse prevention capacity. SW-1 attained an ultimate drift of 1/45.5, which is 44% larger than that of SW-2 (1/66.5). This quantitatively confirms SW-1’s enhanced ability to sustain severe deformation while maintaining vertical load-bearing capacity, as observed in its distributed damage pattern (“Failure modes and damage evolution” section). The integrated connection in SW-1 ensured gradual stiffness and strength degradation, granting the structure a wider safety margin against collapse under extreme earthquake demands. Conversely, the brittle, localized failure of SW-2 (“Failure modes and damage evolution” section) and its rapid post-peak deterioration (“Skeleton curve and characteristic performance parameters” section) resulted in a significantly reduced usable deformation range, despite a nominally adequate μ factor.

Distinct energy dissipation mechanisms and outcomes

Specimen SW-1 dissipated energy through distributed, material-based inelastic mechanisms, including widespread concrete cracking and sequential yielding of reinforcing bars. This stable mechanism resulted in an equivalent viscous damping ratio () that developed steadily and maintained high symmetry between push and pull directions (mean: 0.097 vs. 0.106). Consequently, its cumulative energy dissipation increased robustly throughout the loading history, reaching a high total of 20,400 kN·mm.

In contrast, Specimen SW-2 relied primarily on a concentrated, friction-based mechanism at the mortar interface. This led to an that was initially higher but exhibited significant fluctuation and pronounced asymmetry (mean: 0.114 in push vs. 0.149 in pull), reflecting the unstable, path-dependent nature of sliding friction. Its cumulative energy dissipation rose rapidly in early cycles but stagnated in later stages as the interface degraded, culminating in a substantially lower total of approximately 11,400 kN·mm.

Interpretation and implications

The nearly two-fold difference in cumulative energy dissipation (20,400 vs. 11,400 kN·mm) quantitatively underscores the superior hysteretic performance of the rigid rebar anchorage. The stable and symmetric damping of SW-1 aligns with its ductile, distributed damage pattern (“Failure modes and damage evolution” section) and full hysteresis loops (“Hysteretic behavior” section), confirming a reliable energy dissipation capacity. The higher but asymmetric and unstable damping of SW-2 is consistent with its brittle interface failure and pinched hysteresis, indicating an energy dissipation capacity that is both lower in total and less reliable. For seismic design, this stark contrast highlights that while interfacial slip can provide initial damping, a robust material-yielding mechanism is essential for ensuring high and dependable energy dissipation under large, reversed cyclic deformations, which is critical for structural survival in major earthquakes.

Strain in edge-confinement reinforcement

As shown in Fig. 15, the reinforcement strain in the edge columns exhibited an approximately symmetric tension–compression pattern under reversed loading, consistent with their flexural role. A key quantitative difference emerged in the yield initiation load. For Specimen SW-1, the tensile reinforcement began to yield at approximately 84.5 kN. This earlier yielding aligns with SW-2’s lower global yield load (Table 7) and indicates that its edge columns were compelled to carry a larger share of the shear force sooner, due to the premature loss of composite action at the mortar interface, which disrupted the intended force-sharing mechanism between the infill and the frame.

Strain in wall panel reinforcement

The strain development in the vertical wall reinforcement revealed a fundamental divergence in system behavior (Fig. 16).

In SW-1, the monolithic connection ensured coordinated strain development between the wall panel and the edge columns. Following initial low strain, the wall reinforcement strain increased steadily and progressively after cracking and edge column yielding. This pattern confirms effective shear transfer and composite action, engaging the entire wall section in a unified flexural mechanism to resist lateral forces.

In SW-2, the strain development was discontinuous and localized. After the early failure of the mortar interface, a significant redistribution of shear force occurred. This is evidenced in the strain data by a more abrupt and non-uniform increase in strain at specific locations, suggesting an altered and less efficient internal force path where the load is channeled primarily through discrete regions rather than being distributed across the full wall section.

Synthesis: Linking Micro-Strain to Macro-Performance: The reinforcement strain data provide direct micro-level evidence that corroborates the macroscopic performance differences. The rigid anchorage in SW-1 facilitated a well-integrated, progressive engagement of both the boundary frame and the wall infill, creating a redundant and ductile internal force flow that underlies its distributed failure mode and stable hysteresis. Conversely, the mortar interface in SW-2 caused premature strain localization and early yielding in edge columns, coupled with a disjointed strain distribution in the wall. This micro-mechanism is the direct cause of its brittle, interface-governed failure, altered internal force flow, and the associated rapid stiffness degradation and pinched hysteretic response. Thus, the connection detail decisively controls not only the global force–deformation response but also the very integrity of the internal load path within the structural system.

Discussion

Mechanistic interpretation: the connection as the governing factor in system behavior

The experimental results presented in “Failure modes and damage evolution” through “Reinforcing steel strain” sections provide conclusive evidence that the wall-foundation connection detail is the paramount factor governing the seismic performance hierarchy of the Precast Grid Composite Wall System (PGCWS). The observed, wide-ranging disparities—from failure mode and hysteretic behavior to stiffness degradation and energy dissipation—originate not from incremental differences but from fundamentally distinct force-transfer mechanisms, representing a qualitative divergence in system behavior.

This mechanistic dichotomy is rooted in the constraint condition imposed at the wall base. For Specimen SW-1 (Rebar Anchorage), the connection establishes a rigid, monolithic kinematic constraint. This enforces full composite action, compelling the wall panel, internal grid, and boundary columns to function as an integral unit—a synergy confirmed by the highly coordinated strain development observed in both edge and internal reinforcement (“Reinforcing steel strain” section). The grouted rebars provide positive mechanical interlock, facilitating efficient shear transfer through dowel action and effectively suppressing detrimental interface slip. This integrated mechanism is quantitatively validated by SW-1’s 60% higher mean cracking load (“Skeleton curve and characteristic performance parameters” section, Table 7) and its characteristic pattern of “high initial stiffness with slow, controlled degradation” (“Stiffness degradation” section, Fig. 12). Consequently, energy dissipation is achieved through distributed, ductile mechanisms, including widespread concrete cracking, sequential yielding of reinforcement across the section, and controlled bond-slip. This results in the observed stable, spindle-shaped hysteresis (“Hysteretic behavior”, Fig. 10), a backbone curve with a pronounced hardening phase and gradual post-peak strength decay (“Skeleton curve and characteristic performance parameters” section, Fig. 11), culminating in high cumulative energy dissipation (of ~ 20,400 kN·mm) (“Energy dissipation capacity” section, Fig. 14).

In stark contrast, Specimen SW-2 (Mortar Bed Joint) behaves as two stacked components linked by a brittle, frictional interface. Its performance is dictated by interface-controlled shear transfer, reliant solely on the limited cohesion and friction of the mortar bed. Exceeding this interfacial capacity triggers irreversible slip, abruptly terminating composite action—a phenomenon visually documented during testing (“Failure modes and damage evolution” section). This early interface failure is the root cause of its rapid and severe stiffness degradation within minimal lateral displacement (“Reinforcing steel strain” section). The consequent inability to reliably transfer moments alters the internal force flow, forcing a premature and concentrated shear response, as evidenced by the earlier yielding of edge column reinforcement (“Stiffness degradation” section). Energy dissipation is thus confined to the degrading interface, manifesting as severely pinched, inverse-S-shaped hysteresis loops (“Hysteretic behavior” section), a skeleton curve with negligible hardening and a steep post-peak descent (“Skeleton curve and characteristic performance parameters” section), and ultimately, 56% lower cumulative energy dissipation compared to SW-1 (“Energy dissipation capacity” section).

Synthesis and performance-based design implications

The profound mechanistic differences are systematically reflected across every key performance metric, as synthesized in Table 9. This clear and consistent dichotomy invalidates a one-size-fits-all design approach and logically leads to a performance-based, stratified design philosophy. This philosophy strategically aligns the inherent capabilities and limitations of each connection type with the distinct seismic demands and performance objectives of different building typologies.

Mortar bed joint for low-rise construction (≤ 6 stories): emphasis on construct ability with conservative drift control

For low-rise buildings in low-to-moderate seismic zones, where demands are often strength-governed, the principal justification for this connection is its exceptional construct ability and speed. The experimental data confirm its ability to develop approximately95% of the peak lateral strength of the anchored system (“Skeleton curve and characteristic performance parameters” section). Its application can therefore be viable where construction efficiency is paramount, but only under a strictly controlled design paradigm. This design must explicitly acknowledge its limited deformation capacity (Δu/H = 1/66.5)and unstable post-peak behavior by enforcing conservative inter-story drift limits (e.g., ≤ 1/100)and adopting a primarily strength-based design approach. While a response modification factor (R) of approximately 4.9 can be calculated from the test data (“Skeleton curve and characteristic performance parameters” section), its use must be accompanied by the recognition that it derives from a fragile mechanism, necessitating a larger safety margin in practice.

Rebar anchorage for mid-rise construction (6–15 stories): emphasis on ductility, integrity, and seismic resilience

For mid-rise structures, seismic performance objectives escalate to require substantial inelastic deformation capacity, stable and sizable energy dissipation, and robust system integrity under high drifts. The rebar anchorage connection is indispensable for this performance tier. Its demonstrated superior deformation capacity (up to a drift ratio of 1/45.5), coupled with high and stable energy dissipation, controlled stiffness degradation, and reliable moment transfer, collectively satisfy these heightened demands. This consistent, ductile performance profile strongly justifies the assignment of a response modification factor (R) of approximately 5.0, validating its incorporation into the ductile design of mid-rise PGCWS. Thus, this connection type is fundamental to realizing the system’s intended composite action and achieving the targeted seismic resilience objectives.

Future research directions

This investigation underscores that the seismic resilience of innovative structural systems like the PGCWS is critically dependent on micro-scale connection detailing. To advance this research and facilitate its safe, widespread implementation, future work should prioritize the following avenues:

Connection detailing optimization Exploring hybrid connection details—for instance, a mortar bed joint augmented with supplemental readily installable friction dampers or a limited number of post-tensioned bars—presents a promising path to enhance the hysteretic performance and deformation capacity of systems intended for low-to-mid-rise applications, without substantially compromising constructability.

Conclusion

Based on systematic experimental investigation and mechanistic analysis, the following principal conclusions are drawn:

The wall-to-foundation connection is identified as the predominant determinant of the seismic performance of the prefabricated grid composite wall system. The experimental results demonstrate not merely quantitative variations in performance indices, but a fundamental, qualitative stratification arising from two distinct load-transfer mechanisms: integral composite action (reinforced anchorage) and interfacial frictional slip (mortar bedding). The former imparts ductility, stability, and high energy dissipation capacity to the system, while the latter induces brittle response, rapid performance degradation, and limited deformation capability.

The reinforced anchorage connection comprehensively fulfills the system’s design intent. It effectively establishes rigid, monolithic kinematic constraints, ensuring fully composite action among the precast wall panels, internal grid, and peripheral restraining members. The resulting significant enhancement in cracking load (approximately 60% higher than the mortar bedding connection), coupled with gradual and controllable stiffness degradation, stable and full hysteretic loops, and superior ultimate drift capacity (Δu/H = 1/45.5), provides the essential foundation for realizing the intended advantages of the nested design. These include staged energy dissipation and controlled damage progression, rendering this connection suitable for mid- to high-rise buildings with stringent seismic performance requirements.

A performance-based, tiered application strategy is proposed, contingent upon construction demands and structural objectives. This approach advocates for a discriminating selection of connection type aligned with building height and target performance level, thereby rejecting a unified design methodology:

Mortar Bedding Connection: Its primary merit lies in superior construction expediency. It may be employed in low-rise structures located in regions of low seismicity. Design implementation, however, must explicitly account for its constrained deformation capacity and unstable post-peak behavior. This necessitates stringent inter-story drift limits (e.g., ≤ 1/100) and a design philosophy incorporating substantial strength reserve. The derived response modification factor (R ≈ 4.9) should be applied with appropriate caution.

Reinforced Anchorage Connection: This connection is indispensable for mid- to high-rise buildings targeting seismic resilience, reparability, and systemic integrity. Its demonstrated stable and predictable ductile performance spectrum robustly supports the adoption of a response modification factor of R ≈ 5.0 in design, establishing a reliable basis for performance-based seismic engineering.

Critical pathways for future research are outlined to ensure the long-term reliability and practical viability of this innovative system. Subsequent investigations should prioritize: (1) optimization of connection details, exploring hybrid solutions that harmonize structural performance with constructability; (2) durability assessment, evaluating the long-term influence of environmental actions on the mechanical properties of the connection interfaces; and (3) system-level dynamic validation, utilizing substructure or shaking table testing to scrutinize the system’s dynamic response, higher-mode effects, and overall seismic performance under realistic ground motions..
