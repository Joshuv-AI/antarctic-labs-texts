Dimensional accuracy and degree of conversion of a novel self-polymerized acrylic resin modified with chlorhexidine–halloysite nanotubes: An in vitro controlled study
Authors: Nadia Al Ansari, Suhad Sadeq, Mushriq Abid, Hassan Abed, Rayan Sharka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe integration of chlorhexidine-loaded halloysite nanotubes (CHX/HNTs) into acrylic-based orthodontic devices is a promising preventive measure to mitigate microbial proliferation under removable orthodontic appliances. The study was aimed at evaluating the physical properties of acrylic resin, particularly the degree of conversion (DC) and dimensional accuracy, after the integration of a novel CHX/HNT antimicrobial nanomaterial.MethodsFifty rectangular acrylic polymethyl methacrylate specimens were prepared with varying concentrations of CHX-HNTs. The degree of conversion was assessed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy. Dimensional accuracy was measured with digital caliper, which was used to assess the acrylic indentation dimensions before and after water immersion at multiple time points. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA, Tukey’s tests, and Bonferroni testing following repeated measures ANOVA.ResultsAll experimental groups showed a significantly lower DC than the control group (P ≤ 0.05). No statistically significant differences in the mean dimensions of the control and modified acrylic resins were found at all concentrations of integrated CHX-HNT nanofillers before immersion in water. After water immersion, the control samples, compared with the CHX/HNT nanocomposite samples, showed statistically significant dimensional expansional differences (P ≤ 0.05) directly proportional to the immersion duration.ConclusionsIncorporation of CHX/HNT nanofillers negatively affected the DC but enhanced the dimensional stability of PMMA acrylic resin for as long as 60 days.Keywords: Chlorhexidine digluconate, Degree of conversion, Dimensional stability, Halloysite nanotubes, Removable orthodontic appliances

Introduction

Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) resins are a commonly used material in orthodontics for the fabrication of appliances including removable orthodontic devices, functional appliances, and retainers.1,2 Although advantageous, the inherent porosity of the acrylic resin promotes the formation of microbial plaque on oral surfaces, and retaining components, particularly on acrylic base plates, is considered a major drawback.3 Cold-curing acrylics, because of their lower degree of polymerization than heat-curing acrylics, have greater porosity and consequently greater likelihood of bacterial adhesion, plaque colonization, and water absorption.4, 5, 6

Moreover, removable orthodontic appliances are used within a complex environment characterized by digestive enzymes, a varied microbiota, mechanical stress, and fluctuating pH and temperature. Consequently, this environment favors microorganism growth, supports biofilm colonization, and alters the oral microbiota by promoting the growth of oral pathogenic bacteria such as lactobacilli and Streptococcus mutans.1,3,7,8 Biofilms made up of bacteria and fungi (particularly Candida albicans) can grow around these devices and subsequently cause problems including candidiasis, periodontal disorders, and dental caries.9 To address this problem, enhancing PMMA appliances with antimicrobial materials and compounds has been suggested as a means of effectively decreasing microbial infection and biofilm development.10 The self-sterilization ability of modified acrylic resins has been extensively studied. Nanoparticles such as TiO2, silver, ZnO, and platinum have been incorporated into acrylic resins to make them self-sterilizing.11, 12, 13 However, the potential gradual release of metal ions into the oral environment might compromise the biocompatibility of these newly created resins.14,15

Nanotechnology has substantially advanced the fields of medicine, dentistry, and orthodontics. Halloysite, or Al2Si2O5(OH)4·2H2O, a hydrated layered aluminosilicate belonging to the kaolinite group, is found in nature as hollow cylinders made of many rolled layers with dimensions less than 1 μm. The remarkable physical and chemical features of natural halloysite nanotubes (HNTs) include a high cation-exchange capacity, high porosity, and very high specific surface area.16,17 Incorporation of 7 wt% HNTs into PMMA bone cement has achieved notable improvements in mechanical strength and a decreased polymerization temperature, without altering the setting time. In addition, HNTs can serve as a template that enables both the synthesis and stabilization of nanoparticles, producing various nanocomposites..18,19 A new class of materials called polymer nanocomposites, with nanofillers as reinforcement, have physical and thermo-mechanical properties that can outperform those of their conventional counterparts.20 Shi et al. have found that the augmented polymer matrices, compared with the bulk polymer, have more favorable stiffness, thermal and electrical barriers, dimensional stability, toughness, and fire retardant qualities.21 However, other research has indicated that the inclusion of nanoparticles in acrylic resins might negatively affect certain physical and mechanical properties, to a degree dependent on the concentration and type of nanoparticles used.11 The type of integrated nanoparticles, and their shape, size, level of filler dispersion, and concentration in the polymer matrix, determine the ultimate performance of polymer nanocomposites.22 Therefore, the type and amount of nanofiller must crucially be assessed to balance antibacterial benefits with preservation of the material's structural integrity.2

Chlorhexidine (CHX)-HNT nanocomposites, a newly synthesized biocompatible material produced by incorporating chlorhexidine gluconate within HNTs, have attracted substantial attention in recent studies.23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 These nanocomposites demonstrate broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity with prolonged and sustained release of CHX. This newly developed controlled release system delivers an antimicrobial ingredient for extended durations, thus providing long-lasting protection against microorganism growth. Consequently, this system may enhance patient oral health may be enhanced by limiting infectious diseases and improving treatment outcomes, while having limited effects on the mechanical and physical properties of the resin.28,29

The degree of conversion (DC) and dimensional variations are significant factors that can affect the mechanical and physical properties of acrylic materials. The acrylic dimensional changes caused by polymerization shrinkage significantly influence prosthesis retention and stability.30 Furthermore, high residual monomer concentrations can decrease hardness, strength, wear resistance, and color stability, and can cause tissue irritation or hypersensitivity reactions.31 Developing orthodontic appliances with inherent antimicrobial properties through the successful integration of CHX-HNTs into PMMA resin, while attempting to preserve the mechanical properties of the acrylic, particularly its DC and dimensional stability, may strengthen early oral health preventive and maintenance programs in orthodontic practice. The purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of CHX-HNT nanofiller integration on the DC, and the dimensional accuracy and physical characteristics, of self-curing acrylic resin. The null hypothesis was that incorporating various concentrations of CHX-HNTs would not influence the acrylic material's physical characteristics. The research question guiding this study was how the incorporation of various concentrations of CHX-HNTs into self-polymerized PMMA resin might affect its dimensional accuracy and DC.

Materials and Methods

The investigated material was autopolymerizing methyl-methacrylate resin (Orthocryl, Dentaurum), which was supplied in powder and liquid forms. The sample size was established according to prior in vitro research evaluating the physical properties of prosthetic materials.32, 33, 34 A sample size of ten specimens per group was chosen to provide 80 % power at a 0.05 significance level, ensuring adequate sensitivity to identify significant differences between groups while preserving comparability with prior in vitro research.32, 33, 34 The experimental groups incorporated four concentrations of CHX-HNT nanofiller material (1.0, 1.5, 3, or 4.5 wt%), whereas the control group included acrylic samples with no modifications (0 wt% CHX-HNTs).

The first phase of this research was synthesis of the nanofiller material by entrapping CHX digluconate solution (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) after diluting it inside the halloysite nanotubes (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) according to previously established protocols.25,35 HNTs (1.25 g) were loaded with 5 ml CHX solution, vortexed (20 s), then sonicated for 2 h to achieve dispersion. The cycle of vortexing, end-to-end mixing, and vacuum treatment at 35 °C under 25 mmHg for 1 h was performed twice. Subsequently, to remove any excess liquid, we centrifuged the mixture twice at 3500 rpm for 10 min, then performed vortexing and vacuum treatment. After 7 days of drying in an incubator at 37 °C, the loaded CHX-HNTs were stored in amber jars. All experimental materials and synthesized nanocomposites were characterized with FE-SEM, EDX, Raman spectroscopy, and XRD to confirm the halloysite tubular structure, lumen expansion-based CHX loading, and the hexagonal form of the HNTs.28,29

The DC was examined in accordance with previous investigations using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (TWO PerkinElmer, United Kingdom).31,36, 37, 38, 39 Three cured acrylic specimens from each group were ground into thin films for FTIR analysis with a potassium bromide (KBr) pellet, in absorbance mode. The dimensional changes in the modified nanocomposite resin were assessed according to previously established methods.42,43 A rectangular 3D-printed resin mold was first designed and printed with specialized software (Autodesk meshmixer Version 3.5.474) to fabricate the investigated acrylic samples. The dimensions were 20 mm × 12 mm × 3 mm. The pattern was designed to include four (+) signs at each corner by incorporating four grooves located 2 mm from the edges (Figure 1).

Preparation of the acrylic sample mold. (A) Design of the mold with Autodesk Meshmixer software. (B) Final mold produced through 3D printing.

The inside surfaces of the molds were coated with an appropriate amount of lubricating substance to facilitate the material's retrieval after polymerization. Subsequently, the acrylic resin was mixed according to the manufacturer's guidelines, by combining the liquid and powder in a precise ratio (2.5 g polymer to 1 ml monomer). In the experimental group, CHX-HNT nanofiller was weighed on a sensitive digital electronic balance (Precisa, model 125A, Switzerland) in proportions equivalent to 1 %, 1.5 %, 3 %, or 4.5 % of the weight of the polymer. The nanofiller was then vortexed to ensure uniform distribution before mixture with the liquid monomer. The acrylic resin was inserted into the prepared molds using the sprinkle technique. Curing was performed by one operator following the manufacturer's recommendations. All excess material was eliminated with a conventional tungsten carbide burr, and both surfaces of the samples were polished with sandpaper. The prepared rectangular specimens were maintained in distilled water in an incubator at 37 °C until measurement. The specimens were removed from the water solely for measurement and subsequently returned. Casting wax was used to fill in the (+) marks in the acrylic samples. The measurements were recorded at several time periods, including 7, 30, and 60 days, with indentations serving as reference points. The distances between the centers of the intersection of vertical and horizontal lines of specified marks in the samples were measured with digital calipers with an accuracy of 0.01 mm (Mitutoyo, Mfg. Co., Tokyo, Japan). The reference points in each acrylic specimen were labeled as A, B, C, and D (Figure 2). Six measurements, specifically distances AB, BC, CD, AD, AC, and BD, were recorded for each acrylic specimen. The algebraic norm of these values was defined and calculated as the square root of the sum of the squares of each dimension.44

Acrylic resin sample (20 × 12 × 3 mm) with four “+” reference points at the corners for consistent dimensional measurements.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed with SPSS version 25. Raw data were evaluated using descriptive statistics, including the mean, standard deviation (SD), standard error, and minimum and maximum values. The data were analyzed with one-way ANOVA and Tukey's tests to compare the control and test groups over various time intervals. Bonferroni testing was also used to limit the likelihood of type I error inflation due to multiple comparisons. The statistical analysis was conducted with a 95 % confidence level (α = 00.05).

Discussion

This exploratory in vitro study complements our prior research on the antimicrobial efficiency and mechanical qualities of CHX/HNT-modified acrylic resins. The inclusion of nanoparticles has been found to increase antimicrobial efficacy without negatively affecting essential mechanical properties.28,29 Building on this foundation, the current study focused on the DC and dimensional stability as additional characteristics of interest. The null hypothesis was rejected on the basis of the findings, because the integration of CHX/HNT nanofillers affected the physical characteristics of the acrylic resin.

FE-SEM characterization of the nanocomposite material used in the study confirmed an HNT tubular architecture (76.5 nm diameter, 460.5 nm length) and lumen expansion-based CHX loading with homogeneous CHX-HNT dispersion in PMMA. EDX analysis confirmed the CHX and HNT elemental compositions. The Raman spectra indicated hydrogen bonding between CHX and HNTs, whereas no new chemical bonds formed in the acrylic matrix. XRD patterns confirmed the HNTs' hexagonal shape and decreased crystallinity due to the presence of amorphous CHX.28,29 The studied concentrations of CHX-HNT (1.0, 1.5, 3, and 4.5 wt%) were determined on the basis of a research method that we developed previously.28,29 The percentages were carefully determined by analysis of previous studies on CHX40,45, 46, 47 and halloysite, both separately48, 49, 50 and mixed together.23,25,27,51,52 Consequently, we selected intermediate doses to balance minimal detrimental effects on the material's mechanical and physical properties with effective antimicrobial properties, with the aim of enhancing clinical relevance by ensuring the continued safety, durability, and fit of the modified acrylic resin.

Three acrylic specimens per group were fabricated and analyzed with FTIR to determine the DC. The analysis of triplicate samples was consistent with prior studies31,36, 37, 38, 39 in which FTIR was used as a highly reproducible and sensitive method for evaluating polymerization in acrylic and methacrylate-based dental materials39 to determine relative differences among groups. Each sample underwent individual processing, and several spectra were collected and averaged to mitigate instrument variability and ensure reliable results.

The incorporation of CHX/HNTs into PMMA decreased the DC in a manner directly proportional to the nanofiller concentration. A decrease as high as 6 % was achieved by integrating 4.5 % CHX/HNTs. These findings align with those from prior studies reporting that CHX amine groups decrease DC to an extent directly correlated with concentration, because of their ability to consume free radicals, which are essential for initiating and maintaining the polymerization reaction through protonation.53, 54, 55 The presence of fewer free radicals to drive the conversion of monomer to polymer partially inhibits the polymerization process and decreases the overall DC.54 The decrease in DC has been reported to lead to a less densely cross-linked polymer network that negatively affects other mechanical properties of the resin, such as by decreasing the hardness and flexural strength.28

Several investigations have shown that specimen size, shape, thickness, material type, and tooth presence affect denture base dimensions.42,56, 57, 58 The present study used rectangular acrylic specimens with standardized dimensions to eliminate these factors and enable a reliable comparison, focusing on acrylic resin material modification attributes. As demonstrated in previous studies, this approach avoids denture-shaped specimen confounding issues.44,59 However, quantifying dimensional changes in rectangular plates is difficult; therefore, we calculated the square root of the sum of squares of six distances (AB, BC, CD, AD, AC, and BD) between four reference locations. This method, as previously validated in similar research,33,44,59,60 enabled comprehensive assessment of dimensional changes by accounting for shrinkage and expansion in numerous planes rather than a single linear direction.

Shrinkage and expansion are dimensional changes that are frequently encountered in acrylic resin-based devices and affect appliance fit and the occlusal relationship. The first unavoidable dimensional change in any acrylic resin prosthesis is shrinkage, which occurs during processing and finishing. The second alteration, expansion, commonly occurs when dentures are either stored in a water bath or placed into the mouth and absorb oral fluids.61 Shrinkage in PMMA occurs through a combination of polymerization shrinkage, heat shrinkage, and the strain associated with stress release after deflasking.43,62

Integrating nanoparticles into resin polymers considerably influences their dimensional stability, according to the type, concentration, and dispersion of nanoparticles throughout the resin matrix.63 Increasing the nanofiller concentration can alter the proper dispersion of the nanofillers and consequently lead to stress concentration points or uneven characteristics that result in localized shrinkage or expansion.7,64 Our study revealed that, before water immersion, the CHX/HNT-modified acrylic samples experienced higher polymerization shrinkage, because their mean dimension was non-significantly less than that of the control acrylic samples. The greatest difference was observed among acrylic samples modified with 4.5 wt% CHX/HNT nanofiller. This increased polymerization shrinkage can be explained by the cationic nature of the CHX, which may interact with the initiators or accelerators in the resin system and possibly decrease the availability of free radicals necessary for initiating the polymerization process25,65,66 These results were confirmed by the FTIR results of the DC, which indicated that the modified acrylic samples had a lower DC than the control samples.

Despite the evidence indicating possible negative effects of CHX on dimensional stability, entrapment of CHX inside HNTs improved the dimensional accuracy of the acrylic resin. The experimental acrylic samples exhibited non-significant variations in mean dimensions for all tested nanofiller concentrations when immersed in water for the entire study period (Table 4, Table 5). In contrast, specimens in the control group showed a statistically significant increase in their dimensions after being submerged in water, thereby suggesting more expansion changes; this variation directly increased with immersion time and peaked after 60 days. This enhancement was attributed primarily to the nanoparticles’ ability to enhance the crosslinking density of the resin and consequently improve the dimensional stability. These results were consistent with those from a previous study demonstrating that the addition of nanogold particles to heat-polymerized PMMA resin increases the dimensional stability of denture base materials. Additionally, this might be associated with the ability of the nanofillers to improve the thermal stability, as demonstrated by certain nanoparticles, such as silica, and zirconium and its oxide.67,68

An essential statistical factor in research involving multiple comparisons is the potential inflation of type I error, which heightens the probability of identifying differences that do not exist. In this study, Bonferroni correction, a commonly used, simple, and conservative approach, was implemented to manage the familywise error rate and mitigate this risk, given the limited number of comparisons in this investigation.69 The findings indicated that nanotube-modified acrylic resins had improved dimensional stability; nevertheless, because of the limited sample size and in vitro approach, these findings must be interpreted with caution. Any potential clinical benefits are hypothetical, because oral environmental variables such as temperature changes, saliva content, and masticatory pressures might influence material performance in various ways. Consequently, further in vivo and long-term clinical research is required to validate our findings before they are applied in the therapeutic field. Additional studies are recommended to investigate the effects of CHX-HNT inclusion on other therapeutically significant parameters, such as color stability, resin setting time, and water sorption/solubility, to enhance understanding of the material's long-term performance and clinical efficacy.

Conclusions

This research highlights the ability to integrate CHX-loaded HNTs into PMMA acrylic resin to create an innovative antimicrobial nanocomposite with significant therapeutic application prospects. The integration of CHX/HNTs, particularly at 4.5 %, decreased the DC yet improved the dimensional stability of PMMA acrylic resin, thus alleviating the shrinkage and expansion problems typically seen in resin-based appliances. These findings underscore the potential of CHX/HNT nanofillers to enhance patients’ oral health, as well as the efficacy and longevity of orthodontic materials.
