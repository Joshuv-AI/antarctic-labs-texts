LFA-1 mechanical dose controls DNAM-1 signalling and perforin polarization in NK cells
Authors: Seong Ho Kim, Micah Yang, Isaac T. S. Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Natural killer (NK) cells are essential effectors of the innate immune system, playing a vital role in controlling viral infections and tumour progression by limiting their spread and reducing tissue damage. NK cell activity is tightly regulated by a balance of activating and inhibitory receptors on their surface, with extensive research focusing on how receptor expression levels affect NK cell function. However, the mechanical properties of lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1) are still not well understood. Specifically, how LFA-1 mediates mechanical forces in NK cells has not been fully elucidated. To investigate this, we quantified and modulated LFA-1 tension in NK cells using DNA-based tension gauge tethers (TGTs) functionalized with intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1). By using multiplexed TGTs that combine unzip and shear geometries while maintaining a constant molecular density, we enabled precise control over the mechanical dosage delivered via LFA-1-mediated adhesion by modulating the proportion of ligand-receptor bonds above a defined force threshold. This method allowed fine-tuning of LFA-1-mediated mechanotransduction and its downstream effects, including LFA-1/DNAM-1 colocalization and perforin polarization toward the cell–substrate interface. Our findings demonstrate that high-tension LFA-1/ICAM-1 interactions are associated with enhanced perforin polarization and receptor organization at the immune synapse, consistent with increased NK cell activation.

Introduction

Natural killer (NK) cells are vital effectors of the innate immune system, acting as the first line of defence against viral infections and tumour progression.1,2 Their ability to identify and eliminate abnormal cells without prior sensitization depends on a dynamic interaction between activating and inhibitory receptors expressed on their surface.3,4 This receptor balance controls NK cell activation, cytotoxicity, and immune regulation. A key event in NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity is the formation of a stable conjugate with target cells, a process that critically depends on the integrin lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1).5,6

LFA-1 is a heterodimer of αL (CD11a) and β2 (CD18) chains that binds to intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) on target cells.7 This interaction anchors the immunological synapse, facilitates signal transduction, and enables precise delivery of cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes.5,8 Beyond direct killing, NK cells orchestrate broader immune responses by secreting cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-10, TNF-α, and CCL3/4, thereby influencing the activity of dendritic cells, macrophages, and T cells.2,9 LFA-1 is a mechanosensitive integrin that plays a central role in immune cell activation by linking the cytoskeleton to ICAM-1 on target cells.10–12 This linkage is mediated by adaptor proteins, including talin, kindlin-3, RIAM, paxillin, and vinculin, which together form a dynamic mechanical axis essential for immune surveillance and cytotoxicity.

Biophysical techniques such as atomic force microscopy (AFM), optical tweezers, micropipette aspiration, traction force microscopy (TFM), and micropillar arrays have shown that LFA-1 not only stabilizes cell–cell interactions but also transduces mechanical forces that regulate downstream immune signalling.13–16 AFM, optical tweezers, and micropipette aspiration are well-suited for characterizing single-bond mechanics under controlled conditions, whereas TFM and micropillar arrays are valuable for assessing global, cell-scale force generation.17 However, these methods lack the spatial resolution or molecular specificity to map force transmission through individual receptor–ligand pairs, such as LFA-1/ICAM-1, under dynamic live-cell conditions.

Tension gauge tethers (TGTs) are powerful molecular tension probes for studying mechanotransduction at the molecular level because they can be tuned to different mechanical tolerances to support individual receptor–ligand adhesion.18 DNA-based tension probes have provided insights into force-dependent signalling in T cells by allowing accurate measurement of forces at the TCR (T-cell receptor) and LFA-1/ICAM-1 interfaces.11,12,19 Using TGTs with varying mechanical tolerance, studies have shown that cell spreading and force transmission through LFA-1 improve TCR signalling and antigen sensitivity in T cells, highlighting the essential role of mechanical signal strength in regulating immune responses.11,12 Previous studies with DNA-based tension probes also showed that LFA-1 on T cells transmits forces exceeding 4.7 pN, with localized peak force exceeding 19 pN.11,12,19 Multiplexed tension probes revealed that LFA-1 forces are concentrated at the lamella, while TCR forces localize to the focal zones.12 LFA-1 tension correlates with cytotoxic granule release in cytotoxic T lymphocytes, occurring specifically in areas of high LFA-1 tension rather than TCR tension.11 DNA tension probes measure receptor-specific force magnitudes in real time at molecular resolution, providing spatial and functional information that complements traditional traction force microscopy.20 However, these tools have not yet been systematically applied to NK cells to study immune synapse formation and the mechanical forces underlying target cell recognition and cytotoxic activity.

In this study, we directly measured and manipulated LFA-1-mediated forces in NK cells using ICAM-1-conjugated TGTs with specific mechanical tolerance. We employed a mix of high (shear) and low (unzip) mechanical tolerance TGTs while keeping ligand density constant to regulate the overall mechanical dosage of LFA-1 in NK cells. This approach allowed us to explore how variations in LFA-1 tension distribution affect downstream NK cell responses, including LFA-1/DNAM-1 interaction, DNAM-1 phosphorylation, and the polarization of perforin-containing lytic granules. Our results show that high-tension LFA-1/ICAM-1 interactions serve as mechanical switches that activate intracellular signalling and boost NK cell effector functions. This research highlights piconewton-scale mechanotransduction via LFA-1 as a crucial factor in NK cell activation. We establish that the mechanical dosage, dictated by the amount of high-tension LFA-1/ICAM-1, rather than the number of LFA-1/ICAM-1 bonds, controls the signalling pathways shaping NK cell responses. Using TGTs while maintaining constant ligand concentrations provides a flexible platform for investigating the interaction between LFA-1 forces and other receptors, thereby facilitating analysis of how activating and inhibitory signals are mechanically integrated in NK cell mechanotransduction.

Results

Multiplexed TGTs regulate the cell's mechanical dose and spreading

The TGT is constructed using an established method (Fig. 1a) where the top strand is conjugated with Protein G and BHQ2, and the bottom strand is conjugated with Cy3 and biotin.21,22 ICAM-1-Fc is attached to the top strand through Protein G to bind to LFA-1 on NK cells specifically. The biotinylated bottom strand anchors the TGT to neutravidin on a PEGylated surface to prevent nonspecific interactions. When the mechanical tension from LFA-1 exceeds the DNA duplex's rupture threshold, the strands dissociate, separating Cy3 from BHQ2 and producing a fluorescence signal. This design allows for the accumulation and force-specific detection of mechanical events.

Previous studies using DNA-based tension sensors have shown that LFA-1 experiences forces ranging from 4.7 to 19 pN during binding to ICAM-1 in T cells.11,12,19 To evaluate LFA-1-mediated tension in NK cells, we used two types of TGTs. The unzip TGT has a rupture force of 17–19 pN, while the shear TGT ranges from 50–56 pN (at a 0.1–10 pN s−1 loading rate) (Fig. S1). Their quenching efficiencies were approximately 95% and 96%, respectively (Fig. S2). NK cells generated fluorescent tension signals on the unzip TGT surface, indicating that the LFA-1/ICAM-1 interaction exceeded the 17–19 pN threshold (Movie 1 and Fig. 1a). These cells rapidly ruptured the unzipped TGTs and moved toward adjacent, unruptured regions. Conversely, on the shear TGT surface, NK cells showed full spreading but no detectable fluorescence signal (Movie 2 and Fig. 1a). This indicates that the applied force did not exceed the 50–56 pN threshold. The presence of fluorescence on the unzip TGT but not on the shear TGT confirms that the signal results from specific LFA-1-mediated tension, rather than nonspecific DNA rupture or nuclease activity. Additionally, the shear TGT preserves the native mechanical interaction between LFA-1 and ICAM-1 without rupturing, whereas the strength of the unzip TGT is comparable to or weaker than the LFA-1/ICAM-1 interaction, thereby reducing overall mechanical signalling through LFA-1. To confirm that both cell adhesion and TGT rupture require specific LFA-1/ICAM-1 engagement, cells were seeded onto unzip TGT surfaces lacking ICAM-1 but otherwise identical. No stable adhesion or fluorescence was observed, indicating that substrate attachment and force-induced TGT rupture depend on LFA-1/ICAM-1 binding and are not produced by nonspecific friction or nuclease activity (Fig. S3).

By combining the force-discriminating properties of unzip and shear TGTs, we developed a multiplexed TGT platform by mixing both tether types at different ratios while keeping a fixed total ligand concentration at 400 nM. This enabled us to examine how NK cell mechanical responses vary with ICAM-1 ligands at different mechanical doses. We compared three surface conditions: 0% shear (400 nM unzip), 50% shear (200 nM unzip + 200 nM shear), and 100% shear (400 nM shear). Quantifying LFA-1-mediated cell spreading showed that cells on unzip TGTs had a mean spread area of ∼241 µm2, which was significantly smaller than on 50% shear (325 ± 127 µm2) and 100% shear (447 ± 165 µm2) surfaces (Fig. 1b and c). These results suggest that higher rupture thresholds support more extensive cell spreading by enabling the formation of stable adhesion sites intact.18 We next examined the fluorescence signal from the unzipped construct on the multiplexed TGTs. Because LFA-1-generated forces stayed below the rupture threshold of the shear TGTs (Movie 2), the measured tension signal increased with the proportion of unzip TGTs (Fig. 1b and d). Notably, although cells on 0% shear TGTs were less spread out, the tension signal normalized to cell-bound area was higher than on 50% shear surfaces. This elevated signal is consistent with the greater availability of low-threshold unzip tethers, which are more easily ruptured by LFA-1 forces. Overall, low-threshold (unzip) tethers increase the frequency of rupture events and produce stronger tension signals per unit contact area, whereas high-threshold (shear) tethers resist rupture and support more sustained LFA-1 engagement, accompanied by greater overall cell spreading (Fig. 1c). Thus, cells on the 0% shear surface experience the least total mechanical dosage from LFA-1 tension, whereas on the 100% shear surface, the forces are at their maximum dosage.

Actomyosin contractility and actin polymerization are critical regulators of cell spreading and force generation.21–23 To assess the contribution of myosin II-mediated contractility to LFA-1 tension, we treated NK cells with blebbistatin, a selective myosin II inhibitor.26 Blebbistatin treatment decreased force generation by approximately 30% (Fig. 1e and f). Additionally, treatment with jasplakinolide, an actin filament-stabilizing compound, reduced tension signals, with the degree of inhibition depending on the concentration used (Fig. S4). Collectively, these findings suggest that both actin cytoskeletal dynamics and myosin-driven contractility are crucial for LFA-1-mediated force generation in NK cells.

The observed fluorescence signal mainly reflects the rupture of low-threshold unzip TGTs and may not be sufficient to support stable LFA-1 adhesion formation. In contrast, shear forces, which are not directly indicated by rupture-based TGT fluorescence, are likely responsible for stabilizing LFA-1 adhesions. Therefore, we expected that analyzing these readouts together would reveal how different modes of force transmission contribute differently to tensile loading and stable LFA-1 adhesion formation. To understand the mechanical basis of LFA-1-mediated adhesion and cell spreading, we examined the tension history of NK cells on surfaces with 50% shear functionalization (Movie 3 and Fig. 2a). When the cell membrane was not expanded, early tension was mainly observed at the central zone. The membrane then expanded and contracted, showing tension signals for 180 s. Notably, the spatial and temporal patterns of lamellipodial protrusion and tension generation were not synchronized in real time. To explore the detailed spatiotemporal relationship between cell spreading and tension dynamics, we generated kymographs from cell membrane contours and tension signal distributions (Fig. 2b). The tension history showed a gradual accumulation of signal over the observation period, occurring during cycles of cell spreading and contraction (Fig. 2c). To examine regional dynamics, three cellular zones were analyzed: the inner (central) zone, the outer (peripheral) zone, and the core (Fig. 2b). Tension initially decreased within the inner zone and subsequently increased at the outer zone, followed by the reappearance of tension at the core (Fig. 2d). To capture spontaneous tension generation during cell spreading, the frame-to-frame difference in the cumulative tension signal was quantified. This sequential pattern of tension localization was consistently observed in the accumulated tension maps (Fig. S5). These observations indicate that the inner zone functions as the primary site of early cell adhesion. In contrast, tension at the cell's outer zone was delayed because the lamellipodial extension initially involves membrane protrusion without efficient force transmission, as adhesions had not yet matured. Upon subsequent membrane contraction, strong tension developed at the outer and gradually propagated into the inner zone. This progression in consistent with dynamically coordinated transmission of mechanical forces between outer adhesions and the inner zone.23,24 Overall, the pattern suggests that cells first form strong central adhesions, then extend lamellipodia, and subsequently generate tension that spreads outward during cell expansion and inward during late contraction, ultimately filling the core zone.25

LFA-1 tension is linked to immune reaction signalling

We then expanded our investigation to a more biological question, exploring how mechanical tension modulates signalling pathways related to immune responses. In particular, we focused on DNAM-1, a receptor expressed on educated NK cells that plays a crucial role in NK cell immune signalling.27 Increased DNAM-1 expression enhances NK cell recognition and cytotoxicity toward target cells by promoting the formation of stable target cell conjugates.28,29 The coordinated regulation and colocalization of DNAM-1 and LFA-1 at the activating synapse are essential for the functional competence of mature.30,31 DNAM-1 is tightly linked to the actin cytoskeleton, and this linkage depends on LFA-1 expression.32,33 Engagement of LFA-1 with ICAM-1 triggers outside-in signalling that activates PKC, which then phosphorylates DNAM-1 at Ser329. This event is necessary for DNAM-1/LFA-1 association and stable colocalization at the activating synapse.30,34,35 This interdependence of the two receptors, along with their cytoskeletal coupling, led us to hypothesize that the LFA-1/DNAM-1 axis is sensitive not only to biochemical activation but also to the mechanical stability and tension of LFA-1/ICAM-1 adhesion.

To test this hypothesis, we measured DNAM-1 phosphorylation at Ser329 under various LFA-1 tension conditions after allowing cells to adapt to the mechanical environment for 30 minutes (Fig. 3a).30 Phosphorylation was assessed by immunofluorescence using a Ser329-specific primary antibody, followed by detection with NorthernLight 637-conjugated secondary antibody. Phosphorylation levels were lower on 0% shear TGTs compared to 50% and 100% shear TGTs, indicating that increased LFA-1 tension correlates with enhanced NK cell signalling (Fig. 3b). There was no significant difference in phosphorylation between 50% and 100% shear, suggesting that DNAM-1 signalling saturates once mechanical stimulation exceeds a certain threshold. Because S329 phosphorylation is essential for LFA-1/DNAM-1 association,30 we examined how mechanical forces influence their colocalization. NK cells were incubated for 30 minutes on surfaces engineered to impose varying levels of LFA-1-mediated tension. Co-immunofluorescence staining was performed using AlexaFluor 488-conjugated anti-LFA-1 and AlexaFluor 647-conjugated anti-DNAM-1 antibodies, followed by fluorescence intensity quantification. Pearson's correlation coefficients were approximately 0.2 for 0% shear TGTs and 0.4 for 50% shear TGTs,36 indicating that increased LFA-1 tension enhances the association between LFA-1 and DNAM-1. Furthermore, the surfaces with ≥50% shear TGT exhibited significantly higher LFA-1/DNAM-1 colocalization compared with 0% shear TGTs. This trend mirrors the phosphorylation data, supporting the conclusion that once LFA-1 tension exceeds a threshold (at least 50% shear), DNAM-1 phosphorylation saturates and its association with LFA-1 is maximized. Together, these findings indicate that LFA-1-mediated mechanical adhesion above a threshold level regulates DNAM-1 phosphorylation at S329 and promotes stable LFA-1/DNAM-1 interactions.

LFA-1 tension promotes the perforin polarization

Active LFA-1 engagement with ICAM-1 is critical for directing lytic granule polarization to the immunological synapse and ensuring effective target cell killing, whereas its absence results in nonpolarized degranulation and reduced cytotoxicity.11,37,38 DNAM-1 signalling further enhances NK cell cytotoxicity by promoting actin polymerization and granule polarization.39 Despite earlier reports, it remains unclear how mechanical tension on ICAM-1-bound LFA-1 influences immune responses. Moreover, although we observed increased association of DNAM-1 with LFA-1 under high-tension conditions (Fig. 3c and d), it is unknown whether this tension-dependent interaction regulates lytic granule polarization toward the contact interface remains unknown. Therefore, we investigated perforin polarization by quantifying the distance of perforin-containing granules from the cell–surface interface.

To investigate the impact of LFA-1 tension on perforin polarization, NK cells were seeded on surfaces with 0% and 50% shear TGT. The focus was on the 50% shear condition, as DNAM-1 phosphorylation and LFA-1/DNAM-1 colocalization were already saturated at this level (Fig. 3). Moreover, cells on 50% shear surfaces were pre-treated with blebbistatin to evaluate how reduced mechanical stimulation affects perforin polarization. After 30 minutes of seeding, cells were fixed and stained with anti-perforin antibodies. The perforin polarization index was calculated by measuring the axial distance from the cell–substrate interface to the perforin localization site. On 50% shear surfaces, this distance was reduced by ∼3.8 µm compared to 0% shear TGTs and by ∼0.7 µm compared to 50% shear TGTs treated with blebbistatin, indicating that elevated LFA-1 tension promotes perforin polarization towards the cell–target interface (Fig. 4a and b). The 3D distribution of perforin clearly demonstrated polarized localization (Fig. S6). Cells on 0% shear TGTs maintained a spherical morphology, whereas those on shear TGTs showed pronounced spreading (Fig. 1b). Because cell height directly affects the polarization index, perforin distances were normalized to cell height to account for morphological differences (Fig. 4c). Even after normalization, cells on 50% shear surfaces showed significantly higher perforin polarization (∼11%) towards the basal membrane, where the tension signals originate, compared to cells on 50% shear TGTs treated with blebbistatin. The relative polarization index of 50% shear TGTs with blebbistatin was comparable to that of 0% shear TGTs, indicating that LFA-1-mediated tension is a key determinant of perforin polarization. In NK cells, lytic granules are transported along microtubules toward the MTOC (microtubule-organizing center), which localizes to the immune synapse during target engagement.40,41 In our substrate-based configuration the synapse corresponds to the cell–substrate interface, so the basal redistribution of perforin we observe under high LFA-1 tension is consistent with granule trafficking toward a synapse-localized MTOC. We did not directly measure MTOC position, and whether LFA-1 tension influences MTOC polarization remains to be tested. Notably, blebbistatin treatment reduced cellular tension but maintained a higher number of LFA-1/ICAM-1 bonds, suggesting that the magnitude of LFA-1 tension rather than bond engagement determines perforin polarization (Fig. 4d).

Discussion

In this study, we present the first direct visualization and quantitative analysis of LFA-1-mediated tension generation in NK cells using TGTs. By combining unzip and shear TGTs, we elucidated the spatiotemporal evolution of LFA-1 tension during NK cell adhesion and spreading.

Mechanical dosage reflects the combined effects of the number of adhesion events and the tension of each event. Our findings show that changing the proportion of low- and high-threshold tethers influences the mechanical dosage experienced by individual LFA-1 molecules, which is connected to NK cell function. We demonstrate that LFA-1-mediated mechanical tension regulates DNAM-1 signalling in NK cells by affecting phosphorylation at Ser329, with increased tension leading to higher phosphorylation levels that plateau past a certain threshold. Elevated LFA-1 tension enhances DNAM-1/LFA-1 colocalization, reflecting increased spatial association and coordinated organization of these receptors at the immune synapse. This suggests that mechanically stable LFA-1/ICAM-1 adhesion couples force transmission to receptor organization at the activating synapse, which may in turn promote downstream signalling. These results establish LFA-1 as an active mechanosensor in NK cells that engages in a feed-forward signalling loop. Functionally, this feed-forward mechanotransduction pathway amplifies cytotoxic output rather than simply increasing receptor clustering. Elevated LFA-1 tension promotes DNAM-1 recruitment and phosphorylation, which in turn supports cytoskeleton remodelling and directs perforin-containing lytic granules toward the synapse, thereby enhancing killing efficiency. Disrupting force generation with blebbistatin abolishes granule polarization despite preserved LFA-1/ICAM-1 binding, demonstrating that reduced force magnitude under comparable adhesion engagement impairs this feed-forward reinforcement of signalling and effector polarization. Together, these findings identify a mechanically gated LFA-1/DNAM-1 circuit that converts graded tensile inputs into polarized cytotoxic responses.

A limitation of this study is the limited resolution with which we can define the mechanical threshold governing LFA-1-dependent activation. Intermediate TGTs that rupture within a narrower force range (e.g., 20–40 pN) and localize force application to specific positions within the ligand could address this.18 Given that the leukocyte force loading rate is approximately 4 pN s−1,42 such intermediate probes could test whether sharp mechanical transitions produce disproportionate effects on LFA-1/DNAM-1 colocalization and perforin polarization and in combination with biochemical assays, on downstream events such as DNAM-1 phosphorylation. Multiplexed TGT platforms would further enable mechanical dosage to be decoupled from the number of stable molecular adhesions, discriminating activation driven by force transmission from that driven by adhesion number. Such approaches may allow fine modulation of a mechanically gated LFA-1/DNAM-1 axis and, ultimately, of polarized cytotoxic responses, with potential implications for enhancing immunotherapy through tension modulators at the cell–cell interface.

Methods

Materials

DNA

DNA oligonucleotides were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies with HPLC purification. A common top strand was used: 5′-AzideN-TTTTT-CCGGGCGACGCAGGAGGG-3′-BHQ2. The geometry of the TGTs was defined by the complementary bottom strand. For the shear TGT, the bottom strand was 5′-Biotin-TEG-TTTTT-Cy3-CCCTCCTGCGTCGCCCGG-3′. For the unzip TGT, the bottom strand was 5′-Cy3-CCCTCCTGCGTCGCCCGG-TTTTT-Biotin-TEG-3′.

Cell culture

The NK-92 MI cells were cultured in αMEM containing 2 mM l-glutamine and 1.5 g L−1 sodium bicarbonate, supplemented with 12.5% fetal bovine serum, 12.5% horse serum, 0.2 mM inositol, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 0.02 mM folic acid at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Preparation of ICAM-1-TGT

According to our previous work, with slight modifications, Protein G (PG) was conjugated to the azide-modified DNA top strand using a bifunctional DBCO-maleimide (DBCO-MM) linker.43 PG was first reduced by incubation with TCEP at a PG : TCEP molar ratio of 1 : 15 for 30 minutes, and the reduced PG was purified using micro spin columns. PG was then reacted with DBCO-MM in HEPES buffer (pH 6.8, 1× HBSS) at a PG : DBCO-MM molar ratio of 1 : 10, followed by removal of excess DBCO-MM with micro spin columns. DNA-azide was incubated with DBCO-conjugated Protein G at a 1 : 1 molar ratio overnight at 4 °C. The conjugated top strand was hybridized with the bottom strand at a 1 : 1 molar ratio in TM5 buffer (50 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0) overnight at 4 °C.

TGTs-modified surface preparation

The imaging surface was prepared according to previously published protocols.20,44 Briefly, glass coverslips were cleaned with KOH and methanol. Air plasma treatment (0.4 mTorr for 70 s) was applied to the surface to make it hydrophilic. For amino-silanization, the coverslips were incubated in 94 mL of methanol, 5 mL of acetic acid, and 1 mL of aminosilane for 1 hour in the dark. The coverslips were rinsed three times with methanol and water. After drying with nitrogen gas, the coverslips were treated with mPEG-succinimidyl valerate (SVA) and biotin-PEGSVA (20 : 1 molar ratio) in sodium bicarbonate buffer and incubated overnight in a humidified chamber. The coverslips were rinsed with ultrapure water and dried under nitrogen. A 4-well insert was attached to each coverslip and mounted with a 3D-printed adapter for imaging. The surface was passivated with 1% BSA and washed with imaging buffer. For TGT immobilization, 100 µg mL−1 of neutravidin was incubated on the surface for 10 minutes and washed with HHM5 buffer (1× HBSS, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4). Then, 100 nM TGTs were incubated on the neutravidin-coated surface for 20 minutes and washed with HHM5 buffer.

Confocal imaging

Cells at 2 × 105 cells per mL density were seeded onto the TGTs-modified surface and allowed to spread for 30 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2. FluoroBrite DMEM was used for washing. Cell imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Olympus, FV3000) with a UPLXAPO 60× objective (NA 1.42). Tension imaging was conducted using 561 nm excitation, and time-lapse images were acquired at 3 seconds intervals to capture tension dynamics. Colocalization imaging of LFA-1 and DNAM-1 was performed using 488 nm and 647 nm excitation, respectively. DNAM-1 phosphorylation was imaged using 647 nm excitation. For perforin polarization imaging, 647 nm excitation was used, with z-stack images captured at 0.6 µm intervals along the z-axis.

Immunostaining

Cells were seeded onto TGT-modified surfaces for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 3.7% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After two washes with 1× PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% PBST (0.1% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS) for 10 min. Following three washes with 0.1% PBST, cells were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. For perforin staining, cells were incubated with perforin antibody in 1% BSA for 60 min. For DNAM-1 and LFA-1 staining, cells were incubated with both DNAM-1 and LFA-1 antibodies at a 1 : 40 dilution in 1% BSA for 60 min. For phospho-DNAM-1 staining, cells were incubated with primary antibody at a 1 : 100 dilution for 60 min. After washing three times with 0.1% PBST, cells were incubated with NorthernLight™ 637-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody at a 1 : 200 dilution for 40 min. The labelled cells were then washed three times with 0.1% PBST and then buffer-exchanged with FluoroBrite DMEM before imaging.

Force modelling of TGT rupture thresholds

TGT rupture thresholds were modelled via Monte Carlo simulation and an analytical model, as done in previous work.45,46 Briefly, for unzipping: the TGT sequence, temperature, and concentrations of Mg2+ and Na+ were used to model the zero-force energy landscape of the duplex, with the reaction coordinate being the end-to-end distance of the molecule along the unzipping pathway. The height and distance to the transition state were recovered from this energy landscape. The Bell Model was used to approximate force-dependent lifetime, using a prefactor fit to previous DNA hairpin unzipping experiments.47 For shearing, the same lifetime at zero force was used but the distance to the transition state was estimated loosely based on literature data.48 These values were then plugged into the Bell Model. A Monte Carlo simulation for three different loading rates (0.1, 1, 10 pN s−1) was performed using the force-dependent lifetimes determined by the analytical models, giving the rupture force distributions in Fig. S1.

Image analysis

Fluorescence images were analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ). Background fluorescence was subtracted before quantification. Cell spreading was assessed from cell areas measured in transmitted light images. Fluorescence signals, including tension probe readouts and DNAM-1 phosphorylation, were quantified at the cell–substrate interface by calculating either mean intensity (tension probe data) or integrated intensity (immunostaining data). The three-dimensional distribution of perforin was analyzed using a customized MATLAB script. For image presentation, brightness and contrast were adjusted uniformly across datasets.

Statistics

All experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SD or mean ± SE, as specified in the figure captions. Scatter plots show individual data points with the mean indicated. Statistical significance was assessed using Student's t-test.
