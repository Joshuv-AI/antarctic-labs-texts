Simulation and verification of Micro-LED multi-physics field coupling based on augmented reality head-up display
Authors: Qi Zhu, Chenjie Tang, Yan Zhang, Liang Chen, Gaoyu Dai, Luqiao Yin, Xiuzhen Lu, Jianhua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe blue-green light micro light-emitting diode (Micro-LED) based on Augmented Reality Heads-up Display (AR-HUD) requirements is designed using COMSOL software, and the photo-thermal properties of Micro-LED are coupled with multi-physical fields. By designing the addition of graphene film combined with heat sink for heat dissipation, the thermal management scheme of Micro-LED is further optimized, and the effect of graphene film thickness on the heat dissipation performance is investigated to improve the photoelectric conversion efficiency and reduce the effect of high junction temperature on the performance and reliability of the device. The main factors affecting the performance of a thin graphene emitter are the source power input and the source-emitter contact area ratio, where the thermal performance changes more obviously with increasing power input and decreasing area ratio. In addition, due to the thermal effect and the optical system, the role of solar radiation will depend on the solar energy density to calculate the reverse solar scattering effect on the Micro-LED module. Research has shown that when the sun’s rays are directed into the Micro-LED, its surface radiation will increase dramatically, which can provide the corresponding basis for solving the problem of sunlight backflow.Keywords: Micro-LED, Junction temperature, Graphene film, Solar radiation, MultiphysicsSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Augmented reality head-up display (AR-HUD) technology, as a key innovation, enhances vehicle-assisted driving, in-vehicle entertainment, and navigation systems through real-time interaction and seamless integration of virtual and real elements. Micro-LEDs driven by complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) circuits have emerged as a promising solution for the picture generation unit (PGU) in AR-HUD systems, owing to their self-emissive nature, high brightness, high pixel density, and high contrast. Compared with conventional PGUs such as liquid crystal displays (LCDs) and digital micromirror devices (DMDs), Micro-LEDs enable more compact AR-HUD systems with higher performance, particularly under strong sunlight1–5.

However, sunlight backflow poses a significant challenge for HUD PGUs, especially in AR-HUD applications. HUDs are typically classified into windshield HUDs (W-HUDs) and AR-HUDs based on the virtual image distance (VID), with AR-HUDs capable of achieving VIDs exceeding 7.5 m—nearly three times greater than those of W-HUDs. This extended VID requires free-form mirrors with smaller radius of curvature and larger apertures, which in turn increase the amount of sunlight that can be collected and reflected back to the PGU. As micro-LEDs are an innovative solution for AR-HUD PGUs, the thermal effects caused by sunlight backflow in AR-HUDs have not been fully investigated. Moreover, inadequate thermal management can adversely affect the optical, electrical, and mechanical performance of Micro-LEDs6–10. Graphene is a material with high electrical conductivity and excellent thermal dissipation properties, widely used to enhance the electrical and thermal performance of devices, such as facilitating charge transfer11 and improving the thermal characteristics of solar cells12. Several simulation studies have been conducted to evaluate the thermal performance of graphene, and multi-physics field simulations are also necessary when analyzing optoelectronic devices. Jie Bao13 constructed a three-dimensional model of the power LED packaging structure using finite element simulation software. It can be reached out that when graphene-based film approached the hot spot, its temperature decreased by 40℃ compared with LED without graphene-based film at 100W. Shivani Palakurthy14 verified through micro-LED simulation that graphene as a current expansion layer significantly improves the current uniformity and light output power of leds (17% higher than ITO), and pointed out its high thermal conductivity characteristic, which can be used as an alternative to ITO.

In this paper, we employ multi-physics optical-thermal coupling simulations using COMSOL(COMSOL AB. COMSOL Multiphysics® v6.2. https://cn.comsol.com) software to evaluate Micro-LED chips designed for AR-HUD image source applications. It examines the temperature distribution under various operating conditions, the resulting material stress, and the effectiveness of heat sink configurations in optimizing thermal management.

The Micro-LED model presented in this paper is designed based on the flip chip structure, with the epitaxial structure illustrated in Fig. 1. The epitaxial materials, arranged from bottom to top, are a u-type GaN layer, an n-type GaN layer 1, an n-type GaN layer 2, a multi-quantum well (MQW) light-emitting layer, a p-type GaN layer, and an indium tin oxide (ITO) layer, which serves as the current expansion layer. The MQW layer contains a GaN barrier layer and an InGaN quantum well layer. A common-negative n-electrode is situated between the n-type GaN layer 1 and the n-type GaN layer 2. A circular Au contact layer, acting as a p-electrode, is located above the p-type GaN layer15.

Electrical, thermal and stress multiphysics field simulation

In this paper, the fundamental coupling theory is to apply a forward voltage to the PN junction in the Micro-LED model in order to ascertain the potential and electric field distribution within the model. Concurrently, the Joule heat generated by electricity can be employed as a heat source to influence the thermal distribution of the internal environment of the model. This can then be combined with the environmental state of the model to determine the temperature distribution of the external environment of the model. Furthermore, the temperature distribution affects the potential and electric field distribution within the model. Therefore, the new temperature distribution must be substituted back into the electrical analysis to correct the potential and electric field within the model, thus obtaining the new Joule heat. The new Joule heat is then substituted back into the thermal analysis to obtain a new temperature distribution, and this cycle is repeated until convergence is achieved, resulting in the electrical and temperature limit values. Furthermore, the temperature will influence the stress distribution in the electro-thermal analysis. Once stability has been achieved, the temperature will be substituted into the stress analysis, which will be combined with the external environment of the model. This will enable the stress state of the model to be determined at this point. The stress state will then be substituted into the aforementioned cycle of electro-thermal analysis. Ultimately, this will result in the convergence of the electro-thermal coupling solution.

Graphene thin film thermal solution design and optimization

Due to the urgent need for heat dissipation technology, the study of thermal interface materials has become one of the current research focuses in the direction of optimising thermal management. For example, in the study by Jingkai Liu et al., the thermal conductivity of epoxy resin/boron nitride composites prepared by the hot pressing-induced orientation method is 3.85 W/(m·K)16, while having strong thermal stability and mechanical properties. In addition, the graphene film with a thermal conductivity as high as 5300 W/(m·K), which far exceeds that of conventional metallic materials (e.g. about 400 W/(m·K) for copper and 237 W/(m·K) for aluminium), has also attracted much attention due to its unique optical absorption properties, high transparency of the single layer and low resistivity18–20. However, it is worth noting that this study pays particular attention to the in-plane thermal conductivity of the heat sink material and the size of the applied image source. The in-plane thermal conductivity of graphene is up to 5300 W/(m·K), which is much higher than the thermal conductivity of boron nitride film. In addition, boron nitride film needs to be prepared by high-temperature deposition and other complex processes, and the thickness is large, compared with a single layer of graphene film thickness can even be controlled in the nanometer level, which can better meet the requirements of AR-HUD system on the small size of the Micro-LED.

This study will provide robust data and theoretical foundations for addressing the issue of sunlight inversion in AR-HUDs.

Optical-thermal coupling model analysis

As the primary source of heat in an in-vehicle AR-HUD is solar radiation, this source can be analysed in depth by utilising the ‘External Radiation Source’ feature of the COMSOL software. By combining latitude, longitude, time zone, year and date, the direction of incoming solar radiation can be accurately calculated over the simulation time. For the purposes of analysis, solar irradiance and temperature were measured at Beijing, China, which is located at 116.4°E, 39.9°N, and belongs to the time zone of East 8. In the absence of cloud cover, the solar radiation flux at ground level is estimated to be approximately 1000 W/m2. It was ensured during the modelling process that all ambient surfaces were included in the calculation of the solar radiation, and that the influence of shadowing effects on the distribution of the radiation was fully taken into account. The sun, with a temperature of up to approximately 5800 K, emits primarily short infrared and visible light with wavelengths of less than 2.5 μm. Furthermore, the degree of absorption of short-wavelength solar radiation by various materials is quantified and described by the parameter of solar absorptivity.

The simulation was conducted under three ambient temperature conditions to model the operating mode of the in-vehicle AR-HUD. The first condition is that of the ambient temperature. The period between 10:30 and 15:50 is the most intense period of solar radiation, with the instrument panel receiving direct solar irradiation22. Therefore, the highest ambient temperature of 28 ℃ is set at 14:00. In order to ensure that the in-vehicle AR-HUD functions correctly at any time of the day, the ambient temperature is set as an expression related to a sinusoidal function, with the temperature fluctuating at 20 ℃, where the half-day temperature difference is 8 ℃.

The second environmental factor is the temperature of the air surrounding the vehicle. During vehicle travel, natural and forced convection phenomena occur between the vehicle’s exposed surface and the surrounding air due to the wind. This convection effect has a significant impact on the operational state of the AR-HUD system. In order to simulate this convective effect, this paper sets a convective heat flux boundary condition, which assumes that the heat transfer coefficient of all exposed surfaces is 20 W/(m2·K). The third boundary condition is the angular coefficient of radiation to the environment. The angular coefficients of grey body radiation between all exposed surfaces were considered during the simulation of the on-board AR-HUD model, with the radiative heat transfer between these surfaces subsequently calculated. Nevertheless, these calculated angular coefficients are not treated as a unified entity. It is important to note that there exists a critical but often overlooked angular coefficient, namely the residual angular coefficient. This coefficient is of great importance in characterising the ambient properties of the surrounding area. In the simulation process, it is assumed that the ambient temperature is the same as the ambient air temperature in order to more accurately simulate the operation of the on-board AR-HUD in the actual usage environment.In order to facilitate the simplification of the work, some of the optical paths designed on the AR-HUD are represented by glass panels. Given that the optical component encompasses the radius of curvature of the optical elements, the conic parameters, and the evaluation function based on the equation of the free-form surfaces and the seed curve expansion method, the simplified optical path of the in-vehicle AR-HUD is represented by a glass panel. This facilitates subsequent analogue simulation and analysis with the COMSOL software.

Micro-LED experiment verification

The chip pixel centre spacing utilised in this experiment is 7.5 μm × 7.5 μm, with a chip size of 300 μm. The application instrument is the ACCµRA Plus, a fully automated mode designed for reflow and thermal compression processes with ± 0.5 µm accuracy. The ACCµRA Plus combines high precision, flexibility, and short cycle times, and is dedicated to the production of photovoltaic and silicon photonics applications. The system comprises three principal process modules: the chip preparation module, the chip transportation module and the chip bonding module. The chip preprocessing module is responsible for inverting the chip, the chip bonding module for aligning and soldering the inverted chip and the substrate, and the chip transfer module for acting as a bridge, facilitating the close integration of the work of the chip preprocessing module and the chip bonding module.

The bonding process is based on the principle of solder balls (Solder Ball), which are small bumps attached to the chip pad. Subsequently, the chip is positioned with its top surface facing downwards on the substrate. The assembled unit is then subjected to a process known as temperature reflow, during which the temperature is adjusted over time in order to melt the adhesive or solder ball. Subsequently, a new bonding parameter file is generated, with pressure, temperature and time being set according to parameters such as chip thickness. The microscope is then moved to align the driver substrate with the multiple pixel points of the Micro-LED chip. Finally, the chip (or bump) is secured to the substrate as it cools.

The quality of Micro-LED chips is evaluated through a series of tests, including optical and electrical parameters, alignment accuracy, bond strength, and interface voids. These tests are conducted at the wafer level and can be divided into two categories: contact-type inspection and non-contact-type inspection. Contact-type detection is a representative method for Micro-LED chips and involves contacting the two electrodes and injecting an electric current through miniature electrical probes to record the electrical and luminescence performance of the chip. The most common method for non-contact detection is photoluminescence detection. This technique is based on the principle of achieving luminescence through short wavelength excitation of the multi-quantum well layer of the Micro-LED chip. The quality of the chip is then assessed through the luminescence spectrum.This test employs the contact detection method, whereby the micro-probe is in contact with the electrode of the Micro-LED and external carriers are injected into the light-emitting layer of the Micro-LED by applying a DC voltage to the probe, thereby causing an electroluminescence phenomenon. Subsequently, photoelectric parameters such as the Micro-LED’s operating voltage, brightness, and light-emitting wavelengths are collected in order to assess the chip’s suitability. As illustrated in Fig. 15, the feasibility of the chip’s lighting and the absence of a significant short-circuit problem are verified by applying a DC current in the range of 20 mA to 200 mA to the chip using a probe at various locations. It can be observed that the probe point test has resulted in normal lighting and the absence of short-circuit conditions.

This experiment serves to further verify the thermal performance of Micro-LED chips covered with graphene film. Simulation modelling indicates that, in comparison with the traditional thermally conductive silicon film, graphene film can effectively reduce the heat generation of the chip, solve the heat accumulation problem on the chip, and optimise the thermal management scheme. In this experiment, the thermal imaging method was employed to indirectly assess the heat generation power density of the chip covered by graphene film by applying a current of 0.12 A to the graphene film. The heat generation performance of graphene was determined by measuring the temperature distribution on the surface through the change in temperature of the graphene surface as observed by the thermal imaging camera. Thermal imaging is an imaging technique based on infrared radiation that captures the heat emitted from the surface of an object (i.e. infrared radiation) and converts it into a visual image. Thermal imaging devices typically produce pseudo-colour images with colours indicating different temperature distributions, with common colour mappings being: red for high temperatures and blue for low temperatures. In this paper, the camera is set to a radiance of 0.95 and the reflectance temperature is set to 20 ℃.

Figure 16 illustrates the temperature of the chip coated with a graphene film, with the highest temperature recorded at 80.1 °C, the lowest at 73.3 °C and the average at 77.8 °C. Figure 17 depicts the temperature of the chip without a graphene film coating, with the highest temperature at 86 °C, the lowest at 72.6 °C and the average at 78.6 °C. The temperature of the chip covered with graphene film can be compared with that of the chip not covered with graphene film. The comparison of the two temperatures reveals that the steady distribution of the chip is more uniform, and the heat dissipation problem is more evidently optimised. Comparing Fig. 16 and Fig. 17, we can also see that the heat distribution in Fig. 16 is more uniform, with no obvious high temperature spots, while in Fig. 17, which is not covered with graphene film, unevenly distributed high temperature spots can be clearly observed. It can also be observed that the edge temperature transition of the chip shown in Fig. 16 is more evenly distributed, indicating that the graphene film can be very good at making the heat distribution on the surface of the device more uniform, thus eliminating local overheating.

A comparison of the simulation results with the theoretical calculations based on the formula (14) indicates that the power device coated with graphene film exhibits a 6.8% improvement in heat dissipation performance compared to the uncoated graphene film device at the highest temperature. However, at the lowest temperature, the difference between the two is not significant. The difference in heat dissipation performance between the two devices is not immediately apparent. However, the power device plated with graphene film exhibits a smaller temperature difference at both the highest and lowest temperatures than the uncoated graphene film device. This reduction in temperature difference between the two devices is 6.6 ℃. It can be demonstrated that the addition of a graphene film results in a more uniform temperature distribution within the power device, thereby reducing the thermal damage caused by localised high temperatures. Furthermore, the reduction in temperature difference allows for more efficient heat dissipation, which in turn reduces the temperature residual. The experimental results are in close alignment with the simulation outcomes, indicating that the graphene film plays a pivotal role in optimising the thermal management scheme of Micro-LEDs.

Conclusion

Based on AR-HUD, Micro-LED is selected as the image source, and its electrical, thermal and force properties are coupled with multi-physical field simulation and specific experimental verification through COMSOL. The results demonstrate that, in comparison to thermally conductive silicone grease, graphene film plays a role in optimising the heat dissipation of Micro-LED chips. The heat dissipation effect is influenced by the input power of the power device, the area ratio of the contact surface of the power device and the heat sink, and the power device’s operating frequency. It can be observed that the surface temperature of the Micro-LED and the input power are positively proportional to the input power. When the input power is 10 W, the area ratio is 0.64, and the thickness of the plated graphene film is 4 nm, the enhancement of the thermal performance of the heat sink is 4.6%. Furthermore, when the area ratio of the heat sink and the power device is less than 0.5, the contact surface area ratio on the surface temperature is significant.Furthermore, the utilisation of COMSOL to simulate the simplified light path system and the thermal effect of Micro-LEDs under solar radiation is also of considerable practical value. The introduction of heat transfer equations for thermal convection and thermal radiation enables the simulation of the heat transfer process inside and outside the vehicle exposed to solar radiation. This allows the surface radiance distribution of Micro-LEDs to be evaluated and provides a basis for improvement in the solution of the AR-HUD sunlight backflow problem.
