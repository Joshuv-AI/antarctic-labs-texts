A dataset of corona inception and complete breakdown for sub-atmospheric air under alternating and direct current supply
Authors: Jordi-Roger Riba, Milad Soltany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work presents an experimental dataset for the detection of corona and complete breakdown discharges in air under sinusoidal alternating current (AC) supply and direct current (DC) supplies at different values of the pressure-distance product. The dataset comprises 1764 experiments performed in a low-pressure chamber using two types of electrodes, namely a rod-plane and a sphere-plane. The experiments were conducted under 7 different pressure conditions (from the atmospheric pressure to 1% of the atmospheric pressure), with the electrodes placed at 14 different heights with respect to the flat ground plane (from 100 mm to 0.5 mm). All these tests were done under sinusoidal 50 Hz AC supply, positive DC (+DC) and negative DC supply (-DC). Each experiment consists of three repetitions under the same experimental conditions. The dataset includes the corona inception voltage or the complete breakdown voltage at each experimental condition. This dataset can be useful for modelling and validating accurate predictive discharge models under different pressures and for designing physics-informed insulation systems for more electric and all-electric aircraft.

Background & Summary

The evolution toward More Electric Aircraft (MEA) and All-Electric Aircraft (AEA) architectures is driven by a substantial increase in onboard power demand1. To maintain manageable current levels and minimize the size and weight of cabling and equipment, these systems must operate at significantly higher voltages than traditional pneumatic or hydraulic predecessors2. However, these higher voltage levels—combined with high-frequency switching transients and the requirement for high-density packaging—subject insulation systems to intensified electrical stress. Operating in an aeronautical environment introduces additional stressors, specifically reduced atmospheric pressure, which alters the dielectric properties of the insulation medium. Atmospheric air serves as the primary dielectric medium in most power systems; consequently, its breakdown characteristics have been extensively documented3. While air maintains high dielectric strength under standard temperature and pressure (STP), the application of high-intensity electric fields can trigger ionization, particularly when environmental factors deviate from nominal conditions. As altitude increases, the dielectric strength of air decreases4 because the reduced pressure increases the mean free path and mobility of electrons and ions5. This environment facilitates ionization, leading to two distinct failure mechanisms, i.e., corona discharges and complete breakdown discharges, also known as electric arcs. While often involving low-energy activity, corona discharges cause cumulative chemical and thermal damage on the surface of the insulation, causing premature degradation6. Unlike corona discharges, complete breakdown discharges in air, are high-power discharge events that can result in immediate and catastrophic system failure7.

Historically, uniform field gaps have served as the benchmark for characterizing complete dielectric breakdown. They provide a standardized framework for predicting discharge behaviour and establishing design margins for electrical insulation, parameters that are fundamental to the operational safety and reliability of power systems. A strictly uniform electric field is typically achieved using parallel-plate electrodes. However, other configurations such as sphere-sphere electrode configurations are also utilized to generate uniform or quasi-uniform fields, provided the inter-electrode gap distance d remains significantly smaller than the sphere diameter3. In a uniform air gap, the transition from insulation to conduction is characteristically abrupt. As the applied voltage increases, the medium undergoes complete dielectric breakdown without any previous stable corona activity8. This behaviour makes uniform gaps relevant for empirical studies and the validation of complete breakdown voltage models.

In a non-uniform air gap, the transition to conduction is typically preceded by partial or localized breakdown, commonly referred to as corona discharge9. Unlike the abrupt transition observed in uniform gaps, corona manifests as a localized ionization zone around the high-stress electrode that does not initially bridge the inter-electrode volume5. Corona occurs when the local electric field intensity exceeds the ionization threshold of the medium within a confined region, yet the field strength across the remaining gap is insufficient to sustain a complete breakdown. This localized corona activity typically initiates at voltages significantly lower than the ultimate complete breakdown voltage Vb. The onset of corona activity, as measured by the corona inception voltage (CIV), depends on the dielectric properties of the insulating medium and the temporal dynamics of the applied electric field, such as frequency and waveshape10.

When dealing with inhomogeneous geometries and relatively high pressure-distance (p·d) values, the CIV is governed by the streamer criterion rather than Paschen’s law. This criterion is defined by the integration of the effective ionization coefficient, α¯ along the field line x where the condition ∫α¯dx=K is met, with Kcorona ≈ 9.2 for non-uniform fields in air11. This approach accounts for the localized field enhancement at the electrode tip. However, at low p·d values close to the Paschen's minimum, the Townsend breakdown criterion is the relevant physics model.

Due to the multiphyics and nonlinear nature of discharge inception, analytical formulas often fail to provide sufficient predictive accuracy, even for simple geometries12. Although sophisticated numerical frameworks have been developed to simulate insulation performance, they cannot yet fully account for all environmental and material variables. Physical experimentation, therefore, remains the primary means for characterizing dielectric behaviour, with simulations serving as a supplementary analytical tool.

While uniform fields provide a baseline for dielectric characterization, practical high-voltage components such as conductors, bushings, contacts, and windings possess complex geometries that inherently generate non-uniform electric fields. In these industrial applications, achieving field uniformity is technically impractical. Consequently, practical applications are dominated by non-uniform fields that deviate from Paschen’s law and generally do not operate near the Paschen minimum. This divergence is primarily because partial breakdown, manifested as corona activity, frequently occurs at voltage levels significantly below those predicted by the classical Paschen’s law13. Therefore, quantifying the influence of field divergence on dielectric breakdown is essential for the design of safe and reliable aerospace power systems.

In accordance with classical Paschen’s law for uniform electric fields, the complete dielectric breakdown of air is characterized by a unique dependency, expressed as Ub = Ub(p·d), where Ub represents the complete breakdown voltage and p·d is the product of pressure and gap distance. Conversely, in non-uniform field configurations, this relationship becomes increasingly complex. Experimental evidence suggests that the original shape of the Paschen curve is lost, as distinct bifurcations emerge at varying pressure levels. Consequently, the breakdown characteristics in such geometries must be redefined as a bivariate function, Ub = Ub(p·d,p) to account for the independent influence of gas pressure13.

Historically, Paschen’s law has provided the theoretical basis for predicting complete gas breakdown voltages in high-voltage insulation systems. However, it is well known that Paschen’s law cannot be applied in the presence of highly non-uniform electric fields because classical Paschen’s law assumes field homogeneity. Consequently, experimental results can differ significantly from those predicted by the conventional Paschen model13.

The shift toward More Electric Aircraft (MEA) and All-Electric Aircraft (AEA) architectures requires a transition in high-voltage insulation design, moving away from semi-empirical rules based on uniform field conditions to data-driven frameworks. Therefore, there is an urgent requirement for comprehensive experimental databases that characterize corona discharge behaviour under non-uniform electric fields and low-pressure environments. While classical Townsend and Paschen theories offer a preliminary starting point, they are insufficient for aerospace applications where localized field enhancements—inherent in compact power electronics, high-density connectors, and motor windings—trigger discharge mechanisms that deviate significantly from conventional laws. Developing an extensive empirical database is a critical prerequisite for the validation of numerical simulations and digital twin models. Without robust experimental datasets, engineers are forced to adopt excessive safety margins to mitigate the risk of insulation failure. Furthermore, experimental dataset can also serve as the basis for establishing standardized certification protocols for high-voltage aerospace components, which require a clear definition of the safe operating envelope. Finally, this experimental database can facilitate the design of physics-informed gas insulation systems. This enables safe operation while optimizing insulation size and weight. Notably, physics-informed corona modelling couple corona, streamer and leader physics using self-consistent fluid and particle-in-cell plasma solvers considering photo-ionization, attachment/detachment and space-charge transport14. Corona discharge modelling can now resolve glow-to-streamer transition and field shielding by corona space charge under different applied voltages15.

The experimental dataset was obtained using two electrode configurations: rod-plane and sphere-plane geometries. These geometries were selected due to their widespread adoption in high-voltage engineering for characterizing non-uniform fields, as well as their high reproducibility and suitability for numerical simulation. To account for the influence of voltage polarity and waveform, the study includes measurements under 50 Hz sinusoidal AC, as well as both positive and negative DC supplies. The experimental dataset comprises 1,764 tests conducted within a low-pressure chamber across seven discrete pressure levels, ranging from atmospheric pressure (0.9780 atm) to 1% of atmospheric pressure (approximately 0.0096 atm). The electrode gap distance was varied across 14 heights, from 0.5 mm to 100 mm relative to the grounded plane. Each configuration was performed in triplicate to ensure statistical significance and to assess experimental repeatability. The resulting data includes both corona inception voltage (CIV) and complete breakdown voltage (Ub) and the pressure by distance product (p·d) for each experiment. This comprehensive characterization provides a robust basis for modelling discharge phenomena in sub-atmospheric environments and informs the design of insulation systems for ‘More Electric’ and ‘All-Electric’ aircraft architectures.

Methods

This section details the experimental setup, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Experimental setup. The setup consists of a low-pressure chamber in which the rod-plane and sphere-plane electrodes are placed. It also includes a vacuum pump, a manometer, a high-voltage transformer, a programmable power supply, a voltage probe connected to a multimeter, an oscilloscope and a CMOS imaging sensor to detect discharge pulses, a precision resistor to measure discharge current, and a spark gap to protect the oscilloscope.

Experiments were conducted in a low-pressure, cylindrical stainless steel chamber (300 mm height, 295 mm diameter) featuring a mirror-like finish. The vessel is sealed with a hermetic methacrylate lid equipped with feedthroughs for supply conductors. Pressure levels were monitored using a calibrated RS 1111 digital absolute pressure gauge (0–1700 mbar, accuracy of ±2 mbar, RS Pro, Germany) and regulated between 0.9780 atm and 0.0096 atm using an RS3D vacuum pump (Wigam, Italy; 0.046 m³/min, 0.18 HP).

High voltage was supplied to the electrodes via a programmable power source (APM Technologies SP300VAC600W; 600 W, 0–300 V, 15–1000 Hz) connected to a single-phase high-voltage transformer (Laboratorio Electrotécnico VKPE-36; 600 VA, 0–36 kV, 1:100 turns ratio). For DC testing, the output was processed through a high-voltage rectifier consisting of two series-connected diodes (2CL2FP; 30 kV, 100 mA, 100 ns) and a polypropylene film capacitor filter (40 nF, 20 kV). The electrode voltage was monitored using a Cal Test Electronics CT4028 high-voltage probe (1000:1, 0–39 kV, accuracy better than 3%) interfaced with a Fluke 289 true root-mean-square (RMS) multimeter provided with factory calibration traceable to NIST standards, with specified accuracies of 0.025% for DC voltage and 0.4% for AC voltage measurements. The accuracy expresses how close the recorded value is to the true physical voltage.

Current pulses from the electrodes were measured via the voltage drop across a grounded 5 kΩ non-inductive shunt resistor (±0.01%, Ohmite). Waveforms were captured on a calibrated RTH1004 Rohde & Schwarz oscilloscope featuring a 5 GS/s sampling rate and 10-bit ADC vertical resolution. A 75 V gas discharge tube (EPCOS/TDK) was connected in parallel with the shunt resistor as a protective overvoltage limiter for the oscilloscope input. Pulseless corona discharges under positive DC supply are undetectable via oscilloscope at certain pressures and distances. To capture these events, a complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) imaging sensor (200 MP, 1/1.3´´ sensor size, Samsung ISOCELL HP2) with WiFi communication was used in tandem with traditional electrical sensing. Comparison at the corona inception voltage (CIV) revealed a marginal 0.2% discrepancy between the two methods, defined as the relative difference between the oscilloscope and imaging sensor readings.

The air gap distance between the rod tip and the horizontal ground plane was precisely controlled using a Starrett 124MBZ micrometer (0.003 mm accuracy). Measurements were conducted at discrete intervals ranging from 100 mm down to 0.5 mm (specifically 100, 90, 80, 70, 60, 50, 40, 30, 10, 5, 3, 2, 1, and 0.5 mm). The gap distances were selected to provide sufficient resolution across the full p·d range. Finer steps were applied at small distances, where the discharge behaviour changes more rapidly. Broader steps were applied at larger distances, where variation is more gradual.

Ambient conditions, including pressure, temperature, and humidity, were monitored with a Comet System D4130 portable thermo-hygro-barometer (±0.4 °C, ±2.5% RH, ±2 hPa).

Throughout the testing process, environmental conditions were controlled, with relative humidity maintained at 47% ± 5% RH and temperature at 21 ± 2 °C. These values represent the laboratory condition, and were recorded outside the low-pressure chamber. At reduced pressure levels, the water vapour partial pressure drops proportionally with total pressure, resulting in a lower internal relative humidity compared to ambient conditions. However, the gas will interact with the humidity adsorbed on the chamber surfaces, so the humidity level is not expected to fall exactly proportional with the pressure. To ensure consistency, all experiments utilized electrodes of identical material and geometry. Electrical discharges were characterized using both vertical rod-plane and sphere-plane configurations. Both high-voltage electrodes were fabricated from polished stainless steel with a mirror-like finish. The rod featured a 1.27 mm outer diameter with a sharp 90° edge, while the sphere measured 3 mm in diameter.

To prevent any damage to the test object by limiting the breakdown current, a 12 kΩ protective resistor was placed in series between the source output and the test object (for DC, this was placed before the rectifier) which can be seen in Fig. 1.

In order to observe the effect of small changes in distance and pressure on corona inception and complete breakdown voltages for both geometries, the experiments followed a descending pressure sequence, starting at 100% atmospheric pressure and decreasing to 50%, 30%, 10%, 5%, 2%, and 1%. For each pressure level, the gap distance was changed from 100 mm to 0.5 mm. Once all the distances were evaluated, the experiment moved to a lower pressure level. After each pressure change, the system was allowed to stabilize for 5 minutes to confirm a stable reading on the pressure gauge. Once the tests at each distance were completed, the air in the chamber was replaced by blowing fresh air in with a portable fan. For each pressure level, the test object was carefully cleaned with a lint-free microfibre wipe and isopropyl alcohol to eliminate the effect of dust, moisture and contamination on the CIV. The test objects were not repolished to avoid changing the tip geometry and surface specifications. During corona-only tests, no change was visually observable at the tips of the rod and sphere.

To ensure experimental reproducibility, the test sample was replaced after the breakdown tests for each gap distance were completed. Consequently, the breakdown voltage for each specific distance was acquired using a fresh, unused sample. For each distance, the waiting time of 20 seconds was applied between the three breakdown voltage measurements in order to allow residual charge dissipation.

Given that the pressure gauge has an accuracy of ±2 mbar, the uncertainty at the lowest tested pressure (~9.7 mbar, or 1% atmospheric pressure) is approximately 20%. This is acknowledged as a limitation of the dataset at this minimum pressure level.

Figure 2 shows the electrodes and the experimental assembly. The arithmetic mean surface roughness (Ra) of both test objects was measured to be less than 0.1 µm using a laser scanning microscope.

Electrodes and experimental assembly. (a) Detail of the rod and sphere electrodes. (b) Electrode holder assembly, including the micrometer, the rod and sphere electrodes and the ground plane.

Each experimental condition was defined by the chamber’s internal pressure (p) and the vertical gap distance between the electrode (rod or sphere) and the horizontal ground plane (d). Discharge events were identified by increasing the voltage in steps of 50 V at a rate of ∼50 V/s to minimize the effect of the inception delay. Between each step, a waiting time of 5 seconds was applied. Once the discharge current pulses were detected via the oscilloscope, which monitored the voltage drop across the shunt resistor, the voltage was decreased until no corona activity was observed, then increased again in smaller steps of 5 V to precisely determine the CIV. This procedure was repeated for all distances, pressures as well as for all three repetitions performed for each case under AC and negative DC where corona was pulsed.

Figure 3(a) shows an example of discharge pulses acquired by the oscilloscope between the terminals of the 5 kΩ shunt resistor.

(a) Oscilloscope waveform capturing a representative discharge pulse and magnified view showing the detailed temporal structure of the pulse (sphere-plane electrode, AC supply, p = 0.4870 atm, d = 10 mm). (b) Pulseless corona discharge photograph (sphere-plane electrode, positive DC supply, p = 0.4870 atm, d = 10 mm).

While corona discharges are often pulsed in nature, pulseless glow corona can occur under DC supply. This regime is characterized by a continuous, steady-state current that does not produce the discrete, nanosecond-rise-time pulses. Consequently, a high-bandwidth, high-sampling-rate oscilloscope was used to monitor for the presence (or absence) of pulses, and a high-sensitivity CMOS imaging sensor to visually verify discharge inception and spatial distribution. Since the imaging sensor can detect luminosity even in the pulseless glow mode, these combined methods provide a more reliable verification of inception for both pulsed and pulseless regimes than standard pulse-coupling techniques. As with pulsed corona mode, the voltage was increased slowly in steps of 50 V in pulseless mode. At each step, a long-exposure photo was taken to check the discharge activity at the tip of the test object. The CIV was defined as the voltage at which the first visible luminous region appeared on the CMOS imaging sensor. Figure 3(b) illustrates a pulseless corona discharge captured from the sphere-plane electrode under positive DC supply.

To avoid false positive detections, different measures were taken. The chamber was completely dark during all optical measurements, the lid and the entire inner surface of the chamber were covered with non-reflective black adhesive tape to eliminate stray light and internal reflections. A fixed imaging sensor exposure time of 30 seconds and ISO of 320 were maintained across all experimental conditions. The exposure time of 30 seconds was necessary to integrate the faint photon emission associated with corona inception, which is particularly weak at inception point for small geometries. It should also be noted that a higher ISO results in greater sensitivity, but also increases the amount of noise (stray light) in the image. These values were chosen after testing all possible settings to achieve the best balance. In all pulsed corona conditions, the oscilloscope and the imaging sensor operated simultaneously, and the results were cross-checked to confirm agreement between electrical and optical detection.

Data Record

The dataset is hosted on CORA.RDR16, a federated, multidisciplinary data repository serving Catalan universities, CERCA research centres, and affiliated institutions. A comprehensive readme file accompanies the dataset, providing detailed information regarding the file structure and specific experimental conditions. The dataset is available at CORA.RDR repository17.

In addition to the readme file, the main folder includes six CSV files containing the corona inception voltage and complete breakdown voltage measurements. The data are organized by electrode configuration (rod-plane and sphere-plane), with three files dedicated to each electrode to independently record the AC, positive DC, and negative DC supply measurements. The main folder also includes two extra CSV files containing 20 repetitions of the CIV of both electrodes (rod-plane and sphere-plane) under three analysed voltage types (AC, positive DC and negative DC), which can be useful to determine the statistical repeatability and dispersion of the results.

The six CSV file names systematically encode the experimental conditions using the following format: [ElectrodeType]_[Medium]_[VoltageType].csv. Specifically, the first term indicates the electrode configuration (Sphere or Rod), followed by the insulation medium (Air), and the applied voltage type (AC, NegativeDC, or PositiveDC). For example, the file named Rod_Air_PositiveDC.csv contains data for a rod electrode tested in air under a positive DC supply. This standardized naming convention ensures that key parameters are concisely represented, allowing users to easily identify and retrieve specific subsets of data.

The two additional CSV files, Rod_Air_DataDispersion.csv and Sphere_Air_DataDispersion.csv, provide the experimental CIV data obtained from 20 repeated measurements. The files also report the mean CIV (μ), the standard deviation (σ), and the coefficient of variation (σ/μ). In all cases, σ/μ < 0.4%, falling in the very high precision range. It is important to note that coefficient of variation represents the repeatability of the measurements.

All AC voltage values reported in the dataset are peak values (kV-peak).

Description of the samples

The dataset contains six files representing six experimental conditions. They correspond to two test objects (sphere and rod) and three types of applied voltage: AC, negative DC, and positive DC. Each CSV file contains data for seven pressure levels (100%, 50%, 30%, 10%, 5%, 2%, and 1% atm). For each pressure level, tests were performed at 14 separate distances (100 mm, 90 mm, 80 mm, 70 mm, 60 mm, 50 mm, 40 mm, 30 mm, 10 mm, 5 mm, 3 mm, 2 mm, 1 mm, and 0.5 mm), and for each distance, three repetitions were taken for averaging purposes, resulting in 98 rows per file, with three repetitions recorded as separate columns, giving 294 individual measurements per file.

Technical Validation

To ensure the reliability of the discharge behaviour analyses, an exhaustive technical validation was performed to mitigate the complexities of non-uniform field stochasticity. This process included visual inspection of discharge morphologies to confirm regime transitions, spot testing to verify the repeatability of inception voltages across variable pressures, and statistical analysis to account for the inherent variance of discharge events. These steps were fundamental to ensuring that the dataset accurately reflects stable physical phenomena.

To ensure data integrity, all acquired data underwent visual verification to eliminate anomalies and prevent gaps in the experimental records. This systematic review guaranteed a discrepancy-free dataset, providing a robust foundation for subsequent statistical and physical modelling.

Statistical validation was performed using MATLAB® routines to ensure the repeatability of measurements and the exclusion of outliers. The measured corona inception and complete breakdown voltages at each pressure-distance (p.d) level were compared against established literature values3. A comparative analysis of AC and DC (positive/negative) results was conducted to ensure physical consistency. Deviations from established trends were used to identify and rectify potential systematic errors or setup inconsistencies.

Finally, spot tests were conducted on a representative subset comprising approximately 10% of the experimental measurements, encompassing a diverse range of pressures and electrode spacings. Analysis of this subset confirmed that reductions in both pressure and gap distance resulted in a corresponding decrease in corona and complete breakdown inception voltages, consistent with the behaviour observed leading down to the Paschen minimum. The results demonstrate that the applicability of Paschen’s law reduces at higher p·d products, where field non-uniformity causes the CIV to deviate substantially from reported uniform-field breakdown thresholds (see Fig. 4). These observed trends align with theoretical expectations and the Paschen curve, thereby validating the experimental design and the accuracy of the data collection methodology.

Figure 4 summarizes the data obtained in this work for rod-plane and sphere-plane air gaps, compared against the complete breakdown data reported by Dakin et al.3 compiled from various reliable sources. The close agreement between our data and those of Dakin et al.3 at lower p·d values validates the results presented in this paper.

Figure 4 shows that for each pressure, the curves bifurcate from the original Paschen data3 (representing complete breakdown in uniform field gaps) due to the non-uniform field conditions. For a given pressure, and regardless of the voltage type (-DC, +DC, or AC), as the p·d product decreases, the gap becomes more uniform and UCIV approaches Ub. Conversely, as the p·d product increases, the field becomes more non-uniform, and the disparity between UCIV and Ub widens.

The results presented in Fig. 5 show that the mean relative deviation between Ub and UCIV increases with pressure for both geometries. Notably, the rod-plane geometry exhibits a greater deviation, as it generates a more highly non-uniform field distribution compared to the sphere-plane configuration.

The pressure is expressed as a percentage to improve understanding of the evaluated steps. During the tests, the laboratory atmospheric pressure was measured using a thermo-hygro-barometer. Then, the pressure inside the chamber was adjusted based on the desired percentage. The reported absolute pressure values for the rod-plane and sphere-plane electrodes differ slightly in the dataset since the tests were conducted on different days and the atmospheric pressure was measured independently on each test day. Therefore, each percentage level was calculated relative to the actual atmospheric pressure recorded on the day of the test. Every discharge was monitored using both the oscilloscope and an imaging sensor. As noted previously, the imaging is essential for detecting corona glow, which emits visible light but may not produce a detectable pulse on the oscilloscope. The combined use of both methods ensures the reliable detection and identification of both pulsed and pulseless discharges.

In summary, the rigorous technical validation process—integrating visual inspection, statistical analysis, and empirical spot testing—confirmed the quality and reliability of the dataset. While visual screening eliminated signal anomalies and data gaps, the statistical analysis verified measurement repeatability and the absence of outliers. Furthermore, spot testing confirmed that experimental trends remained consistent with established theoretical expectations. Consequently, this high-fidelity dataset provides a robust foundation for researchers and data scientists to characterize and model corona and complete breakdown discharges in air with high confidence.

Usage Notes

The experimental dataset presented in this work comprises 1,764 tests aimed at detecting corona and complete breakdown discharges in air. These tests were conducted under sinusoidal alternating current (AC) and direct current (DC) supplies across a range of pressure-distance (p·d) product. The study utilizes two common high-voltage electrode geometries: rod-plane and sphere-plane gaps. This dataset is intended to support the development of predictive models for discharge behaviour and the design of insulation systems for more-electric and all-electric aircraft. Furthermore, it serves as a resource for power system applications in low-pressure environments, such as high-altitude power lines.
