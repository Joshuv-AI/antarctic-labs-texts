Decoding long-term aging of ripened Pu-erh tea: sensory equilibrium, metabolic pathways, and chemical markers
Authors: Xuexue Zheng, Silei Bai, Dong Chen, Shuai Wen, Longhao Wang, Huimin An, Haiqian Liang, Hengbin Yao, Yuan Chen, Hongyu Chen, Jing Zhang, Yajie Xue, Jianan Huang, Zhonghua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Natural storage promotes Ripened Pu-erh tea (RPET) aging along with chemical conversion and flavor evolution. Analysis of teas aged 2–40+ years via sensory evaluation, E-tongue, and untargeted metabolomics showed a rise–fall–stabilization pattern in sensory profiles and metabolites. The 15-year sample reached sensory equilibrium with mild bitterness and enhanced sweetness and mellowness. A total of 313 non-volatile metabolites were identified: early accumulation of amino acids, nucleosides, and catechins; mid-stage depletion via oxidation and hydrolysis; and late-stage stabilization of lipids, phenolic acids, and antioxidants. Lipid oxidation products mitigated bitterness, while amino acids, nucleotides, and phenolic derivatives enhanced sweetness, kokumi, and flavor complexity. Fourteen age-dependent metabolites, including α-linolenic acid and L-phenylalaninol, correlated with storage duration strongly. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment highlighted flavonoid degradation, nucleotide turnover, and lipid remodeling as key pathways underlying sensory evolution. These findings provide mechanistic insight and objective markers for RPET quality assessment and storage authentication.

Introduction

Dark tea is a distinctive category of post-fermented tea in China, encompassing several regionally specific types such as Hubei Qingzhuan tea, Guangxi Liupao tea, Hunan Fuzhuan tea, and Yunnan Pu-erh tea, which are classified according to geographic origin and processing methods (Shen et al., 2022). Among them, Pu-erh tea has gained considerable domestic and international attention owing to its unique sensory properties and reported health benefits (Alasalvar et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2019). It is mainly produced in Yunnan Province from mature large leaves of Camellia sinensis var. assamica and is generally classified into raw and ripened types based on processing methods (Ren et al., 2022).

Ripened Pu-erh tea (RPET), a representative of post-fermented tea, is produced through microbial pile fermentation, yielding a mellow and smooth taste with reduced bitterness and a distinctive aged aroma (Ren et al., 2022). Unlike most teas, RPET quality continues to evolve during storage, with natural aging often leading to reduced bitterness and astringency alongside the gradual development of thickness, continuity, and mouthfulness (Lv et al., 2023). This has given rise to the traditional belief that “the older, the better.” However, this view is largely empirical, and the mechanisms underlying the transformation of non-volatile compounds and their contribution to sensory improvement during long-term storage remain poorly understood.

Previous research has primarily focused on the pile fermentation stage, exploring the influence of fermentation parameters (e.g., time, temperature, humidity, microbial composition) on chemical transformations and sensory characteristics. Dominant fungi such as Aspergillus spp. and Eurotium cristatum, along with bacteria such as Bacillus spp., have been reported to facilitate the formation of theaflavins and theabrownins, contributing to color stability and mouthfeel (Ma et al., 2024). Advances in multi-omics approaches—including metabolomics, proteomics, and metagenomics—have deepened our understanding of fermentation mechanisms (Long et al., 2020; Xue et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2019). Nevertheless, storage-induced chemical transformations and their links to sensory changes remain underexplored.

Taste is a key component of tea flavor, arising from the interplay of water-soluble compounds such as polyphenols, catechins, theaflavins, thearubigins, amino acids, caffeine, soluble sugars, and organic acids (Dong et al., 2024; Zhang, Cao, Granato, Xu, & Ho, 2020). In RPET, microbial fermentation and subsequent long-term storage trigger dynamic changes in these components: Catechins and other bitterness-related compounds tend to degrade, while sugars, organic acids, and certain aromatic metabolites accumulate, contributing to a mellow and complex taste. Recent studies suggest that such changes may be associated with kokumi-like sensations, characterized by enhanced thickness and continuity, though the specific chemical contributors remain unclear(Lv et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025).

Although well-stored RPET is reported to improve in taste over decades, several fundamental questions remain: Is there a critical aging period for maximum flavor enhancement? Which chemical or sensory markers correspond to such improvements? Are such trends consistent across samples and storage conditions? Addressing these gaps requires systematic, integrative research.

In this context, the present study explores the dynamic evolution of taste quality and chemical composition in RPET over different storage durations. A combination of quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA), electronic tongue (E-tongue) technology, and untargeted metabolomics was employed. Differential metabolites were identified and annotated through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The findings provide new insights into the biochemical basis of RPET aging, inform objective quality evaluation standards, and contribute to the scientific foundation for standardized aging classification and the high-quality development of the Pu-erh tea industry.

Materials and methods

Tea samples

A total of 23 representative RPET samples were collected, covering storage periods from the 1980s to 2022 (Table S1). To systematically evaluate the effect of storage duration on tea quality, samples were selected at ∼5-year intervals, corresponding to nine storage stages: 2 years, 5 years, 10 years, 15 years, 20 years, 25 years, 30 years, 35 years, and the 1980s (labeled “80ND”). Each sample was coded using a prefix indicating storage duration (e.g., “2Y” for 2 years, “25Y” for 25 years) followed by an individual sample number (e.g., “2Y-S24”, “10Y-S13”).

To ensure comparability across different storage years and minimize potential confounding factors such as raw material variability and processing differences, all samples were selected based on strict criteria. First, all samples were produced from sun-dried green tea (Maocha) of Camellia sinensis var. assamica following the conventional RPET manufacturing process (pile fermentation, steaming, and compression) as stipulated in the national standard (GB/T 22111, 2008). Second, the majority of samples were benchmark formula products (e.g., 7576, 7581, 0081) from renowned manufacturers (e.g., “Suiyue Zhiwei”, “Lancang Ancient Tea”, “Zhongcha”, “Mingjia Pu-erh”). These formula products are known for their consistent blending ratios and stable quality across different production years, making them ideal representatives for studying storage-induced chemical evolution. Third, to account for potential batch-to-batch variations, multiple independent samples were included for each storage stage (e.g., three different samples for the 15-year stage), allowing the identification of common age-dependent trends rather than sample-specific anomalies.

All samples were naturally stored in well-ventilated warehouses located in Dongguan and Foshan, Guangdong Province (mean annual temperature: 22 ± 2 °C; relative humidity: 70 ± 5%), and in Xishuangbanna, Yunnan Province (mean annual temperature: 21 ± 3 °C; relative humidity: 75 ± 8%). These stable natural storage environments helped minimize off-flavor formation and maintain product integrity. The collection included both loose-leaf and compressed forms (tea cakes or bricks). All samples were preserved in their original packaging, free from visible contamination or physical deterioration, ensuring suitability for subsequent analyses.

Chemicals and reagents

Standard compounds (purity ≥98%), including gallic acid (GA), caffeine, theobromine, theophylline, and six catechin standards (EGC, C, EC, EGCG, GCG, and ECG), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) or Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for HPLC-based quantitative analysis.

Mass spectrometry-grade acetonitrile and formic acid (for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis) were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Analytical-grade reagents, including Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, ninhydrin, anthrone, and aluminum chloride, were supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for colorimetric quantification of polyphenols, amino acids, sugars, and flavonoids. Additionally, potassium chloride, tartaric acid, potassium hydroxide, and hydrochloric acid from the same supplier were utilized specifically for the preparation of E-tongue reference and cleaning solutions. Deionized water was prepared using a Barnstead GenPure Pro purification system (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA).

Sensory evaluation

Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA)

Sensory evaluation was conducted by five trained assessors (three males and two females, aged 23–58) from Hunan Agricultural University. Each assessor had completed >90 h of specialized training to ensure consistency in identifying tea sensory attributes. The evaluation followed the national standards Methodology for Sensory Evaluation of Tea (GB/T 23776, 2018) and Geographical Indication Products—Pu-erh Tea (GB/T 22111, 2008). For each sample, 3.0 g of tea was infused with 150 mL boiling water in a standard cupping set and steeped for 5 min to establish the overall sensory profile. QDA was subsequently employed to assess taste attributes. Based on preliminary panel consensus formed during training sessions and with reference to national sensory evaluation standards, six descriptors were finalized: sweetness, umami, kokumi, astringency, bitterness, and sourness. Evaluations were performed independently in an odor-free sensory room maintained at 21 ± 1 °C. Approximately 5 mL of infusion was rated on a structured 10-point scale (0 = none, 5 = moderate, 10 = very strong), with each sample assessed in triplicate on separate days. Final scores were calculated as the mean of all panelists' ratings.

Ethical approval was not required for sensory evaluation at the institute. The study was conducted in accordance with relevant operational standards in China, ensuring voluntary participation, informed consent, and protection of participants' rights and privacy.

Electronic tongue analysis

Taste quality was further assessed using an E-tongue (SA402B, Insent, Atsugi-Shi, Japan). Tea infusions were prepared following the same pre-treatment protocol as in Section 2.3.1, rapidly filtered, and cooled to room temperature prior to analysis. The E-tongue system comprised five taste sensors—CAO (sourness), COO (bitterness), AE1 (astringency), AAE (umami), and CT0 (saltiness)—and a reference electrode. It also quantified aftertaste attributes, including aftertaste-astringency (Aftertaste-A), aftertaste-bitterness (Aftertaste-B), and richness. Each measurement cycle included sensor conditioning, rinsing with a reference solution, sample detection, aftertaste evaluation, and final cleaning. For each sample, two independent infusions were prepared. Each infusion was tested four times, and the average of the last three replicates was calculated. The final taste value was obtained by averaging the results from the two infusions for statistical analysis.

Determination of main chemical components

The conventional chemical components of RPET samples were determined according to Chinese National Standard methods. Moisture content and water extract were measured using the gravimetric method following GB/T 8304-2013 and GB/T 8305-2013, respectively. Total polyphenols (TP) were determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method based on GB/T 8313-2018, while total free amino acids (FAA) were analyzed using the ninhydrin colorimetric method according to GB/T 8314-2013. Total flavonoids and soluble sugars (SS) were determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method and anthrone‑sulfuric acid assay, respectively, according to previously reported procedures (Wen et al., 2024).

Individual catechins (EGC, C, EC, EGCG, GCG, ECG), purine alkaloids (caffeine, theobromine, and theophylline), and GA were determined by HPLC (Waters e2695, Milford, USA) using an Ecosil C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm) at a detection wavelength of 278 nm, following the established protocol in GB/T 8313-2018 and GB/T 23193-2017 with minor modifications (Ouyang et al., 2024). All measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis of non-volatile compounds

Sample preparation

Tea samples (0.5000 g) were extracted with 70% methanol at a 1:50 (v/v) solid-liquid ratio. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 30 min (shaken every 10 min), centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at 10 °C, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane for UHPLC-H-ESI-MS/MS analysis. Pooled quality control (QC) samples were prepared to monitor analytical stability and reproducibility. The consistency of the analytical platform was evaluated by comparing the total ion chromatograms (TIC) of these QC samples (Fig. S1A). The extraction protocol and UHPLC-MS parameters were established based on previously validated methods for tea metabolomics with minor modifications (Ouyang et al., 2024). The methanol-based extraction was employed to maximize the coverage of non-volatile metabolites for untargeted metabolomic profiling.

UHPLC-Q Exactive-Orbitrap-MS conditions

Metabolomic profiling was performed using an ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a Q Exactive-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Separation was achieved on a Hypersil Gold C18 column (1.9 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B), with the following gradient: 0–1.5 min, 0% B; 1.6 min, 5% B; 18.0 min, 18% B; 34.0 min, 95% B; 38.5 min, 0% B; 42 min, re-equilibration. Flow rate: 0.3 mL/min; injection volume: 2 μL; column temperature: 35 °C.

Mass spectrometry settings: spray voltage +3500 V (positive mode) / -2500 V (negative mode); sheath gas: 35 arb; auxiliary gas: 10 arb; ion transfer tube temperature: 325 °C; vaporizer temperature: 300 °C. Full-scan MS1 resolution: 120,000 (m/z 100–1500); data-dependent MS/MS resolution: 15,000; normalized collision energies: 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%.

Data processing and metabolite identification

Raw MS data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for peak alignment, extraction, merging, and matching. Metabolites were identified by (1) comparison with a self-built database of authentic standards in our laboratory (detailed in our previous work, Wen et al., 2024), and (2) matching against the mzCloud online database. Only compounds with both “best match score” and “best match confidence score” > 80 were retained. Additional confirmation was performed using ChemSpider, Human Metabolome Database, BioCyc, KEGG, and the Natural Products Atlas.

Statistical analyses

Prior to statistical analysis, all variables were mean-centered and appropriately normalized and scaled according to their distribution characteristics. Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were conducted using SIMCA-P+ 14 (Umetrics, Umea, Sweden). Heatmaps were generated in TBtools 2.3.1, stacked bar charts and radar plots in Origin 2022b (OriginLab, USA). Pearson correlation, volcano plots, and partial least squares (PLS) analyses were conducted in R 4.2.2, with correlation plots generated via the OmicStudio Cloud platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Student's t-test for pairwise comparisons, performed in SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG pathway database (https://www.kegg.jp/) integrated within MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/home.xhtml), and the corresponding pathway visualizations were also generated on this platform. Differential metabolites were screened using variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.5, p < 0.05, and fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5.

Results and discussion

Sensory profiles and their physicochemical basis across storage duration

To comprehensively assess the taste evolution of RPETs during long-term storage, both QDA and E-tongue analyses were employed. QDA revealed significant differences among samples across storage durations (p < 0.001, Fig. 1A). Sweetness and kokumi increased with aging, peaking at 30Y, indicating enhanced mellowness and richness, consistent with previous reports on aged dark teas (Shen et al., 2022). Conversely, astringency and bitterness decreased substantially—from 8.35 to 3.00 (2–35Y) and 8.60 to 2.95 (2Y–80ND), respectively—likely due to polyphenol degradation during microbial fermentation (Liu, Wang, Shi, & Cao, 2024). Umami declined from 5.00 (2Y) to 2.50 (80ND), possibly reflecting amino acid degradation or involvement in Strecker and Maillard reactions (Gupta et al., 2016; Shen et al., 2022). E-tongue analysis largely corroborated QDA findings (Fig. 1B). Astringency declined from 0.77 (2Y) to 0.34 (25Y), while bitterness generally decreased but showed a pronounced peak at 25Y (0.81), possibly due to temporary metabolite accumulation. Aftertaste-B (bitterness) followed a similar trend, while Aftertaste-A (astringency) steadily decreased to 0.25 (80ND), suggesting smoother mouthfeel in aged samples. Sourness peaked at 15Y (0.76), umami declined early but slightly rebounded at 35Y (0.48), and richness peaked at 15Y (0.80) before gradually declining. Saltiness remained relatively stable throughout storage (0.32–0.59).

To further validate these sensory shifts and provide a macroscopic chemical basis, key physicochemical components were analyzed (Table S2). The water extract content, a crucial indicator of tea infusion “thickness,” peaked at 15Y (53.80 ± 2.81%), providing a material basis for the high richness and balanced mouthfeel observed at this stage. Total polyphenols and total free amino acids—the primary determinants of astringency and umami—exhibited a rise-and-fall trajectory, both reaching maximum values around 10-15Y (9.24% and 1.69%, respectively) before declining in older samples (p < 0.05). Specifically, total amino acids dropped to 1.39% in the 80ND group, aligning with the reduction in umami intensity recorded by both QDA and the E-tongue. Notably, caffeine, which contributes to bitterness, showed a gradual decreasing trend from 4.15% (2Y) to 3.29% (80ND), while GA peaked at 10Y (0.96%) and then significantly decreased. The drastic reduction of individual catechins (e.g., EGCG, EGC, ECG) to trace levels across all aged samples confirms the extensive fermentation and subsequent oxidative polymerization during storage. These macro-level shifts in traditional quality markers provide a material basis for the observed sensory evolution.

Taken together, RPETs evolved from young teas with high astringency and bitterness to older teas with increased sweetness and kokumi. At 15Y, multiple taste attributes—sweetness (5.80), kokumi (5.80), moderate bitterness (7.20), and astringency (7.20)—remain prominent and balanced. This sensory equilibrium is mirrored by the peak in water extract and optimal concentrations of polyphenols and amino acids, suggesting an active phase of flavor transformation, where multiple modalities interact to shape a harmonized and integrated sensory profile.

Overall metabolome analysis of RPETs across storage duration

To investigate the impact of storage duration on metabolite profiles in RPETs, an untargeted metabolomics analysis was conducted on nine sample groups using UHPLC-Q Exactive-Orbitrap-MS (Fig. S1). The reliability of the analytical platform was evaluated using pooled QC samples. As shown in Fig. S1A, the overlaid TIC of QC samples exhibited highly consistent retention times and peak intensities, indicating that the UHPLC-Q Exactive-Orbitrap-MS system maintained excellent stability and reproducibility throughout the experiment. A total of 313 non-volatile metabolites (NVMs) were identified and classified into 14 subclasses, including lipids (63 compounds, 20.13%), organic acids (37, 11.82%), amino acids and their derivatives (33, 10.54%), flavonoids and flavonoid glycosides (29, 9.27%), and others (Fig. 2 & Table S3). Subclasses such as catechins (5, 1.60%) and vitamins (3, 0.96%) were relatively minor, likely due to degradation during post-fermentation storage. Caffeine, although abundant and consistently detected, was excluded from subclass quantification because of its relative stability across storage durations and minimal contribution to compositional variation (Huang et al., 2021).Fig. 2Overall profile of non-volatile metabolites of RPETs (n = 23 samples) across storage duration.Fig. 2

The total peak area of NVMs exhibited a non-linear trend with storage time—moderately high in early storage (2Y—10Y), peaking at 15Y, and declining thereafter—mirroring patterns reported for volatile compounds in aged dark teas (Fig. 1C) (Chen et al., 2024). This suggests dynamic metabolite accumulation and transformation driven by microbial activity, oxidation, and enzymatic reactions, with 15Y potentially representing a chemical maturity point. In terms of relative percentage contents (Fig. 1D), the metabolite composition remained relatively stable from 2Y to 15Y, particularly for alkaloids (broadly defined nitrogen-containing compounds, ∼19–22%), flavonoids (∼5–7%), and amino acid derivatives (∼6–7%). However, substantial compositional restructuring occurred with prolonged aging (≥30Y): lipids increased (∼14% at 2Y → 22% in 80ND), catechins declined (7.19% → 4.69%), flavonoids decreased in mid-aged teas but partially recovered in late aging, heterocyclic compounds generally increased, and phenolic acids peaked early then declined. These patterns reflect both degradation of primary metabolites and generation of secondary metabolites, contributing to aged RPET's unique sensory and bioactive properties.

Dynamic changes of differential metabolites across storage stages

To elucidate the temporal evolution of NVMs in RPETs, multivariate statistical analyses were conducted. Based on relative content profiles, 23 samples were classified into five aging stages: Group 1 (2–10Y), Group 2 (15Y), Group 3 (20–25Y), Group 4 (30Y), and Group 5 (35Y & 80ND). PCA revealed clear stage separation (PC1 41.3%, PC2 28.5%; cumulative R2X = 0.837), supported by HCA (Fig. 1E & F). OPLS-DA confirmed pronounced discrimination across adjacent and extreme stages (R2 = 0.554–0.980), validated by 200 permutation tests (Fig. S2).

Volcano plot analyses highlighted stage-specific asymmetries in metabolite shifts: Group 1 → 2 showed extensive metabolite accumulation, Group 2 → 3 significant depletion, while later transitions were more balanced (Fig. 3A–F). Two critical metabolic reprogramming events were identified: (i) a sharp increase from Group 1 to 2, likely reflecting oxidative modifications and accumulation of secondary metabolites within the first 15 years; and (ii) a pronounced decline from Group 2 to 3, indicative of advanced degradation after 20 years through hydrolysis, volatilization, and secondary reactions. In very old samples (≥35 years), metabolite profiles were dominated by highly stable lipids and phenolic derivatives that resist further breakdown. Integrating volcano plot results with OPLS-DA-derived VIP scores (VIP > 1.5, FC > 2 or < 0.5, p < 0.05), 76 differential metabolites were identified across major classes: lipids (21), amino acids and derivatives (10), organic acids (7), phenols (7), flavonoids (6), carbohydrates (5), nucleotides (5), and others (Table S4). Heatmap visualization (Fig. 3G) revealed stage-associated clustering, with later stages enriched in stable lipids and phenolics, while amino acids and flavonoids declined progressively. Amino acids and nucleosides

Amino acids and nucleosides—key contributors to sweetness, umami, and mellow taste—exhibited dynamic rise–fall patterns (Li, Xiao, Zhong, Bai, & Gao, 2019; Ma et al., 2025). Most free amino acids (e.g., d-glutamine, N-acetylvaline) accumulated modestly before 15 years, then declined sharply after 25 years, consistent with early proteolytic release followed by consumption in Maillard reactions, Strecker degradation, and microbial metabolism (Li et al., 2019). In contrast, 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) increased ∼222-fold at 30Y and remained elevated, suggesting sustained tryptophan degradation affecting bitterness and bioactivity (Gao et al., 2025; Telli, Kazkayasi, & Uma, 2021). Peptides such as cyclo(leucylprolyl) peaked before 30 years, indicating synergistic effects with free amino acids in taste enhancement (Yan et al., 2025). Nucleosides (adenosine, guanosine, uridine) generally accumulated early-to-mid stages, then depleted, while phosphorylated thymidine derivatives (thymidine 5′-diphosphate, TDP; thymidine 5′-monophosphate, TMP) steadily increased even in the oldest samples (12.98- and 9.94-fold), implying ongoing nucleotide salvage or turnover contributing to late-stage smoothness and kokumi effects.

Polyphenols (catechins, flavonoids, phenolic acids, simple phenols)

Polyphenols form the structural backbone of Pu-erh tea flavor. Catechin derivatives (e.g., epigallocatechin 3-O-caffeate) followed a rise–fall–partial recovery trajectory, consistent with oxidative coupling and polymerization reducing astringency while sustaining body and color. Flavonoids displayed heterogeneous trends: aglycones (e.g., quercetin, luteolin) decreased early, then recovered in late stages, reflecting glycoside hydrolysis and microbial enzymatic modifications (Huang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025); less stable flavonoids (e.g., juglalin) decreased continuously, whereas others (e.g., Flavuside A) persisted. Phenolic acids (e.g., GA) peaked around 15Y and declined via oxidative conversion into aromatic derivatives such as vanillic acid (Cheng et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2025); intermediates like 5-galloylquinic acid hydrolyzed and partially rebounded (∼3.67-fold), suggesting microbial or re-esterification activity. Simple phenols (e.g., pyrogallol, 4-sulfocatechol) generally decreased, though pyrogallol occasionally re-accumulated, implying microbial recycling (Hashiguchi et al., 2023; Taniguchi, Okubo, & Masuda, 2023). Sterically hindered antioxidants (2,6-di-tert-butyl-4-ethylphenol) and benzoquinone derivatives accumulated steadily, stabilizing redox balance and liquor color (Choi et al., 2023). Overall, early catechin/flavonoid degradation reduced bitterness, while selective persistence or late-stage reaccumulation of phenolics maintained antioxidant activity and contributed to a mellow, thick mouthfeel. Microbial genera such as Aspergillus and Eurotium likely mediated these transformations via enzymatic hydrolysis and oxidation (Huang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025).

Lipids, carbohydrates and organic acids

Lipids were the most abundant differential metabolites and exhibited diverse trends. Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs, e.g., α-linolenic acid, α-eleostearic acid) accumulated in late-aged samples, likely released from precursors or stabilized by oxidation. Hydroxylated fatty acids (e.g., 9,10-DiHOME, 16-hydroxyhexadecanoic acid) and glycerides (e.g., 1-linoleoyl glycerol) increased similarly, consistent with microbial or oxidative transformations of PUFAs that enhanced viscosity and smoothness (Fang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). Terpenoid and triterpenoid metabolites (e.g., Antcamphorol H, Camphoratin H) accumulated in specific stages, suggesting microbial transformation of terpenoid precursors. Carbohydrates displayed complementary trends: sucrose decreased sharply in mid-aging, while secondary carbohydrate derivatives (e.g., D-ribono-1,4-lactone, 4-O-hexopyranosylhexonic acid) accumulated in older samples, reducing direct sweetness but generating Maillard and caramelization precursors that enriched sweet–mellow taste complexity. In addition, carbohydrates also modulate bitterness and astringency through interactions with polyphenols and amino acids (Liu et al., 2021). Organic acids, including fatty acid-derived dicarboxylic acids (e.g., suberic acid, hexadecanedioic acid) and shikimate/Tricarboxylic Acid Cycle (TCA) intermediates (e.g., shikimic acid, oxalosuccinic acid), fluctuated dynamically across stages, contributing freshness, sour–sweet balance, and aged flavor complexity (Chen et al., 2023; Deng, He, Han, & Chen, 2023; Huang et al., 2023).

Alkaloids and heterocyclic compounds

Alkaloids, particularly caffeine and its derivatives, serve as key modulators of bitterness and stimulant effects. Caffeine remained relatively stable, whereas derivatives such as theophylline and 3-methylxanthine accumulated in late stages, reshaping bitterness from caffeine-dominated to diversified xanthine derivatives (Wang, Qiu, Gan, & Zhu, 2022; Zhu et al., 2022). Theophylline peaked at 30 years (∼9-fold) before declining, suggesting dynamic turnover rather than persistent accumulation. Heterocyclic compounds exhibited stage-specific changes: 3-Acetyl-2,4-dihydroxyquinoline surged at 30Y, contributing bitter, medicinal, or fermented notes (Choi et al., 2023); ethyl 3-hydroxy-4,6-dimethoxy-2-oxoindoline-3-carboxylate peaked early (15Y) then declined, reflecting transient microbial metabolism. These conversions are key drivers of bitterness modulation and flavor complexity during long-term storage.

Vitamins and other minor metabolites

Vitamin-related antioxidants accumulated progressively. Trolox, a vitamin E analog, increased steadily (∼9.94-fold at 35Y), suggesting enhanced antioxidant capacity in aged Pu-erh tea. Microbial metabolism promotes the accumulation of vitamins and related antioxidants, improving taste by reducing bitterness and astringency (Wongmaneepratip, Jom, & Vangnai, 2024; Xiong et al., 2024). Unexpectedly, compounds such as boldenone undecylenate, 4-N-Octylstyrene, and Ganodernoid D were tentatively identified in aged samples. Although not typical tea metabolites, their presence may reflect microbial transformation of phytosterols or exogenous compounds during fermentation, potentially contributing to flavor complexity or bioactivity, warranting further investigation.

Flavor-associated metabolites and sensory correlations across storage stages

Aftertaste-astringency (aftertaste-a) and bitterness (aftertaste-B)

Lipids (e.g., Antcamphorol H, N158; 2-hydroxyethyl 12-hydroxyoctadecanoate, N155) and phenolic acids (e.g., 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, N276) negatively correlated with aftertaste-A (r < −0.70, p < 0.05), suggesting that their decline contributes to astringency attenuation and taste mellowing. Alkaloids such as theophylline (N6) and 3-methylxanthine (N7) negatively correlated with aftertaste-B, consistent with gradual degradation or transformation of caffeine derivatives during aging, reducing bitterness intensity (Ma et al., 2025). Conversely, certain amino acids (e.g., DL-arginine, N24; d-glutamine, N18) and phenols (e.g., 4-sulfocatechol, N307) positively correlated with aftertaste-A, indicating their role in sustaining lingering taste, particularly in mid-aged teas.

Richness and kokumi-like perception

Compounds positively associated with richness included nucleotides (e.g., pseudouridine, N202; cytosine, N197), phenolic acids (e.g., GA, N274; 3-coumaric acid, N290), and amino acid derivatives (e.g., DL-arginine, N24; N-acetylornithine, N27) (r > 0.70, p < 0.05). These metabolites likely enhance kokumi or umami-like sensations by increasing flavor complexity. This is consistent with previous reports indicating that both puerins—formed via condensation of catechins with organic acids, phenolic acids, and amino acids—and flavoalkaloids showed strong positive correlations with kokumi taste during storage (Ma et al., 2025). In addition, phosphate-modified sugars (e.g., 7-O-phosphonohept-2-ulose, N57) and lignan/coumarin derivatives (N121) may act synergistically to enrich tea flavor.

Sourness and saltiness

Sourness correlated positively with phenolic acids (e.g., 3-hydroxy-4-methoxy-5-(sulfooxy)benzoic acid, N286), phenols (e.g., 4-sulfocatechol, N307), and amino acid derivatives (e.g., N-acetylornithine, N27; d-glutamine, N18). These results are consistent with previous studies reporting that carbohydrate degradation, catechin/phenolic ester hydrolysis, and amino acid modifications (methylation, N-acylation) contribute to enhanced sourness in aged black and Pu-erh tea (Ma et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). Conversely, glucogallin (N285), a galloylated phenolic acid, exhibited a negative correlation with saltiness, potentially due to masking effects or structural transformations. These results highlight that phenolic compounds modulate not only bitterness but also subtle taste dimensions such as sourness and saltiness (Cheng et al., 2021).

Potential chemical markers for storage duration

To explore potential indicators of storage time, Pearson correlation analysis was performed between metabolite abundances and storage duration. Fourteen metabolites exhibited strong correlations (|r| > 0.8, p < 0.01; Fig. 5A & Table S5). Notably, 4-N-octylstyrene (r = 0.914), 2,6-di-tert-butyl-4-ethylphenol (r = 0.913), and hexadeca-5,9-dienoic acid (r = 0.911) showed nearly linear increases across storage years, highlighting their potential as robust markers. Lipids such as α-linolenic acid, 14(S)-HDHA, phloionolic acid, and Antcamphorol H also exhibited strong correlations (r = 0.856–0.908). Lipid remodeling is a well-recognized feature of tea aging, and α-linolenic acid in particular has been associated with the formation of volatile aroma precursors (Wei et al., 2023). In contrast, phloionolic acid and Antcamphorol H have rarely been described in tea metabolomics, suggesting that they may represent new lipid-derived markers identified here. Among amino acid derivatives, proline (r = 0.827) confirmed its known role in flavor and fermentation, while L-phenylalaninol (r = 0.814) emerged as a potential new marker (Kottawa-Arachchi, Gunasekare, & Ranatunga, 2019). Alkaloid 3-methyl-7-propylxanthine (r = 0.817) and a complex carbohydrate derivative (r = 0.813) further reflected purine and polysaccharide remodeling. Compounds such as boldenone undecylenate and 4-N-octylstyrene, annotated as “others,” are atypical in tea metabolomics and further MS/MS validation is warranted to confirm their identity.

Beyond these, 24 metabolites showed moderate correlations (0.5 < |r| < 0.8, p < 0.05), while most compounds (n = 275) exhibited no significant correlation, indicating selective metabolic remodeling during storage. PLS regression confirmed the predictive potential of the 14 strongly correlated metabolites (R2 = 0.868; Fig. 5B). Taken together, these compounds provide a promising multi-marker panel for Pu-erh tea storage-year prediction. Nevertheless, since untargeted metabolomics relies on semi-quantitative data, further studies employing targeted absolute quantification (e.g., using Multiple Reaction Monitoring, MRM) are needed to validate the practical application of these candidate markers in quality control and storage classification.

KEGG pathway enrichment and metabolic insights

To further clarify the metabolic mechanisms driving taste evolution in Pu-erh tea during long-term storage, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed based on differential metabolites. Fourteen pathways were enriched, with flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, pyrimidine metabolism, one‑carbon pool by folate, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids being the most significant (Fig. 6 & Table S6).Fig. 6Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment of differential metabolites in RPETs. Flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (p = 0.0034) was the most significantly enriched pathway, variations in quercetin and luteolin were identified as major contributors. These flavonoids are key determinants of bitterness and astringency. Their progressive degradation and polymerization during storage not only reduce sharp bitterness but also contribute to the formation of complex polymeric pigments, thereby improving the mellowness and color of the tea infusion. Pyrimidine metabolism (p = 0.0054) and one carbon pool by folate (p = 0.0150) were also significantly enriched, with metabolites such as uridine, dTDP, dTMP, and adenosine showing clear variations. These nucleotides are known contributors to umami and sweetness in tea (Li et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2025). Their marked decline in mid-aged samples corresponds with the reduced freshness detected by the E-tongue, highlighting nucleotide degradation and turnover are important drivers of freshness weakening during storage. Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (p = 0.0164), including arachidonate and (9Z,12Z,15Z)-octadecatrienoic acid (α-linolenic acid), was significantly enriched. Polyunsaturated fatty acids play a dual role: they contribute to liquor smoothness and thickness, while also serving as precursors of volatile compounds responsible for floral and fruity aromas (Wei et al., 2023). Their transformation during storage thus links non-volatile taste evolution with aroma development.

Other pathways showed moderate enrichment. Flavonoid biosynthesis (p = 0.0675) overlapped with flavone/flavonol biosynthesis, confirming the importance of flavonoid transformation in shaping mouthfeel. Arachidonic acid metabolism (p = 0.0803) and α-linolenic acid metabolism (p = 0.2158) further reflected remodeling of fatty acid metabolism, though metabolites in these pathways declined, likely due to oxidative degradation of long-chain fatty acids, which may weaken smoothness in extended storage. Purine metabolism (p = 0.1497), involving adenosine and guanosine, was enriched but not statistically significant. Nonetheless, the decline of these metabolites is consistent with reduced umami perception, supporting the role of nucleotide metabolism in freshness loss. Similarly, arginine biosynthesis (p = 0.1545; N-acetylornithine) may be linked to aftertaste and sourness regulation through amino acid turnover. Enrichment was also observed in cutin, suberine and wax biosynthesis (p = 0.1545), citrate cycle (TCA cycle) (p = 0.1702), ascorbate and aldarate metabolism (p = 0.1702), starch and sucrose metabolism (p = 0.1857), and galactose metabolism (p = 0.2231). Although not statistically significant, these pathways suggest additional roles in oxidative stability, sourness modulation, and sweetness regulation. For example, sucrose enrichment in starch/sucrose and galactose metabolism may underlie the gradual sweet-mellow perception of aged tea.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidates the sensory evolution and underlying metabolic mechanisms associated with long-term storage of RPET. Sensory analyses revealed a progressive transition from pronounced bitterness and astringency in young teas to a mellow, thick, and well-balanced taste in aged samples. This sensory evolution was supported by the dynamic remodeling of traditional quality components, particularly the peak in water extract and the significant reduction of caffeine and catechins, which collectively contributed to reduced irritation and enhanced smoothness.

Notably, the 15-year stage represented a sensory equilibrium point characterized by balanced taste attributes and optimal concentrations of key chemical components. Untargeted metabolomics further revealed a stage-dependent metabolic remodeling process involving early accumulation, mid-stage transformation, and late-stage stabilization of metabolites.

Fourteen age-dependent metabolites were identified as potential chemical markers for predicting storage duration and evaluating aging status. Future studies will focus on targeted absolute quantification of these markers to validate their application in quality authentication and storage classification of Pu-erh tea.
