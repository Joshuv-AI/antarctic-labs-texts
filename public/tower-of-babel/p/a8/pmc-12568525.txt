Causal Associations Between 4907 Circulating Proteins and Hyperlipidemia: A Two‐Sample Mendelian Randomization Study Based on European Ancestry
Authors: Hui Cheng, Dayuan Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveThis study employed Mendelian randomization (MR) to investigate the causal relationships between 4907 circulating proteins and hyperlipidemia, with the aim of identifying potential drug targets.MethodsInstrumental variables were derived from genome‐wide association study (GWAS) data on 4907 circulating proteins. Causal associations between exposures and outcome were assessed using MR analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to identify circulating proteins most strongly associated with hyperlipidemia. Finally, enrichment analysis was performed on positively associated proteins to identify disease‐related pathways.ResultsMR analysis identified 70 disease‐associated proteins, including 47 positively correlated and 23 negatively correlated with hyperlipidemia. Enrichment analysis revealed significant pathways related to hyperlipidemia, such as the adipocytokine signaling pathway and axon guidance.ConclusionThis study identified several circulating proteins significantly associated with hyperlipidemia. The large sample size minimized confounding factors, providing valuable insights for clinical treatment strategies.

Background

Hyperlipidemia is a chronic condition characterized by abnormal lipid metabolism in the body, manifested by elevated total cholesterol, triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and decreased high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) levels in the blood [1]. This disease poses a significant global health challenge. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, elevated LDL‐C was estimated to be responsible for 3.78 million deaths from ischemic heart disease globally, accounting for 44.3% of all ischemic heart disease deaths, and for 610,000 deaths from ischemic stroke, representing 22.4% of all ischemic stroke deaths [2]. Regarding its treatment, although statins are proven effective, a considerable proportion of patients exhibit statin intolerance or fail to achieve their target lipid levels [3]. Furthermore, statin adherence is notably poor among patients in China. Studies indicate that among primary and secondary cardiovascular disease (CVD) prevention populations, the mean proportion of days covered for new statin users was only 19% over 12 months, with 66% of patients having a coverage rate of less than 20% [4]. Data from the China Patient‐Centered Evaluative Assessment of Cardiac Events Million Persons Project (China PEACE MPP) showed that only 19.3% of 74,830 patients with coronary heart disease and ischemic stroke were using statins [5]. Therefore, identifying novel drug targets is crucial to address this substantial unmet clinical need and to provide alternative therapeutic strategies for millions of patients.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a statistical method that uses genetic variation as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes [6]. The core concept is based on Mendelian inheritance laws, which dictate that genetic variations are randomly assigned at birth, thus mitigating common confounding biases and reverse causality issues prevalent in traditional observational studies [7]. MR has been widely applied in epidemiology, genetics, and biomedical research, particularly in identifying disease risk factors and potential therapeutic targets [8]. By using genetically random variations as proxy exposures, MR allows for a comprehensive assessment of the potential causal association between specific exposures and outcomes [9]. Motivated by the above research, the present study utilizes a large‐scale circulating proteomics database to assess the causal relationship between circulating proteins and hyperlipidemia, with the aim of identifying potential drug targets.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

In this study, 4907 circulating proteins were considered as exposures, and hyperlipidemia was treated as the outcome. The causal relationship between the two was assessed using two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis (Figure 1). The basic assumptions of MR studies are as follows: (1) Genetic variations are strongly associated with the exposure; (2) Genetic variations should be independent of any confounders related to both the exposure and the outcome; (3) Genetic variations influence the outcome solely through the exposure, rather than through a direct effect on the outcome.

Flowchart of the study design.

Data Sources

To minimize population bias, the study was restricted to individuals of European ancestry. The proteomics GWAS data used for the exposure factor was derived from a large‐scale study on protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) in 35,559 Icelandic individuals [8]. This study identified 4907 circulating proteins. The GWAS data for hyperlipidemia was obtained from the IEU database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) under the data set ID: ebi‐a‐GCST90104006 (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ebi-a-GCST90104006/). The data set includes 349,222 individuals of European ancestry (39,961 cases and 309,261 controls), along with 14,502,301 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [10].

Instrumental Variable Selection

The first step in selecting instrumental variables (IVs) was ensuring that the SNPs were significantly associated with hyperlipidemia. The threshold for exposure SNPs was set to p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ to filter out a large number of irrelevant SNPs. To account for linkage disequilibrium, we applied a distance threshold of 10,000 kb and an r² < 0.001 [11]. Furthermore, to minimize bias due to weak instrumental variables, we calculated the F‐statistic and included only IVs with F‐statistics greater than 10 in subsequent analyses [12]. It is important to note that, before each MR analysis, we ensured that the p‐value for the outcome (P_outcome) was greater than the p‐value for the exposure (P_exposure). By restricting the study population to individuals of the same ethnic background (European descent), concerns regarding population stratification were mitigated.

Statistical Analysis

To determine the causal relationship between circulating proteins and hyperlipidemia, we performed MR analysis using six robust methods: the Wald ratio method [13], MR‐Egger regression [14], random‐effects inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) [15], weighted median method [16], simple mode, and weighted mode [17]. When different analytical methods yield divergent conclusions, the IVW method is considered the primary method [18]. For sensitivity analysis, to assess heterogeneity, we conducted the Cochran Q test on the IVW and MR‐Egger methods (p < 0.05 indicates positive heterogeneity, suggesting the presence of heterogeneity in the results) [19]. The MR‐Egger intercept test was used to examine whether genetic variations exhibit directional pleiotropy (p < 0.05 indicates the presence of directional pleiotropy) [20]. A small degree of heterogeneity does not compromise the reliability of the results, as it is attributed to large differences between SNPs; however, the presence of pleiotropy is unacceptable and must be excluded. The Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR‐PRESSO) test serves two purposes [21]: (1) it tests for the presence of horizontal pleiotropy; (2) the global test can identify and exclude outliers of horizontal pleiotropy (p < 0.05 indicates the presence of horizontal pleiotropic outliers, which should be excluded; p > 0.05 indicates no horizontal pleiotropic outliers). This improves the accuracy of the study's findings [22]. Finally, the results of the “no pleiotropy and heterogeneity” MR assumption were evaluated through forest plots, scatter plots, and leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis plots. (Note: All statistical analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.8) in R).

Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the functional enrichment of disease‐related proteins. Enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R (version 4.4.1), the Gene Ontology (GO) database, and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). The GO database categorizes genes into three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) [23]. We also visualized the enrichment results. In the bar plots, the y‐axis represents the name of each pathway, the x‐axis denotes the number of proteins, and the color of the bars indicates statistical significance, with redder colors signifying higher significance of the disease‐related proteins in that pathway. In the bubble plot, the x‐axis represents the gene ratio, the size of the bubbles reflects the number of proteins, and the color indicates significance [24].

Throughout the entire analytical process of this study, the following software and R packages were utilized: R version 4.4.1, along with the clusterProfiler package, TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.8), ieugwasr package, gwasglue package, VariantAnnotation package, grid package, readr package, forestploter package, reshape2 package, circlize package, ComplexHeatmap package, dplyr package, ggplot2 package, ggrepel package, tidyr package, CMplot package, org.Hs.eg.db package, enrichplot package, RColorBrewer package, ggpubr package, and the ComplexHeatmap package. The detailed analytical pipeline and specific code are available from the first author upon request.

Results

Mendelian Randomization Analysis

We performed a two‐sample MR analysis to identify circulating plasma proteins causally associated with hyperlipidemia. Protein quantitative trait locus (pQTL) studies, which investigate the genetic regulation of protein levels in the blood, can provide valuable insights into the causal relationships between proteins and diseases [25]. Cis‐pQTLs were extracted from the deCODE study. Based on the cis‐pQTL selection criteria, we identified 31,315 SNPs (Supporting Information S1: Table 1) and discovered 3440 proteins (Supporting Information S1: Table 2). The hyperlipidemia data were obtained from the IEU database, including 39,961 cases and 309,261 controls. After extracting exposure information and removing reverse chain alignment, 11,592 SNPs were retained (Supporting Information S1: Table 3). The MR analysis identified 1224 proteins associated with hyperlipidemia (Supporting Information S1: Table 4). After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, 70 circulating proteins were identified as significantly associated with hyperlipidemia (P_IVW < 0.05, P_FDR < 0.05), including TIRAP、PPA2、GPI、PDE11A、DNASE2B、CXCL10、CRAT、S100A16、CAPN3、NCR3、DCTN6、ARFIP2、GBP2、QDPR、NAPG、GSN、RNF8、KIR2DS4、RHOC、PREP、ERMAP、UBE2D2、GUK1、PSAPL1、ENG、TINAGL1、NHLRC3、MAGEA10、PLAUR、KRTDAP、PRSS1、SRXN1、FAS、PPIL1、MLF1、PSMB9、IL2RB、FASLG、FADD、CST4、LHCGR、STX2、CCL23、GSTM4、DECR2、NCAM2、GSTM1、FCGR2B、FCGR2A、LCT、IDUA、CDH11、KLK10、ITIH1、CTSF、CTSA、NAT1、THBS4、NPPB、CDSN、AMIGO2、PLTP、TPP1、MBD1、TIMD4、NCAN、LMAN2、IDS、SIGLEC12、PIWIL1. The forest plot (Figure 2) shows p < 0.05, indicating that these proteins are significantly associated with the disease. Specifically, proteins with an odds ratio (OR) < 1 are considered protective factors for hyperlipidemia, while those with an OR > 1 are considered risk factors. Forty‐seven proteins were identified as risk factors, including TIRAP、PPA2、GPI、PDE11A、DNASE2B、CXCL10、CRAT、S100A16、CAPN3、NCR3、DCTN6、ARFIP2、GBP2、QDPR、NAPG、GSN、RNF8、KIR2DS4、RHOC、PREP、ERMAP、UBE2D2、GUK1、PSAPL1、ENG、TINAGL1、NHLRC3、MAGEA10、PLAUR、KRTDAP、PRSS1、SRXN1、FAS、PPIL1、MLF1、PSMB9、IL2RB、FASLG、FADD、CST4、LHCGR、STX2、CCL23、GSTM4、DECR2、NCAM2、GSTM1.

Discussion

Through MR analysis of 4907 circulating proteins, based on large‐scale pQTL studies and publicly available GWAS databases, SNPs were used as instrumental variables. By applying the criteria of PIVW < 0.05 and PFDR < 0.05, a total of 70 disease‐related proteins were identified, with 47 positively correlated and 23 negatively correlated with the disease. Among the 70 proteins significantly associated with hyperlipidemia, the top three proteins with the largest effect sizes included TIRAP (OR = 1.264, 95% CI: 1.056–1.514, p = 0.011), PPA2 (OR = 1.234, 95% CI: 1.043–1.461, p = 0.014), and GPI (OR = 1.218, 95% CI: 1.050–1.414, p = 0.009), indicating their particularly substantial influence on disease risk. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these disease‐related proteins were primarily enriched in 29 pathways, with the highest confidence observed for the pathways “lysosome,” “platinum drug resistance,” “measles,” and “influenza A.”

Toll‐interleukin 1 receptor domain‐containing adaptor protein (TIRAP) is a key intracellular adapter protein within the Toll‐like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway [26]. Its primary function involves mediating signal transduction through TLR2 and TLR4, thereby activating downstream inflammatory pathways such as NF‐κB and promoting the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including TNF‐α and IL‐6. Activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly the TLR4 pathway, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of hyperlipidemia. As a critical “switch” in the TLR4 pathway, TIRAP represents a key molecular link between dyslipidemia and chronic inflammation [26].

The CXCL family exhibits diverse functions and plays unique roles in immune regulation, cancer, metabolic diseases, and inflammatory responses. Koenen and Weber highlighted that CXCL proteins serve as central mediators linking dyslipidemia to inflammation in atherosclerosis [27]. The underlying mechanism involves a positive feedback loop: oxidized low‐density lipoprotein (oxLDL) in the bloodstream activates inflammatory pathways such as LOX‐1 and NF‐κB, inducing vascular cells (e.g., endothelial cells and macrophages) to highly express chemokines including CXCL1 and CXCL8. These CXCL proteins then recruit monocytes into the subendothelial space, where they differentiate into macrophages and engulf lipids to form foam cells, thereby driving plaque formation. Consequently, lipid metabolic disorders and CXCL‐mediated inflammatory responses mutually reinforce each other, jointly promoting disease progression [27].

Fatty acid synthase (FAS) was first proposed and named by Wakil et al. in 1957 [28]. The FAS gene is categorized into two types: Type I and Type II. Mammalian FAS belongs to Type I, which consists of a single polypeptide chain with a relative molecular mass of 250 kDa that forms head‐to‐tail dimers as its primary functional structure. This multifunctional enzyme is located in the cytoplasm [29]. In animals, FAS is a key enzyme in fatty acid biosynthesis, catalyzing the polymerization of acetyl‐CoA and malonyl‐CoA into long‐chain fatty acids. It serves as the major rate‐limiting enzyme in de novo fatty acid synthesis and plays a significant role in fat deposition [30].

Muto et al. suggested that the liver is a central organ in lipid metabolism. Free fatty acids are esterified via the monoacylglycerol pathway to form triglycerides (TG), which are subsequently stored in hepatocytes as lipid droplets or secreted into the serum as very low‐density lipoproteins (VLDL) [31]. FAS is a crucial enzyme in lipogenesis and is essential for TG synthesis. Increased FAS expression can elevate the rate of TG synthesis in the serum and promote fat accumulation in the liver [32]. Zhao Min et al. demonstrated through animal experiments that hyperlipidemia may result from upregulation of genes associated with FAS, leading to increased synthesis of fatty acids and TG [33]. Other studies have also indicated that aberrant expression of FAS disrupts lipid metabolism, contributing to metabolic disorders [34].

Fcy Receptor IIb (FCGR2B) is an inhibitory receptor for the Fc fragment of immunoglobulin G (IgG), primarily expressed on the surface of immune cells such as B cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Its core function is to transmit inhibitory signals that negatively regulate the activation of immune cells, thereby maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing excessive inflammatory responses. The expression level and functional activity of FCGR2B represent an important protective factor influencing the severity of inflammation‐related injury in hyperlipidemia [35]. Studies by Yanice et al. have further confirmed the protective role of FCGR2B against atherosclerosis in mouse models and elucidated its specific mechanism in regulating macrophage‐mediated inflammatory responses [36]. Plasma phospholipid transfer protein (PLTP) is a key protein involved in lipid metabolism, primarily responsible for transferring phospholipids, cholesteryl esters, vitamin E, and other neutral lipids among lipoproteins. Under physiological conditions, PLTP activity serves as a protective factor in maintaining lipid homeostasis by promoting reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), thereby counteracting atherosclerosis [37]. Jiang et al. demonstrated in their study that PLTP deficiency in mice resulted in reduced HDL levels and exacerbated atherosclerosis, supporting its crucial physiological protective function [38].

Lysosome is a critical organelle within cells, primarily responsible for the degradation and recycling of cellular waste and damaged components, playing a key role in lipid metabolism. Thus, lysosomes are closely associated with the development and progression of hyperlipidemia [14]. First, lysosomes play a key role in lipid degradation and storage within lipid metabolism [22]. Additionally, lysosomal dysfunction is often accompanied by lipid metabolism abnormalities and the onset of hyperlipidemia, with lysosomal dysfunction also triggering inflammatory responses, further exacerbating lipid metabolic disorders [39]. Given the essential role of lysosomes in lipid metabolism, studying their function in hyperlipidemia could help understand the pathogenesis of the disease and provide important insights for developing new therapeutic strategies. Moreover, lysosomes can also clear excess intracellular lipids through autophagy, and enhancing autophagy could become a novel strategy for treating hyperlipidemia [40].

Virion‐Ebolavirus, Lyssavirus, and Morbillivirus represent emerging research areas regarding their relationship with hyperlipidemia. Although these viruses are primarily known for their pathogenicity and immune responses, recent studies suggest that viral infections may affect host lipid metabolism through multiple mechanisms, thereby contributing to the onset and progression of hyperlipidemia [41].

Measles, caused by the measles virus, is a highly contagious disease. Although it may seem unrelated to hyperlipidemia, recent studies suggest that viral infections, including the measles virus, may impact lipid metabolism through multiple mechanisms (including inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune cell activation), contributing to the development and progression of hyperlipidemia [42]. First, viral infections, including the measles virus, can alter host cell lipid metabolism to support viral replication and dissemination. This metabolic reprogramming may lead to abnormalities in lipid synthesis and degradation, triggering hyperlipidemia [43]. Measles virus infection may also lead to intracellular lipid accumulation, especially cholesterol and triglycerides [44]. These lipids can alter cell membrane fluidity and signal transduction, impacting lipid metabolism [45]. Second, measles virus infection may provoke a strong inflammatory response, with inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF‐α and IL‐6) activating various signaling pathways (such as NF‐κB and STAT3), further influencing lipid metabolism [46]. Lastly, measles virus infection may influence lipid metabolism by activating immune cells (such as macrophages and T cells), which can secrete inflammatory cytokines and lipid metabolism‐related enzymes [47]. Influenza A virus is primarily known for its impact on the respiratory system; however, emerging evidence indicates that it can also influence host lipid metabolism through multiple mechanisms—including inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and immunometabolic pathways—thereby contributing to the development and progression of hyperlipidemia [46].

Strengths and Limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in its utilization of large‐scale, publicly available summary‐level data from GWAS and pQTL analyses, which ensured sufficient statistical power for the selected instrumental variables. Furthermore, we applied multiple MR analytical methods and conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. The consistency of the results enhances the reliability of our findings. Additionally, the use of genetic variants as instrumental variables mitigated confounding factors—such as age, diet, and behavioral factors—commonly encountered in conventional observational studies, thereby providing stronger evidence for a causal relationship between circulating proteins and hyperlipidemia.

This study also has several limitations. First, the analyses were based exclusively on genetic databases of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our conclusions to other racial or ethnic groups (e.g., Asian or African populations). Future studies should validate these findings in non‐European populations, particularly in East Asian cohorts, using ancestry‐specific genetic data. Second, the validity of the MR analysis relies on three core assumptions. Although we employed multiple sensitivity analyses to address potential biases, the influence of unknown or hidden pleiotropy cannot be entirely ruled out.

Finally, the initial screening criteria in this study were based on statistical significance (FDR‐corrected p‐values) without pre‐specifying a minimum effect size threshold (such as an odds ratio) for identifying significant associations. Future investigations may incorporate predefined effect size thresholds to further prioritize candidate proteins with the greatest biological and clinical relevance for functional validation.

In conclusion, this study reveals potential causal relationships between circulating proteins and hyperlipidemia using the MR approach, providing new scientific evidence for the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of hyperlipidemia. However, further research is required to address the current limitations and to deepen our understanding of the complex mechanisms underlying hyperlipidemia. These findings offer valuable insights for future precision medicine research and the development of intervention strategies for hyperlipidemia.
