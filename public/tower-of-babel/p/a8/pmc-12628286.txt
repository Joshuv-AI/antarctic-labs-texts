A Novel Image‐Based Approach for Analyzing Neutrophil Chemotaxis Using a Boyden Chamber Assay
Authors: Mohammad Umar, Gurpreet Kaur Aulakh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemotaxis is a fundamental biological process in which cells such as leukocytes migrate directionally from one site to another in response to a chemical gradient. This process of cellular migration is critical for execution of appropriate physiological responses to inflammation, as observed during immune defense and wound healing responses. Neutrophils being the first responders to the sites of infection or injury are one such type of cells. A neutrophil chemotaxis assay is a commonly used laboratory technique used to measure the directed movement of neutrophils toward a chemical signal termed a chemoattractant. It is used in immunology and inflammation research to study directed immune cell migration and to assess the effects of various molecules on cell migration. When studying neutrophil chemotaxis, current methods rely on counts of cells that have migrated across a membrane or substrate and observation of the migrated cells under a microscope. While useful, this approach fails to account for cells that have been activated but remain adherent to the substrate or cells that are in the intermediate stages of transmigration. Such limitations restrict the scope of information obtained and may lead to incomplete interpretations of neutrophil chemotaxis. To address this, we developed a new image analysis method that directly addresses the membrane in a chemotaxis chamber called the Boyden chamber. This improved technique allows quantification of neutrophils that have migrated as well as those that are adherent or actively transmigrating. By including all three stages of neutrophil movement, it provides a more comprehensive and physiologically relevant understanding of chemotaxis. It offers researchers a powerful tool to dissect the dynamics of immune cell behavior with greater sensitivity and accuracy, paving the way for deeper insights into immune responses and potential therapeutic interventions in inflammatory diseases. © 2025 The Author(s). Introduction

One of the most important defining features of life is cellular migration, which is an evolutionarily conserved process. Examples span from the swimming of simple unicellular amoeba in search of nutrients to the contraction of the endometrium during childbirth and the dramatic movement of cell layers during gastrulation, a critical period of embryonic development of many animals (Friedl & Gilmour, 2009; Ridley et al., 2003). Directed cell migration is thus a crucial process in many biological functions, such as development, immune defense, tissue repair, and cancer metastasis (Mayor & Etienne‐Manneville, 2016; Petrie et al., 2009). These areas of research have grown rapidly, largely due to breakthroughs in cell and molecular biology that have uncovered key molecules, signaling pathways, and regulatory systems that drive cell movement. Among the most widely studied migrating cells are immune cells, which are responsible for maintaining the body's defense system against infection and disease (Kolaczkowska & Kubes, 2013). Modern research also benefits from sophisticated imaging technologies and innovative genetic tools, though traditional migration assays still play an important role in their evaluation. The combination of new and classic methods, often through interdisciplinary work, has led to a wide range of experimental strategies used to explore cell migration. One classical method is the Boyden chamber assay, first introduced by Dr. Stephen Boyden to study leukocytes (Boyden, 1962). A standard Boyden chamber comprises an upper well used for loading cells, a lower well used for adding a chemoattractant when studying chemotaxis (directed cell motility toward increasing concentrations of soluble attractants), a polycarbonate membrane, and a silicon gasket. The output from the standard Boyden chamber assay is the migration index (MI), also known as the chemotaxis index. It is the ratio of the migrated cells in a treatment group to the migrated cells in the negative control.

Neutrophils are vital components of the innate immune system and act as the first line of defense against bacterial infection and inflammation. They are known for their rapid response and chemotaxis, i.e., the ability to migrate precisely toward chemical signals released by an invading pathogen (pathogen‐associated molecular patterns or PAMPs) or by cells damaged by a pathogen or injurious stimulus (damage‐associated molecular patterns or DAMPs) (Kolaczkowska & Kubes, 2013; Nourshargh & Alon, 2014). Chemotaxis involves complex regulation of internal signaling (Funamoto et al., 2002; Haugh et al., 2000; Kraynov et al., 2000; Lokuta et al., 2003; Servant et al., 2000), cytoskeletal changes (Niggli, 1999; Weiner et al., 1999), and membrane dynamics. Despite advances, the exact hierarchy of mechanisms that guide this process under physiological conditions remain unclear. Various techniques are used to study neutrophil chemotaxis, including the Transwell assay (Pierini et al., 2003), Dunn chamber (Wells & Ridley, 2005), under‐agarose assay (Heit & Kubes, 2003), and time‐lapse microscopy (Servant et al., 1999). These conventional analyses primarily quantify cells that have fully migrated through a membrane, overlooking those that are activated, adherent, or partially transmigrated. Here, we introduce the membrane transmigration index (MTI), a novel metric that measures the proportion of neutrophils located on the lower membrane surface relative to the total number present on both surfaces. This enables quantification of adherent, transmigrating, and fully migrated neutrophils, providing a comprehensive evaluation of chemotactic behavior. We also present an optimized methodology for isolating and purifying primary neutrophils from murine bone marrow, and a quantitative migration analysis using Fiji/ImageJ open software. Our method offers a reproducible, sensitive, and physiologically relevant platform for dissecting the molecular and cellular mechanisms governing neutrophil migration

ASSAY FOR THE MIGRATION OF BONE‐MARROW DERIVED NEUTROPHILS USING THE BOYDEN CHAMBER

In this protocol, we first describe a detailed methodology to isolate bone marrow–derived neutrophils (BMDNs) using a negative selection method that preserves the native state and function of the target cells by avoiding direct attachment of magnetic beads or other markers to the cells. The isolation is performed using bone marrow from C57BL6/J mice and employs the MojoSort Mouse Neutrophil Isolation Kit (BioLegend), although any MACS‐based neutrophil isolation kit can be used. The isolation is illustrated in Figure 1. We then describe the use of the Boyden chamber assay with serial dilutions of the chemoattractant N‐formylmethionyl‐leucyl‐phenylalanine (fMLP) to determine the optimal concentration that leads to neutrophil migration. These steps include the isolation of adherent, transmigrating, and fully transmigrated BMDNs and staining them using a hematology stain. We used the Hemacolor Stain Set (EMD Millipore Sigma), but other commonly used hematology stains such as Wright's or Giemsa can be used. The subsequent protocol describes the calculation of the membrane transmigration index using a novel protocol to analyze neutrophils that are adherent or have partially transmigrating across the membrane.

ANALYSIS OF ADHERENT, TRANSMIGRATING, AND TRANSMIGRATED CELLS

In this protocol, we first describe the process to count transmigrated cells and calculate the membrane transmigration index. We then describe how to analyze membrane neutrophils (adherent and transmigrating) utilizing the open‐source software Fiji/ImageJ. The process is illustrated in Figure 4. The software can be downloaded from https://fiji.sc/ as per operating system compatibility. For our experiments, we used the Windows PC compatible 64‐bit ImageJ 1.54p (Schindelin et al., 2012). The analysis will also use Microsoft Excel.

Membrane image analysis. The flowchart depicts key steps involved in generating the 32‐bit ratio image from the original RGB image of a Hemacolor‐stained membrane. The ratio image is duplicated; one duplicate is filtered to reveal low‐intensity pixels with a minimum filter and the other filtered to reveal high‐intensity pixels with a maximum filter. The minimum intensity image is inverted and then both images are contrasted to merge into a red and green channel composite .tiff image. The latter is then segmented for red and green particles to analyze 25‐2000 pixel2 particles.

COMMENTARY

Background Information

Using the established Boyden chamber and image post‐processing and analysis, we have shown that it is possible to quantify the three stages of neutrophil activation that occur during chemotaxis. These are the loss of l‐selectin and/or gain of integrin‐mediated adhesions during slow rolling, crawling prior to transmigration, and finally directed migration (Xu et al., 2011). An observation that led us to believe in the ability of BMDNs to adhere was that neutrophils in the control group adhere in the absence of any external stimulus or chemoattractant (Fig. 10A, 11D). The use of the Boyden chamber assay implicates neutrophil activation solely based on their protein machinery with or without stimuli such as fMLP, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), or H2O2. The interactions of neutrophils observed with the polycarbonate membrane are most likely through hydrophobic interactions as there is no endothelial or extracellular matrix input (Harvath et al., 1980; Itoh et al., 2008).

We have developed a new metric, the membrane transmigration index of MTI, that measures the proportion of neutrophils that have transmigrated towards the lower side of the membrane (represented in green) in relation to the total number of neutrophils on both sides of the membrane (red + green). This index is limited to values between 0 and 1. Unlike the chemotaxis index, which relies on normalizing the data with respect to the control group (without chemoattractant), the MTI is objective as it computes neutrophil transmigration for each well independent other groups.

The fMLP dose response experiment was fundamental in validating our hypothesis that neutrophils are able to adhere and migrate randomly towards the less‐dense lower chamber. Using this assay, the optimal concentration of the fMLP for neutrophil chemotaxis was ascertained to be 1 pM.

Critical Parameters and Troubleshooting

The key parameter to obtaining robust results is the time of the chemotaxis assay. Since neutrophils have a high baseline migratory tendency, one should avoid increasing the time of the chemotaxis assay or neutrophil migration, as this could result in random migration. It is also important that buffers are freshly prepared and kept on ice.

Statistical Analysis

The MTI and shape/size data outputs (perimeter, Feret diameter, and circularity of red/green cells) are pooled for each group from each of three separate experiments. Because the residuals for these parameters were non‐Gaussian, they were analyzed by the non‐parametric Kruskal‐Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons. The maximum probability of occurrence of differences through chance was set at 0.05 (adjusted p values) in order to be considered significant as computed by GraphPad Prism 10.0 Software.

Understanding Results

Using the protocol described here, we were able to isolate healthy neutrophils (>95% viability) from the bone marrow of the C57BL6/J mouse without exposure to toxic chemicals such as Histopaque or long centrifugations used in density gradient–based separation techniques. We also determined the optimal concentration of fMLP that causes robust BMDN migration. Images and quantification of stained neutrophils from the lower chamber confirm that the highest MI was at 1 pM fMLP (Fig. 3, p < 0.01). As the fMLP concentration increased, migration to the lower chamber decreased, becoming insignificant at 1 nM fMLP (Fig. 3C). It must be understood that the MI is a relative (not absolute) metric as it compares the ultimate number of cells recovered in the lower chamber of a test group relative to those of negative control.

We also stained and imaged cells in the polycarbonate membrane. Figure 9A shows Hemacolor‐staining of membrane BMDNs, and Figure 9B shows pseudocolorized images post‐processing, where adherent BMDNs on the upper surface of the membrane are displayed in red and transmigrated BMDNs on the lower surface are in green. The image analysis reveals progressively higher numbers of BMDNs on the membrane side facing the lower chamber when incubated with increasing concentrations of fMLP up to 10 pM. Quantifying the percentage of the membrane area occupied by BMDNs (Fig. 10A, upper surface in red, lower in green) shows high levels of chemotaxis at all but the 100 pM and 1 nM fMLP concentrations. This was also indicated by the high MTI at 1 pM, 10 pM, and 10 nM fMLP (Fig. 10B). At concentrations >10 pM, we observed a reduction in the MTI (Fig. 10B). There were more neutrophils on the upper portion of the membrane (Fig. 10A, red), which translates to a lower MTI. We also found that cells on lower surface of the membrane had a larger perimeter (Fig. 10C,D) and Feret diameter (Fig. 10G,H) as well as lower circularity (Fig. 10E,F) compared to those on the upper surface of the membrane, indicating the presence of neutrophil aggregates in response to progressively higher dose of fMLP.

We then activated the BMDNs with 10 ng/ml LPS or 0.5 M H2O2 for 30 min prior to the assay to determine the effect of these substances on neutrophil activation and migration in response to 1 pM fMLP. The chemotaxis assay was again conducted for 20 min. Stained cytospins of cells from the lower chamber show the presence of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in the LPS, LPS+fMLP, and H2O2+fMLP treatment groups (Fig. 11A). Membrane analysis revealed that the LPS, LPS+fMLP, and H2O2+fMLP groups induced adherent neutrophils (red) more than transmigrated cells (green) (Fig. 11B‐D, p < 0.01). The MTI revealed lower transmigration in LPS‐treated BMDNs compared to negative control (Fig. 11E, p = 0.1075). The LPS+fMLP (p < 0.0001), H2O2 (p < 0.0001), and H2O2+fMLP (p < 0.01) groups showed larger cell aggregates in comparison to the negative control adherent (red) BMDNs (Fig. 11F). The transmigrated (green) cell perimeter was lower in all treatment groups (p < 0.0001) compared to negative control (Fig. 11G). We conclude that LPS and H2O2 activated the BMDNs and caused the release of extracellular traps that did not end up in any valid MTI (as was possible using fMLP as the chemoattractant), but allowed us to quantify the proportions of adherent and transmigrated cells on the membrane.

Time Considerations

The protocol is divided into four different steps. The first is isolation of BMDNs, which takes ∼3 hr if the person performing the surgery is well trained, but may take 4 hr if not. The second and third are performing the assay and staining the membrane and cytospins. These take another 2 hr. These three steps need to be performed on a single day. Preparing a permanent mount of the slides takes 30 min and the slides are allowed to dry overnight. The final step is the analysis, which takes another 20 min for each image. The total time needed for this protocol is 8 hr not counting the overnight drying of the slides.
