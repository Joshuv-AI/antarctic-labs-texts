Response to the letter: “Comment on The efficacy and safety of multiple acupuncture therapies in primary insomnia: A Bayesian network meta-analysis”
Authors: Liguo Liu, Yiming Chen, Liming Lu, Yu Kui
Published: 2026

We extend our gratitude to the readers for their insightful and meticulous engagement with our recently published article.1 We appreciate the opportunity to clarify our methodology and the interpretation of our findings in light of the critical points raised.

In a letter to the editor by Tang and Chen, the authors raised concerns regarding conceptual heterogeneity across acupuncture modalities, omission of sham controls, outcome selection, risk of bias in included trials, and the assumptions underlying network meta-analysis.

Our response will elaborate on the rationale for our analytical approach, the comprehensive methods used to address the acknowledged limitations of the primary literature and the nuanced interpretation that our findings warrant.

In our analysis, our methodology did not aggregate these distinct therapies into a single "acupuncture" node. Instead, each modality and relevant combination (e.g., AT + MOX, AT + AU) was treated as a separate node within the network. This approach allows for the simultaneous comparison of all included interventions, both directly and indirectly, while preserving their unique identities. The NMA framework is precisely designed to handle such multi-armed comparisons, providing a more comprehensive evidence synthesis than a series of pairwise meta-analyses would allow.2 Our results, which identified specific modalities like ACE and combinations like AT + MOX as having the highest probability of being the most effective interventions, demonstrate that our analysis was indeed capable of differentiating among these therapies, rather than diluting their effects.

The commenters point out the absence of a sham or placebo control arm in our network. The inclusion of sham-controlled trials is indispensable for determining the efficacy of acupuncture beyond its non-specific effects. However, the research question underpinning our study was one of comparative effectiveness, not efficacy in the strictest sense. Our goal was to inform a common clinical dilemma: for a patient with primary insomnia, how do various acupuncture therapies perform relative to conventional hypnotic medications? This is a pragmatic question of high relevance to patients and clinicians seeking non-pharmacological or integrative treatment options. By focusing on comparisons against active, established treatments, our analysis provides valuable information for clinical decision-making in a real-world context. We concede that this design choice precludes us from quantifying the proportion of the treatment effect attributable to placebo or ritual. This is a limitation, which we acknowledged in our original manuscript and reiterate here.

The most critical set of concerns relates to the quality of the primary evidence base: the reliance on subjective outcomes and the risk of bias within the included RCTs.

Our selection of the PSQI as the primary outcome was guided by several considerations. First, insomnia, by its very definition in diagnostic manuals like the DSM-5, is a condition centered on the patient's subjective complaint of dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality.3 While objective measures such as sleep latency and wake after sleep onset, as measured by PSG, are valuable research tools, the clinical target of treatment is the patient's perception of their sleep and the associated daytime dysfunction. Second, the PSQI was by far the most commonly reported outcome measure across the eligible RCTs. To build a dense, connected network, it is necessary to select an outcome that is available in the majority of studies. For these reasons, the PSQI data formed the primary basis for our clinical recommendations regarding the comparative effectiveness of the evaluated therapies.

Furthermore, we are in complete agreement with the concerns raised about the CER. We included the CER analysis for two reasons: completeness and exploration. Given its prevalence in the source literature, excluding it entirely would have meant ignoring a substantial portion of the reported data. By analyzing it, we were able to assess whether its results broadly converged with or diverged from the primary PSQI analysis. However, we wish to state unequivocally that the CER rankings were treated as a supplementary indicator for broadly surveying the research landscape, not as the foundation for our main conclusions. We emphasize that the CER findings must be interpreted with a high degree of skepticism, given the aforementioned methodological weaknesses. Our confidence in the evidence for the CER outcome was, unsurprisingly, rated as very low using the Confidence In Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework—and therefore, the primary conclusions of our paper rest on the more robust PSQI data.

Recognizing these challenges from the outset, we incorporated a rigorous, multi-stage quality assessment into our protocol. First, all 64 included studies were systematically evaluated for internal validity using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB2) tool. This granular assessment of the primary studies formed the foundation for our subsequent evaluation of the network evidence. Second, and most importantly, we did not simply present the statistical outputs of the NMA in a vacuum. We formally graded the certainty of the evidence for each outcome using the CINeMA methodology. This process allowed us to systematically consider how the very issues raised by the commenters—such as the high risk of bias in the underlying studies and the indirectness of certain comparisons—impact the confidence we can place in each specific finding.4 In many comparisons, these factors would have led to downgrading the evidence to moderate, low, or very low certainty. Therefore, while our Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking (SUCRA) values provide a probabilistic hierarchy, they were intended to be interpreted in conjunction with our CINeMA-based certainty ratings. A SUCRA value represents the probability that a given treatment is among the best options; it is not a definitive declaration of superiority.

Concerns about the transitivity assumption are understandable given the prevalence of studies from Chinese settings and variations in baseline characteristics. The assumption of transitivity is a conceptual one, positing that one can make an indirect comparison of A versus C based on trials of A versus B and B versus C, provided that the B arms are sufficiently similar across trials in all clinically relevant characteristics.5 It is a fundamental prerequisite for a valid NMA. While transitivity cannot be statistically proven, we assessed its plausibility by carefully examining the characteristics of the trials included in our paper. We extracted data on these variables and found them to be broadly comparable across the different comparisons in the network. The inclusion criteria for all studies required a formal diagnosis of primary insomnia based on standard criteria, which helps to ensure a relatively homogenous patient population in terms of diagnosis. While unmeasured differences surely exist, we found no glaring or systematic differences across comparisons.

Our NMA, which included 64 RCTs and 4443 participants, was designed as a pragmatic synthesis to guide clinical practice by comparing multiple active acupuncture therapies against standard pharmacotherapy. We contend that while our findings must be interpreted with the caution dictated by the quality of the available primary evidence, the synthesis itself provides a valuable map of the comparative landscape of treatments for primary insomnia.
