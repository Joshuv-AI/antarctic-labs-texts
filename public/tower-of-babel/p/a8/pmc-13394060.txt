Proteomic Analysis Reveals AMPKα‐Mediated Antiandrogen Resistance via Ferroptosis Suppression in Prostate Cancer
Authors: Masaki Shiota, Tokiyoshi Tanegashima, Takahiko Hajime, Miho Ushijima, Shigehiro Tsukahara, Shohei Nagakawa, Satoshi Kobayashi, Takashi Matsumoto, Masatoshi Eto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetastatic prostate cancer often develops resistance to second‐generation androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) such as enzalutamide and darolutamide, limiting their long‐term efficacy. In this study, we applied the high‐precision absolute quantitative proteomics platform iMPAQT to comprehensively profile protein expression changes in multiple prostate cancer cell lines and their ARPI‐resistant derivatives. Our analysis revealed consistent dysregulation of ferroptosis suppressor pathway alongside elevated phosphorylation of AMPKα signaling in resistant cells. While antiandrogen treatment increased lipid peroxidation in parental cells, resistant cells adapted by upregulating key ferroptosis‐regulating proteins including FTH1 and GPX4, thereby evading ferroptosis. Functional assays demonstrated that the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 selectively suppressed proliferation of resistant cells more potently than parental cells. Moreover, combination treatment with AMPKα inhibitors enhanced the anti‐proliferative effect of RSL3. These findings suggest that ferroptosis suppression is a critical mechanism underpinning resistance to ARPIs in prostate cancer. Targeting pathways involved in ferroptosis regulation in combination with AMPKα inhibitors holds promise for overcoming therapeutic resistance and improving patient outcomes.Keywords: AMPKα, antiandrogen, ferroptosis, prostate cancer, proteome analysis

Introduction

Metastatic prostate cancer remains a major cause of cancer‐related mortality in men worldwide. In recent years, substantial therapeutic advances have been achieved through the development of second‐generation androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) including abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide, in combination with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) [1, 2]. Furthermore, triplet therapy integrating docetaxel chemotherapy with darolutamide has further improved patient outcomes [3]. Despite these advances, most patients with metastatic prostate cancer ultimately develop treatment resistance, leading to disease progression and poor prognosis. Elucidating and overcoming the mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance therefore remains a critical unmet clinical need.

Our and others' previous works have identified multiple resistance mechanisms in prostate cancer, particularly those associated with persistent androgen receptor (AR) signaling. These include intratumoral androgen biosynthesis, alterations in AR‐related genes, and the emergence of AR splice variants [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. While AR‐dependent mechanisms account for a proportion of resistant cases, some patients display resistance in the absence of these features, suggesting the existence of additional uncharacterized mechanisms that were unidentified by conventional molecular profiling.

Cancer cells, including those of prostate cancer, are known to exhibit distinctive metabolic phenotypes compared with normal cells, exemplified by the Warburg effect. These metabolic alterations are intimately linked to oncogenesis, disease progression, and therapeutic resistance [10]. Despite growing evidence that metabolic reprogramming contributes to therapeutic resistance, the protein‐level mechanisms underlying these alterations remain poorly defined, largely because of technical challenges in the accurate and comprehensive quantification of metabolic enzymes.

Comprehensive characterization of these metabolic alterations requires accurate quantification of metabolic enzymes. However, traditional proteomic approaches relying largely on relative quantification are limited in their ability to determine the absolute abundance of proteins and assess their true biological impact. Recent advances in proteomic technologies have enabled high‐sensitivity, high‐accuracy absolute quantification. As such method, the targeted quantitative proteomic platform iMPAQT was recently developed [11]. This innovative approach allows the absolute quantification of more than 1100 metabolic enzymes, encompassing approximately 90% of all known enzymes involved in human metabolism.

In the present study, we aimed to elucidate how alterations in metabolic enzymes contribute to treatment resistance in prostate cancer by applying this next‐generation quantitative proteomic approach and to identify novel therapeutic targets for future intervention.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Reagents

Human prostate cancer cell lines LNCaP and 22Rv1 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The C4‐2 cell line was kindly provided by Dr. Martin Gleave (Vancouver Prostate Centre, Vancouver, BC, Canada) [12]. Cells were cultured in RPMI−1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37°C. Enzalutamide‐ and darolutamide‐resistant derivatives of LNCaP (LNCaP/MDV, LNCaP/ODM) and C4‐2 (C4‐2/MDV, C4‐2/ODM) cells were generated and maintained as previously described [13, 14]. Enzalutamide‐resistant 22Rv1 cells (22Rv1/MDV) were established as described previously [15]. Darolutamide‐resistant 22Rv1 cells (22Rv1/ODM) were generated by long‐term culture in medium containing gradually increasing concentrations of darolutamide, followed by maintenance in medium supplemented with 10 ng/mL darolutamide (Figure S1).

Enzalutamide (S1250), dorsomorphin (S7840), RSL3 (S8155), and iFSP1 (S9663) were purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA). Darolutamide (HY‐16985) and iGP‐1 (530655) were obtained from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) and Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), respectively.

iMPAQT Assay

The iMPAQT assay was performed as described previously [11, 16]. Briefly, three independent cultures of LNCaP, LNCaP/MDV, LNCaP/ODM, C4‐2, C4‐2/MDV, C4‐2/ODM, 22Rv1, 22Rv1/MDV, and 22Rv1/ODM cells were processed as reported, and 10 μL of the resulting peptide solution was injected into a liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) system. Mass spectrometry data were imported into Skyline Software (University of Washington) for peak selection. Each peptide was monitored for at least three transitions, and any transition with interference from foreign material was excluded. Quantification was performed for peaks with matching retention times and a Ratio Dot Product > 0.9. The peak area ratio of endogenous peptides to (13C₆/15N₄)Arg‐ and (13C₆/15N2)Lys‐labeled peptides (internal standards) was multiplied by the added QconCAT concentration to obtain absolute quantitative values. Quantitatively measured proteins and their corresponding peptide sequences are listed in Table S1.

GSEA‐Based Pathway Analysis

Based on the proteomic results obtained by iMPAQT, we compared protein expression profiles between parental, enzalutamide‐ and darolutamide‐resistant sublines of LNCaP, C4‐2, and 22Rv1 using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [17, 18]. Fifty‐two gene sets, comprising the HALLMARK collection as well as FERROPTOSIS_DRIVER and FERROPTOSIS_SUPPRESSOR sets, were analyzed [19]. Gene members not detected in the proteomic dataset were excluded before enrichment analysis (Table S2). Enriched pathways were extracted, and Venn diagrams were generated to identify common pathway alterations between parental cells and their resistant sublines.

Antibodies and Western Blot Analysis

Antibodies against FTH1 (#4393), GPX4 (#52455), xCT (#15083), Keap1 (#8047), AMPKα (#5832), p‐AMPKα (#2535), LKB1 (#3050), p‐LKB1 (#3482), CaMKK2 (#16810), and p‐CaMKK2 (#35392) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Anti‐PHGDH (14719–1‐AP), anti‐FSP1 (20886–1‐AP), anti‐ACSL4 (22401–1‐AP), anti‐GPD2 (17219–1‐AP), anti‐GCLC (12601–1‐AP), and anti‐DHODH (14877–1‐AP) were obtained from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA). Anti‐p‐Nrf2 (#2073–1) and anti‐Nrf2 (#2073–1) were purchased from Epitomics (Burlingame, CA, USA).

Whole‐cell extracts were prepared as described previously [14, 20]. Signals were visualized using an ECL detection kit (GE Healthcare Bio‐Sciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA) and imaged with an Ez‐Capture MG system (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan).

siRNAs And Transfection

The PRKAA1 siRNA (Silencer Select siRNA) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Prostate cancer cells were transfected with 5 nM of siRNAs using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's protocol.

Measurement of Lipid Peroxidation, Glutathione and Fe2+

Lipid peroxidation was assessed by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione and Fe2+ using the MDA Assay Kit (M496; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan), GSSG/GSH Quantification Kit II (G263; Dojindo) and FerroOrange (F374; Dojindo), respectively, following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, LNCaP cells were seeded, treated the next day with 10 μM enzalutamide or darolutamide for the indicated durations, and processed for MDA, glutathione and Fe2+ measurement. Absorbance was recorded using an ARVO MX plate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). MDA and glutathione concentrations were calculated from a standard curve generated using the provided standard and normalized to cell counts. Data represent at least three independent experiments.

Prognostic Analysis Based on TCGA Gene Expression Data

RNA sequencing data and corresponding clinicopathological information for patients with prostate adenocarcinoma were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas prostate adenocarcinoma (TCGA‐PRAD) cohort [20]. Patients were stratified into high‐ and low‐expression groups according to the median mRNA expression level of each target gene (PRKAA1, PRKAA2, GPX4, FTH1, and FSP1). Recurrence‐free survival (RFS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between groups using the log‐rank test. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cell Proliferation Assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated as described previously [14, 21]. Briefly, 2.5 × 104 prostate cancer cells were seeded in 12‐well plates and treated with the indicated agents. After 72 h, cells were harvested with trypsin and counted using a Beckman Coulter cell counter (Brea, CA, USA). Results were normalized to the respective data at 0 μM and represent the mean of three independent experiments.

Ferroptosis Pathway Alterations in Second‐Generation Antiandrogen–Resistant Cells

We next examined whether second‐generation antiandrogens could induce ferroptosis in prostate cancer cells. As shown in Figure 3A, MDA levels were increased following enzalutamide or darolutamide treatment in LNCaP cells. Consistently, intracellular glutathione levels were decreased after enzalutamide or darolutamide treatment while Fe2+ levels were increased, suggesting that decreased intracellular glutathione levels and increased iron accumulation resulted in increased intracellular lipid peroxidation (Figure 3B).In addition, we investigated whether the anticancer effect of antiandrogens was dependent on ferroptosis induction. As shown in Figure 3C, ferroptosis suppression using ferrostatin−1 decreased cell proliferation suppression by antiandrogens.

We then assessed the expression of representative ferroptosis‐related proteins that are directly or indirectly related to ferroptosis in LNCaP, C4‐2, and 22Rv1 cells and their resistant derivatives. Compared with their respective parental cells, FTH1 and FSP1 were upregulated in C4‐2/MDV and 22Rv1/MDV cells, respectively, whereas GPX4 expression was increased in LNCaP/ODM, 22Rv1/MDV, and 22Rv1/ODM cells (Figure 3D). Consistently, FTH1 was upregulated in LNCaP/MDV, LNCaP/ODM, C4‐2/MDV, and 22Rv1/ODM by iMPAQT assay (Figure S4). Of note, p‐AMPKα, but not p‐Nrf2, was consistently upregulated in enzalutamide‐ and darolutamide‐resistant cells across all three lines.

Because CAMKK2 and LKB1 are known regulators of AMPKα phosphorylation at Thr172 [24], we analyzed their phosphorylation status. As shown in Figure 3E, p‐CAMKK2 and p‐LKB1 levels were elevated in most resistant sublines, except LNCaP/MDV and C4‐2/ODM, suggesting that alternative kinases may be responsible for AMPKα phosphorylation in these lines.

Furthermore, we have explored the clinical outcomes of expression of ferroptosis‐related genes including PRKAA1, PRKAA2, GPX4, FTH1, and FSP1 using TCGA database. Kaplan–Meier analysis of TCGA‐PRAD cohort demonstrated that higher expression of GPX4 and FSP1 was significantly associated with shorter RFS (p = 0.0338 and p = 0.0470, respectively; Figure 4). In contrast, PRKAA1, PRKAA2, and FTH1 expression levels were not significantly associated with RFS.

Therapeutic Effects of Ferroptosis Inducers Alone and in Combination With AMPKα Inhibitors

Finally, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis suppression in second‐generation antiandrogen–resistant cells. Various ferroptosis inducers were tested, including the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3, FSP1/AIFM2 inhibitor iFSP1, mitochondrial GPDH inhibitor iGP−1, and AMPKα inhibitors BAY‐3827 and dorsomorphin. In LNCaP and LNCaP/MDV cells, only RSL3 suppressed proliferation more potently in the resistant subline (Figures 5A and S5).

We then assessed the effects of RSL3 in other parental and resistant cells. Strikingly, RSL3 inhibited proliferation more potently in all resistant sublines than in their parental counterparts (Figure 5A). To explore potential combination strategies, we tested AMPKα inhibitors together with RSL3. Both BAY‐3827 and dorsomorphin augmented effects with RSL3 in suppressing proliferation of LNCaP, LNCaP/MDV, and 22Rv1/ODM cells (Figure 5B). A consistent result was obtained after PRKAA1 knockdown for AMPKα using siRNA in LNCaP cells (Figure 5C).

Discussion

In this study, we employed an advanced absolute quantitative proteomics platform, iMPAQT, to investigate molecular mechanisms underlying resistance to second‐generation antiandrogens, including enzalutamide and darolutamide, in prostate cancer. Proteomic profiling revealed distinct metabolic alterations in resistant sublines across multiple cell models, with consistent dysregulation of ferroptosis‐related pathways emerging as a hallmark of therapeutic resistance.

Specifically, the “FERROPTOSIS_SUPPRESSOR” pathway was consistently dysregulated in resistant sublines regardless of lineage (LNCaP, C4‐2, and 22Rv1), suggesting a shared mechanism by which prostate cancer cells evade ferroptosis under AR‐targeted therapy. In parental cells, antiandrogen treatment increased lipid peroxidation while suppressing the sex hormone–dependent enzyme MBOAT2, previously shown to induce ferroptosis through phospholipid remodeling in prostate cancer [25]. Resistant cells, however, displayed altered expression of key ferroptosis regulators, including upregulation of FTH1, GPX4 and FSP1, which mitigate oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation to suppress ferroptosis [26, 27]. Although ferroptosis suppressor pathway dysregulation and AMPKα activation were consistently observed across resistant sublines, the expression patterns of individual ferroptosis‐related proteins (e.g., GPX4, FTH1) varied among cell lines, highlighting potential heterogeneity in the molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis resistance. These findings support ferroptosis evasion as a novel contributor to antiandrogen resistance, in line with previous reports showing that AR‐targeting therapies induce oxidative stress and that adaptive responses to this stress promote treatment resistance [21, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Given that oxidative stress drives lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, suppression of ferroptosis may represent a key resistance mechanism to AR‐targeted therapy.

AMPKα signaling emerged as a critical axis in the resistance phenotype. However, its contribution to antiandrogen resistance appears to reflect an adaptive response rather than a direct driving mechanism, as pharmacologic inhibition of AMPKα did not restore sensitivity to antiandrogens (data not shown). Resistant sublines showed widespread upregulation of phosphorylated AMPKα, often accompanied by increased phosphorylation of upstream kinases CaMKK2 and LKB1 [24]. Notably, certain resistant sublines (e.g., LNCaP/MDV and C4‐2/ODM) displayed AMPKα phosphorylation without CaMKK2 or LKB1 activation, implying the involvement of alternative kinases or regulatory inputs. These observations indicate that upstream regulation of AMPKα is heterogeneous among resistant models, underscoring the context‐dependent mechanisms through which AMPKα activation is achieved. Nonetheless, the consistent convergence on AMPKα phosphorylation points to a shared adaptive feature of the resistant phenotype. AMPKα has been implicated in redox balance and metabolic adaptation in stressed cancer cells [33], and energy‐stress–mediated AMPK activation has been shown to inhibit ferroptosis [34]. Thus, AMPKα activation in resistant cells may support ferroptosis resistance by enhancing antioxidant defenses and metabolic flexibility [35]. Collectively, our data suggest that AMPKα signaling modulates ferroptosis susceptibility in prostate cancer, thereby influencing cellular dependence on GPX4 activity.

Previous studies have demonstrated that ARPIs induce intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation through multiple mechanisms [30, 31, 36]. We have recently shown that ARPI treatment promotes intracellular ROS production within peroxisomes via activation of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor signaling [21]. In parallel, ARPIs have been reported to reduce intracellular glutathione levels, thereby limiting GPX4 activity and impairing detoxification of lipid peroxides [31, 36]. Collectively, these findings provide a mechanistic basis for ARPI‐induced accumulation of ROS and lipid ROS and support a model in which ARPI treatment establishes a pro‐oxidant redox environment that increases functional dependence on GPX4 to suppress ferroptosis.

From a therapeutic perspective, targeting the ferroptosis defense system appears promising. Of the ferroptosis inducers tested, the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 selectively suppressed proliferation of resistant cells compared with parental cells, indicating a potential “iron addiction” that can be exploited therapeutically [37]. Although GPX4 expression levels were heterogeneous across resistant models, these findings indicate that resistant cells share a common dependence on iron‐regulated redox homeostasis, rendering them selectively vulnerable to GPX4 inhibition. In contrast, the AMPKα inhibitor alone did not exert a resistant cell–specific antiproliferative effect. However, co‐treatment with RSL3 and AMPKα inhibitors (BAY‐3827 or dorsomorphin) effectively suppressed cell proliferation. Given that ferroptosis evasion is mediated by both GPX4‐dependent and GPX4‐independent pathways, these results suggest that AMPKα inhibition restores susceptibility to GPX4‐targeted ferroptosis induction, revealing a synthetic lethal interaction between AMPKα and GPX4 inhibition. Consistently, recent work has shown that AMPKα inhibition sensitizes cancer cells to GPX4 inhibitor–induced ferroptosis [38].

Although GPX4 inhibition selectively suppressed the proliferation of resistant cells, the lack of ferroptosis rescue experiments constitutes a limitation of the present study. Moreover, despite the conceptual appeal of ferroptosis‐based therapeutic strategies, their clinical translation will require careful evaluation of systemic toxicity and therapeutic selectivity.

Collectively, our findings underscore the role of metabolic plasticity and oxidative stress regulation in prostate cancer cells resistant to second‐generation antiandrogens. By integrating high‐resolution proteomics with functional assays, we identified ferroptosis suppression as a critical mechanism underpinning resistance to ARPIs in prostate cancer. Therapeutic strategies that target ferroptosis suppression, particularly in combination with AMPKα inhibition, may offer a promising approach to overcoming treatment resistance and improving patient outcomes (Figure 6).

Antiandrogen‐resistant prostate cancer cells acquire ferroptosis resistance through AMPKα activation and upregulation of ferroptosis suppressor pathways. This adaptive state creates a therapeutic vulnerability, as GPX4 inhibition, particularly in combination with AMPKα inhibition, effectively suppresses the growth of resistant cells.
