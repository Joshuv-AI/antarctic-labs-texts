Accelerated biological aging, genetic susceptibility, and the risk of peripheral arterial disease: Evidence from the UK Biobank cohort study
Authors: Wenkai Zhang, Peng Qiu, Chenshu Li, Ruihua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral artery disease (PAD) is a condition in which atherosclerosis causes narrowing or blockage of the peripheral arteries, leading to insufficient blood supply to the limbs. Although accelerated biological aging has been recognized as a driving factor in the development and progression of many age-related diseases, its exact role in the risk of developing PAD remains unclear. This study investigated the relationship of biological aging and genetic susceptibility with the risk of incident PAD.MethodsThis prospective study included 403,780 UK Biobank participants free of PAD at baseline. Biological age was estimated from routine clinical biomarkers using the Klemera-Doubal method (KDM-BA) and PhenoAge algorithms. BAA was defined as the residual from regression of biological age on chronological age. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for incident PAD after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates. A polygenic risk score (PRS) was constructed to evaluate genetic predisposition to PAD. We also examined the interaction between genetic susceptibility and accelerated biological aging in relation to PAD risk.ResultsOur findings indicated that accelerated biological aging was significantly associated with an increased risk of PAD, whether assessed by the KDM residual (HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.18–1.24) or the PhenoAge residual (HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.31–1.37). Furthermore, joint association analysis showed that participants with both accelerated biological aging and high genetic risk had the greatest risk of PAD (KDMAge: HR 2.07, 95% CI 1.83–2.34; PhenoAge: HR 6.14, 95% CI 5.30–7.12). A significant additive interaction was also observed between high genetic risk and accelerated biological aging as measured by PhenoAge.ConclusionAccelerated biological ageing were independently and jointly associated with incident PAD. This finding support the potential value of integrating biological ageing metrics into PAD risk assessment.

Introduction

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) affects more than 230 million adults worldwide and is becoming an important component of the global cardiovascular burden [[1], [2], [3]]. Although PAD is uncommon before 50 years of age, its prevalence rises sharply in older adults and is associated with higher risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, limb ischemia, amputation and death [2]. As a result, PAD leads to substantial disability, reduced quality of life and increasing health-care costs, making early risk assessment and prevention a public-health priority [4,5].

Chronological age (CA) is one of the strongest and most consistent risk factors for PAD, and older age is routinely used for risk stratification in clinical practice [6]. However, people of the same CA often differ greatly in organ reserve, inflammation, metabolic status and frailty. CA alone cannot capture this biological heterogeneity of ageing [7]. Some individuals appear “biologically older” than their peers and may develop age-related diseases earlier, whereas others stay relatively “biologically younger” despite similar CA [8].

To better describe this variability, the concept of biological age (BA) has been proposed. BA aims to reflect the overall physiological state of multiple organ systems and has been shown to predict morbidity and mortality more accurately than CA [9]. In recent years, several blood-based algorithms, such as Klemera–Doubal biological age (KDM-BA) and PhenoAge, have been developed and validated in large cohorts [10]. When an BA exceeds the expected value for their CA, this discrepancy is commonly referred to as biological age acceleration (BAA), which reflects a state of accelerated aging. Previous studies have shown that higher levels of BAA are significantly associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all-cause mortality [8]. Although chronological age has been widely established as an important risk factor for PAD, BAA, as an indicator of an individual's true physiological aging status, may have unique clinical value in identifying people who are chronologically younger but already at potential risk for PAD. However, it remains unclear whether biomarker-based BAA is associated with symptomatic PAD and whether this association follows a specific dose-response pattern.

In parallel, genetic predisposition also contributes to PAD risk. Polygenic risk scores (PRS), which aggregate the effects of multiple susceptibility variants identified in genome-wide association studies, can identify individuals at high genetic risk for PAD [11]. Yet it is not known whether the impact of BAA on PAD is similar across levels of genetic susceptibility, or whether BAA and genetic risk act jointly or interact on the PAD risk scale.

Material and methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large, prospective, population-based cohort that enrolled 502,536 adults aged 37-73 years across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010 [12]. At baseline, participants completed a touchscreen questionnaire, underwent a nurse-led interview, and received standardized physical measurements; biological specimens were collected for biomarker assays and genotyping. Further details of the cohort design are available on the UK Biobank website and in previous publications [12]. The study was approved by the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (Manchester, UK).

This study was a secondary analysis of existing UK Biobank data and did not involve new data collection from participants. UK Biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (Manchester, UK), and all participants provided written informed consent at recruitment. The present analysis was conducted under UK Biobank application 88192.

Because the main analyses required complete data on biological age biomarkers, covariates, and genetic information, a complete-case analytic sample was used.Fig. 1 shows the datasets constructed. Evaluation of biological age acceleration

We used two well-validated biological age algorithms: the KDM-BA and PhenoAge (additional details are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Materials) [13]. PhenoAge was derived from nine routine blood measures: albumin, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, C-reactive protein, glucose, mean corpuscular volume, red cell distribution width, white blood cell count, and lymphocyte percentage [14]. KDM-BA was based on forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), systolic blood pressure, and six biochemical markers: alkaline phosphatase, total cholesterol, albumin, creatinine, C-reactive protein, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) [15]. Detailed formulas and the corresponding UK Biobank field codes have been published previously [16]. We calculated individual KDM-BA and PhenoAge values using the BioAge R package for subsequent analyses.

We regressed KDM-BA and PhenoAge on CA, and the resulting residuals were used as measures of BAA. These residuals were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) and categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4), with higher values indicating greater biological age acceleration.

Outcome

Incident PAD was ascertained using multiple UK Biobank data sources, including ICD-9/ICD-10 diagnosis codes, OPCS-4 procedure codes, and self-reported illness information [17]. The full code list is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Polygenic risk score calculation

Imputed genotype data were obtained from the UK Biobank. Details of SNP genotyping, imputation, and quality-control procedures have been reported previously [18]. PAD-associated SNPs were selected from a published genome-wide association study [17]. We analyzed 18 independent SNPs (r2 < 0.05 or >1000 kb apart) after excluding rs62084752 because it could not be harmonized with the target dataset. These SNPs showed significant associations with PAD in a multi-ancestry cohort of 31,307 PAD cases and 211,753 controls, with minor allele frequency >0.05 and P < 1 × 10^-5. Details of the selected SNPs are provided in Supplementary Table 2. A weighted PRS was constructed from these SNPs; additional methodological details are provided in Supplementary Table 2. According to PRS tertiles, participants were classified as having low, intermediate, or high genetic risk.

Assessment of covariate information

Information on age, sex, and the Townsend Deprivation Index was obtained from local National Health Service (NHS) primary care trust registries before UK Biobank enrolment. Body mass index was categorized as underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 to <25 kg/m2), overweight (25 to <30 kg/m2), and obese (≥30 kg/m2). Smoking status and alcohol use were collected by touchscreen questionnaire and classified as never, previous, or current. Educational attainment was dichotomized as college/university degree versus other qualifications. Baseline cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and diabetes were defined using self-report and linked medical records. In addition, participants with systolic blood pressure≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure≥90 mmHg at enrolment were classified as having hypertension. Diabetes was defined as haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)≥48 mmol/mol (6.5%), self-report, or insulin use.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, as appropriate, and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables.

Between-group differences were assessed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Student's t-test or Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

We first estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for incident PAD per 1-SD increase in each BAA metric using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. We then categorized the standardized KDM and PhenoAge residuals into quartiles (Q1-Q4) to further evaluate the associations between BAA and PAD risk. We fitted progressively adjusted models: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for BMI category, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, Townsend deprivation index, and educational attainment; and Model 3 further adjusted for baseline hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes. Potential non-linear relationships between BAA and PAD risk were explored using restricted cubic spline models.

We subsequently stratified analyses by PRS tertiles (low, intermediate, and high). Within each stratum, HRs for BAA quartiles were estimated using the fully adjusted model. Multiplicative interaction was evaluated by including cross-product terms and using Wald tests. Additive interaction was assessed with the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and synergy index (S). We constructed joint-exposure variables for BAA and PRS to estimate their combined associations with PAD and calculated population-attributable fractions (PAFs) to estimate the potential public-health relevance of these exposures under model assumptions. Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for lipid-lowering, antihypertensive, and glucose-lowering therapy, excluded participants who developed PAD within the first 2 years of follow-up, and censored follow-up on December 31, 2019, before the COVID-19 pandemic. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1) and Stata (version 18.0).

Genetic susceptibility and population-attributable fractions

Higher genetic susceptibility, as captured by the PAD PRS, was also associated with increased PAD risk. Compared with participants in the lowest PRS quartile, those in the highest quartile had a 36% higher risk of PAD (HR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.28–1.44; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 6).

Population-attributable fraction analyses suggested that 10.46% (95% CI 8.67–12.25) of PAD incidence could be attributed to high KDM residual and 19.26% (95% CI 7.21–21.32) to high PhenoAge residual, whereas the PAF for high genetic risk was 7.37% (95% CI 5.57–9.17) (Supplementary Table 7). Across PRS strata, the PAFs for PhenoAge residual were consistently larger than those for the KDM residual (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort, higher biological age acceleration was consistently associated with a greater risk of incident PAD, independent of chronological age and conventional PAD risk factors. The association was observed for both KDM and PhenoAge-derived BAA, showed a dose-response pattern, and was stronger for PhenoAge. In addition, genetic susceptibility further increased PAD risk: participants with both high BAA and high PRS had the highest event rates, and additive interaction analyses suggested that their joint association exceeded the sum of their individual associations. Taken together, these findings suggest that biological ageing captures clinically relevant vascular vulnerability that is not fully reflected by chronological age alone.

Growing evidence indicates that ageing is closely linked to vascular disease [19,20], but chronological age is an imperfect surrogate for the biological processes that drive vascular decline. Biological ageing is shaped by both genetic and environmental influences and may contribute to disease through multiple pathways. [21,22]. Consistent with this view, prior studies have shown that physiological ageing metrics provide information beyond chronological age, including associations of epigenetic age acceleration with coronary heart disease and heart failure, proteomic arterial “organ age” with arterial diseases, and age-related epigenetic heterogeneity within atherosclerotic vessel walls [[23], [24], [25]]. Against this background, our findings extend the literature by showing that biomarker-derived biological age acceleration was independently associated with incident PAD, with stronger associations for PhenoAge than for KDM. Because KDM-BA and PhenoAge integrate information across multiple physiological systems, they may better capture the multisystem dysregulation that precedes clinically recognized PAD than chronological age alone. Our results also complement recent work on polygenic risk in PAD. Whereas PRS reflects inherited susceptibility, BAA may capture a more proximal physiological state through which genetic, metabolic, inflammatory, and environmental influences are biologically expressed. This framework may help explain why participants with both high BAA and high genetic risk had the greatest PAD risk in our study.

The clinical relevance of our findings lies primarily in risk enrichment and earlier case finding rather than in changing procedural decision-making. Contemporary PAD guidelines recommend resting ankle-brachial index (ABI) testing in individuals at increased risk and base decisions regarding vascular imaging and revascularization on clinical presentation, hemodynamic assessment, symptom burden, response to guideline-directed medical therapy and exercise, and the presence of chronic limb-threatening ischemia, rather than on biomarker profiles alone [26,27]. In this context, a high BAA should not be interpreted as a diagnostic marker of established PAD or as an indication for vascular imaging or revascularization by itself. Rather, it may be more appropriately viewed as a clinically accessible risk-enrichment signal. For clinicians, this means that in patients whose chronological age or symptoms do not obviously suggest PAD, a markedly elevated BAA—particularly PhenoAge acceleration—may reasonably support a lower threshold for careful symptom assessment, pulse examination, and ABI-based evaluation. This may be especially relevant in patients with subtle or atypical leg symptoms that might otherwise be attributed to musculoskeletal disease, deconditioning, or usual ageing.

Our findings also have implications for preventive care. The present data do not support treating BAA itself as a therapeutic target, nor do they justify using BAA to guide revascularization strategy. Instead, high BAA may help identify individuals in whom more rigorous implementation of guideline-directed preventive therapy is warranted, including smoking cessation, healthy diet, structured exercise therapy, lipid-lowering treatment, blood pressure and glycaemic control, and evidence-based antithrombotic therapy when otherwise clinically indicated [26,27]. Lifestyle factors and co-existing cardiometabolic diseases should also be considered when interpreting the association between BAA and PAD. Unhealthy lifestyle patterns, including smoking, poor diet quality, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol consumption, sleep disruption, and psychosocial stress, may accelerate biological aging through chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, and vascular injury, thereby increasing PAD risk. Conversely, healthy lifestyle patterns and good diet quality may contribute to healthier aging, lower biological age acceleration, and vascular risk reduction. Because the associations remained after adjustment for major lifestyle-related and clinical factors, BAA may be viewed as an integrative marker of cumulative physiological burden that complements, rather than replaces, established modifiable risk factors.

Several limitations should also be emphasized when interpreting these findings. First, this was an observational study; therefore, despite the prospective design, causality cannot be inferred, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Second, 94.5% of participants were White and of European ancestry, which limits generalizability to other populations. Third, KDM and PhenoAge residuals were derived from biomarkers measured only once at baseline, precluding assessment of whether longitudinal changes in BAA provide additional prognostic information. In addition, because the main analyses were based on participants with complete data on biological age biomarkers, covariates, and genetic information, selection bias may have been introduced if participants with missing data differed systematically from those included in the analytic sample. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted in the context of this complete-case design. Fourth, the later period of follow-up overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic. SARS-CoV-2 infection may contribute to vascular risk through systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability, and thrombotic complications, and pandemic-related changes in health-care access may have influenced the diagnosis or ascertainment of PAD events. However, biological age acceleration was assessed at baseline, long before the pandemic, and the associations were materially unchanged in a sensitivity analysis censoring follow-up on December 31, 2019. Nevertheless, because individual-level data on SARS-CoV-2 infection status, infection severity, vaccination status, and post-infection inflammatory status were not available, residual influence of the pandemic cannot be completely excluded. For these reasons, our findings are not yet sufficient to support incorporation of BAA into formal PAD guidelines. Before clinical implementation, further work is needed to externally validate these associations in more diverse populations, determine whether adding BAA to established PAD risk models improves discrimination, calibration, and clinically meaningful reclassification, and test whether repeated BAA measurements track disease progression or treatment response. Ultimately, intervention studies will be needed to establish whether BAA-guided prevention strategies can improve outcomes, particularly in individuals with high genetic susceptibility.

Conclusion

This prospective cohort study shows that biological age acceleration (BAA), derived from KDM and PhenoAge residuals, is associated with a higher risk of PAD. The risk of PAD also increases across genetic-risk strata from low to high. We observed a significant additive interaction and joint effect between BAA and genetic risk. The PhenoAge residual had the greatest relative contribution, as reflected by the highest PAF. KDM and PhenoAge residuals may serve as practical markers to identify individuals susceptible to PAD; when combined with clinical characteristics, they could help refine strategies for PAD prevention and treatment.
