Joint prevention and control, cross-regional governance: An evolutionary game analysis of fiscal environmental protection expenditure strategies
Authors: Lidao He, Shitao Quan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The ability to form a collaborative governance alliance is crucial to the in-depth promotion of pollution prevention and control. Starting from the perspective of regional development imbalance, this paper uses an evolutionary game model to explore how initial strategy choice, game order, financial and environmental protection expenditure efficiency, and total factor productivity affect the formation and stabilization of the cooperative alliance of “joint prevention and control, cross-regional governance”. The study shows that strategic interactions between governments can lead to “cooperative” or “collusive” outcomes and that game order and initial strategy play a key role in the formation of these outcomes. In addition, changes in parameters such as fiscal and environmental expenditure efficiency and total factor productivity can greatly affect the evolutionary path and the speed of convergence. Particularly in less developed regions, fiscal and environmental spending efficiency plays a crucial role in promoting government “cooperation”, which tends to be weakened by increases in total factor productivity. In contrast, economically developed regions tend to favor “cooperation” and “investment” strategies. This study provides valuable policy recommendations for enhancing interregional cooperation in environmental governance.

Introduction

With the significant increase in China's industrialization and urbanization levels, environmental pollution has gradually come to the fore, becoming an important constraint in promoting China's high-quality development. In fact, since entering the new era, the Party and the country have been paying attention to the issue of environmental pollution control [1], from “green water and green mountains are golden mountains” to the “five-in-one” general layout of “ecological civilization construction”. Ecological civilization construction” overall layout. From “green mountains are golden mountains” to the “five-in-one” overall layout of “ecological civilization construction”, and then to the 20th Party Congress, which once again explicitly proposed that “Deeply promote the prevention and control of environmental pollution”, all reflect the determination of environmental pollution control. Unfortunately, due to the existence of pollutant spillover, the governance effect under the territorial governance model has been unsatisfactory. To address this challenge, in recent years, the state has gradually emphasized cross-regional joint prevention and control governance [2]. For example, the 2020 Guiding Opinions on Building a Modern Environmental Governance System advocates cross-regional and cross-basin pollution prevention and control. However, due to the existence of regional economic development imbalance, less developed regions may prioritize economic development and reduce investment in environmental governance, thus making it difficult or unstable to form a “cooperative governance alliance”. Therefore, how to promote the formation and stability of the “joint prevention and control, cross-regional governance” cooperative alliance is an issue that needs to be urgently addressed.

Since the in-depth implementation of China's tax reform in 1994, the authority and resource control of local governments in economic management has significantly increased, and this change has profoundly affected the pattern of environmental governance, highlighting the critical importance of local government behavior for environmental pollution management under the decentralized system [3]. Against this backdrop, academics have extensively explored the contribution of fiscal and environmental protection expenditures to the effectiveness of emission reduction, the effectiveness of environmental regulatory policies [4,5], and the complex strategic interactions among governments, firms, and the public [6,7]. However, a relatively neglected area is the strategic interaction between local governments in terms of financial and environmental expenditures, a dimension that is crucial for understanding the synergistic mechanisms and effectiveness of environmental governance. Based on this, this paper innovatively focuses on the strategic choices of local governments in the area of financial and environmental expenditures. By constructing a fine-grained evolutionary game model, we analyze the trade-offs and choices of local governments at different levels of economic development between the dual objectives of “environmental governance” and “economic development”. This study aims to reveal how these strategic choices affect the formation process and stability of collaborative governance alliances among local governments, so as to provide new theoretical perspectives and practical guidance for optimizing environmental governance policies and promoting inter-regional green collaborative development. It is found that “cooperation” and “collusion” are the final outcomes of the interaction between intergovernmental fiscal and environmental spending strategies, and the order of the game and the initial strategic choices play a key role in the formation of these outcomes. In addition, changes in parameters such as fiscal and environmental expenditure efficiency and total factor productivity significantly affect the evolution path and the speed of convergence. Especially in less developed regions, the efficiency of fiscal and environmental expenditures plays a crucial role in promoting government “cooperation”, which is often undermined by increases in total factor productivity. In contrast, economically developed regions show a preference for “cooperation” and “investment” strategies. We expect that the results of this study will not only expand the boundaries of the field but also contribute to a deeper understanding of the complex interactions between local governments in environmental governance. The possible marginal contributions of this paper are: first, to construct a two-dimensional evolutionary game model from regional heterogeneity to discuss the effects of fiscal and environmental expenditure efficiency and total factor productivity on the formation and stability of governance coalitions; and second, to discuss the effects of different initial government strategy choices on the speed of coalition formation and the stability of coalition formation.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: in the second part, a comprehensive literature review is conducted to systematically sort out and summarize the research results and insights of the predecessors in the related fields, to lay a solid theoretical foundation and academic background for this study; in the third part, the model is constructed, which contains the theoretical background and research hypotheses; in the fourth part, the model is analyzed, which is mainly involved in the analysis of the evolutionary strategy and the analysis of the stability of the system; in the fifth part, we will construct a numerical model to show the effect of the practical application of the model. In the fifth part, in order to more intuitively show the practical application effect of the model, we will construct a numerical model, and through simulating the changes of different parameters, we will examine in depth the specific effects of these changes on the stability of the system and the speed of convergence; in the sixth part, we will draw conclusions and policy recommendations.

Literature review

With the increasing severity of global environmental problems, environmental sustainability has become a focus of attention for academics and policy makers, and scholars have studied environmental sustainability from different perspectives. For example, the impacts of green investment [8], green innovation [9], human capital [10,11], natural resources [8,10], low-carbon energy [12], and green finance [13] on environmental sustainability have been examined. However, achieving environmental sustainability requires safeguarding and enhancing environmental quality, and effective pollution management is an important means to achieve this goal. From the perspective of pollution governance, two types of current literature are more closely related to this paper.

One type of literature mainly uses spatial econometric modeling [3,[14], [15], [16]], Tobit modeling [17], VAR method [18] and logarithmic mean divergence index method [19] to examine the pollution control effect of fiscal environmental protection expenditures, and four views are formed: viewpoint one holds that fiscal environmental protection expenditures play a significant role in promoting pollution reduction, have a significant pollution control effect, and that increasing government expenditures improves environmental quality [20]; viewpoint two holds that fiscal environmental protection expenditures mainly exert the pollution control effect through an indirect ways to exert pollution control effects, such as indirectly affecting environmental pollution through redistribution of public goods and energy consumption in the supply of services [21,22], or by inducing social capital [23], stimulating technological innovation, etc., to promote regional economic development and improve the regional technological level, thus promoting regional pollution reduction [20]; viewpoint three argues that the environmental governance effect of fiscal environmental protection expenditure includes both direct and indirect effects [15,24,25]; viewpoint Four believes that the relationship between fiscal environmental protection expenditure and environmental pollution is an inverted “U”, that is, environmental pollution first increases with the increase in fiscal environmental protection expenditure, and after reaching a certain scale of fiscal environmental protection expenditure [26], environmental pollution will gradually decrease, which indirectly explains the existence of fiscal environmental protection expenditure pollution control effect; however, some scholars believe that fiscal environmental protection expenditure on the environment governance effect can not be generalized to say whether it is positive or negative, but should be analyzed from the period, and conclude that the environmental governance effect of fiscal environmental protection expenditure presents opposite effects in the short and long term [27]; in addition, some scholars believe that although fiscal environmental protection expenditure is crucial to ecological protection and environmental governance, fiscal environmental protection expenditure does not play its due role [3,14].

Another type of literature, which is more closely related to this paper, employs the game theory approach to study the interaction or behavioral strategies among the participating subjects in environmental governance. Considering the spillover of pollutants, one approach is to discuss the strategic interactions among the participating subjects in different scenarios by constructing a cross-border pollution game model, e.g., by constructing a cross-border pollution game model, the interaction of green subsidies [28], clean technologies [29], joint implementation mechanisms [30], government foresight [31], emissions permit trading, and learning-by-doing [32] how to influence the optimal abatement strategy choices of game-participants. Another approach is to construct two- or three-dimensional game models based on vertical organizational relationships to examine the strategic interactions among the game-participating subjects. For example, 1) by constructing a two-dimensional or three-dimensional evolutionary game between the central government and local governments, we examined the interaction between the central government and local governments under the implementation of different environmental policies [33], regulatory strategies [34,35], reward and punishment mechanisms [36], and environmental regulation [5,37] in the case of how governments at all levels choose their environmental governance strategies; 2) by constructing a game model between governments and enterprises, we examined the combination of environmental taxes and fees, government subsidies, environmental regulations, government spending preferences, policy incentives, and government rewards and penalties policies [5,[38], [39], [40], [41], [42]] and other key factors on the strategy choice of participating subjects; 3) Since the public also plays an important role in the process of environmental pollution management [43], some studies have introduced the term “public” to the term “environmental management”. Some studies have introduced “the public” as a participatory subject. In this context, numerous studies have delved into the multifaceted dynamics of environmental regulation strategies. For instance, Duan et al. investigated how tax regulations and pollution penalties shape these strategies [44]. Zhou et al. emphasized the critical roles of incentives and penalties in effective environmental governance [45]. Kazemi & Hosseinzadeh explored the strategic behavior of gaming participants in mitigating the greenhouse effect, offering insights into decision-making processes [46]. Moreover, the complex interactions between corporate pollution governance, government regulation, and public participation have been extensively studied. Y. Chen et al. analyzed corporate strategies and public engagement [47], Kou et al. examined the dynamics between governmental and corporate interactions [7], and C. Wang et al. assessed the broader regulatory landscape's impact on environmental outcomes [6]. Collectively, these studies present a comprehensive view of the diverse approaches and collaborative efforts needed to address environmental challenges, highlighting the importance of strategic, well-regulated, and participatory frameworks.

In summary, regarding the environmental governance effects of fiscal environmental protection expenditures and the strategic interaction between the participating subjects of environmental governance, scholars have conducted comprehensive and in-depth discussions from different perspectives using a variety of methods, and these studies have provided valuable insights into optimizing the pollution governance system and policy tools. However, there are still some shortcomings. First, although scholars have suggested that better environmental governance can be achieved through the “joint prevention and control, cross-regional governance” model, they have yet to shed light on how to effectively promote the formation of such cooperative alliances and what factors may affect their stability. Secondly, in the case of limited financial resources, local governments are often faced with the dilemma of choosing between “economic development” and “environmental governance”, especially due to the existence of regional heterogeneity, the preferences of local governments are different, but the existing research fails to explore the issue from this perspective. However, existing studies have failed to explore the interaction between local governments' fiscal and environmental spending strategies from this perspective. Finally, although many studies have explored the interaction among governments, enterprises and the public by constructing evolutionary game models, it is difficult for these studies to accurately grasp the dynamic evolution of local governments' decision-making behaviors due to the neglect of the initial strategies and game sequences of the game subjects. In order to solve these problems, this paper constructs an evolutionary game model from the perspective of regional heterogeneity to analyze the interaction of fiscal and environmental protection expenditure strategies among local governments and the formation and stability of the collaborative governance coalition, and through numerical simulation, we illustrate the effects of changes in the key variables on the strategic choices of the local governments and the stability of the system.

Model construction

Theoretical background and model assumptions

Theoretical background

To do a good job of environmental pollution management article, we must first clarify the theoretical properties of environmental pollution. As we all know, environmental pollution has a certain negative externality, relying only on the market this “invisible hand” cannot solve the environmental pollution caused by the “market failure” problem, resulting in resource mismatch or inefficiency. At the level of classical economic theory, three measures are usually taken to solve this problem: first, taxation, i.e., the government levies taxes on producers with negative externalities, thus internalizing external costs [48,49]; Secondly, to clarify property rights, the government, as a supervisor, can clearly define the property rights of the polluters and the victims of pollution through negotiation, and use property rights transactions to realize Pareto optimization [50,51],which is also the main connotation of the “Coase Theorem”; Thirdly, increasing government fiscal environmental spending, on the one hand, fiscal environmental spending can directly reduce pollution [15], on the other hand, fiscal environmental spending can reduce pollution by encouraging producers to carry out technological innovation, promoting the production of clean products or the use of clean energy to carry out the reduction of pollution, so as to achieve the goal of pollution control [22]. Although the first two measures can control pollution emissions to a certain extent, the increased costs also inhibit economic development to a certain extent, while the third measure will produce a certain degree of crowding out effect, but the fiscal environmental protection expenditure in reducing pollution at the same time, but also can promote total factor productivity, stimulate economic development, there is a certain degree of economic growth effect, and thus more favored by policymakers. Given that pollutants have a certain spatial spillover effect, and the stronger the spillover, the more obvious the cross-regional governance effect of local fiscal environmental protection expenditure will be [15]. Therefore, local governments should advocate the cooperation alliance of “joint prevention and control, cross-regional management” in the management of pollutants with strong spillover, which not only meets the current practical needs but also is the title of the “Guiding Opinions” which advocates “promoting cross-regional and cross-basin pollution prevention and control”. This is not only in line with the current practical needs but also the right intention of the Guiding Opinions, which advocates “promoting cross-regional and cross-basin pollution prevention and control”.

Model assumptions

Based on the above analysis, it is useful to assume that there are two heterogeneous cities, A and B, whose environmental governance strategies are formulated by Government A and Government B, respectively (in the following, Government A and City A, Government B and City B refer to the same participant.). For example, when Government A chooses the strategy of “investing” in environmental protection expenditure, Government B faces the choice of “investment” or “non-investment” in environmental protection expenditure. In this case, the benefits to City B under different strategy choices are also different: when Government B chooses the “investment” strategy, both City A and City B can obtain more environmental benefits; while when Government B chooses the “non-investment” strategy, not only can City B obtain part of the environmental benefits from Government A's choice of “investment” strategy, but also from Government B's choice of “non-investment” strategy, and City B can obtain part of the environmental benefits from Government A's choice of “investment” strategy. When Government B chooses the “no-investment” strategy, City B can not only get part of the environmental benefits brought by Government A's “investment” strategy but also get the benefits of the environmental protection expenditures invested elsewhere. Of course, Government A, after anticipating the other government's choice of strategy, can also adopt a similar strategy, that is, Government A chooses the “non-investment” strategy to obtain additional benefits and Government B chooses the “investment” strategy to bring part of the environmental governance benefits.

According to the above problem description, a general game model can be constructed. However, the traditional game method usually assumes that the participating subjects are completely rational and the information is completely symmetric [52], which is not consistent with reality. On the one hand, there are often “barriers” between the transmission of information in the region, subject, and across the period, resulting in asymmetric information; on the other hand, it is difficult for the participating subjects to be completely rational due to the asymmetry of information. Therefore, this paper will be based on the limited rationality of the participants, using the evolutionary game method to carry out the analysis. Compared with the traditional game method, evolutionary games not only increase the interaction between actors and improve the exchange and feedback of information but also introduce the concept of time, which makes the game model have dynamic characteristics [53]. This allows different actors to determine their own final strategic choices through continuous trial and error and imitation, and the concept is also applicable to the environmental pollution governance model between local governments. The cooperation model of “joint prevention and control, cross-regional governance” is essentially one of the results of the game between local governments in the process of pollution management. For local governments, the choice of whether to invest in environmental protection expenditures or use the funds to support local economic development is not entirely rational, because of the asymmetry of information and the existence of regional endowment differences. In other words, both local governments cannot reach Nash equilibrium through a single game and need to reach the final stable state through continuous trial and error and imitation. Therefore, this paper seeks the final stable state of strategy interaction between local governments through the evolutionary game model, which can provide a certain theoretical basis for promoting the cooperation alliance of “joint prevention and control, cross-regional governance” to give better play to the effect of environmental governance. Conclusions and policy recommendations

This paper analyzes the strategic interaction of fiscal environmental protection expenditures between neighboring governments based on the evolutionary game model and explores the changes in the direction, path, and convergence speed of the local government's fiscal environmental protection expenditure strategy choices when the relevant parameters change. The findings from this study provide several new insights that both align with and expand upon the existing literature in the field of environmental governance and evolutionary game theory. The main areas of interest are the following.(1)Cooperation or Collusion as End Results. The study's identification of “cooperation” and “collusion” as potential steady states is consistent with earlier research that explores strategic interactions between local governments. The idea that governments might either collaborate on environmental investment or tacitly agree to avoid such investments has been discussed in previous works, particularly in the context of the “tragedy of the commons” and collective action problems. However, this study's explicit modeling of these outcomes as evolutionary steady states provides a more structured and dynamic understanding of how these outcomes might emerge over time, adding depth to the theoretical framework of regional environmental governance.(2)Impact of Game Order and Initial Strategies. The analysis of how the order of play and initial strategies influence the evolutionary outcome is a significant contribution to the literature. Previous studies have often assumed static strategies or focused on equilibrium outcomes without considering the path dependency introduced by initial conditions. This study highlights the importance of initial strategy choices and their role in determining the speed and direction of convergence towards equilibrium, offering a more nuanced understanding of strategic decision-making processes in environmental governance.(3)The impact of Total Factor Productivity (TFP). The findings of this study regarding the differing effects of TFP on developed and underdeveloped regions are particularly novel. While the existing literature has recognized the importance of TFP for environmental outcomes [56], this study goes further by demonstrating that improving efficiency in underdeveloped regions not only benefits those regions but also affects broader regional cooperation dynamics. The negative impact of increased TFP on regional cooperation, particularly the asymmetric effects on developed and underdeveloped regions, is also a unique contribution. This stands in stark contrast to the traditional view that productivity improvements are universally beneficial, highlighting the potential trade-offs between economic growth and environmental cooperation.

The study's identification of “cooperation” and “collusion” as potential steady states is consistent with earlier research that explores strategic interactions between local governments. The idea that governments might either collaborate on environmental investment or tacitly agree to avoid such investments has been discussed in previous works, particularly in the context of the “tragedy of the commons” and collective action problems. However, this study's explicit modeling of these outcomes as evolutionary steady states provides a more structured and dynamic understanding of how these outcomes might emerge over time, adding depth to the theoretical framework of regional environmental governance.

Overall, this study deepens the understanding of regional environmental governance by combining dynamic evolutionary game theory with shadow factors such as the efficiency of fiscal and environmental expenditures and total factor productivity. It explores in more detail how initial conditions, strategic interactions, and regional differences affect the formation of environmental cooperative alliances. These findings provide valuable insights for policymakers aiming to promote cross-regional environmental cooperation and balance economic development and environmental sustainability. In addition, unlike the existing literature, which usually views environmental governance as a static game with predetermined strategies, this study emphasizes the dynamic and evolutionary nature of strategic decisions. It also introduces the notion that productivity gains may hinder rather than help regional cooperation in environmental governance, which challenges the conventional view that economic growth and environmental protection always go hand in hand. This fresh perspective on the role of total factor productivity and the efficiency of fiscal and environmental spending in shaping environmental outcomes opens up new avenues for research and policymaking.

Policy recommendations

Based on the above conclusions, the local government in the fiscal environmental protection expenditure as the main means of environmental governance strategy selection, to promote the “joint prevention and control, cross-regional governance” cooperation alliance of effective play, it is recommended.(1)Enhance Fiscal and Environmental Expenditure Efficiency. Governments, particularly in underdeveloped regions, should prioritize improving the efficiency of fiscal and environmental protection expenditures. This can be achieved by optimizing resource allocation and ensuring that funds are used effectively for environmental projects. By increasing efficiency, underdeveloped regions can contribute more effectively to regional cooperation efforts, thereby strengthening the overall governance alliance.(2)Foster Asymmetrical Cooperation Mechanisms. Given the asymmetric effects of TFP on developed and underdeveloped regions, policies should be designed to account for these differences. This could involve creating flexible cooperation frameworks that allow for differentiated responsibilities and resource contributions based on the economic and environmental capacities of each region. Such tailored approaches can help mitigate the negative impacts of TFP disparities on regional cooperation.(3)Promote Incentive Structures for Environmental Investment. To counteract the tendency of regions to prioritize economic growth over environmental protection, especially in less developed areas, governments should implement incentive structures that encourage environmental investment. This could include financial subsidies, tax breaks, or rewards for regions that demonstrate strong commitment to environmental sustainability and cross-regional collaboration.(4)Strengthen Intergovernmental Coordination and Communication. Establishing formal channels for communication and coordination among neighboring governments is essential for promoting cooperation. Regular meetings, joint task forces, and shared platforms for data and information exchange can help align strategies and enhance mutual trust, making it easier to form and maintain cooperative alliances.

Although this paper analyzes the strategic interactions between local governments by constructing an evolutionary game model, which provides a solid theoretical basis for promoting the formation of the cooperative alliance of “joint prevention and control, cross-regional governance”, this analytical framework still has certain limitations and room for expansion. On the one hand, the current model mainly focuses on the local equilibrium discussion between the two local governments, and has not yet fully considered the comprehensive influence of key external factors, such as central government regulation, and public and media monitoring, which often play an important role in the actual environmental governance process. On the other hand, there is still room for optimization in parameter setting. Although the paper recognizes the existence of differences in the overall strengths of local governments, it does not explore in depth how these relative differences affect the strategic choices of the participants and the stability of the system. In order to overcome these limitations and further enhance the comprehensiveness and accuracy of the study, the research team plans to systematically optimize and expand the model in future work. Specifically, the team will endeavor to construct an analytical framework that is closer to the real situation, not only taking into account the policy orientation and regulatory mechanism of the central government, but also considering the monitoring power of the public and the media as an important variable, in order to more comprehensively reflect the multiple interactions and influencing mechanisms in environmental governance. In addition, in-depth analyses of the effects of relative changes in parameters will be strengthened, especially the dynamic effects of differences in the comprehensive strengths of local governments, with a view to more accurately grasping the behavioral characteristics of environmental governance, and providing strong theoretical support and practical guidance for local governments to formulate more scientific, efficient and forward-looking cooperative governance strategies.
