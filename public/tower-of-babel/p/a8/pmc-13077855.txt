Secondary retrieval and complication management for failed separated instrument removal in the apical third in curved root canals: a case series
Authors: Mei Fu, Jia Guo, Fengting Wang, Benxiang Hou, Chen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeparated instruments in the apical third of the infected root canals can lead to persistent periapical lesions. Retrieval requires appropriate preoperative assessment and a skilled technique, especially in cases of separated instruments in the apical third of curved root canals. Otherwise, retrieval will lead to removal failure and concomitant complications.Case presentationHerein, we report two cases of separated instrument retrieval failure with complications due to inadequate preoperative assessment and an incorrect technical approach. In these cases, we re-evaluated the feasibility of the secondary separated instrument retrieval, including the root canal curvature, type and length of instruments, and thickness of the root canal wall inside the curvature and around the top of the separated instrument. In addition, we describe the technical approach for determining the clasping zone of the separated instrument.ConclusionsThese cases suggest that preoperative evaluation and correct technical approach are crucial. Preventing complications is easier and more important than their management. Once removal fails, it becomes more complex and risky to remove the separated instruments. This case series offers insights into how to manage secondary retrieval, demonstrating the appropriate preoperative reassessment and technical approach.

Introduction

A separated instrument is regarded as a factor in failed root canal treatment, especially in infected root canals. The success rate of root canal treatment decreased from 89% with periapical lesions to 47% when fractured instruments were retained [1]. Although preventing instrument fracture is important, instrument separation is inevitable because of the limitations of the metal smelting technology [2]. In such cases, the instruments are prone to fracture in the apical third of the root canal around or beyond the canal curvature. However, removing these separated instruments poses a high risk of failure without appropriate preoperative assessment and precise technical approaches [3, 4]. If retrieval fails or complications occur, reassessment and secondary retrieval become more challenging.

Re-assessment is essential for managing such complex cases, including the analysis of possible reasons for previous treatment failure and the balance between the necessity of re-retrieval and the risk of complications. The majority of reasons could be associated with the underestimation of the influence of root canal curvature on retrieval difficulty and technical factors [5, 6]. Determining the reasons for failures or complications may lead to great caution during secondary retrieval. When evaluating retrieval necessity, it is important to re-assess the residual dentin at the root canal curvature and around the top of separated instruments [7, 8]. If the remaining dentin requires an invasive amount sacrificed for secondary retrieval, an appropriate technical approach and precise operation can be performed.

Nonsurgical instrument removal should be the first approach, except when the instrument is mostly extruded beyond the apical foramen, as surgical methods involving root-end resection are more invasive and require substantial dentin removal [9]. The nonsurgical instrument removal includes the use of ultrasonics or special devices, such as the Masserann Kit (Micro-mega), Cancellier Extractor Kit (SybronEndo), Endo Extractor (Brasseler Inc.), Endo Rescue (Komet/Brasseler), Micro-Retrieve & Repair System (Superline NIC Dental), and iRS (Dentsply Tulsa Dental) [3]. These protocols are safer, more conservative in terms of dentin sacrifice, and are more successful. However, an incorrect operation may result in a high risk of perforating the root canal, pushing the separated instrument in a further apical direction, or extruding it beyond the apical foramen.

These complications can lead to difficult retrieval and poor prognosis [4, 10]. There are few reports on the management of these challenging cases. Effective reassessment and conservative root canal preparation during secondary removal are required. This case series describes the management of challenging cases. This indicates the importance of appropriate preoperative evaluation and technical approaches for successful retrieval. Furthermore, we demonstrate how to manage cases of retrieval failure or complications that may predispose the tooth to vertical root fractures.

Case report

Case 1: previous retrieval failure accompanied by perforation

Medical record information

A 15-year-old girl presented to Beijing Stomatological Hospital at Capital Medical University in Beijing, China, for evaluation of the separation of instruments from the distal lingual root canal of tooth #30. The tooth had undergone root canal treatment two years prior; however, the patient reported a persistent chief complaint of “biting tenderness.” The primary clinician discovered that the distal lingual root canal (DL) was missing.

Clinical examination

Clinical examination revealed a cotton ball in #30 and tooth tenderness on percussion (Fig. 1Ai). Tooth #30 exhibited physiological mobility with a probing depth of < 3 mm. Periapical radiography revealed a radiolucent lesion (Fig. 1Aii). Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) showed a separated instrument beyond the curve (canal curvature 65°) and a perforation (approximately 0.5 mm × 1 mm) located at the broken end of the separated instrument (Fig. 1Aiii–vii).

Re-assessment for secondary separated instruments retrieval

First-visit errors: The doctor underestimated the severe curvature in the apical area and incorrectly enlarged the canal, which led to perforation at the outside wall of the curve.

Based on the chief complaint and periapical radiographic findings, the separated instrument needed to be removed. For the reassessment of retrieval feasibility, we revealed that the curved outer side of the DL was overcut; however, the curved inner side was not sacrificed. Thus, CBCT revealed that the minimum remaining dentin thickness in the buccal clasping zone (axial view) was 1.0 mm. This was compared with a safety threshold (perforation risk ~ 0.3 mm [8] + ultrasonic tip diameter ~ 0.3 mm = 0.6 mm), confirming a sufficient thickness (Fig. 1Aviii). It could be cut to correct the removal path and create a clasping zone to release the separated instrument. Nevertheless, it was still very difficult to maintain visibility and accurately cut the clasping zone. Because of the separated instrument length, we performed preoperative simulations using the ultrasonic approach. This method was found to be feasible. Therefore, we proposed a treatment plan for the removal of separated instruments with perforation repair. The patient’s parents provided written informed consent.

Retrieval procedure

We opted for ultrasonic technique to remove the separated instruments. First, we created a false mesial wall so that we could use a rubber dam to isolate the affected teeth. Root enlargement of the separated instrument was also made to satisfy its accessibility using the ultrasonic technique. Due to the perforation, we observed bloody secretions oozing from the root canal. We used 2.5% sodium hypochlorite and a root canal suction device to ensure a clear view.

We then observed the perforation and separated the instrument from the gutta-percha. The clasping zone was located in the semi-circular space around the separated instrument in the buccal region (Fig. 1B, iv, v). Therefore, we pre-bent the ultrasonic tip ET25 (Satelec, France) and gradually cut it along the buccal wall of the separated instrument to release the engagement force (Fig. 1B iv, v). The separated instrument was exposed to a pressure of 1 mm. The clasping zone was reduced and the position was changed. These two spots are shown in Fig. B vii. Therefore, we placed the ultrasonic tip in the engagement area and activated it at a power setting 10–20% higher than that in the preparation phase until the fractured instrument was released and exited the root canal.

Post-treatment for perforation and root canal preparation

To avoid perforation and bypass the ledge, we preflexed 2–3 mm of the top manual file toward the buccal region to explore the initial pathway of the canal and prepared the canal until the manual file. Next, we placed the preflexed nickel-titanium file into the canal until it bypassed the ledge and activated it. Finally, the iRoot BP was used to repair the perforation (Fig. 1C).

Follow up

Re-examination one year later showed no symptoms and increased periapical radiolucent density (Fig. 1E).

Case 2: previous retrieval failure accompanied by extruding the separated instrument partially beyond the apical foramen

Medical record information

A 34-year-old man presented to Beijing Stomatological Hospital for the retrieval of broken instruments from the root canal of tooth #13. The patient reported left-sided occlusal pain while chewing. The patient’s medical history indicated that the instrument was separated from the palatal canal during the root canal treatment (Fig. 2Ai). After several attempts at removal by the primary clinician, the separated instrument was gradually removed from the foramen (Fig. 2Aii).

Clinical examination

Clinical examination revealed temporary filling materials. Tooth #13 was found to be tender during the percussion test (Fig. 2Bi). A small low-density area was observed at the apex of the distal root. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with symptomatic apical periodontitis. CBCT showed that the root canal had a severe apical curvature of 40° toward the buccal side (Fig. 2Aiii–iv). The total length of the separated instruments was 6 mm, half of which were positioned outside the root canal.

Re-assessment for secondary separated instruments retrieval

First-visit errors: In the previous treatment, the doctor ignored the severe curvature of the apical area in both the buccal-palatal and mesial-distal directions. Moreover, the ultrasonic tip was incorrectly placed on the top of separated instrument during retrieval.

It was necessary to remove the separated instrument again because of the patient’s complaint, and 3 mm of the separated instrument exceeded the apical foramen. Considering that the half-length of the separated instrument was located outside the apical foramen, both surgical and non-surgical approaches were chosen. However, radiographic findings revealed a relatively short palatal root. Thus, apical surgery with a 3 mm apical resection would result in a crown-to-root ratio approaching 1:1, which would reduce periodontal support and increase the risk of tooth mobility. Therefore, nonsurgical separated instrument retrieval was deemed beneficial. Because the separated instrument was long, we considered using special devices were used for removal. Therefore, we propose a non-surgical approach for this patient. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Retrieval procedure

The separated instrument can be retrieved by proper application of ultrasonic techniques or in combination with retrieval kits. The middle and upper thirds of the root canals are wide. This indicated that enlargement was established in the previous treatment group. We pre-bent the ET25 to the buccal region and brushed the motion against the inner wall to reduce the curvature until we could see the top of the separated instrument (Fig. 2Bii, iii). Because the root canal was S-shaped and the curvature was both buccal-palatal and mesial-distal, the clasping zone was located in the 270°space around the separated instrument (Fig. 2Cii). We gradually cut along the buccal and distal walls to release the engagement force (Fig. 2Ciii). After the separated instrument was exposed to 1.5 mm, the clasping zone was reduced to a semicircular space around the separated instrument (Fig. 2Civ). Although the separated instrument was already loosened, the effect of the ultrasonic energy can only make the separated instrument move horizontally in the buccal palatal direction, rather than popping out in the coronal direction.

Therefore, an inverted conical working tip (TFRK-GPR, Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., China) was placed in the gap to hold the separated instrument and pull it towards the crown. We then prepared the root canal and apical barrier using iRoot BP (Fig. 2Dii).

Follow up

Re-examination six months later revealed no symptoms and increased periapical radiolucency density (Fig. 2Div).

Discussion

The prevalence of instrument separation around or beyond the curve is significantly higher than that in the coronal and middle thirds [11–13]. Retrieval of these separate instruments results in a higher risk of failure or complications. Studies have shown several important predictive factors for improving the retrieval rate and reducing potential risks, including the location, size, length, and type of fractured instrument; root canal curvature; operator experience and fatigue [14–16]. However, in this case series, inappropriate preoperative assessment and incorrect retrieval protocols of the previous treatment led to retrieval failure accompanied by complications.

The challenges in managing retrieval failure lie in balancing the necessity and possible risks of retrieval, particularly the complications. The predictive factors for these cases differed from those for the primary treatment. However, relatively few studies have been conducted on the preoperative evaluation of such cases. Based on our clinical experience, the amount of residual dentin that can be cut is a crucial predictive factor when it is at the top of a separated instrument or at the root canal curvature. In Case 1, we evaluated the thickness of the residual dentin wall inside the curvature and around the top of the separated instrument. CBCT showed that there was still enough residual dentin in the inner wall, both on top of the separated instrument and at the root canal curvature. Therefore, a space inside the curve could be established to place the ultrasonic tip and remove the separated instrument. In addition, perforation complications in Case 1 were predictive factors. The prognosis of Case 1 was more adversely affected by perforation than by the separated instrument itself. Managing perforations is as important as removing instruments. If the perforation does not interfere with the retrieval, repair can be performed before removal. However, when the perforation is close to the separated instrument, it should be removed prior to repair.

Following preoperative assessment, an appropriate technical approach is crucial. Clinicians have proposed recommendations for the preparation for root canal enlargement to satisfy the accessibility of separated instrument. In a recent review, Terauchi delineated the specific sites and the extent of dentin removal using ultrasonic tips when exposed to a separate instrument [3]. However, even after the separated instrument is exposed (sometimes to more than one-third of its total length), it may fail to loosen. Few researchers have explained this condition or proposed management strategies.

The clasping zone refers to one or more areas within the root canal where engagement force occurs between the separated instrument and the canal wall. For instruments fractured due to torsional fracture, the clasping zones are concentrated in calcified regions, ledges, or severely curved areas of the root canal [18, 19]. In contrast, in those resulting from fatigue fractures, the clasping zones were generally concentrated in the curved areas of the root canal [18, 20].

In Case 1, the separated instrument was a nickel-titanium (NiTi) instrument located at the severe curvature of the root canal, which was primarily fractured owing to fatigue. The clasping zone was located at the curvature of the root canal. According to the curvature direction, the clasping zone was distributed within the 90°–180°sector on the inner side of the curvature. We then ultrasonically prepared a 1 ~ 1.5-mm-deep clasping zone. As the depth of the clasping zone increased and the degree of curvature decreased, the contact area between the separated instrument and root canal wall decreased, and the scope of the clasping zone narrowed accordingly.

In Case 2, the separated instrument was a barbed broach located in a severely S-shaped curved root canal that had fractured primarily due to torsion. In this case, the clasping zone was not confined to a single area, because the canal presented two sharp curvatures within the apical third, one located more coronally and the other more apically. After relieving the resistance at the first (more coronal) curvature, the instrument was shifted buccally, and the clasping zone was consequently reduced to the distal aspect of the remaining apical curvature. Therefore, we prepared a distal canal wall to overcome the resistance in this region.

However, unlike in Case 1, the barbed broach in Case 2 extended beyond the apical foramen and its barbed structure penetrated the periapical soft tissues. Therefore, even after relieving the intracanal resistance from the clasping zone, the portion embedded in the soft tissues could not be retrieved via ultrasonic vibration. Specifically, the ultrasonic energy failed because the instrument’s looseness buffered the vibrations and dissipated energy, rather than converting it into an ejection force. Thus, the separated instrument requires a direct vertical mechanical force for its retrieval. Consequently, we successfully extracted the data using an instrument retrieval kit.

In this series, the cases were referred after initial failed attempts at separated instrument retrieval associated with complications. Through preoperative re-assessment, especially the location, length, type of the separated instrument and the residual root canal wall thickness, we confirmed the feasibility of secondary retrieval. By analyzing the clasping zone during the procedure, we were able to retrieve the separated instruments in a precise and minimally invasive manner.

Conclusion

This case series presents the challenges associated with the more complex conditions that occur during the removal of separate instruments, especially in the apical third of severely curved infected root canals. Without a comprehensive evaluation of instrument retrieval feasibility and skilled technical approaches, there might be high risks of retrieval failure or complications. Therefore, it is easier and more important to prevent complications than manage them. When such conditions arise, the need for removal should be reassessed. When removal is feasible, appropriate treatment, including establishing an asymmetric retrieval pathway, confirming the clasping zone, applying suitable retrieval techniques, and executing precise operations, should be performed to optimize prognosis.
