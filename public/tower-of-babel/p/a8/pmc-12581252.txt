Effectiveness of curcumin mouthwash in preventing traumatic ulcers in orthodontic patients
Authors: Chutimon Somprajob, Prim Auychai, Janeta Chavanavesh, Supaporn Suttamanatwong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrthodontic treatment can cause traumatic ulcers within the first month due to friction and irritation. Curcumin, a turmeric extract, exhibits analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and wound-healing properties. This study investigated the effectiveness of curcumin mouthwash in preventing traumatic ulcers in fixed orthodontic patients during the first month of treatment.MethodsIn this double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized clinical trial, 70 patients aged 12–30 years were assigned to curcumin or placebo mouthwash, used twice daily for 28 days after bracket cementation. Primary outcomes were (1) incidence and (2) duration of traumatic ulcers. Secondary outcomes included pain scores, ulcer sites, adverse effects, plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), gingival bleeding index (GBI), and patient feedback. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and SPSS software.ResultsAll 70 patients completed the trial, with 35 participants in the curcumin group (mean age 20.88 ± 4.87 years) and 35 in the placebo group (mean age 18.60 ± 4.20 years). No significant between-group differences were observed in the incidence or duration of traumatic ulcers (p > 0.05). Patients in the curcumin group reported significantly lower pain scores compared with those in the placebo group during the first and second weeks (p < 0.05), indicating an early analgesic effect; however, no significant differences were observed thereafter. The buccal mucosa was the most frequent ulcer site, and orthodontic wax use did not differ significantly between groups (p > 0.05). No adverse effects were reported in the curcumin group, and participants rated its smell as more pleasant. Baseline PI was lower in the curcumin group (p < 0.05), but no post-intervention differences were observed (p > 0.05). Within-group analyses showed a PI reduction in the placebo group and a GI reduction in the curcumin group (both p < 0.05), while GBI remained unchanged.ConclusionsAlthough ulcer prevention was not observed, curcumin mouthwash may serve as an adjunct for early pain relief in orthodontic patients.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05147376), registered on December 7, 2021. Background

Fixed orthodontic treatment can lead to intra-oral problems, including traumatic oral ulcers [1], because orthodontic brackets, bands and archwires can irritate and cause friction on the oral mucosa [2]. These ulcers commonly occur within the first month following cementation treatment [3] but usually resolve without any issues within a span of 14 days [4]. Oral mucosal ulcers may result from a variety of causes, including local mechanical irritation from orthodontic appliances, or systemic conditions such as viral infections that impair mucosal integrity and healing. For instance, during the acute viral infection, a range of oral mucocutaneous lesions, including ulcerations, erosions, and mucositis, have been reported, often linked to immune dysregulation and secondary infections rather than direct viral damage [5]. These challenges underscore the need for safe and effective adjunctive therapies, including natural agents such as herbal medicines. With a long history of use in traditional treatments for various conditions, herbal medicines are increasingly popular in contemporary healthcare. Turmeric, an herbal spice, has been used to treat various ailments [6]. Curcumin, the principal curcuminoid in turmeric rhizomes [7], is recognized for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticarcinogenic properties [8, 9]. Curcumin is considered non-toxic and safe for human consumption [10], even at high doses of standardized turmeric powder or extract [11]. In dentistry, it has been used to manage recurrent aphthous stomatitis [12, 13], oral mucositis [14, 15], and as an adjunct in periodontal therapy [16, 17]. Curcumin accelerates tissue repair by modulating the inflammatory, proliferative and remodeling phases [18]. In vitro studies have shown that curcumin upregulates wound-healing-related genes such as type I collagen (COL1), keratinocyte growth factor-1 (KGF-1) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) [19]. Another study demonstrated that curcumin promotes transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1, type I TGF-β receptor (TGF-βRI), type II TGF-β receptor (TGF-βRII), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression in an in vitro human gingival fibroblast wound healing model [20]. Animal models have shown that curcumin reduces inflammation and accelerates repair of traumatic oral ulcers, resulting in smaller lesion size and faster mucosal closure [21, 22]. Clinical studies support these findings, reporting that curcumin gel significantly reduced pain and ulcer size in aphthous ulcers and shortened healing time in recurrent aphthous stomatitis, with outcomes comparable to topical steroids [12, 13]. Curcumin mouthwash has also been shown to reduce the pain, severity, and duration of radiation-induced oral mucositis compared with placebo or standard treatment [15, 23, 24]. In addition, curcumin has been associated with improved plaque control and gingival health [17], factors that may enhance oral mucosal resistance to mechanical trauma [25].

To the best of our knowledge, no clinical study has evaluated the efficacy of curcumin for the prevention of traumatic ulcers in patients undergoing fixed orthodontic treatment. Therefore, the primary objective of this randomized controlled trial was to assess the clinical effectiveness of curcumin mouthwash during the first month of orthodontic treatment compared with placebo.

Methodology

The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (Study Code: HREC-DCU 2021-044) of the Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05147376) on 07/12/2021.

Study design

This study was a double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized clinical trial with two parallel arms. The trial was designed and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Participants were randomly allocated to either the curcumin mouthwash group or the placebo group according to the assigned intervention.

Participants and eligibility criteria

The study involved individuals undergoing initial fixed orthodontic treatment at the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University. Eligible participants were informed about the study and consented to participate, with selection criteria including those who were (1) aged between 12 and 30 years old, (2) with fixed orthodontics on at least one arch (at least 10 teeth per arch), (3) in good general health, (4) not using any herbal toothpaste or mouthwash in their daily life for the past 2 weeks and (5) not taking any medications that might influence oral ulceration pattern. Participants were excluded from the study if they (1) smoked cigarettes, (2) had a history of hypersensitivity or allergy to turmeric or other mouthwash ingredients, (3) had a history of oral mucosal lesions and skin diseases, (4) presented with oral ulceration or inflammation of unknown etiology and (5) were pregnant or lactating women.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated according to a previous study based on the overall satisfaction of curcumin gel in reducing pain intensity and aphthous ulcer size [12]. The sample size in the placebo and curcumin mouthwash groups was calculated using GPower 3.1.9.7 and was estimated to be 29 participants in each group, with respective significance levels of 0.05 and 0.90. An additional 20% was recruited to account for potential dropouts, resulting in 35 participants per group. This adjustment is consistent with previous oral health trials, where dropout rates of up to 20% are commonly anticipated and considered acceptable [24, 26].

Randomization

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the curcumin or placebo group using computer-generated random numbers. The allocation sequence was concealed in sealed opaque envelopes, and both mouthwashes were provided in opaque and identical coded bottles.

Blinding

This trial was double-blinded. Allocation and distribution of the coded bottles were managed by a staff member who was not involved in outcome assessment. All clinical evaluations were performed by a single examiner blinded to treatment assignment, and blinding was maintained throughout data collection and analysis.

Study products

The curcumin mouthwash was formulated using a turmeric extract solution prepared by dissolving turmeric powder (Ouayun, Bangkok, Thailand; curcuminoid content ≥ 7% w/w) in ethanol. The final formulation consisted of the turmeric extract solution, ultrapure water, xylitol (Futaste Pharmaceutical, China), and food coloring (Great Hill, Bangkok, Thailand), yielding 1 µM (0.000368 mg/mL) curcumin. This concentration was previously identified as optimal for promoting wound healing related gene expression in gingival fibroblasts [20].

The placebo mouthwash had the same composition as the test formulation, except for the active ingredient. Both preparations were freshly made, dispensed in opaque bottles, and stored at room temperature without microbial preservatives to minimize potential side effects. Each participant received three 240 mL bottles of mouthwash and was instructed to rinse with 10 mL twice daily for 28 days. This frequency was chosen to align with standard oral hygiene practices and maintain a sustained intraoral regimen, consistent with previous clinical trials of curcumin mouthwashes [16, 17]. Participants were also advised to refrain from eating or drinking for at least 30 min after rinsing.

Intervention

Participants underwent oral screening examinations either one week prior to or on the day of bracket cementation, with plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), and gingival bleeding index (GBI) evaluated by a single examiner. Intra-oral photographs were captured using a Canon PowerShot G7 X Mark III. Each participant received a unique trial number, three bottles of mouthwash, a tube of sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS)-free, non-herbal toothpaste, and a toothbrush. Daily online record forms were provided for participants to report their experiences throughout the trial. Follow-up assessments for traumatic ulcers and adverse effects were conducted on day 7 and day 28, with ulcers photographed when present. On day 28, PI, GI, and GBI were reassessed, and intra-oral photographs were taken. Because data relied partly on self-reported routines, some variability may have occurred despite clinician verification at scheduled follow-up visits.

Stopping guidelines

Participants who experienced side effects during the intervention immediately discontinued the mouthwash and contacted the investigator. Those who did not use mouthwash for over 7 consecutive days or experienced illness were excluded from the study. All participants were allowed to use orthodontic wax as part of their standard treatment after bracket cementation.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were obtained from participants’ daily online record sheets. The first primary outcome was the presence of traumatic ulcers, reported dichotomously (present or absent). Because outcomes were self-reported, clinician assessments during scheduled follow-up visits were used to verify the presence of ulcer. Traumatic ulcers were identified through differential diagnosis to distinguish them from lesions caused by physical or chemical agents. However, ulcers that developed and resolved between visits may not have been captured, introducing potential misclassification and reporting bias. The second primary outcome was the duration of each ulcer, which was measured by calculating the average healing time for each participant’s ulcer.

Secondary outcomes included the pain score, sites of traumatic ulcers, adverse effects, oral hygiene index and patient comments about the placebo and curcumin mouthwash.

Participants evaluated their pain using a visual analogue scale [27], selecting a number that described the most pain and recording it between 0 and 100. Participants marked the ulcer sites on an oral cavity diagram [28]. Participants also recorded daily discomfort complaints, satisfaction levels and suggestions about mouthwash using Google Forms during the clinical trial.

The PI and GI measurements were recorded at 4 surfaces in 6 teeth, representing the 6 segments of the jaw according to Löe’s definition [29]. The PI was used to classify oral hygiene status (Table 1). Participants with a total score of 1 or less were considered to have good oral hygiene [3]. The GI was used to classify the stage of inflammation (Table 1). Participants with mild inflammation were scored 0.1–1.0, those with moderate inflammation 1.1–2.0, and those with severe inflammation 2.1–3.0 [29]. The GBI was measured at 4 surfaces per tooth of all teeth according to modifications from Ainamo and Bay’s definition (Table 1) [30]. The index required a gentle probe into the gingival crevice.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative variables are presented as frequency, percentage and mean (standard deviation). The gender and incidence of traumatic ulcers were assessed using the chi-square test. As the data did not adhere to a normal distribution, non-parametric tests, the Mann–Whitney U test, were employed, except for age. For the comparison of relationships within groups concerning PI, GI and GBI, the Wilcoxon Signed-Ranks test was applied. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0. Results are reported as observed without adjustment for baseline covariates. The baseline age difference between groups is acknowledged as a potential confounder that may have influenced the outcomes.

Reliability

The study’s reliability was tested by re-examining 10 participants, representing 15% of the sample. To minimize bias, the dental assistant recorded the findings in written form. The examiner’s intra-examiner reliability for PI, GI and GBI was verified using the kappa statistic, yielding values of 0.78, 0.82 and 0.88, respectively, indicating moderate to strong agreement.

Results

The study enrolled 78 patients in February 2022 and concluded in August 2023. The analysis included 70 patients who met the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1).

Incidence of traumatic ulcers

In the placebo mouthwash group, the incidence of traumatic ulcers was 65.7% in the first week and declined progressively to 51.4%, 40.0%, and 28.6% in weeks 2–4. No significant differences were observed between groups at any time point (all p > 0.05). Both groups showed a reduction in ulcer incidence over time, although the decrease was more consistent in the placebo group, while the curcumin group demonstrated a rise at week 4. Duration of ulcers

Patients who used curcumin mouthwash had an average ulcer healing duration of 2.25 ± 1.91 days over the 28-day period, whereas those using placebo mouthwash experienced longer mean ulcer duration of 2.54 ± 4.01 days. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the duration of ulcers between the two groups (p > 0.05; Fig. 4). The findings demonstrated that ulcer healing times were comparable between groups, indicating no measurable effect of curcumin mouthwash on healing duration.

Pain scores

Among the secondary outcomes, participants in the curcumin mouthwash group reported lower pain scores than those in the placebo group during the first and second weeks of the study (p < 0.05; Fig. 5). No significant differences were observed during the third and fourth weeks (p > 0.05). These exploratory findings suggest a potential benefit of curcumin mouthwash that warrants further investigation.

Additional comments and suggestions

Patients expressed preferences for different mouthwash colors, a pleasant smell and taste and a convenient rinse container based on preferences for placebo and curcumin mouthwashes.

Indices

PI

At baseline, mean PI was significantly lower in the curcumin group than in the placebo group, but no between-group difference was observed post-intervention. Intragroup analyses showed reductions in both groups, reaching statistical significance in the placebo group (Table 6). Importantly, plaque scores remained ≤ 1 in both groups throughout the study, indicating that participants maintained good oral hygiene regardless of mouthwash type. Clinically, this suggests that neither curcumin nor placebo mouthwash affected oral hygiene, and patients sustained effective plaque control during orthodontic treatment.

GI

Mean GI showed no significant differences between groups at baseline or post-treatment. Within-group analysis, however, revealed a significant reduction in the curcumin mouthwash group (p < 0.05; Table 6). Overall, both groups exhibited mild to moderate gingival inflammation. Clinically, this reduction may reflect a modest adjunctive effect of curcumin mouthwash, although its impact on gingival health appears limited and likely not clinically significant.

GBI

At baseline and post-GBI score, mean GBI did not differ significantly between groups (Table 6). Gingival bleeding was generally well controlled in both groups throughout the study. Neither curcumin nor placebo mouthwash appeared to exert a clinically significant effect on bleeding outcomes.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial compared curcumin mouthwash with placebo in orthodontic patients, with traumatic ulcer incidence and duration defined as the primary outcomes. No significant differences were found between the groups in ulcer occurrence or healing time, indicating that curcumin did not prevent the occurrence of traumatic ulcers. While no significant differences were observed in the primary prevention outcomes, the pain reduction noted in the curcumin group suggests a potential benefit worth exploring in future trials. Participants using curcumin mouthwash reported significantly lower pain scores during the first two weeks, although no differences were observed thereafter. This transient analgesic effect may be clinically relevant during the critical period of patient adaptation to orthodontic appliances. Consistent with reports in recurrent aphthous stomatitis and oral mucositis [12–15, 24], the analgesic effect of curcumin may be mediated via COX-2 and prostaglandin inhibition [31, 32], though these findings remain exploratory given that pain was a secondary outcome.

Traumatic ulcers remain a common complication of fixed orthodontic therapy. Reported prevalence rates in the literature range from 59% to over 75% among appliance wearers, with most lesions occurring on the buccal or labial mucosa. For example, Baricevic et al. reported that 63% of patients developed mucosal lesions [28], while Kvam et al. found that 75.8% experienced ulcers at least once, although only a small minority reported severe symptoms [33]. Other studies have emphasized that oral ulceration is among the most frequent and bothersome complications of orthodontic therapy, affecting 40–47% of patients [34–36]. Consistent with these findings, the incidence of traumatic ulcers in the present trial ranged from 40% to 60% in both the placebo and curcumin groups, with no significant differences observed. Nevertheless, reliance on patient self-reporting through daily records, despite two follow-up visits, may have underestimated transient lesions that resolved between assessments.

The most frequently affected site was the buccal mucosa adjacent to molar brackets and archwires, consistent with previous studies [28, 35, 37]. Orthodontic wax use did not differ significantly between groups, although slightly fewer patients in the curcumin group reported wax use, possibly reflecting lower pain scores. While wax is commonly used to reduce appliance irritation, clinical trial evidence for its effectiveness in ulcer prevention is limited. One study suggested that benzocaine-containing wax reduced pain more effectively than unmedicated wax [2], but robust evidence remains lacking.

Oral hygiene remained stable in both groups, with plaque index values consistently ≤ 1, indicating good oral hygiene was maintained during treatment. A reduction in the gingival index was noted in the curcumin group, but the difference from the placebo group was not significant and unlikely to be clinically significant. Gingival bleeding index values also showed no meaningful change. These findings suggest that curcumin mouthwash had only a modest effect on oral hygiene indices and did not produce clinically significant benefits.

No serious adverse effects were reported. Minor events occurred only in the placebo group, including one case of mucosal peeling, which led to exclusion from the trial on the first day of participation. Although mucosal peeling is often associated with SLS, the use of non-SLS toothpaste in this study suggests that other components may contribute to this condition at much lower frequencies [38]. Additionally, two patients in the placebo group reported noticeable discoloration of elastic ligatures, which had initially been white but darkened over time. Factors such as the staining potential of pigmented foods and beverages likely contributed to this color change [39].

Both mouthwashes were generally well accepted, with participants preferring the smell of the curcumin mouthwash. Some requested improvements in color, freshness, and container design. However, researchers cautioned that modifying smell or taste could potentially affect the active ingredient.

Several limitations should be noted. First, despite randomization, a baseline age imbalance was present, which may have influenced pain perception, ulcer reporting, and mucosal healing, and should be considered when interpreting the results. Second, reliance on self-reported outcomes introduces potential recall and reporting bias, as ulcers that developed and resolved between visits may not have been captured. Although daily clinician assessments and standardized ulcer size photography could improve accuracy, they may not be feasible due to patient compliance and resource constraints. Third, the relatively short follow-up period limited assessment of long-term effects, and the trial may have been underpowered to detect small differences in ulcer prevention. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to evaluate curcumin mouthwash for the prevention of traumatic oral ulcers in orthodontic patients. Although no preventive effect was demonstrated, early analgesic benefits indicate potential clinical relevance. Further studies incorporating dose escalation, longer follow-up, and objective ulcer measurement are needed to clarify its role as an adjunctive therapy in orthodontics.

Conclusion

Curcumin mouthwash did not significantly prevent traumatic ulcers or alter healing time but demonstrated early analgesic effects. Future well-powered trials with higher concentrations, longer follow-up, and objective ulcer assessment are needed to validate its role as an adjunctive therapy in orthodontics.
