Protocol for a digital health-assisted cluster randomised controlled trial to prevent obesity in preschool children in Jinan, China
Authors: Li Zhang, Shiyu Yan, Chunjie Yin, Yuge Zhang, Huijuan Liu, Mai Gao, Zhenyu Zhang, Xuexia Duan, Haijun Wang, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEarly childhood obesity has become a major public health concern globally. The preschool period (ages 4–6 years) represents a critical window for obesity prevention due to its long-term implications on growth trajectories and behaviour formation. However, existing evidence for effective interventions targeting this age group remains limited and inconsistent, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.Methods and analysisThe Peking University-Smart Monitoring and Responsive Technology for Early Childhood Health is a cluster-randomised controlled trial designed to evaluate the effectiveness and sustainability of a multi-component, digital health–assisted obesity prevention intervention. The trial involves approximately 980 children aged 4–6 years (middle and senior kindergarten classes) from 14 kindergartens, randomly allocated to either the intervention (n=7) or control (n=7) groups. The intervention spans 9 months, followed by a 12-month post-intervention follow-up. Interventions are delivered across kindergartens, families and healthcare systems. A digital Smart Health Platform is integrated into the intervention to provide health education, real-time feedback and interactive support, enabling personalised health management and promoting parental engagement. Data are collected at baseline, 4, 9 and 21 months. The primary outcome is the between-group difference in changes in body mass index Z-score at 9 months. Secondary outcomes include other anthropometric indicators, dietary intake, physical activity levels and other measurements. In addition, a process evaluation will be conducted to assess fidelity and participant engagement, and a health economic evaluation will estimate cost-effectiveness and cost-benefit outcomes.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Shandong Maternal and Child Health Hospital (no. 2025-042). Findings from the trial will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations to inform future childhood obesity prevention policies and practices.Trial registration numberNCT07117149.Keywords: Child, Obesity, Primary Prevention

Introduction

The preschool period is a critical window for child growth and development, during which the rising prevalence of obesity warrants urgent attention. In 2020, an estimated 38.9 million children under the age of five worldwide were overweight or obese, and this number is projected to exceed 40 million by 2030.1 In China, the situation is equally concerning: the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children under six reached 10.4% in 2019.2 Childhood obesity poses substantial threats to both physical and psychological health, increasing the risks of hypertension, musculoskeletal problems and metabolic disorders. More importantly, it significantly elevates the likelihood of developing cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, certain cancers and premature death in adulthood.3 4

Preschool period represents a critical window for obesity prevention. This stage is foundational for the development of long-lasting behaviours—such as dietary habits and physical activity patterns—that may extend into adolescence and even adulthood, with profound health implications.5 6 Children who experienced a more rapid body mass index (BMI) acceleration during the preschool period were at greater risk of becoming overweight or obese in adolescence, with the fastest weight gain observed between ages 2 and 6.7 The preschool period also coincides with early adiposity rebound, which, if occurring earlier than expected, markedly increases obesity risk.8 9 Importantly, evidence suggests that obesity interventions initiated during preschool years may yield superior outcomes in weight reduction and maintenance compared with those introduced in later childhood or adolescence.10

Despite its importance, the current evidence base for obesity prevention in preschool-aged children remains limited and inconsistent,11 12 due to several key challenges. First, the quality of evidence remains insufficient. Many existing trials had unclear or high risk of bias. A recent systematic review indicated that 15 of 25 studies were rated as having moderate-to-high risk of bias.13 High-quality randomised controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in low- and middle-income countries are particularly scarce. In China, evidence from intervention trials targeting preschool children also remains limited, with only a single non-randomised controlled report from Guangzhou.14 Second, there is a lack of effective cross-sector collaboration. Existing literature highlights that multi-level collaboration among early care and education institutions, families and healthcare systems is the cornerstone of successful interventions.15 16 However, China’s childcare structure—where children attend kindergartens 5 days a week and consume most of their meals there—necessitates context-specific collaborative models linking kindergartens, families and healthcare institutions. Third, the effectiveness of digital technologies in this population remains to be established. Mobile health (mHealth) technologies have been reported to enhance access to health information and services and have shown promise in weight management interventions among adults and adolescents.17,19 However, their benefits for obesity prevention in preschool children remain inconclusive.13 20 In parallel, artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming healthcare from generalised to personalised and precision-based health management. In the context of obesity, AI can enable health counselling, goal setting, behavioural feedback and self-monitoring.21 22 Yet, these technologies—digital health—are underutilised in managing healthy weight in young children. Fourth, evidence on the long-term effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of interventions is lacking. Studies with follow-up periods of 36 months have failed to demonstrate sustained effects on weight status in preschoolers.23,25 Moreover, economic evaluations of early childhood obesity prevention remain extremely limited, restricting their utility for policymakers and programme planners.26

To address these critical gaps, we designed a cluster-randomised controlled trial that aims to improve the quality and sustainability of obesity prevention among preschoolers in China through the following innovations: 1) Integrated multi-sector intervention network: Leveraging the structure of China’s early childcare system, we construct a collaborative ‘kindergarten–family–healthcare’ intervention network. 2) Digital health platform: A customised digital health platform is developed to support real-time growth monitoring, intelligent goal setting, dynamic feedback and multi-party communication, facilitating personalised and precise intervention delivery. 3) Stratified intervention approach: A risk-based, three-tiered intervention is adopted based on children’s weight status, optimising resource allocation and cost-effectiveness.

The aim of this study is to evaluate a digital health-assisted, comprehensive obesity prevention intervention among preschool children in China. 1) To assess the effectiveness of the intervention in improving health outcomes, including BMI, BMI Z-scores, growth trajectories and health-related behaviours among preschool children compared with usual practice. 2) To evaluate the sustainability of intervention effects over a 12-month post-intervention follow-up period, particularly with regard to weight status maintenance and behavioural improvements. 3) To evaluate the economic value of the intervention through comprehensive cost-effectiveness and cost–benefit analyses. By addressing these objectives, this study is expected to generate evidence for early childhood obesity prevention in low- and middle-income countries and provide an evidence-based framework for global implementation.

Methods

This protocol was written in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines.27 (See online supplemental appendix 1 27: checklist.)

Trial design and intervention framework

The Peking University-Smart Monitoring and Responsive Technology (PKU-SMART) study is a cluster-randomised controlled trial conducted in Jinan, Shandong Province. A total of 14 kindergartens will be recruited. All participating kindergartens will be assigned in equal numbers to either the intervention or control group. Kindergartens in the intervention group will receive a 9-month lifestyle intervention from October 2025 to June 2026, followed by a 12-month post-intervention follow-up until June 2027. Kindergartens in the control group will not receive any intervention during the study period but will be granted full access to intervention materials after trial completion (figure 1).

The study is being conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Shandong Maternal and Child Health Hospital (No. 2025–042).

The intervention adopts a stratified approach that links families, kindergartens and hospitals through a digital health platform enhanced by AI. AI technologies are used for dynamic monitoring of children’s growth, diet, physical activity and sleep, enabling personalised feedback and behaviour recommendations. This model aims to improve the precision, intelligence and scalability of obesity prevention efforts in early childhood.

Sample size

The primary outcomes of this study are changes in BMI Z-score at 9 s. The expected difference (Δ BMI Z-score=0.13, SD=0.42) is based on previous preschool obesity intervention trials and pilot data28 29 and is considered clinically meaningful, as even modest reductions in BMI Z-score during early childhood have been associated with improved long-term cardiometabolic outcomes. Assuming an intra-class correlation coefficient of 0.02 at the cluster (kindergarten) level and a two-sided significance level of 0.05, a total of 7 kindergartens per group, with at least 56 children per kindergarten, will be required to achieve 80% power to detect the specified differences. Allowing for an estimated 10% loss to follow-up, the required sample size per kindergarten increases to at least 63 children. Therefore, we plan to recruit approximately 70 children per kindergarten, resulting in a total sample size of 980 children across 14 kindergartens.

Participant eligibility

Preschool children enrolled in middle and senior classes (aged 4–6 years) will be recruited, as they are developmentally adapted to the kindergarten environment and typically have stable attendance. The Health Education Department of the Shandong Maternal and Child Health Hospital will coordinate initial contact with local kindergartens. Project staff will communicate directly with kindergarten principals to assess institutional eligibility, introduce the study objectives and procedures and confirm participation willingness. Once kindergartens are confirmed, on-site recruitment will be conducted with the support of teachers. Parents will be invited to attend information sessions where the study purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits will be explained, and informed consent will be obtained either on-site or after further consideration at home.

Kindergarten eligibility criteria include the following1: registered, full-time early childhood education institutions in Jinan2; consent from the principal or headmaster and availability of required staff, including school healthcare providers3; more than 100 children enrolled in the middle and senior classes4; no participation in other obesity-related interventions during the previous year or planned participation during the study period5; expected to remain operational and not relocate during the study period and6 willingness among teachers and parents to cooperate with study procedures, including health assessments and platform-based monitoring.

Eligibility criteria for children include the following: their parents are able to use smartphones and interact with the digital health platform used in this intervention, and they are not expected to change kindergartens within the next year. Children will be excluded if they have obesity due to genetic, endocrine or neurological conditions or medication use; have a history of major organ diseases such as heart disease, hypertension, asthma, liver or kidney disease; follow restrictive or special diets (eg, vegetarianism) or present with feeding disorders or physical conditions that may impair participation in physical activity.

Randomisation and blinding

To ensure a balanced allocation of participants between the study groups, a stratified randomisation procedure will be employed. Given the variation in the number of children across different kindergartens, we will first stratify the participating kindergartens based on their enrolled population size. Following stratification, the kindergartens within each stratum will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group in a 1:1 ratio. Randomisation will be conducted using a computer-generated simple randomisation procedure by an independent third party not involved in the implementation of the study. Blinding of participants and implementers is not feasible due to the nature of the intervention. However, outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to the group assignment throughout the study period to minimise assessment and analytical bias.

Intervention

Theoretical model

This intervention is grounded in the social ecological model (SEM),30 which provides a comprehensive framework for addressing early childhood obesity through coordinated strategies across individual, family, kindergarten and healthcare systems. In addition, the intervention design incorporates elements from the behaviour change technique (BCT) taxonomy,31 which strengthens the structured implementation of the intervention. Key BCTs include providing health-related information, goal setting and action planning and personalised feedback. The integration of SEM and BCTs offers a theoretical foundation for designing the intervention, enhancing both its scientific rigour and real-world applicability.

Intervention stratification and implementation approach

To optimise resource allocation and ensure tailored support, we implement a three-tiered, risk-stratified intervention model. All participating children will be classified into three tiers, according to their weight status. Intervention intensity increases cumulatively across tiers: higher tiers incorporate all components of lower tiers while adding more intensive and personalised measures. Interventions are delivered in three key settings—kindergarten, family and the healthcare system.

Tier 1 (Universal prevention)

This level targets all normal-weight children, with a focus on those exhibiting one or more high-risk behaviours identified through a baseline health behaviour questionnaire. The objective is to reduce obesogenic risk factors and prevent the onset of overweight and obesity. Interventions include teacher training to strengthen health promotion capacity in kindergartens, structured health education activities for children and ongoing monitoring of daily behaviours such as eating speed and physical activity. At the family level, parents are engaged through regular meetings, self-assessment tools and health goal setting via the digital platform, as well as access to multi-format educational resources (eg, booklets, online lectures and videos). These measures aim to create supportive home and kindergarten environments while providing practical guidance to address specific behavioural risks.

Tier 2 (Early identification and management)

This level focuses on the early detection and management of overweight and obesity. Children will be screened monthly through standardised anthropometric monitoring, with results fed back to parents and new cases identified promptly. Children classified as overweight or obese, according to national (WS/T 423–2022) and WHO criteria, are documented in health records and placed under coordinated home–kindergarten management. Parents of children with overweight or obesity will be invited to participate in personalised counselling, conducted flexibly based on family availability (eg, in-person, by phone or video conference). These counselling sessions will address common barriers and strategies for behaviour change to promote early and collaborative management.

Tier 3 (Intensive intervention)

This level targets children with severe obesity. In addition to all measures under Tiers 1 and 2, specialised support will be provided through the healthcare system. Paediatricians and nutritionists will be offered individualised behavioural counselling, ensuring that families have access to expert guidance and tailored care.

Across all tiers, the PKU-SMART Health Platform plays a central role in facilitating communication, providing personalised feedback, delivering educational content and enabling remote consultations. Notably, an AI system is embedded within the family interface, enabling personalised goal recommendations, automated feedback and intelligent interaction to enhance the precision and efficiency of health management (see table 2 for details).

The kindergarten interface is designed to support teachers in implementing and supervising school-based components of the intervention. Teachers can view updated anthropometric data and weight status distributions for children in their class or kindergarten, allowing timely identification of children requiring additional attention. The interface also displays parents’ engagement levels on the platform (eg, completion of viewing health education materials), facilitating coordinated home–school communication. In addition, teachers can upload records of health education sessions delivered in class and post announcements or reminders to parents. This interface strengthens the bidirectional information flow between the kindergarten and families, enabling consistent health messaging and supportive environments across settings.

The healthcare provider interface enables paediatricians and nutrition professionals to participate in remote health management. Providers can respond to parents’ enquiries regarding children’s growth, diet or behaviour; deliver tailored advice and, when needed, offer outpatient services for consultations.

The project management interface is used by the research team through a web-based dashboard. It enables comprehensive oversight of all participating kindergartens, including real-time monitoring of enrolment status, anthropometric data and intervention implementation fidelity. Administrators can manage user permissions, distribute health education content, monitor the delivery and documentation of school-based activities and issue reminders to ensure compliance with study protocols. The interface centralises data management and facilitates quality control.

Intervention quality control

To ensure standardisation and fidelity of implementation across participating kindergartens, the research team will develop detailed standard operating procedures and compile two intervention manuals: one for the project implementation team and one for kindergarten staff. These manuals clearly define the procedures for each step of the intervention, as well as the required frequency and intensity for each component, ensuring consistency and accuracy across all intervention sites.

Training will be provided to all kindergarten personnel involved in the study. Training will cover the overall research process, role-specific responsibilities and operational procedures. Key personnel will include the following: 1) kindergarten principals, responsible for overall coordination and task allocation; 2) healthcare staff, assisting with health assessments and communication with the project implementation team and class teachers and 3) class teachers, responsible for implementing intervention activities, delivering health education sessions and communicating directly with parents.

In addition, the research team will conduct on-site supervision visits to observe real-time implementation. For example, we will conduct spot checks during meal times to assess whether teachers are monitoring dietary behaviours. Issues identified during supervision will be addressed promptly to ensure protocol adherence.

Furthermore, the Smart Health Platform’s management module will be used to monitor fidelity and participation. The platform allows verification of health education delivery, with kindergarten teachers required to upload photos and related materials as evidence of implementation; tracking of parental involvement, including viewing of children’s health updates, access to educational content and submission of health behaviour records. Compliance data will be automatically exported at regular intervals and reviewed by the project team to identify classrooms or families with suboptimal adherence. When low compliance is detected, project staff will communicate directly with class teachers to provide feedback and reinforce engagement strategies.

Control group

In the control group, participating kindergartens will receive no intervention during the study period and will continue their routine educational and management practices without modification. After the study is fully completed, control group preschools will have access to all intervention materials and health education resources developed for this project.

Anthropometric measurements

Children’s anthropometric data (height, weight, blood pressure, waist circumference, body composition) will be collected during health check-ups conducted within a private area of each kindergarten. Children will be measured barefoot and in light clothing. All measurements will be performed by trained research staff who are independent from the intervention implementation team and blinded to group allocation. Measurement standardisation procedures include1 pre-study training for all assessors using standardised protocols,2 calibration of equipment daily before measurements3 and duplicate measurements taken by two independent assessors.

To ensure the quality and integrity of the questionnaire responses, the following measures will be implemented: 1) Logic checks are embedded within the electronic system to prevent illogical or contradictory answers. 2) Mandatory fields are set for all core variables to minimise missing data. 3) Input restrictions are applied to improve data precision (eg, height and weight of family members must be reported to one decimal place). 4) Definitions and examples are provided alongside questions to reduce ambiguity (eg, what qualifies as a ‘sugar-sweetened beverage’ or ‘unhealthy snack’). 4) Completion time is automatically recorded to identify potentially rushed submissions (eg, those completed under 1 min). 5) Response pattern monitoring will be used to flag potential low-quality responses (eg, all answers marked as ‘5’ in a behaviour questionnaire). When low-quality data are detected, parents will be asked to re-complete the questionnaire to ensure data validity.

Data management

Electronic questionnaires will incorporate multiple quality control features, including: logical checks to identify internal inconsistencies; mandatory fields to prevent incomplete submissions; input restrictions (eg, for numerical range values) and minimum completion time requirements. When such errors are identified, the participant will be asked to complete the questionnaire again. Biological sample records will be cross-checked with collection logs. All study data will be entered and stored in a centralised, secure database managed by the Department of Maternal and Child Health, School of Public Health, Peking University. Only authorised personnel designated by the principal investigator will have access to the data. The computers used for storage will be regularly maintained and scanned for security threats by trained staff.

Data monitoring committee

The study is overseen by the Ethics Committee of the Shandong Maternal and Child Health Hospital, which reviewed and approved the trial protocol and consent procedures. Data quality, participant safety and ethical compliance will be monitored internally by the principal investigator and study management team in collaboration with the Ethics Committee. The committee is independent from the study funder, and there are no reported conflicts of interest among its members. Any protocol deviations, adverse events (AEs) or serious concerns related to participant well-being will be reported promptly to the Ethics Committee, in accordance with institutional policies.

Biological sample collection

Saliva samples will be collected at baseline only. The primary scientific rationale is to explore gene-by-intervention interaction effects, that is, whether genetic variants associated with obesity risk moderate the intervention’s impact on BMI Z-score. This exploratory analysis aims to identify subgroups that may benefit more or less from the intervention, informing future precision prevention strategies. The collection procedure is as follows: 1) Children will be required to fast before sample collection. To stimulate salivation, they will be asked to imagine eating sour foods (eg, preserved plums) and gently suck saliva into the mouth. 2) At least 2 mL of saliva will be collected into a pre-labelled 5 mL cryotube placed at the corner of the mouth. 3) Children will be instructed to avoid mixing sputum into the saliva sample. 4) Aliquotting and storage: samples will be grouped by date, sealed in labelled bags (including date and kindergarten ID) and stored at −80 °C for future analyses.

Outcomes

The primary outcome is the between-group difference in the change in BMI Z-score from baseline to the end of the 9-month intervention, calculated based on objectively measured height and weight using the WHO growth reference standards.38 Secondary outcomes encompass a comprehensive set of anthropometric, physiological and behavioural indicators, including1 the change in BMI Z-score from baseline to 21 months (ie, 12-month post-intervention) to evaluate the sustainability of intervention effects2; changes in BMI(kg/m²), waist circumference (cm) and waist-to-height ratio, as well as body composition parameters (including body fat percentage and lean mass) to assess overall and central adiposity3; changes in systolic and diastolic blood pressure to evaluate cardiovascular health and4 the prevalence and incidence of overweight and obesity, defined by both WHO and Chinese national criteria.5 Behavioural outcomes include children’s eating behaviour, parental feeding practices, physical activity and sedentary behaviour. These measures will be collected at baseline, post-intervention (9 months) and/or at follow-up (21 months). (See table 3 for details.)

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses will be performed using R software V.4.4.2, with two-sided tests and a significance level of p<0.05.

Data cleaning and descriptive statistics After data cleaning, including the identification and handling of biologically implausible values and the correction of logical inconsistencies, missing data will be assessed. Biologically implausible values will be identified using WHO growth reference standards39 (eg, BMI Z-score < −5 or > +5, height-for-age Z-score < −6 or > +6). These values will not be automatically removed; instead, they will be verified against source records, and sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the impact of potential data anomalies on results. Missing data will be addressed using multiple imputation techniques under the assumption of missing at random. Continuous variables will be summarised using means and SD, while categorical variables will be described as frequencies and percentages. To compare baseline characteristics between the intervention and control groups, independent t-tests, χ2 tests or Mann-Whitney U tests will be applied as appropriate.

Missing data will be addressed using multiple imputation techniques under the assumption of missing at random. Continuous variables will be summarised using means and SD, while categorical variables will be described as frequencies and percentages. To compare baseline characteristics between the intervention and control groups, independent t-tests, χ2 tests or Mann-Whitney U tests will be applied as appropriate.

Primary and secondary outcome analyses The analyses will be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. To account for the clustered study design (kindergarten-level randomisation), linear mixed-effects models will be used for continuous outcomes and generalised linear mixed-effects models for binary outcomes. Outcomes will be assessed at 4-, 9- and 21-month post-baseline. All models will adjust for clustering effects at the kindergarten level, baseline outcome values and potential confounders, including child’s age, sex, parental education and household income. For continuous outcomes, results will be reported as pre/post-intervention means by group and model-adjusted mean differences with 95% CIs and p-values. For binary outcomes, we will report group proportions pre- and post-intervention and adjusted ORs with 95% CIs and p-values.

Health Economic Evaluation A comprehensive economic evaluation will be conducted from the societal perspective to assess both the cost and economic efficiency of the intervention. Cost data will be collected during the intervention period, including both material costs (eg, maintenance of the digital health platform, printing of educational materials and booklets and other supplies) and personnel costs (eg, time input from kindergarten teachers, healthcare providers and caregivers, calculated based on wage rates). A cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) will be conducted by comparing the total intervention cost to key outcomes such as reductions in BMI Z-score, BMI and obesity prevalence. For the cost-utility analysis (CUA), intervention effects will be translated into quality-adjusted life years gained using published utility weights for different weight status categories in children. Both CEA and CUA will be within-trial analyses using data collected at baseline, 9-month and 21-month assessments. Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) will convert health outcomes into economic benefits, including estimated long-term benefits of achieving a healthy weight in early childhood (eg, reduced healthcare expenditures and increased future productivity). The CBA will combine a within-trial analysis (up to 21 months) with a model-based extrapolation to a lifetime horizon. A discount rate of 5% per year will be applied to both costs and health outcomes for the within-trial and model-based analyses, consistent with pharmacoeconomic evaluation guidelines in China.40 Utility weights and key model parameters (eg, transition probabilities, productivity loss rates) will be derived from published economic evaluations of childhood obesity interventions.41,43

Patient and public involvement

This study integrates a patient and public involvement (PPI) approach throughout the development, refinement and implementation of the intervention.44 During the development phase, parents of preschool children and kindergarten teachers participated in focus group interviews to identify priorities, barriers and preferences regarding healthy weight management measures. Subsequently, a 3-month pilot study was conducted from April to June 2025 in eight preschool classes (176 children) at one kindergarten in Jinan, Shandong Province. In this pilot, children, parents and teachers actively contributed to further refinement of the intervention. Children’s responses were assessed for age-appropriateness and acceptability of the health education sessions. Parents provided feedback on parent meeting participation, willingness to use the digital health platform and preferences for health education materials. In addition, qualitative interviews with teachers and parent representatives explored prioritisation of intervention strategies and suggested adjustments to the content, frequency and intensity of specific components. To ensure ongoing PPI during the trial, a parent–teacher advisory subgroup will be established in each intervention kindergarten. These subgroups will provide continuous feedback on feasibility, acceptability and engagement, allowing for responsive adaptations.

Discussion

The preschool period is a critical stage for growth and the formation of health-related behaviours and is therefore regarded as the ‘optimal window’ for weight-management interventions.45 46 To date, however, effective strategies targeting this age group remain limited—particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where high-quality, scalable and cost-effective evidence is scarce. With the rapid advances in digital health technologies, integrating these tools into childhood obesity interventions offers substantial practical value and dissemination potential. The present cluster-randomised controlled trial is designed in this context to investigate a comprehensive, tiered intervention model that combines multisector collaboration and digital support. Findings from this study are expected to provide robust scientific evidence to inform obesity-prevention policies and intervention strategies for preschool children in China and other developing settings.

This trial has several methodological strengths. First, it establishes a context-specific cooperation network—kindergarten–family–hospital—aligned with China’s early childcare structure. Second, the intervention is theoretically underpinned by the SEM and BCTs, thereby enhancing the sustainability of behaviour change. Third, an AI-enabled digital health platform allows real-time data capture, intelligent feedback and multiparty interaction, which facilitate personalised management and are expected to improve parental engagement and overall adherence. Fourth, the intervention adopts a tiered strategy by stratifying the target population into three levels based on weight status and obesity risk profiles, then delivers interventions with corresponding intensity. This precision approach is designed to enhance intervention efficiency while reducing implementation costs. Finally, the study employs a multidimensional evaluation framework—including anthropometric, behavioural and economic outcomes—and incorporates a 21-month follow-up to evaluate the sustainability of the intervention effects.

The AI-enhanced Smart Health Platform differs from standard mHealth tools through four core functionalities1: personalised goal recommendations: AI processes individual child data to recommend tailored behaviour goals2; emotionally adaptive feedback: AI delivers personalised, emotionally appropriate feedback based on growth trends and behavioural patterns3; intelligent behaviour matching: AI identifies priority behaviour targets and matches them with practical suggestions tailored to user needs4; integrated assessment reports: AI synthesises data from multiple sources (eg, growth monitoring, behaviour modules) to produce comprehensive personalised health reports. The expected mechanism is that AI-enhanced personalisation will improve parental engagement, increase adherence to behaviour change goals and ultimately lead to improved BMI outcomes through sustained behaviour modification.

Potential challenges and limitations are also acknowledged. Adherence is a pivotal determinant of intervention effectiveness. In large, multi-component and long-duration interventions, parental engagement may fluctuate over time. To address this risk, the platform’s backend will continuously monitor usage frequency and behavioural logs, supplemented by push notifications, point-based incentives and personalised feedback. Intervention effects may also be influenced by variation in kindergarten teachers’ delivery quality. Standard operating manuals, comprehensive training and regular on-site supervision have been instituted to minimise such variability. Moreover, parent-reported behavioural questionnaires are subject to potential reporting bias, which will be mitigated by incorporating reverse-coded items and cross-validating responses against weekly behavioural data from the project’s digital platform.

The study is conducted in Jinan, Shandong, which may affect the generalisability of the findings to other regions with different cultural and socioeconomic contexts. Contamination and spillover effects represent additional potential limitations. The 14 participating kindergartens are all located within the same city and may share municipal training networks, teacher professional development activities or informal communication channels. To minimise contamination risk, the following measures will be implemented1: ensure spatial separation between intervention and control kindergartens where possible2; monitor for potential spillover through process evaluation. Any identified contamination will be acknowledged as a limitation in the final analysis, and sensitivity analyses may be conducted to assess its potential impact on effect estimates. In addition, the requirement for smartphone use may limit participation among families with lower socioeconomic status or limited digital literacy, potentially introducing selection bias and affecting both generalisability and health equity. This issue will be considered when interpreting study findings, and participant characteristics will be carefully described to assess representativeness.

In conclusion, this study is innovative and has strong translational potential. By integrating multi-sector collaboration with digital health technologies, it aims to establish an evidence-based, locally adaptable model for preschool obesity prevention and to provide valuable guidance for policy formulation and resource allocation in similar contexts worldwide.
