Air pollution and cognitive performance in late life: the role of lipid profile – results from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA)
Authors: Xi Pan, Robyn L McClelland, Lilah M Besser, Jana A Hirsch, Joel D Kaufman, Jing Cao, Timothy M Hughes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Evidence on blood lipids in the air pollutant-cognition relationship is scarce. In this study, we identified the role of blood lipids in the air pollutant-cognition relationship in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort.Methods:The study sample consisted of 1,518 adults who participated in MESA Exams 1–6 (2000–2018). Outcome were three composite scores of the Cognitive Abilities Screening Instrument (CASI) test, the Digit Symbol Coding test (DSC), and the Digit Span (DS) test at Exam 6. Outdoor residential concentrations of PM2.5, NO2, and NOx between Exams 1 and 5 were assessed using validated spatiotemporal models. Total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) concentrations in blood at Exam 5 were evaluated as mediators or modifiers using linear regression models. Models assessed gender and race/ethnicity related heterogeneity in air pollutant-cognition and blood lipid-cognition associations.Results:The direct effect of NO2 and NOx on DSC scores was significant (ß = −2.64 95 % CI −4.87 to −0.40 for NO2; ß = −3.01 95 % CI −5.20 to −0.83 for NOx, per interquartile range increase), but there was no evidence of mediation by cholesterol levels. The association of CASI scores with NO2 and NOx was modified by LDL-C (β = 0.03 for NO2, p = 0.01; β = 0.02, p = 0.03 for NOx). Associations of cognitive performance with air pollutant exposure and blood lipids did not vary by gender or racial identity.Discussion:TC and LDL-C were not mediators or clinically meaningful modifiers in the air pollutant-cognition relationship.Keywords: Air pollution, Blood lipids, Cognition, Dementia risks, Aging

Introduction

Long-term exposure to ambient air pollution has been identified as an environmental risk factor for cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) in older adults, especially for those living in urban areas who might be exposed to a higher concentration of traffic-related air pollutants (TRAP) compared to their rural counterparts (Ailshire and Crimmins, 2014; Kulick et al., 2020; Schikowski and Altug, 2020; Elissa et al., 2023). Air pollutants of concern include fine particulate matter (≤2.5 μm; PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and oxides of nitrogen (NOx) (Ailshire and Crimmins, 2014; Kulick et al., 2020; Schikowski and Altug, 2020; Weuve et al., 2021; Costa et al., 2020). Recent evidence from human and animal studies suggests that air pollutants increase levels of peripheral oxidative stress and inflammation that cause neuroinflammation (Costa et al., 2020). Neuroinflammation might result in decreased cognitive function and accumulation of β-amyloid, a marker of neuronal dysfunction (Ailshire and Crimmins, 2014; Schikowski and Altug, 2020). Although the possible contribution of air pollution to cognitive decline and AD has been supported by epidemiological studies, evidence on the association of long-term air pollutant exposure with cognitive function is still limited and the results are inconsistent.

The deleterious effects of air pollutants on blood lipid profiles are also highlighted in the recent population health studies. These studies suggest that both long-term and short-term exposure to air pollutants (e.g., NO2, PM2.5) are associated with higher low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and total cholesterol (TC) in blood, which are independent risk factors for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (Kim et al., 2021; Bell et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2023; Yaghi and Elkind, 2015). Oxidative stress and inflammation may be potential mechanisms of air pollution affecting blood lipids (Zhang et al., 2023). Air pollutants can affect lipoprotein level in blood, lipid metabolism, and the whole body through cytokines and chemokines (Zhang et al., 2023).

Lipids are key to neuronal functions and dysfunctional lipid metabolism can induce age-related cognitive decline (Varma et al., 2018; Wu, 2023; Wu et al., 2023), thus blood lipids may be associated with cognitive function. The mechanism for the blood lipid-cognition relationship is unclear and inconsistent results exist among older adults. A growing body of epidemiological evidence suggests that dysfunctional blood lipid parameters, such as high LDL-C or raised TC, may be associated with cognitive decline (McFarlane et al., 2020; Rohr et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2020) or lower domain-specific cognitive function in older adults through mechanisms such as oxidative stress and inflammation, which cause synaptic loss and eventually cognitive impairment (Wu, 2023; Rohr et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020). Some epidemiological evidence shows beneficial effect of elevated LDL-C and TC on cognitive performance in adults aged 80 years old, but not in the 65–79 age group (Lv et al., 2016), while other studies reported no significant associations between blood lipids and cognitive function (Liu et al., 2020; Lv et al., 2016; Iwagami et al., 2021; Ong et al., 2018). Therefore, there is a need to explore the relationship of blood lipids and domain-specific cognitive function.

Recent epidemiological studies suggest that cardiovascular risk factors and conditions, such as high TC and LDL-C, may be one of the important intermediate links between air pollution and the process of cognitive decline (Schikowski and Altug, 2020; Weuve et al., 2021; Saito and Ihara, 2016; Toledo, 2013; Mei et al., 2024). Mei et al. (2024) have found that high blood cholesterol mediates up to 5 % of the joint effect of air pollution and neighborhood socioeconomic status (N-SES) on subjective cognitive function in middle-aged and older Americans from Metro Atlanta, GA (Mei et al., 2024). Blood lipids could also modify the association between air pollution and cognitive function. Identifying the role of blood lipids in the air pollutant-cognition relationship may suggest a promising avenue to modify environmental risks for cognitive health and intervene cognitive decline in older adults. Moreover, recent research suggests that either air pollutant-cognition or blood lipid-cognition association may vary by gender and racial identity (Kim et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2017; Kulick et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2017). Gender and racial identity are social factors that reflect inequalities in socioeconomic status including educational experiences and occupational opportunities at individual and neighborhood levels, and these inequalities may lead to differences in levels of air pollutant exposure, individual access to health care, and individual health (Kim et al., 2019; Kulick et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2017; Anjum Hajat et al., 2013; Jones et al., 2014). However, examination of air pollutant-blood lipids-cognition relationships varying by both gender and racial groups in the same aging cohort is scarce.

To address these gaps, this study examined the influence of long-term air pollution on cognitive performance and the mediation and modification roles of blood lipids in this relationship in a community-based sample of urban older cohorts participating in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA). We hypothesized that long-term air pollutant exposure has an adverse influence on cognitive performance, and this relationship may be mediated or modified by blood lipid concentrations. Conceptual frameworks of these hypotheses are shown in Figs. 1 and 2. Methods

Data and participants

MESA is an ongoing prospective, community-based cohort study initiated to track the prevalence and progression of subclinical cardiovascular disease. The design of MESA and enrollment, consent, and phenotyping of MESA participants has been described in detail elsewhere (Bild et al., 2002). Briefly, 6814 men and women participants were recruited from 6 U.S. metropolitan regions or MESA study sites (Baltimore, MD; Chicago, IL; Forsyth County, NC; Los Angeles, CA; New York, NY; and St. Paul, MN) at the baseline exam (Exam 1) between July 2000 and September 2002, and 5 followed-up exams were completed through 2018. Participants were aged between 45 and 84 years and free of clinical cardiovascular disease at baseline. The 6th follow-up exam (Exam 7) is completed in 2024. Participants provided informed consent, and all procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of each field center. Among the 6814 participants, 1874 participated in the cognitive assessments at both Exam 5 (2010–2011) and Exam 6 (2016–2018) and 1717 participants had valid scores. We combined MESA Exams 5 and 6 with two MESA ancillary studies: MESA Air and MESA Neighborhoods. The designs of MESA Air and MESA Neighborhood study have been described in detail elsewhere (Cohen et al., 2009; Kaufman et al., 2012; Diez Roux et al., 2016; Hirsch et al., 2014). Of those who had valid scores on cognitive testing at both Exams 5 and 6, 1563 participants had the air pollution data between Exams 1 and 5 and blood lipids data at Exam 5. Our final sample consisted of 1518 participants who had complete and valid data on air pollutant exposure, blood lipids, and cognitive performance. Temporal framework

To study the potential mediating roles of LDL-C and TC, the air pollution exposure had to accumulate before the mediators, which must have subsequently occurred before the cognitive outcome. We achieved this by using the year of survey completion to define time periods, to ensure proper temporality. Briefly, we estimated chronic exposure to air pollution between Exams 1 and 5 (air pollution at Exam 5 was not included) and blood lipids at Exam 5, which were after the exposure window. Then, to ascertain cognitive performance status, we used the cognitive performance at Exam 6. We a priori considered this strategy can reflect the correct temporal order for a valid mediation analysis. Measures

Outcomes: cognitive performance

Cognitive performance at Exam 6 was the outcome of the study. The MESA cognitive battery included the Cognitive Abilities Screening Instrument (CASI, version 2) (Teng et al., 1994), the Digit Symbol Coding test (DSC) (Wechsler, 1997), and the Digit Span test (DS, forward and backward) (Wechsler, 1997). Reliability and validity of the cognitive assessments in MESA are available in the Supplemental Materials. The CASI is a test of global cognitive function that contains 25 items representing 9 cognitive domains, including short- and long-term memory, attention, concentration, orientation, language, verbal fluency, visual construction, and abstraction/judgment (Moazzami et al., 2018). Scores from individual items on the CASI were summed to provide an overall cognitive function score (range 0–100). Processing speed and visuomotor ability were assessed by the DSC test. This test measured how quickly simple perceptual or mental operations can be performed. The total DSC scores ranged from 0 to 133. Working memory and attention were measured by the DS. The test was administered in 2 parts and required the participant to repeat spans of numbers that increased in length, first forwards and then backwards. Scores of the forward and backward portions ranged from 0 to 14, respectively. Total DS scores ranged from 0 to 28, which were used in the present analyses. For each test, a higher score indicated better cognitive performance.

Predictors: long-term air pollutant exposure

Long-term air pollutant exposure was defined as annual average concentrations of PM2.5 (μg/m3; micrograms per cubic meter), NOx (ppb; parts per billion), and NO2 (ppb; parts per billion) at individual residential locations between Exams 1 and 5 (2000–2010). Air pollution levels were assessed and predicted using region-specific spatiotemporal models from MESA Air and linked to each address during follow-up. These spatiotemporal models were developed based on the monitoring data from the Air Quality System (AQS) of the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and spatial dense supplementary data specific to MESA Air to capture spatial variations of the air pollutants, including home monitoring for a subsample of participants (N = 86–136) (Cohen et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2023). Geographic variables (e.g., roadway density, land use, and outputs from dispersion models), spatially varying long-term average concentrations, spatially varying seasonal and long-term trends, spatially correlated but temporally independent residuals, and complex monitoring design were used in the models (Jones et al., 2014; Keller et al., 2015; Kirwa et al., 2021). These models predict pollutant concentrations at residences for each 2-week period between 1999 and 2012 (Exams 1–5) and explain 90–97 % of the variation in measurements at participant homes (Keller et al., 2015). Residential mobility was considered and weighted by time period within each MESA study region, while participants who moved outside of the MESA study regions during the study period (Exams 1 to 5) were considered as missing (Wang et al., 2023). The performance of these models ranged from moderate to excellent as assessed by the overall cross-validation (CV) R2evaluated at participant residential locations (range: 0.45 in St. Paul, MN to 0.91 in New York, NY for PM2.5; 0.45 in Chicago, IL to 0.96 in Baltimore, MD for NO2; and 0.50 in New York, NY to 0.98 in St. Paul, MN for NOx) (Keller et al., 2015). A detailed description of the data collection and site selection procedures and the performance of the spatiotemporal models has been previously published (Cohen et al., 2009; Keller et al., 2015). In this study, levels of air pollutant exposure were expressed per interquartile range (IQR) difference. A higher value indicates a higher level of air pollutant exposure.

Mediators or modifiers: blood lipids

Blood lipid concentration was measured by lipid panel laboratory tests at Exam 5. Venous blood samples were collected after a 12-hour fast by certified technicians using standardized venipuncture procedures (Ong et al., 2018). High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; mg/dL) and the TC (mg/dL) were measured using the cholesterol oxidase method (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, Indiana) (Ong et al., 2018). Triglycerides (TG) concentrations (mg/dL) were measured using a glycerol-blanked enzymatic method with the TG GB reagent (Roche Diagnostics) on the Roche COBAS FARA centrifugal analyzer (Ong et al., 2018). In plasma samples that had a TG value less than 400 mg/dL, LDL-C was calculated using the Friedewald equation: LDL-C = TC-(HDL-C)-TG/5 (Ong et al., 2018; Friedewald et al., 1972; Liu et al., 2021).

Covariates

We used covariates from Exam 1 including MESA study site, individual sociodemographic factors, individual lifestyle, diabetes mellitus, medication history, and neighborhood factors. Individual sociodemographic factors included age (in years), gender (female as the reference), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White as the reference vs. Chinese, Hispanic, and Black), educational attainment (technical school, college, and above vs. some college without a degree and below as the reference), marital status (nonmarried as the reference), and annual household income (total gross family income in US. dollars). Individual lifestyle factors included physical activity (measured by using the adapted Cross-Cultural Activity Participation Study (Bild et al., 2002) and calculated as the total number of minutes of moderate and vigorous activity per week multiplied by metabolic equivalent level, MET minutes) (Bertoni et al., 2009), pack-years of cigarette smoking (in years), and alcohol use (current drinking vs. former or never drinking as the reference group). Diabetes was determined according to the 2003 American Diabetes Association (ADA) fasting criteria (being diabetic: a fasting plasma glucose level of at least 126 mg/dL or the use of oral antihyperglycemics or insulin vs. being non-diabetic: a fasting plasma glucose level <126 mg/dL) (Christine et al., 2015). Glucose sample collection and analysis were published elsewhere (Bertoni et al., 2010). Medication history included use of antihypertensives and lipid-lowering medications. Information on medication use was obtained by asking the participant to bring to the clinic containers for all medications used during the previous 2 weeks. The name of each medication, the prescribed dose, and the frequency of administration from the containers was recorded. Participants’ height (m) and weight (kg) were measured by clinicians at each MESA study site and used to calculate their body mass index [BMI; weight (kg)/height2 (m2)]. Each participant was also assessed for elevated levels of depressive symptoms (defined as Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale score ≥16 and/or use of antidepressant medication) (Ong et al., 2018; Garg et al., 2019). Apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype were dichotomized into APOE e4 allele ≥1 as carriers vs. non-carriers (Liang et al., 2013).

Neighborhood factors included percentage of park space, number of parks, population density (including land and water per mile square), and neighborhood disadvantage. All neighborhood measures were calculated within a 1-mile buffer, as prior evidence in this cohort has found this distance to be most salient (Hirsch et al., 2014). Neighborhood disadvantage at the census tract level was previously computed based on percentages of bachelor degree, managerial occupation, all-source income and assets given race/ethnicity from the U.S. Census 2000, American Community Survey (ACS) 2005–2009, and ACS 2007–2011 using principal component analysis (Christine et al., 2015). A higher value indicated more neighborhood disadvantage.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to examine the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants and distributions of variables versus air pollutants, cognitive performance, and blood lipids in the sample. Independent t tests were performed for continuous variables and Chi-squared tests for categorical variables.

We examined the associations of cognitive performance with air pollutant exposure and blood lipids using a set of generalized linear models. MESA study sites, and potential individual- and neighborhood covariates were adjusted for in all analytical models. Per hypothesis on the mediation effect of blood lipids on the association between air pollutant exposure and cognitive performance, we used generalized linear models and the inverse odds weighting (IOW) method (Nguyen et al., 2015; Hossin et al., 2019). It is a counterfactual-based approach that defines direct and indirect effects in a nonparametric framework. That is, the total effect can be decomposed into natural direct and indirect effects without having to fit any model for the mediators (Hossin et al., 2019). The method is flexible and can easily accommodate multiple mediators regardless of their scale (discrete or continuous); nonlinear association of outcomes exposure, mediators, and covariates; and interactions between exposure and mediators or among mediators (Nguyen et al., 2015). Since these weights were used in the direct effect model in lieu of the mediators per se, the mediators remained independent of the exposure. The purpose was to deactivate the potential pathways linking exposure to the mediators, thus generate valid mediation parameters regardless of the presence of exposure-mediator interactions. In this study, each IOW was computed for one specific air pollutant-cognitive domain analytical model (i.e., 3 air pollutants and 3 composite scores of domain-specific cognitive performance) and 9 IOWs were constructed in total. Implementation of each IOW estimation of direct and indirect effects of TC/LDL-C proceeded through 6 steps: 1) obtaining the predicted odds ratio from a linear regression model in which the air pollutant exposure was regressed on TC and LDL-C (mediators of interest) and covariates, 2) computing an IOW by taking the inverse of the predicted odds ration from step 1 for each observation in the exposed group, 3) estimating the direct effect of exposure via linear regression models of CASI, DSC, and DS with IOW obtained from step 2 on the air pollutant and covariates, 4) estimating the total effect of exposure using standard generalized linear models of cognitive performance on the air pollutant and covariates, 5) calculating the indirect effects of air pollutant exposure on CASI, DSC, and DC via TC/LDL-C by subtracting the direct effects from the total effects, and 6) Bootstrapping effect estimates (1000 bootstrap replications) to derive standard errors for direct, indirect, and total effects. The mediation analyses were conducted in Stata V.17.

Per hypothesis on the effect modification of blood lipids on the association between air pollutant exposure and cognitive performance, the main effects of air pollutants and blood lipids and their interaction terms were included in the linear regression models. For ease of interpretation, levels of air pollutant exposure and TC/LDL-C concentration were centered (i.e., subtracting the mean from each case) before computing the interaction terms and estimating the models. Given 18 statistical tests (3 air pollutant predictors × 2 blood lipids modifiers × 3 cognitive outcomes) conducted, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to adjust p values and control the false discovery rate (FDR) at 5 %. We used FDR-adjusted p values to interpret our results. This approach accounts for the increased risk of Type I errors due to multiple comparisons while preserving statistical power. Linear regression models with interaction terms were used to assess the heterogeneity in the association of cognitive performance with air pollution or blood lipids by gender and race/ethnicity.

In sensitivity analyses, first, we examined the blood lipid variation from Exam 5 to Exam 6 and whether the mediation effect of blood lipid was stable over time by using blood lipids at Exam 6 as mediators in the mediation analyses. Second, we examined whether the effect modification of blood lipids in the association of air pollutant exposure with cognitive performance remained similar as the main models by stratifying the sample via the use of lipid-lowering medications at Exam 5. Third, gender and race/ethnicity related heterogeneity in the associations of cognitive performance with air pollutants and blood lipids were examined by stratifying the generalized linear models. Last, we examined the impact of interaction terms of APOE, air pollutant, and TC/LDL-C on each cognitive domain. Modification and stratification analyses were conducted in SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, North Carolina).

Missing data

Participation in MESA has remained consistent over time; 6814 participated in Exam 1 (2000–2002), 4657 participated in Exam 5 (2010–2012), and 3150 completed Exam 6 (2016–2018). Approximately 60 % (1874 out of 3150) of participants in MESA Exam 6 underwent cognitive evaluation. The total proportion of missing observations in our data was 19 % with a range from 0 % to 16 % across the study variables. We used multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE) to handle the missingness. Under the assumption of missing at random, we used SAS ‘PROC MI’ with fully conditional specification (FCS) approach to create 10 imputed data sets to maximize the adjustment for this potential bias (vH, 2003). The imputed data sets were used in the modification and mediation analyses. To address the potential selection bias due to mortality or dropout, we used the inverse probability of selection weights (IPSW) in our modification analyses (using SAS) and the inverse odds weights in our mediation analyses (using Stata). Mortality affects the sample size but does not reduce the statistical power of analyses in this study (results of power analyses were presented in the Supplemental Materials).

Direct, indirect, and total effects for the hypothesized mediation models are presented in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, the direct effect of NO2 on DSC scores was significant (β =−2.64, 95 % CI −4.87 to −0.40) indicating that for each interquartile (11.9 ppb) increment of NO2 exposure in the full model, the DSC score was lower by 2.64. The total effect of NO2 on DSC scores was significant (β = −2.38, 95 % CI −4.42 to −0.34). However, the indirect effect of exposure to NO2 through TC or LDL-C on DSC scores was not significant (β = 0.26, 95 % CI −0.89 to 1.41). The direct effect of NOx on DSC scores was significant (β = −3.01, 95 % CI −5.20 to −0.83) indicating that for each interquartile (23.8 ppb) increment of NOx exposure, the DSC score was lower by 3.01. The total effect of NOx on DSC scores was significant (β = −2.74, 95 % CI −4.70 to −0.79). However, the indirect effect of exposure to NOx through TC or LDL-C on DSC scores was not significant (β = 0.27, 95 % CI −0.73 to 1.27). This means that TC or LDL-C did not mediate the association of NO2/NOx with DSC. However, NO2 and NOx were not associated with CASI or DS scores and the indirect effect of TC and LDL-C on these relationships were not significant. Additionally, PM2.5 was not found to be significantly associated with CASI, DSC, or DS scores.

Discussion

In this study, we found that the direct effects of NO2 and NOx on DSC scores were statistically significant based on our mediation models: higher NO2 or NOx exposure was significantly associated with a lower DSC score though the magnitude of these associations was relatively small. These findings align with prior cross-sectional and longitudinal evidence suggesting adverse impact of toxic gaseous pollutants such as oxides of nitrogen on cognitive function (Kulick et al., 2020; Petkus et al., 2021). One potential interpretation of this finding is that air pollution neurotoxicity may augment underlying brain aging processes, causing damage to brain regions and neural networks essential to maintain general brain functioning in late life (Petkus et al., 2021). We also found that the indirect effects of air pollutants on CASI or DS scores through TC or LDL-D were not significant. These results were consistent with the new epidemiological evidence showing that air pollution acts on cognition might not be clinically through the lipid pathway (Mei et al., 2024). The mechanism of air pollutants acting on cognitive function through the blood lipid pathway is still unclear in the existing literature. However, two speculative explanations were suggested by four recent epidemiological studies with a similar topic (Mei et al., 2024; Grande et al., 2020; Ilango et al., 2020; Peters et al., 2021). First, raised cholesterol commonly goes along with cardiovascular diseases, which has its greater potency as a risk factor for dementia and cognitive impairment. Thus, it is possible that any strong mediation effect of blood lipids would not be observed. Second, the trajectory of lipid profile may be the risk for cognitive decline rather than the absolute blood lipids levels in late adulthood (Peters et al., 2021). Blood lipid levels might fall with age and blood lipids may fall 10–20 years prior to dementia diagnosis (Peters et al., 2021). Our finding showed that LDL-C was negatively associated with CASI scores: elevated LDL-C levels appeared to be detrimental to cognitive function. This aligns with existing research suggesting that high LDL-C levels may contribute to vascular damage, inflammation, or other mechanisms that impair brain function, such as reduced blood flow or increased risk of neurodegeneration (Rohr et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2020; Besser et al., 2023, 2019). The adverse impact of LDL-C on cognitive function highlights the importance of managing cholesterol levels to protect brain health. Moreover, we found that TC was not significantly associated with CASI scores. This inconsistent finding could be caused by different study design, temporality structure, study population, and measures of cognitive function in our study compared to the other studies. Although it is inconsistent with what we expected, this finding supports the null associations between blood lipid concentration and cognitive function reported by prior research (Liu et al., 2020; Lv et al., 2016; Iwagami et al., 2021; Ong et al., 2018).

Moreover, we found that the impact of exposure to NO2 and NOx on CASI scores was modified by LDL-C concentration, although the modification effect was small. This finding suggests that the impact of air pollution on cognitive function changes depending on LDL-C levels, and the combined effect of high air pollution and high LDL-C is less negative than expected based on their individual effects. This interaction suggests that the detrimental effects of air pollution and LDL-C do not simply add up. Instead, high LDL-C levels may alter the way air pollution impacts cognitive function via medical intervention, such as the use of lipid-lowering medicine. Interventions targeting air pollution exposure might have different effects depending on individuals’ LDL-C levels. For example, reducing air pollution might not improve cognitive function in individuals with high LDL-C, if the interaction mitigates its negative impact.

In our sensitivity analyses, we identified this effect modification only existed in participants who used lipid-lower medications at Exam 5. In participants who had hypercholesterolemia and managed their cholesterol to the optimal or near the optimal levels using statins or other lipid-lowering medications, the adverse impact of air pollutant exposure on global cognition were identified as expected. For participants who did not use lipid-lowering medicine, the moderation effect of blood lipids was not statistically significant. Collectively, these findings suggest that the modification role of lipid health in the air pollutant-cognition relationship might not be clinically important or practically significant at individual or population level. However, it is possible that the adverse impact of air pollutant exposure on global cognitive function becomes more evident among participants with hypercholesteremia but regulated their TC/LDL-C to a low level using lipid-lowering medicine, especially statins. We suggest future studies assess the magnitude of TC/LDL-C lowering using statins in the context of air pollutant-cognition relationship. Additionally, drug type and duration of lipid-lowering therapy might be also adjusted for in the association between blood lipids and cognitive function (Ong et al., 2018; Kyriakos et al., 2020; Jamshidnejad-Tosaramandani et al., 2022). There was a lack of data on the duration of lipid-lowering therapy in MESA. This limited our further analysis by the duration of lipid-lowering therapy. Future research is needed to scrutinize these matters in the air pollution-blood lipids-cognition pathway.

In this study, we found that PM2.5 was not significantly associated with cognitive performance, and this might be partially due to the limited spatial and temporal variability in PM2.5 exposure levels in the MESA cohort. The low variability in PM2.5 is not surprising as it has been suggested by prior studies using MESA Air (Wang et al., 2023; Keller et al., 2015). We also found that long-term exposure to air pollutants was not significantly associated with DS scores. This finding is consistent with the recent evidence of another study focusing on air pollutants and cognitive function at Exam 5 using MESA (Wang et al., 2023). The less consistent association between DS scores and neighborhood measures in MESA (Besser et al., 2023; Besser et al., 2019) and different sensitivity of cognitive domains to air pollutants might contribute to this finding. Prior studies suggest domain specific variation in relation to different air pollutant exposure using community- and population-based evidence. For example, exposure to higher levels of NO2, NOx, and PM2.5 had adverse effects on global cognition or working memory (Ailshire and Crimmins, 2014; Schikowski and Altug, 2020; Gatto et al., 2014; Schikowski and Altuğ, 2020; Schikowski and Vierkotter, 2015). Thus, mechanisms of the neurological pathway of cognitive domains affected by specific components of air pollutants or source of exposure require elucidation with more sensitive and specific measures. Additionally, our study did not find gender or race/ethnicity related heterogeneity in the associations of cognitive performance with air pollutant exposure or blood lipids. These findings align with recent findings of similar studies using MESA (Ong et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2022).

There are several limitations of our study. First, although the delayed effect of air pollutant exposure on blood lipid concentration and the subsequent pathway to cognitive performance were examined, our analysis was limited by a short observational period on blood lipids and cognitive performance. Additionally, blood lipids were collected at a specific time on a specific day, which may not reflect changes or patterns of individual blood lipids over time. Second, there may be potential selection bias in effect estimates. Older adults enrolled in MESA Air lived in urban areas, had higher educational levels, and were healthier than the general population (Wang et al., 2023). It is possible that the level of exposure to air pollutants could be high across the MESA sites due to urbanicity so we may not see the variability in long-term air pollutant exposure. Higher educational attainment and better health status may help preserve their cognition from age-related cognitive decline. Third, while the diversity of MESA participants is a strength, participants in MESA were not evenly distributed by site (e.g., Minnesota recruited only White and Hispanic individuals) (Besser et al., 2021), this reduced the sample size for each racial subgroup and statistical power to conduct race/ethnicity stratified analyses. Multi-site cohorts with large samples of each race/ethnicity would be best to address this limitation. Fourth, variability in PM2.5 exposure levels is limited and this is possibly associated with the selected study sites and limitations of the spatiotemporal models in MESA Air, included using time-invariant geographic covariates and potential overfitting caused by conditioning on the time trends and the partial least squared scores (PLS) using the AQS and fixed sites (Keller et al., 2015). Given the substantial geographic variability in pollution levels, particle composition, and potential co-exposures, the effects of air pollution on cognitive performance could vary meaningfully across locations. Therefore, future studies are needed to determine if our findings differ depending on geographic locations. Last, we adjusted the data for most identified and measured potential confounders, but we cannot rule out biases due to unmeasured confounders. There may be complex mixes of medications that could impact outcomes and be patterned by access to medical care (and thus air quality), or nuanced pathways involving life-course structural determinants such as childhood education and exposure or midlife career and occupational risk. These limitations may potentially explain why TC/LDL-C were not found to be statistically significant mediators or play a strong modification role in the association between air pollutant exposure and cognitive performance in this sample.

Despite these limitations, our study is one of the first studies that explore the role of blood lipids in the association of long-term air pollutant exposure with cognitive performance using a racial/ethnically diverse and well-characterized sample of clinically healthy participants. This study contributes to the existing environmental health literature in enriching and replenishing the examination of environment–cognition relationship in older adults and providing the basis for future research to elucidate mechanisms of blood lipids affecting cognitive function and subsequently characterize potential modifiable risks for AD/ADRD. Moreover, findings of the study add new knowledge to the growing science in identifying and characterizing the role of blood lipid in the pathway of long-term exposure to multiple air pollutants to cognitive aging after adjusting for individual- and neighborhood-levels of characteristics. Air pollution monitoring and control can be a potential public health effort to protect brain health and possibly delay the onset of cognitive impairment and dementia in late adulthood.

In conclusion, exposure to a higher level of toxic gaseous pollutants (NO2 and NOx) was associated with domain-specific lower cognitive function in the MESA cohort. Blood lipid concentrations did not appear to mediate or practically modify the air pollutant-cognition relationship. While confirmative studies are required, our findings suggest that screening tests for cognitive decline should be recommended by health care providers to individuals with hypercholesterolemia diagnosis since midlife, especially for those who use lipid-lowering medications. Moreover, drug type and duration of lipid-lowering therapy should be monitored and considered as the evaluation of cognitive health. Our findings also recommend interventions to reduce exposure to air pollutants for healthy older individuals may lead to substantial benefits for their cognitive health.
