Association between asymptomatic detection of distant metastases and survival in breast cancer: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Su Min Lee, Sung Mi Jung, Jai Min Ryu, Byung Joo Chae, Jonghan Yu, Jeong Eon Lee, Seok Won Kim, Seok Jin Nam, Se Kyung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer incidence is increasing worldwide, leading to a growing population of survivors and raising questions about optimal surveillance strategies. Current guidelines do not recommend routine imaging for asymptomatic breast cancer survivors, yet advances in diagnostic technologies may enable earlier detection of distant metastases and potentially improve outcomes. The aim of this study was to evaluate the association between symptom status at the time of distant metastasis detection and post-metastasis overall survival in breast cancer patients.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 7,840 women who underwent surgery for primary breast cancer at Samsung Medical Center between 2010 and 2014 and identified 316 patients who subsequently developed distant metastases. Patients were classified as asymptomatic (n = 204, 64.6%) if metastases were detected during routine follow-up without symptoms, or symptomatic (n = 112, 35.4%) if metastases were diagnosed after symptom onset. Post-metastasis overall survival (PMOS) was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards models according to symptom status, metastatic burden, and breast cancer subtype.ResultsPatients whose metastases were detected while asymptomatic had significantly better PMOS than those diagnosed after symptom onset (p < 0.001). Patients with bone-only metastasis showed more favorable survival than those with multi-organ metastases, indicating that lower tumor burden at the time of diagnosis is associated with better prognosis. In subtype-specific analyses, the survival advantage of asymptomatic detection was confined to hormone receptor (HR)-positive disease (p < 0.001), whereas no clear difference was observed among HER2-positive patients.ConclusionAsymptomatic detection of distant metastases, particularly in HR-positive breast cancer, is associated with improved survival and appears to be linked to lower metastatic burden at diagnosis. These findings highlight the potential relevance of symptom status and tumor characteristics in post-metastatic outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04367-5.

Background

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women worldwide, and its incidence is steadily increasing [1]. Although advances in early diagnosis and treatment have significantly improved survival rates, the increasing number of breast cancer survivors has led to a growing interest in surveillance strategies to detect distant metastases during follow-up period [2]. Since metastases account for the majority of breast cancer-related deaths, it is important to have a surveillance strategy that balances oncological outcomes with the cost and potential negative consequences of testing. Despite advances in diagnostic imaging techniques and approaches to the treatment of metastatic breast cancer, the utility of intensive surveillance targeting asymptomatic metastases remains controversial [3]. The US National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines do not recommend routine surveillance imaging for the detection of recurrence or distant metastases in asymptomatic breast cancer survivors, emphasizing that the decision to screen should be based on the presence or absence of symptoms [4]. This recommendation is based on the lack of clear evidence that early detection of metastases in asymptomatic patients improves overall survival.

However, recent advances in imaging technology have increased the likelihood of early detection of small metastatic lesions, and studies have reported that advances in imaging and biomarker-based surveillance allow earlier detection of metastatic disease and earlier initiation of systemic therapy in asymptomatic patients [5]. However, whether such earlier detection translates into a survival benefit remains uncertain. As diagnostic imaging and treatment technologies for metastatic breast cancer continue to advance, some clinicians have begun to advocate for the development of new strategies to detect metastases early, even in asymptomatic breast cancer patients [6]. These findings suggest the need for new patient-specific surveillance strategies that can improve prognosis through intensive surveillance in certain patients and reduce unnecessary testing in others to minimize the psychological and physical burden of excessive testing.

In this study, we aimed to first determine whether the overall post-metastatic survival (PMOS) of breast cancer patients undergoing intensive surveillance is indeed dependent on the presence or absence of symptoms, and thus whether intensive surveillance is warranted for early detection of metastases in asymptomatic patients. We further aimed to identify other factors that influence PMOS and to characterize the patient population that requires intensive surveillance, in order to optimize the surveillance management of breast cancer survivors and improve the long-term prognosis of these patients by improving existing guidelines.

Methods

Study design and participants

A total of 7,840 patients underwent surgery for breast cancer from January 2010 to December 2014 at Samsung Medical Center. Of these, nine patients with stage IV at diagnosis were excluded; The patients had palliative breast surgery because of wound care or progressive/stable disease on breast lesion despite the improvement of metastatic organ. In addition, patients with locoregional recurrence, lack of follow up or electric medical records after metastasis, or without distant metastasis were excluded. Finally, we conducted a retrospective review of 316 breast cancer patients. These patients were divided into two groups based on presence or absence of symptoms at the time of metastasis detection; the asymptomatic group had 204 patients (64.6%) while the symptomatic group had 112 patients (35.4%) (Fig. 1).

Patient selection flowchart

Patients meeting the inclusion criteria were monitored for distant metastasis during follow-up using imaging and laboratory tests. Physical examination, tumor markers (Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA), Cancer Antigen 15 − 3 (CA 15 − 3)), breast ultrasonography, mammography, breast magnetic resonance imaging chest computed tomography (C-CT), abdomino-pelvic computed tomography (AP-CT) and bone scan (BS) were performed during follow-up, with the type and frequency of imaging varying according to the initial stage and clinical indication.

Imaging and laboratory tests were performed under the South Korean health insurance reimbursement framework and institutional follow-up practice during the study period [7, 8]. C-CT, AP-CT and BS were obtained at the treating physician’s discretion based on the initial stage and clinical indications, including symptoms or signs suggestive of metastasis and/or abnormal test results. In routine follow-up, these evaluations were typically performed at intervals of approximately 6–12 months or, in some patients, every 24 months.

A total of 316 metastatic breast cancer patients was divided into two groups: the asymptomatic group included patients who were diagnosed with distant metastasis without symptoms during regular follow-up, while the symptomatic group included patients who were diagnosed with distant metastasis with symptoms either during or before the regular follow-up period. To address concerns regarding actual imaging utilization in clinical practice, we additionally summarized the frequency of CT and BS performed prior to the diagnosis of distant metastasis according to disease stage and symptom status. These analyses were stratified by neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) use, using pathologic prognostic stage in patients without NAC and pre-treatment clinical stage in patients receiving NAC, and are presented in Supplementary Tables 1,2. Because imaging exposure may be influenced by the length of disease-free interval (DFI) was summarized alongside imaging utilization to facilitate interpretation. Patients diagnosed with distant metastasis received palliative treatment according to our institution’s guidelines. Patients were defined as having a family history of breast cancer when a first- or second-degree relative had a history of breast cancer. During the study period, there were no major changes in national treatment guidelines for primary or metastatic breast cancer that would be expected to systematically influence treatment strategies or post-metastatic outcomes. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (IRB file no. 2020-06-012).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were compared with Student’s t-test, and categorical variables were compared with Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were reported as the number and percentage. DFI and the utilization of CT and BS prior to the diagnosis of distant metastasis were included as covariates in multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to adjust for differences in follow-up duration and surveillance intensity. PMOS was analyzed using univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses, and the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank test was used to compare survival curves of the asymptomatic and symptomatic groups. 95% (95%) confidence intervals and hazard ratios were calculated, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

Patient characteristics

In this study, patients were categorized into two groups based on the presence or absence of symptoms at the time of metastasis detection. Baseline characteristics, including age, type of surgery, histopathology, and pathologic staging, were similar between the two groups. However, BRCA1/2 mutation status differed between the two groups. Hormone receptor (HR)-positive breast cancer was significantly more prevalent in the asymptomatic group (69.7%) compared to the symptomatic group (53.6%) (p = 0.013). In contrast, there was no significant difference in the proportion of HER2-positive patients between the two groups. The proportion of triple-negative breast cancer did not differ significantly. Regarding treatment modalities, the symptomatic group had a significantly higher percentage of patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy (42.9% vs. 28.4%, p = 0.009) and adjuvant radiotherapy (90.2% vs. 76.0%, p = 0.002); however, the proportion of patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy did not differ between the groups. The symptomatic group had a significantly shorter DFI compared to the asymptomatic group (Table 1).

To assess the impact of symptom status on metastatic patterns, we compared the distribution of metastatic sites between the two groups. Among the 316 patients, 84 (26.6%) had metastases to two or more organs, 78 (24.7%) had bone-only metastases, and 154 (48.7%) had metastases to a single organ other than bone. The distribution of metastatic sites did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.287, Table 2).

Prognostic Factors for Post-Metastasis Overall Survival (PMOS)

Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted to evaluate factors influencing PMOS, including symptom status (Table 3). Univariate analysis showed that patients in the symptomatic group had a significantly higher risk of death than those in the asymptomatic group (HR: 2.402, 95% CI: 1.748–3.301, p < 0.001). Patients with multiple metastases had a significantly higher mortality risk than those with bone-only metastases (HR: 2.326, 95% CI: 1.457–3.714, p < 0.001). Similarly, patients with metastases to a single organ other than bone had an increased risk of death compared to those with bone metastases alone (HR: 1.783, 95% CI: 1.154–2.755, p = 0.009).

In multivariate analysis adjusted for confounding variables, patients in the symptomatic group continued to have a higher risk of death than those in the asymptomatic group (HR: 2.541, 95% CI: 1.834–3.521, p < 0.001). After adjustment for DFI and imaging utilization (CT and BS), symptomatic presentation remained independently associated with poorer PMOS, indicating that the observed survival difference was not explained by differences in follow-up duration or surveillance intensity. Additionally, patients with multiple metastases had significantly lower survival than those with bone-only metastases (HR: 2.557, 95% CI: 1.562–4.186, p < 0.001). However, survival outcomes did not differ significantly between patients with metastases to a single organ other than bone and those with bone-only metastases (HR: 1.314, 95% CI: 0.825–2.095, p = 0.250).

During a median follow-up of 33 months after the diagnosis of distant metastases, 160 patients died, including 87 in the asymptomatic group and 73 in the symptomatic group. Multivariate analysis identified symptom status as a significant factor influencing PMOS. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further confirmed that the asymptomatic group had significantly better survival than the symptomatic group (p < 0.001, Fig. 2). These findings suggest that patients whose metastases are detected before symptom onset tend to have a better prognosis.

Subtype-specific analysis

Univariate and multivariate analyses identified HR-positive status as a significant predictor of PMOS, whereas HER2-positive status was not significantly associated with survival. Based on these results, we further analyzed survival differences between HR-positive and HER2-positive patients according to symptom status. Among HR-positive breast cancer patients, the asymptomatic group had significantly better survival than the symptomatic group (p < 0.001). In contrast, among HER2-positive breast cancer patients, survival did not significantly differ based on symptom status after metastasis (p > 0.05, Fig. 3). Absolute PMOS, including median PMOS and 1- and 3-year PMOS rates according to symptom status, are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Overall, asymptomatic detection of distant metastases was associated with longer PMOS compared with symptomatic detection in this cohort.

Discussions

This study revealed several key findings regarding the prognosis of breast cancer patients with distant metastases. First, patients whose metastases were detected while asymptomatic had significantly better survival outcomes compared to those whose metastases were diagnosed after symptoms developed. Second, patients with bone-only metastases had better PMOS than those with multiorgan or visceral metastases, suggesting that a lower tumor burden at the time of metastasis diagnosis is favorable for prognosis and that early detection may lead to enhanced survival. Finally, among HR-positive patients, survival was significantly better when metastases were detected in the asymptomatic state; however, no significant difference was observed in HER2-positive patients. These results suggest that the association between asymptomatic detection and survival may vary by subtype and that, particularly in HR-positive disease, this association likely reflects differences in tumor biology and detection timing rather than a direct survival benefit attributable to early detection.

Current NCCN guidelines recommend a symptom-driven surveillance approach, which includes annual mammography and physical examinations but does not advocate routine imaging or laboratory testing for asymptomatic patients in the absence of clinical signs of recurrence [4]. While this approach may help reduce unnecessary testing, the role of asymptomatic surveillance remains unclear. Some studies have reported that asymptomatic detection of locoregional recurrence or contralateral breast cancer is associated with improved survival compared with symptomatic detection [9, 10]. However, the potential benefit of intensive surveillance aimed at detecting distant metastases in asymptomatic breast cancer survivors remains controversial, and other studies have found that intensive surveillance programs do not provide a survival benefit for asymptomatic breast cancer patients [11–13]. In our study, survival was significantly higher when metastases were detected while asymptomatic, suggesting that early detection and therapeutic intervention before symptom onset may lead to enhanced survival in certain patient populations. However, this does not imply that intensive surveillance is necessary for all asymptomatic patients. Rather, it suggests that intensive surveillance may be warranted in select cases, even in the absence of symptoms. It should be noted, however, that several recent studies and meta-analyses supporting asymptomatic or intensive surveillance included a substantial proportion of retrospective observational studies. As such, these findings may be subject to selection bias, lead-time bias, and confounding by indication, and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

In our cohort, metastatic burden was associated with PMOS: patients with multiple metastatic sites showed poorer survival than those with bone-only metastases. Consistent with previous reports, this pattern supports the prognostic relevance of metastatic burden [14–16]. However, survival outcomes did not differ significantly between patients with bone-only metastases and those with a single non-bone metastatic site after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that metastatic burden, rather than the specific site of metastasis alone, may be more relevant to prognosis.

Many factors influence the risk of distant metastasis and survival outcomes in breast cancer, including the tumor’s biological subtype, size, lymph node involvement, and histologic grade [17, 18]. According to the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database [17], HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer patients are more likely to develop metastases compared to HR-negative, HER2-positive patients, regardless of their metastatic pattern. Additionally, previous studies have reported differences in the time to metastasis and survival outcomes based on tumor subtype [14, 19]. In particular, HR-positive metastatic breast cancer is characterized by a relatively slow rate of disease progression and a strong response to endocrine therapy and systemic treatments such as CDK4/6 inhibitors [20, 21].

This is thought to be a key factor contributing to the significant survival benefit from early detection of metastases. In our study, asymptomatic detection was associated with improved PMOS in HR-positive breast cancer. However, given the indolent natural history of HR-positive disease, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect differences in tumor biology and detection timing rather than a causal survival benefit from surveillance [14, 22]. Accordingly, our results should not be interpreted as supporting routine prolonged imaging surveillance with CT or bone scan in HR-positive patients. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether tailored follow-up strategies can improve outcomes while minimizing unnecessary testing.

Although the incidence of breast cancer has been steadily rising in recent years, advances in early diagnosis and treatment have significantly improved overall survival, leading to an increasing number of breast cancer survivors [9]. This underscores the importance of appropriate follow-up after completion of primary treatment. The findings of this study highlight the need to move away from uniform surveillance strategies and instead consider risk-adapted follow-up approaches tailored to individual patient characteristics. Importantly, given the potential impact of lead-time bias, our results should not be interpreted as supporting intensive surveillance aimed at earlier detection of metastatic disease. In addition to lead-time bias, the observed differences between asymptomatic and symptomatic patients may also reflect underlying biological differences, including tumor aggressiveness and growth kinetics, which could influence both the likelihood of symptom development and post-metastatic survival. Future research should focus on defining follow-up strategies that balance clinical relevance with the avoidance of unnecessary testing, rather than assuming a direct survival benefit from earlier detection.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study conducted at a single institution, there is a possibility of selection bias, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Because this was a retrospective, single-center study, the generalizability of our findings to other populations and clinical settings may be limited. Second, we could not evaluate outcomes according to solitary versus multiple bone lesions or the impact of local therapy for oligometastatic disease because these variables were not systematically captured. Third, the asymptomatic group may have been affected by screening time bias, where early detection of metastases could artificially prolong survival time without altering the natural course of the disease. Forth, all breast cancer patients in this study underwent regular follow-up under the Korean national insurance policy, making direct comparisons with patients who did not receive regular follow-up difficult. An ideal study design would include patients without regular surveillance and stratify them based on the presence or absence of symptoms to more clearly assess the prognostic value of asymptomatic metastases. Fifth, we did not evaluate the costs or cost-effectiveness of different surveillance strategies, and therefore our findings should not be interpreted as supporting intensified imaging-based follow-up from an economic perspective. Therefore, future multicenter, prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the impact of early detection of asymptomatic metastases on survival outcomes and to determine the optimal interval and modality for intensive surveillance strategies. Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates a high survival rate among patients whose metastases were diagnosed while asymptomatic and provides meaningful insights that may contribute to improving surveillance strategies for breast cancer patients, particularly by highlighting the potential benefits of early detection in HR-positive breast cancer patients.

Conclusion

This study revealed several findings regarding the prognosis of breast cancer patients with distant metastases. Patients whose metastases were detected while in an asymptomatic state had a significantly higher survival rate than those diagnosed after the onset of symptoms, and patients with bone-only metastases had a better prognosis than those with multiorgan or visceral metastases. The observed association between asymptomatic detection and post-metastatic survival, including in HR-positive patients, should be interpreted cautiously given the potential impact of lead-time bias and underlying biological heterogeneity. This suggests the need to identify high-risk patients with an increased likelihood of metastasis and apply optimized surveillance strategies to complement traditional symptom-based approaches. Future studies should validate whether these personalized surveillance strategies contribute to improved survival and establish optimal surveillance intervals and methods to develop a more refined breast cancer surveillance system that minimizes unnecessary testing while improving patient outcomes.
