Organic acid accumulation pattern and its key genes in Chinese cherry fruits
Authors: Yan Wang, Jingting Zhou, Xiaocheng Liu, Lisu Hao, Yunfan Yang, Shuaiwei Yang, Hao Wang, Zhenshan Liu, Jing Zhang, Wen He, Yuanxiu Lin, Yunting Zhang, Mengyao Li, Yong Zhang, Ya Luo, Haoru Tang, Qing Chen, Xiaorong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChinese cherry [Cerasus pseudocerasus (Lindl.) G.Don], an economically important fruit species native to southwestern China, plays a key role in regional agriculture. Organic acid composition is crucial for determining organoleptic quality of Chinese cherry, yet the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain unclear.ResultsIn this study, we analyzed organic acid composition in mature fruits from 34 Chinese cherry accessions and tracked dynamic changes throughout fruit development in two landraces with distinct acidity levels (high and low), via HPLC and transcriptomics. Malic acid is the predominant organic acid component, which accounted for 73.66% of total acids. The high-acid landrace exhibited very rapid malate accumulation and relatively fast degradation, while the low-acid landrace showed minor changes in malate levels throughout fruit development. A total of 7,698 DEGs were clustered into six clusters, with DEGs from three clusters being significantly enriched in the “pyruvate metabolism” and “TCA cycle” pathways. Key genes involved in malate biosynthesis (cMDH, PEPC2) and transport (ALMT4, VHP) were up-regulated in the high-acid landrace, while NADP-ME, a gene associated with malate degradation, was down-regulated. Co-expressed network analysis highlighted strong correlations between key structural genes and transcription factors (MYB, ARF, AP2, and bHLH). Notably, CpODORANT1like and CpARF2Blike were identified as potential regulators of acidity, modulating NADP-ME and PEPC2 expression, respectively. These candidate genes were validated through an integrated analysis of phenotypic data and expression pattern of 34 genotypes.ConclusionsOur results suggest that malate accumulation in Chinese cherry is regulated at both the metabolic and vacuolar storage levels. This study deepens our understanding of the mechanisms regulating fruit acidity in Chinese cherry, which could inform future breeding efforts aimed at improving fruit flavor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11969-0.Keywords: Chinese cherry fruits, Organic acid, Accumulation pattern, Key genes, Regulatory network

Introduction

Chinese cherry [Cerasus pseudocerasus (Lindl.) G.Don] (syn. Prunus pseudocerasus Lindl.) is a commercially significant perennial fruit crop in the Rosaceae family, is valued for delicious taste, health benefits, and ornamental appeal [1]. The fruit is rich in nutrients and bioactive components, including carbohydrates, anthocyanins, and phenols. Despite these benefits, previous field investigations and comparative evaluations of Chinese cherry germplasms have highlighted a key limitation: the pronounced sourness, which hinders its fresh consumption and industry development [2]. Fruit acidity is primarily determined by the compositions and concentrations of organic acids, which are crucial to flavor quality. Our previous study suggested that organic acids may be a greater contributor to the flavor quality of Chinese cherry fruits than soluble sugars [3]. Therefore, understanding the variations and dynamic changes in acid components during fruit ripening is essential for improving the flavor profile and guiding genetic improvements in Chinese cherry fruit quality.

The composition and concentration of organic acids vary across species, developmental stages, and tissues. The predominant organic acids in most stone fruits are malic, citric, and quinic acids [4–6]. Apricots and peaches typically contain high levels of both malic and citric acids [7, 8], whereas sweet and sour cherries, as well as plums, are rich in malic acid [9, 10]. During fruit development, malic and citric acids typically increase in the green stage and decrease as ripening progresses [5, 8]. Similarly, malic acid predominates among the organic acids in Chinese cherry accessions [3]. During domestication, a notable decrease in acid levels — particularly malic acid — has become a distinctive feature [3]. However, the patterns of organic acid accumulation throughout fruit development and ripening in Chinese cherry remain unclear.

The accumulation of organic acids in plant cells is primarily regulated by metabolic processes (glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid cycle) and vacuolar storage [11]. The organic acid metabolism initiates with the production of cytoplasmic dicarboxylate anions. Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) captures CO2 through the action of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylate (PEPC), resulting in the formation of oxaloacetate (OAA). Malate is then generated by malate dehydrogenase (MDH) and transported into the vacuole via vacuolar transporters, where it plays a key role in vacuolar acidification [11]. The NADP-dependent malic enzyme catalyzes the conversion of malate to pyruvate in the cytoplasm [12]. Transporters facilitating malate transport, including tonoplast malate transporter (tDT) [13] and aluminum-dependent malate transporters (ALMTs) [14, 15], have been recognized as essential contributors to the transmembrane movement of organic acids and the accumulation of malate in fruit. Tonoplast proton pumps, including V-ATPase (vacuolar-type H+-ATPase), V-PPase (vacuolar-type H+-PPase), and P-ATPase, drive proton transport, thereby promoting the diffusion of malate and citrate into the vacuole [16–18]. Furthermore, various transcription factors, such as AP2 [19], bHLH [20], ERF [21], MYB [22–25], and WRKY [26], regulate the accumulation of organic acids by modulating the expression of genes associated with their metabolism and transport. Additionally, three major quantitative trait loci (QTLs) on LG3 have been reported to control titratable acidity of Chinese cherry [27], which differs from the major QTL on LG6 of sweet cherry [28, 29]. However, the regulatory mechanism underlying organic acid accumulation of Chinese cherry fruit remains unknown.

In the present study, we assess organic acid content and composition in mature fruits from thirty-four representative Chinese cherry accessions with varying fruit acidity levels. We then explore the patterns of organic acid accumulation during fruit development by selecting two representative landraces with contrasting acidity. Through comparative transcriptome analysis, we further identify candidate genes involved in acid metabolism. This study enhances our understanding of the mechanisms governing fruit acidity in Chinese cherry, which may guide future breeding efforts for fruit flavor improvement.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Matured fruits from seven landraces, one wild accession, and twenty-six hybrid accessions of Chinese cherry were harvested for population analysis (Table S1). These samples represented a wide range of titratable acid (TA) values in Chinese cherry [30]. In addition, two landraces, ‘Hongfei’ (HF) and ‘Bijie7-2’ (BJ7-2), were selected to explore the dynamic changes of organic acid accumulation during fruit development and ripening. All accessions were grown at the Cherry Germplasms Repository of Sichuan Province (Chengdu, Sichuan, China) under uniform field management practices. Fruits were randomly collected at maturity in 2022, based on our previous records of maturity dates, fruit size and color [3]. Each accession provided at least 300 g of fruits. For HF and BJ7-2, samples were collected at eight stages of fruit development in 2022, ranging from young to mature fruits (Fig. 2A). At each stage, three biological replicates were collected, each consisting of at least 100 g of fruits. The fruits, with pits removed, were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃ for further analysis.

Determination of organic acid components in Chinese cherry fruit

The TA content was determined by the electroconductivity method with Pocket Brix-Acidity Meter Master Kit (ATAGO, Tokyo, Japan). Approximately 0.5 g of fresh fruit juice was collected and diluted with ddH2O to a final volume of 25.00 g for TA measurements. Each replicate comprises ten fresh cherries, for a total of three replicates per sample point.

Organic acids were extracted from the mixed fruit peel and flesh samples and quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with an Agilent 1260 II HPLC system (Agilent Technologies Co. Ltd., Santa Clara, USA). A total of 11 types of organic acid standard solutions (Yuanye Bio-Tech, Shanghai, China) were used for the determination. The procedure was carried out following the method described in a previous study [3]. Briefly, organic acids were detected using an Agilent G1314F Variable Wavelength Detector (VWD) at 210 nm. A Restek Allure Organic Acid column (4.6 mm × 350 mm, 5 μm) was employed, with the column maintained at 20℃. The mobile phase consisted of 5% methanol and 95% phosphoric acid solution. The flow rate was set at 0.6 mL⋅min−1, with a run time of 25 min. Three biological replicates were prepared per sample point, and both extraction and HPLC analysis were performed on all replicates. The organic acid contents were recorded as g⋅kg−1 DW (dry weight). The standard curve for each acid component determination is presented in Table S2.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Fruit samples were collected for RNA-seq based on the pattern of organic acid accumulation at four stages: hard core stage (S1), turning stage (S2), coloring stage (S3), and mature stage (S4). The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the CNGB database with accession number of CNP0003682. In brief, raw data were trimmed using Trim Galore with Q > 30 and then aligned to the reference genome of Chinese cherry (CNGB database, CNP0006741) using HISAT2 v.2.1.0. The number of reads mapped to each gene was calculated using the HTSeq v2.0.3 software. The FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) values were used to represent gene expression levels.

To investigate the factors contributing to the variations in fruit acidity, we compared the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across fruit development stages and between two landraces. DEGs were identified using the criteria of |log2Fold change| >1 with a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01. TBtools-II software was employed to perform gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05 for enriched pathways [31]. To visualize expression patterns, DEGs were normalized (Z-score) and clustered through the Fuzzy cMeans clustering method. The possible transcription factors were screened and categorized using the TFPlantDB v5.0 online tool (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org, accessed on 31 October 2024) [32]. Hierarchical clustering and heatmap visualization of DEGs were generated by TBtools-II v2.031 [31].

Weighted correlation network analysis

Correlation analysis was conducted between organic acid components, structural genes, and transcription factors involved in metabolic pathways using OmicStudio platform [33]. To explore the relationships between organic acid metabolism and significant DEGs, a correlation network diagram was constructed using pairs with a Pearson correlation coefficient (|r| ≥ 0.7) at p < 0.05. The resulting correlation network plot of acids and DEGs was generated using Cytoscape software v3.7.1 [34].

qRT-PCR analysis

To confirm the transcriptomic data, twelve DEGs related to acid metabolism were chosen, and their expression patterns were evaluated through qRT-PCR analysis. Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR procedure were carried out as described in the previous study [35]. The 2−△△Ct method was employed to obtain the relative expression levels, with β-actin and ubiquitin being double internal reference genes. The gene-specific primer pairs are designed using Primer Premier 5 software and provided in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Organic acid contents were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2020 and expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to measure the correlation between parameters. The data were statistically analyzed with one-way ANOVA and Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05). Statistical differences were compared at different fruit developmental stages and between landraces. Above analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v.27.

Results and discussion

Variation of organic acid components in Chinese cherry fruits

Main organic acid components in mature fruits

The components and contents of organic acids in mature fruits from thirty-four Chinese cherry accessions were determined, with the results depicted in Fig. 1 and Table S1. Titratable acid content varied significantly among genotypes, ranging from 0.30 to 1.14%. Based on the LSD method and a previous study [3], the TA content was categorized into three levels: high acidity (≥ 0.9%, H), moderate acidity (0.6%~0.9%, M), and low acidity (< 0.6%, L) (Fig. 1A). Total organic acid level varied from 24.43 to 110.92 g·kg−1 DW, with a mean value of 52.84 g·kg−1 DW, reflecting a 4.54-fold variation among the studied genotypes. Three major components were identified: malic, quinic, and citric acid, with average concentration of 38.62, 6.39, and 3.56 g·kg−1 DW, respectively (Table S1). On average, malic, quinic, and citric acids accounted for 73.66%, 10.77%, and 7.32% of the total organic acids, respectively, indicating their predominant role in the composition (Table S1). These findings are consistent with reports of other fruiting cherries [3, 5, 6, 36].

For the three acidity level groups, the mean malate content was 57.91, 39.77, and 26.70 g·kg−1 DW, respectively, showing statistically significant differences (Fig. 1B). The proportion of malate was approximately 82% in both the H- and M- acid groups, much higher than that of the L- acid group (61.96%) (Fig. 1C). Quinate content and proportion showed no significant differences among the three groups (Fig. 1B, C). The citrate content and proportion were much higher in the L acidity group than the M acidity group but remained low across all genotypes. Given the stable quinate levels and low citrate levels, we propose that malate content and proportion are the primary contributors to fruit acidity in Chinese cherry. To further explore the organic acid accumulation profile in Chinese cherry fruits, a linear analysis between TA and malic acid content was conducted. A strong positive correlation between TA and malate was observed (r = 0.839) (Fig. 1D) compared to the correlations with quinate and citrate (Fig. S1A, B). A multiple regression analysis further confirmed this finding (Fig. S1C), indicating that malic acid level can serve as an indicator of fruit acidity in Chinese cherry.

Dynamic changes of organic acids during fruit development

Two representative landraces, ‘Hongfei’ (HF) and ‘Bijie7-2’ (BJ7-2), were chosen to illustrate the accumulation pattern of organic acid throughout fruit development process (Fig. 2A). The TA content in both landraces showed a gradual increase during early fruit development, followed by a decline as the fruits matured (Fig. 2B). This pattern is consistent with other stone fruits, such as plum [37] and Chinese dwarf cherry [5], but differs from the trend observed in sweet cherry [38]. In both landraces, TA content increased from stage S1 (0.75%, DAF18) to stage S2 (1.71% for HF at DAF30, for BJ7-2 at DAF36), with no significant differences between the two landraces. However, at stage S3 (DAF42), the TA content in HF fruits sharply decreased by 74.19% to 0.45% and remained stable through the mature stage (S4, DAF54). In contrast, TA content in BJ7-2 fruits decreased more gradually by 33.27% to 1.14% at stage S3, and only slightly at stage S4 to 1.13%. Notably, at stage S3 and S4, the TA content in BJ7-2 fruits was significantly higher than in HF fruits. These differences in total acid contents account for the variation in natural acidity between these two landraces.

Malic acid and quinic acid were identified as the primary organic acids in both HF and BJ7-2 mature fruits, collectively accounting for 89.94% and 89.79% of total acid content, respectively (Fig. 2C, D, F, G). Regarding the accumulation pattern, malic acid content showed significant changes during fruit development and maturation. In both HF and BJ7-2, malic acid content increased by 96.97% and 222.08% at S2 compared to stage S1 but then decreased by 39.88% and 60.29% at S4 compared to stage S2 (Fig. 2C). Malic acid accounted for 66.15% and 68.69% of the total acid in HF and BJ7-2, respectively (Fig. 2G), which is accordance with patterns observed in Chinese dwarf cherry [5] and peach [4]. Quinic acid content exhibited a modest increase during stages S1 and S3, followed by a sharp rise, increasing by 2.62 and 4.40 times at stage S4 compared to stage S3 (Fig. 2D). Quinate contributed 23.43% and 21.10% of the total acid in ripe HF and BJ7-2 fruits (Fig. 2G). In ripe fruits, malic acid and quinic acid contents in BJ7-2 were 2.15- and 1.87- fold higher than in HF, with both differences being statistically significant. Thus, BJ7-2 exhibited a very rapid malate accumulation between S1 and S2 and a relatively fast degradation between S2 and S4, while HF showed relatively minor changes in malate levels throughout fruit development. These patterns in acidity may reflect the balance of acid metabolism during fruit development [39]. In contrast, citric acid content consistently decreased by 88.84% and 90.67% throughout fruit development (Fig. 2E), which differs from the trends observed in Chinese dwarf cherry [5], peach [4], and apricot [40, 41]. In summary, the accumulation pattern of organic acid in Chinese cherry differs somewhat from that in other stone fruits.

Transcriptomic profiling of key genes involved in organic acid accumulation in fruits

Transcriptome assembly, annotation and global gene expression profile

To identify the DEGs, we compared the transcriptome profiles of Chinese cherry landraces at various developmental stages, both within and between the landraces. In the intra-landraces comparison (i), a total of 5,598 DEGs were identified in HF, and 8,470 DEGs in BJ7-2, respectively (Fig. S2A). Throughout fruit development, the number of down-regulated DEGs exceeded the up-regulated ones in both landraces, except between BJ7-2S3 and BJ7-2S4. The most significant gene expression differences occurred from stage S2 to S3, with 1,464 unique DEGs in HF and 2,291 in BJ7-2 (Fig. S2B), making this stage as a critical transition in fruit development. In the inter-landraces comparison (ii), 2,337, 1,285, 2,547, and 2,227 DEGs were identified across the four developmental stages. Notably, 957 DEGs were unique to stage S3 (Fig. S2A, B), highlighting this stage as the most pivotal for distinguishing gene expression differences between the two landraces.

To explore expression patterns through fruit development between HF and BJ7-2, a total of 7,698 DEGs were clustered into six groups using Fuzzy cMeans clustering method (Fig. 3A). Clusters 2 and 5 contained 1,093 and 927 genes, respectively, which showed significantly higher expression levels at the later stages (S3, S4) in both HF and BJ7-2. Cluster 4 comprised 1, 353 genes with higher expressions specifically at stage S3 in BJ7-2. Genes in clusters 1 and 6 were differentially expressed at stage S1, while gene in cluster 3 exhibited similar expression patterns between the two landraces. KEGG results showed that genes in clusters 2 and 4 were primarily enriched in the “Pyruvate metabolism” pathway, whereas clusters 4 and 5 showed significant enrichment in the “Citrate cycle (TCA cycle)” pathway (Fig. 3B).

Key DEGs related to organic acid metabolism and accumulation

Malic acid, the primary organic acid in Chinese cherries, is also a crucial intermediate in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), downstream of glycolysis. In our study, we identified 59 DEGs involved in organic acid metabolism. Specifically, we identified 39, 48, and 30 DEGs (Fig. 4A-C, Table S4) when comparing developmental stages within HF and BJ7-2, and cross all four stages between the two landraces, respectively. Among them, we found 2 citrate synthase (CS), 3 aconitate hydratase (ACO), 2 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (α-OGDH), 4 succinate dehydrogenase (SuDH), 4 malate dehydrogenase (MDH), and 5 malic enzymes (ME). Additionally, we identified 2 malate transporter genes (tDT, ALMT4) and 4 proton pumps (VHA, VHP, VHP1, PH4), 3 PEPCs, 2 PEPCKs, 6 PKs, 2 PPDKs, and four ADHs. The expression levels for most of these genes were elevated during the early stages and remained stable in the later stages (Fig. 4A, B). No significant differences were detected in the expression of PDH, CS, ACO, ICDH between the two landraces across the four stages. However, the expression levels of CpSuDH, CpcMDH, CpALMT4, and CpPEPC2 were considerably higher in BJ7-2 compared to HF, particularly at stage S3 and S4 (Fig. 4C). The CpNADP-ME gene, involved in malate degradation, was downregulated during fruit development in both landraces, with a slight upregulation in HF. In contrast, its expression continuously decreased throughout development in BJ7-2, leading to a lower expression level (log2FC = − 7.07 ~ − 1.64) in BJ7-2, which likely contributes to a higher accumulation of malic acid. The expression of CptDT, CpPKc, CpPPDK, CpPDC, and CpADH was downregulated at S3-S4 in BJ7-2 (Fig. 4C). Consequently, these results suggest that above genes play a key role in the differences in acidity between the landraces during fruit development.

Transcriptional regulation is crucial for gene expression and metabolite control. In this study, we identified 183 TFs from DEGs in clusters 2, 4, and 5. The largest TF families were MYB (32), bHLH (18), NAC (16), bZIP (14), and WRKY (13) (Fig. 4D, Table S5). To uncover key regulatory networks governing organic acid metabolism, we conducted a joint analysis integrating malic acid, citric acid, structural genes, and TFs (Fig. 5). PEPC2 showed positive correlations with cMDH, ALMT4, and VHP, and NADP-ME was also positively correlated with ADH. A negative correlation was detected between cMDH and PPDK. This analysis also revealed several key correlations between TFs and structural genes: ARF5 (Ppseudocerasus_HiC_scaffold_1_3925) expression showed a positive correlation with both ALMT4 expression and malic acid content, while ODORANT1like (3_0187) expression was positively correlated with both NADP-ME expression and citric acid levels. Additionally, three MYBs genes (5_0639, 6_2573, 6_2704) and WRKY57 (1_3091) were positively correlated with malic acid levels, whereas MYB124 (1_2356) and bHLH68like (7_2344) showed a negative correlation. Two AP2 genes (1_4152: protein PPLZ02, 2_3045: AP2EREBP8) and bHLH148 (1_0979) showed positive correlations with the expressions of cMDH, PEPC2, ALMT4 and VHP, while protein indeterminatedomain 2 (7_1656) and bZIP TF TGA2like (2_0483) were negatively correlated with these four genes. Furthermore, WRKY18 (4_4173), WRKY65 (4_0613) and MYB (5_0740) expression were negatively correlated with PEPC2 and ALMT4 expression, but positively correlated with NADP-ME expression. Several other MYB, AP2, ARF, and bHLH genes exhibited significant correlations with the expression of either PEPC2, PKc, NADP-ME, ADH, ALMT4, or VHP. These genes displayed differential expression patterns during fruit development and between the two landraces (Fig. 4D), underscoring their crucial roles in regulating organic acid metabolism in Chinese cherries.

Functional validation of candidate genes

qRT-PCR confirmation of gene expression

To confirm the transcriptomic analysis, we assessed the expression levels of twelve DEGs related to organic acid metabolism using qRT-PCR. The relative expression of these genes varied across different landraces and developmental stages. Overall, the qPCR expression profiles were consistent with the corresponding FPKM values obtained from RNA-seq, demonstrating similar patterns (Fig. 6). Pearson’s coefficients (r) for nine genes were all above 0.61, confirming the accuracy and reliability of the transcriptome data.

qRT-PCR validation of key genes involved in organic acid metabolism during fruit development in low-acid HF and high-acid BJ7-2 landraces. The Pearson’s coefficients (r) were all > 0.61

Validation in mature fruits of 34 genotypes

A total of 34 Chinese cherry genotypes were used to explore the expression patterns of candidate genes associated with organic acid metabolism (Fig. 7). The expression levels of CpcMDH, CpPEPC2, CptDT, CpALMT4, CpVHP, and CpVHP1 were higher in accessions with high TA contents, while CpNADP-ME expression was significantly higher in those with low TA levels. In addition, CpODORANT1like was strongly expressed in accessions with low TA levels, while CpARF2Blike showed higher expression in those with high TA contents. These findings further confirm that these nine genes as key regulators of organic acid regulation across different Chinese cherry genotypes.

Patterns of organic acid accumulation in Chinese cherry fruits

Malate accumulation is influenced by synthesis, degradation, and transport. As previously reported, cMDH promotes the conversion of oxaloacetate to malate, while mMDH primarily participates in malate degradation [42]. Herein, cMDH expression was considerably higher in the later stages of fruit development in the high-acid landrace, whereas mMDH showed no significant difference between them. The results of the present study are consistent with previous findings on the regulation of malate synthesis by MDH in peaches [4] and Chinese dwarf cherry fruits [5]. PEPC, an essential enzyme in malate synthesis, facilitates the accumulation of oxaloacetic acid. Our results revealed high levels of PEPC2 expression in high-acid landraces, which is accordance with the report on Chinese dwarf cherry [5]. The NADP-ME catalyzes the conversion of malate to pyruvate in the cytoplasm, a key step in the degradation of malate [12]. Our results revealed low levels of NADP-ME expression in high-acid landraces, which negatively regulates the accumulation of malic acid. One study found that decreased fruit malate content was mediated primarily by stimulation of NADP-ME activity rather than by changes in the expression of cytosolic NADP-ME genes [43]. However, expression of NADP-ME-related genes has shown a positive correlation with malate content, indicating a possible transcriptional influence on malate metabolism [44, 45]. PK, which catalyzes the transfer of PEP to pyruvate, was found to be much lower in high-acid compared to low-acid landraces, indicating a reduced synthesis of pyruvate in the cytosol of fruit cells in high-acid landrace. This finding contradictes with the results observed in peach fruits [4]. Interestingly, no significant differences were detected in the expression levels of TCA cycle genes (e.g., CS, ACO, and ICDH) between high- and low- acid landraces. These results suggest that malic acid biosynthesis is enhanced and degradation is reduced in high-acid landraces, leading to greater malic acid accumulation. This finding partially differs from previous studies on malate regulation in Chinese dwarf cherry [5] and peach [4].

Malate accumulation is regulated by the intracellular transport between the vacuole and the cytoplasm [11, 42]. Among these DEGs, ALMT4, VHP, and VHP1 were upregulated, while tDT was downregulated during the late stage of BJ7-2 fruit development. Notably, ALMT4 expression showed a significant correlation with malic acid content in Chinese cherry, consistent with reports in peach [4], apple [46], and jujube [15]. The function of tDT in malate and citrate transport appears to vary among species. For instance, knocking out AttDT in Arabidopsis resulted in reduced malate activity [13]. In these mutants, malate decreased and citrate increased, suggesting an antiport transport mechanism [47]. Conversely, heterologous expression of apple MdtDT1 in Arabidopsis enhanced the accumulation of both malate and citrate [48]. The expression level of tDT showed a positive correlation with malic acid and TA contents in Chinese dwarf cherry [5]. The expression of MdtDT in apple was negatively correlated with citrate level, and transient repression of its expression led to increased citrate accumulation [49]. In the case of Chinese cherry, tDT expression was upregulated in BJ7-2S2-S3 but downregulated in BJ7-2S4 compared to HF, despite higher malic acid and lower citric acid levels in BJ7-2S3-S4. However, population analysis revealed that tDT exhibited higher expression levels in the high-acid group. Similar results were also observed for the tonoplast proton pumps (VHP and VHP1). As mentioned above, we suggest that malate accumulation in Chinese cherry fruits is controlled by both metabolic processes and vacuolar storage. This conclusion is supported by previous studies conducted on peach [4, 11].

Transcriptional regulation plays a key role in controlling malate transporter and proton pump activities, with MYB, bHLH, WRKY, ERF, and AP2/ERF family members involved [50]. In apple, the MYB TFs MdMYB1, MdMYB44, MdMYB73 and MdMYB123 regulate malate transporter and proton pump activities, influencing malic acid accumulation and vacuolar acidification [22–25]. MdbHLH3 promotes malate accumulation by binding to the promoter of MdcyMDH [20]. Jujube ZjWRKY7 regulates the expression of ZjALMT4, thereby modulating malate accumulation [15]. The ERF transcription factor MdESE3 can directly target the ERE of MdMa11, MdtDT, and MdMDH12 to promote malate accumulation in apple [19]. In our study, co-expression network analysis revealed that multiple TFs, including MYB, bHLH, AP2, and WRKY, may be involved in malic acid metabolism and transport. These TFs likely regulate malate accumulation by activating or suppressing the expression of cMDH, PEPC2, NADP-ME, and VHP. Through population analysis, we further confirmed two TFs involved in organic acid metabolism of Chinese cherry fruits: CpODORANT1like and CpARF2Blike. They may negatively and positively regulate the fruit acidity by directly modulating NADP-ME and PEPC2 expression levels. Recently, Fu et al. [51] reported that MdMYB2 positively regulates malate accumulation by influencing the expression of MdNADP-ME. Conclusions

The findings of the present work demonstrate the mechanism behind the accumulation of fruit acidity in Chinese cherry. The content and composition of organic acids vary significantly among Chinese cherry accessions. Malic acid accounted for 73.66% of total acids, serving as a key indicator of fruit acidity. High-acid landrace exhibited very rapid malate accumulation and relatively fast degradation, while low-acid landrace showed relatively minor changes in malate levels throughout fruit development. To our knowledge, cMDH, PEPC2, NADP-ME, tDT, ALMT4, VHP, and VHP1, along with two transcription factors (ODORANT1like and ARF2Blike), have been identified for the first time in Chinese cherry fruits. Briefly, cMDH and PEPC2 control malate synthesis, NADP-ME degrades malate to pyruvate, and tDT, ALMT4, VHP and VHP1 mediate the transport of malate from the cytosol to the vacuole. CpODORANT1like and CpARF2Blike may negatively and positively regulate fruit acidity by directly modulating NADP-ME and PEPC2 expression levels. These results suggest that malate accumulation in Chinese cherry fruits is regulated at both the metabolic and vacuolar storage levels. Overall, these findings offer a molecular foundation for further investigation into the regulation of organic acids and may aid in the development of Chinese cherry cultivars with enhanced flavor quality.
