Smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety: the moderating role of neuroticism and conscientiousness
Authors: Haolan Li, Yu Fang, Bingshan Li, Weilun Zhao, Jiangtao Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe widespread adoption of smartwatch technology has fundamentally transformed how individuals engage with physical activity. However, limited research has examined the psychological implications of intensive smartwatch use during exercise. This study investigated the cross-sectional relationship between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety among adult exercisers in China, and examined whether personality traits moderate this relationship.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 486 regular exercisers (mean age = 28.73 years, standard deviation = 7.42; 52.1% female) recruited from fitness centres in Hefei, Anhui Province, China. Participants completed validated measures including the Chinese 7-item Social Physique Anxiety Scale (C-SPAS-7), a purpose-developed Smartwatch Use Intensity Scale (SUIS), and the Big Five Inventory. Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. Hierarchical regression and moderation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro.ResultsSmartwatch use intensity was positively associated with social physique anxiety (β = 0.29, p < .001), controlling for demographic and exercise-related covariates. Neuroticism significantly moderated this association (ΔR² = 0.028, p < .001), with the association being stronger among individuals high in neuroticism. Conscientiousness also moderated the association (ΔR² = 0.012, p = .008), with the association being weaker among individuals high in conscientiousness.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the importance of individual differences in understanding the psychological correlates of wearable technology use. Given the cross-sectional design, causality cannot be established; it remains possible that pre-existing anxiety drives more intensive technology use rather than the reverse. Individuals high in neuroticism may be particularly vulnerable to anxiety associated with intensive smartwatch use, while conscientiousness may serve as a protective factor. Although the moderation effect sizes were small (2.8% and 1.2% additional variance), they represent potentially meaningful individual differences that could accumulate over time. These results have implications for personalised fitness technology design and user wellbeing interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04436-2.Keywords: Smartwatch use intensity, Social physique anxiety, Personality traits, Neuroticism, Conscientiousness, Wearable technology

Introduction

Background and rationale

The adoption of wearable fitness technology has experienced unprecedented growth over the past decade, fundamentally altering how individuals monitor and engage with physical activity [1, 2]. Smartwatches, in particular, have emerged as the dominant wearable device category, with global shipments exceeding 170 million units annually and continuous growth projected through 2029 [3, 4]. These devices integrate physiological monitoring capabilities (heart rate, caloric expenditure, sleep quality, and stress indicators) with social connectivity features such as workout sharing, leaderboards, and competitive challenges [5–7]. While these functionalities offer potential benefits for physical health promotion, questions remain regarding their psychological correlates, particularly among individuals who may be vulnerable to body-related concerns.

Social physique anxiety (SPA) represents a specific form of anxiety that has garnered considerable attention in exercise psychology research. Defined as the anxiety individuals experience when they perceive that others are evaluating their physique negatively [8], SPA is particularly relevant to exercise contexts where bodily presentation and performance are often salient. Research has consistently demonstrated that elevated SPA is associated with reduced exercise enjoyment [9, 10], avoidance of certain exercise settings [11, 12]. and broader psychological distress [13]. Understanding factors that are related to SPA is therefore important for promoting both physical activity participation and psychological wellbeing.

Despite the widespread adoption of smartwatches in exercise settings, the relationship between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety remains inadequately understood. On one hand, smartwatches may offer motivational benefits through goal setting, progress tracking, and positive reinforcement [14–16]. On the other hand, the continuous body-related metrics these devices provide (calories burned, activity scores, and physiological indicators) may be associated with heightened self-monitoring and preoccupation with bodily performance [17–19]. The present study addresses this gap by examining the cross-sectional association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety, and by investigating whether key personality traits moderate this association.

Smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety: theoretical considerations

Self-determination theory and need thwarting

Self-determination theory (SDT) [20] posits that psychological wellbeing is contingent upon the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Within exercise contexts, technology-mediated monitoring may either support or undermine these needs depending on how it is utilised and experienced [21, 22]. When smartwatch use is perceived as externally controlling (emphasising metrics, targets, and comparisons rather than intrinsic enjoyment), it may thwart autonomy and undermine competence perceptions when targets are not met [23]. Such need thwarting has been consistently linked to negative affective outcomes, including anxiety [22].

Digital self-surveillance and the “digital mirror” effect

Objectification theory offers a particularly compelling framework for understanding how smartwatch use intensity may relate to social physique anxiety [24]. This theory posits that the cultural practice of treating bodies as objects to be evaluated leads individuals to internalise an observer’s perspective on their own bodies, engaging in chronic self-surveillance [25]. Smartwatches may extend this self-objectification process into the digital domain by providing continuous, quantified feedback about bodily states and performance. The concept of the “quantified self” (the practice of self-tracking through digital technologies) [18, 26] describes how individuals increasingly relate to their bodies through data and metrics. This datafication of bodily experience may be associated with what scholars have termed “digital self-surveillance” [27], wherein the body becomes an object of constant monitoring and evaluation.

This digital surveillance transforms the relationship between individuals and their bodies in several important ways. First, the persistent availability of bodily data creates opportunities for near-constant self-monitoring, extending surveillance beyond the exercise context into daily life [28]. Second, the quantification of bodily states into metrics (calories, steps, heart rate zones) may relate to evaluative rather than experiential engagement with exercise [17]. Third, when performance data fail to meet expectations or compare unfavourably with goals or social standards, negative self-evaluation may result [29]. These processes parallel the body-checking behaviours observed in individuals with body image disturbance [29], suggesting potential associations between intensive smartwatch use and physique-related anxiety.

Social comparison in the digital fitness ecosystem

Beyond individual self-surveillance, smartwatches increasingly incorporate social features that may relate to anxiety through social comparison processes. Many contemporary smartwatch platforms include leaderboards, workout sharing capabilities, and competitive challenges that enable users to compare their exercise metrics with others [30]. Social comparison theory [31] suggests that individuals evaluate themselves by comparing their attributes and performances with relevant others, and that upward social comparisons (comparing oneself with superior others) can be associated with negative self-evaluations and decreased wellbeing. In the context of fitness tracking, exposure to others’ workout achievements and metrics may be related to social physique anxiety, particularly when one’s own performance falls short of comparison standards [32].

Empirical research examining the psychological correlates of fitness tracking technology has yielded mixed findings. Several studies have documented positive outcomes associated with wearable device use, including increased physical activity, enhanced motivation, and improved self-efficacy [33, 34]. However, emerging evidence suggests potential negative correlates, including obsessive monitoring behaviours, anxiety when devices are unavailable, and unhealthy relationships with exercise metrics [17, 19, 35]. Research from adjacent domains provides relevant insights. For instance, recent work on technology adoption mechanisms [36] suggests that users’ acceptance and sustained use of digital tools are influenced by performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence. Understanding these mechanisms may help explain individual differences in how users relate to fitness technology. Additionally, longitudinal research on anxiety and health outcomes [37] demonstrates that anxiety can dynamically shape individuals’ self-rated health, suggesting bidirectional relationships between psychological states and health-related behaviours that parallel our investigation of smartwatch use and physique anxiety. The present study contributes to this literature by examining individual differences that may relate to vulnerability to negative psychological correlates of intensive smartwatch use.

The moderating role of personality traits

Individual differences in personality traits may play a crucial role in determining how smartwatch use intensity relates to social physique anxiety. The Five-Factor Model (FFM) of personality, encompassing neuroticism, extraversion, openness to experience, agreeableness, and conscientiousness, provides a comprehensive framework for understanding individual differences in psychological responses to environmental stimuli [38, 39]. Prior research has demonstrated that personality traits are associated with both technology adoption patterns [40, 41] and vulnerability to anxiety-related outcomes [42], suggesting their potential relevance as moderators of the smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety association.

Neuroticism, characterised by emotional instability and a tendency to experience negative affect, is particularly relevant to understanding vulnerability to social physique anxiety [42]. Individuals high in neuroticism exhibit heightened sensitivity to negative stimuli, greater rumination, and more intense reactions to perceived threats [43, 44]. In the context of smartwatch use, neurotic individuals may interpret metrics more negatively, engage in more unfavourable social comparisons, and experience greater distress when performance data fail to meet expectations. Research on social media use has demonstrated that neuroticism amplifies associations between online engagement and anxiety symptoms [45]. Moreover, recent research on self-related anxiety processes suggests that individuals high in neuroticism may be particularly vulnerable to anxiety in contexts involving self-presentation and social evaluation [46]. Similar amplifying associations may occur in the context of smartwatch use, with neurotic individuals exhibiting stronger positive associations between intensive technology use and social physique anxiety.

Conscientiousness, reflecting tendencies towards organisation, self-discipline, and goal-directed behaviour, may function as a protective factor in the smartwatch use intensity and anxiety association [47]. Highly conscientious individuals tend to approach tasks systematically, set realistic goals, and exhibit greater emotional regulation [48]. In exercise contexts, conscientious individuals may utilise smartwatch features more adaptively, viewing metrics as tools for structured improvement rather than sources of evaluative threat. Research has consistently linked conscientiousness to more adaptive coping strategies and better psychological outcomes across domains [49, 50]. We therefore hypothesise that conscientiousness will attenuate the positive association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety.

Although we focus on neuroticism and conscientiousness based on their theoretical relevance to anxiety vulnerability and self-regulation, we also conducted exploratory analyses examining the remaining Big Five traits (extraversion, openness, agreeableness) as potential moderators. Extraversion, in particular, could plausibly either strengthen associations (through greater engagement with social comparison features) or attenuate them (through greater social confidence buffering evaluative concerns) [51].

The present study

The present study aimed to examine the cross-sectional association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety among adult exercisers recruited from fitness centres in Hefei, China, and to investigate whether neuroticism and conscientiousness moderate this association. Based on the theoretical considerations above, we tested the following hypotheses:

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 523 participants were initially recruited through convenience sampling from six fitness centres located in Hefei, Anhui Province, China, between March and July 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (a) aged 18 years or older, (b) currently using a smartwatch for exercise tracking at least three times per week for a minimum of one month, (c) engaging in regular physical activity at least twice weekly, and (d) able to read and comprehend Chinese. Exclusion criteria included: (a) diagnosed anxiety disorders or other mental health conditions, (b) use of medications affecting mood or anxiety, and (c) professional athletes. Following data screening procedures (detailed in the Results section), the final analytical sample comprised 486 participants (Mage = 28.73 years, SD = 7.42, range: 18 to 56 years; 253 females, 52.1%). It should be emphasised that this sample represents a specific population, namely regular exercisers who use smartwatches intensively at fitness centres in a single Chinese city. Findings should not be generalised to casual exercisers, individuals who exercise in other settings, non-smartwatch users, or populations in different cultural contexts. A priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 [52] indicated that a minimum sample of 395 participants would be required to detect a small-to-medium effect size (f² = 0.05) with 80% power at α = 0.05 for a regression model with eight predictors.

Procedure

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Lvhai Vocational College of Business (Approval No.: AHLHVB-2025HM-2504) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were approached by trained research assistants at fitness centres during peak hours. Those who expressed interest were provided with an information sheet detailing the study purpose, procedures, and their rights as participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participants then completed a battery of self-report questionnaires in a quiet area of the fitness centre, which took approximately 15 to 20 min. Upon completion, participants received a small token of appreciation (a fitness towel valued at approximately 30 RMB). Data were collected anonymously, with participants identified only by a unique code.

Measures

Smartwatch Use Intensity Scale (SUIS)

Given the absence of validated measures specifically assessing smartwatch use intensity in exercise contexts, we developed a purpose-built scale following established guidelines for scale development [53]. The development process comprised four stages: (1) initial item generation through systematic review of existing technology use scales [45, 54] and qualitative interviews with 20 smartwatch users; (2) content validity evaluation by a panel of five experts who rated items for relevance, clarity, and representativeness; (3) a pilot study (N = 85) examining factor structure and reliability; and (4) confirmatory factor analysis in the main study. Full details of the scale development process, including complete item content and all item-level statistics, are provided in Supplementary Materials (Appendix A, Table A1).

The final SUIS comprises eight items assessing frequency and intensity of smartwatch engagement during exercise (e.g., “I frequently check my smartwatch during workouts”; “My smartwatch data significantly influences how I feel about my workout”). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scores were summed to create a total score (range: 8 to 40), with higher scores indicating more intensive use. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) and acceptable model fit in confirmatory factor analysis (see Table 1 for complete CFA results for both SUIS and C-SPAS-7). Factor loadings for the eight SUIS items ranged from 0.62 to 0.81 (all p < .001), supporting the unidimensional structure. Item-total correlations ranged from 0.54 to 0.72, and corrected item-total correlations exceeded 0.50 for all items, indicating adequate item discrimination. The composite reliability (CR) was 0.88 and average variance extracted (AVE) was 0.52, meeting thresholds for convergent validity.

Social physique anxiety

Social physique anxiety was measured using the Chinese 7-item Social Physique Anxiety Scale (C-SPAS-7), developed and validated by Jin and Fung (2021) specifically for Chinese populations. This measure was selected over the original 12-item SPAS [8] or other shortened versions based on recent psychometric evidence demonstrating that previous versions did not demonstrate acceptable fit in Chinese samples, whereas the C-SPAS-7 showed superior fit indices and measurement invariance across gender [55].

The C-SPAS-7 comprises seven items assessing anxiety related to others’ evaluation of one’s physique (e.g., “There are times when I am bothered by thoughts that other people are evaluating my weight or muscular development negatively”). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). Total scores ranged from 7 to 35, with higher scores indicating greater social physique anxiety. Jin and Fung (2021) reported good internal consistency (α = 0.85 to 0.89) and construct validity. In the present study, the C-SPAS-7 demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88) and confirmatory factor analysis supported the unidimensional structure (χ²(14) = 36.84, p < .001; CFI = 0.96; TLI = 0.94; RMSEA = 0.058, 90% CI [0.035, 0.081]; SRMR = 0.038). Factor loadings for the seven C-SPAS-7 items ranged from 0.65 to 0.84 (all p < .001). The composite reliability was 0.89 and AVE was 0.54. The mean score in our sample (M = 19.24, SD = 5.78) was comparable to normative data from Jin and Fung (2021) for Chinese exercisers (M = 18.67, SD = 6.12) [55].

Personality traits

Personality traits were assessed using the Big Five Inventory (BFI) [56], translated and validated for Chinese populations [57]). The BFI comprises 44 items measuring five personality dimensions: Neuroticism (8 items; α = 0.83), Extraversion (8 items; α = 0.81), Openness to Experience (10 items; α = 0.78), Agreeableness (9 items; α = 0.76), and Conscientiousness (9 items; α = 0.82). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (disagree strongly) to 5 (agree strongly). Subscale scores were computed by averaging relevant items, with higher scores indicating higher levels of the respective trait.

Control variables

Several demographic and exercise-related variables were assessed as potential covariates: age (years), gender (0 = male, 1 = female), body mass index (BMI; calculated from self-reported height and weight), exercise frequency (days per week), duration of smartwatch use (months), and primary exercise type (categorised as: resistance training, cardiovascular exercise, mixed training, or other). We acknowledge that several potentially relevant variables were not measured in this study, including general anxiety levels, exercise dependence, social media use, pre-existing body image concerns, and specific smartwatch features used. The absence of these variables limits our ability to rule out alternative explanations for observed associations, and this limitation is addressed in the Discussion section.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), AMOS Version 27.0 for confirmatory factor analyses, and the PROCESS macro Version 4.1 [58]. Preliminary analyses included examination of missing data patterns, univariate and multivariate normality, and outlier detection using Mahalanobis distance (with p < .001 as the criterion). Confirmatory factor analyses were conducted for the C-SPAS-7 and SUIS to verify their factor structures in the present sample, with model fit evaluated using χ², CFI (> 0.95), TLI (> 0.90), RMSEA (< 0.08), and SRMR (< 0.08) criteria [59]. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations) were calculated for all study variables, and bivariate Pearson correlations were computed to examine relationships among variables.

Given that all variables were assessed via self-report at a single time point, common method bias was evaluated using Harman’s single-factor test [60]. All items from the SUIS, C-SPAS-7, and BFI subscales were entered into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis. If a single factor accounts for the majority of variance (> 50%), common method bias may be a concern.

To test H1, hierarchical multiple regression analysis was employed with social physique anxiety as the dependent variable. Control variables were entered in Step 1, followed by smartwatch use intensity in Step 2. To test H2 and H3, moderation analyses were conducted using Model 1 of the PROCESS macro with 5,000 bootstrap samples and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals. Continuous predictor variables were mean-centred prior to creating interaction terms. Simple slopes analyses were conducted to probe significant interactions at low (− 1 SD), mean (0), and high (+ 1 SD) levels of the moderator variables [61]. We also conducted exploratory moderation analyses for extraversion, openness, and agreeableness, as well as multi-group analyses by gender. Statistical significance was set at p < .05 for all analyses.

Results

Preliminary analyses

Of the 523 initial responses, 37 were excluded due to excessive missing data (> 10% of items), implausible response patterns (e.g., identical responses across all items), or failing attention check items. Multivariate outlier analysis using Mahalanobis distance identified no cases exceeding the critical value. The final analytical sample comprised 486 participants. Little’s MCAR test indicated that remaining missing data (< 2%) were missing completely at random, χ²(142) = 151.34, p = .28. Missing values were handled using expectation-maximisation imputation. Examination of skewness and kurtosis values indicated that all variables fell within acceptable ranges for normality (skewness < |2|, kurtosis < |7|) [62]. Multicollinearity diagnostics revealed no concerns, with all variance inflation factors (VIF) below 2.5 and tolerance values above 0.40.

Harman’s single-factor test indicated that 14 factors emerged with eigenvalues greater than 1.0, and the first factor accounted for 23.6% of the total variance, well below the 50% threshold. This result suggests that common method bias is unlikely to be a significant concern in this study [60].

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations among study variables are presented in Table 2. The mean social physique anxiety score was 19.24 (SD = 5.78) on the C-SPAS-7 (possible range: 7 to 35), which is comparable to normative data for Chinese exercisers [55] and indicates moderate levels of social physique anxiety in the sample. Smartwatch use intensity (M = 26.18, SD = 6.42; possible range: 8 to 40) was significantly positively correlated with social physique anxiety (r = .32, p < .001). Among personality traits, neuroticism showed the strongest positive correlation with social physique anxiety (r = .39, p < .001), while conscientiousness was negatively correlated with social physique anxiety (r = − .21, p < .001). Notably, smartwatch use intensity was significantly correlated with neuroticism (r = .17, p < .001) but not with conscientiousness (r = .06, p = .18). The non-significant zero-order correlation between smartwatch use intensity and conscientiousness, combined with the significant moderation effect reported below, is consistent with the conceptual distinction between main effects and interaction effects. That is, conscientiousness does not directly relate to smartwatch use intensity levels; rather, it moderates the association between smartwatch use intensity and anxiety outcomes. This pattern suggests that conscientious individuals do not necessarily use smartwatches less intensively, but they may process or respond to smartwatch-related information differently than less conscientious individuals. Female participants reported significantly higher social physique anxiety than male participants, t(484) = 3.54, p < .001, d = 0.32.

Main analyses: testing the hypotheses

Smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety (H1)

Hierarchical regression analysis was conducted to test H1. Results are presented in Table 3. In Step 1, control variables (age, gender, BMI, exercise frequency, and duration of smartwatch use) accounted for 11.8% of the variance in social physique anxiety, F(5, 480) = 12.86, p < .001. Gender emerged as a significant predictor (β = 0.19, p < .001), with female participants reporting higher anxiety. BMI was also a significant predictor (β = 0.14, p = .002). In Step 2, smartwatch use intensity was added to the model and was significantly associated with social physique anxiety (β = 0.29, p < .001, 95% CI [0.21, 0.37]), accounting for an additional 7.6% of variance, ΔR² = 0.076, ΔF(1, 479) = 44.28, p < .001. Thus, H1 was supported: smartwatch use intensity was positively associated with social physique anxiety.

Moderation by neuroticism (H2)

To test H2, moderation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (results presented in Table 4). Results indicated a significant interaction between smartwatch use intensity and neuroticism on social physique anxiety, B = 0.36, SE = 0.08, t = 4.50, p < .001, 95% CI [0.20, 0.52]. The interaction term explained an additional 2.8% of variance in social physique anxiety, ΔR² = 0.028. This corresponds to a small effect size by conventional standards (f² = 0.029) [63]. To test H3, a separate moderation analysis was conducted with conscientiousness as the moderator. Results revealed a significant interaction between smartwatch use intensity and conscientiousness, B = − 0.24, SE = 0.09, t = − 2.67, p = .008, 95% CI [− 0.42, − 0.06]. Simple slopes analysis indicated that the positive association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety was strongest at low (− 1 SD) levels of conscientiousness, B = 0.48, SE = 0.06, t = 8.00, p < .001, and at mean levels, B = 0.36, SE = 0.04, t = 9.00, p < .001. At high levels of conscientiousness (+ 1 SD), the association remained significant but was substantially attenuated, B = 0.24, SE = 0.06, t = 4.00, p < .001. This pattern supports H3, indicating that conscientiousness attenuates the positive association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety.

Supplementary analyses

Multi-group analyses by gender

To examine whether the pattern of results was consistent across gender, multi-group analyses were conducted. The main association of smartwatch use intensity on social physique anxiety was slightly stronger among females (β = 0.32, B = 0.29, SE = 0.05, p < .001) than males (β = 0.26, B = 0.24, SE = 0.06, p < .001), though the difference was not statistically significant, z = 0.65, p = .52. Moderation patterns for neuroticism and conscientiousness were similar across genders, with no significant gender differences in the interaction associations (all ps > 0.30). These results suggest that our findings generalise across gender within the studied population.

Exploratory moderation analyses for other personality traits

Additional moderation analyses were conducted with the remaining Big Five traits as exploratory analyses. Extraversion did not significantly moderate the smartwatch use intensity and anxiety association (B = − 0.09, SE = 0.08, p = .26). Similarly, openness (B = 0.04, SE = 0.09, p = .65) and agreeableness (B = − 0.11, SE = 0.08, p = .17) were not significant moderators. These findings support our a priori focus on neuroticism and conscientiousness as the most theoretically relevant moderators in this context.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study investigated the cross-sectional association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety among regular exercisers recruited from fitness centres in Hefei, China, and examined whether neuroticism and conscientiousness moderated this association. Consistent with H1, we found that smartwatch use intensity was positively associated with social physique anxiety. Furthermore, supporting H2 and H3, neuroticism strengthened this positive association while conscientiousness attenuated it. Multi-group analyses indicated that these patterns were consistent across gender. Exploratory analyses found no significant moderating associations for extraversion, openness, or agreeableness. These findings contribute to the growing literature on the psychological correlates of wearable fitness technology and highlight the importance of considering individual differences in this association.

Digital self-surveillance and social physique anxiety

The observed positive association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety aligns with emerging concerns about the potential psychological correlates of fitness tracking technology [17, 19, 32]. This finding extends prior research on fitness trackers [35] to the more sophisticated context of smartwatches, which offer richer data ecosystems and more immersive user experiences. Our results are consistent with the theoretical proposition that smartwatches may function as “digital mirrors” that extend self-objectification processes. The continuous body-related metrics provided by smartwatches, including calories, heart rate, and activity scores, appear to be associated with a surveillance-oriented relationship with one’s body that relates to appearance-related evaluative concerns.

The significant moderating role of neuroticism provides important insights into individual vulnerability in the association between technology use and social physique anxiety. Individuals high in neuroticism, characterised by heightened negative emotionality and stress reactivity [42], exhibited a stronger positive association between smartwatch use intensity and anxiety. This finding resonates with research demonstrating that neurotic individuals exhibit amplified responses to evaluative threats [64] and are more prone to maladaptive self-monitoring behaviours [65]. This aligns with recent research showing that personality traits interact with self-evaluative processes in contexts involving self-presentation [46]. For these individuals, the continuous metrics provided by smartwatches may serve as constant reminders of perceived bodily inadequacies, potentially relating to rumination and negative self-evaluation processes.

Conversely, conscientiousness emerged as a buffer in the smartwatch use intensity and anxiety association. Highly conscientious individuals tend to employ more adaptive self-regulatory strategies, set realistic goals, and maintain emotional equanimity in the face of challenges [47]. These characteristics may enable conscientious exercisers to utilise smartwatch data more constructively without becoming preoccupied with metrics or engaging in deleterious self-evaluation. This finding aligns with research demonstrating that conscientiousness is associated with more adaptive coping styles and better psychological adjustment across various domains [66].

Evaluating effect sizes and practical significance

While our moderation associations were statistically significant, it is essential to consider their practical significance objectively. The interaction terms accounted for 2.8% (neuroticism) and 1.2% (conscientiousness) additional variance in social physique anxiety, corresponding to small effect sizes by conventional standards (f² = 0.029 and 0.012, respectively) [63]. We acknowledge that these effects are modest and should not be overstated. In psychological research, such effect sizes are common for individual moderators but must be interpreted with appropriate caution regarding their real-world impact [67].

From a practical perspective, these small effect sizes have several important implications. First, while statistically significant, the moderating effects of personality traits explain only a small proportion of variance in social physique anxiety beyond the main effects. This suggests that personality represents just one of many factors that may influence how individuals respond to intensive smartwatch use, and interventions targeting personality-based vulnerability alone are unlikely to substantially reduce anxiety outcomes. Second, the modest effect sizes indicate that any practical recommendations derived from these findings should be tentative rather than prescriptive. Third, the practical significance of psychological effects depends heavily on context. Small effects may accumulate over time or interact with other factors to produce larger downstream consequences, but such possibilities remain speculative without longitudinal evidence.

The practical significance of these associations becomes clearer when examining the simple slopes: at high neuroticism, a one-unit increase in smartwatch use intensity was associated with a 0.53-point increase in social physique anxiety, whereas at low neuroticism, the same increase was associated with only a 0.19-point increase, representing a nearly threefold difference. However, whether this differential association translates into clinically meaningful differences in wellbeing over time remains an empirical question requiring longitudinal investigation. Nevertheless, personality traits represent only one class of individual differences that may moderate technology-anxiety associations, and the unexplained variance suggests other factors (e.g., pre-existing body image concerns, social comparison tendencies, specific smartwatch features used) warrant investigation.

Alternative explanations and bidirectional processes

An important consideration is the direction of associations. While our theoretical framework emphasised how smartwatch use intensity might relate to anxiety, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causality, and the reverse direction is equally plausible. Indeed, several alternative explanations deserve careful consideration. Individuals with pre-existing social physique anxiety may be drawn to more intensive smartwatch use as a means of monitoring and “controlling” their bodies and exercise performance. Anxious individuals may seek reassurance through tracking data or use metrics to validate their exercise efforts. This anxiety-driven technology dependence hypothesis is consistent with research showing that individuals with body image concerns are more likely to engage in body checking behaviours [29], and smartwatches may represent a technologically sophisticated form of such checking.

Moreover, a third-variable explanation cannot be ruled out. For example, individuals with high trait anxiety or perfectionism may both experience elevated social physique anxiety and engage in more intensive self-monitoring behaviours, including smartwatch use. Similarly, individuals with exercise dependence or obsessive exercise patterns may exhibit both heightened anxiety and more intensive technology engagement. The observed association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety may therefore reflect shared underlying vulnerabilities rather than a direct relationship between these variables. The correlation between smartwatch use intensity and neuroticism (r = .17) observed in our data is consistent with this possibility. Recent research on cognitive structures related to entitlement and perfectionism [68] and on the relationships between digital behaviours and psychological distress [69] suggests that complex psychological profiles may underlie both technology use patterns and anxiety outcomes.

Furthermore, bidirectional or reciprocal relationships may exist, wherein anxiety promotes intensive tracking, which in turn exacerbates anxiety in a self-reinforcing cycle. Such bidirectional dynamics have been documented in related domains, including social media use and psychological distress [69]. Understanding how anxiety dynamically shapes health-related behaviours and perceptions [37] may provide insights into the potential cyclical relationships between smartwatch use and physique anxiety. Longitudinal research is needed to disentangle these possibilities.

Practical implications

These findings carry several practical implications for stakeholders in the fitness and technology sectors, though all recommendations should be interpreted cautiously given the correlational nature of our findings and the modest effect sizes observed. First, for exercise practitioners and fitness professionals, our results underscore the potential value of assessing clients’ personality characteristics and body image concerns when discussing smartwatch use. Individuals high in neuroticism may benefit from guidance on maintaining a balanced relationship with fitness technology, emphasising process-oriented rather than outcome-oriented metrics. However, given the correlational nature of our findings, practitioners should also consider whether clients’ intensive tracking behaviours may be symptoms of underlying anxiety rather than causes.

Second, smartwatch designers and manufacturers might consider incorporating features that promote psychological wellbeing alongside physical health metrics. Based on our findings, we tentatively propose several design considerations. A “mindful exercise” mode that allows users to exercise without continuous metric display could potentially reduce opportunities for obsessive monitoring. However, it should be noted that we did not directly test the effects of specific features in this study. Different smartwatch functions, such as calorie tracking, heart rate monitoring, activity rings, sedentary reminders, and social leaderboards, may have differential associations with anxiety and may interact differently with personality traits [68]. Future research should examine feature-specific associations to inform more targeted design recommendations.

Third, for individuals using smartwatches in exercise contexts, our findings suggest that self-awareness regarding personality traits and body image concerns may facilitate healthier technology use. Those who recognise high neurotic tendencies might benefit from periodically exercising without their smartwatch, setting flexible rather than rigid performance targets, or focusing on qualitative exercise experiences rather than quantitative metrics.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting these findings. First and most importantly, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. We cannot determine whether smartwatch use intensity is associated with increased anxiety, whether anxiety drives more intensive technology use, or whether bidirectional relationships exist. The language throughout this manuscript has been carefully chosen to reflect statistical associations rather than causal effects, consistent with the cross-sectional methodology. Longitudinal designs tracking changes in both variables over time, experimental manipulations of smartwatch use, and ecological momentary assessment capturing within-person dynamics are needed to establish temporal precedence and potential causal mechanisms.

Second, our sample comprised regular exercisers who use smartwatches intensively (at least three times weekly), recruited exclusively from fitness centres in one Chinese city. This convenience sampling approach introduces selection bias, as participants may represent a particularly health-conscious, technology-engaged segment of the population with high fitness involvement. Our findings should not be generalised to casual exercisers, individuals who exercise outside gym settings, non-smartwatch users, populations in other cultural contexts, or broader population-level claims. Future research should examine these associations in community-based samples, casual exercisers, and cross-cultural contexts.

Third, we did not measure potential mediating mechanisms. The theoretical framework suggests that digital self-surveillance (i.e., heightened body monitoring through continuous metrics) and social comparison processes may mediate the association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety. Although these mechanisms were discussed theoretically, they were not empirically tested in the present study. Future longitudinal research should directly test these mediation pathways, for example, using PROCESS Model 4 [58] to examine whether smartwatch use intensity → digital self-objectification → social physique anxiety, or using Model 8 to test moderated mediation models wherein personality traits moderate specific mediation pathways.

Fourth, we did not differentiate among specific smartwatch features or usage patterns. Different functions, such as calorie tracking, heart rate monitoring, activity rings, sedentary reminders, and social leaderboards, may have differential associations with anxiety and may interact differently with personality traits. This is an important limitation because the mechanisms by which different features relate to anxiety may be entirely different. For example, automatic reminders and activity rings may emphasise goal completion and potential shortfalls, which could be particularly anxiety-provoking for individuals prone to negative self-evaluation. In contrast, social leaderboards and competitive features may primarily affect users with high social comparison tendencies. Critically, the absence of feature-specific measurement in this study prevents us from drawing any conclusions about which particular smartwatch functions are most strongly associated with social physique anxiety, or which features might be modified to reduce potential negative psychological correlates. Future research should examine feature-specific associations to inform more targeted design recommendations.

Fifth, although our SUIS was developed following rigorous procedures and demonstrated good psychometric properties, further validation across diverse populations is warranted. Sixth, while we controlled for several covariates, unmeasured confounders such as pre-existing body image concerns, general anxiety levels, exercise dependence, or social media use could account for observed associations. The absence of measures for these variables represents a notable limitation that should be addressed in future research. Seventh, all variables were measured using self-report instruments collected at a single time point. Although Harman’s single-factor test suggested that common method bias was unlikely to be a significant concern, this test has known limitations and cannot completely rule out method variance effects [60].

Conclusion

The present study provides evidence of a positive cross-sectional association between smartwatch use intensity and social physique anxiety among regular exercisers in fitness centres, and that this association is moderated by personality traits. Specifically, neuroticism strengthens and conscientiousness attenuates the positive association between smartwatch use intensity and anxiety, with these patterns consistent across gender. The observed moderation associations, though small in size (2.8% and 1.2% additional variance), represent statistically significant but modest individual differences in vulnerability. Given the cross-sectional design, causality cannot be established; it remains equally possible that pre-existing anxiety drives more intensive technology use, that third variables account for observed associations, or that bidirectional relationships exist. These findings highlight the importance of considering individual differences when examining the association between wearable fitness technology and psychological outcomes. As smartwatches become increasingly ubiquitous in exercise contexts, understanding which individuals may be more susceptible to potential negative psychological correlates, and identifying the specific technological features and psychological mechanisms involved, will be important for promoting healthy engagement with these technologies.
