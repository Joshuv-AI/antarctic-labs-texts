Association between atherogenic index of plasma and sepsis in critically ill patients with ischemic stroke: a retrospective cohort study using propensity score and machine learning approaches
Authors: Fengwei Yao, Lei Liu, Jian Zhang, Xiaolan Chen, Zhijun He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a severe and frequent complication among ischemic stroke patients during hospitalization. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), as metabolism-related markers, are closely linked to inflammation. However, their relationship with ischemic stroke sepsis remains unclear.MethodsWe examined the potential association between sepsis and elevated AIP Levels in 1,531 ischemic stroke intensive care unit patients from the MIMIC-IV database. Logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, and propensity score matching were used to assess associations. Machine learning models were additionally applied to evaluate model performance with and without AIP and to assess its incremental value.ResultsHigher AIP levels were significantly associated with the development of sepsis. In the fully adjusted model, AIP (OR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.12–2.71) were independently associated with an increased likelihood of sepsis. Analysis showed that patients in the high AIP group (Q3) exhibited the strongest association with sepsis (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.09–2.09), and this association remained robust after propensity score matching (PSM) (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.01–1.82). In machine learning models, AIP was retained as a contributing feature, and its inclusion improved model discrimination.ConclusionAIP were independently associated with sepsis risk and may complement risk stratification in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02737-w.Keywords: AIP, Sepsis, Ischemic stroke, MIMIC-IV, Propensity score matching

Introduction

Stroke remains one of the leading causes of mortality and disability worldwide. The clinical and societal impact of stroke is increasing in tandem with the world’s continuing aging trend. Ischemic stroke accounts for approximately 87% of all stroke cases, typically resulting from interruption of cerebral blood flow and subsequent neurological dysfunction [1, 2]. During hospitalization, patients with ischemic stroke are prone to developing infections, which may progress to sepsis. Sepsis can exacerbate the neuro-immune axis, leading to systemic inflammatory responses and neurotransmitter dysregulation [3–5], thereby increasing mortality, prolonging hospital stay, and worsening functional outcomes. Hence, early identification of high-risk patients susceptible to sepsis is of great significance for timely intervention and improved prognosis.

Recent studies have focused on identifying systemic inflammation through peripheral blood markers. Post-stroke outcomes and complications have been associated with inflammatory indicators such as NLR, PLR, and SIRI [6, 7]. However, these individual markers are limited by suboptimal sensitivity and specificity. Therefore, developing composite indices that integrate metabolic and inflammatory status is of growing interest. Recent evidence suggests that altered lipid, and amino acid pathways can reflect disease severity and may complement conventional markers like CRP or PCT [8]. Furthermore, integrated metabolomic and proteomic approaches have demonstrated potential to classify sepsis subtypes and enhance risk stratification, paving the way for personalized diagnostics and prognostics in critical care settings [9]. The AIP is emerging markers reflecting insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, respectively, closely associated with cardiovascular risk [10, 11], and has been associated with higher risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events [12–14]. AIP indices offer advantages of accessibility, stability, and robust prognostic potential.

Given the interplay between metabolic dysfunction and immune dysregulation, it is plausible that elevated AIP levels—reflecting insulin resistance and pro-atherogenic lipid profiles—may serve as indicators of heightened systemic vulnerability to sepsis in ischemic stroke patients. We therefore hypothesized that elevated AIP levels are associated with a higher risk of sepsis among critically ill patients with Ischemic stroke.

Methods

Study sample

Data from the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.1),, which contains high-quality electronic health records of ICU patients between 2008 and 2019 [15]. Fengwei Yao, who earned authorized access to the database (Certification No. 65083321) after completing the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative program, performed the data extraction. Patients with ischemic stroke were identified based on hospital discharge diagnoses using standardized codes from both the 9th and 10th revisions of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10). Specifically, we included patients with diagnostic codes corresponding to acute cerebral infarction, lacunar infarction, and related ischemic stroke conditions. Inclusion criteria were: (1) adult patients admitted to the ICU; and (2) Patients with more than one ICU admission were included based solely on their first ICU stay. Exclusion criteria: (1) lacking data required for calculating AIP and (2) Patients in whom sepsis occurred prior to the measurement of triglyceride and HDL levels. For preliminary exploration, patients were initially divided into tertiles based on the AIP (Fig. 1). Subsequently, the AIP were each dichotomized based on their median values, and patients were categorized into two assessment groups as follows: T1: Low AIP group (≤ 0.3444957); T2 group: High AIP group (> 0.3444957).

Study flowchart

Variable extraction and definitions

Data extraction was performed using Navicat Premium by executing structured query language (SQL) scripts. Extracted variables were categorized into four major domains: (1) demographic characteristics and basic vital signs; (2) comorbidities; (3) laboratory parameters; and (4) illness severity scores on ICU admission. We extracted the earliest triglyceride and HDL C measurements within a window spanning from 6 h before to 24 h after ICU admission and used these values to calculate AIP. The following formula was used to determine the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) was calculated as: AIP = log10 [triglycerides (mmol/L)/HDL-C (mmol/L)] [16]. Sepsis was defined using the official Sepsis-3 label set provided by the MIMIC-IV database. The 2016 Third International Consensus Definitions serve as the basis for this label set and identifies sepsis as the presence of suspected infection along with an acute increase in SOFA score of ≥ 2 points [17]. Sepsis onset was obtained from the precomputed Sepsis-3 label set. Variables with > 20% missingness (e.g., CRP, total bilirubin) were excluded. Multiple imputation was conducted on variables with fewer than 20% missingness using the Classification and Regression Trees (CART) method from R’s “mice” package. This approach uses decision tree models to anticipate missing values based on the connections between observable variables [18].

To minimize the influence of outliers during model training, we applied Winsorization to triglycerides and HDL-C using the “DescTools” package in R [19, 20]. Values beyond the 1 st and 99th percentiles were replaced with the corresponding percentile values to constrain extreme deviations while preserving overall data distribution. The effectiveness of Winsorization was confirmed by visual inspection using boxplots, which demonstrated reduced influence of extreme values (see Supplementary Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression was selected as the primary analytical method due to its interpretability, suitability for binary outcomes, and robustness in clinical datasets. For the logistic regression models, we employed a structured approach to select potential confounding variables. First, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression was used to identify variables with the stronger associations with sepsis risk. The optimal penalization parameter (λ) was determined using the one-standard-error rule. Variables with non-zero coefficients in the penalized model were selected for multivariable modeling. Importantly, age and sex were forcibly retained due to their clinical relevance. Four multivariable logistic regression models were created to investigate the link between the AIP indices and the occurrence of sepsis. The models were adjusted as follows: Model 1 (unadjusted model): No covariates included; Model 2 (basic adjusted model): Adjusted for sex, age, body temperature, and heart rate’ Model 3 (laboratory-adjusted model): Further adjusted for SOFA score, SAPS II, GCS score, calcium, sodium, hemoglobin, red cell distribution width, and white blood cell count; Model 4 (fully adjusted model): Additionally adjusted for congestive heart failure, mild liver disease, use of vasopressors, insulin therapy, continuous renal replacement therapy, and acute kidney injury.

We used restricted cubic splines with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the AIP distribution to flexibly model the dose–response relationship, balancing flexibility and overfitting as recommended by Harrell.

Additionally, Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to assess covariate balance after matching, with SMD ≤ 0.1 indicating acceptable balance. The findings’ robustness was validated by repeating logistic regression analysis in the matched population. The consistency of correlations across several strata, such as sex, diabetes, heart failure, chronic liver disease, age, hypertension, insulin usage, AKI, and coronary heart disease, was assessed using subgroup analysis. The interaction effects of AIP, and these stratification factors were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests.

Finally, for the construction of machine learning models, we adopted a feature selection strategy optimized for performance. Specifically, we used only the LASSO-selected variables (as described above), combined with the primary exposure variables—AIP indices—to create a consistent input feature set across all algorithms. This approach ensured both model comparability and interpretability. All models were trained using 70% of the dataset, and performance was evaluated on the remaining 30% test set. To minimize overfitting, we applied five-fold cross-validation within the training data, along with grid search–based hyperparameter tuning, using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as the primary performance metric. SVM decisionfunction weights and SHapley Additive Explanations were utilized to enhance the final model’s interpretability and quantify the relative value of each variables [21]. The former captures each variable’s direct effect on the classification margin, while the latter provides an additive, modelagnostic measure of each feature’s average marginal contribution to the outcome.”

Results

Baseline clinical and demographic characteristics

This study comprised 1,531 hospitalized ischemic stroke patients, 413 (26.98%) of whom experienced sepsis while in the hospital, and 1,118 (73.02%) of whom did not. Regarding demographic and physiological parameters, patients in the sepsis group were older (72.88 vs. 70.92 years, P = 0.021) and presented with higher respiratory rates and body temperatures on admission. Although their oxygen saturation was slightly lower, the difference was not statistically significant. The sepsis group had significantly higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores, SOFA scores, and SAPS II scores, indicating a heavier burden of comorbidities and more severe illness at ICU admission. In terms of laboratory findings, patients with sepsis exhibited more pronounced inflammatory and metabolic disturbances, with elevated levels of white blood cell count, red cell distribution width, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, triglycerides, and glucose. In contrast, hemoglobin, red blood cell count, calcium, bicarbonate, and HDL-C were significantly lower, suggesting impaired nutritional and metabolic status. Neurologically, although GCS score was the same in both groups, its distribution differed significantly, indicating a higher likelihood of altered consciousness in some septic patients. Notably, There may be a connection between metabolic dysregulation and sepsis risk, as seen by the considerably higher AIP indices in the sepsis group. In terms of comorbidities and treatment characteristics, the sepsis group had higher prevalence of conditions such as mild liver disease, chronic kidney disease, dementia, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, and hypertension. They were also more likely to receive vasopressors, insulin therapy, and continuous renal replacement therapy. The incidence of acute kidney injury was markedly higher in this group.

These findings collectively suggest that patients who developed sepsis were older, more critically ill, exhibited stronger inflammatory responses, more profound metabolic derangements, and required more intensive clinical interventions. Detailed comparisons are presented in Table 1.

LASSO regression and multicollinearity control

The LASSO method introduces an L1-penalty term to regularize the regression coefficients, thereby enhancing model generalizability and suppressing the influence of redundant variables.

For selecting the regularization parameter λ, we applied the “one-standard-error rule,” which favors the most parsimonious model within one standard error of the minimum mean cross-validated error. The optimal λ value was determined to be 0.02218385, resulting in the selection of 17 factors with the highest relevance to the outcome for inclusion in subsequent modeling.

The results derived from the LASSO model are visualized in Fig. 2. The left panel illustrates the coefficient profiles as a function of log(λ), showing the shrinkage paths of the candidate variables; the right panel presents the cross-validation curve used to determine the optimal penalization strength. The ranked importance of the selected 17 variables is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

LASSO regression for variable selection

In building the final multivariable regression models, we additionally included age and sex as forced covariates, given their well-established influence on clinical outcomes. To ensure model stability and avoid multicollinearity, we performed variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis on all candidate factors. Variables with VIF values > 5 were excluded to reduce the impact of collinearity on parameter estimates. Detailed collinearity diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Association between AIP index and sepsis

Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the link between the AIP indices and the occurrence of sepsis during the intensive care unit stay of an ischemic stroke patient (Table 2). When modeled as continuous variables, AIP remained significantly associated with sepsis after progressive covariate adjustment. In the fully adjusted model, greater AIP levels were independently linked with an increased incidence of sepsis (OR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.12–2.71).

When AIP were categorized into tertiles, a significant relationship with sepsis risk was observed. The risk of sepsis was substantially greater for patients in the highest tertile (Q3) of AIP than for those in the lowest tertile (Q1) (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.09–2.09 for AIP). Tertile-based classification provided a more stable risk gradient than continuous variables, and P for trend values remained significant across models 1 to 3 (P < 0.05).

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the ROC analysis demonstrated that AIP alone exhibited Limited discriminatory power for predicting sepsis, with AUC values of 0.572. Notably, AIP standalone performance was inferior to that of the SOFA score (AUC = 0.597) and the SAPS II score (AUC = 0.691). The AUC of the SOFA + SAPS II model was 0.7012 (95% CI 0.6724–0.7301). Adding AIP increased the AUC to 0.7213 (95% CI 0.6935–0.7491), a significant gain by DeLong’s test. Therefore, metabolic indices are not intended to replace existing clinical scoring systems but rather to serve as adjunctive markers that may enhance accuracy when used in conjunction with established tools. However, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression revealed a significant linear positive association between AIP and sepsis.

Restricted cubic spline and ROC analyses

Sensitivity analysis

In the subset of 1,182 patients with ischemic stroke coded as the principal diagnosis (diagnoses_icd.seq_num = 1), multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that, compared with the lowest AIP tertile (Q1), the highest tertile (Q3) was associated with sepsis risk with odds ratios of 1.77 (95% CI 1.29–2.44; P < 0.001), 1.97 (95% CI 1.41–2.74; P < 0.001), 1.72 (95% CI 1.20–2.46; P = 0.003), and 1.59 (95% CI 1.10–2.31; P = 0.014) in Models 1 through 4, respectively. When AIP was analyzed as a continuous variable, the OR ranged from 1.76 to 2.91. Trend tests were significant in all models (P < 0.05). These findings closely parallel those of the primary cohort, confirming the robustness of the association between AIP and sepsis risk (see Supplementary Tables S2).

PropensityScore–Matched multivariable regression analysis

Patients in the whole study population were classified as high or low based on the median values of the AIP (0.3444957) indices. PSM was conducted between the high and low groups using a nearest-neighbor matching technique with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score to assure match quality in order to further evaluate the findings’ robustness.

The final matched cohorts included 576 pairs for AIP groups. After matching, all covariates demonstrated standardized mean differences (SMDs) < 0.1 (Fig. 4), indicating good balance across baseline characteristics—except for the outcome variable (sepsis) and variables used to compute AIP. Key clinical covariates such as SOFA score and insulin use ratio were successfully balanced post-matching (see Supplementary Tables S3). Multivariable logistic regression was repeated in the matched cohorts (Table 3). After full adjustment for residual confounding, high AIP was associated with occurrence of sepsis (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.01–1.82).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analysis for the AIP index showed a significant correlation between elevated AIP levels and a greater risk of sepsis (Fig. 5). The impact was more noticeable in men, those without diabetes, and people under 65. High AIP was strongly associated with sepsis in patients < 65 years (OR = 4.01; 95% CI 2.15–7.49; P < 0.001), with a significant age interaction (P for interaction = 0.027). Although marginal interactions were noted in some subgroups, the overall results support AIP as a reliable factors.

Subgroup analysis

Machine learning models

Two sets were randomly selected from the total dataset: training (70%) and testing (30%). The training data was utilized to create 10 different machine learning models, which were then tested across both datasets. Following feature selection via LASSO regression, the final set of factors included body temperature, congestive heart failure, mild liver disease, use of vasopressors, insulin therapy, white blood cell count, SOFA score, SAPS II score, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), continuous renal replacement therapy, sodium, hemoglobin, acute kidney injury, serum calcium, red cell distribution width (RDW), heart rate, and AIP index.

As shown in Fig. 6, the support vector machine (SVM) model demonstrated the best AUC in the testing set, with an AUC of 0.788 (95% CI: 0.741–0.835), outperforming all other models. Comparative AUCs for other models were: Logistic regression (0.781), CatBoost (0.780), Random Forest (0.773), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) (0.779), K-nearest neighbors (KNN) (0.757), Neural Network (0.783), XGBoost (0.772), LightGBM (0.729), and AdaBoost (0.671). In summary, the SVM model demonstrates excellent discriminative power across multiple evaluation metrics and achieves the highest AUC value.

Machine learning model evaluation. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves of the support vector machine (SVM) model with and without AIP. Calibration plots comparing predicted and observed probabilities. Decision curve analysis (DCA) showing net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities

To further evaluate the contribution of AIP, we compared model performance with and without the AIP index. An SVM model constructed with 16 predictors excluding AIP achieved an AUC of 0.780 in the test set. When AIP was added, the AUC increased to 0.788, indicating an incremental improvement in discrimination. Calibration plots also demonstrated that the model including AIP was closer to the ideal diagonal line, while decision curve analysis (DCA) showed a higher net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities.

We conducted dual feature importance analyses. As shown in Fig. 7, the left panel presents the standardized feature importance scores from the SVM model, where factors such as SAPSII, white blood cell count (WBC), RDW, AIP, and SOFA score were ranked among the most influential variables based on their contribution to the decision boundary. The right panel displays SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations) values, which offer a unified approach to interpreting individual predictions by quantifying each variable’s marginal effect on the output. SHAP highlighted vasoactive use, WBC, SAPSII, and AIP as major contributors. While the relative rankings differ between the two methods due to their underlying mechanisms—SVM importance being based on margin contributions and SHAP incorporating nonlinear interactions—both consistently identified metabolic markers and severity scores as an important contributing feature.

Discussion

Based on a large cohort of critically ill ischemic stroke patients, this study is the first to systematically evaluate the independent association value of the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) for in-hospital sepsis risk. AIP levels were positively associated with sepsis, and these associations remained robust after adjustment for confounders. Inclusion of AIP improved the SVM model’s AUC from 0.780 to 0.788, with better calibration and greater clinical net benefit. These findings indicate that, despite modest SHAP importance, AIP provides incremental predictive value in sepsis risk assessment.

Our results are in line with recent evidence showing that a higher stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) at admission predicts worse in-hospital outcomes in stroke patients [22]. While SHR reflects acute glucose stress, AIP captures Lipid-related insulin resistance. Together, these findings suggest that both glucose and Lipid metabolic disturbances may contribute to adverse outcomes. Previous studies have confirmed the predictive value of AIP in various metabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease [23–27]. In stroke populations, AIP has been linked to intracranial artery stenosis, incident stroke, and poor functional outcomes [12, 28].

AIP are simple metabolic indicators derived from routine lipid parameters. AIP serve as accessible and validated surrogate markers for IR [29, 30]. It is well established that the IR state is characterized by elevated triglycerides and reduced HDL-C, which promotes the formation of small dense LDL (sdLDL), accelerates atherosclerosis, and enhances systemic inflammation [31–33]. Oxidized sdLDL is readily taken up by macrophages to form foam cells, contributing to plaque instability, tissue hypoperfusion, and multi-organ dysfunction. Mechanistically, IR impairs endothelial function through PI3K/Akt inhibition and GSK3β activation, promoting oxidative stress, leukocyte adhesion, and pro-inflammatory cascades [23, 34]. Concurrently, the lipotoxic state reflected by elevated AIP can disrupt the endothelial barrier and enhance the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines potentially exacerbating early neurological injury [35]. In ischemic stroke patients, the baseline presence of neuroinflammation and immune dysfunction makes them highly susceptible to infection, while underlying metabolic dysregulation may act as a “second hit” that triggers sepsis.

The biological plausibility linking high AIP indices to increased sepsis risk lies in the impaired detoxification of circulating endotoxins. HDL, a key lipoprotein in innate immunity, plays a critical role in binding and clearing lipopolysaccharides (LPS), thereby mitigating systemic inflammation. Levels et al. reported that over 60% of circulating LPS binds to HDL under physiological conditions [36]., and Walley et al. further demonstrated that endogenous lipoproteins, particularly HDL, attenuate the host response to endotoxin through LPS neutralization [37]. These findings are supported by in vivo evidence from Levine et al., who showed that HDL supplementation improved survival in endotoxemic mice by reducing LPS bioactivity and inflammatory cytokine release [38]. In addition, triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) also contribute to LPS clearance and immune modulation [39]. In metabolic disorders, however, reduced HDL-C levels and TRL dysfunction—as reflected by elevated AIP—may compromise this lipoprotein-mediated defense, thereby amplifying systemic inflammation and increasing sepsis susceptibility.

There are several noteworthy advantages to this study. First, it makes use of the vast amount of excellent real-world data from MIMIC-IV, which enables thorough analysis using a wealth of clinical factors. Second, it evaluated the impact of metabolism-related indicators alone and in combination in a methodical manner. Third, the use of propensity score matching (PSM) enhanced causal inference by mitigating confounding.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the retrospective cohort design restricts causal inference, and although we employed multivariable adjustment, propensity score matching, and machine learning models, the possibility of residual confounding remains. Secondly, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms by which AIP may contribute to sepsis remain speculative and require validation in experimental models. Thirdly, AIP were measured only once at baseline, and we did not account for their dynamic trajectories over time, which may influence their performance. Moreover, the dataset (MIMIC-IV) was derived from a single tertiary-care center in the United States, and the study population was predominantly of limited ethnic diversity, which may affect the generalizability of our findings to other populations. In addition, detailed information on the site of infection, pathogen type, and sepsis etiology was not comprehensively available in the database, which may influence the clinical interpretation of our results. Furthermore, due to excessive missingness (> 20%), we did not include potentially important infection markers such as Creactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) in our analysis, which may have introduced selection bias and attenuated the observed associations. Finally, although we employed a multistep temporal validation—restricting to patients with ischemic stroke as the primary diagnosis (diagnoses_icd.seq_num = 1), and excluding any cases where the first AIP measurement occurred on or after the recorded sepsis onset —the absence of a precise timestamp for actual stroke onset in MIMICIV may still allow for minimal temporal misclassification. Future prospective studies with exact strokeonset and laboratory event timestamps are warranted to confirm the stroke→AIP→sepsis sequence and strengthen causal inference. Although we took several steps to reduce the risk of overfitting, including LASSO variable selection, five-fold cross-validation, and validation in an independent test set. The event-per-variable ratio in our study was approximately 24, exceeding the recommended threshold and supporting the statistical stability of the models. Nevertheless, because external validation was not performed, some risk of overfitting remains, and the generalizability of our machine learning findings should be confirmed in future studies.

Several directions require more exploration. First, future prospective studies should aim to determine clinically meaningful cut-off values for AIP in the context of sepsis risk stratification. These thresholds should be derived from outcome-linked ROC curve analyses and validated across independent, multi-center cohorts to improve generalizability and bedside applicability. Second, dynamic monitoring of AIP trajectories over time—rather than single-point measurements—may offer additional prognostic value, particularly in rapidly evolving ICU settings. Given the retrospective observational design, our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Finally, the stronger association in patients younger than 65 years should be interpreted cautiously. This result may reflect more pronounced metabolic–immune interactions in younger patients, but residual confounding cannot be excluded. Therefore, this finding should be considered exploratory and requires validation in future prospective studies.

Conclusion

To conclude, this is the first study to show that the AIP index had a substantial association with sepsis. However, as this was a retrospective single-center analysis, prospective validation studies across diverse populations and clinical settings are needed to confirm the robustness and generalizability of our findings.
