Diabetic hand infections: a case series highlighting diagnostic and therapeutic challenges
Authors: Meryem Billur Miroğlu, Sibel Yıldız Kaya, Rıdvan Karaali, Gözde Dalgan, Çağlar Yılmaz, Berrak Karatan, Neşe Saltoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Clinical data on this condition remain limited.MethodsThis case series included patients hospitalized with diabetic hand infection at a tertiary care center between 2020 and 2026. Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is associated with impaired immune responses, microvascular abnormalities, and delayed wound healing, all of which increase susceptibility to infection. In addition, chronic hyperglycemia impairs neutrophil functions such as chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and intracellular killing, resulting in reduced host defense mechanisms [1, 2].

Although infections are common in patients with diabetes, most published studies have focused on lower extremity infections. In contrast, diabetic hand infections are relatively uncommon and remain less clearly defined in the literature [3, 4]. Despite being less frequent, these infections may cause serious functional impairment because of the complex anatomy and essential function of the hand. The reported incidence of diabetic hand infections ranges from 0.7% to 12% [5]. These infections may show a rapid and aggressive course, progressing to deep tissue involvement, osteomyelitis, gangrene, and even limb loss [5].

Minor trauma is often described as a precipitating factor. Small cuts, puncture wounds, and foreign body injuries may create a portal of entry for microorganisms. In tropical regions, this condition has been termed “tropical diabetic hand syndrome,” which is characterized by rapidly progressive cellulitis, abscess formation, and tissue necrosis following trivial trauma [6, 7]. The microbiological spectrum is broad. Gram-positive bacteria, particularly Staphylococcus aureus, are the most commonly reported pathogens; however, gram-negative bacteria and opportunistic microorganisms may also be isolated, especially in complicated cases [8, 9].

Since the available literature is limited, further clinical data are needed to better define the features and outcomes of diabetic hand infections. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, microbiological spectrum, and treatment outcomes of patients managed at a tertiary care center.

Methods

This case series included patients hospitalized with diabetic hand infection at Cerrahpaşa Medical Faculty between 2020 and 2026. Patients aged 18 years or older with confirmed diabetes mellitus and clinical, microbiological, and/or radiological findings consistent with hand infection were included. Patients aged < 18 years were excluded. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin A1c) levels, laboratory findings, microbiological culture results, imaging findings, and treatment data were obtained from medical records. Radiological evaluation was performed using plain radiography and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Empirical antibiotic therapy was initiated at presentation and subsequently adjusted according to culture results. Surgical interventions, including debridement, abscess drainage, and amputation, were performed when clinically indicated.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Cerrahpaşa Faculty of Medicine Medical Research Ethics Committee (approval number: dT23ef0l). The datasets generated and analysed during the current study are not publicly available due to patient confidentiality and ethical restrictions but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. No financial support was received for the conduct of this study.

Case presentations

Case 1

A 55-year-old woman with an 11-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension presented with swelling and erythema of the right second finger. Her HbA1c level was 12.9%. Incision and drainage were performed, and tissue cultures yielded Enterococcus spp. and methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococcus spp. MRI demonstrated osteomyelitis (Figs. 1 and 2). Although teicoplanin and piperacillin–tazobactam were administered, distal phalanx amputation was required. The patient showed clinical and radiological improvement during follow-up.

Case 2

A 63-year-old woman was admitted with a complicated soft tissue infection of the left thumb that developed after trauma. She had a history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Cultures yielded methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Corynebacterium spp., and Candida spp. Multiple surgical interventions, including debridement and grafting, were required (Fig. 3). She received prolonged antimicrobial therapy, with eventual clinical resolution.

Discussion

Diabetic hand infections remain a relatively under-recognized complication within the broad spectrum of diabetes-related infections. However, despite being less common than diabetic foot infections, they may lead to substantial morbidity because of the complex anatomy and critical functional role of the hand. Their clinical course is often aggressive, with rapid progression to deep tissue involvement and a considerable risk of amputation [3–5]. Therefore, their low frequency should not lead to underestimation, since delayed diagnosis and treatment may result in serious functional loss and permanent disability.

In our series, the median age of the patients was 59 years (range: 49–79 years), which is consistent with previous reports showing that diabetic hand infections are more common in middle-aged and elderly individuals [3–5]. Poor glycemic control was also a notable finding, HbA1c levels were above 7% in all patients and above 10% in two patients. This observation is consistent with previous studies and suggests that inadequate glycemic control may increase both the risk and severity of infection in patients with diabetic hand involvement [1, 10, 11]. Hyperglycemia is known to impair several components of the immune response, including neutrophil chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and intracellular killing, thereby weakening host defense and facilitating the progression of infection [1, 2, 10].

Another important finding in our study was the important role of minor trauma as a triggering factor. Even small injuries appeared sufficient to initiate infection, emphasizing the vulnerability of patients with diabetes to apparently minor skin or soft tissue damage. This pattern is similar to the concept of “tropical diabetic hand syndrome,” in which minor trauma may be followed by rapidly progressive cellulitis, abscess formation, and tissue necrosis [6, 7]. However, these findings suggest that this phenomenon is not restricted to tropical settings but may represent a broader pathophysiological pattern applicable across diverse populations. From a clinical perspective, this point is important because it highlights the need for careful evaluation and early treatment even after minor hand injuries in patients with diabetes.

From a microbiological perspective, our findings support the heterogeneous nature of diabetic hand infections. In our series, polymicrobial infection was observed in 85% of cases, which is in agreement with earlier reports describing the mixed microbiological profile of these infections [8]. Staphylococcus spp. were the most commonly isolated pathogens, but gram-negative organisms were also identified in 28% of patients. This finding supports previous observations suggesting that higher prevalence of gram-negative organisms in complicated infections [9]. The presence of resistant and gram-negative pathogens may complicate treatment; therefore, local microbiological patterns and individual patient characteristics should be considered when selecting initial empirical antimicrobial therapy [12, 13].

Surgical treatment remains a major component of management in diabetic hand infections. Most patients in our study underwent surgical intervention, and the amputation rate was 28%, which appears comparable to rates reported in the literature [5, 14]. This high rate of surgery reflects the aggressive nature of these infections and the limited role of conservative treatment alone in advanced cases. Early debridement and timely drainage are essential for infection control and for preventing further tissue destruction [15]. Even when amputation involves only part of a digit, the functional consequences may still be substantial.

The high frequency of osteomyelitis in our series further highlights the severity of diabetic hand infections. Bone involvement is a well-recognized marker of poor outcome and has been associated with an increased risk of digital amputation [11, 14–16]. Our findings are consistent with this observation and support the importance of early imaging and prompt treatment when deep infection is suspected.

Diabetic hand infections may also have important long-term consequences beyond the acute phase. Even digital amputation has been associated with reduced quality of life and increased mortality in previous studies [17, 18]. In addition, some patients may require further procedures or revision surgery after initial treatment. These findings show that management should focus not only on infection control, but also on preservation of function, rehabilitation, and long-term follow-up.

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective and included a small number of patients. Second, because of its descriptive design, it does not allow the identification of independent predictors of outcome. Nevertheless, given the relative rarity of diabetic hand infections, our findings still provide useful clinical and microbiological information. They also highlight the need for larger multicenter studies to better define prognostic factors and to support more standardized treatment strategies.

Conclusion

Diabetic hand infections should be regarded as a distinct and clinically important complication of diabetes mellitus. Early recognition, appropriate imaging, timely surgical intervention, and culture-guided antimicrobial therapy are essential for improving outcomes. Further studies are needed to optimize management strategies and to identify preventive approaches in high-risk patients.
