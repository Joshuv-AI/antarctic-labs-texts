Postnatal alterations in skeletal muscle fiber composition of myostatin-knockout chickens
Authors: Si Eun Kim, Heesu Jeong, Ismail Shaleh, Soo Hyun Kim, Gap-don Kim, Tae Sub Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In a previous study, we identified a hypermuscular phenotype attributed to both muscle fiber hyperplasia and hypertrophy in myostatin (MSTN)-knockout (KO) chickens generated by clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-associated protein 9 (CRISPR-Cas9) technology. In mammals, MSTN deletion results in increased production of fast glycolytic fibers, accompanied by decreased production of slow oxidative fibers. However, the effects of MSTN deletion on muscle fiber types have rarely been studied in avian species such as chickens. In this study, we analyzed muscle fibers in chickens at various ages to investigate whether MSTN deletion alters muscle fiber composition after hatching. Immunofluorescence and histochemical staining, including ATPase and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydrogen–tetrazolium reductase (NADH–TR) staining, were performed on biceps femoris (BF) tissues of wild-type (WT) and MSTN-KO chickens at hatching and at 10 and 18 weeks. BF muscle fibers were classified into three types (1, 2A, and 2B). MSTN-KO chickens produced more muscle fibers than did WT chickens at all ages. Fiber hypertrophy was not observed in MSTN-KO chickens at hatching, but was detected in all fiber types at 10 and 18 weeks. Compared to WT chickens, there was a significant decrease in Type 1 fibers and an increase in Type 2B fibers in the BF tissues of MSTN-KO chickens, whereas the amount of Type 2A fibers remained unchanged. Furthermore, MSTN deletion led to upregulated expression of genes in the glycolytic pathway and MyoD in skeletal muscles. Thus, MSTN deletion altered muscle fiber composition in chickens, inducing Type 2B fiber dominance and hyperplasia during the embryonic stage, whereas muscle hypertrophy due to nutritional uptake became predominant after hatching.

Introduction

Since the myostatin gene (MSTN, also known as growth differentiation factor 8) was identified in double-muscled cattle (McPherron and Lee, 1997), it has been widely recognized to be a negative regulator of muscle growth and a crucial economic trait in livestock. An abnormal increase in muscle mass has been reported in various animals with MSTN mutation, including humans (Schuelke et al., 2004), rodents (McPherron et al., 1997; Girgenrath et al., 2005), cattle (Grobet et al., 1997; Kambadur et al., 1997), pigs (Qian et al., 2015), and dogs (Mosher et al., 2007). Given the similar actions of MSTN across vertebrates (Rodger and Garikipati, 2008), its disruption appears to lead to a hypermuscular phenotype in mammals as well as in avian species such as chickens (Bhattacharya et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2020) and quail (Lee et al., 2020).

The hypertrophic phenotype observed in MSTN-deficient animals is often accompanied by alterations in muscle fiber composition (Stavaux et al., 1994; Amthor et al., 2007; Girgenrath et al., 2005; Hennebry et al., 2009; Qian et al., 2022). MSTN-knockout (KO) mammals exhibit an enhanced glycolytic phenotype characterized by a significant increase in the number of Type 2B muscle fibers and decreases in the quantities of Type 1 and 2A oxidative fibers (Girgenrath et al., 2005; Hennebry et al., 2009). We previously reported a reduction in the amount of Type 1 fibers in the biceps femoris (BF) muscles of 18-week-old MSTN-KO male chickens, which implied that MSTN deletion induces alterations in muscle fiber distribution (Kim et al., 2020). Such shifts in muscle fiber composition are of considerable importance in the poultry industry because muscle fiber characteristics are directly related to growth rate, feed efficiency, and meat-quality traits (Huo et al., 2021; Mo et al., 2023; Ono et al., 1993; Weng et al., 2022). Artificial selection for fast-growing broiler breeds is characterized by the predominance of glycolytic Type 2B fiber; however, this excessive growth induces myopathies, reduced meat quality, and metabolic stress (Dransfield and Sosnicki, 1999; Petracci and Cavani, 2012). Thus, muscle fiber composition is a key factor influencing poultry productivity and meat quality, with potential consequences for genetic selection, husbandry practices, and consumer acceptance.

In mammals, the skeletal muscle fibers of newborns undergo terminal differentiation, also known as postnatal transition, in which developmental myosin heavy-chain protein (MyHC) shifts to adult MyHC (Agbulut et al., 2003; Strbenc et al., 2006). In cattle, MSTN expression is highest during the embryonic stage, but persists in muscle tissue into adulthood, implying its significant role in regulating muscle development (Shibata et al., 2006). Therefore, MSTN may influence both fiber type determination during embryonic muscle development and the postnatal transition to mature fiber types. A previous study established the first MSTN-KO chickens using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) technology (Kim et al., 2020). However, the temporal dynamics of muscle fiber differentiation following MSTN deletion remain poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear whether MSTN influences fiber type specification in chickens during embryonic myogenesis or primarily regulates the post-hatching transition to mature fiber types. Although the effects of MSTN deficiency on muscle fiber characteristics have been well characterized in mammalian models, few such studies have been conducted in avian species. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential to clarify when MSTN exerts its regulatory role in chickens and to evaluate its potential as a genetic target for poultry breeding.

In this study, we investigated the impact of MSTN deletion on post-hatching differentiation of various skeletal muscle fiber types in chickens. We analyzed BF samples at hatching, 10 weeks, and 18 weeks, and examined the timing of MSTN-mediated regulation of fiber type specification. Our results imply that MSTN may be useful as a genetic marker for improving poultry growth performance and meat quality.

Materials and methods

Preparation of muscle samples

White Leghorn chickens were raised under a standardized management system at the University Animal Farm of Seoul National University in Pyeongchang, Korea. The chickens used in this study were handled according to guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (no. SNU-241206-2) of Seoul National University. To evaluate the temporal effects of MSTN deficiency on post-hatching muscle fiber development, we sampled muscle tissue in three growth stages: hatching (0 weeks), early growth (10 weeks), and pre-sexual maturity (18 weeks). Pectoralis major (PM) and biceps femoris (BF) muscle tissue samples were obtained from hatching, 10-week-old, and 18-week-old wild-type (WT) and MSTN-KO chickens. Muscle tissue weight was measured and compared between WT and MSTN-KO chickens. Cryosection samples were prepared by cutting the tissue samples into three or four pieces (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm × 1.0 cm) that were subsequently frozen in 2-methylbutane chilled with liquid nitrogen. Samples obtained from newly hatched chickens were frozen with optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Tissue-Tek, Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA). Frozen muscle samples were stored at –80°C until analysis.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Frozen samples were cut into 10-μm-thick sections using a cryostat microtome (CM1520, Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at –25°C. Double-staining immunohistochemistry was performed to distinguish slow and fast MyHC and to delineate cell boundaries. The sections were blocked with 10% normal goat serum (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and then primary and secondary antibodies were incubated sequentially. The primary antibodies used were S35, anti-slow MyHC or F59, and anti-fast (DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA); anti-laminin (GeneTEX, Irvine, CA, USA) was also used to recognize the extracellular membranes of muscle fibers. Anti-mouse antibody conjugated with Alexa Flour 488 and anti-rabbit antibody conjugated with Alexa Flour 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were used as secondary antibodies to visualize the slow/fast fiber types and basement membrane, respectively. Anti-MyHC staining was performed overnight, followed by anti-laminin staining. All antibodies were used at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL. Fluorescence staining images were obtained under a microscope (EVOS M5000; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Myosin adenosine triphosphatase (mATPase) staining

Histochemical analysis of adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) activity in muscle fibers was performed as previously described (Brooke and Kaiser, 1970; Guth and Samaha, 1970), with minor modifications. Briefly, to determine ATPase activity under acidic conditions, 10-μm-thick myofiber cross-sections were preincubated in a pre-acidic buffer (pH 4.60–4.80) containing 100 mM potassium chloride and sodium acetate for 10 min. To estimate myosin ATPase activity under alkaline conditions, the sections were fixed in 5% paraformaldehyde solution at 4°C for 5 min, then preincubated for 15 min in a buffer consisting of 20 mM CaCl2 and glycine adjusted to pH 10.60. Following preincubation, the sections were incubated at room temperature for 25 min in an incubation solution containing 2.5 mM ATP. The sections were washed three times for 30 s each with 1% CaCl2, and then reacted in cobalt chloride solution for 3 min. The sections were then washed three times with distilled water and reacted in 1% yellow ammonium sulfide solution for 30 s. Finally, muscle fibers were distinguished based on staining intensity; ATPase-active myofibers were stained black, whereas non-ATPase-active fibers exhibited low staining intensity.

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydrogen–tetrazolium reductase (NADH–TR) staining

NADH–TR staining was conducted as previously described (Dunant et al., 2003). A 2-mg/mL nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT, N5514, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) solution and a 1.6-mg/mL NADH (N8129, Sigma-Aldrich) solution were prepared by dissolution in 0.05 M tris buffer and stored at –20°C until use. The NBT and NADH solutions were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and applied to tissue sections, which were then incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Following incubation, the tissue sections were washed three times with distilled water, dehydrated by sequential immersion in 30% and 60% acetone for 30 s, and then 90% acetone until the purple color was removed. Then, the sequential immersion process was reversed by soaking the sections in 90%, 60%, and 30% acetone. Finally, the sections were washed three times with distilled water. NADH–TR staining patterns were observed using a previously established technique (Peter et al., 1972).

MitoTracker red staining

MitoTracker Red (CMXRos #9082; Cell Signaling Technology) was prepared and used following the manufacturer’s instructions. For mitochondrial staining, frozen tissue sections were incubated in 200 nM MitoTracker Red solution for 20 min at room temperature, and then washed three times with phosphate-buffered solution (PBS) and fixed with cold methanol at –20°C for 15 min. After fixation, the sections were washed in PBS and observed using a fluorescence microscope at excitation and emission wavelengths of 579 and 599 nm, respectively.

Muscle fiber analysis

We used three male and three female individuals from each age group of WT and MSTN-KO chickens for muscle fiber analysis. Three images were randomly captured for each specimen; approximately 1200–1700 fibers were obtained per section. Muscle fiber characteristics such as relative fiber number (%) and cross-sectional area (CSA, µm2) were analyzed using the Image-Pro Plus program (Media Cybernetics Inc., Rockville, MD, USA). Cross-sectional images of BF muscles were obtained using a Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). CSA was measured using the whole muscle.

Muscle fibers were enumerated by summing the number of muscle fibers in each of three images and multiplying the result by the ratio of the image size to the muscle CSA (mm2). The relative fiber area (%) was determined as the ratio of total CSA (µm2) for each fiber type to the total measured fiber area. To calculate the fiber density (number/mm2), the relative fiber area (%) of each muscle fiber was converted to the area (mm2) occupied by the muscle fibers in 1 mm2. Subsequently, this area was divided by the CSA (µm²) of each muscle fiber to determine the number of muscle fibers per mm².

Genomic polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from chicken feather pulp using a salting-out method. Cell lysis buffer with proteinase K was added to a microtube and incubated for 3 h at 55°C. Then, DNA was precipitated with 2-isopropanol and washed with 70% ethanol. Centrifugation was repeated, with the supernatant discarded after each step. Genomic DNA remaining in the microtube was air-dried and dissolved in sterile distilled water. The extracted DNA was stored at –20°C. Genomic PCR was performed using 100 ng DNA per PCR reaction in 35 cycles at an annealing temperature of 60°C. All primers used for genomic PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1. For sex determination, CPE15 and USP1/USP3 primers were used as an internal control and to amplify W-chromosome (female)-specific fragments, respectively. The PCR products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis.

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from BF muscle tissue sampled from WT or MSTN-KO male chickens using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and concentration of RNA were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg RNA using the Superscript III First-strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen). A 20-μL sample of the cDNA synthesis product was diluted with distilled water to a final volume of 40 μL.

To quantify the target gene transcripts, 2 μL of cDNA and 10 pmol of primer were added to 25 μL of qRT-PCR reaction mix. The primers used for reference and target genes are listed in Supplementary Table 2. qRT-PCR analysis was performed using the iCycler iQ Real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and relative mRNA levels were determined using EvaGreen (Biotium, Fremont, CA, USA). The qPCR steps were as follows: initial incubation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 30 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s. After amplification, melting curve analysis was performed to verify the specificity of the PCR products. Relative mRNA levels between different target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the reference. Three biological replicates were performed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance were used to compare total fiber numbers between WT and MSTN-KO samples, and muscle composition among age groups. Significant differences from control groups were evaluated at thresholds of P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001, and P < 0.0001. All data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) based on triplicate analysis.

In both males and females, muscle mass increases resulting from MSTN deficiency were significantly greater in leg muscles than in breast muscles (Supplementary Figure 1). The significant difference in PM weight was observed in 10-week-old female KO chickens, in which PM weight was 62.24% higher than in WT chickens (P = 0.0063; Supplementary Figure 1B). In contrast, the BF samples displayed significant weight differences in both sexes, except at hatching (Supplementary Figure 1B, C).

Immunofluorescence and histochemistry were used to analyze BF muscle fiber types at each growth stage (Fig. 1B). Based on the mammal muscle classification scheme, the chicken BF muscle fibers were divided into slow oxidative (Type 1), fast oxidative (Type 2A), and fast glycolytic fibers (Type 2B). At the hatching stage, most muscle fibers exhibited strong, uniform NADH–TR staining intensity, making it difficult to distinguish Type 2 subtypes. Therefore, the BF muscle fibers of hatchling chicks were broadly categorized into Type 1 and 2 based on MyHC isoform staining. At 10 and 18 weeks, NADH dehydrogenase activity was clearly observed in the F59-positive fiber population, and fibers with strong NADH–TR staining intensity were classified as Type 2A. MitoTracker Red staining was also performed to analyze mitochondrial distribution within the muscle fibers. Notably, Type 1 fibers had greater mitochondrial content at 10 and 18 weeks, whereas Type 2A fibers had greater NADH activity (Fig. 1B).

Comparison of gene expression patterns in BF tissues between WT and MSTN-KO chickens

To examine the expression patterns of myogenic regulatory factors, fiber type-specific marker genes, and glycolysis-related genes, we conducted qRT-PCR analysis of the BF tissues of WT and MSTN-KO chickens (Fig. 6). Significantly upregulated expression levels of MyoD (1.22-fold, P = 0.02408), MyoG (1.64-fold, P = 0.0031), and Pax7 (1.23-fold, P = 0.0227) were found in MSTN-KO chickens compared to the WT (Fig. 6A). The expression levels of myofibrillar protein genes specifically associated with Type 1 fibers (TNNC1, MYL2, and ATP2A2) were significantly downregulated, while those of genes predominant in Type 2 fibers (TNNC2, MYL1, MYBPC2, and CAPZA1) were significantly upregulated in MSTN-KO chickens compared to the WT (Fig. 6B). Next, we analyzed the expression patterns of genes related to glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism (Fig. 6C). All glycolytic-pathway-related genes were upregulated in the BF tissues of the MSTN-KO chickens. The gene expression levels of PGM1, HK1, and GPI, which are involved in the initial steps of glycolysis, were significantly upregulated (2.14-, 1.87-, and 1.51-fold, respectively) in MSTN-KO chickens (Fig. 6C). Lactate dehydrogenases catalyze the interconversion between pyruvate and lactate in the final stages of glycolysis (Fig. 6D). Expression of LDHA increased 2.90-fold (P < 0.0001), whereas LDHB mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in MSTN-KO chickens (P < 0.0001; Fig. 6C). These increases and decreases in LDHA and LDHB expression levels indicate that MSTN-KO muscle is more reliant on anaerobic metabolism than on oxidative phosphorylation for energy production. PDCs and PDKs located within the mitochondria play critical roles in determining the metabolic fate of pyruvate according to the cellular metabolic state (Fig. 6D). Our analyses showed that both PDC (DLD, DLAT, PDHA2, and PDHB) and PDK (PDK1, PDK3, and PDK4) gene groups were significantly downregulated in MSTN-KO muscle.

Discussion

Muscle development and growth are achieved through increased fiber numbers during the embryonic stage, followed by increases in fiber diameter and length after birth (Smith, 1963). Myostatin deficiency in embryos leads to substantial skeletal muscle growth due to increased muscle fiber numbers (hyperplasia) and enlarged fiber size (hypertrophy) (McPherron et al., 1997). In the present study, we determined that the total number of muscle fibers was significantly higher in MSTN-KO chickens than in WT chickens from hatching. Both WT and MSTN-KO chickens consistently maintained total fiber numbers across all ages, aligning with previous research indicating that the overall number of muscle fibers is determined before birth. These results demonstrate that muscle fiber hyperplasia caused by MSTN deletion impacts muscle development during the embryonic stage rather than the growth period.

Muscle fibers in the BF of hatchlings could not be clearly distinguished by NADH–TR staining, likely due to the immature metabolic state of developing myofibers (Li et al., 2020). Immature myofibers that are still undergoing differentiation are expected to exhibit low or diverse enzymatic activity (Nemeth et al., 1989; Sieck et al., 2003), such that histochemical techniques based on metabolic properties have limited applicability in growing muscles (Bottinelli et al., 2000). To evaluate mitochondrial content, we conducted MitoTracker Red staining, which provides a direct marker of mitochondrial mass. However, discrepancies with our NADH–TR staining results indicated that mitochondrial enzyme activity did not always correspond to mitochondrial content. Supporting this interpretation, proteomic analyses by Murgia et al. (2015) showed that enzyme activity was modulated by fiber type-specific functional demands and that the abundance of certain proteins failed to consistently increase with mitochondrial mass when normalized for mitochondrial content. Therefore, to assess the relationship between mitochondrial content and enzyme activity accurately requires single-fiber-level analyses.

We propose two possible hypotheses for potential changes in muscle fiber distribution in MSTN-KO chickens: either inhibition of slow fiber development or fiber type transition during the developmental process. If the observed alteration in muscle fiber composition is attributable to fiber type transition, then it occurs either during embryonic myogenesis or as a consequence of post-hatching remodeling. To confirm post-hatching muscle fiber transition, we analyzed the composition of type 1 fibers in BF tissues. In both the WT and MSTN-KO groups, the numbers of Type 1 fibers remained unchanged over time, ruling out age as an influential factor. These findings support the hypothesis that muscle fiber type transformation occurs as the result of embryonic developmental processes. Matsakas et al. (2010) demonstrated that the conversion of muscle fibers to fast-twitch fibers in MSTN-null mice occurred from the secondary myogenesis stage; at embryonic day (ED) 14.5, significantly greater numbers of MYH-1 positive slow fibers were observed in MSTN−/− muscles than in WT muscles. However, by the end of secondary myogenesis at ED18.5, the quantity of these slow fibers had decreased by approximately 50% compared to the levels observed at ED14.5. These findings imply that the reduction in Type 1 fibers due to MSTN deletion is not the result of inhibited development during embryonic stages, but is more consistent with fiber type conversion to fast-twitch fibers during secondary myogenesis.

MSTN acts as a negative regulator of muscle development by regulating the transcriptional network of myogenic regulatory factors (Thomas et al., 2000; Langley et al., 2002; Joulia et al., 2003). Hennebry et al. (2009) proposed that MSTN deficiency leads to an increased number of Type 2B muscle fibers through enhanced MyoD expression. Significant MyoD mRNA expression has been detected in fast glycolytic fibers classified as Type 2B and 2X (Hughes et al., 1993). Additionally, a previous study reported that MyoD and Myf5 overexpression selectively stimulated MHC IIB promoter activity (Allen et al., 2001). Our results show significant upregulation of MyoD expression in the BF muscles of MSTN-KO chickens, consistent with previous findings in mammalian models. Therefore, the metabolic shift towards glycolysis in MSTN-KO muscles is driven by increased numbers of Type 2B fibers.

The increased muscle mass and associated reduction in fat observed in MSTN mutant animals provide opportunities to improve lean meat production efficiency (Fiems et al., 2012; Kang et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2020). However, increased fetal weight (Oldham et al., 2001) and excessive muscle development in MSTN-deficient cows lead to reduced pelvic inlet size, which increases the risk of dystocia at birth (Arthur et al., 1995). Other issues including increased stress susceptibility, reduced fertility, and lower neonatal survival rates in livestock have also been reported (Arthur et al., 1988; Miar et al., 2014; Cyranoski et al., 2015). In contrast to the double-muscled phenotype observed prenatally in MSTN-deficient mammals, the muscle hypertrophy detected in MSTN-KO chickens appears to be achieved predominantly through compensatory mechanisms driven by nutrient intake during the growth period. MSTN-KO chickens showed no difference in muscle weight at hatching and smaller fiber size compared to WT chickens. However, during post-hatching growth, all fiber types in MSTN-KO chickens showed dramatic enlargement at both 10 and 18 weeks. In mammals, embryos receive a continuous nutrient supply through the placenta, whereas bird embryos rely solely on the finite nutrients contained within the egg. Under such nutrient-limited conditions, cellular hyperplasia during embryonic development may be accompanied by reduced cell size (Glazier, 2022). Therefore, MSTN-KO chickens may have the potential for enhanced livestock productivity and sustainability, while avoiding the birth-related issues commonly observed in mammals. Similarly, MSTN-mutant quail were reported to be heavier at all ages compared to WT quail from eggs of comparable weight (Kim et al., 2023); the mutant quail also had lower feed conversion ratios during the growth period, demonstrating efficient feed utilization and muscle productivity (Lee et al., 2021). Thus, MSTN may be a valuable genetic marker for the selection of poultry breeds with superior growth performance.

This study demonstrated that muscle fiber type transition induced by MSTN deficiency occurs primarily during the embryonic stage rather than after hatching, as revealed by our analysis of skeletal muscle fiber composition in MSTN-KO chickens after hatching. Although MSTN-KO chickens exhibited reduced muscle fiber diameters and lower body weights at birth, they maintained consistent weight gain throughout the growth period and ultimately developed a double-muscled phenotype. These findings imply that MSTN-KO chickens offer significant advantages for meat production, as they bypass the birth-related complications commonly associated with MSTN-deficient mammals.
