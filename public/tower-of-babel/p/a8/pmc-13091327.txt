Latent Supervision: A Method for Improved Performance and Calibration of Machine Learning Classification Models in Ophthalmology
Authors: Hady Yazbeck, Jad Assaf, Tom M Lietman, Jeremy D Keenan, John Jackson, Jayashree Kalpathy-Cramer, J Peter Campbell, Xubo Song, Travis K Redd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeStandard supervised learning assumes deterministic labels (e.g., positive or negative, present or absent), neglecting the diagnostic uncertainty inherent in clinical practice. We propose latent supervision, a novel algorithm which applies latent class analysis to incorporate multiple diagnostic tests or expert opinions to produce probabilistic labels (soft labels) for more accurate, calibrated ophthalmic artificial intelligence classifiers.DesignComparison of calibration and classification performance among ophthalmic computer vision model training techniques.SubjectsEleven thousand three hundred fifty-eight children aged 0 to 9 years evaluated as part of multinational trachoma screening, and 2100 adult subjects with bacterial or fungal keratitis (or both) collated from multiple prior clinical studies.MethodsWe compared latent supervision against supervised learning methods in 2 computer vision scenarios: trachoma screening with labels from different grader teams, and pathogen (fungal vs. bacterial) differentiation in infectious keratitis using labels derived from culture and smear results.Main Outcome MeasuresClassification performance was measured using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, F1 score, and accuracy. Model calibration was assessed by calibration curves and Brier score decomposition.ResultsFor the trachoma screening scenario, the grader 1, grader 2, grader 3, and ensemble supervised models had area under the receiver operating characteristic curves of 0.88, 0.91, 0.90, and 0.93, respectively. The latent supervision model had an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.94 with better calibration. For the infectious keratitis scenario, the culture-supervised model outperformed the smear-supervised model in bacterial keratitis classification (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.87 vs. 0.79), while the opposite was true in fungal keratitis classification (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.78 vs. 0.85). The latent supervision model performed consistently well on both tasks with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.86, and showed good calibration.ConclusionsLatent supervision provides a computationally inexpensive method to train artificial intelligence models using probabilistic labels, which model the diagnostic uncertainty inherent in medicine. In both test cases evaluated, latent supervision appeared to provide benefits both in terms of classification performance and model calibration. The latter is particularly important to improve public trust and enable meaningful clinical implementation.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Latent class analysis, Artificial intelligence, Trachoma, Infectious keratitis

Machine learning classifiers have significant potential for augmentative and automated applications in ophthalmology. These models represent mapping functions from inputs (e.g., images or structured clinical data) to a ground truth disease classification. The prevailing paradigm to train such models is supervised learning, which assumes the labels for each training example reliably approximate this ground truth. However, this is not a safe assumption for most medical applications because gold standard diagnostic information is rarely available at the scale necessary for model development. Most medical conditions, including ocular diseases, are diagnosed using an amalgamation of tests with imperfect sensitivity and specificity or based on expert clinical impression with substantial interexpert variability or even outright diagnostic error.1,2 This remains true even for diseases with well-defined diagnostic criteria or reference standards due to the combination of epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty, as well as error introduced by dichotomization of inherently continuous scales of disease severity.3,4 As a result, there is often substantial “noise” or inaccuracy in the labels used to train ophthalmic artificial intelligence models.

Bayes' error (or the “irreducible error”) of any classifier is proportional to the amount of noise in the labels used to train the model, thus the theoretical maximum performance of a model decreases as training label noise increases.5 Using “soft” or probabilistic labels to quantify the degree of uncertainty in the ground truth status (rather than “hard” or one-hot labels as in supervised learning) may thus result in better classification performance and has been shown to improve the generalizability of machine learning classifiers.6,7 Furthermore, a model trained to approximate an outcome distribution that reflects this inherent diagnostic uncertainty may produce predictions that can be reliably interpreted as the actual probability of disease. It is desirable that a medical classification model should temper its confidence when the diagnosis is uncertain, so that clinicians can factor this uncertainty into their overall decision-making process. In contrast, supervised learning fundamentally ignores diagnostic uncertainty, often producing poorly calibrated and overconfident predictions which can erode clinician and public trust.8

We sought to leverage these theoretical advantages of probabilistic labels for medical machine learning using a form of weak supervision we define as “latent supervision.” This framework adapts a finite mixture modeling technique known as latent class analysis (LCA), which incorporates information from multiple diagnostic tests or expert opinions to estimate each test's diagnostic performance in the absence of a gold standard reference. These estimates can then be used to define a posterior probability of disease or “soft label” for each subject, which is used to train the classification model. In the following we define the latent supervision algorithm and evaluate it empirically using 2 ophthalmic computer vision test cases.

Methods

Latent Supervision Algorithm

The method is performed in 3 steps: (1) estimate the sensitivity and specificity of each indicator variable (i.e., diagnostic test or expert opinion) and the prevalence of the disease(s) of interest using LCA; (2) use these estimates to establish the Bayesian posterior probability of disease for each subject; and (3) train a classifier using the posterior probabilities as labels with a rescaled mean squared error loss function. See Supplemental Methods Section 1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) for a detailed description of the theory and implementation of latent supervision.

We evaluated this technique using test cases involving the development of diagnostic computer vision models for the 2 most common causes of corneal blindness globally.

Test Case #1: Trachoma

Trachoma is one of the oldest known human diseases and the leading cause of infectious blindness globally. Annual mass distribution of macrolide antibiotics in communities with ≥5% prevalence of trachomatous inflammation-follicular (TF) has been demonstrated to be highly effective in preventing blindness from trachoma.9 Trachomatous inflammation-follicular is a clinical diagnosis made according to specific criteria based on the examination of the everted superior tarsal conjunctiva.10 However, despite this well-established definition, there is substantial intergrader variability in the diagnosis of TF.11 The majority of experts believe trachoma eradication is possible, but the most commonly cited barrier to achieving it is lack of accurate, consistent, inexpensive, and scalable surveillance tools to measure TF prevalence.12 To address these issues, we used data from the water, sanitation, and hygiene for control of trachoma in Ethiopia (WUHA) study conducted in rural Ethiopia to train a computer vision model to perform automated diagnosis of TF.13 This consisted of 56 725 images collected during 18 783 examination sessions from 11 358 children aged 0 to 9 years.14 Each set of photographs from a given examination was independently graded for TF by three 2-person teams of trained experts. Each team was masked to the predictions of the other teams; 35% of subjects had TF according to grader team 1, 39% according to grader team 2, and 27% according to grader team 3. The images were then partitioned into 80% train, 10% validation, and 10% test sets using stratified random sampling at the subject level.

To establish supervised learning benchmarks for comparison, we defined 3 sets of labels, one for each grader team's interpretation of whether TF was present. We used latent supervision to establish probabilistic labels for each image, incorporating information from all 3 grader teams. We then trained 4 distinct MobileNetV3 Large deep convolutional neural networks to perform binary TF classification: 3 using supervised learning with grader-defined labels, and 1 using latent supervision with probabilistic labels. The starting model architectures were all identical, all were pretrained on ImageNet, and in each case, we used the RMSProp optimization algorithm. We also evaluated a simple aggregated ensemble of the 3 supervised models by defining the ensemble output as the arithmetic mean of the 3 supervised models' predictions for a given subject.

Model Evaluation

To enable evaluation of model classification performance using standard metrics (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, F1 score, and accuracy), we established a binary label for the ensemble model by majority vote among the 3 grader teams, and similarly binarized the probabilistic labels derived from latent supervision using a threshold of 0.5. To evaluate the calibration of each model, we estimated miscalibration values using the Brier score decomposition and plotted calibration curves (see Supplemental Methods Section 2).

Test Case #2: Infectious Keratitis

Infectious keratitis is the fifth leading cause of blindness globally and can be caused by bacterial, fungal, viral, or protozoal organism invasion into the cornea.15 Each of these pathogen classes requires markedly different therapeutic regimens to eradicate the infection. Optimizing visual outcomes and reducing the risk of blindness from infectious keratitis requires prompt initiation of the correct antimicrobial therapy, but determining the underlying infectious etiology of a corneal ulcer is challenging. The gold standard for identifying the causative pathogen is culture of corneal scrapings, but even with state-of-the-art microbiology capabilities, the sensitivity of cultures is limited, resulting in false-negatives in approximately half of cases.16 Smears (including Gram stain and potassium hydroxide prep) can provide point-of-care diagnostic information but also suffer from limited sensitivity and specificity.16 In the absence of microbiologic data, antimicrobial selection must be made based on clinical impression, which is unfortunately unreliable even among expert cornea specialists.17 Point-of-care tools capable of instantaneously identifying the underlying cause of infection may help address this gap. We collated a database of infectious keratitis photographs from multiple prior prospective clinical studies.18, 19, 20, 21, 22 Because viral and protozoal organisms comprised such a small proportion of the data set, we excluded these cases, resulting in a dataset of 4471 images from 2100 subjects with bacterial or fungal keratitis (or both). Smear results were available for 1604 subjects, 10% of which were positive for bacterial keratitis and 80% for fungal keratitis. Culture results were available for 1253 subjects, 51% of which were positive for bacterial keratitis and 27% positive for fungal keratitis. The data set was partitioned into training/validation/test sets using a 70/15/15% split at the subject level. Two notable features differentiate this test case from the trachoma example: (1) this is a multilabel classification task, as polymicrobial infection may occur; and (2) in this case, we use the results of multiple diagnostic tests as the indicator variables for latent supervision rather than expert opinion.

We used this data set to train 2 supervised learning models to perform multilabel classification of bacterial and fungal keratitis: a “smear model” and a “culture model.” For the smear model, the Gram stain result was used to define whether the case was bacterial, and the potassium hydroxide prep result defined the presence or absence of fungal keratitis. For the culture model, the blood agar culture defined the label with respect to bacterial keratitis, and the potato dextrose agar culture result defined the fungal keratitis label. We then used latent supervision to establish probabilistic labels for each image, incorporating information from all 4 diagnostic tests. We trained 3 distinct EfficientNetB7 deep convolutional neural networks: 2 using supervised learning (the smear model and the culture model), and 1 using latent supervision.23 For this test case we used the Adam optimization algorithm.24 As in the first test case, all models were pretrained on ImageNet. We evaluated the models' classification performance and calibration using the same metrics as in Test Case #1. All hyperparameters for model training are enumerated in Supplemental Methods Section 3, and all source code is publicly available at https://github.com/Redd-Cornea-AI/Latent_Supervision.

For bacterial keratitis classification, the latent supervision model and culture-supervised model performed similarly, while the smear-supervised model had worse performance (though this was not statistically significant in the case of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve). The smear-supervised model was also very poorly calibrated, and predicted that every case was negative for bacterial keratitis (hence the F1 score of 0).

With respect to fungal keratitis, the classification performance was essentially reversed compared to the bacterial keratitis results; the smear-supervised model and latent supervision models performed the best (and comparably to each other), while the culture-supervised model demonstrated worse classification performance and calibration. The results of the LCA and Brier score decompositions for both test cases are included in the Supplemental Results.

Discussion

In this paper, we defined the latent supervision algorithm and evaluated it using 2 ophthalmic computer vision test cases. In the case of trachoma detection, the latent supervision model had similar classification performance compared with the ensemble of all supervised models and accomplished this with better model calibration. In the case of infectious keratitis, each supervised learning model performed relatively well for detecting one class of infection but not the other, while the latent supervision model performed well for both classes and did so with relatively good calibration. These results suggest latent supervision may provide a noncomputationally demanding means to improve the calibration and performance of machine learning classification models in ophthalmology.

We frame latent supervision as a form of weak supervision, combining multiple labeling sources to generate a soft label which better reflects the underlying diagnostic uncertainty. One could also conceptualize latent supervision as an ensembling method, though in this case we are ensembling the labels rather than the models. We compared the results of a more traditional ensembling approach to latent supervision in our trachoma test case, which demonstrated similar classification performance between both methods. However, the latent supervision model had better calibration, seemingly because it was less susceptible to outliers (the grader 3 model in this case) compared with the ensemble model. Furthermore, latent supervision confers the substantial advantage of drastically reduced computational requirements during both training and inference compared with model ensembling methods, given that only one model need be trained and deployed and the computational requirements of the unique steps in latent supervision (latent class modeling and posterior probability estimation) are trivial and only calculated once.

When applied to different classes of diagnostic tests in the infectious keratitis test case, the latent supervision model achieved similar performance to the best performing individual models for each class of infectious keratitis. This suggests the potential for improved generalizability compared with individual supervised learning models. This case also demonstrates the flexibility of latent supervision, which is agnostic to model architecture, optimization algorithm, and classification goal (whether binary, multiclass, or multilabel classification). It also enables better utilization of partially incomplete data compared to supervised learning, while still incorporating an appropriate degree of uncertainty based on limited information. For example, a model trained using supervised learning with labels defined according to culture results requires that these results are available; if they are not, the data point must be excluded. In contrast, if any indicator variable is available for a given subject, a posterior probability of disease can be estimated and used as a soft label for model training. This enhanced capability to incorporate incomplete data is particularly useful with medical datasets, where labeling sources for training data are relatively scarce and expensive to obtain.

Latent supervision builds upon decades of foundational work. Dawid and Skene developed the concept of modeling the accuracy of multiple experts in the absence of a gold standard using maximum likelihood estimation.25 Raykar et al26 later adapted this idea to simultaneously learn the labels and train a classifier using expectation-maximization, though unfortunately this approach is not feasible for deep learning models. Latent supervision adapts these techniques to make them suitable for any gradient-based learning paradigm, including training deep neural networks. Furthermore, inclusion of LCA to define the probabilistic labels allows for incorporation of random effects and model selection procedures which have been well established in the statistics literature.27,28 Finally, the incorporation of the rescaled mean squared error loss function produces well-calibrated model predictions that more accurately reflect the diagnostic uncertainty in the training labels. In an alternative approach to weak supervision, Ratner et al29 conducted foundational work by defining a programmatic labeling process as a generative model incorporating large numbers of weak labeling functions. Latent supervision instead leverages a smaller number of relatively strong labeling sources (e.g., expert opinions or diagnostic tests), rather than a large number of weak labeling functions, as the former is more typical with medical data sets. Overall, latent supervision provides a statistically principled, calibration-aware alternative to existing weak supervision methods, particularly well-suited for clinical domains with multiple imperfect diagnostic indicators.

There are limitations to our study. First, our training and evaluation labels were derived from imperfect reference standards, including subjective grader assessments in trachoma and smear/culture results with imperfect sensitivity and specificity in infectious keratitis. Compared with supervised learning, LCA considerably mitigates this bias by pooling multiple noisy labels into probabilistic soft labels that model uncertainty. Still, it does not fully eliminate systematic biases shared across all sources of labels, which may be reflected in the model's performance. Second, our data sets were drawn from a limited set of geographic and clinical settings, and domain-shift testing was not performed. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other populations, imaging devices, and health systems is yet to be evaluated. Finally, LCA itself has several limitations. Most prominent is the core assumption that the observed diagnostic indicators are conditionally independent given the latent class, which may be violated in clinical datasets due to within-class heterogeneity of test performance or residual association (i.e. local dependence) between indicators (e.g. graders or tests). While our usage of a subject-level random effect is intended to mitigate heterogeneity-driven conditional dependence, it may not fully account for indicator-specific residual associations, which could affect the accuracy of the estimated soft labels.27,28 Latent class analysis also requires that a sufficient number of indicator variables are available to satisfy the model's requirements with respect to degrees of freedom. However, as long as some subjects in the dataset have all the required indicator variable results, an LCA model can be developed and posterior probability labels can then be estimated for any case which has ≥1 diagnostic test result.

In conclusion, latent supervision leverages information from multiple sources to generate probabilistic labels for machine learning classification model training. This approach is computationally efficient, enables inclusion of missing or incomplete data in the model development process, and may produce models with better classification performance and calibration compared with supervised learning. Adopting this approach in ophthalmic machine learning model development increases the likelihood of developing better, more explainable models that can be readily incorporated into clinical workflows to improve the quality and accessibility of patient care.
