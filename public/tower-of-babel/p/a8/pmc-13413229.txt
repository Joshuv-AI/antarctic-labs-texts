Left Atrial Reservoir Strain Combined With Left Ventricular Longitudinal Strain Improves Discrimination of HFpEF in Patients With Hypertension
Authors: Jianan Zhang, Yue Lv, Xiuqun Cao, Lin Liu, Zhe Xu, Qingyue Han, Wenjia Wang, Yang Song, Peishi Yan, Xiaolin Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly identification of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) in hypertensive patients remains challenging using conventional parameters alone. This retrospective study aimed to assess whether combining left atrial (LA) reservoir strain (εs) with left ventricular (LV) longitudinal strain improves the discrimination of HFpEF among hypertensive patients. We included 50 hypertensive patients with HFpEF (HTN‐HFpEF), 56 hypertensive patients without HFpEF, and 50 healthy controls. Cardiac magnetic resonance feature tracking (CMR‐FT) was used to measure LV global longitudinal strain (LVGPLS) and strain rates, as well as LA εs, conduit (εe), and booster (εa) strains. Parameters were compared across the three groups. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of HTN‐HFpEF, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to calculate the area under the curve (AUC). From controls to HTN to HTN‐HFpEF, LVGPLS and strain rate showed progressive impairment, while LAVImax and LAVImin progressively increased (all p < 0.001). Compared to the HTN group and healthy controls, the HTN‐HFpEF group demonstrated significant impairment in LA functional and strain parameters (all p < 0.001). Binary logistic regression analyses revealed that LVMI, LAVImin, LAεs, and LVGPLS remained independently associated with HFpEF. LAεs alone showed the highest diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.790), and the combination of LAεs and LVGPLS further improved the AUC to 0.841. In conclusion, CMR‐FT‐derived LA strain demonstrates high discriminative ability for HFpEF in hypertensive patients. The combination of LAεs and LVGPLS offers optimal diagnostic value and may serve as a sensitive non‐invasive biomarker for identifying HFpEF in this population.

Introduction

With population aging and the rising prevalence of metabolic diseases, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) now accounts for approximately 50% of all heart failure cases, with mortality rates comparable to those of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) [1, 2, 3] Hypertension (HTN) is the most common comorbidity, affecting 80%–90% of HFpEF patients [4, 5, 6]. By 2025, the global number of hypertensive patients is projected to reach 1.56 billion [7]. Given this growing burden, timely detection of cardiac impairment and early intervention in hypertensive patients are essential to prevent disease progression. However, distinguishing HTN with or without HFpEF remains challenging using conventional echocardiographic functional parameters alone.

In recent years, Cardiac magnetic resonance‐feature tracking (CMR‐FT) is a technique that analyzes myocardial pixel motion based on cardiac cine sequences, enabling noninvasive and precise quantification of global and segmental myocardial strain [8]. This method is easy to operate, offers higher image resolution and high reproducibility, and overcomes the limitations of conventional echocardiography, including acoustic window restrictions and operator dependence. A series of studies have shown that LV myocardial strain parameters are significantly reduced in HFpEF patients, and changes in myocardial strain precede alterations in LV functional parameters [9, 10, 11]. However, patients with HFpEF often exhibit impaired LV diastolic function, which in turn leads to left atrial (LA) dysfunction. Notably, LA strain strongly correlated with LV end‐diastolic filling pressure [12, 13], providing a reliable indicator of early LA impairment [14, 15]. Li et al. [16] and Liao et al. [17] found that LA strain parameters were impaired in hypertensive patients before LA enlargement, suggesting the presence of early LA dysfunction. Interestingly, Wang et al. [18]found that CMR‐FT‐derived LA strain outperformed LV strain in distinguishing hypertensive heart disease from HFpEF.

Nevertheless, evidence regarding the incremental value of combining LA and LV strain parameters for identifying HFpEF in hypertensive patients remains limited. Therefore, the aims of this study were: (1) to compare differences in LA and LV function and strain parameters between hypertensive patients with and without HFpEF, (2) to evaluate the ability of LA and LV strain parameters to identify HTN‐HFpEF from HTN, and the diagnostic performance of combined indices, and (3) to analyze the correlations among LA, LV strain parameters, and N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP).

Methods

Study Population

This study retrospectively enrolled hospitalized patients with essential HTN between January 2020 and June 2025, who met the diagnostic criteria for HTN established by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines [19]. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki on Human Subjects and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Central Hospital of Dalian University of Technology (approval No. YN2022‐098‐01). Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All subjects underwent medical history collection, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), and CMR imaging. Patients with concomitant diabetes mellitus, other non‐ischemic cardiomyopathies, and myocardial infarction were excluded, as were those with substandard image quality or imaging artifacts. Ultimately, hypertensive patients who met the following criteria were included in the HTN‐HFpEF group: (I) NYHA Class II‐IV, (II) left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 50%; (III) H2FPEF Score [20] ≥ 3, calculated as follows: BMI > 30 kg/m2 is 2 points; use of two or more antihypertensive medications is 1 point; atrial fibrillation is 3 points; echocardiographic estimation of pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) > 35 mmHg is 1 point; age > 60 years is 1 point; E/e’ > 13 is 1 point; (IV) NT‐proBNP > 125 pg/ml. Patients without symptoms or signs of heart failure and with normal NT‐proBNP levels were included in the HTN group. Meanwhile, healthy control subjects with similar age and sex distribution and without clinical, laboratory, or imaging abnormalities were also enrolled. Echocardiography Acquisition

Echocardiographic evaluation was performed using a commercially available system (Vivid E10, GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Horten, Norway). Echocardiographic parameters were measured at rest in standard parasternal long‐axis and apical 2‐chamber and 4‐chamber views, including early diastolic mitral inflow velocity (E), average early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e’), and the E/e ratio.

PASP was estimated by continuous‐wave Doppler echocardiography. The peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV) was measured from the apical four‐chamber view, and the tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient was derived accordingly. Right atrial pressure (RAP) was estimated from the diameter and respiratory variation of the inferior vena cava in the subcostal view. PASP was then calculated by adding the estimated RAP to the tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient.

Cardiac MRI Acquisition and Analysis

All subjects underwent 3.0 T cardiac MRI (Signa Pioneer, GE Healthcare, USA) using a 32‐channel cardiac phased‐array coil, with respiratory gating and electrocardiographic gating. Standard cine images were acquired in the two‐chamber, three‐chamber, four‐chamber, and contiguous short‐axis views using a balanced steady‐state free precession sequence. Imaging parameters included: time repetition (TR), 2.8 ms; time of echo (TE), 1.0 ms; flip angle of 50 °; slice thickness, 8 mm; gap, 2 mm; field of view (FOV), 320 mm × 350 mm; and matrix of 320 mm × 256 mm.

All images were analyzed using dedicated post‐processing software (cvi42, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Canada). Body surface area (BSA) was calculated from height and weight and used for indexation where appropriate.

LV function measurement: LV endocardial and epicardial contours were automatically outlined on the short‐axis cine view and manually corrected as needed to obtain LVEF, left ventricular cardiac output (LVCO), and LV mass index (LVMI).

LA function measurement: LA endocardial and epicardial contours were automatically outlined on the long‐axis two‐chamber and four‐chamber cine view, and manually corrected as needed; the pulmonary veins and left auricle were placed outside the epicardium during the outlining process, and the LA maximum volume (LAVmax), LA minimum volume (LAVmin), LA presystolic volume (LAVpac). The following formula was used [21], LA total ejection fraction, (LAEF Total) = [(Vmax‐Vmin)/Vmax] × 100%, LA passive ejection fraction, (LAEF Passive) = [(Vmax ‐Vpac)/Vmax] × 100%, LA booster ejection fraction, (LAEF booster) = [(Vpac‐Vmin)/Vpac] × 100%. Three temporal ejection fractions of the left atrium were obtained.

LV myocardial strain measurement: short axis, 2‐chamber and 4‐chamber cine views of endocardial and epicardial contours were automatically outlined in LV end‐diastole by strain module of CVI42, and manually corrected as needed, excluding the LV papillary muscles, to obtain left ventricular global peak longitudinal strain (LVGPLS); left ventricular global peak circumferential strain (LVGPCS); left ventricular global peak radial strain (LVGPRS); left ventricular diastolic global peak longitudinal strain rate (LVdGLSR); left ventricular systolic global peak longitudinal strain rate (LVsGLSR); left ventricular diastolic global peak circumferential strain rate (LVdGCSR); left ventricular systolic global peak circumferential strain rate (LVsGCSR); left ventricular diastolic global peak radial strain rate (LVdGRSR); left ventricular systolic global peak radial strain rate (LVsGRSR) (Figure 2).

Data Reproducibility

To assess measurement reproducibility, 30 randomly selected subjects were reanalyzed. Two radiologists (LY and MXL) with 10 years of experience independently measured LA and LV strain parameters to assess inter‐observer reproducibility. MXL repeated these measurements 1 month later to evaluate intra‐observer reproducibility. The inter‐and intra‐class correction coefficient (ICC) was used to assess the intra‐ and inter‐observer variability of the strain parameters.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS (version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism10 were used for statistical and graphical analysis. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or interquartile ranges (IQRs), and count data were expressed as percentage. Normal distribution was analyzed by One‐way ANOVA analysis, Welch's robustness test, LSD and Tamhane's T2 test, and compared the experimental data among the three groups. Nonnormal distribution was analyzed by Kruskal‐Wallis test, and compared the experimental data among the three groups after Bonferroni correction. Correlations were analyzed using Pearson or Spearman correlation. Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of HTN‐HFpEF. Variables with a p value < 0.01 in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate prediction model using the forward conditional method, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated for each regression model to assess multicollinearity. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) for evaluating the diagnostic performance of single and combined parameters. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ICC values were interpreted as follows: an ICC of ≥ 0.7 indicated good consistency, an ICC between 0.3 and < 0.7 denoted moderate consistency, and an ICC of < 0.3 was considered indicative of poor consistency.

Results

Clinical Characteristics

This study included 50 HTN‐HFpEF patients, 56 hypertensive patients, and 50 healthy controls, with no statistically significant differences in age or sex among the groups. The HTN‐HFpEF group exhibited higher levels of NT‐proBNP, with a median of 764 pg/mL (IQR 325–1382 pg/mL). According to the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, a higher proportion of patients in the HTN‐HFpEF group were classified as NYHA class III/IV compared with the HTN group. Similarly, echocardiographic findings showed that a higher percentage of patients in the HTN‐HFpEF group had an E/e’ ratio > 13 compared with the HTN group (Table 1).

Comparison of LV and LA Parameters for Differentiating HTN‐HFpEF From HTN

Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses revealed that LVMI (OR = 1.060, 95% CI: 1.028–1.094, p = 0.001), LAVImin (OR = 1.135, 95% CI: 1.043–1.236, p = 0.003), and LVGPLS (OR = 1.233, 95% CI: 1.119–1.435, p = 0.004) were independent positive predictors of HTN‐HFpEF, whereas LA εs (OR = 0.857, 95% CI: 0.787–0.935, p < 0.001) was a negative predictor. LAEF booster and LA εa were excluded from models due to multicollinearity (VIF > 10). These findings indicate that increased LVMI, LAVImin, and LVGPLS, as well as reduced LAεs, are independently associated with the presence of HFpEF in hypertensive patients (Table 3). ROC curve analysis demonstrated that LAεs alone achieved the highest diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.790, sensitivity of 56.0%, specificity of 96.4%, and a cutoff value of 19.25%. The combination of LAεs and LVGPLS showed improved diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.841, sensitivity of 76.8%, and specificity of 81.4% (Table 4 and Figure 5).

Between the three groups of subjects, LA εs and εe were weakly correlated with NT‐proBNP (εs, r = −0.248; εe, r = −0.288; all p < 0.05); LVGPLS was weakly correlated with NT‐proBNP (r = 0.322, p < 0.05); LA strain parameters were all moderately correlated with LVGPLS (εs, r = −0.645; εe, r = −0.597; εa, r = −0.452, all p < 0.001) (Figure 6).

Data Reproducibility

All strain parameters demonstrated strong intra‐ and inter‐observer consistency, with intra‐observer ICCs ranging from 0.845 to 0.985 and inter‐observer ICCs ranging from 0.817 to 0.964, confirming the high reliability of the measurements.

Discussion

This study evaluated the role of CMR‐FT‐derived LA and LV strain parameters in identifying HFpEF among patients with HTN. The main findings were as follows: first, both LV longitudinal mechanics and LA phasic mechanics progressively worsened from healthy controls to patients with HTN and further to those with HTN‐HFpEF; second, LAεs showed the best discriminatory performance among the individual imaging markers; third, combining LAεs with LVGPLS provided better overall discrimination than either parameter alone; and fourth, LA strain parameters were moderately associated with LVGPLS, supporting close atrioventricular mechanical coupling.

HFpEF represents a heterogeneous clinical syndrome characterized by complex pathophysiology, with HTN emerging as its most prevalent and deleterious comorbidity [22], Although established diagnostic guidelines exist [23], and a handful of CMR‐FT studies have identified subtle functional distinctions between HTN‐HFpEF and HTN alone [24, 25], achieving accurate and non‐invasive diagnosis of HFpEF continues to pose a substantial challenge in routine clinical practice.

In this study, we observed impaired LVGPLS and strain rate in patients with HTN‐HFpEF despite preserved LVEF, which may reflect subendocardial longitudinal dysfunction, indicating subclinical myocardial systolic dysfunction, and this finding is consistent with previous reports [24, 26]. Previous echocardiographic and CMR studies have demonstrated progressive impairment of GPLS from healthy controls to hypertensive patients and further to those with HFpEF [18, 27, 28]. The present study confirms this phenomenon, further supporting GPLS as a sensitive marker of early myocardial systolic dysfunction in HTN. From controls to HTN to HTN‐HFpEF, we found that LAVImax and LAVImin progressively increased, and LAEF total showed progressive impairment, consistent with atrial enlargement and reduced reservoir function under chronic pressure overload. Compared with controls and hypertensive patients, LAEF passive and LAEF booster were significantly reduced in HTN‐HFpEF but unchanged in HTN. However, CMR‐FT‐derived LAεs and εe showed progressive impairment, indicating that LA strain parameters detect LA dysfunction earlier than conventional LA functional parameters, consistent with previous reports [16, 18, 29]. Notably, in our study, LAεa was elevated in the HTN group, possibly reflecting a compensatory mechanism whereby impaired LV diastolic function and elevated filling pressure reduce LA εs and εe, while LA εa is preserved or mildly enhanced to maintain LV filling. Collectively, these findings may offer novel insights into the identification of subtle systolic and diastolic dysfunction in patients with HTN‐HFpEF.

In distinguishing HTN‐HFpEF from HTN, adjusted binary logistic regression and ROC curve analysis demonstrated that LA parameters exhibited better discriminative performance than LV parameters. Specifically, LAεs emerged as an independent predictor with high diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.790), achieving a cutoff value of 19.25%, followed by LAVImin (AUC = 0.776), both outperforming LVGPLS, which is largely consistent with the study by Wang et al. [18].

The pathophysiological basis for LA strain being superior to LV strain in differentiating HFpEF lies in the close anatomical and functional coupling between the LA and LV. In hypertensive patients, impaired LV diastolic function leads to elevated filling pressures, which are retrogradely transmitted to the LA, causing increased LA wall tension and abnormal myocardial deformation. These changes can be detected by LA strain parameters even when LVEF remains normal [25]. However, LA strain alone reflects the downstream consequence of LV diastolic dysfunction and cannot assess the systolic mechanical abnormalities of the LV itself. LV GPLS alone reflects the upstream longitudinal shortening capacity, but its magnitude of change is limited in the setting of preserved LVEF, making it insufficient to fully reflect the load impact imposed by LV diastolic dysfunction on the LA [18, 24]. Interestingly, this study is the first to demonstrate that combining LAεs with LVGPLS further improves diagnostic accuracy, with the AUC increasing to 0.841, sensitivity of 76.8%, and specificity of 81.4%. This suggests that the combined application of atrioventricular strain parameters has greater potential in diagnosing HFpEF and identifying early systolic dysfunction, as their combination can cover the complete pathophysiological continuum from LV functional abnormalities to LA functional impairment. In early HFpEF, LA booster pump function can be compensatorily enhanced via the Frank‐Starling mechanism to maintain LV filling [30], at which point LA strain alone may underestimate dysfunction due to compensatory effects, whereas the addition of LV strain helps to identify this dynamic compensation‐decompensation relationship. The high degree of atrioventricular functional coupling dictates that a single parameter may not fully characterize the complete picture of HFpEF pathophysiology, and combined assessment can provide incremental diagnostic value. From a clinical perspective, this integrated approach provides a practical imaging tool for early identification of HFpEF in hypertensive patients, facilitating timely risk stratification and potentially guiding more individualized therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes. Indeed, we observed a significant correlation between LVGPLS and NT‐proBNP, consistent with the findings of Gao et al. [29]. Alternatively, LA function may undergo compensatory augmentation to sustain left ventricular filling, during which LA strain increases without a concurrent rise in NT‐proBNP levels, reflecting a temporal and spatial mismatch. Moreover, a significant correlation was observed between LA strain parameters and LVGPLS, reflecting the biomechanical coupling between the LA and LV [17, 31].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, this was a single‐center study with a relatively small sample size, which inevitably introduced selection bias. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate the findings. Second, the absence of T1 mapping and extracellular volume fraction (ECV) parameters precludes a definitive assessment of fibrosis‐mediated strain impairment, highlighting the need for integration of these advanced techniques in future studies. Third, as a cross‐sectional investigation, this study lacks longitudinal follow‐up data on strain parameters, limiting the ability to ascertain whether the combined LAεs and LVGPLS parameters predict clinical outcomes. Prospective longitudinal studies are therefore warranted to validate their predictive performance and inform risk stratification and therapeutic strategies in hypertensive patients. Finally, although the combined strain model showed improved discriminatory performance, formal validation and model‐comparison analyses are needed before clinical translation.

Conclusions

CMR‐FT sensitively detects subclinical myocardial dysfunction in HTN‐HFpEF. LA strain alone effectively distinguishes HTN with HFpEF. The combination of LA εs and LVGPLS provides optimal diagnostic performance and may serve as a sensitive biomarker for detecting HFpEF.
