Elucidating biomarkers of carcinogenesis in ulcerative colitis: insight from Mendelian randomization and transcriptomics
Authors: Xiang Xiao, Xuanyu Wu, Tian Liu, Yi Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveUlcerative colitis (UC) is a recognised precursor to colorectal cancer (CRC), yet the underlying relationship to genetic predisposition remains unclear. This study aimed to assess the genetic susceptibility of UC in relation to CRC risk and to identify potential UC carcinogenesis biomarkers.MethodsWe performed a MR study using summary statistics from genome-wide association studies of UC and CRC. Subsequently, we analysed transcriptome data of UC and CRC and conducted a bioinformatics analysis to identify biomarkers of the canceration of UC.ResultsMR analysis revealed a significant increase in CRC risk associated with genetic susceptibility to UC (OR = 5.276, 95% CI = 1.778–15.652, P = 0.003). Subsequent bioinformatics analyses identified BATF and JDP2 as biomarkers of the carcinogenesis of UC. Upregulation of BATF (HR = 1.493, 95% CI = 1.048–2.126, P = 0.027) and JDP2 (HR = 1.443, 95% CI = 1.016–2.051, P = 0.041) correlated with poorer overall survival in CRC. Single-cell RNA-seq analysis revealed that Treg cell in the CRC microenvironment exhibited abnormal levels of BATF expression. Compared with the UC microenvironment, more Treg cells were distributed in the CRC microenvironment. In addition, significant Treg-T cell communication was observed in the CRC microenvironment.ConclusionGenetic susceptibility to UC significantly correlated with an increased risk of CRC. BATF and JDP2 emerged as key biomarkers in the carcinogenesis of UC. Based on abnormal BATF expression at the single-cell level, Treg cell was identified as important cell involved in UC carcinogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-02608-x.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, Colorectal cancer, Mendelian randomization, Bioinformatics, Single-cell RNA-seq, Treg cell

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a malignancy originating in intestinal epithelial cells, ranking third in incidence and second in mortality worldwide [1]. Despite continuous advancements in CRC management encompassing colonoscopy, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunization and targeted therapy, the prognosis for advanced stages is still not optimistic [2]. Emphasis on CRC prevention has emerged as a pivotal approach in current research [3]. The existing consensus highlights CRC as a consequence of prolonged uncontrolled chronic inflammation [4]. Many observational studies have demonstrated inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) as a significant risk factor for CRC [5–8]. IBD encompasses two primary subtypes, namely ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). Studies report that patients with IBD face a 2.7-fold increased risk of developing CRC compared to the general population [9]. Similarly, a recent observational study from Scandinavia reported that the risk of developing CRC for UC was 1.66 times higher than that of the general population, while patients with CD had a 1.40 times higher risk of CRC than the general population [10, 11]. Another study found that patients with UC were 1.92 times more likely to develop CRC [12]. Thus, UC plays a more critical role in the occurrence of CRC than CD. However, these observational studies are insufficient to explain a potential causal association between UC and CRC. Importantly, the biological basis of this vicious evolution remains unclear. Moreover, effective biomarkers for early detection of UC-related cancer remain elusive.

It is worth noting that a large number of preclinical studies have also focused on the association between UC and CRC. In vitro and in vivo studies have shown that the mechanism of UC development into CRC is complex, involving the abnormality of several signalling pathways such as P53, PI3K/AKT, TLR4/NF-kB and COX-2/PG [13]. In addition, UC is an autoimmune disease, and the occurrence of CRC is also closely related to immune imbalance. During UC, immune cells such as neutrophils rapidly overrecruit from peripheral blood to the colon, and their overactivation in the pro-inflammatory UC tissue environment can lead to tissue damage that induces CRC [14, 15].

Mendelian randomization (MR), employing single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) as the instrumental variable (IV), has emerged as a method to explore the causal relationship between diseases and factors such as small molecules, proteins, drug targets and lifestyle habits [16]. A recent MR study found that primary lung cancer can significantly increase the risk of CRC [17]. Similarly, our study aims to elucidate the causal relationship between UC and CRC at the genetic level using MR analysis. Additionally, a series of bioinformatic analyses were also utilised to mine potential biomarkers underlying UC carcinogenesis (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

MR study

Study design

This study pooled publicly available genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data to assess the potential causal relationship between UC, CD, and CRC. MR studies hinge on three essential assumptions: (1) IV must be strongly correlated with exposure factors; (2) IV remains unassociated with any confounding factors linked to exposure; (3) IV solely influences the outcome through exposure factors (Fig. 1). Given the use of publicly accessible data, no further ethical approval or informed consent was required. This study conformed to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) statement [18].

Data source

GWAS summary data for UC, CD, and CRC were retrieved from the MR Base GWAS catalog (http://app.mrbase.org). To reduce bias due to racial heterogeneity, European GWAS data were employed exclusively [19]. UC’s GWAS summary data from ukb-d-ULCERNAS contained 10,366,063 SNPs from 1903 patients with UC and 359,291 healthy individuals. CD’s GWAS summary data from ukb-a-552 contained 10,894,596 SNPs from 337,199 individuals. The CRC's GWAS summary data from ukb-a-297 included 10,894,596 SNPs from 75,920 individuals.

Selection of instrumental variable (IV)

Following the three core assumptions of MR, IVs underwent screening: (1) SNPs closely related to exposure factors were selected based on a significance criterion of P < 5 × 10–6; (2) SNPs with minor allele frequencies (MAF) less than 0.01 were excluded; (3) F statistics were calculated to assess weak IVs using the following formula [20]:

A value of F < 10 indicated the existence of weak IVs, which may underestimate the statistical efficiency; (4) SNPs in linkage disequilibrium were excluded using the European genome in 1000 genomes as a reference, and the criteria was r2 = 0.001, kb = 10,000; (5) Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated through the MR-Egger regression intercept test.

Statistical analysis

Inverse variance weighting (IVW) served as the primary MR analysis method, complemented by MR-Egger, Weighted median, and Weighted mode to assess the robustness of the IVW result. The odds ratio (OR) evaluated the causal relationship between UC and CRC given the binary nature of the results. Subsequently, leave-one-out analysis and Cochrane’s Q test were performed to assess the sensitivity of the results. Additionally, a funnel plot was drawn to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy. Finally, the statistical power of MR results was calculated on the mRnd website [21]. All statistical analyses were performed using the ‘TwoSampleMR’ package in R software (version 4.3.1). P < 0.05 indicated statistically significant results.

Bioinformatics analysis

Data source

The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was used to obtain data for bioinformatics analysis. The datasets were retrieved with the keywords ‘ulcerative colitis’ and ‘colorectal cancer’. The inclusion criteria encompassed colon tissue samples from both patients with UC and patients with CRC, ultimately utilising the GSE3629 dataset, comprising transcriptomic data from 53 patients with UC and 68 patients with CRC. In addition, single-cell RNA data from 3 CRC and UC colon samples were obtained from the GSE231559 (GSM7290763, GSM7290769, GSM7290772) and GSE214695 (GSM6614354, GSM6614355, GSM6614356) datasets, respectively.

Selection and expression analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The ‘Limma’ package screened DEGs using a threshold of P < 0.05 and log2(FC) > 4 or log2(FC) < − 4. The ‘ggplot2’ and ‘pheatmap’ packages were used to draw the volcano map and heatmap of DEGs, respectively. Subsequently, the mapped genes of the SNPs, obtained through the GWAS catalog database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/), were cross-referenced with DEGs using Jvenn for intersection analysis [22]. A non-parametric test compared the expression of genes in patients with UC and CRC.

Functional enrichment and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analyses

Functional enrichment, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses, was performed using the ‘ClusterProfiler’ package. Subsequently, the genes were entered into the STRING database with a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 to map the PPI network [23].

Identification of biomarkers

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.com) provided gene expression and overall survival (OS) data for patients with CRC. All patients were divided into a high- and low-expression group according to the median gene expression. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests compared OS differences between the groups. The P-value and hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) were obtained via log-rank test and univariate Cox regression. Genes that significantly affected the OS of patients with CRC were regarded as node genes for subsequent verification and analysis. Subsequently, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted using the ‘pROC’ software package, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was evaluated to identify biomarkers genes, with AUC > 0.7 as the cutoff [24]. The identified biomarkers underwent gene–gene interaction (GGI) network analysis using the GeneMANIA database to predict interacting genes and their biological functions [25].

Single-cell RNA data analysis

The R 4.3.3 was used to conduct single-cell RNA-seq data analysis. First, the “NormalizeData” and “ScaleData” functions in the Seurat package (v5.0) were used for data normalization. The data dimensionality was then reduced through the “FindVariableFeatures” and “RunPCA” functions. The “RunHarmony” function was then used to remove batch processing effects. Next, the “FindClusters” and “UMAP” functions were utilized for cell clustering and visualization. Then, using CellMarker 2.0 (http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/) database to identify the cells. Since then, the CellChat software package (v1.5.0) was employed for cell-to-cell communication analysis.

Results

MR study revealed the causal association between UC, CD, and CRC

The MR analysis utilised 28 SNPs as IVs, and the F statistics of all SNPs were greater than 40, indicating the absence of weak IVs (Table S1). Notably, IVW results revealed a significant increase in CRC risk associated with genetic susceptibility to UC (OR = 5.276, 95% CI = 1.778–15.652, P = 0.003). Additionally, MR-Egger (OR = 5.084, 95% CI = 0.443–58.36, P = 0.203), Weighted median (OR = 5.501, 95% CI = 1.031–29.351, P = 0.043) and Weighted mode (OR = 6.961, 95% CI = 0.792–61.206, P = 0.115) yielded similar results to IVW (Table 1, Fig. 2A, B). Cochrane’s Q test indicated no heterogeneity among the 28 SNPs (P = 0.633; Table 1). Leave-one-out analysis revealed that the causal relationship between UC and CRC did not change significantly after the removal of each SNP (Fig. 2C). The funnel plot showed a roughly symmetrical distribution of SNPs, indicating no horizontal pleiotropy in the results (Fig. 2D). Comparing with a previous study reporting a HR of 1.92 for CRC in UC, the MR analysis achieved 100% statistical efficiency at α = 0.05 [12].

Bioinformatics analysis

Selection and expression analyses of DEGs

Analysis of the GSE3629 dataset identified 15,199 DEGs, comprising 7,026 upregulated and 8,173 downregulated genes (Fig. 4A, B). In addition, 14 mapped genes of the 28 SNPs used in the MR analysis were obtained from the GWAS Catalog database. Eight genes (HLA-DRB1, DLD, BATF, CCL20, JDP2, SLIT1, IL23R, and SYNGR1) overlapped between DEGs and mapped genes (Fig. 4C). Notably, HLA-DRB1, DLD, BATF, JDP2, SLIT1, and IL23R exhibited significant differential expression in patients with UC and patients with CRC (Fig. 4D).

Functional enrichment and PPI network analyses

GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses were performed to elucidate the potential functions of these six genes. GO analysis highlighted these six genes’ involvement in T-helper cell lineage commitment, alpha–beta T cell lineage commitment, and myeloid leukocyte differentiation. It was also involved in the dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase complex, tricarboxylic acid cycle enzyme complex, MHC class II protein complex, external side of plasma membrane and other cellular components, immune receptor activity, sulfur compound binding, MHC class II receptor activity, oxidoreductase activity, acting on a sulfur group of donors, NAD(P) as acceptor and other molecular functions (Fig. 5A). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed enrichment in IBD and Th17 cell differentiation pathways (Fig. 5B). PPI network analysis displayed a close interaction between HLA-DRB1, IL23R, BATF and JDP2 (Fig. 5C).

BATF and JDP2 were identified as biomarkers of the cocarcinogens of UC

The impact of the aforementioned four genes on the OS of patients with CRC in the TCGA database was assessed using a log-rank test. HLA-DRB1 (HR = 0.936, 95% CI = 0.658–1.334, P = 0.716; Fig. 6A) and IL23R (HR = 0.759, 95% CI = 0.532–1.081, P = 0.126; Fig. 6B) did not exhibit a statistically significant influence on the OS of patients with CRC. Conversely, BATF (HR = 1.493, 95% CI = 1.048–2.126, P = 0.027; Fig. 6C) and JDP2 (HR = 1.443, 95% CI = 1.016–2.051, P = 0.041; Fig. 6D) significantly impacted the OS of patients with CRC. Notably, higher expression levels of these genes were associated with shorter OS. Furthermore, ROC curves demonstrated that BATF (AUC = 1.000) and JDP2 (AUC = 0.986) exhibited a robust discriminatory capacity between CRC and UC (Fig. 6E). GGI analysis investigated the potential mechanism underlying UC carcinogenesis involving BATF and JDP2. The findings revealed that BATF and JDP2 primarily interacted with JUNB, DDIT3, IFI35 and IRF4, which are predominantly associated with the RNA polymerase II transcriptional regulatory complex, transcriptional regulatory complex, IL-4 production regulation, DNA template transcription in stress response and other biological functions (Fig. 6F). However, correlation analysis showed no significant relationship between the expression level of two molecules and clinical features of CRC (Fig. S1).

Uncontrolled Treg-T cells communication was observed in UC carcinogenesis

After pre-processing the single-cell RNA-seq data of CRC and UC by quality control, normalization, and batch correction, we clustered the data and identified 10 different cell clusters. These cell populations were labeled with CellMarker 2.0 and identified as B cell (marker: DERL3, JCHAIN, IGHA1), DCLK1 + progenitor cell (marker: CTLA4, CCR6, TNFRSF4), fibroblast (marker: MUC2, SCNN1A, SPINK4), goblet cell (marker: IGHG4, IGHG1, IGHG3, IGHGP, IGHG2), mast cell (marker: GZMK, CD8B, KLRD1), paneth cell (marker: CSF1R, CD300E, CLEC10A), plasma cell (marker: BANK1), secretory progenitor cell (marker: LUM), T cell (marker: PCLAF, TYMS), and regulatory T (Treg) cell (marker: TPSB2, TPSAB1) (Fig. 7A). Compared with UC, B cell and plasma cell in CRC microenvironment were reduced, while Treg cell and goblet cell were increased (Fig. 7B, C). Notably, Treg cell in the CRC microenvironment demonstrated lower BATF expression (Fig. 7D). The cellchat analysis showed that Treg cells had stronger communication with other cells in the CRC microenvironment, especially with T cells (Fig. 7E–G). This difference was mainly due to the different binding of Treg cell ligands (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, HLA-E, HLA-F, CLEC2B, CLEC2C, CLEC2D, CD55) to T cell receptors (CD8A, CD8B, KLRB1, ADGRE5) (Fig. 7H). In summary, uncontrolled Treg-T cells communication may be an important cause of UC carcinogenesis.

Discussion

As one of the most prevalent malignancies, CRC imposes a substantial economic and societal burden globally. While early-stage CRC boasts a 5-year survival of 90%, late-stage cases have only a 14% survival rate [26], highlighting the imperative of preventive measures. This study focused on the progression of UC to CRC. Utilising two-sample MR analysis, we observed a significant genetic link between UC and increased CRC risk, corroborating prior observational studies [27]. Subsequent bioinformatics analysis identified JDP2 and BATF as potential biomarkers for UC’s transition to CRC.

The MR analysis encompassed 28 SNPs in 14 genes as IVs (CCL20, IL23R, TSBP1-AS1, HLA-DRB1, LINC03004, PIGCP2, SLIT1, LINC01620, SYNGR1, BATF, RPL23AP12, DLD, CCL20, SLIT1, LinC01620, JDP2 and LINC02940). Subsequently, we identified 15,199 DEGs between UC and CRC from the GEO database. Finally, six genes exhibited significant differential expression between UC and CRC (HLA-DRB1, DLD, SLIT1, IL23R, BATF, and JDP2). Based on the initial core hypothesis of MR, these six genes were deemed crucial in the onset and progression of UC, a view that has been substantiated by recent research. Moreover, numerous studies have provided evidence supporting HLA-DRB1 as a genetic susceptibility gene for UC [28–30]. Additionally, another study reported the downregulation of DLD in UC mouse models, suggesting its potential involvement in the immune microenvironment of UC via the copper death pathway [31]. Similarly, IL23R has been identified as the most frequently altered gene in UC, and it is speculated to facilitate the transition from UC to CRC through the PI3K-related inflammatory pathway [32]. A study utilising single-cell transcriptomics demonstrated the active expression of FOXP3+/BATF+ T cells in inflamed UC, thereby confirming the significant involvement of BATF in the progression of UC [33]. Notably, these genes also play a pivotal role in the development of CRC. HLA-DRB1*13:01 allele was previously identified as a potential marker for predicting genetic susceptibility to CRC tumours [34]. Additionally, Mir-653 was reported to enhance the proliferation of CRC cells and suppress apoptosis by negatively regulating DLD expression [35]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that SUV39H2 possesses the capability to interact with the SLIT1 promoter, thereby impeding the transcription of SLIT1 through the facilitation of H3K9 trimethylation [36]. Consequently, this molecular mechanism fosters the proliferation and metastasis of CRC. Furthermore, in vivo studies have revealed the existence of BATF-dependent IL-23R+ CD4+T cells within both murine and human CRC tissues, with their expression being closely associated with the promotion of CRC [37]. Additionally, JDP2, a transcriptional regulator governing cellular growth and cell cycle progression, exhibits a significant inhibitory effect on tumour growth [38]. Furthermore, the insufficiency of UHRF1 acetylation, facilitated by TIP60, has been demonstrated to impact the proliferation of CRC cells by inducing the re-expression of JDP2 [39]. Collectively, these crucial genes exert a fundamental influence on the initiation and progression of UC and CRC.

Functional enrichment analysis highlighted these genes’ involvement in pathways like IBD and Th17 cell differentiation. Th17 cells, which are derived from Th0 cells upon stimulation by IL-6 and IL-23, primarily secrete pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-17 and IL-22, which have been implicated in the pathogenesis of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases [40]. For instance, the significance of Th17 cells in colon cancer associated with colitis (CAC) has been established. Fu et al. reported that IL-17RD contributed to the exacerbation of CAC through its promotion of Th1, Th2 and Th17 differentiation, while simultaneously inhibiting Treg proliferation [41]. Similarly, Lin et al. observed that Cortex periplocae possessed the ability to modulate the intestinal flora in mice by suppressing Th17 cells, thereby impeding the progression of CAC [42]. Furthermore, the protein CTLA-4, recognised for its capacity to downregulate T cells, played a crucial role in the initiation and advancement of both UC and CRC [43, 44]. Notably, a recent study identified IL-17, secreted by Th17 cells, as a reliable biomarker for predicting the presence of anti-CTLA-4 and PD-1, suggesting a potential association between Th17 cells and CTLA-4 [45]. Furthermore, CTLA-4 expression was also upregulated in Th17 cells upon CD28 activation [46]. Additionally, BATF, a biomarker of UC carcinogenesis, was enriched in the Th17 cell lineage commitment. Recent studies have demonstrated the crucial role of BATF in Th17 cell development, specifically in stabilising Th17 development by inhibiting the formation of the STAT5-ETS1-RUNX1 complex [47]. Therefore, we speculated that BATF regulates Th17 cell differentiation by inhibiting the expression of STAT5, thus promoting UC malignant degeneration. Based on abnormal BATF expression at the single-cell level, Treg cell was identified as important cell involved in UC carcinogenesis. Previous studies showed that patients with CRC have increased Treg cells in peripheral blood and tumor tissue [48, 49]. Foxp3 + Treg cell-derived IL-10 has been shown to be an important promoter of lung metastasis in CRC [50]. Another study found that Treg cell-mediated IL6 + neutrophil tumor recruitment was an important pathway for the development of inflammation-driven CRC [51]. In addition, inhibition of Treg cell infiltration has been shown to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [52].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the mechanism of UC malignancy by combining MR studies with bioinformatics analysis, providing a foundation for other malignancy-related studies. However, there are certain limitations to this study. First, the MR analysis only included European population data and hence the causal inference of this study cannot be generalised to other populations. However, the inclusion of a single population avoids bias due to population heterogeneity, thus ensuring the reliability of the MR analysis results. Second, as only publicly available data were used in this study, further verification using in vivo or in vitro studies is required to elucidate the specific biological mechanism of BATF in UC malignant transformation. Nevertheless, these findings showed promising clinical translational value and provided a promising direction for future research, especially in providing promising biomarkers for the prevention and early diagnosis of CRC.

In conclusion, this study underscores that genetic susceptibility to UC increases the risk of CRC development. Notably, BATF and JDP2 emerged as key biomarkers involved in the UC-to-CRC progression. This progression might be closely related to abnormal Th17 cell differentiation, a process mediated by BATF. The treatment of UC could serve as a crucial strategy in preventing CRC.
