The effects of a concerning older adult abilities health education program to promote appropriate decision among acute myocardial infarction patients
Authors: Alin Metprommarat, Samoraphop Banharak, Ladawan Panpanit, Sutin Chanaboon, Surachat Sittipakorn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Older adults have the highest incidence of delayed treatment, complications, and deaths compared to other age groups. Nevertheless, knowledge and understanding about acute myocardial infarction, especially symptoms and perception of calling for 1669 (emergency services), are needed to inform care-seeking decisions during an acute myocardial infarction. This study aimed to explore the effectiveness of a health education program among patients with acute myocardial infarction. A single-blind randomized controlled trial was applied, and one hundred twenty-two hospitalized older patients with acute myocardial infarction were included between June 2022 and February 2023. The intervention group received a health education program and a handbook; the control group received routine care. The study outcomes were measured before and after receiving the intervention. Finally, t-tests, Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-square, Wilcoxon Signed Rank test, and adjusted analysis compared the outcomes. The 115 remaining for data analysis comprised 57 in the control and 58 in the intervention groups. We found that knowledge, perceived susceptibility, benefits of calling 1669, barriers, self-efficacy, cues to action, the possibility of calling 1669, and the first of action when having an acute myocardial infarction were improved. However, two patients in the control group (1455 min to make a decision), while only one in the intervention group (240 min), were readmitted. In conclusion, a health education program improves self-reported knowledge, belief, and decision-making. However, future research should investigate readmission rates and response times further. Finally, educational programs should be tailored to accommodate older adults’ specific needs and limitations on clinical practice and research.Clinical Trial Registration Number: TCTR20220602001 on 02/06/2022

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is caused by coronary artery narrowing or obstruction. The obstruction results in insufficient blood flow to the heart muscle, leading to myocardial infarction and chest pain1. However, most patients, especially older adults, cannot clearly identify the location and characteristics of pain2. Instead, they present with other non-specific symptoms to cardiovascular diseases, known as symptoms other than chest pain. The non-specific symptoms that often confuse older adults about whether they are experiencing symptoms of AMI, causing delays in seeking and/or receiving treatment, include shortness of breath, heavy sweating, and nausea3. Patients who come to the hospital with pain in their backs and tight epigastric pain are 7.2 times more likely to be delayed in receiving treatment than those with chest pain1. These non-specific symptoms cause many patients with AMI to delay visiting the emergency department and receiving treatment1,4, resulting in various complications, such as heart failure, shock, arrhythmia5, right ventricular dysfunction6, and ruptured papillary muscle1,7. These complications can cause sudden death in older adults8,9.

In 2030, the World Health Organization estimated that up to 23 million people would die from stroke and coronary heart disease, accounting for 48% of all fatalities from non-communicable diseases10. In the case of those who survive, high treatment costs and disabled conditions would affect their quality of life1. According to a survey by the American Heart Association (AHA) in 2019, there were approximately 673,000 patients with acute coronary syndrome, of whom 665,000 were patients with AMI, and 8000 cases had unstable angina5. In Thailand, data as of December 1, 2022, revealed that in 2021, there were 21,870 patients with AMI, of whom up to 33.54% died11. It was found that the majority of AMI patients aged 60 years and over and this group had a fatality rate from AMI as high as 130.39 per 100,000 population12. These statistics affirm that AMI is a major global problem, which is commonly found in older adults and causes complicated health problems, economy, and quality of life.

Background

In 2023, Thailand had a total population of 13,064,929 aged 60 years and over, accounting for 20.08% of the total population13. Older adults are at an age where their decision-making is slower than in other age groups because the functioning of the nervous system and brain deteriorates due to the aging process, multipathology, and polypharmacy, as well as symptoms other than chest pain14. This deterioration causes older adults to be confused about whether the symptoms they are experiencing are actual symptoms of AMI or not. Their average time to stop by the emergency department was 66 min, and up to 74.3% made a decision later than 20 minutes15, which delayed them by more than two hours when traveling time was added16. Receiving treatment at least 20 min later will cause ischemia, resulting in heart muscle injury and necrosis or infarction. The longer the delay, the larger the damaged area of the heart muscle (time is muscle), which can lead to irreversible damage. As a result, some decide to seek treatment at the hospital later than they should have, leading to complications and death3,16,17.

According to the literature review, many factors caused older adults with AMI to delay treatment, such as an unfavorable physical condition and inconvenient travel18. Socioeconomic factors included age (older adults), gender (female), marital status (single, divorced, or separated), low level of education, and low income, low level of education, and low income. Environmental factors included the use of personal cars, living alone, the symptom experience not matching expectations, low income, and living in rural areas3. People with a low level of education mostly lack knowledge about AMI, resulting in a delay in the decision to receive treatment19. Patients who know the symptoms of AMI are more likely to go to the hospital 13.7 times faster than those who do not know the symptoms20. Additionally, the area of residence also influenced the older adults’ decision to receive treatment. Most older adults perceived that they were not at risk of AMI, especially older females. Due to their perception, in the case of symptoms other than chest pain, they could not link their non-specific symptoms to AMI, then delay the decision to seek treatment21. However, suppose older patients perceive themselves to be at risk. In that case, they may link their symptoms and risks with heart abnormalities and decide to receive treatment promptly, even if their symptoms do not match their expectations of symptoms of an AMI. If older adults have more knowledge about AMI and perceive that AMI is a serious health problem, they may decide to seek treatment at the hospital more quickly22. Research has also shown that some patients were concerned about travel expenses and the cost of calling emergency medical services. Moreover, some patients did not know the phone number and how to use emergency medical services, resulting in delayed treatment15.

A study was conducted at a heart center within a super-tertiary hospital, which surpasses the level of care provided by standard tertiary hospitals, serving as major regional institutions that offer highly specialized services and often function as national or regional centers of excellence. This hospital examined newly diagnosed patients with AMI, including both ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) cases. Between October 2020 and October 2021, a total of 665 AMI cases were identified, with 344 (51.73%) involving older adults23. Patient history revealed that many older adults presented with non-specific symptoms—such as fatigue, orthopnea, loss of appetite, and dizziness—rather than the classic symptom of chest pain. In several cases, patients delayed seeking medical attention for hours or even more than a day after symptom onset, which subsequently led to the development of complications. Furthermore, most older patients demonstrated limited knowledge regarding AMI risk factors, symptoms, consequences, and appropriate perceptions and responses to acute events caused by AMI.

Based on the Health Belief Model (HBM), people are believed to adapt their behaviors to prevent or reduce the severity of illness if they have an appropriate perception of susceptibility, severity, benefits, and barriers. Moreover, Individuals are more likely to engage in appropriate health behaviors—such as making prompt decisions during an AMI event—if they perceive themselves to be at risk, understand that delayed decision-making can lead to increased severity, recognize AMI as a life-threatening condition, believe that early action leads to better outcomes and recovery, and are aware that calling for ambulance services is both easy and cost-free24. A program developed based on the health beliefs of older adults at risk of coronary heart disease could encourage older adults in the community to have more appropriate health perceptions25. A previous study promoting an appropriate knowledge and perception of AMI reported that the program could promote more knowledge about AMI and find appropriate ways to manage symptoms of AMI26. However, no specific studies were found in older adults and patients who received hospital treatment. There were studies among community-dwelling older adults, mixed age group studies, or those at risk of AMI25–27.

Based on the knowledge gap above, the researcher was therefore interested in developing and studying the effects of a health education program for older adults with concerning abilities to promote knowledge, accurate beliefs, informed decision-making, and timely care-seeking among older patients with AMI who were hospitalized at the heart center of a super-tertiary hospital. The program was developed with careful consideration of age-related limitations among older adults to ensure the intervention is appropriately tailored to this specific population. In addition, the presence of non-specific symptoms—symptoms other than chest pain—must be taken into account when designing interventions. This approach ensures not only that the structure and delivery of the intervention align with the functional capacities of older adults but also that the content is tailored to their specific health conditions and the target disease addressed in this study. The study results will guide nurses and other healthcare professionals in caring for this patient group and provide them with knowledge while they are in the hospital. Moreover, the findings can inform the development of appropriate discharge education, enabling older patients and their caregivers to understand better and recognize the symptoms of AMI. This increased awareness may encourage timely hospital presentation, thereby reducing the risk of delayed treatment and subsequent AMI-related complications or mortality.

Aims

To study the effects of a concerning-older-adult-abilities health education program on knowledge, belief, decision-making, and the time of decision-making when readmission among older patients with AMI.

Hypothesis

Older adults who attended a health education program concerning older adults’ abilities will improve their knowledge, beliefs, decision-making, and decision-making time when readmitted with AMI compared with baseline and those in the control group.

Methods

Design

This study is a single-blinded randomized controlled trial with a two-group pre-posttest design. Before collecting data, the study protocol was registered at Thai Clinical Trial Registration with the clinical research code TCTR20220602001 on 02/06/2022, and it was then conducted and reported strictly according to the registered protocol and CONSORT guidelines.

The sample size calculation indicated that 114 (57 in each group) was enough for this study. However, the researcher adjusted the sample size, which might have been lost by 5%, resulting in a total sample of 122 patients, comprising 61 in the intervention group and 61 in the control group.

The inclusion criteria included: (1) New patients with AMI aged 60 years and over, both male and female, who were admitted to a heart center of a supper tertiary hospital in the northeastern region and were diagnosed with both STEMI and NSTEMI (2) Have the ability to read and write Thai (3) Patients with no memory loss and oriented times and places were included, as assessed by the Thai Mental State Examination (TMSE)29 (4) No problems with hearing, seeing, and speaking (5) No depression assessed by the Thai Geriatric Depression Scale (TGDS)30 and (6) A stable condition defined by the absence of concerning signs and symptoms, including the absence of chest pain or other discomfort, along with typical vital signs. These included blood pressure within the normal range (systolic 90–120 mmHg, diastolic 60–90 mmHg), a respiratory rate of 16–22 breaths per minute, a heart rate of 60–100 beats per minute, and a body temperature between 36.3 and 37.4 °C. Then, the researcher used simple random sampling by drawing lots to determine the allocation of participants to the intervention and control groups, ensuring that all participants had an equal chance of being admitted to either group. The results of the drawing indicated that the control group initially started, followed by the intervention group. Then, the researcher included the participants using systematic random sampling, where every second patient was included. The randomization was continued until 61 patients were obtained in each group. However, seven participants from both groups dropped out during data collection—two due to death and five due to loss of contact. To address this issue, an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was employed. Additionally, dropout rates and reasons for attrition were transparently reported using a CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1), allowing readers to assess the potential risk of bias.Fig. 1CONSORT Flow diagram of the study.

Data collection procedure

After completing the simple random sampling and systematic random sampling, the research assistant measured the pretest outcomes using the research instruments. Then, sixty-one participants in the control group received routine nursing care; however, the other sixty-one participants received the same plus a health education program provided by the principal researcher for four weeks. Finally, the research assistant measured the post-test outcomes after the participants had completed the program. For this study, participants were unaware of their group assignment, as neither the evaluator nor the research assistants were disclosed. The research assistant did not work at the study ward and participated only in the pretest and post-test, arriving when called by the registered nurse at the ward for outcome evaluation.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was submitted and approved by the Office of the Khon Kaen University Ethics Committee in Human Research, with research number HE651034, on April 4, 2022. All methods were performed according to the relevant guidelines and regulations of this committee. The researcher explained the research information and allowed older adults to make independent decisions and have sufficient time before signing the consent form. Older adults could withdraw from the study at any time, and their decision did not impact nursing care and treatment during their hospitalization. While requesting consent, the researcher met with the participants, built relationships by introducing himself, and informed the research objectives. Then, the research assistant obtained consent to participate in the research, as outlined in the consent form. All study participants signed an informed consent form before data collection was started.

Intervention

The health education program was developed based on a systematic review and meta-analysis31, and the review findings were integrated into the Health Belief Model24. Details of the program are as follows: On the first day admitted to the hospital, the research assistant conducted a screening assessment of older adults, evaluating their knowledge, beliefs, and decision-making processes for treatment using an interview and questionnaires (Pretest). The day before discharge, the registered nurse researcher provided a 45-min face-to-face health education program. For improving older adults’ knowledge, belief, and decision-making, health education content included risk factors of AMI, signs and symptoms of AMI, the severity of AMI, benefits of calling 1669 (emergency medical services in Thailand), barriers to receiving treatment, cues to action, and perceived self-efficacy in disease prevention. Following that, a question-and-answer session was held. A handbook was used as a teaching aid and then given to the participants to review at home. This handbook was designed to be specific and suitable for this group of patients, including the use of background and font colors, font sizes, font styles, and words that are appropriate for changes in perception associated with the aging process. The handbook’s content was similar to that of face-to-face health education, which included pictures related to the topics to capture their attention and facilitate a clear understanding of the content. The key message was a sentence warning about symptoms indicating an AMI that older adults must be aware of and urgently seek help from emergency medical services (1669). An example of a key message was such as “When you feel tightness in your chest, please immediately call 1669”, which is a classic symptom of AMI. In addition, this also included other key messages of symptoms other than chest pain of AMI, such as “In case of sweating, tightness in the chest, pain in the arms/neck/shoulders, fainting and losing consciousness, vomiting, nausea, heart palpitations, shortness of breath, and fatigue, please also call 1669.” The symptoms were expressed using the Thai language in a culturally resonant and stylistically poetic manner, with rhyming and phonetically harmonious wording that enhances memorability and ease of pronunciation for older adults. After the activity ended, the researcher gave the posttest questionnaire envelopes to the older patients so they could review them during the telephone interview (no need to return them). At weeks 1, 2, and 3 after discharge, the researcher telephoned to follow up after providing the health education program. The knowledge, beliefs, and decision-making of older adults were reviewed, lasting 30 min on average. After that, older adults were allowed to ask questions and inquire about information. In week 4, the research assistant made a follow-up phone call to assess the participants’ knowledge, beliefs, and decision-making regarding treatment receipt (Posttest). The research assistant read the questions and collected the data by phone. The readmission rate was measured within 28 days after discharge (Fig. 2).Fig. 2The health education program.

The health education program.

Another group received routine nursing care. On the first day of admission, the research assistant conducted a screening assessment of older adults, evaluating their knowledge, beliefs, and decision-making processes for treatment using an interview and questionnaires (Pretest). The day before discharge, the staff provided regular nursing care by the ward’s standard discharge planning procedures. Data analysis

The researcher analyzed the data using SPSS Version 28 software, licensed by the university. Demographic data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including percentages, means, and standard deviations. The comparison of differences in demographic data was analyzed using Chi-square and Fisher exact tests. Knowledge, belief, and decision-making regarding treatment seeking before and after the intervention were analyzed across groups using the Mann–Whitney U test and within groups using the Wilcoxon Signed Rank test and Paired t-test, as some variables observed non-normal distributions. The first action was to call 1669 when experiencing an AMI between groups, which was analyzed using the Chi-square test and within groups using McNemar’s test. Finally, some demographic and baseline data differed between the control and intervention groups, so adjusted analysis was applied using linear and binary logistic regression to explore the actual effectiveness of the program.

Validity, reliability and rigor

The researcher developed this health education program based on a systematic review and meta-analysis, integrating it with the Health Belief Model. Then, five experts from diverse professions, including two cardiologists, two cardiac nurses, and one gerontological nurse instructor, were asked to evaluate content validity and provide recommendations, with a content validity index of 9.8024. After that, the researcher trial piloted the health education program with eight patients with similar characteristics to the study participants in order to test the feasibility of using the program, evaluating the results of the program, summarizing problems, obstacles, and recommendations of the health education program and handbook for older adults with AMI according to the recommendations of the older adults. A feasibility and program difficulty analysis was made, and comments were provided. The research results indicate that the program was practical and effectively improved patients’ knowledge, beliefs, and decision-making. This pilot study was published in Thai33. In addition, the researcher prospectively registered protocol before conducting the study to avoid bias and strictly followed the CONSORT guidelines to elevate the validity and reliability of this trial study. Moreover, two research assistants, both holding master’s degrees in nursing and experienced in data collection and cardiovascular research, collected data this study. They received training tailored to the study’s data collection procedures prior to the study’s commencement. Significantly, the assessors were not involved in administering the intervention and were blinded to group allocation, which helped prevent bias in the outcome assessment process.

Discussion

Older patients receiving a concerning-older-adult-abilities health education program had higher knowledge about the risk factors of AMI and signs and symptoms of AMI after attending the intervention compared to before the intervention and the control group. This study examined the use of a program to promote knowledge and beliefs about health among older adults at risk of heart disease in the community, revealing that the program encouraged older adults to acquire more knowledge and hold appropriate health beliefs about cardiovascular disease25. Its effectiveness can be explained by the fact that this developed health education program provided individuals with face-to-face instruction and used learning materials concerning older adult abilities, such as a handbook for older adults with AMI. The handbook was designed to be specific and suitable for older adults. These enabled older patients to see clearly, memorize the knowledge gained from studying handbooks more easily, and accurately follow the recommendations in the handbook. This technique is consistent with studies on the contrast of letter colors with background colors, which have found that using dark letters on a bright background or bright letters on a dark background makes people with low vision see images most clearly. Spacing the letters would facilitate reading, and using short messages would make remembering more accessible for people with low vision and deteriorated memory34. In addition, this program included a follow-up phone call after knowledge was provided once a week for three consecutive weeks, as studies have shown that the knowledge gained by older adults participating in the program can last only one week35. The follow-up calls monitored and encouraged older adults to be alert and aware. This strategy helped increase the program’s efficiency and ensure the knowledge remains.

Older patients attending the health education program had more perceived benefits of calling 1669, perceived self-efficacy in AMI situations, and perceived cues to action in the event of AMI after the intervention than before the intervention and the control group. However, they had lower perceived barriers to receiving treatment for AMI after attending the intervention than before attending the intervention and in the control group. They had more perceived susceptibility to AMI after attending the intervention than before and in the control group. It can be explained that knowledge and perception are closely linked—one must first know a subject to perceive and become aware of it36. Knowledge provision and reminders are considered an external influence for older patients to increase their perception of AMI. These results were based on the health belief model, which states that components related to internal and external cues to action, advice from other people, illness, and reminders from doctors or staff encourage people to gain an appropriate perception24. This finding also yielded similar results to a study that provided knowledge through video media to patients with AMI before discharge, utilizing the health belief model concept. The study’s results revealed that after providing knowledge, patients perceived more benefits and severity than before the intervention37. However, our study found that older patients receiving the health education program had no difference in the perceived severity of AMI before and after attending the intervention. In this regard, perceived severity is crucial for taking immediate action or making a decision24. Future studies and the implementation of this program should consider adding content and activities to increase perceived severity further.

The decision-making to call 1669 among the experimental group after attending the health education program was higher than before attending and in the control group. It can be explained that when older patients gained knowledge about AMI through individual face-to-face health education, the use of a teaching handbook, and follow-up calls to review their knowledge, they would have a more accurate perception or awareness of AMI and perceive the possible harms of delayed treatment. As a result, the older patients could theoretically manage their symptoms better, calling 1669 when an AMI occurred. This finding consisted of a study on factors affecting decision-making in patients with AMI, which revealed that, in addition to knowledge about the symptoms of AMI, other factors, such as perceived susceptibility and the perceived benefits of action, also promote appropriate action when an AMI situation occurs38. In terms of knowledge, it was found that knowledge about AMI was negatively related to the time of decision-making to receive treatment. In other words, if patients had more knowledge about AMI, it could potentially encourage them to spend less time deciding to receive treatment39. Regarding health perception, a study found that the perceived impact of illness, such as severity and complication, and the perceived self-efficacy to control or manage their illness affected the decision to receive treatment in patients with AMI40.

In the case of the first action, after older patients attended the health education program, we found that the rate of calling 1669 in the event of an AMI occurrence was higher than before attending the intervention compared to the control group. It can be explained that the health education program for older patients in the intervention group caused these older adults to be concerned about the effects of AMI and promote their behaviors in order to prevent harm from AMI. Our findings were based on the Health Belief Model theory, which posits that individuals will exhibit appropriate health behaviors and avoid contracting the disease when they perceive their risk and its potential harm24. In this program, older adults gained knowledge about the risk factors, signs, and symptoms of AMI, which helped them make informed decisions. This finding also consisted of a study that found that using a warning communication program would cause the participants to increasingly choose the symptom management method of calling 166941. Providing knowledge increases a person’s awareness of the symptoms and the importance of seeking treatment in the hospital when symptoms of AMI occur. The time from symptom onset to hospital treatment decreased by approximately 4.7% per year, while the use of ambulance services for transporting patients with AMI symptoms increased by 20%42.

Limitations

This health education program is appropriate for hospitalized older adults with AMI who have stable conditions and can receive individual face-to-face health education. Moreover, the handbook was also used as part of the program as a learning material. Therefore, older adults must be literate. The program may have limitations in applying to other groups of older patients, such as those in critical situations and those who are illiterate. It may be necessary to adjust the pathways or forms of health education to distribute knowledge and key messages to other older patients. Moreover, the study period was relatively short. As a result, there were only three cases of readmission, making the number of readmission cases and the time of decision-making in the AMI situation insufficient for data analysis. Furthermore, it is unknown what the effect will be beyond the 4-week follow-up period. Thus, future studies should be longitudinal, spanning approximately 1–3 years, to increase the possibility of readmission and gather sufficient statistical data to analyze and explore the program’s effectiveness on these two variables.

Conclusion and recommendation

The concerning-older-adult-abilities health education program increased knowledge about risk factors and symptoms of AMI among hospitalized older adults, resulting in higher appropriate health beliefs, perceived self-efficacy, and perceived cues to action; however, it also led to lower perceived barriers. These results affected health behaviors at home; when an AMI occurred, older patients could make appropriate decisions by calling 1669. Therefore, in discharge planning among older adults with AMI, it is recommended that knowledge about AMI be increasingly provided to older adults, and they should be encouraged to be more aware of using emergency medical services or calling 1669 when an emergency occurs at home. Caregivers should also participate in receiving the education provided to older adults, as they are key individuals in providing care and taking part in decision-making in almost all situations. Due to the limitations in older adults’ ability to receive information, they may forget or not recall all the information. Therefore, caregivers play a crucial role in helping individuals remember such information. Moreover, caregivers play a crucial role in the older adult’s decision to receive treatment. Involving caregivers from the time of admission until discharge from the hospital is important to maximizing care effectiveness. In addition, our findings support the implementation of a health education program as part of routine care for older patients with AMI. Moreover, symptoms other than chest pain of AMI in older patients should be promoted among healthcare staff to recognize these non-specific symptom presentations quickly and provide care for older patients with AMI promptly. Additionally, longitudinal studies should be conducted to monitor the persistence of knowledge, beliefs, and decisions in real-life situations, as well as the time elapsed between decision-making and the occurrence of an AMI, to explore whether older adults who receive the program utilize the services when an AMI occurs and to identify any differences in decision-making time.
