Platelet homeostasis and heterogeneity in inflammation and sepsis: mechanisms, biological effects, and clinical potential
Authors: Yiyun Pan, Wenling Zhao, Beibei Yu, Ji Zhang, Mengdi Qu, Ruiwen Jiang, Tingye Xu, Dongliang Li, Hao Zhang, Changhong Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome resulting from a dysregulated host immune response to infection and is frequently accompanied by coagulation abnormalities and multiple organ dysfunction. As central regulators at the intersection of hemostasis, inflammation, and immunity, platelets play a critical role in the initiation and progression of sepsis. Beyond their traditional function in hemostasis, platelets actively participate in immune modulation, inflammatory amplification, and immunothrombosis. This review systematically summarizes the molecular and cellular mechanisms through which platelets contribute to organ injury in sepsis, with particular emphasis on platelet–immune cell interactions. We discuss how these processes drive the development of acute lung injury, myocardial depression, hepatic and renal dysfunction, and disseminated intravascular coagulation. In addition, we examine the dynamic alterations in platelet phenotype and function during sepsis and their clinical implications. Ultimately, we evaluate the therapeutic potential of antiplatelet strategies, addressing current challenges and future directions for translating platelet-targeted interventions into effective treatments for sepsis.

Introduction

Sepsis is a critical clinical syndrome characterized by acute organ dysfunction arising from an abnormal and maladaptive host immune response to an infectious injury [1]. According to the latest epidemiological data, mortality from sepsis in intensive care units varies worldwide, ranging from 20% to 40% [2]. Sepsis can trigger a chaotic and uncontrolled immune response in which normally protective inflammatory reactions become dysregulated. The responses are characterized by extensive pro-inflammatory pathwaysand anti-inflammatory pathways (immunosuppression and cellular exhaustion), ultimately resulting in widespread tissue injury, vascular dysfunction, and organ failure [3, 4]. In sepsis, excessive thrombin generation, inflammatory alterations in the microenvironment, and endothelial injury collectively initiate platelet activation. Thrombin (factor IIa), a serine protease generated from its inactive precursor prothrombin, not only converts fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin but also acts as a potent mediator of platelet activation. Specifically, thrombin activates platelets by cleaving the G protein-coupled protease-activated receptors (PARs) 1 and 4 on the platelet surface, thereby promoting platelet signaling, granule secretion, and aggregation [5]. Through a series of positive feedback reactions, this process not only drives systemic coagulation but also amplifies systemic inflammation via crosstalk with immune and vascular cells. Ultimately, these derangements contribute to coagulation abnormalities, multi-organ failure, and death in sepsis [6, 7].

Platelet dysfunction is common in sepsis [7]. Platelets are highly active blood cells that play essential roles in adhesion and aggregation and are critical to hemostasis [8]. In addition, due to their pathophysiological functions, platelets can influence disease progression in the context of inflammation, immune responses, tissue regeneration, angiogenesis, and cancer [9, 10]. Platelet dysfunction encompasses pathogen- or inflammation-induced platelet hyperactivation, platelet hyperresponsiveness in the late stages of sepsis, and thrombocytopenia (TCP). Excessively activated platelets release platelet granules containing large amounts of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and other proteins are also translocated to the platelet membrane. Platelet activation also promotes the translation of intracellular mRNA and pre-mRNA, thereby enhancing vascular permeability, thrombosis, and inflammation [11, 12]. Dysfunctional platelets markedly aggravate inflammation, coagulopathy, and adverse outcomes; therefore, platelets are considered an important therapeutic target [13, 14].

This review aims to summarize the multilayered impairments of platelet function in sepsis, with a particular focus on their interactions with immune cells, including communication with neutrophils, monocytes, and endothelial cells via adhesion molecules, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles, thereby promoting immunothrombosis. Secondly, we will review the mechanisms and clinical significance of TCP in sepsis. In addition, the role of platelets in sepsis-related organ dysfunction will be briefly discussed, particularly microthrombosis and injury of the lung, kidney, heart, and liver. This review concludes by systematically assessing current and novel therapeutic approaches, with particular emphasis on strategies that selectively target critical platelet-derived mediators and their potential to enhance clinical outcomes.

Overview of platelet

Platelet structure

Platelets are anucleated blood cells with a discoid shape, slightly convex on both sides, and a diameter of 2–5 μm. The study of platelets began in 1842 when Donné observed “tiny particles” in the blood. In 1882, Bizzozero officially named platelets and established them as the third type of blood cell, distinct from erythrocytes and leukocytes, and revealed their core role in thrombosis formation [15]. In the latter half of the 20th century, adhesion receptors Glycoprotein Ib-IX-V complex (GPIb/IX/V) and Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GPIIb/IIIa, αIIbβ3) as well as their release reactions were clarified, and the mechanism of aspirin’s action was established. In the early 21st century, the discovery of Toll-like receptors and CD40L redefined platelets as components of the immune system (Fig. 1) [16].

Platelets possess a bilayer membrane structure enriched in membrane proteins, which consists of a phospholipid bilayer containing membrane glycoproteins that interact with various ligands, including soluble ligands that activate platelets or immobilized ligands present in the vessel wall or other cells. Platelets thereby mediate functions including coagulation, inflammation, antimicrobial defense, tissue regeneration, and metastasis [17]. Beneath the lipid bilayer lies a membrane skeleton attached to the cytoplasmic side of the platelet plasma membrane, which plays an important role in platelet shape change and in the translocation of receptors and granules to the surface. Inside the platelet, there are organelles such as mitochondria, lysosomes, glycogen granules, and storage granules (Fig. 2) [18].

Platelets are equipped with a wide array of adhesion-related molecules that are distributed on their surface membranes as well as stored within intracellular granules, which participate in hemostasis, thrombosis, and cell–cell interactions, including GPIb/IX/V, GP IIb/IIIa, and glycoprotein VI (GPVI). GPIb/IX/V is an abundant membrane receptor complex that serves as the major platelet receptor for von Willebrand factor (vWF). The GPIb/IX/V complex is composed of four transmembrane proteins, including the GP1bα, GPIbβ, GPIX, and GPV subunits [19]. GPIbα has been reported to bind to a wide range of ligands, including thrombospondin, P-selectin, and thrombin [19, 20]. Binding to vWF initiates signaling events that enhance the affinity of platelet integrin GPIIb/IIIa (αIIbβ3), promoting its binding to vWF or fibrinogen and further mediating platelet aggregation [21, 22]. The activation of αIIbβ3 through inside-out signaling in platelets can be initiated by multiple soluble stimuli, including epinephrine, adenosine diphosphate, thromboxane A₂ (TXA2), and thrombin [23, 24]. When platelet receptors such as GPIb/IX/V or GPVI are engaged by their ligands, Src family kinases are activated, promoting talin recruitment to the integrin β3 cytoplasmic tail and driving structural rearrangements that enhance the affinity of the αIIbβ3 extracellular domain [25, 26]. Outside-in signaling via integrin αIIbβ3 is initiated upon fibrinogen binding, and additional interactions with the γ-chain KQAGDV motif of fibrinogen facilitate platelet crosslink, mediating irreversible stable adhesion and aggregation and thereby promoting thrombus growth [27, 28]. Platelet GPVI is expressed both on the platelet plasma membrane and in α-granules [29]. GPVI can be activated by collagen, leading to phosphorylation of immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) and subsequent phosphorylation and activation of PLCγ2, thereby promoting stable platelet adhesion and spreading [30]. Current evidence suggests that inhibition of GPVI–collagen interactions may represent a potential antithrombotic strategy [31, 32].

Platelet granules were first observed in the late 19th century and include dense granules, α-granules and lysosomes [32]. α-Granules are unique to platelets, with approximately 50–80 present in each platelet, making them the most abundant granule type. α-Granules contain proteins synthesized by megakaryocytes and processed through the rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi complex before vesicular packaging, including thrombospondin and P-selectin, as well as proteins acquired from plasma through endocytosis, such as fibrinogen and the majority of platelet-derived coagulation factor V (FV) [33–35]. Platelet reorganization and α-granule secretion are essential for coagulation [36]. Platelet dense granules (DGs) are the smallest granules (~ 150 nm), appear as electron-dense bodies under electron microscopy, and number approximately 3–8 per platelet, containing calcium, adenosine monophosphate, and serotonin. Upon platelet activation, DGs release multiple agonists, including adenosine diphosphate (ADP), thereby initiating platelet activation [37]. DGs contain high concentrations of calcium ions that are released upon platelet activation, further enhancing platelet activation and aggregation and contributing to hemostasis [38]. Lysosomes harbor heavily glycosylated membrane components, including lysosome-associated membrane proteins (LAMPs), which protect the enclosed hydrolytic enzymes by forming a stabilizing membrane barrier. These hydrolytic enzymes act on platelets captured during thrombus formation and may ultimately contribute to remodeling of the injury site [39].

Source-related heterogeneity

Platelets are generated through the fragmentation of membrane extensions from megakaryocytes (Mks), which originate from bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) that undergo lineage-directed proliferation and differentiation toward the megakaryocytic lineage, ultimately maturing into large polyploid cells. Platelets are subsequently released into the circulation [40]. Multipotent HSCs in adult bone marrow possess robust self-renewal capacity and are capable of replenishing all myeloid and lymphoid blood cells. Considerable heterogeneity exists among HSCs, among them, vWF⁺ HSCs represent a thrombopoietin (THPO)-dependent HSC subset capable of short-term platelet-biased reconstitution [41]. Studies indicated that differences in HSC-to-Mks differentiation pathways may contribute to platelet heterogeneity. On the one hand, Flk2/Flt3⁻ HSCs generate megakaryocytes through the MPP2 intermediate, ultimately producing circulating platelets [42, 43]. On the other hand, the establishment of the Flk/Switch mouse model demonstrated that HSCs can generate platelets via a non-Flk pathway. Megakaryopoiesis through the non-Flk pathway constitutes a rapid differentiation route that is typically activated when bone marrow megakaryocyte numbers are reduced or growth is suppressed. Although platelets generated through this pathway lack lineage complexity, it enhances platelet replenishment [42–44]. By contrast, when platelets are selectively depleted, the relative contributions of the Flt3⁻ and Flt3⁺ pathways are not significantly altered [45]. In mice models, non-Flk–mediated platelet production increases with aging, and platelets generated through this pathway cause thrombocytosis under steady-state conditions and exhibit hyper-responsiveness to platelet agonists, thereby exacerbating injury-induced thrombosis [46].

Megakaryocytes reside in the bone marrow, lung, and spleen, and their anatomical location contributes to the generation of diverse platelet populations. In septic conditions, the spleen can function as an expanded site for megakaryopoiesis and platelet generation. Using a murine cecal slurry model, investigators showed that bipotent megakaryocyte–erythroid progenitors relocate from the bone marrow to the spleen, where they mature into megakaryocytes that produce CD40L^high platelets. Platelets derived from splenic megakaryocytes exhibit high levels of CD40 ligand (CD40L) expression, thereby enhancing immune stimulation and driving the generation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [47].

It is estimated that approximately 7–50% of platelets originate from the lung. CFSE labeled lung megakaryocyte–derived platelets showed that under steady-state conditions, approximately 5–10% of circulating platelets originate from lung-resident megakaryocytes [48, 49]. Single-cell RNA sequencing of myeloid-enriched cells from bone marrow and lung revealed that lung megakaryocytes (MkL) exhibit gene expression profiles resembling antigen-presenting cells, including expression of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1), and C-C chemokine receptor type 7 (CCR7). A long-lived subset of lung megakaryocytes derived from HSCs via an Flt3-independent pathway boosts platelet production during acute and infection-related TCP [49, 50]. In addition, researchers pointed out that the pulmonary microenvironment dictates megakaryocyte immune differentiation and thereby drives platelet heterogeneity. Lung is continuously exposed to pathogens and secretes cytokines such as IL-33, which promote the immune differentiation of MkL. Bone marrow–derived megakaryocytes that migrate into the lung acquire the immune phenotype of lung megakaryocytes (Fig. 3) [48].

Platelet role in hemostasis

Hemostasis refers to the physiological suppression of bleeding at sites of vascular injury. CLEC-2–GPVI and vWF–GPIb-IX interactions allow platelets to bind to collagen, thereby activating platelets. Activated receptors, including P2Y12, P2Y1, and PARs, stimulate intracellular signaling cascades to promote αIIbβ3 activation. It ultimately leads to the formation of a primary platelet plug. Beyond serving as the primary structural component of the initial platelet plug, they act as both a critical catalytic platform and a regulatory reservoir during the coagulation cascade. Upon activation, platelets undergo a loss of membrane phospholipid asymmetry, resulting in the externalization of phosphatidylserine (PS) to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane. This creates a negatively charged catalytic surface that serves as a crucial platform for the assembly of the tenase and prothrombinase complexes. This localization dramatically accelerates thrombin generation and restricts fibrin clot formation precisely to the site of vascular injury [51]. Platelet α-granules contain approximately 20% of the body’s FV. Upon platelet activation, this pool of FV is rapidly released and converted to FVa locally, sustaining efficient prothrombinase complex assembly even in settings of systemic plasma FV depletion [52]. Concurrently, platelets store and secrete tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI). Once exposed on the activated platelet surface or released into the microenvironment, TFPI inhibits the TF-FVIIa complex, preventing the over-amplification of the coagulation cascade. Subsequently, fibrin recruits additional platelets and ultimately forms a secondary platelet clot [53, 54].

In addition to responding to injury, platelets also participate in maintaining vascular integrity under homeostatic conditions. Platelets are recruited individually to protect the vascular barrier, preventing vascular leakage through hemostasis without forming clots. Under inflammatory conditions, platelets support leukocyte recruitment to sites of inflammation while simultaneously preventing hemorrhage caused by leukocyte transmigration, thereby contributing to the integrity of inflamed vessels. Platelets are capable of homing to regions of neutrophil transmigration by detecting local gradients of fibrinogen deposited within the tissue [55]. Activation of the actin–myosin cytoskeleton and GPIIbIIIa-mediated Gα13/c-Src/14-3-3ζ signaling cascades endows platelets with autonomous migratory capacity [56]. ABL/c-Src inhibitor dasatinib, which is used clinically, primarily interferes with platelet migration without markedly impairing classical platelet functions, providing a novel mechanism underlying dasatinib-associated platelet dysfunction and bleeding [31, 32]. Even in healthy individuals, microscopic gaps may exist within the vasculature. It has been reported that severe TCP causes reversible leakage of 40-kDa dextran from capillaries but does not affect the leakage of 70-kDa albumin, indicating that platelets are able to maintain vascular integrity under physiological conditions [33]. Gupta et al. reported that this function is mediated through interactions between platelets and GPVI ligands, followed by PLCγ2 signaling and dense-granule release. This interaction induces transient platelet activation accompanied by limited dense-granule secretion, and the release of angiogenic cytokines and growth factors, thereby positively supporting endothelial junction integrity. However, in sepsis conditions and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), activated platelets have been shown to increase permeability in the systemic and pulmonary circulation. Moreover, during the early stages of ARDS, activated platelets have been demonstrated to enhance leakage of protein-rich fluid, leukocytes, and erythrocytes, leading to the pathological features of diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) [57].

Overview of platelet subpopulations

After activation, platelets will differentiate into multiple functional subtypes. The microenvironment plays a critical role in the behavior of platelets in both homeostasis and disease states: a broad range of stimuli, including coagulation factors, cytokines, pathogens, and immune cells, can convert platelets to different activation states. Among these phenotypes, aggregative platelets, procoagulant platelets and COAT platelets are key examples, which mediate the regulatory roles of platelets in both physiological balance and pathological conditions (Fig. 4) [58].

Aggregating platelets

High-resolution two-photon fluorescence microscopy revealed that platelets within thrombi formed under flow conditions exhibit pronounced functional compartmentalization, in which fibrin-binding platelets aggregate into discrete clusters, whereas non-aggregated platelet regions display PS exposure [59]. Single-cell analysis using mass cytometry further demonstrated this distinction by defining the former as aggregatory platelets characterized by active surface integrin αIIbβ3, absence of PS expression, and preserved membrane integrity, which clearly distinguishes them from procoagulant platelets and enables thrombus consolidation through clot retraction [60]. The formation of aggregatory platelets is tightly associated with αIIbβ3 activation. Signaling mediated by GPVI or PAR1/PAR4 receptors triggers Ca²⁺ influx and protein kinase C activation via PLCβ and PLCγ pathways, thereby driving the Rap1b–Talin1–Kindlin-3 complex to convert αIIbβ3 to a high-affinity conformation. Under venous and intermediate arterial shear conditions, aggregating platelets promote fibrinogen binding and platelet–platelet cohesion, forming stable platelet aggregates that constitute the core of the thrombus and give rise to the primary hemostatic plug. The aggregation response is further strengthened by the release of agonists such as ADP, TXA₂, and serotonin, which activate neighboring platelets and enhance integrin-mediated aggregation [61]. TXA₂ synthesis and secretion not only induce vasoconstriction but also cooperate with ADP on the P2Y₁₂ receptor to maintain aggregation stability, thereby amplifying platelet aggregation. Early studies identified P-selectin as an essential molecule for maintaining platelet aggregate stability. Unlike interactions with P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) or GPIb, P-selectin–ligand interactions stabilize the initial GPIIb/IIIa–fibrinogen binding, allowing the formation of extensive and robust platelet assemblies, and P-selectin density has been reported to correlate with aggregate degree. Functional inhibition of P-selectin significantly reduces both the number and size of platelet aggregates [62]. Beyond enhancing adhesion, this platelet phenotype favors crosstalk with monocytes, suggesting its contributory role in linking thrombosis with inflammatory processes.

Defects in signaling cascades that drive platelet aggregation and contraction result in bleeding disorders. Bernard–Soulier syndrome is characterized by functional defects in the GPIb receptor, leading to defective platelet attachment to the vascular surface, whereas Glanzmann thrombasthenia arises from deficiency or dysfunction of integrin αIIbβ3, which prevents effective platelet aggregation [61]. During sepsis, surface expression of P-selectin and GPIIbIIIa on platelets increases following trauma. However, agonist-induced further upregulation of receptor expression is impaired. In addition, platelet adhesion to collagen is reduced in patients compared with healthy controls [63]. Platelet aggregatory function is decreased in sepsis compared with uninfected controls. Reduced responsiveness to agonist-induced aggregation has been observed within the first 48 h after intensive care unit admission, and this impairment persists for an extended period. Dysfunctional platelet mitochondrial activity may contribute to defective aggregation and is associated with sepsis severity [64, 65].

Procoagulant platelets

Procoagulant platelets are defined as platelets exhibiting extremely high levels of surface PS, which facilitates the formation of the prothrombinase enzymatic complex on platelet membranes [66]. P-selectin expression helps distinguish these platelets from dead platelets. In vitro, generation of procoagulant platelets requires synergistic stimulation by thrombin and collagen receptors, whereas in vivo, a single stimulus such as platelet–collagen interaction or shear stress is sufficient to induce their formation. These platelets undergo αIIbβ3 inactivation accompanied by supramaximal calcium influx and mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, leading to balloon-like morphological changes. Proteolysis of cytoskeletal proteins further promotes microvesiculation, which is another hallmark of procoagulant platelet activation. The transmembrane protein TMEM16F translocates PS from the inner to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, resulting in abundant PS exposure on the surface of procoagulant platelets [67]. PS exposure provides a platform for assembly of coagulation factors Va, VIIIa, IXa, and Xa, markedly enhancing thrombin generation. By serving as scaffolds for coagulation factor complexes, procoagulant platelets functionally act as cellular bridges between primary and secondary hemostasis. During the early phase of vascular damage, procoagulant platelets act in concert with aggregating platelets to initiate procoagulant responses via integrin αIIbβ3 (GPIIbIIIa)/Gα13- outside-in signaling and GPVI-dependent pathways both in vitro and in vivo [67, 68]. Theses pathways are known to exert proinflammatory effects and release inflammatory mediators such as inorganic polyphosphate and microparticles, which are associated with arterial and venous thrombosis, inflammation, and prevention of bleeding [69–71]. Low levels of procoagulant platelets are associated with platelet dysfunction and bleeding tendency. For example, the Scott syndrome represents the first clinical bleeding disorder linked to defective platelet procoagulant activity [72]. Conversely, excessive formation and abnormal activation of procoagulant platelets are also associated with clinical events. Oxidative stress lowers the calcium threshold required for procoagulant platelet formation by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, and hyperglycemia increases procoagulant platelet generation while enhancing platelet–neutrophil interactions. A study reported that hyperglycemia exacerbates ischemic stroke–induced brain injury, blood–brain barrier disruption, and deterioration of neurological and motor functions [73]. Similarly, hyperglycemia-induced metabolic reprogramming of vascular endothelial cells promotes platelet hyperreactivity and arterial thrombosis, with endothelial YAP acting as a key regulator by activating the YAP/PGE₂/EP3 cascade [74]. Researchers also reported that dyslipidemia represents a risk factor for clinically significant thrombotic events. The platelet receptor CD36 enhances platelet reactivity by recognizing circulating oxidized lipids and promotes thrombosis by activating redox-sensitive signaling molecules [75]. In sepsis, disruption of vascular integrity, increased permeability, enhanced tissue factor exposure, and elevated vWF release drive platelet transition toward a procoagulant state. Platelet consumption within these thrombi may contribute to the TCP observed in sepsis. Meanwhile, CD40L released from activated platelets further stimulates endothelial activation, creating a self-perpetuating vicious cycle [63].

COAT platelets

Dale et al. described COAT platelets as a platelet subpopulation generated by combined stimulation with collagen and thrombin, capable of binding secreted α-granule proteins, including factor V, fibrinogen, fibronectin, and von Willebrand factor in a transglutaminase-dependent manner [72, 76, 77]. The α-granule proteins become immobilized on the platelet surface, providing a procoagulant surface that supports further fibrin generation. Serotonin can be covalently attached to platelet- or plasma-derived procoagulant proteins by tissue transglutaminase and FXIIIa, and this process enhances the procoagulant phenotype of COAT platelets [78]. In contrast, procoagulant platelets do not express α-granule proteins on their surface. The formation of COAT platelets involves a series of events, including early activation of integrin αIIbβ3, thrombin signaling, and the action of transglutaminases such as factor XIII [79, 80]. Functionally, these platelets preferentially bind factor Xa (FXa), maintain platelet-derived FV activity, and enhance prothrombinase activity, making them key contributors to stable clot formation. Evidence suggests that infections, including sepsis, can act as acute triggers for stroke, elevating risk within a relatively short timeframe. Furthermore, the post-sepsis risk of stroke is notably higher among younger patients. Interestingly, COAT platelets play a dual role in this disease [81]. A study reported that younger platelets are more likely to transform to COAT platelets and strongly supports thrombin-generating activity [82]. Measurement of COAT platelet levels in 271 patients with non-lacunar stroke showed that lower COAT platelet levels were associated with severe bleeding complications after acute stroke, with an optimal cutoff of 36.5%, and bleeding complications occurred in 0.6% of patients with COAT platelets ≥ 36.5% versus 19% in those with COAT platelets < 36.5%. These findings provide evidence that COAT platelets are a potential risk-stratification biomarker in stroke [83]. Higher levels of COAT platelets have been associated with transient ischemic attack (TIA) and early stroke recurrence in patients with large-artery stroke [82, 84, 85]. However, COAT platelets cannot bind to each other and can only be recruited into forming aggregates through aggregating platelets. Researchers found that COAT platelets play a critical role in balancing clot formation. This subpopulation further modulates coagulation by providing a platform for TFPI, a key physiological inhibitor of tissue factor that prevents occlusive thrombus formation [86]. In platelet aggregation assays, COAT platelets partially inhibit platelet aggregation [87].

Some studies suggest that platelet functional status is closely associated with the occurrence and progression of neurological diseases. In non-lacunar ischemic stroke, elevated proportions of COAT platelets correlated with poorer performance on cognitive screening tests three months following stroke. During sepsis, the spleen may serve as an expanded site for megakaryopoiesis and platelet production. Previous studies have suggested that COAT platelets may serve as a peripheral biomarker for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) to predict progression from mild cognitive impairment to AD [88]. A prospective study involving 74 patients with amnestic mild cognitive impairment demonstrated that increased COAT platelet levels predicted a greater likelihood of conversion to AD over a median 24-month follow-up, potentially indicating early amyloid-associated or inflammatory alterations [89].

Platelet in tissue repair and regeneration

Platelets are among the first responders following tissue injury, and evidence from models of liver, skeletal muscle, and skin injury indicates that platelet depletion impairs regenerative processes, whereas platelet-rich plasma promotes structural recovery [90, 91]. Activated platelets release a variety of chemokines, including CTAP-III, CXCL7, platelet factor 4 (PF4), and RANTES, which regulate the recruitment, proliferation, and activation of adult stem cells, fibroblasts, neutrophils, and monocytes [92]. Together with signals emanating from injured tissues, platelets can stimulate the regenerative activity of adult stem cells. Platelet-derived growth factors HGF and SDF-1 are upregulated after myocardial ischemia and recruit mesenchymal stem cells and bone marrow–derived progenitor cells to the heart, respectively, thereby enhancing cardioprotection [93]. Neutrophils recruited by platelet-derived chemokines CXCL5 and CXCL7 clear necrotic muscle fibers and secrete pro-regenerative signaling factors, whereas impairment of this platelet-derived chemokine signaling hinders this process [90].

When activated, platelets secrete multiple growth factors from α-granules, including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), TGF-β, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1), thereby promoting tissue repair, with platelet-derived VEGF playing a critical role in skeletal muscle proliferation and regeneration. Platelet-derived PDGF signaling facilitates communication between the endoderm and myocardium, guiding cardiomyocyte migration toward the midline during cardiac fusion. The platelet-derived Npas4l–Bmp6 signaling pathway participates in regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation during heart regeneration. Other platelet-secreted proteins, such as Hbegfb, Vegfc, and Serpine1, may also contribute to cardiac regeneration [94]. In sepsis, VEGF exhibits a dual role, as it is associated with angiogenesis but at high concentrations promotes vascular permeability [95].

Angiogenesis plays a pivotal role in tissue repair. Both stimulatory molecules and inhibitory molecules are present in platelets, and the balance between them promotes angiogenesis in injured tissues requiring increased perfusion. Once the requirements for vascular growth are met, the production of growth factors decreases and angiogenesis inhibitors increase, thereby suppressing further angiogenesis [96]. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) contains a high concentration of autologous platelets and has therefore become an important therapeutic approach for promoting healing [97].

Platelets as immune sentinels

Beyond their established role in hemostasis, platelets play critical roles in regulating acute and chronic inflammation and in enhancing anti-pathogen defense. Resting platelets can be induced by a variety of immune stimuli, including immune complexes, collagen, ADP/ATP, reactive oxygen species, and activation of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome through Fc receptors (FcRs) or Toll-like receptors (TLRs). Activated platelets are capable of releasing multiple immunostimulatory mediators and possess antigen-presenting capacity, thereby forming complex interaction networks with diverse immune cell types [98, 99].

FcγRIIA is an activating Fc receptor for the constant region (Fc) of immunoglobulin (Ig), exhibiting low affinity for monomeric IgG but high affinity for IgG-containing immune complexes. Although FcγRIIA is widely expressed in humans, the FcγRIIA gene is absent from the murine genome [100]. Consequently, transgenic mice expressing human FcγRIIA have been generated to facilitate in vivo studies of this receptor. The number of FcγRIIA receptors on human platelets has been reported to range from 900 to 5,000 across studies, while intrapersonal expression levels may vary during disease states. Upon binding immune complexes, FcγRIIA is activated through receptor crosslinking, enabling recruitment of Src family kinases (SFKs) and subsequent phosphorylation of tyrosine residues within the ITAM domain. Functionally, this pathway drives granule exocytosis, arachidonic acid metabolism, integrin αIIbβ3 activation, platelet aggregation, PS externalization, and extracellular vesicle release [101–103]. H1N1 influenza virus can induce platelet activation via IgG–FcγRIIA interactions, leading to the generation of inflammatory lipid mediators such as 12-HETE and TXA₂, as well as platelet-derived microparticles. In FcγRIIA transgenic mice, virus-induced TCP can be partially recapitulated following viral infection, whereas wild-type mice lacking this receptor show no such response, suggesting a role for FcγRIIA in post-viral TCP [101]. In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), immune complex stimulation via platelet FcγRIIA induces the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Subsequently, released mitochondria and mtDNA are highly immunogenic structures that stimulate immune cells to produce type I interferons and can also deposit in the kidney in association with immune complexes, thereby triggering or exacerbating lupus nephritis [104]. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIC) is an immune-mediated complication of heparin therapy caused by antibodies directed against complexes of PF4 and heparin. Surface expression of FcγRIIA on platelets is increased during the acute phase of HIC and correlates with disease severity [105]. FcγRIIA binds anti-PF4/heparin immune complexes and, in conjunction with GPIIbIIIa, triggers Src/Syk-mediated platelet activation that culminates in thrombus formation [106]. Platelets possess intrinsic bactericidal activity, which is partly attributed to the recognition of phagocytosed bacteria by FcγRIIA and the subsequent activation of platelets. Through this pathway, platelets can eliminate trapped bacteria either via FcγRIIA-dependent uptake of bacteria or through the release of antimicrobial peptides [107].

Platelets express all Toll-like receptors at the protein level and therefore represent the most widely distributed innate immune sensors in the circulation [108]. Platelets predominantly express TLR4 as a homodimer, which can be activated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). Activation of TLR4 induces P-selectin exposure and mediates leukocyte rolling on stimulated endothelial cells and heterotypic platelet–neutrophil interactions, without triggering classical platelet aggregation [109]. This process has traditionally been attributed to signaling through the MyD88–PKG–cGMP axis; however, platelet-specific deletion of Myd88 does not alter outcomes in a murine Klebsiella pneumoniae infection model, indicating the existence of alternative pathways [110]. Although initial TLR activation is crucial for pathogen elimination, excessive or prolonged TLR signaling can lead to a hyperinflammatory state. Specifically, upon TLR4 activation, bacteria trigger the translocation of tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) to the outer mitochondrial membrane, ultimately promoting the production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) [111]. Notably, studies have shown that TLR4 inhibitors can effectively alleviate lung and kidney injury in mouse models of LPS-induced sepsis [112]. Intracellular TLRs, including TLR3, TLR7, and TLR9, recognize pathogen-derived nucleic acids within endosomes. TLR7 is an intracellular TLR located in both human and murine platelets that recognizes single-stranded RNA and plays an essential role in antiviral immunity in mice. During influenza infection, platelets internalize the virus and secrete complement component C3 and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) via TLR7-mediated signaling [113]. It has been reported that although TCP in murine sepsis may be partly attributed to TLR7 signaling, TLR7 activation can increase the survival rate of patients with viral sepsis. Conversely, other studies have highlighted the detrimental effects of TLR7 activation in sepsis, including excessive platelet activation and subsequent functional exhaustion [114]. Notably, TLR7 deficiency preserves platelet function in septic mouse models and mitigates the pathogenic activity of exosome-mediated platelet activation [115]. These findings illustrate the dual role of TLR7 in sepsis, positioning it as both a key regulator and a potential therapeutic target for sepsis-induced platelet dysfunction.

Both human and murine platelets express NLRP3, a cytosolic protein complex that regulates host immune responses to infection and tissue injury. Both extracellular heme and HMGB1 can stimulate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to caspase-1 activation and the subsequent secretion of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. Clinical studies suggest that blockade of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway is beneficial in patients with sickle cell disease and other hemolytic disorders [116, 117]. The NLRP3 inflammasome plays a critical role in sepsis, particularly in the amplification of inflammatory responses, organ injury, and coagulation dysfunction. Mechanistically, NLRP3 activation exacerbates sepsis-associated organ damage—including septic cardiomyopathy (SCM)—by promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, inducing oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, as well as enhancing cell apoptosis [118]. Furthermore, NLRP3 is closely implicated in LPS-induced coagulation abnormalities, and its excessive activation may drive immune-inflammatory responses and the development of sepsis-induced coagulopathy (SIC). Although several NLRP3 pathway inhibitors have demonstrated potential therapeutic benefits in preclinical models, the precise mechanisms underlying its activation and its clinical applicability warrant further investigation and validation [119].

Interactions between pathogens and platelets

Platelets-bacteria interaction

Bacterial infection is one of the most common causes of sepsis. Bacteria interact with platelets either directly or indirectly, can trigger platelet activation, manipulate platelet functions, disrupt platelet-mediated immune responses, or exacerbate thrombosis. In addition, bacteria can adhere to endothelial cells, disrupt the endothelial barrier, and expose the procoagulant subendothelial matrix [120]. In 1901, researchers proposed a link between platelets and bacterial infections, demonstrating that platelets can form aggregates with Vibrio cholerae [121]. Platelets express multiple receptors and secretory granules, including GPIb, αIIbβ3, TLR2, and TLR4, which participate in platelet–bacteria interactions [120]. TLR2 recognizes lipoteichoic acid and peptidoglycan derived from Gram-positive bacteria, whereas TLR4 binds LPS from Gram-negative bacteria and triggers platelet activation, characterized by P-selectin and tissue factor expression. These mechanisms may therefore represent promising therapeutic targets for diseases such as sepsis [122].

Platelet activation by bacteria occurs through multiple mechanisms, encompassing indirect interactions mediated by plasma proteins and direct engagement of bacterial secretory molecules. For example, the fibrinogen-binding serine-aspartate repeat protein G (SdrG) of Staphylococcus epidermidis is capable of directly binding platelet GPⅡb/Ⅲa [123]. Similarly, serine-rich protein A (SrpA) from Streptococcus sanguinis, as well as the surface proteins GspB and Hsa from Streptococcus gordonii, exhibit sialic acid–dependent binding to GPIb [124].

More commonly, bacterial mechanisms involve initial binding to plasma proteins, acting as bridging molecules, followed by interaction with platelet receptors, rather than direct binding to platelets [125]. Likewise, fibrinogen-binding proteins (Fnbp) on the surface of streptococci, as well as other proteins such as the fibrinogen-binding protein FbsA from Staphylococcus epidermidis, first bind fibrinogen and subsequently interact with the platelet GPⅡb-Ⅲa receptor [126, 127]. Soluble fibrin and thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1) released from activated platelets may enable PavB and pneumococcal surface protein C (PspC) to bind to platelet GPIIb/IIIa in the presence of TSP-1 [128]. In bacterial sepsis, the cytoplasmic domain of platelet GPIbα exacerbates sepsis-associated platelet hyperactivation and inflammation through a protein kinase C–dependent pathway, providing important insights for the development of effective therapies using protein kinase C inhibitors to combat bacterial infections [129].

In addition, bacterial secreted or shed products exert significant effects on platelet function. α-toxin from Staphylococcus aureus and the secretion of staphylococcal superantigen-like protein 5 (SSL5) can directly activate platelets [130]. In contrast, bacteria-released lysophosphatidic acid can subsequently suppress platelet activation via the platelet-activating factor (PAF) receptor. Porphyromonas gingivalis releases gingipains, key virulence determinants that induce platelet activation by activating platelet surface–expressed PAR-1 and PAR-4 [131].

Interestingly, bacteria induce platelet aggregation through a distinct “all-or-nothing” mechanism, in which strong aggregation occurs once a threshold bacterial concentration is reached, whereas no aggregation is observed below this threshold [132]. In platelet suspensions, the addition of general soluble agonists such as ADP induces platelet aggregation within seconds. By contrast, the introduction of bacteria into platelet suspensions results in a delayed, concentration-dependent aggregation response. The delay time observed during bacterial-induced platelet aggregation varies by bacterial species, ranging from several minutes to up to 10 min. It suggests the involvement of different interaction mechanisms. Shorter lag times are normally considered as an implication of platelet-bacteria direct interactions, whereas longer lag times suggest indirect interactions.

Platelets-virus interaction

In 1959, Danon and colleagues used electron microscopy to observe influenza virus particles integrated within platelets, opening a new field of research into virus–platelet interactions [120]. Subsequent research demonstrated that numerous small RNA viruses—including Dengue virus (DENV), Coxsackievirus group B (CVB), human immunodeficiency virus-1 (HIV-1), Influenza A Virus (IAV), and Hepatitis C virus (HCV)—are capable of platelet binding and internalization, whereas larger DNA viruses such as Herpesviridae-1 (HSV-1) attach to platelets but do not undergo cellular uptake [133]. Despite structural diversity, these viruses can attach to platelets and induce activation, thereby eliciting direct antiviral immune functions [134, 135]. During viral infection, platelets constitute a powerful first line of defense. However, some viruses have evolved strategies to use platelets as shelters and transport systems in viral infection [136].

The most direct interaction between platelets and viruses occurs through physical contact mediated by receptor binding. Integrins are particularly critical among platelet receptors, as they mediate platelet adhesion, recognize specific viral strains, and facilitate viral entry [136]. The abundantly expressed β3 integrins frequently interact with viruses, such as pathogenic hantaviruses [137]. Adenoviruses interact with platelets via αIIbβ3 and αVβ3 integrins [138]. Furthermore, HIV-infected cells secrete the transactivator of transcription (TAT) protein, which promotes platelet activation by concurrent engagement of integrin β3 and the chemokine receptor CCR3 [135]. Besides, sialic acids on the platelet surface act as cellular receptors that interact with highly glycosylated viral glycoproteins. Sialic acid–containing glycans are capable of binding multiple viruses, including encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) and influenza virus [139, 140]. In addition, several cytokine receptors can mediate interactions between viruses and platelets. For example, platelets can bind HIV via dendritic cell–specific intercellular adhesion molecule-3–grabbing non-integrin (DC-SIGN) as well as CLEC-2 [141, 142]. Ebola virus and hepatitis C virus also bind DC-SIGN through the viral glycoprotein E2 [143, 144]. Toll-like receptors are abundantly expressed on platelets, and dengue virus–induced platelet activation also involves CLEC-2 [145]. Dengue virus nonstructural protein 1 (NS1) interacts with TLR4 and TLR2, but only TLR4 is capable of mediating platelet activation [146]. TLR2 is also important for platelet interactions with human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) [147]. Physical interaction between platelets and viruses induces platelet activation, as evidenced by upregulated P-selectin and CD63, thereby enhancing endothelial adherence and coagulative responses [145, 148, 149].

Platelets are capable not only of binding viral particles but also of internalizing them [135]. Dengue virus particles can bind to and be internalized by platelets via DC-SIGN and heparan sulfate [150]. Internalization of viral particles by platelets can stimulate intracellular pattern recognition receptors, such as TLRs, as observed in platelet interactions with HIV, echoviruses, and influenza virus, a process involving activation of TLR7 [151]. Viruses are engulfed into endocytic vesicles following fusion with vesicles and granules, where they are killed by antimicrobial peptides during vesicle–granule fusion. Studies using autologous blood from patients demonstrate that platelets rapidly internalize and digest influenza A virus through vesicle–granule fusion, indicating that platelets contribute to viral clearance during influenza infection. However, internalization of the hepatitis C virus enhances viral persistence by protecting the virus from degradation (Fig. 5) [140].

Platelets in sepsis

Platelet-immune cell interactions

Platelet-monocytes interactions

In septic conditions, platelets can bind to circulating monocytes, forming platelet–monocyte aggregates (PMAs). Using a murine cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model of polymicrobial sepsis, flow cytometric analysis of circulating PMAs after infection revealed rapid platelet activation. Researcher found that PMAs increased significantly within 4 h after infection, peaked at 24 h, and persisted for up to 48 h [152]. Upon activation, platelets release multiple chemokines, including PF4 and C–C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5), that recruit monocytes to sites of inflammation [153]. Activated platelets rapidly upregulate surface expression of CD62P/P-selectin, which is subsequently recognized by PSGL-1 on monocytes, initiating PMAs formation [154]. This interaction is further stabilized through additional receptor–ligand interactions. For example, GVI–CD147 interaction and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 interactions with fibrinogen bound to activated platelets. PMA formation depends on calcium (Ca²⁺)- and can be reversed by removal of calcium chloride (CaCl₂) from the culture medium.

Platelet interactions show a bias toward CD16-expressing monocytes. Upon platelet attachment, classical CD14⁺⁺CD16⁻ monocytes increase their expression of CD16. This special type of monocyte has a proinflammatory phenotype characterized by enhanced secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, increased FcγR-mediated phagocytosis. This phenotypic shift promotes enhanced cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) expression and subsequent prostaglandin E₂ production within monocytes [155]. Other studies have shown that PMAs can reprogram monocyte cytokine production, leading to tissue factor expression, which in turn promotes monocyte CD16 expression and proinflammatory cytokine production via PAR1 and PAR2 activation [156]. These monocyte populations display elevated expression of antigen-presentation markers and release multiple inflammatory mediators. Formation of platelet–monocyte aggregates stimulate production of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), IL-8, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [157]. In monocytes and platelets, S100A8/A9 and IFITM2/3 genes are significantly upregulated in patients with sepsis and remain elevated through the stage of persistent inflammation, immunosuppression, and catabolism syndrome (PICS), indicating that these genes play key roles in sepsis progression [158]. It has been proposed that the simultaneous increases in these genes in both cell types may result from intercellular communication, such as increased PMA formation in sepsis. Some studies suggest that platelet-derived TGF-β may activate proinflammatory monocytes by inducing S100A8/A9 expression in monocytes [159].

PMAs are sensitive biomarkers of platelet activation and have important clinical relevance in sepsis [160]. In septic patients, PMA levels are significantly higher than in age- and sex-matched patients with non-septic syndrome. Elevated levels of circulating platelet–monocyte aggregates correlate with worse clinical outcomes in sepsis, including increased mortality and thrombotic events [161–164]. However, there is no direct evidence to suggest that PMA is an independent driver of sepsis progression.

In addition to aggregate formation, platelets secrete chemokines that regulate monocyte trafficking and phenotypic maturation. PF4 stimulates cytokine synthesis through JNK-dependent signaling and augments monocyte phagocytic capacity via PI3K, Syk, and p38 MAPK activation [153, 165, 166]. Moreover, PF4 cooperates with IL-4 to accelerate monocyte differentiation toward an antigen-presenting phenotype [167]. Activated platelets engage CR3 on monocytes and macrophages, triggering intracellular ROS production and facilitating histone citrullination and formation of monocyte extracellular traps (METs) in renal tissue, leading to tubular injury [168].

Platelets – neutrophils interactions

Activated platelets participate in neutrophil migration by releasing soluble mediators from granules, including chemokines, serotonin, and HMGB1 [169]. Platelets can recruit both platelets and neutrophils to sites of injury, further enhancing their activation and driving NETs release, while NETs in turn provide a scaffold for platelet adhesion, amplifying systemic inflammation and coagulation responses.

During sepsis, upregulation of P-selectin and CD40L on platelets promotes neutrophil adhesion via PSGL-1–P-selectin and CD40–CD40L signaling axes, leading to integrin activation. Mac-1 can directly bind platelets via GPIbα, JAM-3, and ICAM-2, or indirectly bind activated αIIbβ3 through fibrinogen bridging, promoting the formation of platelet–neutrophil aggregates (PANs) [170, 171]. Functionally, this process links hemostasis with immune responses. The effects of PANs on innate immunity are complex, as PANs participate in pathogen clearance through Toll-like receptor signaling cascades. However, its dysregulation leads to excessive thrombosis. Specific stimulation of platelets with TLR agonists increases PANs formation and improves survival in mice [172]. Contradictorily, increased PANs levels are accompanied by elevated markers of immunothrombosis in plasma and lung autopsy specimens [173]. Beyond sepsis, these circulating PANs are implicated in various disorders such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, and systemic lupus erythematosus [169].

In addition to PANs formation, platelets can drive NETs formation through multiple mechanisms and play a critical role in sepsis progression [174]. NETs are web-like chromatin structures released by neutrophils, composed of extracellular DNA decorated with histones, myeloperoxidase, and elastase [175]. Platelets induce NETs release through GPIb–Mac-1 interactions. In addition, platelet-derived extracellular vesicles carrying the TLR7/8 ligand miR-21 can stimulate NET formation, which can be effectively inhibited by TLR8 antagonists, NOX2 inhibitors, or miR-21 inhibitors [176]. Besides, platelet-derived HMGB1 and/or microRNAs can bind neutrophil TLR4 and activate PKG-dependent reactive oxygen species production, triggering Akt/mTOR-associated autophagy pathways and inducing NETs formation [175]. Monitoring platelet HMGB1 levels may provide new insights for sepsis therapy. Emerging evidence indicates that S100A8/A9 released during sepsis induces platelet pyroptosis through TLR4-dependent mechanisms. This cascade results in the liberation of oxidized mitochondrial DNA and enhances NETs formation, which subsequently releases more S100A8/A9, establishing a self-amplifying inflammatory circuit [177, 178]. Importantly, platelet-derived STING, an endoplasmic reticulum–associated sensor, mediates inflammatory responses to pathogen signals. It promotes platelet activation and NETs generation and exacerbating septic thrombosis. During sepsis, elevated cGAMP enhances STING–STXBP2 interactions, facilitating SNARE complex formation, granule exocytosis, and subsequent sepsis-associated thrombotic complications. Yang et al. developed a peptide, C-ST5, that blocks the interaction between STING and STXBP2, which may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for sepsis-induced thrombosis [179].

In severe septic states, NETs contribute to host defense by entrapping and eliminating invading pathogens, however, their excessive formation leads to microthrombosis and organ damage. NETs-induced intravascular coagulation depends on cooperative interactions between NETs and histone H4 and inorganic polyphosphate from platelets [175]. In vivo infection model, a dynamic NET–platelet–thrombin axis was shown to promote intravascular coagulation and microvascular dysfunction in sepsis. Within septic environments, NETs display dual roles, exerting both protective and inflammatory effects. On one hand, they contribute to infection control, while on the other hand, they promote microcirculatory dysfunction and tissue and organ injury. Existing studies suggest that assessment of NET generation serves as an independent predictor of DIC development and mortality in critically ill individuals [180].

Platelets – T cell Interactions

T cells are an essential population of lymphocytes that can be broadly divided into two subsets: CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells. In sepsis, platelets act as crucial immune mediators that suppress T-cell responses, often leading to impaired immunity. CD8⁺ T cells, also known as cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), recognize and eliminate infected, malignant, and senescent cells. In sepsis, the number of CD8⁺ T cells is reduced [181, 182]. During sepsis, platelet surface expression of MHC-I and their capacity for antigen cross-presentation are significantly increased, leading to reduced CD8⁺ T-cell numbers, decreased CD8⁺ T-cell proliferation, and impaired intracellular interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production, thereby affecting immune responses [180]. Functional responses of CD8⁺ T-cells are also impaired, increasing the risk of secondary infections and adverse outcomes.

In vitro co-culture experiments of human CD4⁺ T cells with autologous platelets have demonstrated that platelets can influence the differentiation of Th1, Th17, and regulatory T (Treg) cells as well as the production of associated cytokines, exerting distinct regulatory effects on different CD4⁺ T-cell subsets. Low concentrations of PF4 bind to TGFBRIII and enhance TGFβ signaling, thereby promoting CD4⁺ T effector cell responses. In contrast, high concentrations of PF4 directly bind to TGFBRII and block the interaction between TGFβ and TGFBRII, resulting in inhibition of TGFβ signaling [183]. Moreover, platelet depletion significantly suppresses regulatory T-cell and Th17 responses in the spleen. Conversely, T cells can influence platelet function by releasing cytokines, including IFN-γ and IL-2, which promote platelet activation, enhance leukocyte adhesion, and induce cytotoxic effects [184]. Nevertheless, further investigation is required to elucidate the roles of platelets in T-cell trafficking, differentiation, and activation during sepsis.

Platelets – B cell interactions

Acting as essential mediators of adaptive immunity, B lymphocytes differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells during infection. They are also essential for antigen presentation and participate in immune regulation through cytokine secretion. Platelet–B cell interactions are closely associated with CD40/CD40L signaling [185]. Studies have identified a negative correlation between platelet counts and monocyte and B-cell numbers. Therefore, TCP may increase monocyte and B-cell numbers and alleviate sepsis-induced immunosuppression, demonstrating a protective role of platelets in sepsis. Nevertheless, these findings are limited to European cohorts, calling for broader studies examining platelet, monocyte, and B-cell dynamics in early sepsis [186].

Platelets – endotheliocyte interactions

Sepsis-induced endothelial damage promotes platelet adhesion to a procoagulant endothelium, resulting in vascular leakage, intensified coagulation, and subsequent multiorgan dysfunction. Under steady-state conditions, the vascular endothelium is covered by glycocalyx. This layer is composed of structural proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans together with plasma-derived proteins like albumin and antithrombin, serving to restrain platelet–endothelium contact. During sepsis, the endothelium suffers from severe damage, glycocalyx shedding, and apoptosis. The endothelium barrier switches from an anticoagulant to a procoagulant state, which leads to widespread leukocyte and platelet adhesion [187]. Meanwhile, adhesion molecules, including ICAM-1 and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) are relocated to the endothelial surface [188]. As sepsis progresses, platelets release platelet-derived microparticles (PMPs) under thrombin stimulation, which transfer miR-233 to endothelial cells and alleviating ICAM-1–dependent vascular inflammation [189]. This finding may provide new insights into sepsis therapy. In sepsis, bacterial wall constituents stimulate endothelial pattern-recognition receptors, leading to IL-1 and TNF-α release and upregulation of adhesion molecules, including vWF and P-selectin. This provides conditions for platelet activation and aggregation [190]. vWF facilitates platelet–endothelial bridging and functions as a biomarker of endothelial damage and thrombotic risk. Platelet activation and endothelial injury elevate vWF levels, which interact with GPIb-IX-V and further increase endothelial permeability. It has been reported that isatin E prevents sepsis progression by inhibiting endothelial hyperpermeability and platelet–endothelial interactions [191]. Meanwhile, platelet interactions with endothelial cells via CD62P/CD40L synergize with pathogen DNA–activated cGAS–STING signaling to drive inflammation and leukocyte infiltration [185, 192]. These findings may therefore identify potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of sepsis-induced thrombosis. Researchers reported that targeting micron-scale polymeric particles has been shown to reduce platelet adhesion to inflamed endothelium in both ex vivo mouse blood flow models and in vivo LPS-induced systemic inflammation models, thereby alleviating thromboinflammation (Fig. 6) [193].

Platelets and organ failure in sepsis

Platelets in ARDS

Sepsis is the most common extrapulmonary cause of ARDS, accounting for approximately 32% of ARDS cases. ARDS is defined by disruption of the alveolar–capillary barrier, leading to pulmonary edema and profound hypoxemia. Studies have shown that sepsis-induced ARDS is more severe than ARDS caused by other etiologies, resulting in poorer prognosis and higher mortality [194]. Platelets play complicated roles in sepsis-associated ARDS. On the one hand, clinical data support a protective role of platelets, as TCP is a risk factor for the development of ARDS and poor outcomes in ARDS patients [195]. Trem-like transcript 1 (TLT-1) is primarily derived from platelet α-granules and exhibits dual effects in murine models of ARDS. Supplementation with TLT-1 restores fibrinogen deposition and reduces bleeding and tissue injury, whereas excessively high plasma TLT-1 levels indicate depletion of platelet reserves, leading to systemic dissemination of TLT-1–fibrinogen interactions, DIC and exacerbation of ARDS [195].

On the other hand, excessive platelet activation may promote lung injury. Elevated concentrations of platelet-derived α-granule proteins are detected in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, with particularly pronounced increases in PF4. Lowering PF4 concentrations in both circulation and lung tissue confers protective benefits to respiratory function [196]. Circulating megakaryocytes originating from bone marrow and splenic sinusoids lodge at pulmonary vascular bifurcations and generate individual platelets into the pulmonary circulation via cytoplasmic extensions into the lung circulation [197]. A separate subset of interstitial lung megakaryocytes displays unique characteristics, with transcriptomic profiles enriched for immune functions such as pathogen sensing, phagocytosis, and antigen presentation [198]. In ARDS, platelet activation is mediated by CLEC-2 through tyrosine kinase signaling, meanwhile, GPVI engages collagen and signals through spleen tyrosine kinase in murine platelets [199]. Upon activation, platelets secrete PMPs and release proinflammatory mediators including proteases, IL-1, and TNF, contributing to tissue injury. PMPs also promote transcellular transport of arachidonic acid and upregulate endothelial cyclooxygenase-2, TXA2, and ICAM-1, increasing endothelial and alveolar epithelial permeability and exacerbating lung injury [200]. In addition to releasing proinflammatory mediators, platelets can induce endothelial inflammation through interactions with P-selectin–expressing endothelial cells mediated by the integrin receptor GPIb/IX/V. Platelet activation enhances neutrophil recruitment and platelet–neutrophil aggregate formation, which disrupts the alveolar–capillary barrier and results in accumulation of protein-rich alveolar edema fluid. For example, elevated platelet–neutrophil aggregates increase pulmonary vascular leakiness, whereas activated leukocytes and platelets damage endothelium through TNF-α and platelet-activating factor release, leading to edema and hypoxemia. Besides prompting cytokine storm, platelets can drive pulmonary microvascular thrombosis and thrombotic inflammation in sepsis-associated ARDS [57, 201]. The HMGB1 released by activated platelets, as an important damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), can recruit and activate monocytes and neutrophils, promoting the formation of platelet-leukocyte aggregates in the lungs [202]. Monocytes, upon stimulation by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), are activated through pattern recognition receptors [PRRs] and present the activated TF on their surface and in the microvesicles they release. They are then released locally at the infection site, thereby initiating the extrinsic coagulation pathway [203]. The formation of NETs by neutrophils further exacerbates this process: NETs can sequester and bind to TF, directly promoting the activation of the extrinsic coagulation cascade; at the same time, the neutrophil elastase (NE) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) in NETs can cleave and inactivate natural anticoagulant molecules, such as tissue TFPI and thrombomodulin, leading to a shift of the coagulation-anticoagulation balance towards a hypercoagulable state [201]. Platelet–neutrophil interactions promote the formation of NETs. Although NETs can trap viruses, excessive NETs formation leads to lung tissue damage, enhanced immunothrombosis, and microvascular injury due to histones and neutrophil granule proteins [204]. Studies using CD41-deficient mice demonstrated that inhibition of platelet aggregation or blockade of platelet–neutrophil interactions with CD18 antibodies significantly reduces NET formation and lung injury without impairing viral clearance [205].

Platelet in septic cardiomyopathy

SCM is a severe complication characterized by acute cardiac dysfunction driven by systemic infection and inflammation, accelerating cardiovascular failure and worsening microcirculatory dysfunction and hypoperfusion. SCM is a major cause of death in sepsis and directly affects patient prognosis [204–206]. Nitric oxide–induced and sepsis-derived platelet exosomes generate reactive oxygen and nitrogen species via NADPH oxidase and NOS II, inducing caspase-3 activation and apoptosis in target vascular endothelial cells and contributing to myocardial injury [207]. Recombinant human thrombopoietin has been shown to ameliorate myocardial injury and improve inflammatory markers in sepsis patients, providing a potential therapeutic approach for SCM [196]. NLRP3 which functions as an intracellular immune sensor that detects cellular stress and danger signals [207, 208]. Acting within a feed-forward loop, blockade of the NLRP3 signaling not only dampens NLRP3 activation in platelets but also reduces platelet activation induced in CLP mice models [209]. In NLRP3-deficient platelets, αIIbβ3 signaling is markedly reduced while IL-1β release is observed, suggesting that the NLRP3 inflammasome contributes to hemostasis and arterial thrombosis through IL-1β release and αIIbβ3 signaling [210]. Activated platelets with NLRP3 can release a large amount of ROS and inflammatory mediators. The ROS derived from these platelets can directly induce mitochondrial damage in cardiomyocytes, intensify the oxidative stress load, and further promote the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome within cardiomyocytes. Eventually, this leads to cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and exacerbates cardiac damage [211–213]. Targeting NLRP3, ulinastatin exerts cardioprotective effects by suppressing inflammatory cytokine release and downregulating NLRP3 inflammasome activation, with high doses providing significant myocardial protection [214].

Platelets in septic liver injury

The liver is central to immune and metabolic regulation during sepsis, and sepsis-associated liver injury (SALI) frequently occurs in critically ill patients. Mortality rates in septic individuals with hepatic dysfunction exceed those with pulmonary failure, reaching 54–68% [215]. Platelet activation is closely associated with sepsis-induced liver injury. In patients with acute liver failure, elevated circulating microparticle levels are associated with systemic inflammatory response syndrome, severe hepatic encephalopathy, and death or the need for liver transplantation. Upregulation of P-selectin promotes platelet adhesion, activation, and apoptosis of hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells [215–217]. Platelet-derived extracellular vesicles have been shown to induce endothelial apoptosis in sepsis models. In murine bile duct ligation and sepsis models, platelet–leukocyte interactions are bidirectional, with leukocytes enhancing platelet activation and triggering coagulation cascades while promoting NET formation. Excessive immunothrombosis disrupts hepatic sinusoidal microcirculation, inducing or exacerbating liver injury [215, 217]. The acute liver failure model revealed that the release of CHI3L1 triggers the expression of podoplanin on macrophages, thereby promoting platelet aggregation and mediating inflammation through CLEC2 [218].

Glaucocalyxin A inhibits platelet activation and protects the liver from complement overactivation by downregulating C3a receptor expression through blockade of the RIP1/RIP3/AKT pathway in platelets, thereby preventing Escherichia coli–induced acute liver dysfunction [219]. Although platelet inactivation may prevent sepsis-induced liver injury, platelet depletion can be fatal because platelets are essential for Kupffer cell–mediated clearance of Staphylococcus aureus and other pathogens [220].

Platelets in acute kidney injury

Sepsis is a major contributor to acute kidney injury (AKI), representing approximately 45–70% of AKI cases among critically ill patients [221]. Platelet aggregation leads to thrombus formation in the renal microcirculation, leading to a reduction in the blood supply to the renal tubular epithelial cells. While activated platelets release mediators such as VEGFA, platelet-activating factor, and serotonin, increasing endothelial permeability and causing plasma leakage and hemoconcentration. These processes result in hypoperfusion, facilitate transendothelial migration of bioactive substances, promote interstitial inflammation, and ultimately cause renal injury [222]. Several studies indicate that increased platelet-derived extracellular vesicles during sepsis exacerbate septic AKI. One suggested mechanism implicates ARF6-driven activation of ERK/Smad3/p53 signaling in renal tubular epithelial cells, leading to inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and oxidative injury [223]. Platelet-derived extracellular vesicles can also carry inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and participate in proinflammatory host immune responses [224]. The miR-223-3p released by platelet exosomes can inhibit the NLRP3-related inflammasome, thereby suppressing the pyroptosis phenomenon in endothelial cells. Compared with healthy individuals, the decreased expression of miR-223-3p in patients with sepsis aggravates the inflammatory response in acute kidney injury caused by sepsis [225]. Platelet-derived transthyretin has been reported to play an important role in sepsis-associated AKI by further inducing renal cell apoptosis [226]. Sepsis-induced platelet activation contributes AKI by facilitating P-selectin–mediated neutrophil recruitment to the kidney, a key step in ischemia-related renal injury. Inhibition of neutrophil infiltration has been shown to protect renal morphology [227].

Aspirin suppresses platelet function and reduces inflammation, and analysis of the MIMIC-IV database revealed that high-dose aspirin may reduce ICU length of stay and 30- or 90-day mortality in sepsis-associated AKI patients [228]. However, TCP is common in AKI patients and is often associated with increased mortality risk, thus more research should be conducted to explore the role of platelets in sepsis-associated AKI [229].

Thrombocytopenia

Thrombocytopenia frequently occurs in patients with severe sepsis, affecting as many as 55% of critically ill individuals. It is defined as a platelet count below 150 × 10⁹/L [230]. Sepsis-associated TCP (SAT) is an important indicator reflecting disease severity. A study including 419 pediatric sepsis patients demonstrated that children with septic shock who developed TCP within 24 h of shock onset had a 28-day mortality risk increase of 40% [231]. Another retrospective cohort study of 7,981 patients found that a decrease in platelet count of ≤ 10% was independently associated with reduced 30-day mortality [231]. TCP can result from multiple mechanisms, including impaired platelet production, accelerated platelet destruction due to inflammation, and immune-mediated platelet clearance [232, 233].

There is a significant increase in pro-inflammatory macrophages in sepsis patients and animal models, and the phosphorylation level of BTK is closely related to TCP. Inhibition of BTK signaling in macrophages can inhibit their pro-inflammatory polarization through the Rap1/NF-κB pathway, improve megakaryocyte production, and restore platelet count [234]. A number of studies have found that thrombopoiesis in sepsis is often activated or compensated. Clinical and animal studies have shown that sepsis can significantly increase the proportion of reticulocyte and the level of thrombopoietin [235]. In conclusion, SIT is not caused solely by insufficient platelet production, but by a combination of increased consumption and an imbalance in immune-mediated regulation of platelet production in the context of activated platelet production.

Studies indicate that sepsis triggers the production of large amounts of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10, leading to thrombosis and endothelial dysfunction [235]. Microbial pathogens and their toxins injure the vascular endothelium, leading to platelet activation. Platelets adhere to damaged endothelium, and combined with the hypercoagulable state, this leads to massive platelet consumption [236, 237]. In the early stage of sepsis, platelet reactivity is increased, and activated platelets aggregate with leukocytes to form platelet-leukocyte aggregates. These, in turn, promote platelet degranulation, which in turn activates NETs [238]. In animal models, NETs formation leads to rapid and severe TCP by promoting platelet adhesion and aggregation, contributing to platelet consumption [239]. However, recent studies have found that septic TCP patients generate fewer neutrophil–platelet complexes, however these complexes display a distinct phenotype. They contain a higher proportion of PD-L1 + cells and CXCR4 compared with non-TCP patients. Moreover, although extracellular DNA release upon stimulation is reduced, plasma levels of MPO-DNA complexes, MPO, neutrophil elastase, and S100A8/A9 are elevated in TCP patients, suggesting neutrophil exhaustion after hyperactivation. Platelet function is impaired in TCP patients, as evidenced by reduced levels of sCD40L, PF4, and TGF-β [240].

In addition, immune-mediated platelet destruction is another cause of TCP. Platelets coated with autoantibodies are cleared in the spleen or liver through interactions with Fcγ receptors. Autoantibodies may mediate platelet destruction via complement activation or desialylation. Desialylated aged platelets are cleared by the hepatic Ashwell-Morell receptor (AMR). These observations imply that platelets may be phagocytosed during sepsis, accelerating platelet consumption [241, 242]. Additionally, circulating levels of IL-18 and IL-35 inversely correlate with platelet counts in sepsis, suggesting their involvement in TCP development [243].

Disseminated intravascular coagulation

Disseminated intravascular coagulation is a frequent and fatal complication of sepsis, tightly linked to platelet activation and responsible for substantial mortality [244]. Sepsis-induced DIC usually manifests as thrombotic DIC, often accompanied by organ dysfunction, while bleeding events are relatively uncommon [245]. In sepsis, platelets are activated through multiple mechanisms, including stimulation by damaged endothelium, thrombin-mediated activation, and interactions with immune cells. The over-activation of coagulation, the failure of anticoagulation and the inhibition of fibrinolysis eventually promote the formation of extensive microthrombosis and promote the occurrence and progression of sepsis-associated DIC.

Activated platelets in sepsis promote NET formation, contributing to intravascular coagulation. MPO and histones within NETs activate platelet surface G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), leading to the release of PF4 and PDGF, thereby promoting thrombosis [246]. Moreover, platelet activation results in the release of procoagulant mediators including vWF and PF4, thereby promoting aggregation and microvascular thrombosis. Platelet activation markers, including soluble TLT-1 (sTLT-1) and nerve injury-induced protein 1 [NINJ1], are closely associated with DIC. sTLT-1, expressed by platelets and megakaryocyte lineages and is significantly elevated in septic patients and are considered correlated with the clinical manifestations of DIC, translocates to the cell surface to promote platelet aggregation [247]. Zhou et al. identified NINJ1 as a conserved platelet cell death marker in humans and mice, strongly linked to membrane rupture. Targeting NINJ1 limits complete platelet degranulation and slows the development of sepsis-associated DIC [248].

DIC is associated not only with a hypercoagulable state but also, due to depletion of coagulation factors and platelets, often results in severe bleeding, further complicating management. Therefore, effective treatment must both inhibit excessive coagulation system activation and preserve adequate hemostatic function to prevent severe bleeding complications (Fig. 7) [249].

Antiplatelet therapy in sepsis

Cyclooxygenase inhibitors

Cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitors not only have traditional anti-inflammatory effects in the treatment of sepsis, but may also exert multi-target protective effects through immune regulation, antioxidation and improvement of the therapeutic effect of antibacterial treatment. As a classic COX inhibitor, aspirin demonstrates distinct biological actions depending on the administered dose. At low doses, aspirin permanently blocks COX-1 activity, limiting platelet functional capacity and diminishing secretion of thromboxane A₂, an important amplifier of platelet activation. By comparison, higher aspirin doses inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2, thereby interrupting arachidonic acid metabolism and the synthesis of thromboxanes and prostaglandins [253, 254]. Furthermore, aspirin promotes the production of the lipoxin-like mediator aspirin-triggered 15-epi-lipoxin A₄ (ATL) via COX-2–mediated mechanisms. ATL enhances nitric oxide signaling and suppresses IL-8 and myeloperoxidase generation, contributing to the resolution of inflammation and limiting leukocyte recruitment [255]. When serum levels exceed therapeutic ranges, aspirin primarily modulates COX-2 and NF-κB pathways, leading to broad inhibition of inflammatory cytokine and chemokine expression. This enhanced ATL synthesis is accompanied by a heightened propensity for bleeding complications.

Data derived from the MIMIC-IV cohort indicate that aspirin use is linked to lower mortality in patients with sepsis-related encephalopathy, kidney injury, and hepatic dysfunction [255–257]. Aspirin therapy has additionally been associated with attenuation of sepsis-induced cardiac damage without a corresponding rise in gastrointestinal hemorrhage [258]. Importantly, optimal aspirin dosing appears to be organ-specific in sepsis. Among individuals with sepsis-associated encephalopathy, higher mortality was observed in patients receiving 325 mg/day of aspirin compared with those treated with 81 mg/day [256]. Conversely, in sepsis-related acute kidney injury, aspirin doses exceeding 300 mg/day may offer greater benefit than lower-dose regimens [255]. Mirabito demonstrated that prostaglandins—especially PGI₂—mediate angiogenesis inhibitor–associated hypertension and renal damage, while high-dose aspirin lowers systemic and urinary PGI₂, conferring renal protection during sepsis [257]. Multiple investigations have further shown that preadmission aspirin use is associated with reduced mortality among septic patients [258–260]. Despite these findings, experimental evidence supporting aspirin’s protective effects in sepsis remains inconsistent. In a phase II randomized trial involving early sepsis patients, aspirin failed to improve organ function relative to placebo and was linked to a higher incidence of serious bleeding events. Such outcomes may reflect pronounced heterogeneity among study populations rather than dose-specific modulation of defined biological pathways. Additional studies are necessary to clarify the mechanistic basis of aspirin’s effects in this setting [261]. An Australian randomized trial found that administration of 100 mg/day aspirin failed to decrease sepsis-associated mortality in older individuals living in the community [262]. Moreover, a meta-analysis reported that aspirin therapy was linked to higher rates of ICU-acquired sepsis, longer ventilation periods, and prolonged ICU hospitalization, resulting in greater morbidity [263]. Collectively, these data suggest that both the efficacy and risks of aspirin in sepsis are highly dependent on patient subpopulation and dosing strategy.

COX-2 inhibitor and a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID). Compared with other NSAIDs, it is renowned for reducing the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding. In the CLP-induced multi-bacterial sepsis model, celecoxib alleviates systemic inflammatory responses and improves organ function by activating the SIRT1 signaling axis and inhibiting the expression of COX-2, NF-κB, and various pro-inflammatory cytokines. At the same time, celecoxib can enhance the antibacterial effect of antibiotics, significantly reducing the bacterial load in multiple organs [264]. Moreover, a low dose of celecoxib can alleviate lung, kidney, and liver damage in sepsis animal models, reduce oxidative stress levels, and improve antioxidant defense capabilities, thereby significantly improving survival rates [265]. PTUPB represents a novel strategy that exerts anti-inflammatory and organ-protective effects by reprogramming lipid metabolism. In the sepsis model induced by CLP, PTUPB simultaneously inhibits COX-2 and blocks the degradation of epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs) mediated by soluble epoxide hydrolase, thereby increasing the levels of endogenous anti-inflammatory lipid mediators. This drug significantly improves the survival rate and clinical scores of sepsis mice, reduces liver, lung, and kidney function damage [266]. However, the clinical application of celecoxib and PTUPB still requires further evaluation.

P2Y12 inhibitors

By limiting platelet activation and aggregation while influencing immune signaling and leukocyte movement, P2Y12 inhibitors may mitigate maladaptive ischemic immune responses in sepsis. Common P2Y12 antagonists include clopidogrel and ticagrelor [267].

Clinically, P2Y12 inhibitors are usually combined with aspirin rather than used alone. A retrospective analysis revealed that aspirin combined with ticagrelor was associated with a lower multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus infections than combinations involving clopidogrel [268]. Ulloa et al. further showed that ticagrelor preserves platelet function and enhances pathogen clearance, supporting its use as adjunct treatment in MRSA bacteremia [269]. In mouse models of intra-abdominal sepsis, clopidogrel attenuated platelet activation, weakened platelet–leukocyte crosstalk, and reduced pulmonary damage [270]. Using a CLP sepsis model, Albayati et al. demonstrated that clopidogrel modulated platelet-driven Treg expansion and function, leading to altered platelet–Treg interactions, reduced splenic pathology, and improved survival [271]. In an LPS-induced human sepsis model, Thomas et al. showed that both clopidogrel and ticagrelor reduced levels of inflammatory and thrombotic biomarkers such as IL-6, TNF-α, and D-dimer. Ticagrelor further limited ultrastructural remodeling of nascent fibrin clots relative to clopidogrel [272].

Nevertheless, results evaluating clopidogrel’s therapeutic benefit remain conflicting. Analysis of MIMIC-IV data indicated that P2Y12 inhibitor use reduced hospitalization duration without affecting 30-day mortality in sepsis [273]. In contrast, treatment with P2Y12 antagonists was linked to a higher incidence of gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Another LPS-based mouse model demonstrated that genetic deletion of P2Y12 resulted in heightened leukocytosis, increased cytokine release, and exacerbated pulmonary injury [274]. Because clopidogrel depends on cytochrome P450–mediated hepatic activation, its effectiveness in sepsis may be reduced by liver dysfunction, inflammatory states, or pharmacologic interactions. Furthermore, combined P2Y12 inhibitor therapy was associated with increased ischemic stroke incidence, possibly reflecting selection bias [260]. By contrast, ticagrelor demonstrates reproducible anti-inflammatory and organ-protective effects across clinical studies and CLP or cecal slurry models, likely owing to its direct pharmacologic action [275]. Sepsis represents a multifaceted and biologically heterogeneous disease state. Differences in model systems, illness severity, and disease phase may explain variable responses, underscoring the need for selective application of P2Y12 antagonists. In addition, bleeding risk related to P2Y12 blockade is not fully defined, necessitating large clinical trials to validate whether preclinical observations apply to human sepsis. Post hoc analyses based on the PLATO trial and research results from the Veterans Health Care Systems of Israel, Denmark, the United States, and large electronic health databases have shown that the use of ticagrelor is associated with a lower risk of S. aureus bacteremia, sepsis, and pneumonia, and in some studies, it has reduced infection-related mortality [276].

CD40L targeted therapy

In sepsis, excessive activation of platelets not only contributes to microthrombosis formation but also enhances the inflammatory response and intestinal barrier damage by releasing CD40L. Cilostazol significantly reduces the proportion of CD40L⁺ platelets and the level of soluble CD40L in the circulation, thereby improving the intestinal barrier function of septic mice and increasing survival rates. Mechanistically, CD40L derived from platelets can activate the CD40–TRAF6 signaling pathway on enteric glia cells (EGCs), induce enhanced reactivity of EGCs, promote the release of TNF-α and IL-1β, and inhibit the production of S-nitroso glutathione, ultimately leading to the downregulation of epithelial tight junction proteins and increased intestinal barrier permeability. Inhibiting platelet activation or directly blocking the CD40L–CD40–TRAF6 signaling axis can alleviate excessive activation of EGCs, restore anti-inflammatory/protective signals, maintain the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier, and thereby alleviate multi-organ dysfunction [277].

GPVI targeted therapy

GPVI is a collagen receptor on the surface of platelets. It plays a crucial role in the formation of microthrombi, the induction of NETs release, and immune thrombosis. Cath-HG is a novel cathelicidin derived from the skin of Hylarana guentheri, with a short peptide length and low toxicity. It exhibits significant anti-platelet and organ-protective effects in sepsis models. Xiong et al. discovered that Cath-HG can specifically inhibit collagen-induced platelet aggregation, with its molecular target being platelet GPVI, a key receptor that mediates platelet binding to exposed collagen and triggers immune thrombosis. By inhibiting the GPVI signaling pathway, Cath-HG significantly alleviates sepsis-related excessive platelet activation, microthrombosis, and platelet consumption, thereby alleviating sepsis-induced TCP. At the same time, this effect is accompanied by the reduction of multi-organ inflammation and tissue damage as well as an increase in survival rate. The in vivo FeCl₃-induced thrombosis model further confirmed the antithrombotic ability of Cath-HG, supporting its effective inhibition of platelet-mediated vascular damage in vivo. Overall, this study clearly identified GPVI as a potential target in sepsis treatment and proposed Cath-HG as a novel candidate drug with both antithrombotic and immunomodulatory potential, providing a new direction for the development of anti-sepsis treatment strategies targeting platelet activation [278].

Conclusions

The essence of sepsis is a complex syndrome characterized by the interweaving of uncontrolled host inflammatory responses and coagulation disorders. Platelets are at the core of this network. As effector cells with both hemostatic and immune functions, platelets reshape the balance between inflammation and coagulation in sepsis through dynamic interactions with various cells.

In interactions with neutrophils, platelets promote NETs formation via P-selectin/PSGL-1, CD40L, and HMGB1, thereby enhancing the ability to clear pathogens but also exacerbating microthrombus deposition and microcirculatory disorders. The platelet-monocyte/macrophage complex amplifies cytokine release through TLRs inflammasome signals, promoting immune amplification and tissue damage. The interaction with endothelial cells is a key link in the vascular dysfunction of sepsis: platelets adhere to damaged endothelium, release thromboxane A₂, chemokines, and microparticles, thereby increasing endothelial permeability and promoting procoagulant phenotypic transformation, thereby promoting disseminated intravascular coagulation and organ hypoperfusion. Additionally, platelets can also have functional interactions with adaptive immune cells such as lymphocytes and dendritic cells, affecting antigen presentation and immune polarization.

It is worth noting that platelets exhibit staged changes during the sepsis process: in the early stage, they are characterized by excessive activation and immune thrombosis formation, while in the late stage, they may present with functional exhaustion and a decrease in quantity, resulting in weakened hemostatic function and increased bleeding risk. This “excessive activation - functional exhaustion” dynamic transformation suggests that platelets are not only important executors of host defense but also potential amplifiers of organ damage.

Future research may focus on the specific functions of platelet subgroups, immune metabolic reprogramming, and the molecular mechanisms of platelet-immune cell interactions. Based on precise staging and risk stratification, targeted intervention strategies should be explored to control inflammation and thrombosis formation while maximizing the physiological hemostatic and immune defense functions of platelets.
