Left-Dominant Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy in a Young Athlete
Authors: Sofia Esteves, Daniel Inácio Cazeiro, Tatiana Guimarães, João R. Agostinho, João Abecasis, Tiago Pereira da Silva, Sância Ramos, Fausto J. Pinto, Nuno Cardim, Rui Plácido
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArrhythmogenic left ventricular cardiomyopathy (ALVC) may present with a myocarditis-like inflammatory “hot phase” and carries the risk of ventricular arrhythmias.Case SummaryA 21-year-old athlete presented with hemifacial and arm paresthesia and chest discomfort. He had parietal ischemic lesions, elevated high-sensitivity I troponin, left ventricle (LV) dilation, apical hypokinesia, apical thrombus, and multifocal nonischemic late gadolinium enhancement with edema. A cardioembolic stroke due to myocarditis with an LV thrombus was assumed. However, he had progressive LV remodeling with persistent late gadolinium enhancement. Endomyocardial biopsy showed low-grade inflammation. Genetic testing identified a pathogenic MYBPC3 variant. The patient declined exercise restriction and experienced exercise-triggered ventricular fibrillation. A subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was inserted, and he remained asymptomatic.DiscussionThe presentation and longitudinal course supported ALVC rather than myocarditis.Take-Home MessagesALVC can mimic myocarditis and may be unmasked by exercise exposure. Serial cardiac magnetic resonance, endomyocardial biopsy, and genetic testing can confirm the diagnosis.

History of Presentation

A 21-year-old professional soccer player presented to a local hospital with sudden left arm and hemifacial paresthesia and 2 weeks of nonexertional chest discomfort and fatigue. On admission, vitals and both cardiopulmonary and neurologic examinations were unremarkable. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed sinus bradycardia (43 beats/min) with Q waves in DI and aVL, as well as biphasic T waves in V1 to V3 (Figure 1). Laboratory testing revealed marked high-sensitivity I troponin elevation (4,202 ng/L) with mildly elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (131 pg/mL) and low c-reactive protein (0.12 mg/dL). Urgent transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated left ventricle (LV) dilation indexed left ventricular end-diastolic volume 103 mL/m2, with mild apical hypokinesia, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 61%, reduced global longitudinal strain (−16.5%, worse in mid-basal septum), and a mobile, oval LV apical mass (14 × 9 × 8 mm) adherent to the inferior apex—highly suspicious of LV thrombus (Figure 2). Brain magnetic resonance imaging confirmed multiple small acute ischemic lesions in the subcortical white matter and left inferior parietal cortex, consistent with embolic phenomena. Past Medical History

The patient had an unremarkable past medical history, as well as a family history.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis included acute viral/lymphocytic myocarditis, arrhythmogenic LV cardiomyopathy presenting in an inflammatory “hot phase,” cardiac sarcoidosis, autoimmune myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy with superimposed myocardial inflammation, and, less likely, ischemic myocardial injury. Ischemic heart disease was considered unlikely at presentation because of the patient’s age, lack of cardiovascular risk factors, nonischemic cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) pattern, and later normal coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA). Cardiac sarcoidosis was considered but was not supported by extracardiac manifestations, atrioventricular conduction disease, ventricular arrhythmias at presentation, or imaging features suggestive of granulomatous disease. Autoimmune myocarditis and other systemic inflammatory causes were also considered; however, there were no systemic symptoms, extracardiac manifestations, or laboratory clues to support a multisystem inflammatory disorder. Giant cell myocarditis was considered to be unlikely given the relatively indolent presentation, preserved initial LVEF, absence of electrical instability at index admission, and biopsy findings showing only scant interstitial inflammation. Although the index event resembled acute myocarditis, the combination of athlete-atypical ECG abnormalities, LV thrombus despite preserved LVEF, extensive multifocal late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), and subsequent progressive remodeling raised early concern for an arrhythmogenic substrate, ultimately favoring arrhythmogenic left ventricular cardiomyopathy (ALVC).

Investigations

CMR corroborated LV dilation and showed myocardial edema (T2/short tau inversion recovery) in apical lateral/inferior and mid-basal anterior/lateral/inferior segments. LGE was nonischemic, with mid-wall nodular septal enhancement and subepicardial enhancement in mid-inferior, inferolateral, anterolateral, and apical segments, extending slightly to the anterior/inferior apex (Figure 3). The apical mass exhibited absent first-pass perfusion and a persistent hypointense (“etched”) core on LGE at long TI, with low T1/T2 signal—features diagnostic of avascular thrombus. Management

The patient was anticoagulated with warfarin because of the LV apical thrombus and embolic cerebral ischemic lesions. Given the concern for an underlying arrhythmogenic substrate, he was strongly advised to avoid competitive and high-intensity exercise. At discharge, a strategy of close clinical, imaging, and biomarker surveillance was planned, including serial echocardiographic and CMR reassessment.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 3-month follow-up, despite counseling, the patients remained physically active. Clinically, he was asymptomatic and did not have signs of heart failure. However, high-sensitivity I troponin persisted elevated. TTE showed continued LV dilation with preserved LVEF and mildly reduced global longitudinal strain. Repeat CMR demonstrated progressive LV remodeling (indexed left ventricular end-diastolic volume: 148 mL/m2, LVEF: 52%), moderate right ventricular dilation without regional abnormalities, and an unchanged LGE pattern (Figure 4). Considering the persistent biventricular dilation and myocardial injury, an endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) was performed and revealed myocytolysis (ischemic type), mild edema, and scant interstitial inflammation (Figure 5), compatible with subacute myocarditis-like injury. Comprehensive genetic testing was performed. Classical desmosomal genes typically associated with arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy—including DSP (desmoplakin), PKP2, DSG2, and DSC2—were not identified. However, a pathogenic variant in myosin-binding protein C3 (MYBPC3) (c.1484G>A; p.Arg495Gln) was discovered. While MYBPC3 is traditionally linked to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, sarcomeric variants are increasingly recognized as causes of left-dominant arrhythmogenic phenotypes without overt hypertrophy. Shortly thereafter, during a professional match, the athlete suffered out-of-hospital cardiac arrest due to ventricular fibrillation. Basic life support was started immediately (estimated no-flow of 10 minutes), 3 shocks were delivered, and 2 cycles of advanced cardiovascular life support were given. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after 14 minutes. In the emergency department, he was hemodynamically stable; ECG showed sinus rhythm without acute ischemic changes; TTE revealed transient global hypokinesia (LVEF: 40%-45%), improving to 52% by discharge. Coronary CTA excluded coronary artery disease. A CMR was repeated and was unchanged from the prior. A subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was inserted for secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death. Recommendations regarding sports participation were discussed within a shared decision-making framework, balancing the patient’s preferences with the estimated risk of recurrent ventricular arrhythmias and contemporary guideline recommendations. In this context, competitive sports participation was strongly discouraged. Discussion

This is a case of ALVC fulfilling the Padua diagnostic criteria,1,2 presenting with a myocarditis-like “hot phase” characterized by embolic cerebral infarcts secondary to an LV thrombus.3 In fact, it illustrates how ALVC may initially manifest as a myocarditis-like syndrome in a young athlete.3,4 The apical regional dysfunction created a substrate for intraventricular blood stasis and thrombus formation, explaining the embolic brain lesions despite only a mid-range reduction in LVEF. Indeed, the embolization risk associated with LV thrombus is driven primarily by regional wall motion abnormalities and myocardial injury, rather than by global systolic function alone.5

Serial CMR imaging documented progressive LV dilation and stable, extensive LGE, a pattern consistent with ALVC rather than resolving viral myocarditis.3,6,7 EMB revealed low-grade inflammation without evidence of a definitive viral etiology, further supporting an arrhythmogenic substrate.7

Although MYBPC3 mutations are classically associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, increasing evidence shows that sarcomeric gene variants may also express an arrhythmogenic LV-predominant phenotype, highlighting the importance of comprehensive genetic testing in suspected ALVC cases.8

In ALVC—whether desmosomal or sarcomeric—vigorous and sustained exercise acts as both a trigger and disease amplifier. Heightened catecholaminergic stimulation, increased wall stress, and repetitive microinjury lower the threshold for malignant ventricular arrhythmias and accelerate structural myocardial remodeling.9 Observational studies in genotype-positive individuals demonstrate earlier phenotypic conversion and higher arrhythmic burden in those exposed to endurance or competitive sports.9

Accordingly, contemporary sports cardiology guidelines (including the 2020 European Society of Cardiology and recent American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association recommendations) discourage high-intensity or competitive exercise in arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, even in early disease stages, while promoting shared decision-making.10,11 In this case, premature return to professional sport likely intersected with an electrically unstable substrate, precipitating ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest.

Conclusions

This case highlights arrhythmogenic LV cardiomyopathy as an important diagnostic consideration in young athletes presenting with a myocarditis-like “hot phase.” Regional LV dysfunction with preserved or mildly reduced global systolic function may nevertheless create a highly thrombogenic substrate, leading to systemic embolization. Serial CMR demonstrating progressive ventricular remodeling with persistent, extensive LGE, together with nonspecific inflammatory findings on EMB, favors ALVC over self-limited myocarditis. Recognition of sarcomeric gene variants as potential causes of LV-predominant arrhythmogenic phenotypes expands the genetic spectrum of the disease. Finally, this case underscores the critical arrhythmic risk associated with premature return to high-intensity sports in arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy and reinforces current recommendations for exercise restriction in affected individuals.
