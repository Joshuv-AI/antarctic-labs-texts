Nucleotide motif-guided selection of plasma microRNA biomarkers in trauma
Authors: Boyang Ren, Ruoxing Li, Chien-Yu Lin, Chanhee Park, Sheng Wang, Andrew O Suen, John Kessler, Shiming Yang, Rosemary Kozar, Lin Zou, Brittney Williams, Ziyi Li, Peter Hu, Wei Chao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTrauma remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in part due to complex pathophysiological responses. Yet our abilities to predict these changes are limited. Plasma miRNAs have been proposed as DAMPs that drive immune response and organ injury. Here, we test a panel of plasma miRNAs—selected based on next-generation sequencing and nucleotide motifs identified via a machine-learning algorithm—for their abilities to predict subclinical pathophysiological injuries. We find marked and severity-dependent increases in the miRNA biomarkers following trauma, which are closely associated with various injury markers. AUROC indicates that these biomarkers possess strong diagnostic and predictive abilities in overall trauma severity, organ injury, coagulation, endothelial activation, and inflammation. In a combined cohort of trauma and sepsis, miR-224-5p and miR-145-5p emerge as particularly effective in differentiating the two critical illnesses. These observations offer insights into potential values of the plasma miRNAs in the prediction of critical pathophysiological injury in trauma.Subject areas: Diagnostics, Health sciences, Internal medicine, Medicine, Traumatology

Introduction

Trauma is accountable for 13% of deaths and remains the leading cause of mortality among individuals age 1–44 years in the US.1 While advancements in the clinical management of traumatic injury have improved survival, the morbidity and mortality of severe injury are still unacceptably high in part due to complex pathophysiological responses leading to secondary organ injury.2 Current treatment of trauma remains limited to supportive therapies and targeting individual pathophysiology. Moreover, patients with trauma are heterogeneous in demographics, causes of injury, underlying pathophysiological responses, and responses to treatments, and as a result, their post-injury clinical trajectories are often unpredictable. These challenges highlight the need for tools that could help predict these critical pathophysiological responses, such as inflammatory storm, coagulopathy, endotheliopathy, and organ injury following trauma.

Trauma elicits the rapid activation of innate immunity and repair mechanisms to restrict the damage.3,4,5 However, the innate immune activation can become grossly imbalanced and drive excessive inflammatory response, endothelial activation and injury, cellular barrier dysfunction, and coagulopathy when initial injury is severe and sustained.3,6 In the absence of infection, danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), likely released from injured cells, are the presumed driver in maintaining and exacerbating the sustained activation of the innate immune system via pattern recognition receptors and subsequent hyperinflammation, coagulation, endotheliopathy, and organ injury.3,6 As such, a forward feedback loop of tissue damage is formed leading to inflammation that in turn causes further cellular and organ impairment.

Recent studies have found that extracellular RNA, isolated from the plasma, tissues, and cells under various critical conditions such as trauma and sepsis, is a strong innate immune activator.7,8,9,10 Plasma RNAseq revealed that extracellular microRNAs (ex-miRNAs) are the dominant biotype of plasma small RNA and are markedly differentially expressed in patients with trauma, demonstrating a unique signature of plasma miRNAs in trauma.10 Circulating plasma miRNA profiling reportedly can differentiate human sepsis and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS).11 miRNAs are small, single-stranded (ss) noncoding RNAs12 that primarily regulate gene expression inside the cell by binding to target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation and translation inhibition.13,14 However, increasing studies have shown that miRNAs also exist in extracellular spaces, including circulation15,16,17,18 and various body fluids.19,20,21 These extracellular (ex) cell-free miRNAs are packaged into and carried by various plasma macromolecular complexes, including EVs,22 high-density lipoprotein (HDL),23 and Ago-2.24 Some, not all, of ex-miRNAs can function as DAMP by binding and activating Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) and inducing innate inflammation.7,8,9 In critical illness, such as trauma and sepsis, ex-miRNAs have been proposed as a key driver10,25,26 in innate immune response and organ injury and as a potential trauma biomarker.27

In this study, we developed a computer exhaustive search algorithm and identified five nucleotide motifs uniquely presented in the sequences of proinflammatory miRNAs. The utility of the nucleotide motifs to identify and predict the proinflammatory property of ex-miRNAs is tested and validated in two independent sets of miRNAs. Based on the next generation RNA sequencing data and guided by the newly identified five nucleotide motifs, we selected a panel of 12 plasma miRNAs as candidates for trauma biomarker testing. In a cohort of 48 patients with trauma, we found that the plasma concentrations of the miRNA biomarkers were markedly upregulated at the time of admission, closely correlated with the patients’ injury severity score (ISS), and a wide spectrum of plasma pathophysiological markers linked to organ injury, coagulation, endothelial activation, and inflammation. Moreover, the miRNA biomarkers exhibited strong diagnostic and concurrent predictive abilities in overall trauma severity, organ injury, coagulation, endothelial activation, and inflammation in patients with trauma.

Results

Study design

Figure 1 illustrates the overall design of the current study – motif discovery, biomarker selection, and biomarker evaluation. Previous studies show that some, but not all, upregulated plasma miRNAs in mice and humans after traumatic injury promote cytokine and complement production via TLR7 in an in vitro cell culture system.10 In this study, we tested the hypothesis that specific nucleotide sequences or motifs exist that determine the proinflammatory property of miRNAs and can be used to guide the selection of miRNA biomarker candidates for trauma-induced inflammation and organ injuries. To achieve this, we developed a computer exhaustive search algorithm and identified five nucleotide motifs embedded specifically in pro-inflammatory miRNAs (Figure 1, left panel). Next, to select miRNA biomarker candidates, 10 patients with severe injury and 10 healthy controls were analyzed for RNAseq-based plasma RNA profiling. Based on the differential expression (DE) of the plasma miRNAs and the presence of any one of the five nucleotide motifs in their structures, we selected a panel of plasma miRNA biomarker candidates that were upregulated in patients with trauma and possessed a proinflammatory property (Figure 1, middle panel). Finally, we tested the panel of miRNA biomarker candidates in a cohort of 48 patients with trauma and 24 healthy subjects for their association with various trauma pathophysiology markers known to be linked with organ injury, coagulation, inflammation, and endothelial activation. We also tested the performance of the miRNA biomarkers in predicting secondary organ injury after initial trauma (Figure 1, right panel).

Overall study flow

The study consisted of the following three parts: (1) Motif discovery (left panel): Five pro-inflammatory motifs were identified via a computer exhaustive search algorithm; (2) Biomarker selection (middle panel): A panel of 12 plasma miRNA biomarkers candidates were selected based on the DE of plasma miRNAs in patients with trauma and guided by the pro-inflammatory miRNA motif; (3) Biomarker evaluation (right panel): The plasma miRNA biomarker candidates were quantified in the cohorts of control subjects (n = 24) and patients with trauma (n = 48), tested for their correlations with various trauma pathophysiological markers, and evaluated for their predictive performance in secondary organ injury after trauma.

Machine learning-guided discovery of pro-inflammatory motifs in miRNAs

To identify the nucleotide motifs embedded specifically in pro-inflammatory, but not in non-inflammatory, miRNAs, we developed a computer exhaustive search algorithm and took a machine learning approach for automated motif selection. As illustrated in Figure 2A, we first generated a training dataset containing 33 mouse miRNAs that are upregulated in the plasma of trauma mice.10 Among the 33 miRNA mimics tested in an in vitro cell system (cultured bone marrow-derived macrophages, BMDMs), 18 of them triggered a dose-dependent CXCL2 (Chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 2) production while the other 15 did not (non-inflammatory) as shown in Figure 2B. Utilizing the search algorithm (Table S1), a list of 513,599 possible motifs ranging between 2mer and 10mer within the training set of 33 miRNAs was identified. The list of motifs was then shortened to 306 after we excluded those motifs that appeared in the non-inflammatory miRNAs, those that appeared in less than 25% of pro-inflammatory miRNAs, and those with duplicates. Thus, the remaining motifs were only present in the pro-inflammatory miRNAs (Figure 2A). Next, we developed an automated motif-finding algorithm to pinpoint the most essential motifs linked to the pro-inflammatory property of miRNAs by utilizing the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model.28 By applying cross-validation and tuning the value of the regulator in the LASSO model, only essential motifs would be left with non-zero coefficients while retaining the highest model performance monitored by F1 score (Figure S2). As a result, five motifs – (1).UUC, (2) U..UU., (3).A …..UU., (4) G.UU. and (5) UU … U (“.” represents a wild card) – were identified specifically link to the 18 pro-inflammatory miRNAs of the training set (Figure 2A), and a total of 41 pro-inflammatory motifs were discovered in the training set of miRNAs with a relaxed LASSO algorithm (Table S2). These data indicate that the five motifs are the most prevalent in the 18 pro-inflammatory miRNAs but are completely absent in the 15 non-inflammatory miRNAs. Figure 2B details the 33 miRNAs – 18 pro-inflammatory and 15 non-inflammatory – and their tested vs. motif-based predicted results of CXCL2 production in BMDMs. As illustrated in Figure 2B and Table 2, the sensitivity of the individual motifs to identify pro-inflammatory miRNAs in the training set was between 56% and 61% with the specificity at 100%. However, the five motifs together – i.e., presence of any of the five – could distinguish pro-inflammatory miRNAs from non-inflammatory miRNAs with 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity.

We next validated our algorithm in two separate sets of miRNAs - Test set 1 consisted of 20 mouse miRNAs upregulated in either a mouse model of sepsis or polytrauma in a previously published plasma small RNA-seq database (Figure 2C).10,26 Test set 2 consisted of 38 human miRNAs that were found upregulated in the plasma of our human trauma cohort based on previous plasma small RNA-Seq (Figure 2D).10 Both sets of miRNAs were completely different from those in the training set. In these test sets, a dose-dependent cytokine CXCL2 production by cultured BMDMs treated with the miRNA mimics was considered the “ground truth” to define the “pro-” vs. “non” inflammatory” miRNAs (Figure S3). As illustrated in Figures 2C and 2D and detailed in Table 2, the 5 motifs together (i.e., any one of the five motifs) offered an overall prediction with 92% sensitivity and 86% specificity in Test set 1 and 84% sensitivity and 69% specificity in Test set 2. These data suggest that five miRNA nucleotide motifs possess strong sensitivity and reasonable specificity to predict the pro-inflammatory property of miRNAs and were used to guide the selection of miRNA biomarker candidates in patients with trauma.

Nucleotide motif-guided selection of proinflammatory miRNA biomarkers

To select plasma miRNA biomarker candidates, we first identified the upregulated plasma miRNAs in response to trauma based on our published RNA-Seq data (n = 10, trauma vs. n = 10, health) at the time of admission.10,25 Among 401 miRNAs detected in the human plasma, 151 were upregulated with a fold-change (FC) > 1.5 in patients with trauma. We then selected the most upregulated and abundant miRNAs in trauma plasma by the top 50% FC and top 50% count per million (CPM), applied the motif-finding algorithm to the top 38 miRNAs, and identified a panel of 12 miRNAs with at least one pro-inflammatory motif (Table 3).

Plasma miRNA biomarkers are markedly elevated in patients with blunt polytrauma

Next, we measured the plasma cell-free miRNA biomarker candidates using dPCR in the expanded cohorts of trauma (n = 48) and healthy controls (n = 24). We were able to quantify the copy number of the miRNA biomarkers and found that all 12 miRNA candidates were highly expressed and markedly upregulated in trauma patients as compared to the healthy controls (Figure 3A). The fold-changes of trauma vs. control subjects ranged from 6.7 (let-7c-5p) to 78.2 (miR-145-5p). The fold-change was injury severity-dependent. In severely injured patients (ISS > 15), 10 out of the 12 miRNA biomarker candidates exhibited significantly higher plasma levels than those in moderately injured patients (ISS ≤ 15) (Figure 3B). Of note, out of 48 trauma plasma samples and 12 miRNAs tested - a total of 576 data points, four data points were outliers (miR-199-5p, miR-125b-2-3p, miR-95-3p) with very low miRNA copy numbers, which happened to be from two patients with moderate severity (ISS = 8 & 5). The heatmap in Figure 3C illustrates the three groups of cohorts – healthy control, patients with trauma with ISS ≤ 15, and patients with trauma with ISS > 15 – and their relative plasma expression of the 12 miRNA biomarkers. The heatmap reveals that patients with trauma were highly heterogeneous in their plasma levels of the 12 miRNA candidates, but some patients with trauma, particularly many severely injured with ISS > 15, showed a cross-board upregulation of the 12 miRNA biomarkers.

Plasma miRNA biomarkers are closely correlated with trauma injury markers

The pathophysiology of trauma-induced secondary injury is complex. Immediate effects of trauma include the direct and indirect mechanical force on tissues, which induces tissue damage, contusions, hemorrhage, fractures, and disrupted barrier immune defenses.3,5 Subsequent innate immune responses driven by DAMPs lead to excessive inflammation, endothelial injury, and coagulopathy. These pathophysiological events, often interlinked, result in hemodynamic instability, microvascular thrombosis, vascular leak, tissue hypoxia, secondary organ injury, and other clinical complications. To capture the trauma response state, we designed an extensive panel of mediators and injury markers that are sensitively linked to the trauma pathophysiology. Utilizing a Luminex-based multiplex assay system, we measured 14 such endotype markers present in the plasma at the time of admission, which included (1) various organ injury markers, (2) pro-coagulants, (3) pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and (4) markers of endothelial activation. As illustrated in Figure 4A, there was a marked and injury severity-dependent increase in the brain injury markers S100B29,30 and enolase30 among the trauma cohort. A similar increase was observed with the lung injury markers of ANG2/ANG1.31 D-dimer, a fibrin degradation product, was markedly elevated, as were the two plasma pro-coagulants, P-selectin and tissue factor, in these patients with trauma. Traumatic injury also led to vascular inflammation as evidenced by a significant increase in the pro-inflammatory mediators IL-6, TNFα, IL-8, and CXCL2 in the plasma. Also observed was marked endothelial cell activation and injury. Syndecan-1, a proteoglycan expressed on endothelial cells and a marker for endothelial injury, was notably increased in severely injured patients with ISS > 15. Both vWF-A2 and VCAM-1 exhibited an injury severity-dependent and marked increase after trauma. We next performed Spearman’s correlation analyses between the panel of 12 miRNA biomarkers and the various plasma protein injury and inflammatory markers. As illustrated in Figure 4B, we discovered that within the trauma cohort, majority of the 12 ex-miRNA biomarker panel was either moderately (r > 0.4, p < 0.01) or strongly (r > 0.7, p < 0.01) correlated with various plasma inflammatory and injury markers, such as brain injury (S100B and enolase 2), coagulation (P-selectin, tissue factor and D-dimers), inflammation (IL-6 and IL8), and endothelial injury markers (syndecan-1, vWF-A2). It is noteworthy that ANG-1, CXCL-2, and VCAM-1, which did not display severity-dependent upregulation, showed no correlation with the plasma miRNA biomarkers.

Plasma miRNA biomarkers are correlated with aspartate aminotransferase/alanine transaminase, lactate, and injury severity score in trauma

Standard and routine clinical blood tests at the time of admission provide critical information about trauma pathophysiology such as organ injury, coagulopathy, and tissue metabolism. As shown in Figure 5A, the cohort of patients with trauma at the early stage exhibited only limited secondary organ injury with elevated AST and ALT, two liver injury markers, and serum lactate, a sensitive indicator of tissue ischemia and anaerobic metabolism. Most of the patients with trauma only exhibited normal coagulation function as evidenced by normal PTT, PT, and INR, and normal kidney function with normal creatinine and BUN. While white blood cell counts were abnormally high in injured patients, platelets and hemoglobin remained normal. Clinical injury markers that remained within the normal range showed no correlation with plasma miRNA expression, whereas with the injury markers that were elevated at admission, the miRNA biomarkers exhibited a close correlation, including AST/ALT, lactate, and somewhat with WBC (Figure 5B). Importantly, with the exception of miR-224-5p and let-7c-5p which did not exhibit severity-dependent elevation (Figure 3B), the miRNA biomarkers were closely correlated with the patients’ overall injury severity score (ISS), which was based on injury severity in 6 different body regions and usually calculated at the late stage when patients were discharged from the trauma center (Figure 5B).

Prediction of trauma pathophysiological responses using miRNA biomarkers

In a cohort of 34 trauma and 17 healthy controls, the Random Forest model demonstrated that the panel of 12-miRNA biomarkers could distinguish the trauma and healthy groups with a cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (CV AUROC) of 0.98 [95% CI, 0.95–1] (Figure 6A). When the upper 98% values of the injury markers in the control group were used as the classifiers, the miRNA panel demonstrated a strong performance in identifying various trauma injury markers in brain injury, coagulation, inflammation and endothelial activation with the respective CV AUROC values of 0.90 [0.80–0.98] (Enolase 2), 0.97 [0.95–1] (S100B), 0.85 [0.76–0.94] (P-selectin) and 0.86 [0.94–0.99] (D-dimer), 0.97 [0.93–1] (IL-6), 0.93 [0.86–0.98] (IL-8), and 0.96 [0.93–1] (VCAM-1) and 0.97 [0.93–1] (vWF-A2) (Figure 6A, solid red line). The predictive performance of the models was further validated in a separate set of trauma (n = 14) and healthy (n = 7) cohorts, where high CV AUROCs were also achieved (Figure 6A, green dashed line). In comparison and as a negative control, demographic factors such as age and sex had a weaker performance with the CV AUROC between 0.55 and 0.67 (Figure 6A, gray line). Not surprisingly, for some injury markers that were unchanged or only slightly elevated in the trauma cohort − such as the lung injury markers ANG1 and ANG2, tissue factor, TNFα, and syndecan-1 − the miRNA biomarker panel did not perform as well as the ones noted above (Figure S4A).

Furthermore, we evaluated the predictive performance of the 12-miRNA panel in distinguishing between severe and moderate injury. As illustrated in Figure 6B, the CV AUROC values were 0.72 [0.55–0.88] for severe injury (based on their injury severity score (ISS) > 15)32 and severe brain injury 0.83 [0.66–1] (Enolase 2) and 0.91 [0.81–0.99] (S100B). Similarly, the CV AUROC values for liver injury were 0.84 [0.71–0.96] (ALT), 0.79 [0.63–0.94] (AST), for inflammation, they were 0.70 [0.50–0.90] (IL-6) and 0.67 [0.45–0.90] (IL-8). For metabolic acidosis, it was 0.72 [0.57–0.88] (lactate), and for coagulation indicator D-dimer, it was 0.71 [0.50–0.92]. Again, age and sex as a reference were not associated with the normality and severity of various organ injury and had near 0.5 AUROC values, indicating their poor performance in the trauma injury marker prediction. The severity-prediction model of the panel of miRNA biomarkers performed similarly in the validation set (Figure 6B, green dashed line). In comparison, the miRNA biomarker panel performed poorly to distinguish the levels of coagulation factors (P-selectin, tissue factor, PTT, PT, INR), endothelial cell activation markers (syndecan-1 and VCAM-1), and lung injury markers (ANG1 and ANG2) within trauma cohorts likely due to the lack of either substantial post-trauma responses or severity-dependent increase (Figure S4B). Finally, we evaluated whether ISS could distinguish the severity of the quantitative injury markers. The results indicated that ISS had a partial success in distinguishing brain injury severity (Enolase2, AUROC = 0.70), plasma lactate (0.74), D-dimer (0.80) and IL6 (0.71) (Figure S5).

The miRNA biomarker panel exhibits disease-specific performance in trauma vs. sepsis

We tested the performance of the 12-miRNA panel of trauma biomarkers in the trauma and sepsis cohorts. Our published plasma small RNAseq results10,25,26 revealed that among 212 upregulated miRNAs among the trauma and sepsis cohorts, only 51 (24.1%) of them were shared by both groups, whereas 100 (47.2%) were uniquely upregulated in the trauma cohort (Figure 7A). Specifically, the 12-miRNA biomarkers were markedly upregulated in patients with trauma but only modestly in patients with sepsis (Figure 7B). Digital PCR quantification of the plasma miRNAs in the trauma (trauma, n = 48; control, n = 24) and sepsis (sepsis, n = 47; control, n = 24) cohorts confirmed their preferential upregulation in the trauma cohort (Figure 7C). Principal component analysis (PCA) based on the 12-miRNA panel (Figure 7D) revealed a partial separation between patients with trauma and sepsis, suggesting distinct molecular signatures. In contrast, PCA using conventional biomarkers, such as IL-6,33,34,35,36 tissue factor,37 and lactate,38 did not differentiate the two patient groups, as their expression patterns largely overlapped (Figure 7E). Notably, the variance explained by the first principal component (PC1) was substantially greater for the 12-miRNA panel (64.4%) compared to the conventional biomarker panel (45.9%), indicating that the miRNA panel provides a more robust discriminative signal between trauma and sepsis. These findings suggest that the 12-miRNA panel captures biological differences between the cohorts more effectively than traditional trauma and sepsis markers.

Next, we evaluated the predictive ability of the two miRNAs – miR-145-5p and miR-224-5p – in the cohort of trauma vs. healthy controls. The CV AUROC of the two miRNAs in identifying trauma was 0.88 [0.77–1] and 0.98 [0.85–1.00], respectively, whereas their CV AUROC values were significantly lower in sepsis at 0.68 [0.63–0.90] and 0.56 [0.47–0.78], respectively (Figures 8A and 8B). Similarly, for the prediction of organ injury as measured by various injury markers of brain injury, coagulation, inflammation, and endothelial cell (EC) activation, miR-145-5p and miR-224-5p performed consistently better in trauma with significantly higher CV AUROC values than in those of sepsis cohorts (Figures 8A and 8B). In contrast, IL-6 showed similar predictive CV AUROC values in diagnosis and organ injury in trauma and sepsis cohorts (Figure 8C). In comparison, tissue factor showed moderate to weak prediction performance in both trauma and sepsis diagnosis and organ injury.

Finally, we performed ROC analysis to evaluate the diagnostic performance of these biomarkers in the combined cohort of patients with trauma and sepsis, and compared them with that of IL-6, TF, and lactate.39 The selected miRNAs – miR-224-5p and miR-145-5p – showed a strong performance with CV AUROC of 0.91 and 0.90 (Figure 9) in differentiating trauma from patients with sepsis. The cut-off value of miR-224-5p for the differential diagnosis of patients with trauma vs. sepsis is 1.8 × 105 copies/mL plasma, with a sensitivity of 85% and a specificity of 87%, whereas the cut-off value of miR-145-5p for the same diagnosis is 1.0 × 107 copies/mL, with sensitivity and specificity both at 89%. Importantly, in comparison with the two miRNAs, IL-6, tissue factor, and lactate exhibited significantly lower CV AUROC values in their performance of differential diagnosis.

Discussion

Our findings provide proof-of-concept that a panel of 12 proinflammatory miRNAs − abundantly upregulated in patients with trauma and possessing the specific nucleotide motifs in their sequences − may serve as biomarkers for traumatic injury. These miRNAs are closely linked to key pathophysiological responses such as systemic inflammation, coagulation, and endothelial activation, and demonstrate strong potential for predicting clinical injury severity and early trauma responses, as evidenced by a diverse group of sensitive protein injury markers. Notably, these injury markers and mediators, while not standard clinical laboratory tests, offer an important molecular lens into the wide spectrum of subclinical pathophysiology informative for trauma response state and disease processes. These findings support the notion that plasma proinflammatory ex-miRNAs and the subsequent innate immune responses are the molecular drivers that may be responsible for some of the key pathophysiological responses to traumatic injury, such as inflammation, coagulopathy, endothelial activation, and secondary organ injury.10,25,40

Numerous trauma biomarkers have been reported, most of which are linked to the downstream pathophysiology, such as cell injury, endothelial shedding, coagulopathy, and organ dysfunction.39,41,42,43,44 For example, several proposed trauma biomarkers, such as IL-6, TNFα, C-reactive protein (CRP), and syndecan-1, are products of innate immune activation and endothelial injury in response to traumatic injury or infection. As such, they are often non-specific and unable to distinguish between infectious and non-infectious critical conditions, such as sepsis and trauma. This limitation underscores the need for novel trauma biomarkers that are disease-specific and capable of predicting organ dysfunction and adverse outcomes with greater precision and specificity. In other instances, proteins and nucleic acids released from injured cells following trauma are proposed as biomarkers. For example, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase-L1 (UCH-L1), S100B, and neuron-specific enolase (NSE), are reported as biomarkers45 and have been investigated for their diagnostic and prognostic values in TBI46,47,48,49,50,51 and non-TBI polytrauma.52,53,54,55 Several studies have shown that plasma miRNAs are differentially expressed upon TBI and have been proposed as biomarkers.50,56,57,58 Using miRNA array and Luminex multiplex assay, Vincente and colleagues report a close correlation between a few selected plasma miRNAs and clinical parameters in patients with polytrauma.27

In our study, we selected the panel of 12 plasma miRNAs as biomarker candidates based on the three criteria: (1) their large upregulation in patients with trauma as compared with healthy subjects, (2) their high abundance in the plasma samples, and (3) the presence of pro-inflammatory motifs in their nucleotide sequences. The computer searching algorithm-based machine learning strategy enables us to identify and validate the five motifs that offer strong prediction for the pro-inflammatory function of the tested miRNAs with excellent sensitivity and moderate specificity. Mechanistically, it is noteworthy that the five essential motifs all include a UU dimer. Recent crystallographic studies demonstrate that TLR7 functions as a dual receptor for guanosine- and uridine-containing single-stranded (ss) RNAs. Successive uridine-containing ssRNAs bind TLR7 with high affinity, whereas single-uridine–containing ssRNAs exhibit reduced binding affinities.59,60 Thus, UU-containing motifs may promote miRNA binding to and activation of TLR7, thereby enhancing the pro-inflammatory function of miRNAs. For example, the importance of the UU dimer within the UU … U motif is demonstrated by the loss of miR-146a-5p–stimulated, TLR7-dependent inflammatory cytokine production when the motif is mutated to AU … U or UA … U..26 Moreover, while the conventional role of cellular miRNA is to regulate gene expression by binding to target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation and translation inhibition, these 12 extracellular plasma ss miRNAs are less likely to function as gene regulator in target cells. Our previous mechanistic studies have shown that double-stranded (ds) miR-146a duplex mimic, but not mature ss miR-146a-5p, binds to Ago-2 − the effector of RISC (RNA-induced silencing complex) − when added to cultured macrophages, and subsequently induces the degradation of its target gene, irak-1.25,26 Conversely, it is extracellular ss miR-146a-5p, not ds miR-146a duplex, that is able to induce cytokine production in vitro in a TLR7-dependent manner. Thus, the identification of these specific motifs represents a unique strategy that links the miRNA sequences and their proinflammatory property. Combined with RNA-seq and digital PCR quantification of plasma miRNAs, nucleotide motifs could be considered to identify proinflammatory miRNAs for biomarker selection and potential therapeutic intervention in trauma and other clinical conditions where innate immune activation and inflammation are a critical part of disease pathogenesis, such as sepsis,61 shock,3,61 reperfusion injury,62,63 neurodegeneration,64 pain,65 and cancer metastasis.66

Our data demonstrated a disease-specific performance of the miRNA biomarker panel in trauma and sepsis diagnosis and injury prediction. RNAseq revealed distinct plasma miRNA profiles between the two critical illnesses, with most of the 12 miRNAs showing greater upregulation in trauma as compared to sepsis, highlighting a trauma-specific miRNA signature. The PCA data suggest a better discriminatory power of the miRNA panel between trauma and sepsis when compared with the conventional biomarkers such as IL-6, tissue factor, and lactate, which were completely overlapping between the two cohorts of patients. Moreover, two of the miRNA candidates, miR-224-5p and miR-145-5p, achieved high diagnostic accuracy in the combined cohort of trauma and sepsis, with AUROCs of 0.91 and 0.90, respectively, which significantly outperformed IL-6, tissue factor, and lactate. Thus, these miRNAs exhibited trauma-specific predictive ability for diagnosis of organ injuries compared to sepsis, whereas IL-6 showed a much similar performance in both conditions, reflecting its broad role as a pleiotropic inflammatory mediator commonly seen in both trauma and sepsis. Enhanced disease specificity of biomarkers could enable more precise clinical diagnoses and targeted therapeutic strategies.

Our data revealed a diverse group of physiological and pathophysiological injury markers in the cohort of patients with trauma that are linked to liver injury, brain injury, coagulation activation, systemic inflammation, and endothelial activation. It is noteworthy that traumatic brain injury (TBI) was excluded from the trauma cohort based on clinical presentation and diagnostic tests. The increase in the injury markers – S100B and enolase – in these patients may reflect secondary, subclinical brain injury following non-TBI trauma. There was no clinically recognizable coagulopathy or acute kidney injury in these patients, given the normal INR and BUN/Creatinine at the time of admission, although plasma P-selectin, tissue factor, and D-dimer were markedly increased, reflecting an active pro-coagulation process at the early stage of injury. In fact, majority of the patients (63%) had relatively mild injury with ISS ≤ 15. The samples were collected very early at the time of arrival before clinical coagulopathy and acute kidney injury could potentially develop. Nevertheless, the finding that the plasma miRNA biomarkers exhibit a strong correlation with the various protein injury markers suggests a potential mechanistic link between the plasma miRNAs and these pathophysiological responses and supports previous reports that extracellular miRNA and its downstream TLR7 signaling may play an important role in inflammatory response,26 endothelial dysfunction,67 organ injury,10,25,26,67,68 and coagulopathy69 during sepsis and trauma.10,25

The current study provides a proof-of-concept that the 12 miRNA biomarker candidates – selected based on their abundant upregulation in plasma and unique motif structures – confer strong abilities in differentiating patients with trauma and sepsis and concurrently predicting clinical injury severity scores, coagulation, endothelial activation, and cytokine storm. If validated in future prospective studies, the plasma miRNA biomarkers could help identify individuals who are at risk for secondary multi-organ injury and adverse clinical outcomes such as coagulopathy and mortality following severe injury, and as such, to guide individualized clinical trauma management.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations exist in the current study. While the ROC data in the training set was validated in a separate cohort, the sample size in both cohorts was relatively small. The small sample size could impact the ROC analyses and performance assessment of the predictability of the miRNA biomarkers. An external and larger trauma cohort will be needed to further test the utility of the miRNA biomarkers to generalize their clinical application. Moreover, patients with blunt trauma in this study had relatively modest severity without associated mortality. While various early and sensitive markers (e.g., S100B, tissue factor, D-dimer, IL-6, vWF-A2) significantly increased following the initial trauma, most clinical, perhaps less sensitive, parameters related to organ injury (e.g., BUN, Creatinine, PTT, PT, INR) remained within the normal ranges at time of trauma center arrival when blood samples were drawn. Analyzing longitudinal plasma samples in more severely injured patients in a future prospective study will help quantify the ongoing development of trauma-induced secondary organ injury and other adverse clinical outcomes and further evaluate the prognostic value of these novel miRNA biomarkers. Finally, the lack of longitudinal data in this study was a limitation. While our statistical modeling provides valuable information about the ability of these miRNA biomarkers in identifying the concurrent pathophysiological responses following trauma, their values in predicting the future secondary organ injury will need to be tested in a longitudinal study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

This prospective observational trauma cohort study was conducted at the Shock Trauma Center of the University of Maryland. The inclusion criteria include: 1) patients at age between 18-45 of male and female, 2) patients with serious injury defined as one of the following: blunt injury to chest, abdomen, or pelvis, two or more long-bone fractures (tibia, femur, or humerus), and traumatic amputation proximal to wrist or ankle. Exclusion criteria include: 1) vulnerable population (pregnancy and prisoners), 2) two or more systemic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking, 3) penetrating injury to the chest or abdomen, 4) unknown time of injury, 5) suspected toxic ingestion, 6) traumatic brain injury (TBI), CT evidence of intracranial hemorrhage, 7) burns > 50% BSA, and 8) immunosuppressed. For the healthy cohort, age and sex-matched between age 18-45 were recruited with the exclusion criteria of 1) vulnerable population and 2) subject with two or more systemic diseases. Informed consent was obtained for each subject or their legally authorized representative. Patients were treated according to standard of care per institutional and national guidelines. We divided the study cohort by allocating 70% of the first-recruited trauma patients (n=34) and age- and sex-matched healthy controls (n=17) to the Training set and the remaining 30% of trauma patients (n=14) and healthy controls (n=7) to the Validation set. Sepsis patients (n=47) were diagnosed based on Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores at the time of intensive care unit admission. The enrollment criteria for the sepsis cohort include 1) Patients with a clinical, surgical, or radiological identified source of infection, 2) having organ dysfunction with SOFA score ≥2, and 3) between the ages of 18 and 80 years. Exclusion criteria include vulnerable populations (e.g., pregnancy, cardiac arrest, critical illness with no infection, hematological malignancy). For control, we recruited age/sex-matched subjects with no more than one systemic disease condition such as diabetes, hypertension, or COPD. Sepsis was diagnosed at the time of ICU admission.

Animal use

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. After euthanized under general anesthesia, mouse bone marrow cells were isolated from femur and tibia under sterile conditions as described previously.10

Bone marrow-derived macrophages

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were prepared from eight to 12-week-old male mice as described previously.26 Bone marrow cells from the tibias and femurs were suspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 5% horse serum,10 ng/ml M-CSF, and penicillin/streptomycin at a density of 2×106/mL and cultured in 96-well plate at 100 μL/well. Two days after incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2, culture medium was changed once before miRNA treatment.

Raw 264.7 macrophage cell line

Murine macrophage cell line Raw 264.7 were cultured in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplied with L-glutamine,10% and penicillin/streptomycin at a density of 3×105/mL and cultured in 96-well plate at 100 μL/well.

Method details

Computer searching algorithm for miRNA pro-inflammatory motif identification

The algorithm consists of three main components: building a motif list from a training set of 33 miRNAs, selecting pro-inflammatory motifs, and executing an automated motif-finding algorithm to narrow down to the most essential motifs. 1) Motifs ranging from two to ten nucleotides long within miRNA sequences in the training dataset were generated, including those with wildcards. 2) Three exclusion criteria were applied to the motif list; First, motifs that appear in non-inflammatory miRNA sequences; Second, motifs that showed up in less than 25% of pro-inflammatory miRNAs in the training set were excluded. Third, duplicate motifs defined by the same core sequence with differently existed wildcards on either end of the core sequence will be assess for their fitness in the training set, if the performance of duplicates were the same, sequence with the shortest length will be kept. 3) Finally, an automated motif-finding algorithm to pinpoint the most relevant motifs linked to inflammatory responses were developed based on a penalized LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regression model. A 5-fold cross-validation was used to tune the hyperparameter, Lambda, that controls the strength of the L1 regularization term in the LASSO model was adjusted so that the model retained the highest F1 performance score while utilizing the minimal number of non-zero coefficient motifs. Detailed workflow for the motif algorithm can be found in Table S1.

Patient clinical data

For trauma patients, blood samples were obtained at time of arrival. De-identified patients’ demographic and clinical information was obtained for further analysis. The clinical information included patients’ demographics, injury mechanism and pattern. The clinical metrics included injury severity score (ISS), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (Alk phos), total bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), lactate, prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), international normalized ratio (INR), and platelet count (PLT), white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), and potassium (K).

Plasma processing

Whole blood samples were collected in K2-EDTA tube and centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 minutes at room temperature. Supernatants were centrifuged at 1000 x g for 10 minutes at 4°C, transferred, and centrifuged again at 14,000 x g for 10 minutes at 4°C. Plasma was stored at -80°C until further use.

Plasma RNA extraction and miRNA quantification by digital PCR

Plasma RNA was extracted using Qiagen miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Advanced Kit. Specifically, after adding lysis buffer, 250 μL of human EDTA-plasma was mixed with the spiked-in cel-miR-39-3p and proceeded to RNA purification. Purified RNA was eluted in 20 μL nuclease-free water. Six μL of RNA was used for cDNA synthesis using Qiagen miRCURY LNA RT Kit. The LNA-enhanced primers were designed for Qiagen miRCURY LNA miRNA PCR Assays, and miRNA copy numbers were quantified by a digital PCR platform from Qiagen (QIAcuity One). To calculate the recovery rate and correct for the loss of miRNA during plasma RNA extraction, an equal quantity of the spike-in cel-miR-39-3p was used for cDNA synthesis, quantified using digital PCR without going through the RNA extraction step, and termed as the “added spike-in”. The plasma concentration of target miRNA (copies/mL) was calculated as: detected miRNA copies × normalizing factor (added spike-in / detected spike-in copies) / plasma volume used.

miRNA mimics

Single-stranded miRNA mimics were synthesized as the same ribonucleotide sequence as human mature miRNAs with modification of phosphorothioate linkages in between the ribose to enhance nuclease resistance and cell transportation. MiRNA mimics were synthesized from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) and reconstituted in RNase-free H2O.

miRNA treatment of cells

Cells were incubated for 2 hours with serum-free medium supplemented with 0.05% BSA. Synthetic miRNA mimics were packaged with Lipofectamine 3000 before added to cell cultures. For Test set 1 and Test set 2, miRNAs were titrated at the concentrations between 3 - 500 nM in cultured media. Eighteen hours later, media were assayed for CXCL2 production using ELISA. miRNAs that induced 1) a dose-dependent increase of CXCL2 in culture medium and 2) at least 1.5-fold increase CXCL2 production were defined as pro-inflammatory miRNAs.

Luminex multiplex for plasma marker quantification

The concentrations of plasma protein analytes were determined by Luminex Assay (R&D systems), a fluorescent bead-based multiplex immunoassay platform. The analyte panel specifically designed for this study included S100B (S100 calcium-binding protein B), enolase2, ANG1 (angiopoietin1), ANG2 (angiopoietin2), P-selectin, tissue factor, d-dimer, IL-6 (interleukin-6), TNFα (tumor necrosis factor alpha), IL-8 (interleukin-8), CXCL2 (Chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 2), Syndecan-1, vWF-A2 (von Willebrand factor A2 domain) and VCAM1 (vascular cell adhesion protein 1). Plasma samples were thawed on ice and centrifuged at 12,000 x g for 10 min. The supernatants were applied to the antibody-conjugated magnetic bead and proceeded further following the manufacturer’s instructions. The fluorescent antibody reaction was measured on a Luminex 200 machine from Luminex xMAP Technology and the analysis was performed with the Luminex xPONENT software.

Predictive modeling and performance evaluation

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic and predictive performance of miRNA biomarkers. To assess their ability to distinguish trauma vs. controls on the panel of injury markers, the binarization thresholds were defined by the 97.5th percentile of values in the control cohort. To evaluate the miRNA biomarkers’ ability to predict disease severity, injury markers from the Luminex assays or clinical data were binarized based on the corresponding median values within the trauma or sepsis cohort. Random Forest model of all 12 miRNA biomarker candidates to each marker was built. Model performance was assessed using 2-fold cross-validation (CV) repeated 100 times. The mean sensitivity and specificity across CV iterations were used to generate Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROC) curves and calculate the Area Under the ROC curve (AUROC) and the corresponding confidence intervals (95% CI). DeLong’s test was used to provide a formal statistical comparison between two AUROCs.

Data cleaning

Luminex data under detection levels were set to 0. Missing clinical data were noted N/A and skipped in plotting and analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Software and packages

R Statistical Software (v4.4.0), package pROC (v1.18.5), ROCR (v1.0-11), randomForest (v4.7-1.1), dplyr (v1.0.10), ggplot2 (v3.5.1), ComplexHeatmap (v2.20.0) and ComplexUpset (v1.3.3), Prism GraphPad 10 were used for statistical analysis and data plotting. Power calculation

Based on our plasma RNA-Seq data from a small cohort of severely injured patients (N=10) and age and sex matched healthy controls (N=10),10 the statistical power reached 0.86 when miRNA biomarkers were measured in the cohorts of 48 trauma patients and 24 controls assuming standard deviation of 12% and a minimum difference in means of 30% between groups.

Statistics analysis

All data analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v4.4.0). Data were tested for normality with QQ plot and Shapiro-wilk test. For variable with a normal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk test, p > 0.05) in all comparison groups, a t-test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction was used. For non-normally distributed variables, a Wilcoxon test with Holm-Bonferroni correction was used. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r) was used to assess the correlations between miRNA and measured variables. Statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends. The statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. Asterisks in the figures were defined as ∗∗∗∗, p<0.0001; ∗∗∗, p<0.001; ∗∗, p<0.01; ∗, p<0.05.
