Phenotypic and biochemical diversity of indigenous apricot (Prunus armeniaca L.) genetic resources
Authors: Aitazaz Alam, Muhammad Tahir Akram, Muhammad Mumtaz Khan, Muhammad Moaaz Ali, Rashad Qadri, Abdul Haseeb Ahmed, Khalid F Almutairi, Lidia Sas-Paszt, Walid F A Mosa, Iqra Mubeen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApricot is a globally cultivated temperate fruit with high nutritional and economic value. Gilgit Baltistan (GB), Pakistan, is a major apricot-producing region with unique geographic surroundings and rich germplasm diversity. However, the indigenous apricot accessions in this area have not yet been scientifically explored. This study provides the first integrated evaluation of 19 local apricot accessions using multivariate statistical analysis, including descriptive statistics, principal component analysis, correlation analysis, and hierarchical cluster analysis, to comprehensively assess morphological, pomological, and biochemical diversity among indigenous apricot accessions for conservation and crop improvement, and to identify elite accessions with promising horticultural and nutritional traits. The results revealed substantial variations in morphological traits, skin cracking susceptibility (56.270%), followed by texture of flesh (55.110%), flesh color (52.830%), over color pattern (49.970%), and harvesting maturity (49.430%). In correlation analysis, significant positive correlations were observed among key morphological and quality traits, including suture size with overcolor (r = 0.67) and fruit color with flesh color (r = 0.65), while harvesting maturity showed a negative correlation with color pattern (r = − 0.61), and stone separation was negatively associated with flesh juiciness (r = − 0.60). In biochemical assessments, notable diversity was recorded in total soluble solids (11.393–24.267 °Brix), titratable acidity (0.438–0.698%), total phenolic contents (27.721–121.078 mg GAE/100 g), total flavonoid contents (80.738–154.075 mg QE/g), reducing sugar (5.432–13.452%), total sugar (7.775–19.578%), and non-reducing sugar (2.172–6.448%). Furthermore, total lycopene contents (TLC), total anthocyanin contents (TAC), antioxidant activity (AOA), and total tannin contents (TTC) showed varied amounts from 2.880–19.720 mg/kg, 77.941–122.412 mg L⁻¹, 50.340–94.100%, 1.660–20.609 mg TAE/100 g, respectively, highlighting the unique nutraceutical potential. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) exhibited 78.582% of the total variability, highlighting fruit size, sugar composition, antioxidant-related traits, and color attributes as major contributors to diversity. In the biochemical correlation analysis, the strongest positive correlation was observed among total sugars, reducing sugars, non-reducing sugars, lycopene content, and crude fiber. Based on agronomic and biochemical quantitative traits, hierarchical cluster analysis divided these accessions into three clusters, and ‘Yakyar’ and ‘Hawalpa’ were identified as the most diverse accessions in biochemical characteristics. This study is novel in providing the first comprehensive phenotypic and biochemical characterization of GB apricot germplasm, revealing highly diverse and nutritionally valuable accessions that can be targeted for breeding, conservation, and functional food development.Keywords: Characterization, Correlation analysis, Diversity, Pomological

Introduction

Apricot (Prunus armeniaca L.) is an eminent stone fruit that belongs to the family “Rosaceae” and genus “Prunus” [1]. It originated from Armenia, was domesticated in Central Asia, and later spread across Eastern and Western regions [2]. It’s a highly diverse fruit crop, being cultivated in varied climates including cold regions of Siberia, subtropical regions of North Africa, deserts of Central Asia, and humid areas of Japan and eastern China [3]. The major apricot-producing countries are China, Iran, Pakistan, Uzbekistan, Morocco, Algeria, Ukraine, and the USA [4]. Pakistan, located in South Asia, strategically lies at the intersection of South Asia, the Middle East, and Central Asia, sharing borders with India to the east, Iran and Afghanistan to the west and northwest, respectively.

Pakistan is the 7th largest apricot-producing country, with an annual production of approximately 169,000 tons, and the provinces Gilgit Baltistan, Baluchistan, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) are the largest contributors of apricot production in Pakistan [5, 6]. However, Gilgit Baltistan (GB) is the most prominent of these provinces in apricot production, producing 170,680 tons annually [6]. Geographically, this area lies in northern Pakistan and is bordered by the Himalayas, the Karakoram, the Hindukush, and the Pamir Mountains. It shared its northwest border with Afghanistan, its northern border with China, its south and southeast borders with Jammu and Kashmir, India, and its southwest border with Azad Kashmir, Pakistan. This area has a rich diversity of wild and native apricots. In GB, apricots are traditionally cultivated by natives as a vital source of livelihood and are commonly dried for use during the cold season. Apricots are widely grown throughout this region along roadsides, in parks, at ancient forts, in courtyards, and in vacant spaces, making them an integral part of this province’s culture and landscape.

Apricot is also known as the “Golden egg of the sun” due to its appealing morphological attributes, including color, aroma, sweet taste, and attractive fruit size [7]. Additionally, it is a rich source of health-beneficial phytochemicals comprised of antioxidants (phenolic contents, carotenoids, organic acids, tannins, etc.), sugars (fructose, glucose, and sucrose), vitamins (vitamins A, C, and E), and minerals such as calcium, potassium, magnesium, manganese, sulfur, iron, and sodium [8]. It prevents diabetes, fatigue, neurosis, depression, and other diseases caused by deficiencies of trace elements [9]. The presence of lycopene in apricots makes them anticarcinogenic and reduces blood cholesterol levels, thus helping prevent cardiovascular disease. The antioxidants in apricots make them anti-inflammatory and anti-cerebrovascular diseases and prevent non-communicable diseases [10]. Traditionally, apricots are used to treat diseases such as tumors, cough, fever, anemia, bronchitis, Alzheimer’s, hemorrhage, and to improve eyesight, fertility, and spasms [11].

Based on geographical zones, scientists have divided apricots into six ecological zones, including the Central Asian ecological group (CAG), the European ecological group (EG), the Dzhungar-Ili ecological group (DZG), the North China ecological group (NCG), the Northeast Asian ecological group (NAG) and the East China ecological group (ECG) [12]. However, these zones show distinct differences in morphological and physiological traits, with apricots well adapted to the specific ecological conditions of each zone [13]. It is reported that CAGs are rich in apricot diversity and there is significant variation in fruit (size, color, and flesh), sugar contents, flowering habit, and they are tolerant to cold, drought, and salinity [12]. Among stone fruits, apricots exhibit high genetic diversity across accessions and cultivation regions.

Assessing genetic diversity is critical for selecting superior and elite accessions [14]. The intense shifts in climatic patterns have signified the need to save the depleting precious apricot germplasm resources from extinction. Wild plant germplasm has always been recognized as a great source of variation, better adaptation and resiliency to climate change. In fruit crops, local accessions and landraces are largely chosen because they exhibit excellent taste and aroma, in addition to other important traits [15]. The diversity within germplasm helps evaluate the existing accessions. Morphological, biochemical, and genetic characterizations are essential tools for the identification, classification, and comparative analysis of plant traits among accessions [16]. Moreover, diversity augments the breeding programs for the development of climate-resilient, high-yielding accessions with improved fruit traits. Knowledge of plant diversity helps in investigating evolutionary history and the genetic relationships between accessions.

Despite the recognized importance of apricot genetic resources in Central and South Asia, the comprehensive characterization of indigenous Gilgit Baltistan (GB) is unexplored and limited. This study integrates with a comprehensive evaluation of combined morphological qualitative, quantitative, and detailed biochemical analysis of indigenous apricot accessions from this mountainous region. Further, multivariate analysis in this experiment illustrates key traits driving diversity and elite accessions with superior accessions for breeding and conservation.

In the context of global sustainability challenges, the conservation and utilization of plant genetic resources play a vital role in achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Apricot, as a nutritionally rich fruit crop, has significant potential to contribute to food security and sustainable agriculture in mountainous regions such as Gilgit Baltistan. The characterization of indigenous apricot germplasm is essential for identifying resilient and high-quality genotypes that can support crop improvement under changing climatic conditions. Therefore, this study aligns with SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) by enhancing food and nutritional security, SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production) through the sustainable use of genetic resources, and SDG 15 (Life on Land) by promoting biodiversity conservation. This study also offers new insights into the adaptation and nutritional profiling of GB apricots, supporting the strategic utilization of climate-resilient accessions.

As the trees were grown under natural field conditions, their exact ages varied. Therefore, only healthy, mature, and actively bearing trees with relatively uniform vigor were selected to ensure consistency in observations. Three trees per accession were selected. The selection was based on perennial fruit crop studies, where multiple trees are used as biological replicates to minimize error chances as this sample size is sufficient to represent genetic consistency under similar environmental conditions. For morphological characterization, leaf traits, including leaf blade base shape, leaf margin and leaf base apex angle were noted. For pomological characterization, 30 fruits per plant were harvested at horticultural maturity from selected accessions and transported in an icebox container to the Vegetable Physiology and Precision Agriculture Lab, Department of Horticulture, PMAS Arid Agriculture University, to prevent deterioration. The pomological traits included were harvesting maturity, fruit color, separation of stone, cavity depth, suture, apex, fruit attractiveness, flesh color, fruit-eating quality, aroma, fruit juiciness, flesh texture, skin cracking susceptibility, stone shape, stone surface, flesh color, fruit shape, ground color, over color pattern and over color were noted. These traits were examined according to the guidelines described in the apricot descriptors of UPOV and IBPGR [17, 18]. It was observed that a few fruits of some accessions were damaged during postharvest handling. For accuracy, twenty fruits of individual accessions were examined, and means were noted to maintain uniformity across accessions and to avoid bias due to within-tree variation, ensuring adequate sample size for statistical analysis.

In quantitative assessments, fruit agronomic traits including fruit size, fruit length, fruit thickness, fruit volume, fruit weight, pulp weight, stone weight, stone size, Pulp/stone ratio, leaf blade length, leaf blade apex length, petiole length, number of nectarines, leaf area, leaf length, leaf width and color readings through chroma readings (L, a*, and b* values) were measured [19]. For biochemical assessment, total phenolic contents, total flavonoid contents, antioxidant activity, catalase, peroxidase, Superoxide dismutase, total anthocyanin content, reducing sugar, total sugar, non-reducing sugar, total carotenoid contents, protein, total tannin content, Chlorophyll a (chl-a), Chlorophyll b (chl-b), titratable acidity, pH, ascorbic acid, total soluble solids and total lycopene contents were analyzed. The digital fruit firmness tester (Wagner Fruit Firmness Tester model FT-327), having an 8 mm plunger tip, was used to measure fruit firmness, and its unit was expressed in kg/cm². For the biochemical analysis, the harvested fruits were crushed in a juicer machine and then passed through a muslin cloth. The extracted juice samples were then poured into plastic bottles. These samples were stored in a refrigerator/ultra-freezer. The frozen juice from each germplasm was then used according to the parameter protocols.

Total soluble solids (°Brix), pH, and titratable acidity (%)

In biochemical analysis, the total soluble solids of samples were assessed by following as defined by Juhnevica-Radenkova [20]. In this method, fruit juice was poured into a digital refractometer, and the reading was recorded in °Brix. The pH and titratable acidity of each accession were measured [21].

Total phenolic contents (mg GAE/100 g)

The total phenolic contents (TPCs) were determined through the Folin–Ciocalteu method [22]. To assess the total phenolic content in apricot accessions, an aliquot of 1 mL of extracted juice and 1 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (diluted 1:10) was prepared in a conical flask. After 5 min, 2 ml Na2CO3 (7.5%) with distilled water was added to bring the volume to 25 ml, and incubated for 2 h. Later, the absorbance was measured at 765 nm using the spectrophotometer (model U-2900, SR No. 26E82-018), and the results were reported as mg GAE/100 g.

Total carotenoid content (mg/g) and chlorophyll (mg/g)

Chlorophyll and total carotenoid contents were determined using the methodology explained by Karatas et al. [23]. In this method, 1 ml of fruit sample was homogenized with 5 ml of acetone (80%) using a cold porcelain mortar on an ice bath. The resulting mixture was blended with 1 ml of sodium sulfate, filtered, and then acetone was added to make a 10 ml solution. This reaction mixture was centrifuged at 2600 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Absorbance was measured at 470, 645, and 663 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Total flavonoid contents (mg QE/g)

The total flavonoid content (TFC) of apricots was determined following the protocol of Zloch et al. [24]. In this method, 1 mL of the extracted sample was taken in Eppendorf tubes, along with the addition of 100 µL aluminum chloride (10%) and 100 µL sodium carbonate (10%). After mixing, double-distilled water was added to bring the volume to 3 ml. The resulting mixture was then kept at room temperature for 60 min and absorbance was measured at 415 nm using the spectrophotometer (model U-2900, SR No. 26E82-018).

Ascorbic acid (mg/100 g) and antioxidant activity (%)

To analyze the ascorbic acid content in apricot accessions, 10 ml of the extracted juice sample and 5 ml of diluted HCl (1%) were mixed in a beaker. Subsequently, the 1.5 ml was poured into Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 30 °C. Then, 1 ml of supernatant was taken in the 3 ml cuvette and absorbance was recorded at 243 nm on a spectrophotometer (model U-2900, SR No. 26E82-018) and its unit was expressed in mg/100 g [25]. The antioxidant activity was measured following the protocol described by Akram [21] and was expressed as a percentage (%).

Total anthocyanin content (mg L⁻¹ )

The total anthocyanin content was determined using the pH-differential method [26]. To calculate the anthocyanin content in apricot juice, polyphenol extracts were diluted separately with 0.025 mol L⁻¹ hydrochloric acid–potassium chloride buffer (pH 1.0) and 0.4 mol L⁻¹ sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5). 3.8 mL of each buffer solution was mixed with 200 µl of the extracted juice sample in test tubes. After mixing thoroughly, samples were kept in a water bath at 20 °C for 60 min, and the absorbance was measured at 530 nm and 700 nm on a spectrophotometer (Model: SP-3000 plus Optima, Japan).

Lycopene content (mg/kg)

In this method, 0.6 g of fruit sample, 5 ml ethanol (95%), 5 ml acetone, and 10 ml hexane were kept in an ice bath and vortexed at 200 rpm by using an orbital shaker for 20 min. Afterward, 3 ml of distilled water was added, vortexed for 5 min to promote phase separation, and the solution was left for 5 min. The supernatant was taken, and absorbance was measured at 503 nm by using a spectrophotometer [27]. Lycopene was expressed as mg/kg.

Total tannin contents (mg TAE/ 100 g)

The Folin-Ciocalteu method was used to determine total tannin content (TTC) in apricots [28]. In this method, 100 µl of apricot extract juice was mixed with 100 µl FC reagent and then 500 µl sodium carbonate (7.5%) was added along with 1 ml distilled water. Afterward, it was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 725 nm. Its unit was taken as mg TAE/100 g.

Sugar analysis (%)

Total sugar contents, reducing and non-reducing sugar contents were determined as the method explained by Pithava [29]. According to this method, a reaction mixture was prepared by diluting a 10 ml juice sample with 100 ml of distilled water in a 250 ml conical flask. Subsequently, 25 ml lead acetate (2%) and 10 ml potassium oxalate (7%) were added, filtered, and titrated against Fehling solutions (5 ml each Fehling solution) with the addition of 2–3 drops of methyl blue. The titration continued until the red brick color and precipitates appeared in the heated solution. Reducing sugar was expressed in percentage (%).

For total sugar analysis, 25 ml of the previously prepared reaction mixture, with the addition of 5 ml HCl (1 N), was added to a beaker and left overnight in the dark. After 24 h, 2–3 drops of NaCl and phenolphthalein were added, and the mixture was titrated against Fehling solution (5 ml Fehling A and Fehling B) with methyl blue as a color indicator. Titration continued until a red brick color appeared in the heated solution. The total sugar contents were expressed in percentage (%) and non-reducing sugar was determined by subtracting the reducing sugar from the total sugars and expressed in percentage (%).

Enzyme analysis (SOD, POD, and CAT)

In the enzymes, SOD, POD, and CAT were analyzed [30]. This reaction involved the incorporation of 1.5 ml sample juice with 1.5 ml buffer solution of potassium phosphate (pH 5 with 1% (w/v) insoluble polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP) and 1 mmol⋅L − 1 EDTA), centrifuged it at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, pipetted the resultant supernatant for assessment of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT). SOD activity was determined by the inhibition of nitro blue tetrazolium at 560 nm observation in a spectrophotometer at 45 s and 60 s intervals. POD activity was assessed at 470 nm by measuring tetraguaiacol formation using guaiacol. However, CAT activity was analyzed by measuring H2O2 breakdown. The units of enzyme activity of SOD, POD and CAT were measured in the U mg-1 protein.

Total protein content (%)

For protein analysis, 1.5 ml was homogenized in extraction buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. An aliquot of the supernatant was used for protein estimation. Briefly, 100 µL of the extract was mixed with 5 mL of Bradford reagent containing Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 dye and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a spectrophotometer. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was used as the standard protein, and a standard curve was prepared to calculate protein concentration. The total soluble protein content was expressed in percent (%).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to determine maximum, minimum, mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variance. Principal component analysis, correlation, and cluster analysis were used to evaluate the accessions based on similarities and dissimilarities. Hierarchical cluster analysis was conducted using Euclidean distance as the dissimilarity measure and Ward’s minimum variance method as the linkage criterion. Ward’s method was selected because it minimizes within-cluster variance, thereby producing compact and homogeneous clusters, which are particularly suitable for multivariate datasets comprising quantitative traits. Principal Component Analysis (PCA), such as descriptive statistical analysis, scree plot, biplot, and hierarchical cluster analysis, was performed using XLSTAT software. However, correlation analysis was conducted using the R statistical program.

Correlation analysis among qualitative traits of apricot accessions

Using 22 qualitative morphological traits, 19 apricot accessions were evaluated through correlation analysis (Fig. 3), where red indicates positive correlations and blue indicates negative correlations. Several significant positive correlations (p < 0.05) were observed among the studied traits. A strong positive association was observed between suture size and overcolor (r = 0.67). Fruit color exhibited significant positive correlations with flesh color (r = 0.65) and fruit eating quality (r = 0.52), indicating a close relationship between external appearance, internal pigmentation, and sensory attributes. Similarly, ground color was positively correlated with fruit apex (r = 0.54). In addition, cavity depth was positively associated with the texture of flesh (r = 0.50), while fruit eating quality also showed a moderate positive relationship with fruit shape (r = 0.50).

Heatmap correlation analysis of qualitative attributes among apricot accessions

In contrast, several significant negative correlations were also identified. Harvesting maturity exhibited a negative correlation with over color pattern (r = -0.61). Separation of stone was negatively correlated with flesh juiciness (r = -0.60) and stone surface (r = -0.57). Furthermore, flesh juiciness showed a negative association with over color (r = -0.52), while over color pattern was negatively correlated with leaf blade base shape (r = -0.57). A negative correlation was also observed between apex and aroma (r = -0.50). Overall, these results indicate that both positive and negative associations exist among morphological and quality traits, reflecting complex interactions that can be useful for trait selection and improvement in apricot breeding programs.

Principal component analysis of qualitative traits

Eigenvector analysis divided twenty-two qualitative attributes into seven components. The cumulative variation up to the first seven factors was 78.846% as depicted in Fig. 4. Table 3 indicates the contribution of each factor to the variation. The results demonstrated that PC-1 had the highest variation, 20.515% and traits such as apex, attractiveness of fruit, and fruit color were dominant traits in variation among accessions. In PC-2, the variability was 18.643% and prominent traits were over color pattern, over color and leaf blade angle of apex. PC-3 had a variation of 10.432% and over-color, stone shape, and texture of flesh, and harvesting maturity were prominent traits. Similarly, in PC-4, the variation percentage was 8.480% and the highest contributors to diversity were leaf margin and, fruit color. In PC-5, the variability percentage was 7.559% and the contributory traits in accession diversification were leaf blade base shape, cavity depth and separation of stone. In PC-6, variability noted was 7.050%, and the major contributors were stone shape and flesh color. While in PC-7, variability was 6.167% and major contributor to variability was leaf blade angle of apex.

Biplot analysis of qualitative variables

A biplot was constructed based on 22 qualitative traits of 19 apricot accessions (Fig. 5). It shows the relationship between morphological attributes and accessions. In this biplot, the right-sided upper quadrant of the biplot comprises five traits, including, leaf base angle of apex, over color, suture and apex with four accessions ‘Ali sha khas-2’, ‘Safaid geri’, ‘Marghulam’, and ‘Kharbolij’. It indicated that these traits were dominant in these accessions. While the upper left quadrant consisted of only two variables ‘skin cracking susceptibility’ and ‘overcolor pattern’ and there traits were frequently present in accession ‘Neli pewand’, ‘Hawalpa’, ‘Kharpacholi’, ‘Nari’ and ‘Pisana’.

PCA biplot for twenty-five morphological attributes and nineteen apricot accessions

Contrastingly, the lower right-sided quadrant morphological variables, such as attractiveness of fruit, flesh color, fruit color, fruit eating quality, ground color, fruit shape, harvesting maturity, stone shape, leaf margin, stone surface, and leaf blade base shape. Discussion

Qualitative traits

Morphological characterization is a basic tool for identifying and discriminating plant accessions. These traits differentiate accessions based on physical visual attributes. In genetics, morphological characterization helps identify elite accessions with desirable traits, group species by physical traits, sort distinctive accessions, and provide a foundation for taxonomy and classification [31]. Further, morphological characterization is cost-effective and quick when screening is required. The present study was conducted on wild-grown accessions of Gilgit Baltistan, which showed a high level of morphological diversity.

In this study, moderate to high variability was also recorded for traits such as cavity depth (CV = 43.860%), suture (CV = 43.320%), flesh color (CV = 52.830), ground color (CV = 40.060%), over color (CV = 45.900%), over color pattern (CV = 49.970%), apex (CV = 33.610) and texture of flesh (55.110%). These traits are directly linked to fruit appearance and external quality, indicating strong diversity in visual attributes among accessions.

Fruit traits such as color, size, firmness, aroma and flesh texture are key traits which attract consumers and increase demand. These traits were also found to be diverse in the findings of Zargar et al. [32], who determined that fruit color, fruit shape, fruit aroma, fruit suture, and stone shape were the most diverse morphological traits in apricot accessions. In apricot, the fruit aroma profile is a key criterion in selecting superior apricot cultivars, as it strongly influences consumer acceptance, overall fruit quality, and the market value of both fresh and processed products [33].

Likewise, differences in harvesting maturity and skin cracking susceptibility indicate variation in adaptation to the local climate. In our finding, harvesting maturity (CV = 49.430%) and skin cracking susceptibility (CV = 56.270%) showed high variability among the apricot accessions. Harvest maturity is a key breeding parameter that identifies early-, mid-, and late-maturing cultivars. This trait helps breeders mark ecological zones for a particular crop. The availability of early, mid and late maturing enhances market supply of fruits, reduces market glut, and ensures food security. Similar findings were reported by Basile et al. [34], who noted that apricot genotypes varied in maturity, harvest time, flesh color, leaf shape, and other fruit characteristics. Further, significant variations were noted in flesh juiciness (CV = 37.710%) and texture of flesh (CV = 55.110%). These traits are critical for postharvest handling and shelf life [35]. Leaf morphological traits, including leaf margin (CV = 42.020%), leaf blade base shape (CV = 28.160%), and leaf base apex angle (CV = 29.350%), also demonstrated variability that reflects genetic diversity and adaptability to environmental conditions. Variation among accessions is due to numerous factors. The morphological traits of apricots are geographically location-dependent, which reflect adaptation to local environmental conditions [36]. In adoption, plants mitigate ecological stress by regulating physiological and genetic processes that result in morphological variation [37]. Pomological traits are regulated by quantitative trait loci (QTL), and their expression, activation, and functionality depend on accession. Similarly, fruit ripening and maturity are controlled by the amino-cyclopropene-1-carboxylate oxidase (MaACO-1) enzyme, and variation in MaACO-1 activity results in differences in maturity and ripening [38]. In addition, variation in morphological traits arises from mutations in genes that regulate phenotypic traits [39].

Quantitative traits

The morphological quantitative characteristics, such as fruit size, fruit length, fruit width, fruit thickness, stone weight, and stone size, are directly related to crop productivity [40]. These characteristics influence market and farmer acceptability of any crop [41]. In our study, significant variability was observed in morphological quantitative parameters such as fruit size, fruit length, fruit width, fruit thickness, pulp/pit ratio, leaf area, leaf blade length, fruit volume, leaf apex length, pit weight, and color attribute (a* and b* value). These variables were the most influential in determining apricot diversity in this study. Kumar et al. [42] also noted that fruit length, fruit diameter, fruit weight, stone length, stone diameter, pulp/stone ratio, and stone weight were prominent attributes that contributed to apricot diversity. Similar findings were reported by Shamsolshoara et al. [43], who highlighted fruit dimensions, flesh color, pulp weight, and stone/pit weight as key traits for evaluating Prunus species.

In diversity studies, accessions are further evaluated based on their relationships using multivariate methods, such as correlation and cluster analyses. In this study, pomological traits such as fruit size, fruit length, fruit diameter, fruit thickness, fruit volume, flesh weight, and pulp weight showed maximum positive correlations. Similar patterns were reported by Chuhan et al. [44] and Khadivi-Khub et al. [45], who observed a strong positive correlation among fruit parameters. Similarly, the results of the PCA analysis were consistent with Rakida [46], who observed 87.49% total variability and identified the harvesting season, fruit length, and fruit width as the major contributors in apricot. A similar trend was noticed by Vidaković and Pojalk [47], who reported 78.8% variability among methodical attributes and determined the fruit dimensions and seed dimensions as the significant factors in the variability of pear genotypes.

In fruits, biochemical assessments are of utmost importance, as they inform about the nutritional status of crops. Fruits are rich in bioactive compounds such as phenolics, flavonoids, antioxidants, lycopene, carotenoids, and vitamins A, C, and E [48]. Bioactive compounds are beneficial to human health and also play a role in plants. Therefore, assessment of biochemical composition contributes to breeding programs for selecting desired genotypes [49]. In our findings, notable diversity in biochemical traits was observed among apricot accessions from Gilgit Baltistan. In this study, variations in TA (0.438–0.698%), TSS (11.393–24.267 °Brix), and pH (4.103–6.400) were observed. These findings were consistent with those of Tipu et al. [50] and Ali et al. [51], who examined the same range of TA, TSS, and pH in apricots.

Chlorophyll typically degrades during fruit ripening; however, its persistence in some accessions is associated with incomplete degradation or retention of green pigmentation in the fruit flesh or peel. In the present study, chlorophyll-a (0.000–0.007 mg/g) and chlorophyll-b (0.001–0.017 mg/g) were detected in very trace amounts. Such minimal levels may indicate that certain genotypes retain residual chlorophyll even at full maturity, particularly those exhibiting greenish or partially green flesh characteristics.

In apricots, certain genotypes are known to exhibit greenish flesh even at maturity, which could contribute to detectable chlorophyll levels. Overall, this variation may also reflect underlying genetic differences among apricot accessions and their distinct ripening behavior under the environmental conditions of Gilgit Baltistan.

Likewise, variation was noted in bioactive compounds such as total phenolic contents (27.721 to 121.078 mg GAE/100 g), total flavonoid contents (80.738 to 154.075 mg QE/g), total lycopene contents (2.880 to 19.720 mg/kg), total tannin contents (1.660 to 20.609 mg TAE/100 g) and total anthocyanin contents (77.941 to 122.412 mg L⁻¹). The results are in agreement with İmrak et al. [48], Ruiz et al. [52], Göttingerová et al. [53], Ali et al. [51], Akin et al. [54], Rampáčková et al. [55], and Göttingerová et al. [56], who reported similar ranges for phenolics, flavonoids, carotenoids, anthocyanins, and ascorbic acid in apricots.

The apricot accessions of Gilgit Baltistan of the Central Asian ecological group (CAG) are widely recognized as one of the richest reservoirs of apricot genetic diversity. Compared with other ecological groups such as the European (EG), North China (NCG), and East China (ECG) groups, CAG germplasm is characterized by greater phenotypic variability in fruit size, color, flavor attributes, and biochemical composition. The high level of morphological and biochemical diversity observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports from major apricot-producing regions such as Europe [19], Turkey [23] and China [57]. The magnitude of variation observed in Gilgit Baltistan accessions is comparable and, in some traits, even higher, particularly in biochemical attributes such as phenolics, flavonoids, antioxidant activity, and enzyme activities. This suggests that the studied germplasm represents a valuable and genetically diverse component of the broader CAG pool. The observed diversity may be attributed to long-term natural selection, geographic isolation, and adaptation to diverse microclimatic conditions of the Himalayan region.

Besides bioactive compounds, sugars play an important role in fruit quality, determining flavor and sweetness and serving as an energy source [58, 59]. It is also sometimes used as a maturity indicator for horticultural crops [60]. In our study, significant variation was observed in total sugar, reducing sugars, and non-reducing sugar contents, which ranged from 7.775% to 19.578%, 5.432% to 13.452%, and 2.172% to 6.448%, respectively. Similar results were noted by other researchers in apricots [61]. The positive correlation between TSS and total sugars observed in this study is consistent with findings by Fan et al. [57] and Ledbetter et al. [62]. The strong association between antioxidant activity and phenolic contents agrees with the results reported by Drogoudi et al. [63]. PCA results are also supported by Khadivi et al. [64] and Tareen et al. [65], who reported high variability driven mainly by phenolic contents, flavonoids, and antioxidant activity.

In this study, the enzymatic activities of these enzymes, SOD (8.389 to 19.734 U mg-1 protein), POD (0.450 to 7.911 U mg-1 protein), and CAT (2.400 to 22.425 U mg-1 protein) were observed in the following ranges. During stress, plants release various enzymes that mitigate environmental stress [66] and actively promote fruit growth and development [60]. These enzymes play a central role in the detoxification of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are produced during fruit ripening, senescence, and maintain post-harvest life by regulating reactive oxygen species activity [67, 68]. Enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) have great potential in scavenging reactive oxygen species during unfavorable conditions [69]. Superoxide dismutase acts as the first line of defense by converting superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide, which is subsequently broken down into water and oxygen by catalase and peroxidase. Higher activities of these enzymes are associated with improved oxidative stress tolerance, reduced cellular damage, and better maintenance of membrane integrity [70]. The wide range observed in this study suggests that certain accessions possess stronger antioxidant defense systems, which may enhance fruit quality attributes such as texture, nutritional stability, and storage potential. Therefore, the inclusion of enzymatic parameters adds significant value by providing insight into the physiological and postharvest performance of apricot germplasm. These findings were like the results of Ahmed et al. [71], Waseem et al. [72] and Keçe et al. [73], who noted similar ranges in enzymatic activities.

The accessions ‘Yakyar’ and ‘Hawalpa’ were distinct by their unique positioning in multivariate analyses and their divergence in key agronomic and biochemical traits. In the hierarchical cluster analysis based on quantitative attributes, both accessions formed an independent cluster (C3), clearly separated from the other groups, indicating a high level of dissimilarity in their overall trait profiles. This separation is further supported by the PCA biplot, where these accessions were positioned away from the central axis, reflecting their strong contribution to total variability. They were primarily associated with biochemical attributes such as total phenolic contents, flavonoids, antioxidant activity, and enzymatic components (catalase and peroxidase), which exhibited high coefficients of variation in the dataset. Such traits are known to be strongly genotype-dependent and are often influenced by environmental conditions, suggesting that these accessions may possess unique metabolic adaptations to the local agro-ecological conditions of Gilgit Baltistan. Therefore, their distinct clustering pattern is likely driven by a combination of genetic divergence and environmental adaptation, resulting in unique biochemical and agronomic profiles that set them apart from the remaining germplasm [74].

The diversification of phytochemical attributes in plants is primarily genotype dependent [75]. These compounds are further regulated by ecological conditions through enzyme-mediated pathways that strongly influence biochemical composition [76]. Since environmental factors vary across regions, changes in climate and soil conditions lead to noticeable differences in plant biochemical traits [77]. In addition, specific genes and their expression levels play a major role in shaping phytochemical profiles [78].

The findings of this study have important implications in the context of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The identification of diverse apricot accessions with superior agronomic and nutritional traits supports SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) by contributing to the development of improved, nutrient-rich cultivars. Furthermore, the conservation and characterization of indigenous germplasm align with SDG 15 (Life on Land), emphasizing the protection of plant biodiversity and sustainable ecosystem management. Additionally, the efficient utilization of these genetic resources for breeding and value addition is consistent with SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), promoting sustainable agricultural practices and resource use.

Conclusions

This study evaluated phenotypic, agronomic, and biochemical diversity among the indigenous apricot accessions of Gilgit Baltistan. Considerable variation was noted among accessions for fruit quality traits, including fruit size, firmness, total soluble solids, and bioactive compounds. The correlation and multivariate analysis clarified the relationships among traits, enabling the identification of key attributes associated with desirable fruit quality and potential yield performance and helped distinguish accessions based on phenotypic and biochemical characteristics. This detailed information will be helpful for accession conservation and for future breeding programs. However, this study was limited to a small number of accessions and yield data were not noted due to farmer concern, as they did not allow harvesting and irregular fruit maturity. In future studies, a larger number of accessions, including yield and multi-year trials, should be conducted for evaluation. Further studies using molecular markers would provide valuable insights into the genetic relationships among the accessions.
