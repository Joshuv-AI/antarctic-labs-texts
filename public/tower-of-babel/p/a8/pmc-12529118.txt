Mass Production and Outside Test of Microfluidic Paper Based Analysis Devices for Determining Chromium Ions in Industrial Wastewater
Authors: Qing’er Yao, Yuxiang Shen, Xiuxia Li, Jiangyue Bai, Yina Dong, Shiqi Xu, Chunpan Zhang, Zihang Wang, Xiaolu Xiong, Yanbo Yang, Junfeng Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hexavalent chromium ions in industrial wastewater can bring serious pollution to the environment and a variety of harms to human health. It is necessary to develop a novel technique to determine the heavy ions. In this work, we designed a special machine to enhance the production of microfluidic paper based analysis devices (μPADs, ∼1000 μPADs/h), which could be used to determine the industrial wastewater. We optimized the diphenyl carbamide solution and the volume of liquid to be tested. Finally, we established a fitting curve of the standard Cr concentration and color intensity from the μPADs. We carried out outside experiments in an industrial park, which needed a great amount of stable and repeatable devices for the test. In the concentration range of 250–600 mg/L, 74% of the measurement results had a relative error of less than 20%; in the concentration range of 50–250 mg/L, 72% of the measurement results had a relative error of less than 20%. Our results demonstrated that the accuracy and repeatability satisfied the requirements for wastewater detection, which could be extended to industrial application.

Introduction

Chromium is widely used in various industrial fields such as electroplating, leather tanning, and stainless steel production. Those factories can produce a large amount of industrial wastewater containing hexavalent chromium ions, which may bring serious pollution to the environment. As well known, hexavalent chromium is a highly toxic ion classified as a Group 1 carcinogen based on its identification by the International Agency for Research on Cancer. Hexavalent chromium can easily enter the human body through the digestive tract, respiratory tract, and skin mucosa of the human body. Such ions can bring a variety of harm to the human body, including skin irritation, tissue and cell damage, digestive system damage, bone growth obstruction, and liver and kidney toxicity. Therefore, the determination of hexavalent chromium ions in industrial wastewater is necessary for human health and environmental protection.

Normally, testing organizations employ several typical techniques to detect the chromium ions in wastewater, such as spectrophotometry, fluorescence spectrometry, atomic absorption spectrometry, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, high performance liquid chromatography, and ion chromatography. − Although these analytical techniques have high sensitivity and accuracy, they often require additional pretreatment experiments, long analysis time, expensive instruments, high maintenance costs, and trained operators. , Instead, some researchers reported fast, convenient, and low-cost portable detection methods like microfluidic paper based analysis devices (μPADs). − Additionally, colorimetric detection methods based on metal nanoparticles have emerged as promising alternatives, utilizing mechanisms such as nanoparticle aggregation or etching processes for chromium detection. , A comprehensive review by Alahmad et al. has summarized the recent developments of μPADs specifically for chromium species detection, highlighting various fabrication methods, detection techniques, and analytical performance across different studies. Recent advances have also demonstrated the versatility of paper-based microfluidic platforms for detecting various environmental toxins, including the development of indirect fluorescence sensing mechanisms for nonfluorescent substances using molecular imprinting technology. The μPADs have hydrophilic cellulose fibers, forming a network of capillaries in which hydrophilic liquids can be penetrated and transported by capillary forces without external driving force. − In order to limit and control the flow of liquid in hydrophilic paper, hydrophobic materials such as wax are usually used to build hydrophobic barriers to form limited microfluidic channels. The unique advantages of μPADs, including easy fabrication using established patterning methods, economical cost, equipment-free flow manipulation, and reagent storage capabilities, have made them a promising analytical platform with growing applications across various fields. Though these characteristics make it possible for the μPADs to be used in the fields of rapid water detection, this method has no efficient results to prove that they can provide accurate and reliable detection results after mass production.

In this work, we developed a series of routes to make μPADs applicable in the chromium detection in industrial wastewater. By designing and making a special machine for dropping a test kit on the μPADs, we could fabricate the μPADs very fast and stably. A quick fabrication of μPADs helped us to obtain more test results from the industrial wastewater, enhancing the optimization of the μPADs. Our product was used to detect the chromium in wastewater from the Sanjiao Industrial Park of Zhongshan City and obtained an excellent response. Such a technique can also be extended to detect other heavy ions in other industrial parks.

Experiments

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were analytical reagent grade, and solutions were prepared in deionized Milli-Q water. Hexavalent chromium standard solution (1000 μg/mL in H2O) was purchased from Aladdin, USA. Diphenyl carbamide (DPC) was purchased from Shanghai Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. A series of different concentrations of hexavalent chromium standard solutions were obtained by diluting 1000 μg/mL hexavalent chromium standard solution with Milli-Q water. Sulfuric acid and phosphoric acid were diluted to 0.2 mol/L with Milli-Q water. DPC was dissolved in acetone. The above three kinds of solutions were mixed in a ratio of 1:1:1.5 to obtain the Cr colorimetric reagent.

Design and Fabrication of the μPADs

The manufacturing process of the μPADs is shown in Figure . According to our previous reports, , the μPADs were designed by Adobe Illustrator software. A single chip was a square with a size of 4 × 4 cm, including three parts: the central area, six microchannels, and six detection areas. The circular central area was connected to the fan-shaped detection area through the microchannels. A classical wax printer (Xerox ColorQube 8580) was employed to print the designed μPAD patterns onto Whatman grade qualitative filter paper. Then, the paper was heated at 150 °C for 105 s on a plant heater to enhance the wax on the front side, completely diffusing into the back side to form a hydrophobic barrier. The back of the paper was sealed with a PET film to prevent leakage of the solutions through the bottom part of the paper during the detection process. The base plate for the μPADs was readied. The following step was adding the complexant and chromogenic agent to the detection zones. After the design was fixed, we planned to employ the screen printing technique to increase the production in the future. Recent advances have demonstrated that screen-printing techniques using alternative barrier materials like polyurethane acrylate (PUA) can provide facile, fast, and low-cost strategies for fabricating μPADs while overcoming the limitations of expensive wax printers and addressing fabrication challenges such as electrostatic adsorption during printing.

Colorimetric Detection

The detection process is shown in Figure . First, 2.5 μL of the prepared Cr colorimetric reagent was added to the detection area of the μPADs, followed by drying it for 5 min at room temperature. Such a process needs to be repeated for the second time. To obtain a standard curve about the color depth and the Cr concentration in the solution, we have to prepare the standard Cr solution in the range of 50–600 mg/L. Then, we add 1–2 μL of Cr standard solution into the central areas and wait for 5 to 10 min. The standard solution will diffuse into the six detection areas by the channels to form a color reaction with the colorimetric reagent. To identify the color depth correctly, we used a similar set up with our previous work: an enclosed dark space (30 cm × 15 cm × 15 cm) was used to exclude the influence of outside light, a circular LED lamp was used to act as a stable light source, and an industrial camera (DW1200, produced by Jierui Weitong company) was used to capture images. In the real used method, we can take the images and obtain the analysis results simultaneously. In this work, we copied the image data into the lab computer, and ImageJ software was used to pick up the values of different color intensities from the captured images. All of the data about the Cr concentration and color intensity were fitted and analyzed by Origin software to establish a standard curve.

Detecting heavy ions with μPADs, including adding the colorimetric reagent into the reaction zones, adding the tested solution into the center zones, capturing the images, and analyzing the results in the black box.

Preparation for Industrialization

For the detection of hexavalent chromium ions in industrial wastewater, the wastewater to be tested was added to the central areas of the prepared μPADs. The color intensity in industrial wastewater can be measured. The related concentration of hexavalent chromium ions in wastewater could be calculated by comparing with the fitting curve from the standard solution above. Due to the great amount of analysis requirements, we developed a quick method to prepare μPADs by a special machine. The new machine helped to fabricate thousands of high performance μPADs per day, which satisfied the needs of the test in an industrial park.

As shown in Figure , a special machine has been designed for the production of μPADs, which can produce about a thousand chips per hour. The mechanized production mode replaces the lengthy manual drip adding procedure, while manual dripping in previous studies often caused inconsistent reagent volumes and positioning errors. Mechanized production saves a lot of labor costs while fundamentally eliminating the errors introduced by manual dripping. It is also more in line with the industrial park’s requirements for standardized and mass production of μPADs. In fact, mass production is limited by dropping medicine into the PADs. Therefore, we developed an auto machine to drop the liquid with medicine into each section of the PADs. We have added a video in the Supporting Information, which helps the readers to understand how it works.

Results and Discussion

Optimization of the Diphenyl Carbamide Solution inside the μPADs

DPC is a special colorimetric reagent for detecting Cr ions. The initial optimized range of the DPC concentration was selected to be 2–10 mg/mL, which was dropped into the detection areas of the μPADs. However, all of the DPC concentrations can cause nonuniform color in the detection area when reacting with the Cr standard solution. Therefore, we further increased the DPC concentration used to fabricate the μPADs. As showing in Figure a, the detection area exhibits quite uniform color reaction. Furthermore, we should investigate the effect of DPC solution with different concentrations on Cr colorimetric detection. Due to the difficulty of DPC dissolving in acetone, we selected three concentration of DPC solution, 15, 20, and 25 mg/mL. From the results indicated in Figure , the higher the concentration of Cr, the darker the reaction color and the higher the color intensity values. When the DPC concentration is 15 mg/mL, the color intensity showed an overall upward trend, and it was roughly divided into two sections, 50–250 and 250–600 mg/L, of fitting curves with different slopes, which is shown in Figure d. When the DPC concentration is 20 and 30 mg/mL, the resulting color intensity is higher than that of 15 mg/mL. The linear relationship between the Cr concentration and the color intensity is similar to the first condition, which is also divided into two ranges of 50–250 mg/L and 250–600 mg/L. However, the color intensity intends to saturation when the concentration of Cr reaches above 500 mg/L, especially 550 and 600 mg/L. On the other hand, the solution with high DPC concentration can be easy to make a precipitation reaction. Therefore, we selected 15 mg/mL DPC to prepare our μPADs. We added 2.5 μL of the prepared Cr colorimetric reagent into the detection area of the μPADs, followed by drying it for 5 min at room temperature. Such a process was repeated for the second time to complete the preparation.

Optimization of the Volume of Liquid to be Tested

As the size of the μPADs is limited, the volume of the liquid to be tested cannot be too much to overload the whole chips. It should be optimized, which is also helpful for the standard use and design of automachines in the future. We selected three kinds of liquid volume, 1, 1.5, and 2 μL. After a great amount of repeated experiments, we found that the liquid volume can affected the analysis results, especially in the range of 400–600 mg/L. As shown in Figure a, when the dropped volume is 1 μL, the color reaction area is smaller than the other two conditions, while the color is quite uniform in the reaction area. When the dropped volume increases to 1.5 and 2 μL, the area of the color reaction expands throughout the whole detection area, while the color also appears uneven. To quantify the effects above, we use ImageJ software to get the gray values from the reaction area, including the maximum value (the value with the deepest color in the color reaction area), the minimum value (the value with the lightest color in the color reaction area), and the mean value (the average color intensity of the whole reaction area, which can be obtained directly from the selected area by the software). As shown in Figure a, when the dropped volume is 1 μL, the three values, max value, min value, and mean value, all show a steady upward trend with Cr concentration, which agrees well with our expectation; when the dropped volume is 1.5 μL, the min value shows a fluctuated behavior, while the max value decreases at 600 mg/L; when the dropped volume is 2 μL, all three values show an abnormal decrease above 550 mg/L Cr. According to previous experiments, the gray value should become larger with increasing Cr concentration. In fact, only the 1 μL volume of the liquid to be tested exhibits the expected performance. The possible reason may be that an overload of liquid changes the reaction process in the detection areas due to the limited chemicals in those areas. Therefore, we choose a dropped volume of 1 μL as the optimized parameter to establish the standard curve.

Selection of DPC Chemicals

The clarity and color of the reaction liquids will cause some interference in the colorimetric detection. They increase or decrease the color intensity, causing the measurement error to deviate from the true value. DPC is a white, crystalline powder. However, we found that different brands of DPCI presented different colors. As shown in Figure , we selected several different brands of products. The DPC from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. shows a white powder and colorless transparent solution after dissolving in acetone. The DPC from Meryer Oct. Batch shows brown powder and cloudy solution after dissolving in acetone. The Macklin and Meryer Mar. batches are all yellowish powders, while their acetone solutions are slightly different: one is reddish-brown, and another one is pale yellow. The different colors between different brands may indicate their different production processes. Although the color differences do not affect its chemical properties, they may affect the color intensity of the detection area, which brings some errors to the analysis results. Therefore, we choose the DPC from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.,Ltd., which can make an acetone solution colorless and transparent, helping to eliminate the interference of physical properties on the color intensity.

Outside Experiments in the Industrial Park

With the optimization above, we finally established a fitting curve of standard Cr concentration and color intensity, as shown in Figure a. The curve was divided into two sections: 50–250 mg/L and 250–600 mg/L. The statistical results indicate that the color intensity/gray value has a linear correlation with the logarithm of the Cr concentration. The fitting formulas of those two linear relationships are as follows: y=0.34x+99.16 1 y=0.07x+161.78 2 where y is the gray value and x is the Cr concentration. The linear correlation coefficients R 2 of eqs and are both 0.98. The detection limit of this method can achieve ∼1 mg/L by using this method.

Outside experiments in the industrial park. (a) Standard curve of Cr concentration and color intensity of the μPADs; (b) industrial wastewater from the Sanjiao Industrial Park; (c) distribution of the color intensity of the μPADs for the same water sample; (d, e) repeatability tests for two different concentration sections: 250–600 mg/L and 50–250 mg/L.

Based on those standard linear relationships, we can try outside experiments in the industrial park. The test was carried out at New Dayu Wastewater Treatment Co., Ltd., Sanjiao Town. Figure b shows the conditions of the factory and the light-yellow water sample from that factory. Thanks to previous automachines, we can prepare thousands of μPADs. In fact, we made a lot of tests about the wastewater and obtained more than 600 test data from each water sample by using our μPADs. Those tests helped us to estimate the repeatability of those μPADs. The results are consistent with Gaussian distribution, and most results are located at an accumulation point. For example, the concentration of Cr ions in wastewater is measured as 618.6 mg/L with μPADs for one water sample, which is 605 mg/L by the Third-Party Laboratory Test, with a relative error of just 2.2%, as shown in Figure c. Also, the confidence interval is between 588.8 and 648.4 mg/L. We also made a lot of repeatability tests for two different concentration sections, and the results are in Figure d,e. In the concentration range of 250–600 mg/L, the average value is 227.1 mg/L with the standard deviation of 18.7, and the confidence interval is between 221.8 and 232.5. Among those results, 74% of the measurement results have a relative error of less than 20%, while 50% of the measurement results have a relative error of less than 10%. In the concentration range of 50–250 mg/L, 72% of the measurement results have a relative error of less than 20%, while the proportion of relative error of less than 10% accounted for 36%, the proportion of relative error greater than 10% and less than 15% accounted for 20%, and the proportion of relative error greater than 15% and less than 20% accounted for 20%. The above data demonstrates that our μPADs have significantly improved repeatability and stability compared to manually prepared devices, addressing the key limitations observed in previous μPAD-based detection methods. Moreover, the errors are also in line with those of New Dayu Wastewater Treatment Co., Ltd. Those results prove that the accuracy and repeatability satisfy the requirements for wastewater detection.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed a route to detect hexavalent chromium ions in industrial wastewater. The μPADs were designed and optimized in the aspects of the diphenyl carbamide solution and the volume of liquid to be tested. A standard curve of the Cr concentration and color intensity of the μPADs was built. In order to make the test in the industrial park, we designed a special machine to produce a large number of μPADs with cheap and stable performance. With this technique, we carried out outside experiments in the Sanjiao Industrial Park of Zhongshan City. By comparison with the standard analysis results, our products showed good performance: in the concentration range of 250–600 mg/L, 74% of the measurement results had a relative error of less than 20%; in the concentration range of 50–250 mg/L, 72% of the measurement results had a relative error of less than 20%. Our results demonstrated that the accuracy and repeatability were satisfied to the requirements for wastewater detection. Such a technique could be expected to be extended to other industrial applications for detecting heavy ions.
