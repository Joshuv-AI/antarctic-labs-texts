Feasibility of 3D photoacoustic tomography for the assessment of rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Nam Huynh, Olivia Francies, Edward Zhang, Jiaqi Zhu, Jessica Manson, Paul Beard, Andrew A Plumb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess whether 3D photoacoustic tomography (PAT) using the Fabry-Perot (FP) scanner can (1) reliably detect synovitis in participants with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and (2) assess the severity of active inflammation.MethodsA total of 247 3D-PAT images of the finger and wrist joint from 11 healthy volunteers and 9 patients with RA were obtained using the FP scanner. Patients underwent power doppler ultrasound (PDUS) assessment of RA disease activity. The 3D-PAT images were acquired over a 15 × 15 × 10 mm3 volume in <15 s. The images were assessed quantitatively by segmentation of the vasculature using k-means clustering followed by skeletonization to measure vascular path length (PAT-VPL). Patient and volunteer PAT-VPL were compared using t-tests and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC).ResultsThe 3D-PAT differentiates between unaffected and affected joints in patients with RA, showing a statistically significant difference in PAT-VPL (unaffected joint: 11.88 mm, affected joints: 45.7 mm, P < .001). The ROC-AUC was 0.91 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.81-0.96), with sensitivity and specificity of 86% (95% CI: 72%-95%) and 78% (95% CI: 60%-90%), respectively. PAT-VPL was associated with PDUS-based clinical severity grade, with a correlation coefficient of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.67-0.8). Significant differences in PAT-VPL were observed between each clinical severity group.ConclusionsThe FP scanner generates rapid high-resolution quantitative 3D-PAT images of the synovial microvasculature that can be used to distinguish between inflamed and non-inflamed joints, and assess the severity of active inflammation.Advances in knowledgeThe 3D-PAT FP scanner reliably distinguishes between affected and unaffected finger and wrist joints in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Compared to previous 2D PAT scanners used to image joint inflammation, it offers superior image quality and the prospect of mitigating operator-dependent variations in probe positioning by virtue of its 3D imaging capability.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by inflammation of the synovial membrane which can lead to pain, debility, and irreversible joint damage.1–4 Effective management of RA requires suppressing active inflammation by treating patients promptly and monitoring them frequently, with a “treat-to-target” strategy that aims to achieve remission or low disease activity.5,6 This requires accurate assessment and monitoring of inflammatory burden. Physical examination is the most commonly used method of disease activity assessment, but cannot detect low level inflammation that nonetheless can cause joint damage. Power Doppler ultrasound (PDUS) is used clinically to detects synovial neo-angiogenesis, a marker of synovial inflammation. However, it is limited in its ability to detect slow blood flow in microvessels and thus can miss the angiogenesis of early-stage synovial hyperplasia.7–9 PDUS also exhibits operator-dependent variability. MRI offers higher sensitivity than PDUS but typically requires exogenous contrast, is expensive and time-consuming and thus is not widely used in clinical practice. Several novel optical10 and ultrasound11–13 techniques have been explored but remain at the research-phase. There is therefore a need for new clinical tools that can overcome the limitations of existing methods in order to detect early inflammation and quantify inflammatory burden to permit effective treatment management of RA.14

Photoacoustic tomography (PAT) is an emerging non-invasive imaging technique that could address the limitations of existing imaging modalities.15 It works by illuminating the tissue with pulsed near infrared laser light. Optical absorption by haemoglobin within the vasculature produces impulsive heating and the subsequent generation of broadband ultrasound waves. By detecting these waves at the skin surface, an image of the vasculature can be reconstructed. The strong optical absorption by haemoglobin makes PAT highly sensitive for small blood vessels thus lending it to the detection of inflammation-induced changes in synovial vascularisation.

PAT has previously been applied to the assessment of hand joint inflammation.16–23 Although synovial vascular changes associated with RA were observed, the piezoelectric-based scanners used in these studies provided 2D images of modest quality. An alternative PAT scanner based on the Fabry-Perot (FP) ultrasound sensing concept offers superior image quality and full 3D images promising a more detailed and comprehensive visualization of the synovial vascular anatomy.24,25 The feasibility of using this type of scanner for visualizing joint inflammation has been previously investigated in a small-scale exploratory case study.25 Here we aimed to assess whether 3D-PAT using the FP scanner can reliably detect synovitis and assess the severity of inflammation in participants with diagnosed RA.

Methods

Participants and protocols

Following ethical approval (patients: 16/NS/0108; volunteers: 1133/001), healthy volunteer controls were recruited from the Medical Physics and Biomedical Engineering Department, University College London, and were eligible providing they did not have a diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis. Adult patients (18+ years) recruited prospectively from the Rheumatologist-led ultrasound clinic at University College London Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust with a diagnosis of RA and a clinical ultrasound assessment showing synovial thickening and/or increased PDUS in the small joints of the hand or wrist were selected. Exclusion criteria for both cohorts were as follows: (1) unable to provide informed written consent; (2) had severe skin disease precluding safe, comfortable placement of the PAT and ultrasound imaging probes used in the study.

All patients underwent sonographic assessment of their hand joints, using a General Electric (GE) Logiq S8 R2 ultrasound machine with a 4-15 MHz linear transducer (ML6-15, GE) ultrasound probe, as part of their routine clinical assessment. Grayscale Ultrasound (GSUS) and Power Doppler Ultrasound (PDUS) were used to provide a combined clinical severity grade (CSG) for synovitis using the EULAR-OMERACT scoring system.26 This joint-based score varies from 0 to 3; CSG 0 indicates normal joints and CSGs 1, 2, and 3 indicate minimal, moderate, and severe synovitis, respectively. Patient demographics, current and previous treatments and clinical and biochemical assessments of disease severity (Table S1) were recorded.

Both volunteer and patient cohorts underwent 3D-PAT scans of their hand joints performed by a radiologist and a medical physicist. All 3D-PAT acquisitions were facilitated with targeted US co-localization using an Ultrasonix Sonix MDP ultrasound machine with L14-5/38 (5-14 MHz) and L40-8/12 (8-40 MHz) probes. A subset of the healthy control cohort underwent a second 3D-PAT scan after a 6-month interval to assess repeatability.

Fabry-Perot (FP) PAT scanner

The 3D-PAT scanner used in this study is shown in Figure 1. The scanner operating principles and performance have been described in detail previously.25 In brief, it comprises a pulsed 100 Hz tunable excitation laser system for generating the photoacoustic waves and a 16-beam FP ultrasound scanner for detecting them. Figure 1A illustrates its working principles. Nanosecond laser pulses are emitted by the excitation laser and coupled into a delivery optical fibre. The light emerges from the distal end of the fibre producing a large diameter (>20 mm) beam incident on the FP ultrasound sensor (FPUS) head, the acoustically sensitive element of which is a thin film polymer structure that acts as a Fabry-Perot interferometer (FPI). Since the FPI is transparent to the excitation laser wavelength, the beam passes through the FPUS and illuminates the underlying tissue. Absorption of the laser light by the tissue generates ultrasound waves which propagate to the FPI, where they modulate its optical thickness and thus its reflectivity. The latter is then detected by scanning an array of 16 focused interrogation laser beams across the surface of the sensor. In this way, the distribution of the ultrasound waves incident on the sensor can be mapped enabling a 3D PAT image to be reconstructed.

Fabry-Perot PAT scanner: (A) system schematic, (B) photographs of the scanner (excluding excitation laser system) and its use to scan a patient’s hand joint. Abbreviation: FPUS = Fabry-Perot ultrasound sensor.

The scanner provides 3D images of the microvasculature to depths of up to 15 mm with a 50-150 µm spatial resolution and a lateral field-of-view of 15 × 15 mm2. In this study, photoacoustic signals were acquired at ∼20 000 spatial points in steps of 106 µm over this area. The total scan time was <15 s and the time to reconstruct an image was <0.5 s. The laser wavelength was set to 875 nm which penetrates deeply in tissue while still providing appreciable haemoglobin contrast. All images were acquired using a laser fluence below the safe maximum permissible exposure for skin.27 The images were reconstructed using a k-space algorithm28 and displayed as colour-to-depth encoded Maximum Intensity Projections (MIPs: x-y, x-z, and y-z).

As Figure 1B shows, the scanner is a mobile cart-based system with the imaging probe mounted on an articulated arm. To acquire an image, the imaging probe is positioned over the finger (proximal interphalangeal/metacarpophalangeal joint) or wrist joint, a bolus of ultrasound gel is inserted between the FPUS and the skin to provide acoustic coupling, and the scan sequence commenced.

The sum of the pathlengths of the individual branches of the 3D skeleton obtained in step (iii) was then estimated. This metric is denoted the “PAT vascular pathlength” (PAT-VPL) and taken to be a measure of the synovial vascularization and thus the level of inflammation. If no vascular structure is detected within the ROI, the method produces no skeleton and thus returns PAT-VPL = 0, indicating the absence of inflammation.

Statistical analysis

The primary objective of this study was to assess the extent to which 3D-PAT can differentiate between affected joints (CSG 1, 2, 3) and unaffected joints (ie, clinically normal joints with CSG 0) in patients and volunteers, with the latter serving as healthy controls. To do this, we compared the PAT-VPL metrics obtained from 3D-PAT images of affected joints in patients with those from (1) unaffected joints in patients and (2) joints of healthy controls, using a 2-sample t-test, with P < .05 as significant. In addition, we compared the PAT-VPL of each joint with its corresponding CSG.

To demonstrate the ability of 3D-PAT to distinguish between affected and unaffected joints in patients, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was calculated, using the joint-based CSGs (CSG > 0) as the reference standard.

To evaluate repeatability, we compared the PAT-VPL metrics obtained from images acquired during 2 separate visits of a control cohort subset using a Bland-Altman plot and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) calculated with R version 4.41 for Windows (psych package with average measurements, 2-way mixed-effect model).

Results

A total of 22 participants were recruited, 11 healthy volunteers and 11 patients. No participants were excluded based on the predefined exclusion criteria. However, 2 patients were excluded prior to full analysis because adequate ultrasound (US) data was unavailable. Therefore, the final study population consisted of 11 volunteers (4 females, 7 males, aged 22-63 years, mean 37 years) and 9 patients (8 females, 1 male, aged 33-75 years, mean 55.8 years). Of the 9 patients, 8 had the relevant clinical data to calculate a DAS28 score, which is a measure of RA disease activity that incorporates the assessment of tenderness and swelling of 28 predetermined joints, a measure of CRP or ESR, and a patient global visual analogue scale (Table S1). Among them, 5 had highly active disease (DAS28 > 5.1), 2 had moderate disease activity (3.2 < DAS28 ≤ 5.1), and 1 had low disease activity (2.6 ≤ DAS28 ≤ 3.2).

A total of 208 finger and wrist joints were examined in the study, with 126 from volunteers and 82 from patients. Of the 82 patient joints, 11 were excluded from the final analysis due to missing US severity grades. Table 1 presents the number of hand joints, categorized by CSG and the joint, used in the final analysis. In total, 247 images were used: 126 from volunteers’ initial visits, 50 from volunteers’ second visits, and 71 from patients. A subset (<25%) of the acquired image data has been presented in the case study reported in reference.25

The patient 3D-PAT scans took place between 12 and 56 days (mean 23.4 days) after the clinical assessments. Eleven healthy volunteers attended for 3D-PAT scans, and 5 (2 females, 3 males, aged 23-32 years, mean 27 years) returned for repeat scans 6 months later.

All participants found the use of the 3D-PAT scanner highly acceptable, both in degree of comfort and the duration of the scan procedure. There were no symptomatic side effects from 3D-PAT scans for any participant. No discomfort was reported either immediately following image acquisition or in the days thereafter.

Comparison between joints of healthy volunteers and patients

All images obtained in the study were analysed quantitatively using the method shown in Figure 3. Statistical analysis was applied to the estimates of the PAT-VPLs extracted from the 3D-PAT images of 126 joints from 11 heathy volunteers and 71 joints from 9 patients. Of the 126 joints examined from healthy volunteers in their initial visit, 71 showed no signal at the joint and thus returned a PAT-VPL of 0. Of the 71 patient joints, 7 also showed no synovial signal, all of which were identified as unaffected (CSG 0).

The PAT-VPL values obtained from healthy volunteer joints, unaffected joints, and affected joints from patients are summarized in the box charts shown in Figure 4A and B. There was a significant difference (P < .001) between patients’ unaffected joints (mean PAT-VPL = 11.88 mm, SD = 11.01 mm) and affected joints (mean PAT-VPL = 45.7 mm, SD = 33.7 mm). Moreover, there were also significant differences (P < .001) between joints from healthy volunteers (mean PAT-VPL = 4.8 mm, SD = 7.4 mm) and both patient unaffected and affected joints.

PAT-VPL was highly reliable in distinguishing between unaffected and affected joints in patients (Figure 4C). Discussion

We have shown that the FP-based PAT scanner provides detailed 3D images of the synovial vasculature. This enables both subjective visual assessment and objective quantification of anatomical vascular detail of the hand and wrist joints in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, including inflammatory-driven angiogenesis occurring at the joint margin and in synovial hypertrophy. The scanner is well tolerated by patients, safe, and convenient to use, with short scan-times (<15 s per joint) that are compatible with clinical workflow.

When comparing the 3D-PAT measurement of synovial vascularization with clinically validated sonographic severity grading, we found significant differences between both affected and unaffected joints in patients with RA; and between patient and healthy volunteers’ joints. We also found a robust relationship to disease severity; joints with severe disease exhibited progressively larger PAT-VPL than those with moderate, mild and no active disease, which in turn was greater than control participants. These results are consistent with previous PAT studies that have confirmed the presence of hyperaemia at an inflamed joint, and suggest that 3D-PAT could be an objective technique for diagnosing rheumatoid arthritis and monitoring treatment effectiveness.

Interestingly, we observed significant differences between healthy volunteers’ and unaffected patients’ joints, with higher PAT-VPL for the latter group. This suggests that these patients have sub-clinical low-level inflammation that PAT is detecting, but PDUS is not. As others have suggested,17,18,21 we believe this could be indicative of PAT being more sensitive than PDUS since it detects haemoglobin directly, unlike PDUS which exploits flow as an indirect marker.

Unlike previous 2D-PAT scanners, our system can produce rapid high-resolution 3D images, providing more structural detail of the synovial vasculature as well as more recognizable anatomically realistic images for clinicians who will ultimately be using this tool in a clinical setting. In addition, its 3D imaging capability offers the prospect of mitigating operator-dependent variations in probe positioning that afflict 2D scanners. This is because the assessment of the synovial vasculature is based on a full 3D visualization, rather than a single image slice that depends on the probe position selected by the operator. Although the current prototype scanner has proved to be effective and convenient to use, there are several technical advancements that would facilitate its clinical adoption. Faster image acquisition by further parallelization of the FP sensor read-out scheme and the use of compressed sensing techniques could reduce acquisition time to less than a second to reduce procedure time and facilitate probe navigation.25 In addition, the current probe head uses a bulky and heavy galvanometer-based scanner mounted on an articulated arm. This can make it challenging to accurately position the probe head on the highly contoured topography of the hand joints. By using a miniature lightweight micro-electromechanical electrostatic scanner, a hand-held probe of similar size and form to a conventional clinical ultrasound probe could be realized. In the current study, we imaged at only a single wavelength as required to visualize the structure of the vasculature. However, there is the potential to acquire additional diagnostic information by imaging at multiple wavelengths in order to spectroscopically estimate blood oxygen saturation and thereby characterize hypoxia, a known hallmark of inflammation. Moreover, a 3D ultrasound imaging capability could be incorporated30 by using a dichroic absorptive coating deposited on the FP sensor. This would enable dual-mode PAT-ultrasound images to be acquired with the PAT image of the synovial vasculature superimposed upon a greyscale ultrasound image of the joint morphology. This could help improve the localization of the synovial ROI for inflammation assessment, visualize morphological abnormalities such as synovial thickening, and aid probe positioning.

Our study had several limitations. First, the study population consisted of a small number of patients, although the total number of joints evaluated was of a reasonable size (n = 71). These data should therefore be regarded as exploratory, but justifies further assessment with larger patient numbers. Second, for some patients there was an interval between the ultrasound scan and the 3D-PAT scan, so the assigned clinical severity grade may have changed by the time of the 3D-PAT scan. Ideally these would have been performed contemporaneously but this was not possible for pragmatic reasons in the present study. Finally, the limits of agreement for the repeatability analyses were wider than hoped, indicating that further work is needed to ensure that the ROI can be reliably recognized so that measurements are accurate and reproducible.

Conclusion

In summary, the FP-based PAT scanner generates rapid 3D high-resolution images of the synovial vasculature in patients with active synovitis. A quantitative 3D-PAT image biomarker of joint inflammation has been developed which allows differentiation between inflamed and non-inflamed joints and assessment of the severity of active inflammation. Compared to PDUS evaluation of joints, 3D-PAT may prove to be more sensitive and less operator dependent. In addition, the cost of a commercial scanner is expected to be comparable to that of a clinical ultrasound system and thus substantially lower than the cost of MRI. These attributes suggest 3D-PAT could find application as a sensitive, easy-to-use and cost-effective handheld tool for the routine clinical assessment of patients with rheumatoid arthritis in several contexts. By enabling the early detection of synovitis in joints with sub-clinical inflammation, 3D-PAT could identify those patients that require prompt treatment to avoid joint damage. By detecting unchanged or ongoing inflammation in patients who are not responding to treatment or clinically in remission, it could also allow for medication alterations at an earlier stage. This could ultimately provide better patient care and could reduce morbidity and costs associated with this as well as unnecessary extra tests.
