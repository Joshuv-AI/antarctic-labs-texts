Recurrent infection-associated hypokalemia leading to the diagnosis of Gitelman syndrome: a case report
Authors: Xiu Yan, Wuriliga Gong, Zhengliang Wei, Hongwei Jin, Heyuan Wang, Guang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGitelman syndrome (GS) is an inherited renal tubulopathy characterized by chronic hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and metabolic alkalosis.Case presentationWe report a case of a 36-year-old GS woman, who experienced recurrent respiratory tract infections over three years, and each episode was associated with severe hypokalemia and variable potassium requirements across episodes. Despite intermittent potassium supplementation, mild hypokalemia persisted between episodes. During a recent acute respiratory infection, the serum potassium dropped to as low as 1.80 mmol/L, accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6(IL-6). This observation describes a temporal association between infection and hypokalemic episodes but does not establish a causal relationship. The genetic testing identified compound heterozygous variants in SLC12A3. After initiation of regular oral potassium supplementation and infection-prevention measures, no further hospitalizations occurred during follow-up.ConclusionThis case illustrates how recurrent infection-associated hypokalemia may unmask previously unrecognized Gitelman syndrome and underscores the importance of early recognition of Gitelman syndrome and adequate long-term potassium supplementation to mitigate complications of chronic hypokalemia.

Background

Hypokalemia is a clinically important electrolyte disturbance that predisposes patients to a spectrum of cardiovascular and neuromuscular complications. It is defined by a serum potassium concentration below 3.50 mmol/L, with values less than 2.50 mmol/L indicating severe hypokalemia, which is a medical emergency that, if unrecognized, may precipitate life-threatening arrhythmias or cardiac arrest [1]. The clinical manifestations are diverse, and frequently involve the cardiovascular system, skeletal muscles, gastrointestinal tract, central nervous system, and respiratory musculature [2]. In particular, profound muscle weakness may be the dominant presenting symptom [3].

Hypokalemia arises from diverse etiologies, including excessive renal or gastrointestinal potassium loss, inadequate dietary intake, and intracellular redistribution of potassium [1]. A comprehensive diagnostic approach requires a high index of clinical suspicion, early recognition, and the timely correction of potassium deficits alongside the targeted management of the underlying cause [4]. Importantly, when an apparent precipitant is identified, coexisting contributory factors are often overlooked, potentially undermining optimal patient care and long-term outcomes.

Gitelman syndrome (GS) is a rare autosomal recessive tubulopathy caused by variants in the SLC12A3 gene, which encodes the thiazide-sensitive sodium-chloride cotransporter (NCCT) in the distal convoluted tubule [5]. These genetic alterations impair sodium and chloride reabsorption, resulting in characteristic biochemical abnormalities, including hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis, and hypotension [5]. Since its initial molecular characterization by Simon et al. in 1996 [6], an increasing number of GS cases have been reported worldwide. We describe a case of GS in a patient who presented with recurrent upper respiratory tract infections and episodic hypokalemia. Although this condition is typically manageable under basal conditions, a substantial proportion of individuals with GS experience acute exacerbations during periods of physiological stress.

The present case report describes a patient with GS in whom recurrent respiratory infections were temporally associated with episodes of severe hypokalemia, highlighting the implications for clinical recognition and management. Notably, the mechanistic pathways underlying this association remain incompletely understood, and the role of respiratory infections as clinical stressors in precipitating hypokalemic episodes warrants further clinical observation and characterization. In patients with recurrent infections or electrolyte disturbances, recognizing the clinical relevance of undiagnosed Gitelman syndrome is critical. While infection-induced exacerbation of Gitelman syndrome is common, this case illustrates how a potential genetic cause was uncovered through the worsening infections, offering new insight for clinical practice. Standard electrolyte imbalance management may fail to reveal underlying genetic disorders, especially when hypokalemia is recurrent.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 36-year-old woman with Gitelman syndrome, height 164 cm, weight 64 kg, body mass index (BMI) 23.8 kg/m², presented with a 3-year history of intermittent limb weakness, which acutely worsened following a one-day febrile illness (38.30 °C), The patient had no relevant family medical history. Her occupation involved outdoor work, and she reported frequent respiratory infections. She reported normal water-drinking habits and took no oral medications or supplements. During episodes of acute respiratory infection, the patient reported reduced oral intake due to fever and fatigue, although precise dietary potassium intake could not be quantified. There was no history of diuretic or laxative use, vomiting, or diarrhea. The patient reported four prior hospitalizations for symptomatic hypokalemia (serum potassium: 1.80–2.50 mmol/L), and each was associated with episodes of upper respiratory tract infection or bronchitis. The delayed diagnosis during previous episodes was attributed to insufficient medical standards at the time. Potassium levels were partially corrected to 2.80–3.80 mmol/L with oral potassium chloride supplementation (Table 1). On admission, the vital signs of the patient were, as follows: blood pressure, 96/70 mmHg; heart rate, 92 bpm; respiratory rate, 20 breaths/min. The lung auscultation revealed coarse breath sounds without crackles. The neurological examination revealed grade III muscle strength in all limbs, diminished tendon reflexes, and no pathological signs. Serum creatine kinase levels were within normal limits and the symmetric muscle weakness without sensory deficits or pathological reflexes, together with rapid resolution after potassium correction, supported a metabolic rather than neuromuscular or central nervous system etiology.

The initial laboratory evaluation revealed severe hypokalemia (1.80 mmol/L), hypomagnesemia (0.45 mmol/L), elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, 120 mg/L; interleukin-6 [IL-6], 260 pg/mL; high urinary potassium excretion (55 mmol/day), suggesting renal potassium wasting (Table 2). The transtubular potassium gradient (TTKG) was calculated as approximately 6.8 (using spot urine potassium 21.90 mmol/L, assuming urine osmolality 500 mOsm/kg and plasma osmolality 280 mOsm/kg), indicating inappropriate renal potassium wasting. Additional urine tests showed urinary sodium 152 mmol/L, urinary chloride 150 mmol/L, urinary calcium 0.73 mmol/L (24-hour urinary calcium 1.24 mmol/24 h, low), and urinary phosphorus 9.34 mmol/L (24-hour urinary phosphorus 15.88 mmol/24 h). Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated metabolic alkalosis: pH 7.49, PCO₂ 42 mmHg, PO₂ 86 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 32.0 mmol/L, base excess 7.8 mmol/L, Na 137 mmol/L, K 2.5 mmol/L, Ca 1.09 mmol/L, glucose 6.2 mmol/L, lactate 1.0 mmol/L. Plasma renin and aldosterone levels were elevated, consistent with secondary hyperaldosteronism in GS. Furthermore, the Mycoplasma pneumoniae antibody titer was elevated (1:320), and the overall clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings were consistent with an acute respiratory infection.

Subsequently, the genetic testing identified compound heterozygous variants in the SLC12A3 gene: c.635G > A (p.Gly212Asp) and c.2864G > A (p.Arg955Gln), based on GRCh38 reference genome. The p.Gly212Asp variant is rare (absent in gnomAD) and classified as likely pathogenic per ACMG guidelines (criteria: PM2 rare, PP3 computational evidence, PS3 functional studies in literature). The p.Arg955Gln variant has been previously reported in GS patients, with low allele frequency in gnomAD (< 0.0001 in East Asian populations), and classified as pathogenic (criteria: PS1 same amino acid change, PM3 trans with pathogenic variant, PP4 phenotype match). Compound heterozygosity was inferred from the phenotype, as parental trio analysis was not performed (Table 3). The family pedigree is presented in Fig. 2.

The treatment included intravenous and oral potassium chloride (total 20 g over 3 days). Within 48 h, the serum potassium increased to 3.10 mmol/L, and muscle strength recovered to grade V. By day three, IL-6 decreased to 80.00 pg/mL, and urinary potassium excretion dropped to 30 mEq (Table 2).

Diagnostic evidence chain

The diagnosis of Gitelman syndrome was established through a stepwise evaluation in accordance with CARE guideline principles. First, the patient presented with recurrent episodes of severe symptomatic hypokalemia accompanied by muscle weakness, each episode associated with acute respiratory infection, with persistent mild hypokalemia documented between episodes. Second, biochemical evaluation consistently demonstrated hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and metabolic alkalosis. Urinary studies revealed inappropriate renal potassium wasting and low urinary calcium excretion, findings characteristic of a distal convoluted tubule transport defect. Third, hormonal assessment showed activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, supporting chronic renal salt loss rather than extrarenal potassium depletion. Finally, next-generation sequencing identified compound heterozygous variants in the SLC12A3 gene. Taken together, the clinical presentation, biochemical abnormalities, urinary electrolyte profile, and genetic findings confirmed the diagnosis of autosomal recessive Gitelman syndrome [5–7].

Post-discharge course and follow-up

After discharge, the patient was initiated on long-term oral potassium magnesium aspartate (equivalent to 3 g/day of potassium chloride), and received pneumococcal and influenza vaccinations. The serial follow-up at three and six months revealed improved stability of serum potassium (3.20–3.40 mmol/L) and serum magnesium (0.68–0.72 mmol/L), along with the continued suppression of inflammatory markers (IL-6 decreased to 25 pg/mL, and subsequently to 18 pg/mL; Table 4). The patient remained free of symptomatic recurrence, meaning no further episodes of intermittent limb weakness.

Patient perspective

The patient reported that recurrent episodes of muscle weakness during febrile illnesses significantly interfered with daily activities and caused considerable anxiety due to repeated hospitalizations. Following the diagnosis and initiation of regular potassium supplementation, the patient experienced improved symptom control and greater confidence in managing the condition. Understanding the underlying diagnosis was perceived as helpful in reducing uncertainty and improving adherence to long-term treatment.

Discussion and conclusions

The present case underscores the diagnostic complexity of hypokalemia, particularly when recurrent upper respiratory infections obscure the underlying etiology. The patient presented with episodic muscle weakness occurring during febrile illnesses, with a recurrent pattern of hypokalemia following upper respiratory infection episodes. Ultimately, the compound heterozygous variants in the SLC12A3 gene confirmed the diagnosis of GS. Importantly, this case does not support a mechanistic role of inflammatory cytokines in renal potassium handling, but rather documents a temporal clinical association observed during acute illness. This finding underscores the importance of considering inherited renal tubulopathies in patients with persistent or unexplained electrolyte disturbances, reinforcing the need for a comprehensive and systematic diagnostic approach in cases of recurrent hypokalemia.

GS is a hereditary salt-wasting nephropathy characterized by hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis, and normotension or hypotension, attributable to the defective function of the NCCT in the distal convoluted tubule. This defect leads to renal losses of potassium and magnesium, the activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and impaired urinary concentration capacity [5]. Although GS typically manifests during adolescence or early adulthood, its clinical presentation is highly variable and often insidious, contributing to frequent underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis in early stages. Despite its classification as a generally benign tubulopathy with minimal risk of progressive renal impairment [5], recurrent episodes of profound hypokalemia can induce neuromuscular dysfunction and cardiac arrhythmias, and markedly impair quality of life, affecting a substantial proportion of patients [6]. These observations underscore the imperative for the early recognition and targeted management of acute respiratory infection (ARI)-associated electrolyte derangements in GS to mitigate long-term complications.

Reduced oral potassium intake during acute illness, superimposed on chronic renal potassium wasting inherent to Gitelman syndrome, represents an equally plausible explanation for the severity of hypokalemia observed.

Despite the appropriate treatment, the patient continued to present with chronic mild hypokalemia, underscoring the intrinsic limitations of present therapeutic strategies in fully restoring electrolyte homeostasis in GS. Nevertheless, the marked reduction in symptom burden and hospitalization frequency demonstrates meaningful clinical improvement. These findings highlight the need for further clinical studies to better characterize factors contributing to infection-associated electrolyte instability in GS.

In summary, this case highlights the importance of considering inherited renal tubulopathies in patients with recurrent hypokalemia, particularly when episodes are temporally associated with acute infections. Recurrent infection-associated electrolyte decompensation may delay recognition of underlying Gitelman syndrome. Early diagnosis, patient education, infection prevention, and optimized electrolyte supplementation are essential to reduce morbidity. As a single-case observation, these findings are descriptive and do not establish causality. Figures and tables illustrating longitudinal electrolyte changes are not intended to support mechanistic, pathophysiological, or causal inference. This case highlights the importance of considering genetic testing in patients with recurrent hypokalemia, particularly those with chronic infections. Early identification of conditions like Gitelman syndrome can prevent recurrent exacerbations of hypokalemia and guide appropriate treatment strategies. Genetic screening should be considered in clinical practice, especially in patients with infection-related electrolyte imbalances.
