Trends in Intensity Modulated Radiation Therapy Utilization for Definitive Treatment of Intact Cervical Cancer, 2004-2019
Authors: Sarah S Lee, Christopher R Weil, Leslie Boyd, Lindsay M Burt, Fumiko Chino, David K Gaffney, Fauzia Shaikh, Gita Suneja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe objective of this study was to identify temporal trends in intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) use for patients with cervical cancer who received definitive chemoradiation.Methods and MaterialsPatients diagnosed with stage IB2-IVA cervical cancer between 2004 and 2019 and treated with definitive chemoradiation were identified in the National Cancer Database. The primary outcome of interest was the use of IMRT versus 3-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3DCRT) over time. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with the receipt of IMRT.ResultsOverall, 16,974 patients were included: 5388 (32%) received 3DCRT, and 11,586 (68%) received IMRT. IMRT use increased from 31% in 2004 to 75% in 2019 (P < .01). Clinical factors associated with increased IMRT use were stage III disease compared with stage I (odds ratio [OR], 1.3; CI, 1.0-1.5) and lymph node involvement (OR, 1.4; CI, 1.2-1.6). The demographic factors associated with increased IMRT receipt were treatment in the western US (OR, 1.5; CI, 1.2-1.8) and in the southern US (OR, 1.3; CI, 1.1-1.5) compared with the northeastern US, and living more than 50 miles from the treatment facility (OR, 1.2; CI, 1.0-1.5). Compared with White patients, Black patients (OR, 0.8; CI, 0.6-0.9) and patients receiving care at community hospitals compared with academic medical centers (OR, 0.7; CI, 0.6-0.7) were less likely to receive IMRT.ConclusionsIMRT utilization for cervical cancer has increased over the last 15 years, especially for patients with advanced-stage disease or lymph node involvement. Understanding shifts in policy, demographic trends, and practice patterns may provide additional insight into the adoption of advanced technologies.

Introduction

In 2024, approximately 14,000 patients in the United States were diagnosed with cervical cancer, and more than 4000 died from this disease.1 The prognosis of patients with cervical cancer varies widely depending on the stage, with a 5-year survival of more than 90% for early-stage, localized disease and less than 20% for patients with metastatic disease.2 For patients with locally advanced cervical cancer, the standard-of-care treatment is external beam radiation with concurrent platinum-containing systemic therapy, followed by intracavitary brachytherapy.3

Intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) is an advanced radiation therapy technique that delivers conformal radiation to desired targets while minimizing the dose to surrounding organs at risk. For patients with cervical and uterine cancer in the postoperative setting, IMRT has been associated with reduced acute and late genitourinary and gastrointestinal side effects compared with 3-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3DCRT) techniques, including up to an 18% decrease in diarrhea frequency and 8% decrease in fecal incontinence, and overall 50% decrease in grade ≥2 late toxicities.4, 5, 6 However, compared with 3DCRT, the conformality of IMRT and user variation in target delineation could place optimal target coverage at risk due to the displacement of the uterus, cervix, and upper vagina from day-to-day changes in bladder and rectal filling.7, 8, 9, 10 3DCRT techniques are less susceptible to partial geographic misses of the target due to changes in bladder or rectal filling, at the expense of higher radiation doses to organs at risk and less homogeneous coverage.

IMRT has been increasingly adopted based on extrapolating comparable outcomes with decreased toxicity in other disease sites11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 and limited prospective data for the intact cervix.4,5,17,18 The objective of this study was to identify trends in the use of IMRT over the last 15 years in the United States for patients with intact cervical cancer receiving definitive chemoradiation and to explore factors associated with its uptake.

Methods and Materials

The National Cancer Database (NCDB) is a joint sponsorship of the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society and contains clinical oncologic data from hospital registries from more than 1500 Commission on Cancer-accredited facilities.19 The data represent more than 72% of newly diagnosed cancers in the United States.20 The NCDB contains deidentified, patient-level data that are compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

We identified patients aged 18 and older with the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics stage IB2-IVA cervical cancer diagnosed between 2004 and 2019 in the NCDB. All included patients received concurrent chemoradiation. Patients who underwent primary surgical management, did not receive external beam radiation to the pelvis, received radiation with an unspecified technique or to a palliative dose, or had unspecified or incongruent radiation targets were excluded.

The primary outcome of interest was the proportion of patients receiving IMRT versus 3DCRT over time. The secondary outcomes included demographic and clinical factors associated with the receipt of IMRT. The demographic covariates included age at diagnosis, race, neighborhood-level education and income, Charlson-Deyo comorbidity index, insurance status, facility type, location (eg, metropolitan, urban, rural), geography (eg, Northeast, Midwest, South, West), and distance from treatment facility. This distance was defined as the difference between the patient’s residential longitude and latitude and the hospital’s longitude and latitude, based on the patient’s central zip code and the hospital’s location. Medicaid expansion state, which was defined by the state in which the patient received treatment and the year of expansion, if any, was also included.20,21 Clinical covariates of interest included stage, histology, grade, lymph node involvement, and receipt of brachytherapy. A subset analysis of patients from 2015 to 2019 was performed to explore temporal patterns in a more contemporary cohort.

All analyses were performed with STATA version 17. A Cochran-Armitage test was performed to assess trends over time. Wilcoxon rank-sum and chi-square tests were used to identify clinical and sociodemographic differences in the cohort. Multivariable logistic regression assessed factors associated with the receipt of IMRT and reported the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A P value of .05 or less was considered significant. This study was deemed exempt from the institutional review board given the deidentified nature of the data.

Results

Description of the patient cohort

A total of 16,974 patients with locally advanced cervical cancer treated between 2004 and 2019 were included: 5388 (32%) received 3DCRT, and 11,586 (68%) received IMRT. The Consolidated Standards in Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram is shown in Fig. 1.

Abbreviations: 3DCRT = 3-dimensional conformal radiation therapy; IMRT = intensity modulated radiation therapy.

In the overall cohort, 75% (n = 12,701) were White and 37% (n = 6345) were privately insured, as shown in Table 1. The median age was 53 years (IQR, 43-64 years). Approximately half (44%, n = 7410) of the patients received care at a community hospital and lived a median of 11.4 miles from the treating facility (IQR, 5.0-26.9 miles). Most of the patients (80%, n = 13,505) had squamous histology. Only 60% (n = 10,143) of patients received brachytherapy in addition to external beam radiation. External beam radiation boosts were delivered to 20% (n = 3339) of patients, and 15% (n = 2540) received no boost; the remainder were unreported or unknown. The percentage of patients receiving brachytherapy boosts increased from 40% in 2004 to 69% in 2019, while the percentage of patients receiving external beam boosts and boost omissions decreased from 30% to 25% and 25% to 6%, respectively, over the same period. Of the 80% (13,542 of 16,974) of patients receiving a radiation boost, 75% (n = 10,143) received brachytherapy, and 25% (n = 3339) received external beam radiation therapy boosts.

Primary outcome and factors associated with IMRT

In 2004, 31% of patients received IMRT, rising to 75% by 2019 (P < .01). Using time as a continuous variable, the use of IMRT increased over time (P < .01) (Fig. 2). For the entire cohort, there were no associations between receipt of IMRT and age, education level, income, insurance, or medical comorbidities, as shown in Table 2. The demographic factors associated with an increased likelihood of receipt of IMRT included treatment in the southern US (OR, 1.3; CI, 1.1-1.5) or the western US (OR, 1.5; CI, 1.2-1.8) compared with the northeastern US, traveling more than 50 miles to the treatment facility (OR, 1.2; CI, 1.0-1.5) compared with traveling less than 50 miles, and treatment in a state with Medicaid expansion after 2014 (OR, 1.6; CI, 1.3-1.9) compared with no expansion state. Factors associated with a decreased likelihood of receipt of IMRT included treatment at a community hospital compared with an academic center (OR, 0.7; CI, 0.6-0.7) and Black race compared with White (OR, 0.8; CI, 0.6-0.9).

Regression analysis of factors associated with receipt of IMRT

Abbreviations: IMRT = intensity modulated radiation therapy; OR, odds ratio; PI = Pacific Islander; Ref = reference.

Clinical factors associated with receiving IMRT included stage III disease (OR, 1.3; CI, 1.0-1.5) compared with stage I, and lymph node involvement (OR, 1.4; CI, 1.2-1.6) compared with no lymph node involvement. Patients who received external beam boosts were more likely to receive IMRT than those who received brachytherapy boosts (OR, 1.8; CI, 1.6-2.1), while those who received no boost were more likely to receive 3DCRT (OR, 0.8; CI, 0.7-0.9).

Subset analysis of the 2015-2019 cohort

Of the 10,044 patients diagnosed between 2015 and 2019, 72% (n = 7265) received IMRT, with the percentage increasing from 68% (855/1255) in 2015 to 75% (2201/2928) in 2019. There were no significant differences in clinical or demographic characteristics, though a slightly higher percentage of patients received brachytherapy (64%, n = 6461, P = .03) in this contemporary cohort compared with the overall 2004 to 2019 cohort.

Compared with White patients, Black patients (OR, 0.8; CI, 0.6-0-0.9) and Native American and Alaska Natives (OR, 0.3; CI, 0.1-0.8) were less likely to receive IMRT. Patients were more likely to receive IMRT if they lived in the western US than in the northwestern US (OR, 1.7; CI, 1.2-2.4). Similar to the overall cohort, clinical factors associated with increased IMRT receipt included stage III disease compared with stage I (OR, 1.5; CI, 1.1-2.0) and positive lymph nodes compared with negative (OR, 1.4; CI, 1.1-1.8). Patients receiving no radiation boost were still more likely to receive 3DCRT (OR, 0.6; CI, 0.5-0.8) than those receiving brachytherapy. The distance to the treatment facility and facility type were not significant in this cohort (Table E1).

Discussion

In this national cohort study of patients with locally advanced cervical cancer who underwent definitive chemoradiation over the last 15 years, approximately two-thirds of patients received IMRT, with increasing uptake over time. Black, Native American, and Alaska Native patients were less likely to receive IMRT than White patients. The treatment facility and geographic location influenced the receipt of IMRT, as patients treated at community hospitals were less likely to receive IMRT than patients treated at academic medical centers. Regional differences, such as those in the southern and western US, also affected the receipt of IMRT. Patients with lymph node involvement were more likely to receive IMRT, and those receiving external beam boosts were more likely to receive IMRT in the initial course than those receiving brachytherapy boosts.

This study showed that IMRT was increasingly adopted for patients with intact cervical cancer during the study period. A prior NCDB study of patients with locally advanced cervical cancer from 2004 to 2011 showed that IMRT use overall increased from 3% to 14%.22 Our study provides an updated analysis for the last decade, with similar trends in increased uptake of this technology. In locally advanced cervical cancer, dosimetric studies demonstrating the safety and limited toxicity profiles of IMRT have been published.23, 24, 25 Yet, the evidence on the oncologic benefit of IMRT has been mixed. A prospective, nonrandomized series demonstrated no difference in the recurrence rates between 3DCRT and IMRT, but improved overall survival with IMRT.5 Two small randomized trials showed lower toxicities with IMRT than 3DCRT, but no recurrence-free or overall survival differences.17,18 Given the small number of patients included in each study, relatively short-term follow-up, and the recency of these publications, it is unlikely that these studies have impacted IMRT adoption. Extrapolation from prospective data in other gynecologic sites (eg, postoperative uterine and cervical cancer) may have contributed to the increase in uptake of IMRT. These trends also demonstrate providers’ enthusiasm for using advanced radiation technologies to improve patient outcomes by reducing toxicity.

Our study suggests that practice patterns and the uptake of novel technologies are influenced by geography and treatment facility type, as demonstrated in prior NCDB studies.26, 27, 28 In the more recent cohort, distance to facility and treating facility were no longer significantly associated with IMRT receipt, perhaps due to the increased uptake of this technology across all facility types. A recently published paper by the American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) recommends implementing a comprehensive quality assurance program for IMRT that includes adequate staffing and resources, ongoing equipment maintenance, and standardized processes.29 The fact that one-quarter of patients still received 3DCRT in 2019 suggests that not all clinicians favor IMRT in this setting, or may be more comfortable with 3DCRT due to plan robustness or the resources required for IMRT planning and delivery. With 3DCRT, static pelvic fields are less sensitive to changes in the positioning of the uterus, cervix, or upper vagina, ensuring a more robust plan at the expense of additional high-dose radiation to organs at risk. With IMRT, variable rectal and bladder filling can move the target out of the radiation field; conformal targeting of the uterus, cervix, and upper vagina, and cross-sectional imaging may be necessary to ensure that there is no significant shift in the planning target volume. This requires additional staff and equipment resources, dosimetry and physics expertise, potentially longer patient treatment time, and patient training to ensure consistent bladder fill and consistent bowel habits.

Moreover, financial incentives and insurance coverage for IMRT may have influenced practitioners’ adoption of IMRT. According to a study of time-driven, activity-based costing of definitive radiation therapy for locally advanced cervical cancer based on national Medicare rates, brachytherapy costs were 67% of the total costs, but had the lowest reimbursement, and IMRT had the highest rate of reimbursement, nearly doubling that of 3DCRT.30 A recent study comparing ASTRO recommendations with commercial insurance coverage showed that most policies did not allow coverage for IMRT for intact cervical cancer treatment.31 Commercial insurance approval for advanced radiation technologies is inconsistent and lags behind ASTRO’s recommendations, posing additional barriers to implementation. The intensity of resources needed, along with insurance implementation barriers, may be a reason for lower adoption of IMRT in nonacademic medical centers.

Although evidence-based novel technologies and treatment approaches can be slow to infiltrate real-world clinical practice for all cancer sites, cervical cancer is particularly plagued by disparities in prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes.32, 33, 34, 35 For example, the use of brachytherapy and treatment times of less than 8 weeks are associated with improved cancer outcomes. However, an NCDB study showed that from 2004 to 2015, only 59% of patients received brachytherapy, and only 29% completed treatment within 8 weeks, and Black, publicly insured, low-income, and rural patients were less likely to receive the standard-of-care treatment.36 Our study mirrored similar racial disparities in the uptake of novel treatment modalities. Notably, Black patients and Native American and Alaska Natives were less likely to receive IMRT in the most recent period. This finding is consistent with other reports, suggesting that the awareness of the impact of structural racism on cancer disparities has not translated into improved access or outcomes.37,38 In addition to generating evidence to support novel therapeutic approaches, health equity interventions and implementation research are needed to ensure improved access for all patients. Expanding on implementation science frameworks to address equity may be beneficial, including the identification of barriers and facilitators, integrating equity into implementation models, focusing on reach and access from the initial diagnosis, and designing and selecting interventions specifically for underserved populations and low-resource communities.39,40

The strength of this study is the large number of patients included in the analysis, the careful selection of appropriate patients with all necessary treatment variables, and the capture of trends reported over 15 years. The study is limited by the retrospective nature of the hospital-level data, which do not capture all patients treated across the country and may not equally represent academic versus private provider practice patterns, nor capture specific individual facility-level variations in technology adoption. In particular, the NCDB does not represent all cancer cases in the United States, and information on the approximately 30% of cancer cases not treated at a Commission on Cancer-accredited facility is not available. The NCDB does not thoroughly capture treatment intent, adherence to treatment, or patient factors that may have influenced the decision to offer IMRT versus 3DCRT, nor can we assess the quality of radiation therapy delivered, organ motion mitigation, target volumes more granular than “cervix” and “pelvis,” image guidance used, or other ancillary treatment support data. We also did not adequately capture external factors, such as the implementation of the Affordable Care Act or the publication of ASTRO white papers, that may have impacted the uptake of novel technologies.

Overall, IMRT use increased for patients receiving definitive management of cervical cancer over time. The adoption trends were disparate in their uptake, as patients treated at community hospitals were less likely to receive IMRT, and Black patients were less likely to have access to these more modern technologies. Appropriate use and clinical support requirements for this technology, in combination with efforts to make it more accessible, are needed.
