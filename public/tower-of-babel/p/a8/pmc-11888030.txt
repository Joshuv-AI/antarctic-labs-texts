Atlantoaxial rotary subluxation in children after trampoline injury: A critical but overlooked diagnosis
Authors: Vijayanth Kanagaraju, Sri Deepa Kolathupalayam Srinivasan, Sherief Elsayed, Ravi Kumar Ponnappan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Atlanto-axial rotary subluxation (AARS) is rare but recognized as a significant cervical spine injury following a fall or physical impact on the head or neck in children aged 5–15 years. Trampoline use has been increasing among children, as have the trampoline-related injuries (TRI). We present a case series of AARS following trampoline injuries, highlighting clinical presentation, diagnosis, and management, especially in an emergency setting. We report four children with an average age of 8.5 years (range 6–12 years) presented to our hospital emergency service with neck pain and torticollis following a TRI. The diagnosis was confirmed through X-ray and computed tomography imaging, revealing Fielding and Hawkins type 1 AARS. Conservative treatment with analgesics, muscle relaxants, and a cervical collar led to recovery in three cases, while one required halter cervical traction for persistent deformity. All patients recovered completely, with no long-term complications. AARS in children is largely a type 1 injury with a favorable prognosis. Early recognition and management are crucial in preventing complications. The increasing incidence of neck injuries caused by trampoline use in children necessitates strict safety regulations regarding its use.

INTRODUCTION

Trampolines were originally designed for use in circuses and as a training tool for fighter pilots during World War II. Since then, they have evolved into a popular recreational activity for children worldwide. As the popularity of trampolines among children grew, so did the number of trampoline-related injuries (TRI), particularly in children aged 5–15. Children often suffer from orthopedic and neurological injuries, primarily affecting the upper extremities. Older children are more likely to experience lower extremity and soft-tissue injuries.[12] Cervical spine injuries, although infrequent in comparison to extremity injuries, are a significant concern due to the potential for neurological damage and long-term morbidity.[34] Atlanto-axial rotary subluxation (AARS) is an uncommon and often benign injury to the upper cervical spine in children, typically resulting from minor head or neck trauma, which could be detrimental if left unattended.[4] This case series explores the presentation and management of AARS in children following TRI.

CASE REPORT

Between October 2015 and February 2022, four children (two aged 8, one aged 6, and one aged 12 years) presented to our hospital emergency service with severe neck pain and torticollis following trampoline injuries. The injuries occurred in a recreational park setting, with one child injured while performing a somersault and the others during hard landings on the shoulder or back on the trampoline mat. All patients exhibited the characteristic “Cock-Robin” posture with a neck deformity [Figure 1].

Diagnostic imaging, including plain radiographs and computed tomography (CT) scans, confirmed Fielding and Hawkins type 1 AARS in all cases. Radiographs showed an asymmetry in the distance between the dens and the lateral mass of the atlas, with a fixed rotation of the atlas over the axis vertebra.[Figure 2] CT imaging with three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction further demonstrated subluxation of the atlanto-axial joint [Figures 3 and 4].

A soft cervical collar, oral analgesics, and muscle relaxants were used in conjunction with controlled cervical stretching exercises that the parents performed at home. Three children fully recovered within a week, while one child underwent a supervised home head halter traction therapy for a week, resolving persistent deformity. DISCUSSION

TRI are on the rise, with over 100,000 cases reported annually in the United States. The majority of TRI arise as a result of numerous users on a single mat at the same time. TRI ranges from minor soft-tissue injuries to fractures and dislocations.[12] Cervical spine injuries, although less common than extremity injuries, can result in serious consequences, including neurological impairment and mortality.[34] AARS is an uncommon injury in children between 5 and 15 years involving the upper cervical spine that typically follows a trivial trauma such as a fall or a blunt injury to the head or neck.[5] Only one report has been published, which documented two instances of AARS in children caused by TRI.[6]

AARS in children can be caused by trauma, inflammation, infection, and ligament laxity. The transverse ligament (TL) stabilizes the AA joint complex, preventing anterior translation of the atlas across the axis. Furthermore, the alar ligament (AL) prevents the AA joint from rotating excessively. Infections in the retropharyngeal region can spread to the periodontoid venous plexus, causing inflammation and TL, AL insufficiency. Children’s steep dens-facet angle and ligamentous laxity make the atlas susceptible to rotary subluxation. In addition, the presence of a meniscus-like synovial fold at the AA facet joints in children limits the reduction of the subluxated joint. Inflammation or trauma-induced increased laxity of ligaments and capsular structures is the major etiology.[789]

Plain radiography, CT with 3D reconstruction, and MRI are imaging studies that may be performed. Plain radiographs are difficult to obtain due to the difficulties in positioning the child’s head and neck. In the open-mouth view, a plain radiograph would exhibit an asymmetry in the dens and the lateral mass interval on both sides, as well as anterior displacement of the atlas over the axis on the lateral view, CT reveals the AA joint subluxation and atlas displacement, and MRI evaluates ligament integrity, spinal cord injury, and infection.[10]

Fielding and Hawkins introduced a classification system for AARS in children, which is widely accepted. AARS is classified into four types based on the direction and degree of rotation and translation. Type 1 is the most common and benign type, characterized by unilateral facet subluxation with an intact TL and the center of rotation maintained within the odontoid. Type 2 involves unilateral facet subluxation with anterior displacement of the atlas over the axis and an underlying incompetent TL. Type 3 involves bilateral facet subluxation and anterior displacement of the atlas over 5 mm, associated with TL and AL insufficiency. Type 4 is a posterior displacement of the atlas, often associated with an odontoid deficiency. Types 3 and 4 injuries, though uncommon, are frequently associated with neurological impairments.[5]

Phillips and Hensinger’s therapy approach is widely used to treat AARS in children. In many children, the symptoms are usually mild and subluxation reduces spontaneously. Children who visit the hospital within a week are treated with a soft cervical collar, analgesics, muscle relaxants, and controlled neck stretching. Those who arrive 1 week to 1 month after the injury are treated with hospital or home-based cervical traction using a head halter. Those who report after a month with neck deformity receive cervical traction with a halo and are recommended to continue on the halo for 6 weeks after reduction. If signs of instability, neurological impairment, or irreducible deformity persist after three months, posterior AA joint fusion surgery is advocated.[10]

In this case series, all four patients were managed conservatively, with only one requiring cervical traction at home for a week and continued on a cervical collar for a month after the reduction of AARS. Conservative treatment is often successful if initiated early, but delayed treatment may necessitate more aggressive interventions, such as halo cervical traction or, in rare cases, surgical stabilization.

Despite existing safety guidelines, trampoline injuries continue to rise. Public health interventions and stricter enforcement of trampoline use regulations are essential to reduce the incidence of severe injuries like AARS.[111213]

CONCLUSION

AARS is an uncommon and benign condition, but it has the potential to cause serious injury in children. Any child with neck discomfort and deformity after a TRI, even after a seemingly innocuous trauma, should not be overlooked. Early recognition, diagnosis, and appropriate management, particularly in the hospital emergency setting, are crucial for avoiding consequences such as persistent deformity or neurological impairment. The growing number of trampoline-related AARS emphasizes the need for more stringent safety measures and public awareness about trampoline use.
