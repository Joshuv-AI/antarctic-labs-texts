Incidence and predictors of early neonatal mortality among neonates admitted to neonatal intensive care units in public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia: a multicenter prospective cohort study
Authors: Yusuf Adem Melese, Destaye Guadie Kassie, Sofiya Hamid Agumas, Habtamu Sewunet Mekonnen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly neonatal mortality remains a major public health concern in Ethiopia, particularly among high-risk neonates requiring intensive care. However, prospective evidence from general hospital settings is limited. This study aimed to determine the incidence and predictors of early neonatal mortality among neonates admitted to neonatal intensive care units in Northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA multicenter prospective cohort study was conducted in seven public general hospitals from April 11 to July 14, 2025. Neonates aged < 7 days were followed from NICU admission until death, discharge, or completion of seven days of life. Data were collected using structured interviews and chart reviews. Modified Poisson regression with robust variance was used to identify predictors of early neonatal mortality. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Adjusted risk ratios (ARR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.ResultOf the 380 enrolled neonates, 369 (97.1%) were included in the final analysis. Most neonates (86%) were admitted within the first 24 h of life, and 55.6% were male. A total of 63 deaths occurred during follow-up, yielding a cumulative incidence proportion of 17.1% (95% CI: 13.2–20.9). Independent predictors of early neonatal mortality included prolonged rupture of membranes ≥ 12 h (ARR: 2.48; 95% CI: 1.58–3.88), perinatal asphyxia (ARR: 2.31; 95% CI: 1.42–3.75), prematurity (ARR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.15–3.88), and early-onset neonatal sepsis (ARR: 1.92; 95% CI: 1.16–3.16).ConclusionEarly neonatal mortality remains high among general hospital-admitted neonates in Northwest Ethiopia and is strongly associated with preventable maternal and neonatal predictors. Strengthening obstetric care, neonatal resuscitation, and infection prevention strategies is essential to reduce mortality. However, these findings are specific to NICU-admitted populations and should not be interpreted as population-level estimates.

Background

The early neonatal period (ENP), defined as the first seven days of life [1], represents a critical transition from intrauterine to extrauterine existence [2]. During this time, newborns undergo rapid physiological adaptation, increasing their vulnerability to morbidity and mortality [2, 3]. Survival depends on various factors, including maternal health, intrauterine conditions, the quality of care during birth, and the newborn’s ability to adjust to the extrauterine environment [2].

According to the World Health Organization, early neonatal mortality refers to the death of a newborn within the first seven days of life [4]. Neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) play a central role in improving survival outcomes by providing specialized care for premature and critically ill neonates [5].

Despite substantial global progress in maternal and child health over the past three decades, neonatal mortality remains a major public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [4]. Neonatal deaths account for nearly half of all under-five mortality worldwide [6], with approximately 75% occurring within the first week of life [7, 8]. In 2023, an estimated 1.7 million newborns died during this period, with more than 80% of these deaths occurring in low-resource settings [9]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate share of this burden, contributing nearly half of global neonatal deaths [4, 9–11]. Furthermore, a significant proportion of these deaths is concentrated in a small number of countries, including Ethiopia, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Nigeria, Tanzania, and Uganda, highlighting persistent geographic inequities in neonatal survival [12].

Although Ethiopia has made considerable progress in improving maternal and child health indicators through sustained health system strengthening and implementation of national strategies, reductions in neonatal mortality have been comparatively slower [13–15]. As a result, the country continues to rank among those with the highest neonatal mortality burdens globally [16]. In some regions, including Amhara, neonatal mortality rates remain particularly high. National reports also indicate an increase in early neonatal mortality between 2016 and 2019, underscoring the need for renewed attention to this critical period [17, 18].

The causes of early neonatal mortality vary across settings and are strongly influenced by healthcare infrastructure and resource availability [7]. In high-income countries, prematurity and congenital anomalies are the leading causes of neonatal death [9]. In contrast, in low-resource settings such as Ethiopia, early neonatal deaths are predominantly attributable to preventable and treatable conditions, including prematurity [19–22], birth asphyxia [23–25], and early-onset neonatal sepsis [26–28]. In addition, maternal and intrapartum factors, such as prolonged labor, prolonged rupture of membranes [29–36], and maternal medical complications, substantially increase the risk of adverse neonatal outcomes [17, 34, 37, 38]. These determinants reflect persistent gaps in the continuum of maternal and newborn healthcare services.

Recognizing the burden of neonatal mortality, global and national initiatives have prioritized neonatal survival as a key public health agenda [39]. Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2 aims to reduce neonatal mortality to at least 12 deaths per 1,000 live births by 2030 []. In alignment with this target, Ethiopia has committed to reducing neonatal mortality to 21 deaths per 1,000 live births by 2025 []. To achieve these goals, the country has implemented multiple strategies, including expanding skilled birth attendance, strengthening essential newborn care and NICU services, promoting kangaroo mother care, and enhancing healthcare provider competencies [2, 5, 23, 39, 42].

Despite these efforts, early neonatal mortality remains unacceptably high [43, 44]. Further reductions in overall neonatal mortality are unlikely without substantial improvements in survival during the first week of life. Although previous studies in Ethiopia have examined neonatal mortality, many were retrospective and conducted in tertiary referral hospitals. Such settings often manage more critically ill neonates and may not reflect routine care in general hospitals, which serve as key referral centers for primary facilities.

Moreover, prospective evidence on early neonatal mortality in general hospital NICUs remains limited, particularly in Northwest Ethiopia. Addressing this gap is essential for generating context-specific evidence that can inform targeted interventions.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the incidence and predictors of early neonatal mortality among neonates admitted to NICUs in public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia using a prospective follow-up design.

Objectives

General objective

To determine the incidence and identify predictors of early neonatal mortality among neonates admitted to public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia in 2025.

Specific objectives

To determine the incidence proportion of early neonatal mortality among neonates admitted to public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia.

To identify independent predictors of early neonatal mortality among neonates admitted to public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study setting

The study was conducted in the neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) of seven public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia: Injibara, Shegaw Motta, Finote Selam, Ayira, Humera, Metema, and Debark. These hospitals were located across the Northwest Ethiopian highlands and the adjacent western lowlands, a landscape of rugged mountains, fertile plateaus, deep gorges, and river valleys. The catchment area was predominantly rural. Together, the hospitals served an estimated population of over ten million people, functioning as key referral centers for surrounding primary hospitals and health centers. The hospitals lay approximately 110 km to 500 km from the regional capital, Bahir Dar, and 360 km to 1050 km from Addis Ababa.

The number of admitted neonates varied over time. On average, each hospital had approximately 33 NICU beds and managed about 203 neonatal admissions per month. The NICUs were staffed by pediatricians, general practitioners, and neonatal nurses. In all study hospitals, the NICU team received babies presenting with a complaint and assessed whether they met the unit’s admission criteria. The units provided essential services including basic neonatal care, phototherapy, blood transfusion, and respiratory support with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). Available equipment included CPAP machines, incubators, radiant warmers, oxygen cylinders or oxygen concentrators, and phototherapy units.

Study design and duration

A multicenter prospective cohort study was conducted in the NICUs of seven public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia between April 11 and July 14, 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (valid April 9 – July 15, 2025). Neonates were enrolled between April 11 and July 9 and were followed prospectively until they reached 7 days of age or experienced the primary outcome (death), whichever occurred first. The final participant completed follow-up on July 14, 2025.

Population

The source population comprised all neonates admitted to NICUs in public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia. The study population included neonates aged < 7 days admitted during the data collection period and their mothers. The study units were randomly selected neonate–mother pairs meeting the eligibility criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Neonates aged < 7 days admitted to the selected NICUs whose mothers were present and provided informed consent were included. Neonates with major congenital anomalies incompatible with life, those born to critically ill mothers, and abandoned neonates were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedures

The sample size was determined using two approaches based on the study objectives. For the first objective, a single population proportion formula was applied using an incidence of early neonatal mortality of 17.4% [], a 95% confidence level, a 4% margin of error, and a 10% allowance for non-response, yielding a final sample size of 380 neonates.

For the second objective, a double population proportion formula was used to assess predictors of early neonatal mortality, assuming a 95% confidence level, 80% power, and a 1:1 ratio of exposed to unexposed groups [45, 46]. The largest calculated sample size for this objective was 119 after adjustment for non-response.

The final sample size (n = 380) was determined by selecting the larger value to ensure adequate statistical power. The sample was proportionally allocated to each hospital based on the average number of neonatal admissions in the three months preceding the study.

A systematic random sampling technique was applied within each hospital. The sampling interval (K) was calculated by dividing the total number of neonatal admissions by the allocated sample size, yielding an interval of approximately three. A random starting point between 1 and K was selected using a lottery method, and subsequently every Kth eligible neonate was included. When a selected neonate did not meet the eligibility criteria, the next eligible neonate was recruited.

Study variables

The dependent variable was early neonatal mortality (yes/no).

Independent variables included

Maternal sociodemographic predictors

Maternal age, marital status, education, occupation, and residence.

Neonatal predictors

Gestational age, birth weight, Apgar score, immediate crying, perinatal asphyxia, respiratory distress syndrome, early-onset neonatal sepsis, hypothermia, breastfeeding initiation, respiratory support, resuscitation, kangaroo mother care, and blood group incompatibility.

Maternal and obstetric predictors

Parity, birth interval, obstetric history, pregnancy complications, maternal illness, duration of labor, duration of rupture of membranes, fetal presentation, mode of delivery, birth attendant, place of delivery, tetanus toxoid vaccination, and antenatal care, including folic acid supplementation.

Operational definitions

Early neonatal period

The first seven days of life following birth [4].

Early neonatal mortality (ENM)

Death of a newborn within the first seven days of life after showing signs of life at birth [4].

Follow-up period

Time from NICU admission until death, discharge, or completion of seven days of life.

Prolonged labor

Labor lasting ≥ 18 h [35].

Prolonged rupture of membranes

Rupture of membranes lasting ≥ 12 h before delivery [35].

Major congenital anomaly

Considered present if the newborn exhibits one or more structural or functional defects, including anencephaly, myelomeningocele, encephalocele, severe congenital heart disease, tracheoesophageal fistula, or congenital diaphragmatic hernia [36].

Early onset neonatal sepsis

Systemic infection occurring within the first seven days of life, confirmed clinically and/or by laboratory findings [2].

Prematurity

Birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation [2].

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using structured, pretested, interviewer-administered questionnaires and neonatal chart reviews. These tools were adapted from previous studies [45–49] and the national neonatal registration system. Data collection encompassed maternal sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric history, neonatal clinical variables, and follow-up outcomes.

Baseline data, including sociodemographic characteristics and obstetric history, were collected on the first day of enrollment. Subsequently, neonatal clinical variables and outcomes were gathered daily throughout the follow-up period (from admission until seven days of neonatal age) or until an event of interest (e.g., death) occurred.

Primary data were obtained through face-to-face interviews with mothers within 24 h of admission, following clinical stabilization. Secondary data were extracted from neonatal medical records. Attending physicians verified all outcomes. Data collection was carried out by four neonatal nurses and three comprehensive nurses, trained and supervised by four senior neonatal nurses and three pediatric nurses.

Data quality assurance

Data quality was ensured through multiple measures implemented throughout the study. Before data collection, data collectors and supervisors received one day of training on the study objectives, procedures, ethical considerations, and data collection techniques.

The questionnaire was initially developed in English, translated into Amharic, and back-translated into English to ensure consistency and accuracy of the content. A pretest was conducted among 5% of the sample at Kombolcha General Hospital, and necessary modifications were made based on the findings.

During the data collection period, supervisors conducted daily monitoring and reviewed completed questionnaires for completeness, consistency, and accuracy. The principal investigator and supervisors performed regular quality checks to identify and address errors, missing information, and inconsistencies.

Data entry was performed using EpiData with programmed validation checks to minimize data entry errors. Data cleaning, including range and consistency checks, was conducted before analysis. All collected data were handled confidentially and stored securely, with access restricted to authorized members of the research team.

Data processing and analysis

Data were coded, cleaned, edited, and entered into EpiData version 4.6 before being exported to STATA version 17 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were performed, and findings were presented using frequencies, percentages, tables, and figures. The outcome variable was coded as survived (0) and died (1).

A modified Poisson regression model with robust standard errors was used to identify predictors associated with early neonatal mortality. This method was selected because the incidence of the outcome was greater than 10%, making prevalence/risk ratios more appropriate than odds ratios for estimating associations. To account for the potential correlation of outcomes among neonates born within the same health facility, we estimated cluster-robust standard errors using the Huber-White sandwich estimator, with clustering specified at the facility level. Given the small number of clusters (n = 7), we applied a small-sample bias correction to the variance estimator to prevent underestimation of standard errors.

Variables with a p-value ≤ 0.20 in the bivariable analysis were entered into the multivariable regression model. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (mean VIF = 1.15). Model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using deviance and Pearson chi-square statistics. Adjusted risk ratios (ARRs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and statistical significance was declared at a p-value < 0.05.

Potential attrition bias was assessed by comparing baseline characteristics between participants included in the final analysis and those lost to follow-up using Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Participant flow and characteristics

During the study period, 380 neonates were prospectively enrolled and followed from NICU admission until mortality, discharge, or completion of seven days of life. Of these, 369 (97.1%) were included in the final analysis. Eleven neonates (2.9%) were excluded due to loss to follow-up: seven transferred to other facilities and four discharged against medical advice (Fig. 1).

Regarding maternal characteristics, the majority of mothers (87.8%) were aged 24–34 years, with a mean maternal age of 28.9 years (SD ± 2.3). Nearly half of the mothers (41.7%) resided in rural areas, while almost all were married (98.1%). More than half of the mothers (53.4%) were housewives (Table 1).

Neonatal characteristics

Among the 369 neonates included in the study, 19.0% were preterm (< 37 weeks of gestation), with a mean gestational age of 38.4 weeks (SD ± 2.6). At admission, 56.9% of neonates had hypothermia (< 36.4 °C), and 26.0% required respiratory support, of whom 16.3% received intranasal oxygen. At birth, 12.7% required resuscitation, 36.3% had a 5-minute Apgar score < 7, and 17.1% did not cry immediately after birth. Furthermore, 27.9% of neonates did not initiate breastfeeding within the first hour of life (Table 2).

A total of 912 admission diagnoses were recorded among the 369 neonates, with a mean of 2.47 diagnoses per neonate (SD ± 1.12). The most frequently recorded admission diagnoses were hypothermia (56.9%), neonatal sepsis (46.1%), low birth weight (26.8%), perinatal asphyxia (20.3%), prematurity (19.0%), and meconium aspiration syndrome (14.7%) (Table 2).

Maternal medical and obstetric characteristics

Among the 369 mothers, the majority were multiparous (66.1%) and had attended antenatal care (92.7%). However, only 66.1% completed the recommended four or more antenatal care visits. Most neonates (97.8%) were delivered at a health facility, with 53.1% of deliveries occurring at the study site.

Regarding mode of delivery, more than three-quarters of mothers (76.2%) had spontaneous vaginal deliveries. The majority of neonates (92.1%) were singleton births. Prolonged rupture of membranes (≥ 12 h) was reported in 22.2% of deliveries, while prolonged labor (≥ 18 h) occurred in 11.2%.

Obstetric complications during pregnancy were reported among 16.5% of mothers. The most common obstetric complication was pregnancy-induced hypertension/preeclampsia (45.2%). Additionally, 22.7% of mothers had pre-existing medical conditions, with hepatitis B (32.9%), hypertension (29.3%), and HIV infection (23.2%) being the most frequently reported conditions (Table 3).

Incidence of early neonatal mortality

A total of 380 neonates were enrolled in the study. Of these, 11 (2.9%) were lost to follow-up, including 7 neonates transferred to other facilities and 4 discharged against medical advice. The follow-up period ranged from a minimum of one hour to a maximum of seven days, and 369 neonates were included in the final analysis. During the early neonatal period, 63 deaths were recorded, resulting in a cumulative incidence proportion of 17.1% (95% CI: 13.2–20.9), equivalent to 171 deaths per 1,000 admissions (95% CI: 132–209) (Fig. 2).

Causes of early neonatal mortality

Among the 63 neonates who died during the early neonatal period, early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS) was the most frequently recorded diagnosis, accounting for 63.5% of deaths. Perinatal asphyxia (PNA) was the second most common diagnosis, reported in 41.3% of neonatal deaths. Other frequently recorded conditions included respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) (23.8%), neonatal jaundice (22.2%), and meconium aspiration syndrome (20.6%) (Fig. 3).

Predictors of early neonatal mortality

Bivariable analysis

Fourteen variables met the inclusion criterion (p < 0.20) for the multivariable model: antenatal care visits (CRR: 1.56, 95% CI: 0.99–2.44, p = 0.051), prolonged rupture of membranes ≥ 12 h (CRR: 2.99, 95% CI: 1.94–4.59, p < 0.001), duration of labor ≥ 18 h (CRR: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.10–3.16, p = 0.019), obstetric complications (CRR: 1.83, 95% CI: 1.12–2.97, p = 0.015), maternal medical problems (CRR: 1.75, 95% CI: 1.10–2.78, p = 0.018), gestational age < 37 weeks (CRR: 2.14, 95% CI: 1.35–3.36, p = 0.001), no cry at birth (CRR: 1.80, 95% CI: 1.10–2.91, p = 0.018), 5-minute Apgar score < 7 (CRR: 1.69, 95% CI: 1.07–2.65, p = 0.022), sepsis (CRR: 2.04, 95% CI: 1.27–3.26, p = 0.003), respiratory distress syndrome (CRR: 1.75, 95% CI: 1.05–2.89, p = 0.031), perinatal asphyxia (CRR: 2.75, 95% CI: 1.78–4.24, p < 0.001), jaundice (CRR: 1.74, 95% CI: 1.03–2.92, p = 0.035), initiation of exclusive breastfeeding (CRR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.00-2.50, p = 0.046), and birth weight < 2499 g (CRR: 1.36, 95% CI: 0.85–2.18, p = 0.197) (Table 4).

Multivariable analysis

After adjusting for potential confounders using modified Poisson regression, four variables remained independent predictors of early neonatal mortality. (Table 4)

Prolonged rupture of membranes (≥ 12 hours)

Neonates born to mothers with prolonged rupture of membranes had a significantly higher risk of death compared to those with shorter durations (ARR: 2.48; 95% CI: 1.58–3.88; p < 0.001).

Perinatal asphyxia

Associated with a 2.31-fold increased risk of early neonatal mortality (ARR: 2.31; 95% CI: 1.42–3.75; p = 0.001).

Prematurity (< 37 weeks gestation)

Preterm neonates had a 2.11-fold higher risk of death compared to term neonates (ARR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.15–3.88; p = 0.015).

Early-onset neonatal sepsis

Associated with an increased risk of mortality (ARR: 1.92; 95% CI: 1.16–3.16; p = 0.011).

Discussion

This multicenter prospective study assessed the incidence and predictors of early neonatal mortality among neonates admitted to NICUs in public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia. The study found a cumulative incidence of 17.1% (171 deaths per 1,000 NICU admissions), indicating that mortality remains concentrated in the first week of life among high-risk neonates requiring facility-based care. This estimate reflects a high-risk NICU population and should not be interpreted as a population-level neonatal mortality rate.

The observed incidence is comparable to findings from studies conducted in Bahir Dar and Addis Ababa [45, 46]. This consistency may be explained by similarities in study populations, as all were conducted in public hospitals with comparable admission patterns, including early admission within the first 24 h of life and similar clinical profiles. In addition, the leading causes of mortality, perinatal asphyxia, prematurity, and early-onset neonatal sepsis, were consistently reported across these settings, further supporting the comparability of findings [2, 9].

However, the incidence observed in this study was lower than that reported at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital [50]. This difference may be attributed to variations in referral level and case mix. As a national referral center, Tikur Anbessa manages a higher proportion of critically ill neonates, including extreme prematurity and complex congenital conditions, which are associated with higher mortality risk. Differences in study design may also contribute, as retrospective studies are more prone to incomplete data capture, whereas the prospective design used in the present study allows for more accurate outcome ascertainment. Finally, differences in inclusion criteria likely influenced the estimates; in particular, neonates with major congenital anomalies incompatible with life were excluded from the present study, as such neonates have a very high risk of mortality and accounted for 34.4% of mortality in the Tikur Anbessa study, which would be expected to increase overall mortality estimates in that cohort.

In contrast, the incidence was higher than that reported in regional and international studies, including those conducted in Dessie, the Somali region, Uganda, and Cameroon, as well as population-based estimates [11, 12, 17, 47, 51–54]. This discrepancy is likely due to differences in study populations and methodologies. Facility-based studies limited to NICU admissions typically include more severely ill neonates and therefore report higher mortality rates compared to community-based studies that include healthier newborns. In addition, prospective follow-up designs tend to capture early deaths more accurately than retrospective studies, which may underestimate mortality [39].

Prolonged rupture of membranes (≥ 12 h) was the strongest predictor of early neonatal mortality in this study. Neonates born following prolonged rupture of membranes had a 2.48-fold increased risk of death compared to those with shorter durations. This finding is consistent with evidence from multiple studies conducted in Ethiopia and other low-resource settings [31–36]. The association likely reflects an increased risk of ascending infections, which can lead to neonatal sepsis and subsequent mortality [55]. These findings highlight the importance of timely identification and management of prolonged rupture of membranes, including infection prevention and appropriate obstetric interventions. Nevertheless, given the observational design, causal inference cannot be established, and residual confounding may persist.

Perinatal asphyxia was a significant predictor of early neonatal mortality. Neonates diagnosed with asphyxia had more than twice the risk of death compared to those without this condition. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Ethiopia and other sub-Saharan African countries [12, 23–25, 31, 32, 46]. The association likely reflects impaired oxygen delivery during labor and delivery, leading to hypoxic injury and multi-organ dysfunction [56, 57]. Variations in access to timely and effective neonatal resuscitation, as well as differences in intrapartum care quality, may further influence outcomes [58]. However, unmeasured confounding and inter-facility variability cannot be excluded. Strengthening intrapartum monitoring and neonatal resuscitation remains important, and timely, effective ventilation at birth has been associated with improved survival in infants with intrapartum-related hypoxia [59].

Prematurity was another independent predictor, with preterm neonates experiencing a significantly higher risk of early death (2.11-fold) compared to term. This finding is consistent with existing evidence across low- and middle-income countries [19–22, 32, 50, 51]. The increased risk may be explained by physiological immaturity, including underdeveloped respiratory, immune, and thermoregulatory systems, which predispose preterm infants to complications such as respiratory distress, hypothermia, and infection [2, 30, 44, 60]. However, as this is an observational study, residual confounding and unmeasured clinical or contextual factors cannot be excluded. These findings underscore the need to strengthen both the prevention of preterm birth and the management of its complications within NICUs.

Early-onset neonatal sepsis was also independently associated with increased mortality. Neonates with sepsis had nearly twice the risk of death compared to those without infection [26–28, 47, 52]. This association likely reflects the rapid progression of infection in neonates with immature immune systems, as well as potential delays in diagnosis and treatment. Limited diagnostic capacity and variability in infection prevention practices may further exacerbate this risk [61]. However, as this is an observational study, residual confounding and differences in case severity cannot be excluded. Strengthening infection prevention, early detection, and timely management of neonatal sepsis remains essential, particularly for neonates with identifiable risk factors such as prolonged rupture of membranes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths. The multicenter prospective design enabled systematic and real-time data collection, minimizing recall bias and improving the accuracy of outcome ascertainment. Inclusion of multiple public general hospitals enhances the applicability of the findings to similar facility-based neonatal care settings. Furthermore, the use of modified Poisson regression provided directly interpretable measures of association, and the high follow-up completion rate strengthened the internal validity of the findings.

However, these limitations should be considered. First, as a facility-based study conducted among NICU-admitted neonates, the findings may not be generalizable to neonates born at home, those managed in primary healthcare facilities, or the broader community population. Second, although loss to follow-up was minimal, the possibility of selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Third, residual confounding from unmeasured factors may have affected the observed associations, as is inherent in observational studies. Fourth, excluding neonates with major congenital anomalies may have resulted in an underestimation of the overall mortality burden. Fifth, some clinical diagnoses, including neonatal sepsis and perinatal asphyxia, were based primarily on clinical assessment and may therefore be subject to misclassification. Finally, the analysis did not account for potential clustering effects at the facility level or changes in clinical status over time during NICU admission, which may have influenced neonatal outcomes.

These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, particularly regarding generalizability and causal interpretation.

Conclusion and recommendation

This multicenter prospective study demonstrated that early neonatal mortality among neonates admitted to NICUs in public general hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia remains substantial. Early neonatal mortality was associated with prolonged rupture of membranes, perinatal asphyxia, prematurity, and early-onset neonatal sepsis, highlighting the importance of strengthening preventive and timely management strategies for these conditions.

At the clinical level, NICU healthcare providers should reinforce evidence-based practices, including early recognition and management of neonatal sepsis, effective neonatal resuscitation, infection prevention measures, and comprehensive care for preterm neonates. At the obstetric level, strengthening intrapartum monitoring, appropriate use of the partograph, and timely management of complications such as prolonged rupture of membranes and birth asphyxia are essential.

At the health system level, hospital administrators and policymakers should prioritize the availability of essential neonatal equipment, adequate healthcare staffing, and continuous professional development in maternal and neonatal care. Strengthening referral systems and ensuring timely access to advanced neonatal services may further contribute to improved outcomes.

Since this study was conducted among NICU-admitted neonates in public general hospitals, the findings should be interpreted within this specific clinical context. Direct comparisons with population-level neonatal mortality indicators expressed per 1,000 live births should therefore be made cautiously.

Given that this study was conducted among NICU-admitted neonates in public general hospitals, the findings should be interpreted within this specific clinical context and are not directly generalizable to community or population-level settings. Comparisons with national or global neonatal mortality targets expressed per 1,000 live births should therefore be made with caution.
