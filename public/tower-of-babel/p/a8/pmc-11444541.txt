Vermian multinodular and vacuolating lesion of unknown significance discovered following syncope: a case report

Multinodular and Vacuolating Posterior Fossa Lesions of Unknown Significance (MV PLUS) represent a unique subset of benign cystic lesions primarily involving the sub-tentorial structures of the brain. However, it’s worth noting that many of these multinodular cystic lesions can also manifest in supratentorial areas1,2. Despite their typically asymptomatic nature, they can occasionally present with clinical symptoms, such as seizures, headache, syncope for supratentorial lesions, and vertigo, particularly if involving the cerebellum for sub-tentorial lesions. The advent of MRI has revolutionized the detection and characterization of these lesions, revealing their characteristic appearance as small subcortical nodular lesions with high intensity in T2-FLAIR sequences.

The recognition of intracranial multinodular and vacuolating lesions is relatively recent, with the pioneering study on supratentorial lesions conducted by Huse and colleagues in 20133. Despite increasing awareness, the underlying pathophysiology of these lesions remains elusive, resulting in a lack of detailed anatomopathological classification or prognostic scoring system. Consequently, diagnosing and managing these lesions pose significant challenges for clinicians.

In this context, the authors of this study aim to elucidate the characteristics and diagnostic complexities associated with MV PLUS lesions by presenting images from an emblematic case. Through a comprehensive analysis of the imaging findings and clinical presentation, this case serves as a valuable illustration of the nuances involved in diagnosing and managing MV PLUS lesions. Additionally, it underscores the importance of ongoing research efforts aimed at unraveling the mysteries surrounding these intriguing intracranial anomalies.

Case presentation

The patient, a 52-year-old female, presented with a history of subjective dizziness persisting for the past 6 months, with the most recent episode accompanied by a syncope-type event. Neurological examination revealed ataxia and a positive Romberg sign. The initial presentation of the patient with dizziness and syncopal attacks warranted a comprehensive diagnostic approach. The patient underwent a detailed neurological examination and a thorough workup, including routine blood tests, electrocardiogram (ECG), and echocardiography, to rule out cardiovascular causes. Additionally, vestibular function tests were conducted to evaluate potential inner ear disorders. No significant abnormalities were identified in these investigations, leading us to consider the MRI findings as the most plausible explanation for her symptoms.

Imaging studies were crucial in the diagnostic process. MRI revealed an area of altered signal in the vermian and paravermian regions, characterized by the low signal intensity in T1-weighted sequences and high signal intensity in FLAIR sequences (Figs. 1, 2). The lesion appeared multicystic and nodular without evident perilesional edema (Fig. 3). No compression of the fourth ventricle nor midline shift was noted, and no pathological enhancement was observed following gadolinium administration. Spectroscopy did not reveal significant peaks of major metabolites in the examined volumes of interest (Fig. 4).

Coronal three-dimensional multiplanar reconstructions in T1-weighted MRI sequence exhibit discrete hypointense foci localized predominantly within the vermian and paravermian regions, suggestive of nodular lesions with decreased signal intensity.

Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery MRI demonstrates a compact, cluster-like hyperintense signal alteration without accompanying perilesional vasogenic edema, notably concentrated anteriorly towards the IV ventricle, indicative of a distinct lesion pattern.

T2-weighted imaging reveals a complex multivacuolar lesion extending from the vermian to the posterior wall of the IV ventricle, characterized by multiple cystic nodules exhibiting variable signal intensities and well-defined margins, consistent with a heterogeneous pathological process.

Spectroscopy analysis performed with long echo time (TE) multivoxel technique indicates the absence of pathological metabolite peaks, such as elevated choline or inverted N-acetylaspartate, ruling out features suggestive of malignant pathology and supporting a non-neoplastic etiology.

Following appropriate corticosteroid and antivertiginous therapy, the patient demonstrated clinical improvement and was discharged. Surgical intervention was not deemed necessary. The patient was advised to undergo neuroradiological follow-up for monitoring purposes. A subsequent MRI performed at 12 months showed a neuroradiological picture that was essentially unchanged, indicating stability in the patient’s condition. We have followed the Surgical CAse REport 2023 criteria for the preparation of this case4.

Discussion

Multinodular and Vacuolating Lesions of Unknown Significance (MVNT) stand as intriguing enigmas within the realm of neuroimaging and neuropathology, representing a complex category of benign lesions with distinctive characteristics. Initially described in 2013 by Huse and colleagues, the understanding of MVNTs has evolved through subsequent research endeavors, encompassing numerous case series and individual reports that have contributed to elucidating their unique histopathological and radiological features1,2.

These lesions, typified by small, clustered nodules sharply demarcated from surrounding tissue, present both diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Recent efforts, notably by Lecler and colleagues, have expanded the scope of MVNTs to include infratentorial lesions, thereby broadening our understanding of these intriguing neuroanatomical phenomena3. This expansion, termed MV PLUS, sheds light on a previously underexplored aspect of MVNTs, emphasizing the importance of considering both supratentorial and infratentorial presentations3.

One of the key features defining MVNTs is their characteristic appearance on MRI. While most lesions exhibit isointensity or mild hypointensity on T1-weighted images relative to normal white matter, infratentorial MV PLUS lesions often demonstrate hypointensity, providing valuable clues for differential diagnosis5,6. Additionally, the presence of cystic or cyst-like components within MVNTs adds complexity to their radiological profile, necessitating comprehensive evaluation to distinguish them from other intracranial lesions7.

Advancements in neuroimaging techniques have unveiled various advanced MRI features associated with MVNTs, offering insights into their diverse radiological manifestations8. These include nodules with suppression or partial suppression on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, as well as the central dot sign—a hypointense signal at the center of an otherwise hyperintense nodular lesion observed in some cases3,9. Moreover, histologically proven MVNTs have exhibited cystic or cyst-like nodular lesions that were isointense to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) on both T2- and T1-weighted images, underscoring the heterogeneous nature of these intriguing lesions10.

Despite significant progress in characterizing MVNTs, challenges persist in their classification and management. While generally considered benign with a stable clinical course, uncertainties remain regarding their cellular origin, potential for malignant transformation, and optimal therapeutic strategies11. Furthermore, the differential diagnosis of MVNTs encompasses a wide array of entities, including cerebellar hamartomas, astrocytomas, dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumors, and dysplastic cerebral gangliocytoma, necessitating a meticulous approach to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate management12–14.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for MV PLUS lesions includes cerebellar hamartomas, astrocytomas, dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumors (DNET), and dysplastic cerebral gangliocytoma. The exclusion of these entities was based on a combination of imaging characteristics and clinical presentation:

Cerebellar hamartomas: These typically exhibit a solid appearance without the multicystic nature seen in our patient’s MRI. Additionally, hamartomas often show calcifications, which were absent in this case.

The characteristic MRI findings of multinodular and vacuolating lesions with high T2-FLAIR intensity, absence of significant enhancement, and lack of perilesional edema, along with a stable clinical course, strongly supported the diagnosis of MV PLUS.

Peculiar and unique aspects of the case

Clinical presentation: The patient presented with dizziness and syncope, which are atypical symptoms for MV PLUS lesions, thus broadening the spectrum of clinical manifestations associated with these lesions.

Imaging characteristics: The lesion exhibited classic features of MV PLUS on MRI, including multicystic and nodular morphology, high T2-FLAIR intensity, and absence of significant enhancement or edema. These imaging characteristics were pivotal in establishing the diagnosis and excluding other potential differential diagnoses.

Management and outcome: The case underscores the benign nature and stable course of MV PLUS lesions, as evidenced by the patient’s clinical improvement with conservative management and stable imaging findings over a 12-month follow-up period.

Contribution to literature: Given the rarity of MV PLUS lesions and the limited number of reported cases, this case contributes valuable information to the existing body of knowledge. It highlights the importance of recognizing these lesions, understanding their benign nature, and avoiding unnecessary surgical interventions.

The recent inclusion of MVNTs in the WHO Classification of central nervous system tumors underscores the growing recognition of their clinical significance and highlights the need for standardized diagnostic criteria and management guidelines13. However, significant gaps persist in our understanding of these intriguing lesions, warranting further research endeavors to unravel the mysteries surrounding their etiology, natural history, and optimal management strategies.

Conclusion

MV PLUS lesions are benign multinodular cystic lesions, which are relatively rare given their recent introduction in the scientific literature. To date, there have been limited studies on these lesions, and no significant developments or changes have been reported. They exhibit a stationary nature and do not show signs of progression over time, making them amenable to monitoring through MRI studies during subsequent follow-ups. Further research is needed to better understand the pathophysiology and long-term implications of these lesions. Overall, this case provides a comprehensive understanding of MV PLUS lesions, reinforcing their characteristic features and the importance of accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.
