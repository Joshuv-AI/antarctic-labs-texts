Sudden cardiac arrest in low-risk patient during transurethral resection of the prostate under spinal anesthesia: Acute myocardial infraction a possible cause? Case report

Introduction and importancy

Sudden cardiac arrest (CA) after spinal anesthesia (SA) is a relatively rare but potentially catastrophic event. The true incidence varies between 1.3 and 18 in 10,000 [1]. SA for a transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) is the preferred and common practice, but both SA and TURP are associated with rare incidents of CA [2]. The possible causes for CA could be anesthesia, surgery, or patients' related complications, which we reviewed in the discussion. We report sudden CA at the end of TURP without any known patient risk and anesthesia or surgery precipitating factors. We cannot attribute any definite cause for this complication, except acute myocardial infarction (MI), which is induced by surgical stress.

This case report is compliant with the SCARE Guidelines and submits a completed SCARE checklist [3].

Presentation of case

A 53-year-old male patient with no comorbidities and not taking any medication, with a BMI of 24.1, is scheduled for a TURP. The surgery is planned as the first case of the day, set to begin at 7:50 AM. The day prior to the surgery, the patient underwent assessment by the senior anesthesiologist. Apart from chronic back pain, there were no significant findings. The patient was instructed to fast from midnight onwards. All preoperative investigations, including Complete Blood Count, organ function tests, and random blood glucose levels, fell within normal ranges (Table 1) preoperatively ECG was normal sinus rhythm. Upon auscultation, both the first and second heart sounds (S1 and S2) were clearly audible, with no indication of cardiac murmurs or gallops.

Surgery was scheduled under SA. The patient was transferred to the operating room at 07:35 AM. Baseline monitoring included 5‑lead electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring, non-invasive blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), and oxygen saturation measurement. Initial vital parameters were within normal limits, with a regular HR of 68 per minute, BP at 119/72 mmHg, arterial oxygen saturation measured with the pulse oximeter (SPO2) at 98 %, and ECG showing normal sinus rhythm (Fig. 1). An 18G intravenous (IV) cannula was inserted into the left upper limb and connected to a Ringer's lactate infusion. The patient received 200 ml of fluid infusion before the procedure. The pre-anesthesia assessment note was revised after written informed consent was verified by the attending Anesthetist.

Baseline vital sign at the beginning of procedure 7:50 AM (A) and 7 min after chest pain complain or after 67 1, 2 min of the procedure at 8:57 AM (B).source: intraoperative patient monitor.

According to the scheduled procedure, SA was administered by a highly experienced chief anesthesia professional. This was accomplished using a 24-gauge spinal needle. A combination of 3 ml of 0.5 % heavy bupivacaine and 10 μg of fentanyl was injected at the L3-L4 interspace (between the third and fourth lumbar vertebrae) while the patient was seated. The needle was inserted smoothly and effectively on the initial attempt, causing no trauma. Within seven minutes, a sensory block reaching up to the T10 level (thoracic vertebrae) was successfully achieved. Subsequently, the patient was positioned in lithotomy for the surgery.

5 % dextrose in water was utilized for bladder irrigation. The irrigation fluid was at a height of 1.75 m. The patient, who was not sedated, remained stable and engaged in communication with both the surgical and anesthesia teams. An hour into the procedure, the patient experienced chest pain and overall weakness. Non-invasive BP was measured at 112/81 mmHg, HR at 78 beats per minute, and SpO2 at 100 % without supplemental oxygen on the monitor. Radial pulse was detected at full volume and regular rhythm. There were no changes noted in the ECG reading.

After confirming the sensory block level at T10, the patient was reassured. However, suddenly, the patient experienced severe chest pain accompanied by a mild decrease in HR (62/min), even though the surgery was nearly completed. The surgeon promptly ceased the procedure, called for assistance, and initiated consultations. Meanwhile, the patient received IV atropine (0.5 mg) and fentanyl (50 μg). Additionally, the patient was supplied with 100 % oxygen at 4 l per minute via nasal catheter. Due to the unavailability of arterial blood gas analysis in our facility, it was not performed. Although there were no significant changes observed in the ECG, BP, HR, and SPO2 (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).chest pain was radiated to left arm. In addition to chest pain the patient starts to exhibited symptoms such as cold sweats, vomiting, confusion, and subsequent unconsciousness.

Systolic, diastolic pressure and pulse rate from 7:50 am to 9:00 am taken from patient vital sign monitor. NB: Measurement was every 5 min but changed to every 2 min after 8:55 am.

Endotracheal intubation with a size 7 tube was promptly conducted, followed by manual positive pressure ventilation with 100 % O2. As the radial pulse weakened, even though ECG, HR reading is normal (Fig. 1) IV epinephrine (1 mg) was administered. Despite these efforts, the ECG showed asystole, and no carotid pulse could be discerned, auscultation reveals no cardiac sound resulting in the diagnosis of CA.

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) began, following an initial cycle of 30 chest compressions paired with 2 ventilations and the administration of additional IV Epinephrine 1 mg, heart rhythm restoration was not achieved. Subsequently, after one cycle of CPR, pulseless electrical activity (PEA) which exhibited chaotic and disorganized electrical activity. We believe it might have been fine ventricular fibrillation, even though it appeared similar to asystole on the monitor. A defibrillator was employed, delivering 150 joules using a biphasic manual defibrillator. Despite ongoing chest compressions and ventilation, with Epinephrine administered every 3 min, there persisted some PEA. Consequently, the defibrillator was utilized in 3 cycles, incrementally increasing joules up to 200, while resuscitation efforts continued uninterrupted. However, there was no discernible change; rather, the condition deteriorated to asystole. CPR endeavors persisted for an hour due to indications of life, notably an end-tidal carbon dioxide reading ranging from 4 to 6 mmHg. The CPR team was led by senior anesthesiologist and adhered to the American Heart Association Guidelines for Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiovascular Care [4].

Surgery was conducted by senior urologist. The surgery proceeded smoothly with no complications, and the total blood loss was approximately 100 to 150 ml when the chest pain initially emerged. During the procedure, 32 L for bladder irrigation and 600 ml of Ringer's lactate were administered IV. Only ten minutes elapsed between the onset of chest pain and CA. Throughout this time, there were no notable alterations in BP, HR, or ECG readings until the patient perspired and subsequently lost consciousness (Fig. 2). There was an attempt to bring continues 12 lead ECG monitor from nearby ICU but CA occurred before arrival of monitor.

The family was notified about the incident, including the start and end times of the CPR. Following this, the anesthesia, surgery, and resuscitation team convened to discuss the event. They concluded that acute MI was the most probable cause of the CA, despite the results of the autopsy. The family was provided with counseling and advised to proceed with an autopsy and blood test, but they declined and chose to take the deceased body with them. The institute lacked the legal authority to compel them to undergo an autopsy and blood test.

Discussion

SA is generally regarded as a straightforward and relatively safe procedure. However, despite its perceived simplicity, life-threatening complications can arise for various reasons [5]. In this case, we aimed to identify the specific surgical, anesthesia and patient related factors that contributed to the CA experienced by the patient. Unfortunately, we were unable to pinpoint a definitive cause that could fully explain the incident.

The occurrence of life-threatening situations associated with SA, such as high spinal and bupivacaine toxicity, is deemed improbable in this case. This is primarily because the level of block was assessed an hour before the onset of chest pain and when chest pain began, confirmed to be at the T10. Additionally, there were no sign and symptom of bupivacaine toxicity, such as seizures, cardiac arrhythmias, or hypotension. As noted in the literature, except having ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) physical status I or II, the patient did not exhibit risk factors for bradycardia and CA under SA, including being under the age of 50, having a baseline HR < 60/min, using beta blockers, experiencing sensory level blockade above T6, having a prolonged PR interval on ECG, or having vagotonia [6].

Although it is uncommon for TURP syndrome to occur during a procedure lasting less than an hour, in order to mitigate the risk of excessive fluid absorption, we adhered to specific precautions. These included limiting the resection time to < an hour, ensuring that the irrigating fluid bag was initially positioned no >30 cm above the operating table, and reducing this distance to 15 cm during the final stages of resection. Additionally, the patient was positioned in lithotomy without Trendelenburg [7].

A study conducted in the UK by Amr Hawary et al. extensively discussed the signs and symptoms of TURP syndrome, such as restlessness, hypertension, headache, tachycardia, tachypnea, hypoxia, and convulsions. Importantly, throughout the procedure, this particular case did not exhibit any signs indicative of TURP syndrome. Therefore, we can confidently rule out the occurrence of TURP syndrome in this instance [7,8].

During and after surgery, there can be a significant increase in the occurrence of Pulmonary Embolism (PE) [9]. The primary clinical manifestation of PE in conscious patients is respiratory function abnormalities and disturbances in pulmonary gas exchange, which are among the initial changes observed when venous emboli become lodged in the lungs. Additionally, classic intraoperative signs associated with PE include hypotension and tachycardia, alongside perioperative occurrences such as hypoxemia or sudden decrease in end-tidal CO2 [10]. Surprisingly, this patient exhibited none of these clinical signs.

During the pre-anesthetic evaluation, the patient's medical history, physical examination, and documented investigation data revealed no indications or symptoms of cardiovascular disease. Additionally, Lee's Revised Cardiac Risk Index, a validated and commonly employed scoring system for predicting major cardiac complications in major elective non-cardiac surgeries (Table 2), was utilized. In this case, the predicted cardiac event rate score was 0 points, corresponding to a predicted risk of 0.4 %, placing the patient in the lowest risk category [11].

Additional significant risk factors comprise recent MI, recent placement of coronary stents, elevated preoperative high-sensitivity troponin-I levels, valvular heart disease, decompensated heart failure, and arrhythmias. Although high-sensitivity troponin-I testing was not conducted, it's noteworthy that the patient did not possess any of the aforementioned risk factors [11].

Some documents characterize MI as the death of myocardial tissue that happens in a clinical context consistent with acute myocardial ischemia. The most frequent diagnostic criteria usually entail an elevated troponin level along with either ischemic symptoms or ischemic findings on ECG. Regrettably, in this case, there was no time to measure troponin levels in addition the medical team was actively involved in resuscitation efforts.

In a prospective study, Twelve hundred patients (8.0 %) suffered MI in non-cardiac surgery (MINS), and 58.2 % of these patients would not have fulfilled the universal definition of MI. Only 15.8 % of patients with MINS experienced an ischemic symptom [12]. This article may support our case report that most patient didn't fulfilled the universal definition of MI.

ECG monitoring is standard practice during both anesthesia and recovery periods. Augmenting the standard 3‑lead ECG with precordial leads for patients at risk of myocardial ischemia enhances its sensitivity. A recent study indicated that utilizing 2 or more precordial leads is necessary to achieve a sensitivity for MI of 95 % or higher. Sensitivity increases when combining leads, with V4 and V5 demonstrating 90 % sensitivity, leads II and V5 showing 80 % sensitivity, and leads II, V4, and V5 reaching a sensitivity of 96 %. However, it's worth noting that none of these combinations achieve 100 % sensitivity.it is believed that ECG monitor in operation theater are not designed for this degree of accuracy.

The patient underwent monitoring using a 5‑lead ECG, covering all possible views (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF, and V), yet no indications of MI were detected. Compared to 12‑lead ECGs, those with 3 or 5 leads provide a less detailed picture and information. Five‑lead ECG monitoring is akin to 3‑lead monitoring but incorporates two additional electrodes to enhance ST elevation readings. Despite the patient experiencing severe chest pain and cold sweating, no abnormalities were observed on the ECG. Additionally, recent evidence suggests that many patients may experience MI during the perioperative period without meeting the diagnostic criteria for MI [[13], [14], [15]].

Many articles prove That V5 lead is the most sensitive lead of the ECG for detecting MI. which can be seen in 12 Lead ECG but not visible in our monitor. A study in 69,948 non cardiac surgery patients, the 5 lead ECG demonstrated both a low specificity and sensitivity for detecting of perioperative MI [16,17].

There were no abnormal ECG readings in this patient. The time lapse between the onset of chest pain and CA was only 10 min. Throughout the incident, the patient's BP, HR, and oxygen saturation remained within normal ranges. The anesthesia team did not have sufficient time to confirm the diagnosis by measuring cardiac enzymes, conducting continuous monitoring with a 12‑lead ECG or echocardiography, and administering antiarrhythmic medications, aspirin, and a glyceryl trinitrate infusion. Given the resource limitations in our country, the absence of a continuous 12‑lead ECG monitor in the operating room may have contributed to the delay in confirming the diagnosis of MI.

The symptoms of MI can vary widely. While some individuals experience mild symptoms, others may suffer from severe manifestations. Additionally, there are cases where individuals may exhibit no symptoms at all. In some instances, sudden CA can be the initial symptom of an MI, while in others, the onset of an MI can occur suddenly.

Common MI symptoms include: Chest pain that may feel like pressure, tightness, pain, squeezing Pain or discomfort that spreads to the shoulder, arm, back, neck, Cold sweat, Fatigue, Heartburn, Lightheadedness or sudden dizziness, Nausea, Shortness of breath.

Chest pain or angina that keeps happening and doesn't go away with rest may be an early warning sign. Angina is caused by a temporary decrease in blood flow to the heart [19].

There are multiple definitions of MI, which utilize various criteria such as bio markers, ECG, imaging, and others. For instance, Type 3 MI is defined in situations where MI is diagnosed without biomarkers (such as troponin) because the patient died before blood samples could be collected, before cardiac biomarkers could rise or cardiac biomarkers were not collected [20].

Jernberg et al.'s study Continuous 12‑lead ECG monitoring for ST-segment changes provides real-time prognostic insights and significantly improves early risk assessment in patients showing symptoms suggestive of acute coronary syndrome, even when ECG findings do not diagnose acute MI [21].

In this instance, the definition of MI may correspond to the updated 2008–09 World Health Organization (WHO) definition of MI under Category C. This classification defines probable MI as occurring when there are ECG changes indicative of MI or when there is incomplete information on cardiac biomarkers in an individual presenting with symptoms of ischemia and no evidence of a non-coronary cause [22].

Hai-bin wei team study the use of laser technologies significantly lower the risk of complication associated with prostatectomy even with large prostate and as Cadwell suggest the use of modern ST segment monitoring in the operation theater which display ST segments depression numerically may help in early diagnosis of MI [23,24].

In this case, the probable diagnosis made posthumously hinges primarily on typical symptoms of MI, such as Type 3 MI, given that these symptoms occurred immediately before CA. With only a ten-minute window available, there was not enough time for further investigation and treatment.

Conclusion

TURP procedure under SA in addition to anesthesia and surgery complication it may increase risk of acute MI in non-high risk patient. We can suggest that some patient didn't reveal MI finding in 5 lead ECG despite symptom.

Given the challenges in detecting MI across all cardiac structures using standard 3 or 5 lead ECGs, in the operation theater it is advisable to use recent monitor which display ST segments change numerical or the immediate availability of continues 12‑lead ECG monitoring or echocardiography in the operating theater for emergency diagnosis.

There is a need for a new protocol for managing MI, especially in resource-limited settings. In conscious patients presenting symptoms such as severe chest pain, fatigue, cold sweats, and vomiting, it is recommended to administer antiarrhythmic and coronary vasodilator medications even without a confirmed diagnosis. Changing practice to laser technology which is less risk.

Perform coronary angiography preoperatively for patient above the age of 50.

This case adds to a series of recently published accidents that occurred during TURP under SA in healthy patients.
