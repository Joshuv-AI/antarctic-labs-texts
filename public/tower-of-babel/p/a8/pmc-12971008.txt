Effect of the sodium glucose transporter-2 inhibitor dapagliflozin on urine and blood biomarkers in dogs with heart disease
Authors: Laura K Massey, Jessica L Ward, Alexandra V Crooks, Emilie Guillot, Jose Ceron, Camilla Rubio, Victoria Convey, Melissa Tropf, Mei-Jyun Ciou, Jonathan P Mochel, Mark A Oyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSodium-glucose transport 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) decrease morbidity and mortality in human patients with heart failure through a variety of proposed mechanisms, including natriuresis and glucosuria. The effects of SGLT2i on urine sodium (uNa) and urine glucose (uGlu) concentrations in dogs with heart disease have not been evaluated.Hypothesis/ObjectivesAdministration of the SGLT2i dapagliflozin to dogs with heart disease will be well-tolerated and increase uNa and uGlu concentrations. Laboratory results and biomarker assays will provide insight into potential mechanisms of action.AnimalsTen client-owned dogs with heart disease with or without congestive heart failure.MethodsProspective multicenter open-label uncontrolled study. Blood and urine biochemistry and oxidative and inflammatory biomarkers were evaluated at baseline and 5-7 days after 0.45-1 mg/kg/day PO dapagliflozin.ResultsA treatment effect was observed for uGlu (baseline, 14 mg/dL; IQR, 2-19 mg/dL; treatment effect +3102 mg/dL; IQR, 2390-4387 mg/dL; P = .002) but not for uNa (baseline, 75 mmol/L; IQR, 28-129 mmol/L; treatment effect, −41 mmol/L; IQR, −96-21 mmol/L; P = .12). Serum total thiol concentration increased (baseline, 145 μmol/L; SD, 32 μmol/L; treatment effect, +7 μmol/L; SD, 9 μmol/L; P = .04), suggesting an antioxidative effect. Serious adverse effects, including hypoglycemia or ketoacidosis, were not observed.Conclusions and clinical importanceShort-term PO dapagliflozin was well-tolerated and associated with glucosuria but not natriuresis. Clinical benefit of SGLT2i in dogs with heart disease might include non-natriuretic mechanisms, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, or metabolic effects.Keywords: congestive heart failure, diuretics, glucosuria, natriuresis

Introduction

Sodium-glucose transport 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) lower morbidity and mortality in human patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D).1 Blood glucose concentration is lowered by blockade of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 receptor in the proximal tubules of the kidneys, thereby increasing urine glucose (uGlu) and sodium (uNa) concentrations. In addition to blood glucose control, SGLT2i significantly decrease risk for heart failure, renal injury, atherosclerosis, and death from cardiovascular or renal causes, which represent major causes of death in humans with T2D. Subsequent studies that included humans with primary causes of heart disease without T2D reported that SGLT2i lowered cardiovascular morbidity and mortality,2-4 and SGLT2i such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin currently are recommended for a variety of types and stages of heart disease in humans. Despite their use and clinical efficacy, the underlying mechanisms of SGLT2i action are incompletely understood.2,5 A variety of different hemodynamic, metabolic, renoprotective, and cardioprotective mechanisms have been proposed, including osmotic diuresis, natriuresis, altered cardiac and renal substrate utilization, antioxidative effects, anti-inflammatory actions, and antifibrotic properties.6-8 It is important to investigate these mechanisms to determine clinical utility in veterinary species.

In veterinary medicine, use of SGLT2i in dogs and cats with a variety of different diseases, including diabetes mellitus and kidney and heart disease, is an area of interest and investigation,9-13 but few data regarding the use of SGLT2i in veterinary species with heart disease are available. We sought to explore the tolerability and effects of short-term PO dapagliflozin administration on serum glucose concentration (sGlu), uGlu, urine and blood electrolyte concentrations, and serum oxidation and inflammatory biomarker concentrations in dogs with heart disease. We hypothesized that dapagliflozin would increase fractional excretion of glucose (FEGlu) and sodium (FENa), increase uGlu and uNa, and be well-tolerated. Our aim was to provide insights that would help inform future longer-term studies of mechanism, efficacy, and safety related to this class of drug in dogs with heart disease.

Materials and methods

A prospective multicenter open label and uncontrolled study was designed and approved by the Institutional Animal Use and Care Committees of the University of Pennsylvania and Iowa State University. Informed owner consent was obtained. Dogs weighing 5-22 kg with echocardiographic left heart enlargement or systolic dysfunction presented to the Matthew J. Ryan Veterinary Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania and the Lloyd Veterinary Medical Center of Iowa State University were recruited. Inclusion criteria included an echocardiographic examination performed within the previous 90 days that identified left heart dimensions exceeding cited reference or clinical staging values,14,15 including ventricular 2-dimensional long axis end-diastolic normalized dimension ≥ 1.7, left ventricular 2-dimensional long axis end-systolic normalized dimension ≥ 1.2, or 2-dimensional short-axis left atrium to aorta dimension ratio (LA:Ao) ≥ 1.6. Dogs with and without a history of congestive heart failure (CHF) were eligible. Dogs receiving any of the following drugs PO were eligible as long as the dose had not been changed within 7 days before inclusion: furosemide, torsemide, spironolactone, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, pimobendan, thiazides, amlodipine, nitrates, digoxin, taurine, carnitine, β blockers, calcium channel blockers, fish oil, coenzyme Q10, and antiarrhythmics. Exclusion criteria included history of diabetes mellitus, serum creatinine concentration > 1.8 mg/dL, ≥ 2+ proteinuria, or > 1 granular cast per high power field in urine samples with urine specific gravity < 1.030. Changes to any cardiovascular medications or the primary diet fed by owners during the study period were not allowed.

At baseline evaluation, blood and urine were collected for biochemical analysis including serum sodium (sNa), potassium (sK), chloride (sCl), calcium, albumin, creatinine (sCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and plasma N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) concentrations. Urine sodium, uGlu, urine chloride, urine potassium (uK), and urine creatinine (uCr) concentrations and urine specific gravity (USG) from urine samples obtained by voiding or cystocentesis were measured. Whole blood samples were used to measure blood ketones, specifically β-hydroxybutyrate and glucose concentrations (Nova Vet Ketone/Glucose meter, Nova Biomedical, Waltham, MA USA). The uNa to uCr ratio (uNa:uCr) and uNa to uK ratio (uNa:uK) were calculated. Fractional excretion of sodium (FENa), potassium, and FEGlu were calculated using the following formula as an example: FeNa = (uNa × sCr)/(sNa × uCr) × 100%.16 Proportion of electrolyte-free water excretion (EFWE) was calculated as (uNa + uK)/sNa where values close to 0 represent a larger proportion of EFWE than higher values.17 Biochemical and urine assays were performed in the clinical laboratories of the 2 participating sites. Owners were asked to complete a previously published questionnaire about their dogs’ quality of life.18

Aliquots of serum were stored at −80°C for batched analysis of oxidative stress and inflammatory markers. Inflammation and oxidation panels were performed as previously described and included assays for inflammatory markers including ferritin, C-reactive protein, haptoglobin, paraoxonase 1, uric acid, and butyrylcholinesterase as well as oxidative stress markers including cupric reducing antioxidant capacity, Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity, peroxide activity, reactive oxygen-derived metabolites, advanced oxidation protein products, corrected glutathione peroxidase, total oxidative status, and total thiol content.19-22

Once daily PO dapagliflozin (Farxiga, AstraZeneca) was administered by the owners as either a single 5 mg tablet for dogs weighing ≤ 11 kg or a single 10 mg tablet for dogs weighing > 12 kg, which resulted in a dosage between 0.45 and 1.0 mg/kg/day. Owners were instructed to administer dapagliflozin in the morning with food at the same time each day, including the day of the reevaluation visit. Revaluations were performed 5-7 days after starting PO dapagliflozin and scheduled to occur at approximately the same time of day as the baseline evaluation. Blood and urine testing was repeated at the reevaluation visit in the same manner as the baseline evaluation.

Data were tabulated and tested for normality using D’Agostino–Pearson tests. Normally distributed data are shown as mean and SD and non-normally distributed data are shown as median and interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons among timepoints were made using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Treatment effect was calculated by subtracting baseline results from treatment results. To maximize sensitivity and generate hypotheses for future studies, we did not correct for multiple comparisons. Statistical analysis was performed using commercial software (Prism 9.5.1 for macOS, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA).

Results

Eleven dogs, including 6 dogs (6/11, 55%) from Iowa State University and 5 dogs (5/11, 45%) from University of Pennsylvania were recruited for the study. One dog from Iowa State University experienced diarrhea after receiving its first PO dose of dapagliflozin was withdrawn from the study, and the diarrhea resolved without treatment. Data from the remaining 10 dogs were analyzed. Eight dogs (8/10, 80%) had echocardiographic evidence of myxomatous mitral valve disease based on abnormal leaflet thickness and prolapse and moderate to severe mitral regurgitation, 1 dog (1/10, 10%) had idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy based on echocardiographic evidence of severe left ventricular myocardial failure, and 1 dog (1/10, 10%) had echocardiographic left atrial and left ventricular dilatation and electrocardiographic evidence of atrial standstill. Six dogs (6/10, 60%) were male, and 4 dogs (4/10, 40%) were female. Mean body weight was 11.1 kg (SD, 4.5 kg), and the mean age was 10.5 years (SD, 3.4 years). The mean left atrial to aortic root diameter ratio was 2.1 (SD, 0.4). The median normalized left ventricular end-diastolic internal diameter was 1.9 (IQR, 1.8-2.1) and median normalized left ventricular end-systolic diameter was 1.1 (IQR, 1.0-1.2). Ten dogs (10/10, 100%) dogs were receiving PO pimobendan. Three dogs (3/10, 30%) had a history of CHF and were receiving PO loop diuretics, including furosemide in 2 dogs (2/10, 20%) and torsemide in 1 dog (1/10, 10%). Seven dogs (7/10, 70%) were receiving angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), and 3 dogs (3/10, 30%) were receiving spironolactone. The mean administered dapagliflozin dose during the study was 0.66 mg/kg PO q24h (range, 0.47-0.96 mg/kg/day).

The median duration of time from the morning PO administration of preexisting cardiac medications to blood and urine collection at baseline was 5.8 h (range, 3.5-7.0 h). The median duration of time from morning PO cardiac drug administration, including dapagliflozin, to blood and urine collection on the day of study reevaluation was 5.7 h (range, 3.3-6.6 h). Blood biochemistry and urine assay results at baseline and effect of PO dapagliflozin are shown in Table 1. A positive treatment effect on uGlu and negative treatment effect on sGlu concentration was observed (Figure 1). Treatment decreased uK and uCr concentrations but did not affect uNa concentration (Figure 2). Median FEGlu increased from baseline to reevaluation whereas median FENa did not change between timepoints (Figure 3). Inflammatory and oxidation marker assay results are shown in Table 2 and a treatment effect on the anti-inflammatory biomarker haptoglobin and antioxidative stress biomarker total thiol was observed.

Discussion

A main finding of our study was the marked increases in uGlu and FEGlu in the absence of clinically meaningful changes in sGlu, sNa, or ketone concentration. These data support the effect and tolerability of SGLT2i in dogs with heart disease with respect to causing substantial glucosuria without adverse effects related to hypoglycemia or ketonemia. Our results closely mirror studies of other patient populations receiving SGLT2i, including healthy23 and obese dogs24; diabetic rats, dogs, and cats11,13,25-27; and humans with T2D1,28 or heart disease,3,4 all of which documented marked glucosuria without clinically relevant hypoglycemia. In a study of dogs with T2D,11 PO canagliflozin increased FEGlu nearly 4-fold and decreased serum glucose concentration from baseline without inducing clinically apparent episodes of hypoglycemia or ketosis. In another study of dogs with T2D,13 PO enavogliflozin over a range of different dosing schedules did not result in significant changes in fasting glucose or serum ketone concentrations.

A second finding of our study was the absence of changes in uNa and FeNa, which at first consideration appears inconsistent with the effect of SGLT2i in blocking both glucose and Na uptake from the proximal tubule lumen. In human patients, the effect of SGLT2i on FeNa and uNa is variable and can be time dependent.29 An increase in uNa after administration of SGLT2i appears to be negated over time or transient in humans29 as well as dogs.23 One study30 in humans reported that acute natriuresis was decreased by increased distal nephron sodium absorption, increased interstitial kidney urea concentrations, and activation of vasopressin. Whether similar renal and neurohormonal adaptations occur in dogs could be explored using previously reported methods and assays.23,31,32 A review33 of 15 different trials in humans reported lack of objective evidence of durable decongestion or volume depletion after SGLT2i administration for up to 3 months after the start of treatment. These results suggest that the clinical benefit seen in human patients is not merely a result of the drug’s natriuretic and diuretic potential.5,6,34,35 Our data in dogs with heart disease provide clues regarding how we might similarly anticipate SGLT2i to work in dogs with heart disease. In humans with heart failure, a multitude of SGLT2i effects other than natriuresis, including metabolic, oxidative, inflammatory, renoprotective, and neurohormonal suppressive effects, have emerged as the most likely mechanisms.6,8,36-38 The diversity of potential mechanisms has important implications on hypothesis generation for future studies and selection of clinical and surrogate outcomes in dogs. For instance, future studies might look less at hemodynamic and congestive signs, such as dyspnea scores, or measures of volume status, such as body weight, natriuretic peptides or PCV, and more at metabolic, neurohormonal, oxidative, or inflammatory markers as surrogate measures of effect. A lack of substantial natriuresis suggests that SGLT2i work alongside, as opposed to replace, loop diuretics, and still require nephron blockade of Na resorption from the loop of Henle and distal convoluted tubule. Without substantial durable natriuretic or diuretic effects, SGLT2i might be similar to other pleotropic drugs, such as ACEIs and aldosterone antagonists, which act on many different disease pathways, other than simply hemodynamics.

Increasingly, the heart disease phenotype is associated with a pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative state that is both a result and effect of disease.21,39,40 Our study measured markers of oxidative stress and inflammation unrelated to diuresis and natriuresis. Two markers (haptoglobin and total thiol) increased between timepoints. Haptoglobin is a moderate acute phase protein and antioxidant that traps proinflammatory hemoglobin dimers that result from disintegration of hemoglobin tetramers damaged by oxidative stress.41 Low haptoglobin concentration as well as haptoglobin genotypes associated with decreased binding to inflammatory molecules are associated with higher risk of major adverse cardiac events.42 In human diabetic patients, low haptoglobin can be treated with antioxidant supplements, such as vitamin E.43 Thiols are sulfur-containing antioxidants that neutralize and lower concentrations of reactive oxygen species.44 Low total thiol concentrations are observed in humans with heart failure and are a risk factor for increased mortality.45 In dogs with myxomatous valve disease or idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy, total thiol concentration is decreased in dogs with severe disease as compared with healthy dogs.21 Our results have some similarities with a previous study21 in which alleviation of clinical signs of CHF using diuretic, positive inotrope, and renin angiotensin aldosterone system drugs was associated with increased thiol concentration in dogs, the difference being our results were observed in dogs with less severe disease without clinical signs. Another biomarker of interest is uric acid, which is a marker of oxidative stress and inflammation in heart failure strongly associated with severity and poor clinical outcomes.46 Uric acid is of particular interest because of glucose inhibition of uric acid reabsorption by urate transporter 1 in the proximal tubule.46,47 In some of the largest SGLT2i heart failure trials in humans,48-50 SGLT2i was associated with increased urinary uric acid excretion and decreased serum uric acid concentrations. Observational studies report an association between increased serum uric acid concentration and loop diuretic administration, and coadministration with SGLT2i decreases serum uric acid concentration by increased urinary excretion.51,52 In future studies, measurement of both serum and urine uric acid concentrations could shed light on whether uric acid is an important biomarker in dogs receiving PO SGLT2i. Our results warrant further investigation into the potential action of SGLT2i on inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways.

Another of our findings was that blood ketone concentration was unchanged by SGLT2i administration. Blood ketones were measured in our study to monitor both for adverse events such as euglycemic ketoacidosis, as well as to explore potential beneficial effects of dapagliflozin on metabolism, such as a mild increase in ketone concentration. In humans, euglycemic ketoacidosis is a rare but clinically important complication of SGLT2i caused by a combination of low insulin and high glucagon concentrations, and its clinical presentation and treatment are similar to diabetic ketoacidosis, except for the absence of severe hyperglycemia.53 On the other hand, a mild increase in ketones associated with SGLT2i might be beneficial in heart disease, because ketones are an important alternative fuel source in the failing heart.5 The normal healthy heart predominantly metabolizes fatty acids, but in the failing heart, ketones serve as an important efficient alternative fuel source and energy supply.54,55 In animal models56-58 and in humans59 receiving SGLT2i, mild degrees of ketosis are associated with preserved myocardial adenosine triphosphate production and improved clinical outcomes. In our study, euglycemic ketoacidosis was not detected nor did we find changes in blood ketones after treatment, but assessment was limited to measurement of β-hydroxybutyrate. Future studies exploring the metabolic mechanisms of SGLT2i could employ more comprehensive methods, such as tissue or blood metabolomics or positron emission tomography.60

In humans with T2D, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease, SGLT2i decrease blood pressure and glomerular damage and improve renal oxygen delivery and mitochondrial function and are strongly renoprotective despite potential changes in volume status or renal perfusion.61,62 In one study of human patients with heart failure,3 dapagliflozin was associated with a small but significant increase in serum creatinine concentration, but the incidence of serious adverse renal effects was lower in the treatment group compared with placebo. These data are consistent with the general consensus that the risk of worsening failure is lowered in patients receiving SGLT2i.4,7 We were unable to evaluate the long-term effects of SGLT2i on kidney function, but blood markers of renal function and hydration status remained unchanged, which supports the tolerability and low risk of acute adverse renal events with the dosage used in our study.

In our study, short-term PO dapagliflozin in dogs with heart disease was well-tolerated with the exception of 2 events. One dog was presented with lower urinary signs shortly after study completion, which resolved with empirical antibiotic treatment. In humans receiving SGLT2i, glucosuria mildly increases the risk of urinary tract infections,4,63 but the magnitude and clinical importance of this risk is controversial.64 Another dog developed diarrhea after the first dose of study drug on the day of enrollment. Diarrhea was not among the reported adverse effects of SGLT2i administration in a large meta-analysis65 of > 30,000 humans but diarrhea was among the most frequently reported adverse effects in cats receiving SGLT2i for treatment of diabetes.9,26

Our study had some limitations. Small sample size and short study duration could have prevented us from detecting important changes associated with treatment, but we observed changes in uGlu, FEGlu, and inflammatory and oxidation biomarkers that can help inform larger studies. Small sample size could have caused type II errors with respect to our negative findings involving FENa, sNa, ketones, BUN, and creatinine, but our results align with previous studies in humans and dogs. Our cohort included dogs with a variety of different heart diseases, stages, and background medications, and additional studies in more specifically defined patient populations are needed. Because the duration of treatment in our study was short, longer studies that examine the effects of SGLT2i on important clinical outcomes, potential adverse effects, and quality of life are needed. Our study did not include a control group or randomization, but the paired nature of observations allowed us to draw some conclusions about tolerability and effect on important blood and urine markers.

In conclusion, short-term once daily PO administration of dapagliflozin at dosages between 0.47 and 0.96 mg/kg/day was well tolerated and associated increases in uGlu and FEGlu without increases in uNa or FENa. An effect of SGLT2i on inflammation and oxidation was suggested by observed changes in serum concentrations of biomarkers between study timepoints. Collectively, these data support the notion that in dogs with heart disease, the potential benefits of SGLT2i are multifaceted and that studies of SGLT2i should consider a broad range of mechanisms beyond diuresis and natriuresis.
