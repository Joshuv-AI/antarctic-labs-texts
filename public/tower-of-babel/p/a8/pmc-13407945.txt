Daratumumab in combination with GDP chemotherapy in CD38-positive relapsed/refractory peripheral T-cell lymphoma: a phase 2 study of the Fondazione Italiana Linfomi
Authors: Mario Ballerini, Elisa Lucchini, Emanuele Ravano, Jessica Rosa, Caterina Patti, Annalisa Arcari, Federica Cavallo, Laura Ballotta, Giacomo Loseto, Andrea Evangelista, Valentina Tabanelli, Francesco Zaja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs) are rare, aggressive malignancies with poor outcomes, particularly in the relapsed or refractory (R/R) setting. Given the frequent CD38 expression in PTCLs, this multicenter, open-label, single-arm phase II study (FIL_Dara-GDP; EudraCT 2018-002644-91) evaluated daratumumab, an anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody, combined with gemcitabine, dexamethasone, and cisplatin (D-GDP) in CD38-positive systemic PTCLs. The trial aimed to achieve a complete response (CR) rate of ≥ 40% after four cycles. Eligibility required ≥ 5% CD38-positive tumor cells and one or two prior treatment lines. The study was terminated early after the enrollment of eight of the planned 35 patients due to unacceptable hematologic and non-hematologic toxicities. Grade 4 thrombocytopenia and grade 3–4 neutropenia occurred in 75% and 63% of patients, respectively, with two toxicity-related deaths. No CRs were achieved, and the overall response rate was 13%. Median progression-free and overall survival at six months were 12.5% and 25%, respectively. The high hematological toxicity observed likely reflects the possible additive myelosuppressive effect of daratumumab to GDP in a pretreated PTCL population. Although daratumumab has shown therapeutic potential in T-cell malignancies, its combination with intensive chemotherapy appears unsuitable in this setting. Future studies should explore safer, targeted approaches to optimize CD38-directed therapy in PTCL.Keywords: T-cell lymphoma, Daratumumab, GDP, CD38, Relapsed/refractory, PTCL

Introduction

CD38 is frequently expressed in peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs) and, as experienced with other monoclonal antibody-based regimens, the addition of an anti-CD38 agent to chemotherapy might offer a therapeutic advantage over standard chemotherapy alone. Based on this rationale, the present study investigated the combination of daratumumab with gemcitabine, dexamethasone, and cisplatin (GDP) in a cohort of patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) CD38 positive systemic PTCLs. Unfortunately, the trial yielded negative results and was terminated early due to an unacceptable level of both hematologic and non-hematologic toxicities.

PTCLs represent a rare, heterogeneous group of aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphomas with poor prognosis. Particularly in the R/R setting, therapeutic options remain limited, and outcomes are generally poor in terms of progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), with fewer than one in three patients alive at five years [1].

CD38 expression has been previously documented in nearly 60% and 80% of patients with PTCL not otherwise specified (NOS) and angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL), respectively; conversely, CD38 positivity is rarely observed in anaplastic large cell lymphomas [2].

Methods

Fondazione Italiana Linfomi (FIL) promoted a multicenter, open-label, single-arm phase II trial (FIL_Dara-GDP; EudraCT 2018-002644-91) to evaluate the use of daratumumab, a human monoclonal anti-CD38 antibody, in combination with GDP (D-GDP) in patients with R/R PTCLs. Eligibility criteria included a documented diagnosis of CD38-positive PTCL-NOS, AITL or other nodal lymphomas of follicular T-helper (TFH) cell origin as defined in the 2017 edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification; [3] age between 18 and 75; ECOG performance status from 0 to 2; previous treatment with one or two lines of therapy; informed consent; ethical committee approval of the participating centers. Diagnosis was centralized (Division of Haematopathology, IEO, European Institute of Oncology IRCCS, Milan, Italy). Only patients with a percentage of CD38-positive tumor cells ≥ 5% in the relapse biopsy, or in the most recent biopsy in the case of refractory patients, were considered eligible for protocol study treatment. Immunohistochemical staining for CD38 was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) whole tumor sections using the Autostainer Link 48 system (Agilent Dako) after antigen retrieval (PT Link at 92 °C for 5 min in EnVision Flex Target Retrieval Solution, High pH). A primary antibody against CD38 (clone SPC32, dilution 1:80; Leica Biosystems) was applied.

The study was designed to test whether the addition of daratumumab to GDP could improve the complete response (CR) rate to at least 40% after 4 cycles of treatment, based on an A’Hern’s Single-Stage Phase II design with a planned accrual of 35 patients. Early stopping rules for safety and futility were incorporated, including the monitoring of cumulative toxicity and response rates. Treatment consisted of up to 6 courses of D-GDP induction (with daratumumab dosed 8 mg/kg on days 2 and 9 of cycle 1, and 16 mg/kg in subsequent courses), followed by daratumumab maintenance every 4 weeks up to 24 courses in responders not eligible for allogeneic stem cell transplant (allo-HSCT). Patients in CR or partial response (PR) after 6 courses of induction and eligible for transplant were addressed to allo-HSCT consolidation.

Results

The study was prematurely terminated after enrollment of only 8 of the planned 35 patients, due to the early crossing of predefined toxicity boundaries. Among the 8 patients enrolled across multiple centers, the median age was 64.5 years (range 62–72.5), 75% were men, and 88% had Ann Arbor stage IV disease. Half had bone marrow involvement and 50% presented with systemic B symptoms. Prior therapies included CHOEP, CHOP, or CHOP-based regimens followed by autologous HSCT in some cases (the characteristics of the patients at the time of entry into the study are summarized in Table 1).

The Dara-GDP toxicity profile was particularly concerning. Grade 4 thrombocytopenia occurred in 75% of patients, and grade 3 or 4 neutropenia in 63% despite the use of standard G-CSF prophylaxis was mandatory. Additional serious adverse events included pneumonia in one patient, cardiovascular complications (one patient experienced a pulmonary embolism and another an acute myocardial infarction), and febrile neutropenia in one patient. Two patients died from toxicity-related complications: one due to septic shock and one due to cardiac failure following acute myocardial infarction. The toxicity was substantial and of rapid onset, often precluding the possibility to complete the induction phase.

Outcomes in terms of efficacy were also disappointing. No complete responses were observed after induction (CR rate 0.0%, 95% CI: 0.00–37%), and only one patient achieved a PR, showing an overall response rate of 13.0% (95% CI: 0.00–43%). The estimated progression-free survival (PFS) at six months was 12.5% (95% CI: 0.66–42.3%), and overall survival (OS) at six months was 25.0% (95% CI: 3.71–55.81%). Treatment was discontinued early in 6 of 8 patients (75%), with all patients dying, primarily due to progressive disease.

Discussion

These findings are in contrast with prior experiences using GDP in R/R PTCL, where toxicity appeared more manageable, and response rates were higher, even if in cohorts of patients with a lower median age than ours [4, 5]. These negative results may reflect a combination of several factors, including a frail cohort with unfavorable disease biology, the use of GDP rather than a less toxic chemotherapy backbone, and a possible additive or synergistic toxicity introduced by the combination with daratumumab.

Despite the disappointing outcome of our trial, it is well established that daratumumab provides significant clinical benefit in multiple myeloma [6, 7], and there is a growing evidence of the possible therapeutic effect of daratumumab also in other lymphoproliferative disorders and, specifically, in T-cell malignancies [8]. The DELPHINUS phase II trial demonstrated that adding daratumumab to multi-agent chemotherapy could induce meaningful responses in pediatric patients with relapsed/refractory acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) or lymphoblastic lymphoma (LBL), showing an overall response rate of 83.3% and CR rates from 30 to 60% in T-cell ALL and T-cell LBL subgroups with a manageable safety profile [9]. A recent phase I trial combined daratumumab and venetoclax with aclarubicin, cytarabine, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, etoposide (CAGE regimen) in adults with late R/R T-precursor ALL/LBL, reporting promising activity with CR near to 50% and tolerable toxicity profile [10]. Additional reports describe complete remissions in heavily pretreated early T-precursor ALL/LBL with regimens including daratumumab, venetoclax, azacitidine, and dexamethasone, and exploratory use of daratumumab plus nelarabine in refractory lymphoblastic lymphoma, supporting its potential synergy with cytotoxic or targeted agents [11, 12]. In peripheral lymphomas, case reports have documented the efficacy of daratumumab in combination with attenuated chemotherapy regimens such as cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine and prednisone at 50% of the dosage (mini-CHOP), particularly in frail patients with plasmablastic lymphoma [13]. Conversely, a phase II trial for patients with relapsed or refractory natural killer/T-cell lymphoma nasal type explored daratumumab as a single agent with an acceptable safety profile but limited efficacy with a 25% of overall response rate, no CR achieved and short duration of response, underscoring the need for rational combinations to potentiate anti-tumor activity [14]. Collectively, these data indicate that while daratumumab holds promise in T-cell neoplasms, its clinical effectiveness and safety strongly depend on patient selection, quantitative CD38 expression, and the choice of chemotherapy backbone.

The high hematological toxicity observed in our trial likely reflects the possible additive myelosuppressive effect of daratumumab to GDP in a pretreated PTCL population. Previous studies in multiple myeloma highlighted the myelosuppressive effect of daratumumab when combined to other agents. The CASSIOPEIA phase III trial comparing daratumumab plus bortezomib, thalidomide, and dexamethasone (D-VTd) with VTd alone showed a higher incidence of grade 3–4 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia (28% versus 15% and 11% versus 7%, respectively) in the daratumumab arm. Furthermore, when comparing stem cell mobilization outcomes, the median number of CD34 + cells collected was 6.3 × 10⁶/kg in the D-VTd group versus 8.9 × 10⁶/kg in the VTd group, with 22% of patients in the D-VTd arm requiring plerixafor compared to 8% in the VTd arm [6]. Similar results were also found in the GRIFFIN phase III trial which compared daratumumab plus bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (D-VRd) to VRd [7]. The mechanism sustaining daratumumab myelotoxic effect is not yet clear; in vitro studies do not support a direct effect of daratumumab on hematopoietic stem cells [15], and therefore its potential influence on the bone marrow microenvironment, particularly in the context of multi-agent therapeutic regimens, warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the excessive toxicity of D-GDP combination appears to have determined the negative results of this study. Future studies could explore less intensive combinations possibly integrating daratumumab with novel targeted agents as venetoclax or drugs with epigenetic action. Such strategies may help balance efficacy and safety, maximizing the potential of CD38-directed immunotherapy in this challenging disease setting. We believe that sharing these results is essential to optimize the design of future studies and to prevent redundant or potentially harmful strategies in this difficult-to-treat patient population.
