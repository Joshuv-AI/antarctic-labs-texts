Patterns of proactive health behaviors and associated factors among middle-aged and older women in China: a latent class analysis
Authors: Yujie Lei, Hongxia Zhuo, Kangning Li, Zuxia Li, Huiwu Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo address the challenges of aging, the Chinese government has introduced the localized concept of “proactive health”, which emphasizes individual responsibility for health, proactive health information seeking, and health-promoting actions. This study aims to identify latent classes of proactive health behaviors and associated factors among middle-aged and older Chinese women using latent class analysis.MethodsA serial cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from 4,243 middle-aged and older women who underwent physical examinations at a tertiary hospital in Changsha City, China, between 2017 and 2023. Sociodemographic characteristics, laboratory data, medical history, and proactive health behavior data were extracted from Health Management Center. Latent class analysis was employed to classify proactive health behavioral patterns, followed by multinomial logistic regression to identify factors associated with class membership.ResultsAmong middle-aged and older women, proactive health behaviors are categorized into three latent classes: Comprehensive Health Practitioners (29.44%), Selective Health Maintainers (54.75%), and High-Risk Behavior Engagers (15.81%). Multivariate logistic regression identified the following factors associated with proactive health behaviors: age, body mass index, education, occupation, family history of diabetes, family history of cardiovascular disease, menopausal status, psychological problems, and self-rated health status.ConclusionThe majority of middle-aged and older Chinese women are categorized as Selective Health Maintainers in terms of proactive health behaviors. In nursing practice, it is essential to develop personalized plans targeting each latent class. Future efforts should focus on enhancing proactive health education for middle-aged and older women, encouraging the adoption of various healthy behaviors to improve health outcomes and facilitate healthy aging.ImpactThis study offers significant implications for nursing practice by identifying three distinct latent classes of proactive health behaviors among middle-aged and older Chinese women. It aids hospitals and communities in developing personalized intervention measures for this demographic, enhancing health conditions, and actively addressing the challenges of aging.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Middle-aged and older women, Proactive health behavior, Associated factors, Latent class analysis

Introduction

The global population aged 60 and above is expanding rapidly. According to the World Health Organization [1], the number of older adults is expected to rise to 1.4 billion by 2030 and to 2.1 billion by 2050, underscoring the urgency of addressing aging-related challenges. This demographic shift amplifies the burden of age-related health disparities, particularly among middle-aged and older women. China, as one of the fastest-aging nations, reported 310 million individuals aged 60 years or older in 2024, accounting for 22.0% of its population [2]. Chronic diseases show high incidence and frequent multimorbidity in middle and old age, with notably higher prevalence among women [3]. Unaddressed, these cumulative risks significantly compromise later-life health outcomes. Therefore, improving the health of middle-aged and older women is of critical importance.

To address the challenges posed by population aging, China has introduced the indigenous concept of “Proactive Health” [4], a policy-oriented model emphasizing individual responsibility, prevention, and active public engagement in wellness [5]. This strategy involves collaboration across society, but places individual behavioral choices at its core [6]. On an individual level, it promotes proactive health behaviors, which include seeking health information and adopting health-promoting activities. This approach positions individuals as primary managers of their own health and emphasizes their agency in making informed choices [7]. The “Healthy China Action (2019—2030)” plan [8] has proposed a series of significant actions, including a rational diet, restriction on smoking and alcohol, regular exercise, healthy sleep habits, regular physical examinations, and the popularization of health-related medical knowledge. These actions aim to transform the concept of proactive health into concrete actions and promote the improvement of comprehensive health literacy.

Mounting evidence suggests that modifiable health behaviors have a significant impact on the well-being of middle-aged and older women. These behaviors include adequate fruit and vegetable consumption [9], limited intake of sugar-sweetened beverages [10], smoking cessation [11], alcohol moderation [11], regular physical activity [12], sufficient sleep [13], and routine health screenings [14]. However, existing studies have predominantly examined these behaviors in isolation, failing to capture their synergistic or trade-off effects when adopted concurrently [11, 15]. Moreover, a holistic investigation that integrates both fundamental daily maintenance behaviors and higher-order proactive activities is lacking [6, 16]. Furthermore, few studies have focused on the unique sociocultural context of aging women in China, which is crucial for understanding their health behaviors [6, 16]. Finally, previous studies predominantly rely on traditional variable-centered analysis, which assumes sample homogeneity and may obscure the existence of meaningful subgroups with unique characteristics [17].

To fill in the research gaps, this study employs latent class analysis (LCA) [18] to explore the heterogeneous profiles of proactive health behaviors among middle-aged and older Chinese women. LCA offers practical and clinical insights that enhance traditional variable-centered research. From a practical perspective, it can facilitate accurate risk prediction, customized public health strategies, and targeted resource allocation, ultimately reducing health disparities. From a clinical perspective, it provides a roadmap for improved clinical decision-making, personalized patient management, proactive risk mitigation, and enhanced patient engagement, ultimately leading to improved individual health outcomes. This approach not only provides an evidence base for community nurses to implement stratified health management but also facilitates the transition of active health from a theoretical paradigm to nursing practice. Traditionally, the role of community nurses has been defined by direct clinical care and basic public health services [19]. However, the profession is evolving toward a more proactive, population-focused approach that addresses complex health challenges [19]. Our study is grounded in this expanded scope, positioning community nurses as primary implementers of advanced health management strategies. Our findings provide evidence that supports and formalizes this extended role, ultimately improving patient outcomes and resource allocation within the community setting.

Methods

Design

This study employed a serial cross-sectional analysis using data collected from the Health Management Center of a large general hospital in Changsha, China, from January 2017 to December 2023. All data for any given participant were collected at a single time point and extracted from the most recent annual health examination record. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist was used as the guideline in this study [20].

Participants and data collection

Data were extracted from the health examination database using the archival ID as the unique identifier, encompassing general demographic information, physical examination results, laboratory test findings, and lifestyle questionnaire data. To ensure confidentiality, all personally identifiable information (including names, addresses, phone numbers, and medical record numbers) was removed by the database administrators during the data export process. We recruited female participants aged 45 years or older with normal communicative capacity, ultimately enrolling 4,243 eligible individuals in the study. Sample size

The recommended sample size for LCA is 500 participants, as larger sample sizes provide greater statistical power to identify latent classes [18]. The sample size of 4,243 in this study meets the requirements and is considered adequate for the analysis.

Measurement

Proactive health behaviors

The proactive health behaviors examined in this study are grounded in the core principle of “proactive health,” with particular emphasis on the two dimensions of personal responsibility and self-empowerment [4]. Based on the two dimensions and in line with the “Healthy China Action (2019–2030),” we define proactive health behaviors as comprising two tiers: fundamental daily maintenance behaviors and higher-order proactive activities. Fundamental daily maintenance behaviors include balanced diet, moderate exercise, and adequate sleep, which reflect the principle of taking personal responsibility for one’s health through daily practices. Higher-order proactive activities include actively acquiring health information and participating in health management, which reflect the concept of empowerment, demonstrating an individual’s ability and initiative to seek resources and make informed choices to optimize their health.

The Proactive Health Behavior was derived from the Health Checkup Self-Assessment Questionnaire (HCSAQ) [21]. The HCSAQ comprises six domains: (1) medical history, (2) physical symptoms, (3) lifestyle habits, (4) psychological stress, (5) sleep quality, and (6) health literacy. This questionnaire has been validated [22] with good reliability and validity, and has been utilized in several published studies [23, 24]. The health management center involved in the current study utilized this instrument for data collection. In this study, proactive health behaviors were categorized into two groups: healthy behaviors and risky behaviors. Proactive healthcare

Proactive healthcare is a composite variable derived from China’s “Proactive Health” initiative, emphasizing empowerment and agency in personal health management. It positions individuals as primary agents in maintaining their health through preventive measures and information acquisition [4, 5]. We operationalised it with two dichotomised behaviours: (a) health check-ups (healthy: ≤1 year, risky: >1 year) [28] and (b) proactive acquisition of medical knowledge (healthy: yes, risky: no). Since the low prevalence of risky behavior in each component alone (4.7% and 22.2%, respectively) could compromise latent class stability [18], a composite variable was created. Participants were classified as exhibiting overall risk in “Proactive healthcare” if they reported risky behaviors in either component.

Sociodemographic information

Sociodemographic information of the study population included age, educational level, marital status, and occupation category. There were two age categories:45–59 years and ≥ 60 years. The education levels were classified as follows: Senior high school and below, junior college or undergraduate, and master’s degree or above. Marital status was categorized as married and unmarried (single/divorced combined due to limited sample size). Occupational status was categorized as unemployed (including retirees) and employed (encompassing manual and non-manual labor).

Laboratory data measurement

Clinical experimental data were acquired through standardized measurements and blood sample collection performed by trained healthcare personnel, followed by laboratory analysis. Anthropometric measurements included blood pressure (BP), body weight, height, waist circumference (WC), and body mass index (BMI) calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (kg/m²). BMI was categorized according to Chinese criteria as follows [29]: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight(18.5–24 kg/m²), overweight(24–28 kg/m²), and obese(≥ 28 kg/m²). Biochemical analyses included fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), plasma triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).

Medical information

Medical information was exported from electronic health systems. Cardiovascular family history (encompassing hypertension, coronary heart disease, and stroke), diabetes family history, menopausal status, parity, history of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and gestational hypertension were obtained via self-reported data. Self-rated health was categorized as poor, moderate, or good. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure > 85 mmHg (measured on ≥ 2 occasions) or on treatment. According to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes (2024 edition) [30]FPG (mg/dL) was categorized as follows: normal (< 100), prediabetes (100–125), and diabetes (≥ 126 or taking prescribed oral hypoglycemic medications or insulin). Metabolic syndrome was diagnosed per the harmonized criteria requiring the presence of ≥ 3 of the following conditions: (1) abdominal obesity (WC ≥ 85 cm), (2) hypertriglyceridemia (TG ≥ 1.70 mmol/L), (3) reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L), (4) hypertension, and (5) hyperglycemia [30].

Psychological problems were evaluated using a self-administered mental health questionnaire embedded within the HCSAQ [24]. This instrument consisted of 9 items, distributed across three subscales: perceived stress (2 items), anxiety symptoms (3 items), and depressive symptoms (4 items). Each item was scored on a 3-point Likert scale: 0 (never), 1 (rarely), and 2 (frequently), with higher total scores indicating poorer mental health. Subscale-specific thresholds were applied to classify participants: a score > 2 for stress, > 3 for anxiety, and > 4 for depressive symptoms indicated clinically significant psychological concerns. Participants were dichotomized into two groups: no psychological problems (all subscales below thresholds) or presence of psychological problems (exceeding any subscale threshold). In this study, the Cronbach’s coefficient was 0.914, indicating good reliability.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval No. 2025020295).

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using R version 4.3.3 and Mplus 8.3. Descriptive statistics were provided as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Chi-square tests were used for within-group comparisons of categorical variables. Missing values (height: 1.81%, weight: 1.81%, systolic blood pressure (SBP): 0.94%, diastolic blood pressure (DBP): 0.94%, waist circumference (WC): 0.87%, triglycerides (TG): 0.19%, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C): 0.19%, and fasting blood glucose (FBG): 0.24%) were addressed through multiple linear imputation to preserve statistical power and minimize bias. Following imputation, body mass index (BMI) was recalculated using the imputed height and weight values to ensure data completeness for subsequent analyses.

LCA identified subgroups based on proactive health behaviors (binary). Models with 1–6 classes were compared using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT), and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) [18]. Lower values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC indicated better model fit. A significant p-value (p < 0.05) for BLRT and LMRT supported the superiority of the k-class model over the (k − 1)-class model [31]. Due to the large sample size (> 3,000 participants), the statistical stability of entropy-a measure of classification certainty-was deemed unreliable and thus excluded from model selection criteria [32]. Final model selection integrated both statistical fit indices and clinical interpretability to ensure meaningful subgroup characterization [31].

Multivariate logistic regression was conducted to examine the factors associated with latent classes, with significance set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Multicollinearity was acceptable (all VIF < 5.0). To control for potential time-related confounding, the 7-year participant enrollment window (2017–2023) was collapsed into three time periods defined by significant changes in healthcare access and public health policy related to the COVID-19 pandemic [33]: 2017–2019 (pre-COVID), 2020–2021 (during COVID, with strict containment measures), and 2022–2023 (post-COVID). This classification simultaneously balances external event milestones, sample-size distribution, and statistical efficiency. A chi-square test was performed to compare the distribution of latent classes across the three enrollment time periods. In the multinomial logistic regression, “enrolment time period” was added as a categorical covariate to adjust for its potential confounding effects.

Results

Demographic characteristics and medical information of the sample

The study included 4,243 middle-aged and older women (mean age 45–59 years, 79.21%). Most individuals had a normal BMI (64.86%), a college/bachelor’s education (65.73%), were employed (73.56%), and were married (98.23%). Family history was uncommon, as 58.45% reported no history of cardiovascular disease, and 91.16% reported no history of diabetes. Postmenopausal status (79.02%), parity (98.33%), and absence of gestational diabetes (98.87%) or hypertension (95.78%) were prevalent. Self-rated health was moderate in 59.27%, with 80.01% reporting no psychological distress. See more details in Table 1.

Latent class analysis of proactive health behaviors

Based on the fit indices of the LCA (Table 2), models with 1 to 6 classes were compared. The following results were confirmed: (1) As the number of classes increased from one to six, AIC, BIC, and aBIC all decreased, with the 3-class model achieving the lowest BIC value; (2) the LMRT and BLRT of the 3-class model were significant, whereas those of the 4-class models were not, meaning that the 3-class model is statistically better than the 2-class and 4-class models; (3) All three latent classes in the selected model exhibited robust Category probabilities (> 5%), ensuring clinical interpretability and minimizing overfitting. The 3-class model was selected based on a comprehensive review of fit indices, clinical meaningfulness, and interpretability. A chi-square test revealed no significant difference in latent-class distribution across the three enrollment periods (p > 0.05).

Class 1 was named Comprehensive Health Practitioners (n = 1,249, 29.44%), exhibiting low risk across all proactive health behaviors, thereby embodying a comprehensive health practice pattern. Class 3 was named High-Risk Behavior Engagers (n = 671, 15.81%), demonstrating high risk in multiple behaviors—particularly in insufficient fruit and vegetable intake, inadequate physical activity, and high consumption of SSBs—although their performance in tobacco and alcohol avoidance was relatively acceptable. Class 2 was named Selective Health Maintainers (n = 2,323, 54.75%), exhibiting a behavioral pattern intermediate between Class 1 and Class 3. They performed well in terms of SSB consumption, tobacco and alcohol avoidance, and sleep quality, but poorly in terms of fruit and vegetable intake, as well as physical activity. Although Class 2 and Class 3 showed similar distribution patterns, Class 2 exhibited better health behavior profiles in almost all indicators, with notable differences in SSBs (0.821 vs. 0.289) and sleep (0.813 vs. 0.593). Class 2 exhibited a mixed pattern that can transition to better proactive behavioral patterns after targeted interventions. In contrast, Class 3 represented a convergence of multiple high-risk behaviors that warrant more urgent and comprehensive interventions.

Associated factors of latent classes for proactive health behaviors

To investigate the factors associated with proactive health behaviors among middle-aged and older women, a multinomial logistic regression analysis was conducted. The outcome variable was the three latent classes of proactive health behaviors, with Class 1 as the reference group. Compared to Class 1, participants who were employed and had psychological problems were significantly more likely to belong to Class 2, indicating these factors as risk factors for Class 2 (all p < 0.05). Additionally, participants who were overweight or obese, engaged in physical labor and mental labor, and had psychological problems were more likely to belong to Class 3, identifying these characteristics as risk factors for Class 3 (all p < 0.05). Conversely, participants aged ≥ 60 years, with junior college or undergraduate education, graduate degree and above, a family history of diabetes, a family history of cardiovascular disease, and good self-rated health status were more likely to belong to Class 1 (all p < 0.05). Discussion

This study is the first to apply LCA to identify patterns of proactive health behaviors and their associated factors among middle-aged and older women. The findings demonstrate the applicability of this method in capturing heterogeneity within the study population. Participants were categorized into three groups based on their behaviors: Comprehensive Health Practitioners (Class 1, 29.44%), Selective Health Maintainers (Class 2, 54.75%), and High-Risk Behavior Engagers (Class 3, 15.81%). Overall, the study’s middle-aged and older women mostly partake in proactive health behaviors, aligning with previous findings [11, 26]. This indicates that most middle-aged and elderly women tend to adopt healthier behaviors, particularly avoiding smoking and drinking, possibly driven by a heightened awareness of health in response to age-related bodily changes [34]. It should be noted that this study employed an extended data collection period (2017–2023), which spanned the COVID-19 pandemic—a major public health disruption. Thus, the identified latent classes of proactive health behaviors among middle-aged and older women may be confounded by dynamic shifts in societal context, public policies, and individual behavioral adaptations that occurred during this period.

In this study’s analysis of three proactive health behavior categories, all groups performed well in avoiding smoking and alcohol, possibly due to the female composition of the sample. Fruit and vegetable consumption was notably lower than other behaviors across all groups, aligning with prior studies [35] that link this to economic factors, such as the high cost of healthy foods [36], and reduced chewing and digestive capabilities in older adults [37]. Therefore, in nursing practice, it is recommended to collaborate with nutritionists to design personalized dietary plans (e.g., providing soft foods or pureed fruits and vegetables) and promote low-cost nutritional intervention programs to improve the dietary structure of this group, thereby enhancing their proactive health literacy and encouraging the adoption of healthier eating habits.

Class 2 showed a mix of healthy and risky behaviors, aligning with the Health Belief Model [38]. This pattern focuses on “salient risk behaviors” (such as smoking, alcohol consumption, sugary drink intake, and sleep) while neglecting “hidden long-term risks” (such as inadequate physical exercise and low utilization of proactive healthcare). Therefore, tailored nursing interventions such as personalized health education and regular follow-up support should be implemented to reinforce positive behaviors and address neglected long-term risks. Nurses can utilize regular follow-up calls or messages to this group and gently remind them of their health goals, creating a low-intensity but supportive external cue for behavior maintenance.

Class 3, characterized by low fruit and vegetable intake, frequent sugary beverage consumption, and inadequate physical activity, despite moderate sleep and healthcare engagement, presented the most significant health risks, consistent with lifestyle risk clustering in previous studies [11, 15]. For these groups, practical interventions are key. Nurses should offer concrete “swap” ideas (e.g., replacing sugary drinks with water or tea). They should guide women to accessible, low-intensity activities, such as daily walking or community-based exercise classes, thereby strengthening their proactive health capabilities and encouraging the practice of diverse health behaviors.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed multiple factors associated with proactive health behaviors of middle-aged and older women. Compared to women aged 45–59 years, those aged 60 years or older were more likely to belong to the Comprehensive Health Practitioners, consistent with the findings of Zheyu Wang et al. [39]. This inclination may be due to increased health consciousness and perceived risks associated with aging [40]. Furthermore, China’s mandatory retirement age for women is 55 years, and studies show that retirement in China is associated with healthier lifestyle habits, including reduced smoking and drinking, as well as increased exercise [41, 42]. However, it is essential to recognize that retirement may also lead to potential income reductions and changes in social roles, which can negatively impact health behaviors and outcomes during retirement [43]. The retirement paradox in China reflects a potential trade-off between the increased time retirees have for health-promoting activities and the adverse health effects of decreased socioeconomic status. This complex interplay warrants further study, especially regarding gender-specific impacts and the differences between urban and rural populations.

Participants with higher education were also more likely to be Comprehensive Health Practitioners, highlighting the influence of health literacy on behavior, likely by enhancing access to health information and critical analysis skills [44]. Therefore, nurses should develop health materials that are easy to understand for distribution, ensuring accessibility for individuals with varying literacy levels. Interestingly, women with a family history of diabetes or cardiovascular disease were more likely to be Comprehensive Health Practitioners. This finding aligns with previous research [45], where heightened risk perception prompts preventive behaviors. Screening for family history during health assessments could help identify individuals who may benefit from early and intensified health guidance. Furthermore, women who self-rated their health as good were more likely to be categorized as Comprehensive Health Practitioners, consistent with prior evidence [44]. Self-rated health status may reflect objective health conditions and psychological resilience, both of which drive sustained health behaviors, with subjective health perception being consistent with health behaviors [46]. Nurses can reinforce this by positively affirming patients’ healthy choices during consultations, thereby boosting their self-efficacy and motivation to maintain those behaviors.

However, postmenopausal women were more likely to be Comprehensive Health Practitioners, differing from previous studies [47]. This discrepancy may be attributed to socioeconomic factors not controlled for in this analysis. Specifically, health behavior disparities among postmenopausal women may be influenced by variables such as income and education [47]. This study only analyzed the factor of menopause and did not combine it with other factors. Therefore, comprehensive health practitioners should develop personalized menopause management plans that address both physiological changes and psychological adjustments, focusing on reinforcing the sustainability of their health behaviors.

Our study showed that employed middle-aged and older women were more likely to be categorized as High-Risk Behavior Engagers or Selective Health Maintainers, aligning with previous findings [48]. This pattern may result from work stress, time constraints, and family responsibilities hindering their ability to practice proactive health behaviors. Additionally, women with psychological issues were also prone to fall into these categories, likely because conditions like anxiety and depression can weaken self-regulation and decision-making abilities, reducing their self-management skills [49]. Nurses should regularly utilize brief screening tools to assess psychological distress, provide basic counseling, guide patients toward available mental health resources, and teach them practical techniques for stress alleviation.

Notably, overweight and obesity were significant risk factors for High-Risk Behavior Engagers compared to Comprehensive Health Practitioners, consistent with previous studies showing that a lack of physical activity, poor diet, and alcohol consumption could lead to obesity [50]. The relationship between obesity and high-risk behaviors is bidirectional: unhealthy behaviors can cause weight gain, and obesity can decrease motivation for physical activity [51]. Therefore, targeted interventions for High-Risk Behavior Engagers should prioritize psychological support and weight management, while also increasing screening and management of high-risk behaviors.

Limitations and recommendations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design cannot track dynamic changes of these groups over time or establish causal relationships. Additionally, although we stratified the 7-year enrollment period (2017–2023) to adjust for time-related confounders, significant societal, policy, and behavioral shifts during this extended timeframe may still affect the robustness of our findings. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to track behavioral changes (e.g., from peri- to post-menopause, shifts in employment) and confirm causality, while also assessing the stability of these behavioral patterns over time and disentangling individual-level factors from period effects. Second, the single-center sampling strategy yields a homogeneous population and excludes vulnerable groups, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should recruit diverse patient populations from multiple centers and expand recruitment efforts to include participants from vulnerable or hard-to-reach populations using various strategies. Third, relying on self-reported behavioral data may introduce recall bias and lead to inaccurate assessments. Future studies should consider supplementing self-reports with more objective measures, such as accelerometers, sleep monitoring devices, food diaries, and biomarkers (e.g., cotinine for smoking, HbA1c for diabetes), to obtain more reliable data.

Fourth, the use of composite variables, such as “smoking/alcohol use,” can obscure the individual effects of each behavior, potentially leading to an ecological fallacy and misinterventions. To avoid these pitfalls, future larger-sample studies are needed to gather individual-level data and analyze the effects of each behavior separately. Fifth, psychological problems were assessed using a 9-item subscale from the HCSAQ, rather than the widely established tools (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7). While the overall HCSAQ has been verified in prior research, the specific 9-item subscale used to assess psychological problems requires further validation in future studies to ensure comparability. Sixth, this study did not thoroughly examine the influence of family on health behaviors, despite including family history as a covariate. Future research can investigate how family and cultural dynamics influence health behaviors by focusing on caregiving roles, intergenerational support, and specific cultural beliefs.

Seventh, there were missing confounders, such as socioeconomic factors (income, healthcare access) and environmental determinants (urban/rural residence, community resources), due to the reliance on pre-existing data in this retrospective analysis. Future prospective studies should include socioeconomic mediators in regression models to clarify pathways and test measurement invariance across subgroups, thereby validating latent classes. Eighth, we would like to acknowledge the cultural specificity of certain key study variables, such as “proactive healthcare”, which may reflect Chinese healthcare infrastructure rather than individual agency. Future international studies are needed to validate these behavioral patterns across diverse populations to assess their cross-cultural applicability. Finally, the quantitative data-driven analysis in our study may not capture the full complexity of the health behavior patterns. Future studies should adopt a mixed-methods approach to integrate qualitative insights, such as time constraints and cultural beliefs.

Conclusion

This study identified three latent classes of proactive health behaviors among middle-aged and older women in China: Comprehensive Health Practitioners, Selective Health Maintainers, and High-Risk Behavior Engagers. These latent profiles were significantly associated with age, overweight/obesity status, educational attainment, occupation, menopausal status, psychological problem, self-rated health status, and diabetes/cardiovascular family history. Our findings offer an empirical foundation for discerning behavioral heterogeneity in Chinese proactive health management and designing person-centered intervention frameworks within this population. Therefore, it is recommended that future efforts focus on enhancing proactive health awareness and developing effective health communication strategies tailored to middle-aged and older populations. Encouraging the adoption of multiple proactive health behaviors can improve health outcomes and promote healthy aging.
