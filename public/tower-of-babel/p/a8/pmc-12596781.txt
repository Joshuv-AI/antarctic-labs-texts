Rare thoracic arachnoid cyst following costovertebral tumor resection
Authors: Maïla Bounemra, Mohamed Dehmani Yedeas, Majdi Ben Romdhane, Sameh Achoura, Houssem Messaoudi, Ahmed Harbaoui, Ridha Chkili, Khaled Radhouane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Spinal arachnoid cysts are rare and pose a substantial clinical challenge due to their varied etiologies, ranging from acquired (i.e., including iatrogenic) to congenital. They most frequently involve the thoracic spine, where the spinal canal is the narrowest, heightening the risk of significant cord compression/myelopathy.Case Description: Following resection of a thoracic costovertebral tumor, a 55-year-old female developed a symptomatic thoracic arachnoid cyst. After the dural defect was repaired, the patient’s symptoms resolved.Conclusion: This article highlights the risk of developing symptomatic post-operative iatrogenic thoracic arachnoid cysts following the resection of thoracic tumors. Once the dural defect is repaired, lesions typically do not recur, and patients remain asymptomatic.

INTRODUCTION

Spinal arachnoid cysts are rare, and occur as either congenital or acquired (i.e., iatrogenic mostly attributed to surgery). They usually involve the thoracic spine, where they typically cause myelopathic deficits. Here, following resection of a thoracic T8/T9 tumor, a 55-year-old female developed dyspnea and chest pain attributed to a magnetic resonance (MR)-documented dorsally compressive arachnoid cyst; once the dural defect was repaired, the patient remained asymptomatic.

CASE REPORT

A 55-year-old female presented with right-sided mid-thoracic and right lower extremity radicular pain. The thoracic computed tomography (CT) showed a T8-T9 fluid-filled mass with extensive peripheral calcification located in the right posterior mediastinum, adjacent to the posterior arch of the eighth rib [Figure 1]. During tumor resection, a small dural tear occurred at the nerve root origin and was repaired. Immediately postoperatively, however, the patient had severe headaches and vomiting attributed to a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak causing intracranial hypotension. Notably, the symptoms improved with conservative management, and the patient was discharged 7 days later. The histopathological diagnosis was consistent with a costal osteochondroma.

(a) Thoracic computed tomography scan in bone and (b) parenchymal windows showing the massively calcified costovertebral tumor at the T8-T9 level (yellow arrow).

Presentation 6 months later with pleural effusion of CSF

Six months later, the patient presented with dyspnea and chest pain, but a normal neurological examination. The chest X-ray showed a thoracic opacity with regular borders [Figure 2]. The thoracic magnetic resonance imaging revealed a cystic lesion in the right costovertebral gutter at the T8-T9 level, with a thin spur extending into the right intervertebral foramen [Figure 3]. There was no pathological epidural enhancement of the thick-walled cyst. Thus, the post-operative diagnosis favored an arachnoid cyst.

(a) Chest X-ray face and (b) profile showing a thoracic opacity (red arrow) with regular borders in the right posterior mediastinum.

(a) Axial and (b) coronal contrast-enhanced T1-weighted magnetic resonance images revealing a cystic formation (yellow arrow) at the right costovertebral gutter at T8-T9 level with a relatively thick wall corresponding to the arachnoid cyst.

Surgery

Utilizing a midline incision, a right-sided T8-T9 level laminectomy was extended laterally to expose the dural tear at the shoulder of the nerve root (i.e., the origin of the large arachnoid cyst). The cyst was punctured, its neck was ligated, and it was excised; the stump demonstrated no residual CSF leak. A water-tight closure was performed, followed by placement of a suction drain. The post-operative recovery was uneventful, and 4 months later, the patient remained asymptomatic, with normal imaging findings [Figure 4].

DISCUSSION

Congenital, or acquired (i.e., including idiopathic postoperative) spinal arachnoid cysts pose a unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenge.[4] They consist of diverticula of the dura mater, arachnoid, or nerve root sheath, and accumulated CSF within the extradural or intradural space. Majority of these lesions occur in the thoracic spine.[7]

Iatrogenic spinal arachnoid cysts

Iatrogenic spinal arachnoid cysts typically occur due to dural tears (DT) occurring during spinal surgery. Although DTs recognized intraoperatively may be immediately repaired, other unrecognized DTs go untreated. Through these DT, CSF escapes from the subarachnoid space into the surrounding arachnoid or epidural tissues, creating a potential cavity which, over time, can expand due to a “ball-valve”/one-way valve mechanism. This allows CSF to accumulate in the cyst during systolic pulsations or increases in intrathecal pressure, leading to progressive cyst enlargement.[1-3,5,7] Gradual expansion of the cyst increases the risk of mechanical cord compression/myelopathy/radiculopathy.[7]

Radiologic diagnosis of iatrogenic arachnoid cysts

MRI remains the diagnostic gold standard for diagnosing iatrogenic thoracic arachnoid cysts. Isointense T1-weighted and hyperintense on T2-weighted MR images best show these cysts that do not enhance with contrast. MRI also readily evaluates the extent of attendant spinal cord or root compression and the extent of myelomalacia.[7]

Surgery

Surgical resection remains the treatment of choice for symptomatic arachnoid cysts. Surgery may include: cyst resection, marsupialization, fenestration, and cyst shunts (i.e., pleural, peritoneal).[8] Cysts must be completely resected, and the communicating pedicle occluded, followed by meticulous repair of the dural defect to eliminate the “ball-valve” refilling mechanism. CONCLUSION

A 55-year-old, following resection of a T8-T9 costal osteochondroma, developed a post-operative dorsal iatrogenic arachnoid cyst that required cyst removal, obstruction of the communicating pedicle, and repair of the dural defect, resulting in symptom resolution.
