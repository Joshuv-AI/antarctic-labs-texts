Severe 180° Penile Torsion With False Meatus and Intact Prepuce Resembling Normal Anatomy in a 1.5‐Year‐Old Afghan Child: A Rare Case With Literature Review
Authors: Turyalai Hakimi, Mohammad Anwar Jawed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCongenital penile torsion is a rare rotational anomaly of the penis, usually directed counterclockwise, and may occur in isolation or with other penile malformations. It is usually asymptomatic but may lead to cosmetic or functional concerns in severe cases. The surgical technique involves penile degloving, resection of Buck's fascia, making incisions at the penile base, tunica plication, and the use of a dorsal dartos flap. Here we present a 1.5 years old child with 180° counterclockwise penile torsion, with false meatus and normal prepuce.Keywords: dartos, detorsion, false meatus, meatoplasty, penile torsion, prepuce

Introduction

Congenital penile torsion (CPT) is a relatively uncommon anomaly characterized by axial rotation of the penis, most often occurring in a counterclockwise direction. The degree of torsion varies from mild (approximately 30°) to severe (> 90°) and may present either as an isolated defect or in association with other genitourinary anomalies, such as hypospadias. Although the condition primarily carries cosmetic and potential functional consequences, it remains underrecognized and underreported, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Globally, the exact prevalence of CNPT is unclear. Isolated torsion has been documented in 1.7%–27% of neonates, while severe torsions exceeding 90° occur in approximately 0.7% of cases. According to the degree of glanular rotation, penile torsion (PT) is categorized as mild (< 45°), moderate (45°–90°), or severe (> 90°). Nonetheless, most cases (approximately 87%) exhibit a torsion angle ranging from 10° to 20°. Surgical intervention is generally recommended for torsions exceeding 60°, mainly to address cosmetic concerns and to prevent potential long‐term functional complications [1]. Parents commonly seek medical evaluation due to the abnormal cosmetic appearance and worries about potential functional problems in the future [2].

Key Clinical Message

Severe congenital penile torsion may remain undetected in uncircumcised children with an intact prepuce. A dartos flap technique provides a safe and effective method for detorsion with excellent cosmetic and functional outcomes. Modification of the balanced dartos flap provides a safe and reliable method to correct severe penile torsion while preserving the neurovascular bundle.

Case History/Examination

We present a case of a 1.5‐year‐old child who was brought to the Pediatric Surgery Unit of our teaching hospital with a complaint of an ectopic urethral meatus. According to the parents, the child was taken for routine circumcision to a medical practitioner, likely a circumcisionist who performs circumcision and minor surgical procedures in our country. The procedure was deferred after the practitioner noted an abnormal position of the urethral meatus and subsequently referred the patient to our unit. There was no family history of similar anomalies, and the mother did not have regular antenatal check‐ups due to financial constraints.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

On physical examination, the patient was found to have an abnormal meatal opening as false meatus associated with PT. Routine laboratory and biochemical investigations were within normal limits. The patient was therefore prepared for definitive surgical management.

Under safe general anesthesia (GA), the patient was placed in the supine position on the operating table and taken for surgery. Intraoperative findings revealed a false meatus, dividing the external urethral meatus into a lower patent opening for urination and an upper blind pouch, consistent with the external false meatus. In addition, a severe PT of approximately 180° was observed (Figure 1). Meatoplasty combined with circumcision was performed initially, followed by a detorsion procedure. The detorsion procedure consisted of complete penile degloving to identify the underlying cause of the torsion, followed by division of fibrotic bands to relieve tension. After complete penile degloving, a dartos flap was mobilized and carefully adjusted to achieve a balanced distribution of rotational forces rather than unilateral rotation. This allowed gradual correction of torsion while avoiding excessive tension on the neurovascular structures (balanced dartos flap). Fixation was achieved using 6–0 Vicryl sutures (Figure 2).

The procedure was completed uneventfully, resulting in restoration of the normal penile axis. The patient was discharged on the third postoperative day with a normally appearing penis and satisfactory outcome (Figure 3). The patient was followed for three postoperative months, during which no complications or concerns were observed.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Severe congenital penile torsion may remain undiagnosed until circumcision or evaluation for associated anomalies. Surgical correction using a balanced dartos flap technique can achieve excellent cosmetic and functional outcomes with minimal complications.

Discussion

PT was first described by Horton and Devine in 1973 as an abnormal three‐dimensional twisting of the penile corporal bodies [3]. CPT is characterized by a rotational anomaly of the penile shaft. The exact incidence and underlying cause remain uncertain, as the condition seldom leads to clinical complaints. Consequently, its frequency and clinical significance may be underestimated, with limited data available in the literature [4]. Its occurrence has been described as common, uncommon, or uncertain [5].

The underlying cause of penile malformations is still uncertain. Some authors have documented familial cases, suggesting a genetic component in the development of this condition. Recently, Ahmed et al. reported CPT in two siblings, with the older child exhibiting an isolated 180° torsion and the younger showing an isolated 20° curvature [6]. Several studies propose that CPT results from an abnormal connection between the penile skin and the dartos fascia [7].

PT is generally considered a benign anomaly. The primary issue reported is an abnormal urinary stream, observed in 60% of cases in one study, though it was unclear whether any of these children also had hypospadias. The extent to which this malformation affects adult function is difficult to determine. In a survey of 12,307 adult men assessed at a sexual dysfunction or infertility clinic, 12% were found to have PT. Overall, only 2% sought corrective cosmetic surgery, and none reported sexual dysfunction attributable to the torsion [8]. Although radiologic modalities such as cavernosography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are highly valuable in demonstrating the manner in which the corporal bodies are twisted. The indications for surgical intervention in penile rotation include cosmetic concerns, parental anxiety, and potential impairment of sexual function [9]. Nonetheless, clinical necessity for surgical correction has been reported in only about 4% of cases [10]. While most children show no symptoms, parents often seek correction for cosmetic reasons. Mild cases of isolated PT are best managed with a conservative approach [11]. Several surgical techniques have been described for the correction of PT. These include penile degloving with reattachment, excision of Buck's fascia, the modified Nesbit procedure, dorsal dartos flap rotation, fixation of the tunica albuginea to the pubic periosteum, and diagonal corporal plication. Correction may also be achieved through mobilization of the urethral plate and urethra, with all of these approaches reported to produce satisfactory outcomes [12, 13]. Additional techniques, such as suturing of the tunica albuginea, have been described for use in more complex or challenging cases. Other techniques, such as corporopexy, which involves fixation of the tunica albuginea to the pubic periosteum, is considered a more invasive procedure with a higher risk of postoperative complications, as well as diagonal corporeal plication sutures placed parallel to and at a safe distance from the neurovascular bundle [14]. In comparison with alternative corrective methods such as corporopexy and corporeal plication, the dartos flap approach is less invasive and is associated with a lower risk of procedure‐related complications [11]. Patients with penile torsion require careful management, as this rare anomaly may impact overall quality of life. We advocate a multidisciplinary follow‐up strategy that addresses medical, psychosexual, surgical, and reproductive considerations. Once patients reach key developmental milestones, routine assessments should include inquiries into their psychological, physical, cultural, social, and sexual well‐being. While many of these factors are modifiable and amenable to intervention, they are interdependent and should be evaluated holistically rather than in isolation [15].

The index case was initially considered by the parents to be a normal child without any genital abnormality, since the prepuce was intact. The patient was referred to our surgical unit by a local medical practitioner following the identification of a meatal defect. During the surgical evaluation for the meatal defect, our team observed a severe degree of PT, which had not been previously identified. This highlights that, in some cases, thorough preoperative assessment may be overlooked. Although our center does not have any adopted protocol to perform corrective procedures for PT, the lead author's extensive experience in managing various types of hypospadias, including complex cases, allowed us to modify the approach. We utilized a half dartos pedicle flap derived from both the ventral and dorsal aspects of the penis (balanced dartos flap), to maintain the natural alignment of the dartos layers, successfully achieving detorsion. The balanced dartos flap technique is regarded as highly effective, being minimally invasive while preserving the penile neurovascular bundle, and it also contributes to reduced intraoperative bleeding and shorter operative times. In our country, circumcision is universally practiced among males, which facilitates early detection and timely correction of such anomalies during childhood. Additionally, conventional dorsal dartos flap techniques typically rely on unilateral rotational force to correct torsion. In contrast, our approach emphasizes balanced force distribution, which may reduce the risk of overcorrection, residual torsion, and neurovascular compromise. In uncircumcised individuals, PT often remains undiagnosed until later in life, making surgical correction more challenging and sometimes associated with additional penile abnormalities. In complex cases, postoperative erection may also pose a concern. Nonetheless, when surgical correction is appropriately planned according to the type and severity of the anomaly and performed by an experienced pediatric urologist, the outcomes are typically excellent, both cosmetically and functionally. We employed a minimally invasive approach by dividing the tension bands and using a balanced dartos flap (BDF), thereby avoiding extensive dissection that could damage the neurovascular bundle (NVB) and cause complications, resulting in a simpler procedure with excellent outcomes. In conclusion, this case emphasizes the critical importance of early identification through thorough genital examination prior to circumcision, in order to prevent missed diagnoses and to enable timely referral for definitive surgical management, thereby reducing potential long‐term psychological and physiological sequelae.
