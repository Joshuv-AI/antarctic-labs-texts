Synergistic effects of hybrid fibres and natural rubber latex on the thermal stability and mechanical properties of high-volume fly ash concrete
Authors: Hassaan Bin Tariq, Roszilah Hamid, A. B. M. A. Kaish, Shahrizan Baharom, Anis Azmi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Structures exposed to high temperatures must have enhanced thermal stability and mechanical properties. It is essential to utilise sustainable materials in the endeavour to achieve this objective. This study investigates the combined effects of natural rubber latex (NRL) and hybrid fibres in high-volume fly ash (HVFA) concrete. It introduces a novel approach of utilising NRL as a partial cement replacement at varying percentages of 2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, and 10%. Replacing cement with 2.5% NRL resulted in optimal performance. Higher percentages of NRL enhanced the concrete’s workability, with 10% NRL producing a maximum slump of 54 mm. Adding 2.5% and 5% NRL significantly reduced water absorption by 12–18% compared to the control sample. Compressive strength decreased with higher NRL contents. The smallest reduction of 8% occurred with 2.5% replacement, and the highest loss of 55% was at 10% replacement. In contrast, tensile and flexural strengths improved by 14% and 31%, respectively, after 28 days of curing. The concrete with 2.5% NRL retained more strength at elevated temperatures, with a loss of strength of 20% at 600 °C compared to 25% for the control. The microstructural analysis revealed that the optimal NRL percentage resulted in a denser matrix, supporting the mechanical results. Generally, incorporating 2.5% NRL with hybrid fibres in HVFA concrete enhances durability and thermal resistance while maintaining satisfactory strength, offering a sustainable approach for high-performance applications.

Introduction

Concrete has long been the backbone of modern infrastructure, valued for its strength, versatility, and durability. However, as engineering demands evolve, it is essential to enhance the fire resistance, mechanical performance, and sustainability properties of concrete1,2. Traditional concrete often performs poorly under extreme environmental conditions, such as high temperatures, severe chemical exposure, and structural stresses, resulting in degradation and reduced service life3. This has compelled researchers to explore alternative materials and innovative mix designs that improve concrete’s resilience while maintaining cost-effectiveness and environmental sustainability4.

One of the key advancements in concrete technology is the incorporation of high-volume fly ash (HVFA) as a supplementary cementitious material. Fly ash enhances the durability and workability of concrete while significantly reducing CO2 emissions from cement production5–7. Many studies have attempted to enhance the mechanical strength, crack resistance, and ductility of concrete by incorporating hybrid fibres, such as steel, polypropylene, and bamboo8,9. Concrete reinforcement with HVFA and hybrid fiber offers a promising approach to producing more sustainable and resilient concrete structures.

Researchers have identified natural polymer modification as a promising approach to further improve concrete performance. Natural rubber latex (NRL) is derived from the Hevea brasiliensis tree. It is a renewable material with excellent adhesion, flexibility, and water resistance10,11. Adding NRL to concrete alters its matrix structure, thereby improving impermeability, reducing porosity, and enhancing resistance to chemical attacks12,13. Few studies on NRL as an additive have investigated the outcome of incorporating it in combination with HVFA and hybrid fibres14,15. It is crucial to understand NRL’s contribution to the overall performance of high-performance hybrid fiber-reinforced HVFA concrete, especially under elevated temperatures, to develop more resilient construction materials.

Previous studies have explored different approaches to improve concrete performance. For example, Afroz et al.16 observed that incorporating hybrid fiber into HVFA concrete significantly increased the tensile strength and ductility. They achieved optimum results when incorporating ultra-fine fly ash with 1.5% steel and 0.5% polypropylene fiber. Researchers investigating the resistance of polymer-modified concretes highlighted their enhanced durability in aggressive environments. There is also research on using NRL as a concrete modifier. Muhammad et al.17 incorporated NRL into concrete and evaluated its overall performance. The notable improvements in flexural and tensile strengths highlight the importance of determining the optimum dosage. Similarly, exposure to high temperatures can induce significant changes in the microstructure, which affect the microstructural integrity and overall mechanical performance. Novák and Kohoutková18 investigated the microstructural changes in fiber-reinforced concrete exposed to high temperatures. They observed the synergistic effects of the fibres in reducing crack propagation. With the growing interest in incorporating sustainable natural polymers into concrete, several studies have examined the performance of NRL-modified concrete under elevated temperatures.

Ismail et al.19 investigated the behaviour of NRL-modified normal-strength concrete when exposed to high temperatures. The overall performance of the NRL-modified concrete at temperatures ranging from 100 °C to 300 °C was comparable to that of conventional concrete. However, increasing the percentage of NRL and extending the exposure to elevated temperatures had adverse effects on the mechanical properties. Althoey et al.20 incorporated NRL into a bamboo-reinforced modified concrete. Incorporating 1% NRL improved the compressive, flexural, and tensile strengths. However, the proportion of NRL and fiber must be equal to obtain the desired results. A SEM–EDX analysis showed that NRL enhanced the concrete’s microstructure by reducing voids and enhancing bonding, indicating its potential as a sustainable modifier.

Awoyera et al.2 incorporated 1%, 1.5%, and 3% NRL into concrete and evaluated their performance when exposed to varying high temperatures of 300 °C, 800 °C, and 1,000 °C. The control mix maintained a higher compressive strength at most temperature levels, and the mix with 1% NRL has strength at 800 °C.

The study outcomes in Table 1 show that NRL often reduces the compressive strength of concrete. It can also enhance durability and improve flexural and tensile performance. Some studies reported higher strength gains when incorporating both NRL and fibres. Others reported much higher water absorption at certain percentages of NRL, which is a major drawback. Few studies thoroughly investigate the interaction of NRL in high-performance concrete containing supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), particularly regarding their bonding behaviour and performance under thermal stress. Moreover, most previous research has investigated NRL as an additive instead of a partial cement replacement, leaving its role in sustainable binder systems unclear. These inconsistencies emphasise the necessity for comprehensive investigations into the use of NRL as a cement replacement in high-volume fly ash concrete, particularly with hybrid fibres, to achieve superior mechanical properties and thermal performance. The objective of this study is to evaluate the thermal effects of incorporating hybrid fibres and natural rubber latex (NRL) into high-volume fly ash high-performance concrete. It determined the optimum NRL replacement percentage based on compressive strength. The results were used to assess and enhance the concrete’s fire resistance.

This study assessed the concrete’s fresh, mechanical, and non-destructive properties. It employed the Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM), Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) to analyze the microstructure before and after exposure to elevated temperatures, and X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis to identify the crystalline composition of materials. The non-destructive tests (NDTs), including Ultrasonic Pulse Velocity (UPV) and Rebound Hammer, were conducted to evaluate surface hardness and internal integrity. The compressive strength, loss of strength, loss of mass, and physical observations were assessed after exposing the sample to specific temperatures. The study outcome can serve as a reference for future investigations and provide valuable insights into natural rubber latex-modified high-performance concrete.

The Darex Super 20 superplasticiser was used to enhance the flow and workability of the concrete. It is a naphthalene sulfonate-based superplasticiser and allows significant water reduction. This low-viscosity liquid has a density of 1.2 ± 0.02 kg/L and does not contain chloride.

Experimental setup

Compressive, split tensile, and flexural strength

The preparation of fresh concretes follows the guidelines of ASTM C19231. The concrete was prepared in a tilting drum mixer and poured into cube, cylindrical, and beam moulds. They were then used to evaluate the compressive, split tensile, and flexural strength. Each specimen was cast in three layers, with each layer compacted by applying 25 strokes of a tamping rod. After casting, the moulds were covered with a polythene sheet and left undisturbed for 48 h for initial hardening. However, mixes containing fly ash exhibited prolonged setting times and required additional time for proper hardening. Once sufficiently hardened, the specimens were removed from their moulds and submerged in a water tank maintained at 27 ± 2 °C for curing until the designated testing age. The compressive and split tensile strength tests were conducted on Days 7 and 28, following the ASTM C39 and ASTM C496 standards, respectively32,33. The flexural strength was determined using the guidelines of ASTM D6272 through a four-point bending test setup34. Water absorption

The water absorption of the control and NRL-modified concrete specimens was evaluated in accordance with ASTM C64235. The test was conducted on 100 mm cubes after 28 days of standard curing. The specimens were immersed in water for 24 h, after which the surfaces were wiped dry with a piece of cloth to obtain the saturated surface-dry (SSD) weight. The specimens were then oven-dried at 110 °C for 24 h, and their dry weights were recorded. The samples were re-immersed in water, and their submerged weights were measured using a specific gravity apparatus.

High-temperature test

The concrete specimens were exposed to high temperatures in a controlled laboratory furnace with an automatic temperature regulation system and a maximum capacity of 1,000 °C. The concrete cubes (100 mm3), including control and optimum NRL-modified specimens, were cured in water for 28 days. The heating of the specimen followed the RILEM TC 129-MHT guidelines36 at a controlled rate of 5 °C per minute up to 300 °C and 600 °C, followed by a two-hour holding period to ensure thermal equilibrium. The specimens were then left to cool naturally in the furnace under room temperature conditions.

The effects of high-temperature exposure were based on mass loss, residual strength, and visual inspection. The mass loss was determined by weighing the specimens using a digital balance before and after the heating. The compressive strength test was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of ASTM C109. FE-SEM and EDX characterization

The microstructural analysis of the modified concrete mixes employed a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) equipped with a Supra 55 VP system from ZEISS, Germany. The specimens were subjected to Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX), configured as part of the FESEM unit from Oxford Instruments, to assess their elemental compositions. The microstructural and elemental characteristics of the control and NRL-modified concrete were analysed using the FESEM/EDX. The specimens subjected to SEM and EDX analysis were those that fractured during compressive and flexural strength assessments after 28 days of curing. To prevent further hydration, the samples were subjected to a freezing and drying process before being coated with gold for SEM imaging. The EDX scans targeted the regions exhibiting slight differences in elemental distribution, corresponding to the morphological characteristics observed in the SEM images at a magnification of 5000x.

Results and discussion

Workability

The freshly mixed NRL-modified concrete exhibited significantly better flowability during mixing, placement, and compaction compared to the control mix. Figure 4 shows the impact of the latex contents on the workability (slump) of the hybrid fiber-reinforced, high-volume flash, natural rubber latex-modified concrete, assessed immediately after mixing. These results indicate a steady increase in slump with higher latex replacement percentages. In its fresh state, the modified concrete maintains sufficient workability for casting and proper compaction for approximately one hour (± five minutes). However, the latex particles, which initially formed weak chains, gradually gained rigidity over time. This process leads to reduced homogeneity of the mix, as some mixing water was trapped within the spongy structure of the latex.Fig. 4Slump values of the control and latex-modified concrete specimens. They show better workability with higher latex contents.

Slump values of the control and latex-modified concrete specimens. They show better workability with higher latex contents.

The workability of the specimen increased marginally and remained unchanged thereafter, even when incorporating fibres. This observation contradicts the results of previous studies, which found that incorporating NRL resulted in reduced slump29. The key difference lies in the approach: this study used latex as a cement replacement, while previous studies used it as an additive. The replacement approach contributed to higher slumps, although the fibres limited the extent of enhanced workability compared to the findings of Ismail et al.19. The superior workability of the modified mix can also be attributed to the dispersed latex particles and the incorporation of high volumes of fly ash, which reduced the friction between aggregates and enhanced their movement. According to Alaka & Oyedele, this so called 'ball-bearing effect’, attributed to the spherical morphology of particles, reduces the friction between aggregates in the fresh state, thereby enhancing the initial workability37. As the latex coalesces, this effect diminishes, leading to a gradual loss of fluidity. Despite this, the presence of latex still significantly enhances the overall workability and ease of handling of the modified concrete.

Water absorption

The water absorption results in Fig. 5 show that the water absorption of NRL5C is minimal. Water absorption increased with higher latex contents, leading to greater absorption. This trend suggests that incorporating less than 5% latex does not produce optimal water resistance. This is because the mixes contain inherent voids, which directly influence absorption behavior (Fig. 11a). NRL2.5C has a lower water resistance than NC. However, the superior water resistance of the NRL5C over normal concrete indicates an optimum balance at this dosage. Conversely, incorporating more than 5% latex reduced the water exclusion efficiency, likely due to excess latex exceeding the amount required for effective void filling. The excess latex accumulates at the interface boundaries, hindering proper aggregate compaction. Water withdrawal around hydrocarbon particles is a critical aspect of void formation in latex-modified concrete. Bala Muhammad et al.28 reported that incorporating up to 10% latex resulted in inter-particle void formation after 28 days of dry curing. This suggests that the moisture expulsion during prolonged air curing or heat treatment, such as the conditions in the water absorption test, could further increase void contents. Additionally, previous studies observed that NRL increases the water absorption of concrete38. In this study, replacing the cement with 10% latex resulted in a 42% higher water absorption compared to the control mix. However, water absorption decreased by 2.6% at a 5% cement replacement, demonstrating the significant impact of latex content on the concrete’s absorption behavior. For the apparent density, replacing cement with NRL did not result in a noticeably lower density of the modified concretes. This result is unsurprising, as the density of latex is similar to that of cement. Moreover, incorporating fly ash and hybrid fibres might have contributed to maintaining the density of the mixes. Proper compaction and thorough mixing of the latex into the concrete also contributed to the minimal change in density.

Surface rebound values for the control and latex-modified concrete mixes.

The decline is due to polymer modification, altered hydration mechanisms, and fiber-matrix interactions. The NRL forms a polymer network within the cementitious matrix, reducing stiffness and increasing flexibility. This results in lower surface hardness, as indicated by the rebound hammer. Although high fly ash contents refine the pore structure and enhance long-term strength, they also delay early hydration, which further reduces matrix stiffness39. Hybrid fibres improve ductility and crack resistance. Steel fibres enhance toughness, while polypropylene fibres mitigate shrinkage rather than contribute to compressive strength. The effect of hybrid fibres on rebound values concurs with the findings of Najm et al.40. The incorporation of fibres significantly impacts early-age rebound values by resisting the impact in weaker concrete. As concrete strength increased, this effect diminished, particularly after 28 days of curing, when the concrete matrix begins to influence the rebound value. Therefore, the combined effects of NRL, fly ash, and hybrid fibres produce a more flexible and less rigid surface, leading to lower rebound values. While latex modification improved durability and ductility, excessive amounts reduce surface hardness. This emphasises the need for an optimal dosage that balances mechanical performance and durability.

Split tensile strength

Figure 9 shows the direct tensile strength of the control and modified concrete. The optimal cement replacement percentage is 2.5% NRL. This replacement percentage increases the split tensile strength by 19.7% after 7 days of curing and 14.05% after 28 days compared to the control mix. These results emphasise the superior tensile performance of modified concrete compared to its compressive strength. They align with the distinct failure mechanisms under tensile and compressive loading. Concrete failure mechanisms differ significantly under tensile and compressive stresses. Under compressive loading, the material’s matrix undergoes progressive compaction with higher stress but without immediate fracture47. This phenomenon enables the concrete to withstand higher compressive loads before undergoing significant cracking or failure. The latex films interspersed between sand particles and aggregates provide limited resistance under high compressive stress. However, incorporating hybrid fibres enhances load distribution, bridges microcracks, and delays catastrophic failure to a certain extent, thereby improving compressive performance to a particular phase48. Failure occurs under tensile loading when the applied force exceeds the interparticle bonding forces, resulting in immediate rupture due to the brittle nature of concrete. Since the tensile strength of concrete is approximately one-tenth of its compressive strength, the stress levels at failure are significantly lower. The lower stress allows the latex films and fibres to significantly improve tensile performance. It is worth noting that hybrid fibres provide a more solid reinforcement network than single fibres or fiber-free mixes. The effectiveness depends primarily on the type of fiber, as each fiber has a different impact. For example, steel fibres can improve resistance to large cracks and enhance stiffness under dynamic loading, while polypropylene and basalt fibres control microcracks and delay crack initiation49. These observations collectively highlight the distinct contributions of NRL and hybrid fibres in improving the tensile properties of concrete.Fig. 9Split tensile strength of the control and optimal latex-modified concrete.

Flexural strength

Figure 10 shows the flexural strength of the control and modified concrete after 7 and 28 days of curing. The specimen with 2.5% NRL exhibits the greatest improvement, 26.6% after 7 days of curing and 31.4% after 28 days, compared to the control mix. Unlike compressive or tensile loading, flexural loading generates a stress gradient across the specimen, where the tensile stresses at the bottom fibres govern failure50. NRL improves matrix cohesion and interfacial bonding, delaying the initiation of flexural cracks. Polymer film formation with in the cement matrix enhance ductility by allowing limited deformation before fracture. This is especially beneficial in bending scenarios, where progressive crack growth dictates performance. Moreover, hybrid fibres enhance post-cracking behaviour by bridging macro- and micro-cracks, redistributing stress after the first crack appears, and contributing to higher toughness and energy absorption. This combination prevents sudden brittle failure, ensuring a more gradual load deflection response. These enhancements are less pronounced in mixes with single fibres or without NRL, as the synergy between polymer modification and hybrid reinforcement provides a more continuous and resilient crack control mechanism. These observations are consistent with earlier findings that polymer fiber systems exhibit superior toughness, residual strength, and serviceability under flexural loads, making them particularly advantageous in applications subject to repeated or cyclic bending stresses23,51.Fig. 10Flexural strength of the control and optimal latex-modified concrete.

Liquid-state SEM analysis is not feasible for examining the microstructure of natural rubber latex (NRL). Therefore, the latex was dried and subjected to FE-SEM analysis. The analysis provided insights into the morphology of NRL in its solid state, revealing its polymeric network and surface characteristics (Fig. 1). It is essential to understand the morphology of NRL, as it influences the interaction between latex and the cementitious materials in concrete.

Microstructural evaluation of the control and latex-modified concrete specimens was through a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FE-SEM) operated at 15 kV with secondary electron imaging detectors. It provided valuable insights into the microstructural characteristics of the concrete mixes. Concrete behaviour under elevated temperature conditions

Post-heating compressive strength and strength loss

Figure 13 shows the compressive strength of the specimens after exposure to different temperatures. A continuous rise in temperature caused a corresponding decline in compressive strength for both types of concrete. However, the comprehensive strength shows an increasing trend at 300 °C. This increasing trend is especially true for the control mix. The control mix has an initial compressive strength of 56.17 MPa, while the compressive strength of the optimum mix is 51.71 MPa before exposure to elevated temperatures. The control mix exhibits steadily increasing compressive strength when exposed to a temperature of 300 °C, reaching 60 MPa or a 6.82% gain compared to its initial strength (Fig. 13). In contrast, the optimum mix has a compressive strength of 49.8 MPa at 300 °C, a marginal decline of 3.69% (Fig. 14).Fig. 13Compressive strength of the control and optimal NRL2.5C specimens at varying temperatures.Fig. 14Percentage of loss of strength of the control and optimal NRL2.5C specimens at elevated temperatures.

Compressive strength of the control and optimal NRL2.5C specimens at varying temperatures.

Percentage of loss of strength of the control and optimal NRL2.5C specimens at elevated temperatures.

At 600 °C, the control and optimal NRL2.5C specimens experienced significant strength reductions compared to their initial strengths. The control mix has a compressive strength of 42 MPa, a 25.22% loss, while the compressive strength of the optimal mix is 41 MPa, corresponding to a 20.73% loss. Despite the reduced compressive strength, the differences between the control and optimal mixes were minimal at 600 °C, suggesting a similar declining trend under extreme heat exposure. The optimal mix experienced less degradation in compressive strength due to the early degradation of NRL films once the temperature exceeded 300 °C. As the films degrade, they coagulate into floc-like structures that fill the minute pores, providing internal reinforcement against thermal stresses. This mechanism allows the optimal mix to retain more strength than the control concrete at the same temperature exposure59. The polymer network acts as a buffer against cracking due to high temperatures, minimising structural degradation, which can also be further verified in the microstructural image shown in Fig. 17c.

In contrast, conventional Portland cement concrete made with 100% OPC and siliceous aggregates is more prone to thermal cracking and strength deterioration under extreme conditions. Typically, its compressive strength declines significantly beyond 300 °C60. According to Savva et al.61, the type of binder and aggregate materials play a crucial role in strength retention, with strength reductions of approximately 11% at 100 °C and up to 68% at 600 °C. The decrease in compressive strength and changes in concrete colour are due primarily to structural transformations and material decomposition during heating62. Various complex processes, including shrinkage, expansion, decomposition, and crystal breakdown, occur at elevated temperatures. According to Ansari et al.63, concrete inherently contains different forms of water, including capillary, physically absorbed, interlayer, and chemically-bound water within calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) and calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2). As heating begins, free water migrates outward and eventually evaporates as steam.

In this study, the control mix (high-volume fly ash with nano silica) exhibited a notably enhanced strength at 300 °C, while the NRL-modified concrete did not show similar improvements. This lack of strength gain was due to the latex film in the mix restricting the rehydration process by limiting the access of cement particles to the pores. Nevertheless, since both mixes were HVFA systems containing nano silica, the overall strength loss was not as severe as conventional OPC concretes. This aligns with Alhawat et al.64, who reported that HVFA concrete exposed to elevated temperatures in tunnel linings retained better residual strength and showed fewer physical deteriorations than silica fume-modified concrete. Specifically, HVFA concrete retained about 66% of its design strength, compared to 62% in silica fume mixes, further underscoring its superior resistance at elevated temperatures.

Crack formation under elevated temperatures

This study employed an RILEM approach to evaluate the effects of high temperatures on concrete specimens. The specimens were subjected to the desired peak temperatures of 300 °C and 600 °C for two hours to ensure a uniform heat exposure. After removal from the furnace, the specimens were left to cool in an open environment in ambient conditions. There are no visible cracks on the concrete specimens subjected to a peak temperature of 300 °C. Polypropylene fibres can prevent crack resistance and spalling since they melt between 160 °C and 170 °C. They form micro-channels or voids in the concrete, which allow trapped steam to escape, reducing internal pressure and preventing explosive spalling and cracking. Therefore, these synthetic fibres are effective in controlling crack resistance and spalling, particularly those related to steel fibre, as they are crucial in maintaining the overall structural integrity18. However, the specimens subjected to a peak temperature of 600 °C exhibited cracking. The cracking is due to the greater thermal stress induced at higher temperatures and the rapid cooling in the open environment. These factors contributed to the thermal gradients between the specimens’ surface and core. The crack formations at 600 °C support the study by Khan et al.65. This result suggests that the cooling method has a significant impact on the extent and severity of cracking. Rapid cooling of the specimens in an open environment intensified the thermal shock they experienced, potentially resulting in micro-crackings and surface fissures. In conclusion, the crackings at 600 °C highlight the impact of the peak temperature and the cooling process on the structural integrity of the specimens. The absence of cracks on specimens exposed to a 300 °C temperature suggests that the thermal stress was insufficient to cause visible damage under the applied cooling regime. Additionally, the fibres help prevent the disintegration of the matrix within the concrete. Practical implications for fire resistance applications

The collective effects of strength loss, crack formation, and colour transformation at elevated temperatures highlight significant practical implications. The reduced strength at elevated temperatures highlights the grave implications for fire-resistance applications. Although all mixes experienced some level of compressive strength loss, the optimal NRL2.5C concrete retained a comparatively higher residual strength, suggesting its ability to delay structural deterioration when exposed to high-temperature fire, such as 600 °C. This property is especially beneficial in critical infrastructures, where maintaining load-bearing capacity, even under severe thermal stress, can extend evacuation time and reduce the risk of collapse. Concerning crack development, the formation of wider and deeper cracks in the control specimens indicates higher thermal incompatibility between the cement matrix and the aggregates, which accelerates the pathways for heat and gases. In contrast, the relatively finer cracks in the modified concrete can help slow down heat penetration, thus preserving structural cohesion. Finally, the colour change from gray to whitish gray or brown, which is more pronounced at higher temperatures, serves as a visible indicator of material degradation, as summarised in Table 7. For practical scenarios, such discolouration can serve as a post-fire diagnostic tool when assessing the extent of thermal damage and inform decisions on repairs or demolitions.
