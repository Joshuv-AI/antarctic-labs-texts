Sociodemographic and health vulnerabilities associated with disaster exposure: insights from ECHO cohort
Authors: Shazia Shaukat, Melissa Melough, Tarang Parekh, Jee Won Park, Jennifer Horney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisasters disproportionately impact women, particularly those of childbearing age, by exacerbating existing health disparities and disrupting access to essential healthcare services. This cross-sectional study examines the sociodemographic and health vulnerabilities associated with disaster exposure among women using harmonized data from U.S.-based cohorts in the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) dataset. A total of 3,142 women were included, with 17.6% reporting lifetime disaster exposure. Sociodemographic differences were observed, with disaster-exposed women being older, more likely to be married or cohabiting, and having higher educational attainment compared to non-exposed women. Health disparities were evident, with disaster exposure associated with higher rates of diabetes, hypertension, and depression. The frequency of disaster exposure further exacerbated these vulnerabilities, with women exposed to multiple disasters exhibiting the highest prevalence of chronic conditions and mental health challenges. Notably, disparities in disaster exposure were observed across racial and ethnic groups, with White women being overrepresented among disaster-exposed individuals, while Hispanic women were underrepresented. These findings highlight the need for targeted disaster preparedness and response efforts that address the cumulative health burden of repeated disaster exposure. The study underscores the importance of integrating disaster exposure into maternal health policies and interventions to mitigate long-term health consequences for vulnerable populations. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs to assess causal pathways and develop resilience-building strategies for at-risk groups.Keywords: Disaster exposure, Maternal health, Sociodemographic disparities, Chronic disease, Mental health, ECHO cohort

Introduction

The increasing frequency and intensity of disasters globally underscores the urgent need to understand their far-reaching impacts on diverse populations. While immediate consequences, such as morbidity and mortality, often dominate disaster response efforts, the longer-term health and socioeconomic repercussions receive comparatively less attention. These effects are not distributed equally across populations, with women—particularly those of childbearing age—facing unique and intersectional challenges due to biological, social, and economic factors that influence their health and recovery.

Women of childbearing age represent a uniquely vulnerable population during and after disasters, owing to their reproductive health needs and caregiving responsibilities [29]. Disasters often disrupt access to essential health services, including prenatal and postnatal care, family planning resources, and routine healthcare, which can have cascading effects on maternal and child health [42]. Biological factors, such as the increased physiological demands of pregnancy, heighten women’s susceptibility to stress and chronic conditions in the aftermath of disasters. For instance, studies have shown that disaster-induced stress during pregnancy can result in complications such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and increased risk of maternal mortality [6]. However, women's vulnerability extends beyond the biological realm, as they frequently face overlapping social and economic challenges that can exacerbate the impact of disasters [27, 50]. These include limited access to resources, disruptions in employment and income, increased caregiving responsibilities, and insufficient social support networks—all of which can significantly hinder women's ability to recover [9].

The impacts of disasters extend well beyond the immediate aftermath, often exacerbating existing health conditions [30, 41]. Long-term physical health challenges include increased prevalence of cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension, metabolic disorders like diabetes, and respiratory issues stemming from exposure to environmental pollutants during disasters [13]. These chronic conditions are often compounded by a lack of consistent healthcare access during recovery, further delaying treatment and exacerbating disparities [20].

In addition to physical health problems, mental health challenges represent a significant long-term burden for women exposed to disasters. Chronic stress, anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are common among disaster survivors and can interfere with daily functioning and caregiving roles [35]. The psychological toll of disasters can also interact with physical health, contributing to behaviors such as smoking, poor diet, and reduced physical activity, which increase the risk of chronic diseases [26]. For pregnant women, these behaviors can further heighten the risks of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes [16, 39]. Addressing these multifaceted health vulnerabilities of women is crucial for promoting more equitable and effective disaster preparedness and response strategies.

Sociodemographic factors, including age, education, marital status, and socioeconomic status, play a critical role in determining how women experience and recover from disasters [21]. These factors influence access to resources, healthcare, and social support networks, which are essential for resilience and recovery. For example, women from lower socioeconomic backgrounds are more likely to experience prolonged recovery periods due to limited financial resources and reduced access to healthcare services [43]. Similarly, education levels can impact awareness of available support systems and the ability to advocate for health needs during recovery [18]. Individuals with lower educational attainment may face challenges in accessing post-disaster support systems, potentially due to limited knowledge or resources [12]

Disasters also exacerbate pre-existing inequities, disproportionately affecting marginalized groups[2, 15]. Single mothers, women from racial and ethnic minorities, and those living in poverty often face compounded vulnerabilities due to systemic inequalities that limit their ability to recover from a disaster [11]. Understanding the interplay between sociodemographic factors and disaster exposure is therefore essential for designing equitable disaster response and recovery strategies that prioritize the needs of these high-risk populations such as pregnant women.

Despite growing evidence of the short-term physical and mental health impacts of disasters (such as respiratory issues, vector and water borne diseases, malnutrition, stress, anxiety, substance abuse), significant gaps remain in understanding their long-term consequences [9, 27]. Most studies focus on acute outcomes, such as injuries or immediate reproductive health complications such as preterm birth, gestational diabetes and hypertension, leaving the progression of chronic conditions and mental health challenges underexplored. Moreover, few studies have examined how sociodemographic factors shape the long-term health trajectories of disaster-exposed women compared to unexposed groups [20]. While long noted as a limitation of disaster research [33], the lack of longitudinal data limits the ability to craft interventions that address the root causes of health disparities in disaster recovery efforts or to track their effectiveness over time and across disaster events.

To address these gaps, this study leverages the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Cohort, which provides a diverse and comprehensive observational cohort of 107,000 + participants including women and their children [14]. This study investigates the sociodemographic factors and health vulnerabilities associated with disaster exposure among women in this population, with a focus on long-term physical and mental health outcomes. A secondary aim of the study is to explore how these associations vary across groups exposed to multiple disasters in their lifetime. This study will retrospectively analyze the ECHO Cohort data to examine the unique health vulnerabilities and disparities experienced by disaster-affected women and will inform the development of more equitable and effective disaster preparedness and response strategies.

Methods

This study utilizes data from the U.S.-based ECHO Program, an National Institutes of Health (NIH) -funded initiative established in 2016 to investigate how early environmental exposures influence maternal, fetal, and child health outcomes [19]. ECHO is a comprehensive research program that integrates data previously collected from multiple pre-existing longitudinal cohort studies across the United States (US) with new data collected using a common protocol. By pooling data from diverse cohorts, ECHO is a comprehensive dataset that provides a unique opportunity to explore health outcomes across various geographic regions, socioeconomic backgrounds, and racial/ethnic groups.

The ECHO program is designed to harmonize data collection across cohorts through a centralized protocol, ensuring standardization and comparability of measures related to environmental exposures, health outcomes, and demographic characteristics [17]. The harmonization effort, done by the ECHO Data Analysis Center (DAC) [7], enables researchers to assess associations with consistent definitions and methodologies while maintaining the integrity of cohort-specific characteristics. Participating cohorts contributed data on maternal health, childhood development, and environmental influences, such as disasters, environmental pollutants, and social stressors. The harmonization done by DAC has produced derived tables that cover participant registration, demographics, health history, and pregnancy data.

ECHO’s centralized approach to data harmonization included standardized assessments for disaster exposure, maternal characteristics, and health outcomes. Each cohort adhered to the ECHO-wide Data Collection Protocol, which was developed to ensure that critical variables, including sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and health indicators, were collected in a consistent format. De-identified data were used for analysis, ensuring participant confidentiality and adherence to the ethical guidelines. This study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Delaware and determined to be exempt (Reference Number: 2225666–1). Access to the ECHO dataset for this study was obtained from NIH Data and Specimen Hub [14] and includes raw data as well as derived tables developed by NIH and ECHO-DAC.

The ECHO Program’s structure allows for the inclusion of diverse populations, including those disproportionately affected by environmental and social stressors, such as disasters. This diversity strengthens the generalizability of findings, enabling a deeper understanding of how disaster exposure impacts maternal health across different sociodemographic and geographic contexts. However, it is important to note that the geographic information was not available as a part of the anonymized data analyzed for this research.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The ECHO release 2 dataset includes 69 prospective birth cohorts that have enrolled 63,215 women and their children across the US. The data includes records for 32,311 pregnancies among 21,801 individuals. Those who responded to the Life Stressors Checklist—Revised (LSC-R) were shortlisted as possible population for this study (n = 3569). The final study sample was obtained by keeping only the first responses from the same individuals leading to a final sample of 3,142 women (ages 14–46) from 14 unique ECHO cohorts spanning an enrollment period from 2003 to 2022. Each woman was uniquely identified by their anonymized participant ID and had data on disaster exposure, sociodemographic characteristics, and physical and mental health outcomes discussed further in the subsequent sections.

Disaster exposure assessment

The disaster exposure was assessed using the Life Stressor Checklist-Revised (LSC-R), a validated tool designed to capture exposure to significant life stressors, including disasters [47]. At the time of enrollment, the participants self-reported whether they had experienced a disaster and the frequency of their exposure (categorized as single exposure for one event or multiple exposures for two or more events). Based on these responses and distribution of data, participants were classified into three groups for this study: non-exposed (no reported disaster exposure), single exposure (one reported disaster event), or multiple exposures (two or more reported events).

Maternal characteristics

In the ECHO dataset, in addition to the DAC derived tables, Maternal Medical Records Abstraction (MMRA) and Pregnancy Medical Conditions Information (PMCI) tables were the primary sources for maternal characteristics used in this study. Maternal characteristics included maternal age, marital status, education level, race/ethnicity, household annual income, and pre-pregnancy BMI. Maternal age was either directly reported or when missing, was calculated based on the participant’s date of birth and the year of ECHO reported pregnancy. Education levels were self-reported at the first research visit and categorized as high school or less, some college, or college graduate. Race and ethnicity were also self-reported and harmonized into five categories: non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic/Latina, Asian/Pacific Islander, and Other (including Native American, Alaskan Native, multiracial, or other). Pre-pregnancy BMI was extracted from the DAC derived tables and categorized as non-obese and obese.

Health conditions

Health conditions were identified from the aforementioned medical records (MMRA) and self-reported (PMCI) data tables. The data were categorized into physical health, mental health, and behavioral characteristics. Physical health outcomes included chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and asthma. Other chronic conditions (e.g. liver problems, kidney issues, and heart conditions), if reported, were summarized under a chronic condition variable. Among mental health outcomes only depression was reported in the harmonized data. From behavioral health outcomes smoking, alcohol use, and drugs consumption are modeled in this study as negative for only those with no usage history. These health outcomes were examined for their associations with disaster exposure overall (primary aim) and with different frequencies of disaster (secondary aim). We hypothesize that women exposed to disasters are more likely to experience poorer health outcomes, with the severity of these outcomes increasing as the frequency of disaster exposure rises.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations. As this is a descriptive analysis, we retained missing categories to preserve sample size and highlight patterns in data availability that may reflect structural inequities. Bivariate statistical analyses were performed to assess the relationships between disaster exposure and sociodemographic and health variables. Specifically, chi-square tests were used for categorical variables, and t-test and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) were used for continuous variables. All analyses were conducted using SAS software’s SAS OnDemand for Academics [38]. An alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance for all tests.

Results

Sociodemographic and health characteristics of disaster-exposed vs. non-exposed groups (Aim 1)

Of 3,142 individuals in the study sample, 554 (17.6%) women reported lifetime disaster exposure. Women exposed to disasters exhibited several significant sociodemographic and health differences compared to non-exposed women. Results of this analysis are shown in Table 1. Women exposed to disasters were statistically significantly older on average (mean age = 29.42 years, SD = 6.33) compared to those not exposed (mean age = 28.27 years, SD = 5.94). The prevalence of disaster exposure was significantly higher among Hispanic women (24.7% vs. 18.6%) compared to non-exposed. By race, higher proportion of women with exposure identified as White (51.3% vs. 46.3%). Marital status varied, with a greater proportion of exposed women being married or cohabiting (29.2% vs. 26.0%, p = 0.0067). Educational attainment was higher in the exposed group, with 59.6% reporting education beyond high school compared to 53.1% in the non-exposed group (p < 0.0001). Disaster exposed women reported higher levels of income compared to those not exposed (p < 0.0001).

Behavioral factors, including smoking, alcohol use, and drug usage did not significantly differ by disaster exposure status. However, health conditions revealed some differences. Diabetes was more prevalent among exposed women, as was hypertension. Those exposed to disaster reported higher levels of depression (33.75% vs 31.30%, p = 0.0135) compared to non-exposed individuals. Frequency of disaster exposure and associated characteristics (Aim 2)

Of the disaster exposed women (n = 554), approximately one-third (n = 169) reported exposure to multiple disasters in their lifetime. Results of association across these groups are reported in Table 2. Age was significantly different across the three groups with highest mean age reported in women with single exposure (mean = 29.90, SD = 6.33, P < 0.0001). Racial and ethnic disparities were also prominent. The proportion of White women increased with disaster exposure, from 46.29% in the non-exposed group to 46.75% in the single-exposure group and 61.54% in the multiple-exposure group. The proportion of Black women decreased with disaster frequency, from 34.51% (non-exposed) to 23.90% (single exposure) and 30.77% (multiple exposures). Hispanic ethnicity was observed the least in the multiple exposure group (10.65%). Educational attainment showed an inverse relationship with disaster frequency, with higher proportions of women with education beyond high school in the single-exposure and multiple-exposure group.

Discussion

This study highlights significant sociodemographic and health-related differences among women enrolled in the ECHO cohort and exposed to disasters compared to those who were not exposed, as well as variations based on the frequency of disaster exposure. Women in the ECHO Cohort with disaster exposure were more likely to be older, have higher socioeconomic status, and have higher rates of chronic physical health conditions, such as diabetes and hypertension, compared to their non-exposed counterparts. It is important to note that the ECHO cohorts include women that have given birth and were recruited while pregnant or immediately following childbirth. These are a vulnerable group that has not been widely studied for long-term consequences of disaster exposure, hence highlighting the crucial contribution of this study. It is important to note here that the observed associations are unadjusted and may reflect cumulative exposure or age-related risk.

Sociodemographic differences

Disaster exposure was associated with notable sociodemographic differences. Exposed women were, on average, older than their non-exposed counterparts. This may reflect that older women are more likely to have lived through multiple disasters over their lifetimes, aligning with research suggesting cumulative exposure to disasters increases with age. These results are consistent with other studies that have shown that increasing age is associated with higher vulnerability to the adverse health impacts of disasters [4, 5, 23, 28, 31, 36].

Racial and ethnic differences were also evident, with White women being disproportionately exposed to disasters and Hispanic ethnicity was less common among women with disaster exposure. We have seen few other studies describing the racial and ethnic disparities in disaster exposures [16], however, studies have noted that racial and ethnic minorities are more vulnerable to the impacts of disasters due to systemic inequalities and socioeconomic disadvantages [24, 49].

Marital status and educational attainment were also associated with disaster exposure, with a greater proportion of exposed women being married or cohabiting and having education beyond high school. These findings differ from those widely reported in the literature [45, 46, 48]. These patterns may suggest that disaster response and recovery efforts need to account for the diverse sociodemographic profiles of affected populations to ensure equitable resource allocation [44]. The choice to have a child after disaster exposure may also be influenced by cultural factors and resources which can explain the higher socioeconomic status of the exposed group.

Health disparities

Health disparities were evident among disaster-exposed women, with higher prevalence rates of chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension [10]. These findings are consistent with evidence linking disaster exposure to long-term physical health consequences, mediated by chronic stress, disruption of healthcare access, and exacerbation of preexisting conditions [20]. The progressive increase in chronic disease prevalence with disaster frequency further highlights the cumulative burden of repeated exposures on maternal health [37]. The higher rates of chronic diseases among disaster-exposed women, especially those with multiple exposures, emphasize the critical need for interventions that cater to the specific health needs of these vulnerable populations and that are supported over time to mitigate chronic health outcomes.

Asthma prevalence did not differ between exposed and non-exposed groups. This finding may reflect differences in the acute versus chronic impacts of disasters or the influence of other factors, such as geographic location or environmental conditions that were not directly assessed in this study [32]. Behavioral factors, including smoking, alcohol use, and drug use, were not associated with disaster exposure. This contrasts with some studies that report increases in substance use following disasters, emphasizing the need for further research to explore how these behaviors may vary by disaster type, severity, and population and over time [3, 25].

Higher rates of depression were reported in the exposed population which is consistent with previous studies [1, 40]. This highlights the psychological toll of disaster exposure and the need for comprehensive mental health screening and care as part of disaster response and recovery efforts [37].

Implications of frequency of exposure

The progressive increase in age, racial/ethnic disparities, and prevalence of chronic conditions with the frequency of disaster exposure underscores the potential compounding effects of repeated exposure to disasters. Women in the multiple-exposure group had the highest prevalence of chronic diseases, hypertension, and diabetes, emphasizing the cumulative health toll of living through multiple disasters [22]. These findings are consistent with the"cumulative disadvantage theory” [8] which posits that repeated exposure to adverse events exacerbate health and social inequities over time. The disproportionate burden of chronic conditions among disaster-exposed women, particularly those with multiple exposures, highlights the need for targeted disaster preparedness and response efforts that prioritize the unique health needs of this vulnerable population as well as longitudinal research that tracks these long-term outcomes [34].

Strengths and limitations

This study leverages the harmonized and diverse data from the U.S.-based ECHO Program, which integrates data from multiple cohorts across the US, enabling robust analysis of sociodemographic and health disparities in a diverse sample. The use of ECHO data is a key strength, as it allows for the examination of disaster exposure and health outcomes in a diverse population that includes groups disproportionately affected by environmental stressors. The harmonized approach to data collection ensures consistency across cohorts, increasing the reliability and comparability of the findings. Additionally, this study provides valuable insights into the compounding effects of repeated disaster exposure, which is an understudied area in maternal health research.

However, limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Firstly, some of the findings in this study were contrary to the common perception that disasters disproportionately impact the most vulnerable populations. This may be attributed to the high proportion of missing data and should be interpreted with caution. In addition, the study participants represent a unique subset of the population (women enrolled in ECHO cohort and having given birth between 2003–2022). While the ECHO dataset includes a diverse population, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all women, particularly those living in rural areas or in countries with differing disaster profiles. The anonymized dataset used in this study did not include geographic information such as rural/urban residential status. Additionally, variability in recruitment strategies and regional differences in disaster exposure types among ECHO cohorts may have introduced unmeasured heterogeneity that was not accounted for in the analysis. Disaster exposure and certain health behaviors, such as smoking and alcohol use, were self-reported, which may have introduced recall or social desirability bias. This study used a retrospective approach, which allowed for the analysis of disaster exposure and its associations with maternal health outcomes based on available historical data. However, the lack of information about the timing of disease onset relative to disaster exposure limits the ability to establish a temporal relationship or causality. For example, it is unclear whether health conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, or chronic diseases were preexisting, exacerbated by the disaster, or developed as a result of disaster-related stress and environmental factors. Similarly, the time elapsed since the disaster event could influence the observed associations, as the long-term effects of disaster exposure on health may differ from immediate or short-term effects.

This limitation highlights the challenge of disentangling the directionality of associations in retrospective studies where the exact temporal sequence of exposures and outcomes is not available. Future studies should aim to incorporate prospective designs or collect data on the timing of health outcomes relative to disaster exposure to better elucidate potential causal pathways and identify critical windows of vulnerability or opportunities to build resilience.

The analysis relied on bivariate associations, and while key sociodemographic and health variables were included, unmeasured confounders, such as access to healthcare, availability of mental health services, or preexisting health disparities, may have influenced the observed relationships. The observed associations are unadjusted and may reflect cumulative exposure or age-related risk. Moreover, the study could not differentiate between the type or severity of disasters, which may have varying impacts on health outcomes. While disaster frequency was assessed, the analysis did not account for variations in timing, intensity, or recovery periods, which could differentially influence maternal health outcomes.

Conclusion

This research provides critical insights into the potentially lasting health implications of disasters on women, highlighting sociodemographic vulnerabilities. The study seeks to inform policies and interventions that enhance resilience and promote equitable recovery for women of childbearing age. These findings will guide disaster risk management practices to address the chronic and systemic challenges faced by women.

The results offer important insights into the sociodemographic and health vulnerabilities associated with disaster exposure, underscoring the need for targeted public health interventions and equitable disaster preparedness strategies. These findings contribute to the growing evidence on disaster-related health disparities and serve as a foundation for future research. Public health strategies should prioritize equitable disaster preparedness, response, and recovery efforts, addressing the cumulative health burden of repeated disaster exposure. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs, adjust for confounders, and examine psychosocial and contextual factors mediating the relationship between disaster exposure and maternal health outcomes.
