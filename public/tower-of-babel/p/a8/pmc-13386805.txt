Butyrate attenuates house dust mite-induced activation of 16HBE bronchial epithelial cells in association with modulation of gene expression
Authors: Roos E M Verstegen, Jennifer Hotie, Margarita Bikineeva, Suze van Brummelen, Johan Garssen, Gert Folkerts, Linette EM Willemsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe airway epithelial barrier is the primary defense line of the lungs against allergens, such as house dust mite (HDM). A disrupted airway epithelial barrier plays a major role in asthma development. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are bacterial fermentation products that are associated with prevention against allergic inflammation. In this study, we aim to elucidate the direct preventative effects of SCFAs, particularly butyrate, on the HDM-induced bronchial epithelial cell (BEC) activation and barrier disruption in-vitro.Materials and methodsImmortalized human 16HBE cells were exposed to HDM to simulate allergen-induced BEC inflammation. Pre-exposure of 1-1000 µM acetate, propionate or butyrate were studied for their ability to prevent epithelial activation. Histone deacetylase activity of cells was measured after pre-incubation with 100 or 200 µM butyrate and combined butyrate and HDM exposure. In a transwell, air-liquid interface (ALI)-culture, the effects of 100 µM butyrate on barrier integrity (transepithelial resistance (TEER) and 4 kDa FITC-dextran permeability) and inflammation were assessed in presence and absence of HDM. To gain broader insight in the effects of butyrate, RNA sequencing (RNAseq) was performed using 16HBE cells exposed to HDM, butyrate or both.ResultsPre-incubation with butyrate protected against the production of HDM-induced IL-8 (1-100 µM), CCL5 (1-100-1000 µM) and CCL20 (1-10-100 µM) by solid phase cultured 16HBE-cells, although 1000 µM butyrate increased IL-8 and CCL20 release. Pre-incubation with acetate and propionate showed similar effects. 100 µM butyrate did not suppress HDAC activity, while a dose of 200 µM butyrate did. In ALI-culture, HDM increased barrier permeability, and pre-incubation with 100 µM butyrate tended to prevent this. Additionally, HDM-induced CCL20 production was significantly decreased by butyrate. RNAseq revealed 100 µM butyrate to significantly affect biological processes of the cells linked to gene expression, mitochondrial function, cell division and cell signaling. These effects were observed both in absence and presence of HDM-exposure.ConclusionButyrate, at physiologically relevant doses, modulates gene expression of 16HBE cells, affecting amongst others post-transcriptional and mitochondrial pathways. In ALI-cultures, butyrate prevented epithelial activation (CCL20) and tended to prevent HDM-induced barrier defects. These findings highlight the relevance of SCFAs such as butyrate in maintaining bronchial epithelial cell homeostasis relevant for prevention and control of allergic airway disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03718-y.Keywords: Allergic asthma, Airway epithelial barrier, House dust mite, Butyrate, RNA sequencing

Introduction

The airway epithelial barrier serves as the primary defense line of the lungs protecting against exposure to exogenous agents, including microbes and allergens. The epithelial barrier consists of several protective components, such as a mucus layer, antimicrobial peptides, and tight junctions and adherens junction proteins, which tightly connect bronchial epithelial cells (BEC) [1]. The bronchial epithelium plays a central role in the development and progression of allergic asthma. Beyond maintaining mucosal homeostasis, it contributes to airway inflammation and remodeling by actively regulating both innate and adaptive immune responses to pathogens [2]. BECs express pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect molecular structures derived from pathogens and allergens. Upon exposure to irritants, airway infections, and/or airborne allergens, particularly when activation is persistent or excessive, BECs can produce alarmins, cytokines and chemokines. These mediators recruit and activate dendritic cells (DCs), thereby promoting allergic sensitization and type-2-cell-mediated immunity [1, 3]. Disruption of airway epithelial barrier has been recognized as a key factor in the pathogenesis and development of asthma [4].

House dust mite (HDM)-allergy affects approximately 1–2% of the global population [5]. HDM species vary per geographic region, with 13 species identified worldwide [6]. Among the HDM-derived allergens, Der p 1 and Der p 2 are recognized as the most abundant and potent contributors to HDM-induced allergic responses [7]. Certain HDM allergens, such as Der p 1, possess proteolytic activity. Their proteinase activity can cleave tight junction proteins within the airway epithelium [8], thereby compromising epithelial barrier integrity. As a result, HDM allergens can more readily penetrate the epithelial barrier, facilitating their uptake by recruited DCs in the lungs. Other HDM allergens, including Der p 2, are capable of interacting with PRRs, such as Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) [9], as well as protease activated receptors (PARs) such as PAR-2 [3]. Binding of HDM to these receptors results in epithelial cell activation and the subsequent secretion of various chemokines and cytokines. These mediators promote the recruitment of innate immune cells such as DCs, ILC2s, basophils and eosinophils. For instance, chemokine ligand (CCL)-20 recruits DCs [3], CCL5/RANTES contributes to eosinophil recruitment [10], and IL-8 has been associated with the chemotaxis of neutrophils and T-cells [11, 12]. The production of these inflammatory mediators is crucial for the further development of type-2 immune responses and the progression of allergic asthma [13]. Consequently, preventing inappropriate activation of BECs and strengthening epithelial barrier integrity represent important strategies in the prevention and management of this disease.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are metabolites produced through the fermentation of dietary fibers by the gut microbiota. These metabolites exert systemic effects and have been shown to play a protective role in several diseases including asthma [14]. The most abundantly produced and widely studied SCFAs are acetate, propionate and butyrate. Limited data are available on the systemic SCFA concentrations. Total SCFA levels are estimated to range from ± 375 µmol/L in the portal blood to ± 79 µmol/L in the human peripheral blood [15]. SCFAs, including butyrate, exert their biological effects through multiple mechanisms, including histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition and modulation of membrane bound G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs).

Inhibition of HDACs by SCFAs results in epigenetic modifications that alter gene expression. This process promotes transcriptional activation, allowing the expression of anti-inflammatory genes [16]. Such changes in gene expression may contribute to the prevention of allergic diseases [17]. Among the SCFAs, butyrate is recognized as a particularly potent HDAC inhibitor. Through this mechanism, butyrate has shown to restore and prevent inflammation-induced epithelial activation and barrier disruption in both intestinal epithelial cells [18] and esophageal epithelial cells [19]. In addition to HDAC inhibition, SCFAs exert biological effects through binding to specific GPCRs, primarily GPR41, GPR43 and GPR109A. Depending on the cell and location of the GPCR, binding of the SCFAs to the GPCR induces specific downstream pathways, such as anti-inflammatory cytokine production, HDAC reduction and FoxP3 induction [14]. In ex vivo-cultured mouse colon tissue, butyrate induced IL-18 production via activation of GPR109A, thereby contributing to the suppression of intestinal inflammation [20].

Although associations between SCFAs and protection against (pulmonary) allergic inflammation have been reported, and their effects on the intestinal epithelium have been described, the direct preventative effects of SCFAs –particularly butyrate– on HDM-induced airway epithelial inflammation remain insufficiently characterized. In this study, the direct effects of SCFAs, with a specific focus on butyrate, were investigated in relation to the HDM-induced epithelial activation and loss of bronchial epithelial barrier integrity.

Materials and methods

Bronchial epithelial cells and culture conditions

The SV40-transformed and immortalized human bronchial airway epithelial cell line 16HBE14o- (kindly provided by the Utrecht Medical Center, the Netherlands), from here on called 16HBE, was used for all experiments. Cells were grown in 1x Minimal Essential Medium (MEM) (Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 10% Fetal Calf Serum (FCS) (BioWest, France, S1810-500)(heat inactivated for 45 min at 56 °C) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL and 100 µg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Cells were cultured in 75 cm2 or 25 cm2 flasks (Greiner Bio-One, Austria) at 37 °C at 5% CO2 atmosphere and used from passage 31–61. The medium of the cells was refreshed every other day and cells were passaged approximately once per week, when cells reached confluency (± 90%).

Experimental conditions

Standard flat-bottom cell culture experiments were performed in 96-wells plates (seeding density 2*104 cells)(Corning Inc., USA), 48-wells plates (Corning Inc.) (seeding density 2.5*104 cells) or white clear bottom 12-wells plates (Corning Inc.) (seeding density 7–9*104 cells). Medium was refreshed every 2–3 days. Experiments started when the cells in the wells reached confluency.

Transwell experiments were performed using 12-well transwell plates with 0.4 μm pore polyester membrane inserts (Corning Inc.) Cells (seeding density 105 cells/well) were grown under liquid submerged culture conditions, with apical and basolateral fluid volumes set to 500 µL and 1500 µL respectively. As in the standard flat-bottom culture experiments, medium was refreshed every 2–3 days and experiments started when the cells in the wells reached confluency. Continuation of the experiment under air-liquid-interface (ALI) conditions entailed no apical and 900 µL basolateral medium. Continuation of the experiment under submerged conditions entailed 250 µL apical and 900 µL basolateral medium. Supernatant was collected from each compartment at the end of experiments and stored at -20 °C until further usage.

Reagents

House-dust-mite treatments were performed by making a solution of HDM (Greer, Switzerland; Batch nr: XPB70D3A25) in cell culture medium except with 1% instead of 10% FCS, control media also contained 1% FCS (more information in results section). In transwell-experiments, HDM was administered apically. Short-chain fatty acid treatments were performed by making solutions of sodium acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, Art #S2889), sodium propionate (Sigma-Aldrich, Art #P1880) or sodium butyrate (Sigma-Aldrich, Art #303410) in the previously mentioned cell culture medium. Trichostatin A (TSA)(Sigma-Aldrich, Art# T8552) was dissolved in DMSO during storage. For treatments a solution of this TSA solution in cell culture medium was prepared. In transwell experiments, SCFAs or TSA were administered at the basolateral side.

Cell proliferation / cell viability assays

WST-1 cell viability assay for mitochondrial activity (CELLPRO-RO, Roche, Switzerland) were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, to assess the effects of treatments on the metabolic activity of the cells. Cell culture supernatant was aspirated at the end of an experiment, followed by addition of WST-1 solution (1:10 dilution of stock in cell culture medium with 1% FCS) to the cells. After 30 min incubation at 37 °C at 5% CO2 atmosphere, absorbance was measured with the GloMax®Discover (Promega, USA) at 450 nm. Cell viability was calculated relative to the negative control (100%).

Barrier integrity and permeability assays

In the transwell experiments, Trans-Epithelial Electrical Resistance (TEER) measurements were performed using a Millicell ERS-2 V-Ohm meter (Merck, USA), in order to determine epithelial barrier integrity. For ALI-culture experiments, 100 µL medium was added apically one hour before measurements. After the measurements this medium was aspirated again.

At the last day of transwell experiments, paracellular permeability of the epithelial cell monolayer was measured using 4 kDa Fluorescein Isothiocyanate(FITC)-dextran (FD4) (Sigma-Aldrich, Art #46944). Culture medium was changed to PBS in the apical and basolateral compartments. After 1 h, FD4 in PBS (100 mg/mL) was added to the apical compartment. After 30 min, 100 µL supernatant was removed from the basolateral compartment and kept in a black flat bottom 96-wells plate (Corning Inc.). Using the GloMax®Discover, the plate was measured at Ex/Em = 475/520 nm respectively, against a standard curve.

Protein expression using ELISA

Concentrations of IL-8 (R&D Systems, USA), CCL5 (R&D Systems) and CCL20 (R&D Systems) in the cell supernatants were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs). Measurements were performed according to the manufacturers protocol, with the following adjustments; 50 µL coating with capture antibody, blocking with 150 µL Reagent Diluent, 50 µL sample, 50 µL detection antibody solution, 50 µL working dilution of HRP-conjugate, 50 µL substrate solution, 25 µL stop solution. Plates were measured using the GloMax®Discover with absorbance set at 450 nm and 600 nm.

HDAC activity measurement

Nuclear protein extraction

After detaching cells using trypsin (Gibco, Art #25300-062), cells were transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf’s and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C. After washing the cells two times (500 g, 5 min, 4 °C ) with ice-chilled 1xPBS (Merck), pellets were resuspended with cytoplasmic fraction buffer. This buffer contained HEPES (10 mM, pH 7.5), MgCl2 (1.5 mM), KCl (10 mM), NP40 (0.5%), Milli-Q water and protease inhibitor tablets (Merck). Cells were lysed for 10 min at 4 °C on a rotator, vortexed for 10 s and then centrifuged at 12.700 rpm for 1 min at 4 °C. The cytoplasmic protein fraction was collected and stored at -80 °C. The cell pellet was again washed two times (12 000 rpm, 1 min, 4 °C) with ice-chilled 1xPBS. Cell pellets were then exposed to nuclear fraction buffer, containing RIPA Lysis and Extraction Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Art #89900), MgCl2 (1 mM), Benzonase Nuclease (0.25 KU) and protease inhibitor tablets, for 30 min, while being regularly vortexed and staying on ice. After centrifugation (12.700 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C), the nuclear protein fraction was collected, quantified by means of a Pierce BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and stored at -80 °C until further analysis.

The wells were washed three times and detection antibody was added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Gene expression using RT-PCR and RNA sequencing

RNA isolation for RT-PCR

RNA isolation was executed using the RNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN, Netherlands) and RNAse-free DNase set (QIAGEN, Art #79256), according to manufacturer’s protocol. Shortly, cells were collected using RLT-buffer from the kit with the addition of 1% 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, Art #63689), and stored at -20 °C if not used immediately. Using the kit, cells were placed in spin columns, and washed with the supplied buffers. Finally, cells were treated with DNase and eluted into collection tubes. RNA was quantified using NanoDrop™ One/OneC Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and was then divided into aliquots containing 500 ng genetic material. Unused RNA was stored at -80 °C.

cDNA synthesis and RT-PCR

cDNA was synthesized from isolated RNA samples using the iScript™ cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, USA) and T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad), according to manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed with the C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler with CFX96 Real-Time System reaction module (Bio-Rad). iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) with PrimePCR SYBR Green Assay primers (Bio-Rad) for house-keeping gene RPS-13 (qHsaCIP0041394), IL-8 (qHsaCED0046633), CCL5 (qHsaCID0011644) and CCL20 (qHsaCID0011773) were used. Data were normalized to the RPS-13 gene and mRNA expression levels, expressed as fold change (normalized to untreated control), were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

RNA sequencing

Isolated RNA samples were sent to Utrecht Sequencing Facility (USEQ, Utrecht, the Netherlands) for sequencing. Sample quality control (QC) on the sequence reads from the raw FASTQ files was done using FastQC (v0.11.9). TrimGalore (v0.6.7) was used to trim reads based on quality and adapter presence after which FastQC was again used to check the resulting quality. rRNA reads were filtered out using SortMeRNA (v4.3.6) after which the resulting reads were aligned to the reference genome fasta (GCA_000001405.15_GRCh38_no_alt_plus_hs38d1_analysis_set.fna) using the STAR (v2.7.10b) aligner. Follow up QC on the mapped (bam) files was done using Sambamba (v0.8.2), RSeQC (v5.0.1) and PreSeq (v3.2.0). Read counts were then generated using the Subread FeatureCounts module (v2.0.3) with the Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.106.ncbi.gtf gtf file as annotation. The raw feature counts (read counts) were further analyzed with the web application iDEPv2.01 [21]. Threshold for gene expression was set at minimum lower counts per million (CPM) of 1, in at least 2 libraries. Subsequently, the data was transformed for clustering using EdgeR: log2(CPM + c), with pseudo count (c) = 4. A hierarchical clustered heatmap was then generated with an average linkage using Pearson as distance measure. The differential expression analysis in DEG1 was performed with the DESeq2 method with a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.05 and a minimum fold-change of 1.5, while enabling independent filtering of lower counts. Selected comparisons included: HDM vs. Control; BUT vs. Control; and HDMBUT vs. HDM. GAGE pathway analyses were performed, per comparison, using different pathway databases; (1) Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process, (2) GO Molecular Function and (3) Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), all with a pathway significance cut-off of 0.05. The top 20 pathways of each database were further investigated.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism software (10.4.1). Data were tested for adherence to a Gaussian distribution and transformed when required. Analyses including two dependent variables were analyzed by a two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. In case of missing data, a mixed effects analysis with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was performed. In absence of a significant interaction, a paired t-test was performed to validate the model and a repeated measures one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test (selected comparisons) was performed to assess the effects of interventions.

Analyses including one dependent variable were analyzed using a repeated measures one-way ANOVA, or mixed-effects analysis (missing data), with Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test (selected comparisons). When data did not adhere to the Gaussian distribution, a Kruskal-Wallis test or Friedman test, with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test (selected comparisons) was performed. In all statistical tests we accounted for matching per n, p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Pre-incubation with 1-100 µM, but not 1000 µM butyrate, prevents HDM-induced lung epithelial cytokine and chemokine production

To develop a model for HDM-induced BEC activation we compared HDM exposure on the 16HBE cell-line in absence and presence of 1% FCS (Fig.S1). FCS was omitted since we did not observe HDM-induced activation of the 16HBE cells in pilot experiments. Other studies reported to have used serum-deprived medium when exposing 16HBE cells to HDM [22, 23], which also proved successful in the current study. Based on these results, the dose of 50 µg/mL HDM in 1% FCS medium was chosen.

In the first experiment, the effects of 48 h pre-incubation with different concentrations of butyrate on HDM-induced activation of 16HBE cells were explored at 24 h in solid phase 96-wells plates (Fig. 1A). Metabolic activity decreased both upon HDM-exposure and upon exposure to 1000 µM butyrate in presence or absence of HDM, 1-100 µM butyrate did not affect viability (Fig. 1B). Despite slight decrease in metabolic activity, cells exposed to HDM produced higher concentrations of the pro-inflammatory markers IL-8, CCL5 and CCL20 compared to control cells (Fig. 1C-E). Concentrations of 1 and 100 µM butyrate significantly prevented HDM-induced IL-8 and CCL5 production. However, 1000 µM butyrate showed contrasting results, by significantly decreasing CCL5, while increasing IL-8 and CCL20 concentrations. The effects of acetate and propionate on HDM-exposed cells showed a similar pattern (Fig.S2), although doses of 1000 µM acetate nor propionate increased IL-8 and CCL20 concentrations.

200 µM butyrate tends to decrease HDAC activity in HDM-activated 16HBE cells

Generally, the effects of butyrate on inflammation are attributed to HDAC inhibition [24]. To verify if this contributed to the cytokine suppressive effects of butyrate, the effects of 100 µM or 200 µM butyrate on HDAC activity were studied in HDM-exposed 16HBE-cells cultured in 12 wells plates (Fig. 2A). HDM did not significantly increase production of IL-8 or CCL20 upon HDM-exposure in this experiment at the 24 h time-point (Fig. 2B, C), nor CCL5 (data not shown). 100 and 200 µM butyrate treatment did decrease IL-8 production in control conditions, and 200 µM tended to reduce CCL20. However, butyrate did not affect these cytokines in the presence of HDM. Peak mRNA expression of IL-8 and CCL5 after HDM-exposure arose at 5 h (Fig.S3). Therefore, cells were collected at the 5 h time-point to determine HDAC activity. HDM exposure did not affect HDAC activity in 16HBE cells (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, 100 µM butyrate did not affect HDAC activity, but a decreasing trend of HDAC activity as found when cells were pretreated with 200 µM butyrate, both in the absence and presence of HDM.

Butyrate decreases CCL20 production and improves epithelial barrier permeability of HDM-exposed 16HBE cells in ALI-culture

After establishing the potential of butyrate on HDM-activated 16HBE cells in flat-bottom cell-culture plates, we further expanded our studies by addressing the effects in a transwell-model. Although literature describes both cases of successful [25] and unsuccessful[26] barrier development using 16HBE cells in air-liquid-interface (ALI) culture, our experiments show comparable kinetics and height in TEER between ALI and submerged conditions (Fig.S4A, B). In both conditions TEER values were consistently high (> 700 Ωcm2). Therefore, we continued with (semi-)ALI-culture experiments, including 48 h pre-exposure with butyrate (100 µM) and 24 h of butyrate plus HDM exposure (Fig. 3A).

TEER measurements on day 10, did not indicate any effects of HDM, nor butyrate on the epithelial barrier resistance (Fig. 3B). However, functional permeability of the epithelial layer, as measured by using FD4 as paracellular permeability marker, was significantly increased by HDM-exposure. This effect tended to be counteracted by butyrate (Fig. 3C). In the ALI-culture, apical HDM-exposure increased both IL-8 and CCL20 production by the 16HBE cells, which was demonstrated both in the apical and basolateral compartment (Fig. 3D-G). Exposure to butyrate did not prevent HDM induced increase in IL-8 production (Fig. 3D, E), however, butyrate did prevent rise in CCL20 production (Fig. 3F, G). These effects of butyrate were not demonstrated in a submerged model (Fig.S4C-H). A subset (N = 4) of the experiment was compared to the effects of HDAC inhibitor trichostatin A (TSA) (Fig.S5A). TSA prevented HDM-induced rise in barrier permeability (Fig.S5C), and showed inhibition of HDM induced CCL20 production, albeit only in the apical compartment (Fig.S5D-G).

100 µM butyrate upregulates mRNA expression of genes related to posttranscriptional processes in both control and HDM-exposed cells

In Fig. 1, anti-inflammatory properties of 100 µM butyrate on HDM-exposed cells were observed. Based on Fig. 2, the dose of 100 µM butyrate was not able to inhibit HDAC activity. In Fig. 3, we established that butyrate can partially protect against HDM-induced impaired barrier permeability and reconfirmed its potential anti-inflammatory properties. To further explore the mechanisms by which 100 µM butyrate affects HDM-activated lung epithelial cells, RNA sequencing was performed on HDM-exposed 16HBE cells, in absence or presence of 48 h (refreshed at -24 h) pre-incubation with butyrate in two independent experiments. To identify the optimal time-point for RNA sequencing analysis, a time-course experiment was conducted (Fig.S3A), revealing peak mRNA production of IL-8 and CCL20 to be at 5 h post HDM exposure (Fig.S3B-D).

14,793 genes passed the expression threshold of at least one CPM in two libraries. Although only 14 genes were upregulated in the HDM-exposed cells compared to control (Fig.S6A), analyses revealed butyrate to upregulate 297, while downregulating 15 genes in control (Control vs. Butyrate). In HDM-exposed cells, butyrate upregulated 300, while downregulating 20 genes (HDM vs. HDM+Butyrate). An heatmap overview, including the top 10 of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (all upregulated) can be found in Fig. 4A, B. Of these DEGs in the control-butyrate and HDM-HDM+butyrate comparisons, 213 genes appeared in both comparisons and thus were affected by butyrate in absence and presence of HDM. DEGs were clustered by biological process and molecular function, using GAGE pathway analysis. After HDM exposure, analyses of the biological processes show upregulated processes that play a role in innate immunity, cytokine signaling and immune regulation, indicating heightened activation of the 16HBE cells (Fig.S6B). Analysis focused on the effect of butyrate revealed that in both control and HDM-exposed 16HBE cells, butyrate treatment significantly upregulates biological processes centered around regulation of gene expression and cell division, particularly at the post-transcriptional and cellular information flow (Table 1). Molecular function pathway analyses revealed downregulation of molecular functions involved in detection of external signals and transducing them to intracellular responses (Table 2). Discussion

SCFAs have been associated with the prevention of allergic inflammation in the lungs; however, their direct effects on the airway epithelium following exposure to HDM allergen exposure remain largely unknown. In this study, we investigated the direct effects of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly butyrate, on house dust mite (HDM)-induced activation of 16HBE cells and the associated impairment of epithelial barrier function. Pre-incubation with 1-1000 µM acetate and propionate and 1-100 µM butyrate protected against the production of IL-8, CCL5 and/or CCL20 by 16HBE cells upon HDM-exposure. The 100 µM dose butyrate did not inhibit HDAC activity, whilst a higher dose (200 µM) did. In ALI-culture, HDM increased barrier permeability, while pre-incubation with 100 µM butyrate tended to attenuate this. Additionally, HDM-induced CCL20 secretion by 16HBE cells was significantly prevented by basolateral butyrate exposure. RNA sequencing indicated that pre- and coincubation with butyrate significantly affects gene expression of genes related to biological processes of RNA and DNA modulation and division, and downregulated molecular function involved in detection of external signals and cell signaling. In general, these effects of butyrate were observed both in absence and presence of HDM-exposure.

Studies reporting the effects of SCFAs on airway epithelium use relatively high doses, ranging from 0.5 to even 10 mM [15, 27–29]. Remarkably, we found that pre-incubation with doses of 1 µM of all three SCFAs was already sufficient to suppress the production of at least one cytokine by 16HBE cells upon HDM-exposure. Though, it should be noted that the effects were not overall consistent between experiments. However, the dose of 100 µM butyrate did generally interfere with HDM induced 16HBE activation and/or barrier disruption. Data on in-vivo SCFA concentrations in the lungs are limited. Systemic concentrations have been estimated to be around 70 µmol/L for acetate and 4–5 µmol/L for propionate and butyrate [15]. In a study with participants without respiratory disease, combined propionate and butyrate concentrations in the exhaled breath condensate were ± 104 µM and ± 28 µM in the broncho-alveolar lavage fluid [30]. This indicates that the dose of 100 µM chosen to be used in this study potentially reflects the in-vivo situation, making the observed effects more relevant.

Notably, a dose of 1 mM butyrate slightly decreased metabolic activity, while it increased IL-8 and CCL20 production by HDM exposed 16HBE cells but maintained CCL5 suppression. This effect was not observed for 1 mM acetate nor propionate. High doses of butyrate can induce apoptosis in epithelial cells [31], possibly by activating the JNK/AP1 pathway [32]. However, since 1 mM butyrate differentially affected chemokine production, the reduced metabolic activity may also mark functional changes in the HDM exposed 16HBE cells. In immune cells such as macrophages and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), relatively low doses of butyrate have anti-inflammatory effects, while higher dosages induced inflammatory cytokine release. In human macrophages 100 µM butyrate was found to suppress LPS induced TNFα, while high dose butyrate (10 mM) enhanced IL1β secretion [33]. Similarly in T-cells, low dose (100 µM) butyrate increased regulatory T-cell development, this effect was lost using a higher dose (1mM), while yielding a Th1 effector phenotype [34]. Furthermore, increasing doses of butyrate induced IL-8 release by PBMC, and 2mM butyrate intensified PamCys3 (TLR2 agonist) induced IL-8 release [35]. In addition, only high dose butyrate facilitated cholera vaccine induced CCL20 release by intestinal epithelial cells, alike HDAC inhibiter TSA [36]. Thus, depending on the cell type, condition, type of stimulation and butyrate dose, anti-inflammatory properties of butyrate may switch towards activation of immune defense.

In Caco-2 cells (relatively) low butyrate doses had barrier protective effects, while high doses decreased cell viability and impaired the barrier [37]. Nevertheless, others observed increased barrier resistance in 16HBE cells exposed to 1 mM butyrate while restoring HDM-induced barrier impairment [38].

An important observation in this study is the protective effect of the physiologic concentration butyrate (100 µM) against HDM-induced CCL20 production. CCL20 is a key player in the allergic asthma cascade, as it attracts immature DCs and binds the CCR6 receptor on these cells [39, 40]. Interestingly, the secretion of CCL20 upon HDM exposure is quite allergen specific, as other aeroallergens, such as cockroach and ragweed, do not evoke this response in 16HBE cells [40]. Because of its significant role in facilitating type 2 sensitization and inflammation, CCL20-CCR6 is considered as a therapeutic target in respiratory disease [41]. Decreasing CCL20 concentrations have great potential in prevention of type 2 inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness as observed in mice [42, 43]. Future studies could explore the effects found in this study by performing a co-culture model with 16HBE cells and monocyte-derived DCs, and consecutively study the DC function in driving type 2 responses of naïve T-cells. This to analyze the effect of HDM exposed 16HBE on activation and differentiation of these cells, as has previously been described for intestinal and bronchial epithelial cells [44, 45].

To gain insight in the mechanism by which butyrate might prevent HDM activation, an HDAC activity assay was performed. In HDM exposed 16HBE cells HDAC activity remained unchanged, while butyrate did change the activity in absence and presence of HDM, but only at the dose of 200 µM butyrate. Butyrate is a potent class I and IIa HDAC inhibitor, which would explain this finding [46]. It indicates that HDAC inhibition indeed plays a role in counteracting the inflammatory response induced by HDM. However, the lower concentration of butyrate (100 µM) did not show inhibitory effects in the HDAC activity assay, while this concentration did reduce IL-8 and CCL20 levels, and partially protected against increased barrier permeability in the current experiments. Possibly, the anti-inflammatory effects of 100 µM butyrate depend less on HDAC inhibition and are mediated via other mechanisms, such as signaling through GPCRs [16]. Interestingly, in the RNA-seq analyses we observed increased GPCR-activity upon butyrate exposure in HDM-activated 16HBE cells. This indicates that 100 µM butyrate possibly exerts its effects partly via GPCR-binding, although actions via HDAC inhibition cannot be excluded.

To gain more insight in the effects that butyrate exerts of HDM-activated 16HBE cells, RNA sequencing was performed on cells pre-exposed to butyrate for 2 days and consequently exposed to butyrate and HDM for 5 h. Interestingly, only 15 genes were differentially expressed between control and HDM-exposed cells. The exposure time was chosen based on a pilot experiment in which increased mRNA levels of IL-8, CCL5 and CCL20 were observed. RNA sequencing applies stringent statistical filters. Consequently, genes might not have passed the thresholds of the RNA-seq analyses. Nevertheless, the genes that were found to be differentially expressed were all related to innate immunity, cytokine signaling and immune regulation, indicating a heightened inflammatory state.

Butyrate altered gene expression in 16HBE cells to a larger extend. However, none of the 15 genes that were induced by HDM were directly modified by butyrate, nor was there overlap in the pathways affected by the DEGs. By contrast, butyrate affected around 213 genes both in control as well as HDM-exposed conditions. 206 genes were affected by butyrate either in the control or HDM condition. Nevertheless, the pathways related to the DEGs are relatively similar. In both settings, thus in control as well as HDM-exposed cells, butyrate downregulates molecular function pathways related to the catalytic activity acting on both RNA and tRNA. This is reflected by the other post-transcriptional gene regulation pathways that revealed to be downregulated, including helicase, nuclease, and several transferases. The indication that butyrate acts on pathways that regulate gene expression via post-transcriptional processes has been introduced before [47]. In several epithelial cell lines, butyrate inhibits mRNA stabilizing proteins (RNA binding proteins, RBPs), which reduces the expression of inflammatory genes [47]. In the current context of airway epithelial activation by HDM, genes related to innate immunity, cytokine signaling, and immune regulation were upregulated. Butyrate did not affect expression of these genes. Therefore, we hypothesize that butyrate either directly or indirectly via modification of expression of other genes, impairs the post-transcriptional processes on mRNA. Possibly via inhibiting mRNA stabilizing proteins, thereby reducing translation of mRNA to inflammatory proteins including CCL20. It must be noted that these post-transcriptional effects are likely still occurring via the HDAC inhibitory function of butyrate [48]. Consequently, in contrast to our HDAC activity assay, the RNA sequencing data, which may be more sensitive, hint that butyrate indeed inhibits HDAC activity in HDM-exposed 16HBE cells.

In both control and HDM-exposed conditions, butyrate upregulates pathways that control the flow of ions across the cell membrane, e.g. gated channel activity and ion channel activity. Similarly, in a study were 16HBE cells were exposed to Alternaria alternata, a fungal species which spores can induce allergic airway inflammation, ion flux across the epithelium increased [49]. This was linked to epithelial barrier disruption and pro-inflammatory signaling. In the current study no effects were observed by HDM and/or butyrate on the TEER, indicating the transepithelial barrier resistance. However, butyrate tended to reduce HDM-induced permeability. While both methods are indicators of the barrier integrity, TEER estimates passive diffusion of ions between the apical and basolateral compartment, while FD4 is an inert marker for paracellular permeability [50]. Ion fluxes can be disturbed, for example when epithelial cells actively secrete ions like for example chloride, functional paracellular permeability FD4 omits this problem. The key players in epithelial barrier integrity are tight junctions (TJs) and adherens junctions [51]. HDM allergens can damage TJs via e.g., protease activity and via binding to protease-activated receptor-2 (PAR2) [3, 8, 9, 51]. Especially TJ proteins occludin and ZO-1 are known to be affected [52]. Future studies, including protein measurements and/or imaging techniques, should provide insight in effects of butyrate on tight junction integrity. Interestingly, another study that exposed submerged 16HBE cells to 200 µg/mL HDM, demonstrated a significant modest decrease in TEER within 48 h [38].

Lastly, we found that butyrate downregulated mitochondrial gene expression in HDM-exposed cells. Mitochondrial dysfunction is clearly associated with asthma pathophysiology and explored as therapeutic target [53].

To stay close to the in-vivo situation, and to confirm the epithelial activation studies while performing the barrier studies, ALI-cultures were used. 16HBEs were exposed from the basolateral side with butyrate, and from the apical side with HDM. However, for the apical exposure HDM was added in medium, which terminated the air-interface on the last day of the experiment. The use of a nebulizer to administer HDM to the apical side would be a technical improvement of this model. Of note, in this study we were able to perform an ALI culture with 16HBE cells where TEER developed equally well and with similar kinetics as compared to the corresponding submerged condition. Results in literature differ in successfully forming an epithelial barrier when using 16HBE cells under ALI conditions [25, 26]. However, we did not observe the sudden drop in TEER that was previously described by He et al. [26], but rather showed a stable epithelial resistance similar in height compared to submerged conditions. Indeed, others also previously showed that 16HBE cells in ALI-culture do form tight junctions and cell polarity [25], which relates to our observations and supports the use of ALI cultured 16HBE cells as model to study bronchial epithelial barrier function.

In general, the 16HBE cell line is considered suitable for proof-of-concept studies, as it forms polarized monolayers and tight junctions and can be convenient for transport and barrier assays [54]. However, it is immortalized and shows cellular heterogeneity and differences from primary tissue in gene expression and functional responses [55]. Therefore, it is important to confirm and extend the findings of this study in a physiologically relevant model using primary bronchial epithelial cells.

This study demonstrates the potential of physiologically relevant concentrations of butyrate to modulate airway epithelial responses to aeroallergen HDM. Notably, butyrate prevented HDM-induced secretion of CCL20 and the increase in epithelial barrier permeability. These findings suggest a promising avenue for the development of preventative and potentially therapeutic strategies targeting allergic airway diseases. The observed effects at a relatively low concentration of 100 µM butyrate, indicate that butyrate may exert broad regulatory effects on gene expression, thereby modulating airway epithelial responses. The effects appear to involve transcriptional changes in genes amongst others associated with post-transcriptional regulation and mitochondrial pathways. Furthermore, in contrast to previous reports, this study demonstrates that 16HBE cells can be successfully cultured in ALI-conditions. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of investigating SCFAs as modulators of epithelial-immune interactions in the context of allergic asthma. At the same time, the complexity of butyrate’s effects warrants further investigation in more physiologically complex models, particularly those using primary epithelial cells.
