Insights into Sudbury’s deep architecture: a revised geodynamic model for Earth’s largest ore-bearing impact structure
Authors: Rasmus Haugaard, John G. Spray, Ademola Q. Adetunji, Rajesh Vayavur, Mike A. Hamilton, David Snyder, Saeid Cheraghi, John Ayer, Kristin Henry, Taus R. C. Jørgensen, Guido Serafini, Richard Smith, Lisa Gibson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Impact cratering has played a fundamental role in Earth’s geological history and has resulted in the formation of some of the world’s most productive economic mineral deposits. Here, we investigate the crater architecture of one of the top three largest terrestrial impact structures: the unique 1.85-billion-year-old Sudbury basin (Canada), the richest crater-hosted mineral district in the world. The integration of two, recently completed ~ 3 km ultra-deep drill holes combined with high-resolution geophysical imaging provides new evidence for a concentric uplifted horst-like feature. This indicates that Sudbury is a peak-ring structure with at least two concentric fault systems internal to the collapsed crater rim; sharing similarities with the Schrödinger impact crater on the Moon. A radially-orientated crater-floor trench accommodates a recently discovered > 30-million-tonne nickel-copper-platinum group element ore body. Our proposed model provides a revised framework to evaluate cratering processes and related mineral resources associated with a terrestrial peak-ring impact structure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51979-w.

Introduction

Large terrestrial impact structures are key to our deeper understanding of cratering processes responsible for not only shaping the early continental crust but also the formation of essential mineral resources1–4. In contrast to many planetary bodies, the Earth’s continental crust has, for the most part, been tectonically deformed, eroded and reworked throughout its history. Consequently, the three-dimensional structure of impact craters is poorly understood because of lack of examples studied with deeply probing geological and geophysical methods. The three largest known impact structures preserved on Earth are (e.g.,5): the Vredefort structure (South Africa), which is eroded to the base of its transient cavity; the Chicxulub structure (Mexico), which is relatively well preserved but buried and lies half onshore and half offshore, with its central uplift located in the transition zone where deep probing methods are impractical; and the Sudbury structure (Canada). Sudbury, despite having undergone significant later thrust deformation that distorts its radial symmetry, preserves key sections in the northern part of the basin that allows for deep probing to reveal details of crater architecture and related ore mineralization well inside the crater rim structure. The Sudbury impact structure was formed at 1.85 Ga and is among the best-exposed, accessible and complete of Earth’s larger impact craters, even though it has been deformed following impact6–10 (Fig. 1a). The impact event facilitated the formation of one of the richest Ni-Cu-platinum group element (PGE) ore systems, with production totalling more than 22 million tons of Ni and Cu in the last ~ 130 years11. Sudbury continues to be an essential future resource of critical minerals and key metals. A complete “stratigraphy” of the impact structure has survived. This includes a ~ 1.5 km thick uppermost interaction zone (comprising breccia and reworked materials), known as the Onaping Formation, overlying a < 5 km thick impact melt-sheet, now differentiated into the Sudbury Igneous Complex (SIC), associated ore bodies at its base, footwall breccia, and underlying basement (target rock) lithologies. Since its discovery, our knowledge has grown from Sudbury being interpreted as an igneous intrusion (e.g.,12,13) to a hypervelocity impact structure6,14. Over the last 60 years, estimates of the crater’s original diameter have ranged from an initial 55 km6 through to a more recent ~ 260 km15–17. However, uncertainty persists regarding exactly what crater form Sudbury originally possessed due to a lack of preservation of its original morphology. Two contenders are: multi-ring basin and peak ring structure (e.g.,10,18,19). How the latter structure is generated is still debatable. The formation of ring structures at Chicxulub have been simulated by the use of hydrocode collapse modelling, where the solid target rocks behave as a fluid-like substance (acoustic fluidization), combined with observations from seismic reflection and drill core data (e.g.,20,21). The simulations mimic the different stages of how the peak ring structure forms when the outward collapse of the central uplift is thrusted on top of the inward collapse of the transient cavity rim. Such adaptation of hydrocode modelling to the Sudbury structure is more difficult to achieve as the original geometry is less known and, from a field perspective, target rock fault systems are deemed responsible for the main part of the deformation during the crater modification phase.

Here we explore Sudbury’s architecture and a newly discovered > 30 million tonne Ni-Cu-PGE ore body based on (1) recent high-resolution seismic reflection and magnetotelluric (MT) profiles obtained from the eastern part of the structure’s North Range and (2) two recent ~ 3 km deep drill holes that penetrate the SIC and underlying basement gneisses. Importantly, because the North Range of the structure has undergone less modification since impact, this data provides insight into the nature of the impact-generated melt sheet and target rock contact. The impact crater model places important constraints on the mechanisms behind crustal adjustment, modification, tectonics and mineralization following one of the largest known terrestrial impact events, as well as helping to constrain the probable original form of the structure.

The Sudbury impact structure

The remaining core of the impact structure forms a 60 × 30 km elliptical basin that rests on Paleoproterozoic rocks (Huronian Supergroup) to the south and Archean gneisses (Levack gneiss complex) of the Superior Province to the north (Fig. 1a). Sudbury retains a complete section through a differentiated igneous impact melt sheet. This comprises a sequence of what is historically referred to as norite-gabbro-granophyre lithologies (although they are strictly intermediate to acid rocks of monzonitic to granitic composition derived from the continental crust; e.g.,22). Locally, the lower part of the norite sequence and the top part of the footwall gneiss host world-class magmatic Ni-Cu-PGE ores11. Basin excavation during impact created space for later epiclastic sedimentation as recorded by the fine-grained shale and sandstone deposits of the Onwatin and Chelmsford Formations, which overlie the breccias of the Onaping Formation (Fig. 1b). The impact occurred in an active orogenic belt where the resulting structure was deformed during the ca. 1860 − 1840 Ma Penokean Orogeny18, followed by the ca. 1773 − 1691 Ma Yavapai and ca. 1600 − 1400 Ma Mazatzal Orogenies, the 1.45 Ga Chieflakian event, and the c. 1235 − 945 Ma Grenville Orogeny23,24. The extent to which each of these events affected the SIC and its proximal footwall remains difficult to resolve and continues to be debated, in part because the Palaeoproterozoic deformation events collectively contributed to the dominant NE-SW structural grain of the southern Sudbury area (e.g.,24,25). Nevertheless, deformation was strongly localized in the southern part of the Sudbury structure, particularly along discrete zones such as the South Range Shear Zone (Fig. 1a) where crustal shortening resulted in overthrusting along low angle faults26. In the North Range, and in the relatively undeformed foreland to the north of the SIC, deformation and shortening has been less pronounced27.

Geological evidence supports the erosional remnant of the Sudbury impact structure originally representing a complex crater with concentric (ring) structures located at certain scalable distances from a geometrical centre of impact (Fig. 1a). They appear to follow a square-root-of 2 distribution28. Multiple lineament analysis applied to Landsat imagery suggests that the “centre of impact” is located ~ 10 km south of the South Range (Fig. 1a;15). Field evidence from north of the SIC implies that at least two concentric rings are developed, which have been interpreted as impact-generated fault systems (Fig. 1a;16,29): (1) A ring fault (Ring 2) at ca. 45 km radius defined by the Hess offset dike, and which separates the central uplift from the outer annular trough, and (2) A ring fault (Ring 3) at ca. 65 km radius, possibly representing the inner edge of the collapsed transient cavity rim and the maximum extent of the impact melt-sheet. The position of Ring 3 correlates with the maximum extent of the radial Foy Offset dyke and the outer limit of the zone where shock and/or thermal removal of the magnetization of pre-impact Matachewan dikes occurred15,16. Notably, the Levack gneiss complex immediately north of the SIC comprises key high-grade metamorphic assemblages, which have originated from mid-crustal levels that were uplifted as a consequence of the unloading and inward collapse following excavation5,30 Crystallization ages of these target rocks define two Archean clusters: an older 2668 − 2658 Ma group, and a younger 2647 − 2644 Ma group31.

Results

Drill core geology and footwall geochronology

The two recently completed deep drill holes in the Northeast Range (Fig. 1b) reveal the main impact lithologies to a depth of ca. 3.1 and 2.8 km, respectively (Fig. 2a). In both holes, the majority of the lithologies are dominated by the SIC that thickens towards the centre of the basin. Clear lithological transitions are seen at the granophyre,quartz gabbro and norite contacts at around 1350 m in the north hole and 1500 m in the south. At the base of the SIC, a ca. 200 m thick Sublayer Norite occurs, which typically occupies embayment structures (channels) in the footwall gneiss (Figs. 1 and 2;11). At ~ 2250 m (north hole) and ~ 2600 m (south hole), the transition from the SIC to the footwall gneiss is marked by a breccia zone (footwall breccia), varying in thickness from ~ 100 m (north hole) to 350 m (south hole; Fig. 1). This zone comprises fragmented target rocks of primarily granite gneiss that have been metamorphosed to high grade via contact metamorphism from the overlying superheated impact melt-sheet (SIC; Fig. 2). Between the Sublayer Norite and underlying gneiss are two variants of semi- to massive sulfide mineralization as: (1) contact mineralization dominated by Ni sulfides (Fig. 2b), and (2) footwall mineralization dominated by Cu sulfides (Fig. 2c). Down both holes, the brecciated zone changes into non-brecciated gneissic basement rock with a distinctive pre-impact metamorphic fabric emphasized by amphibole and biotite layering (Fig. 2d and e). Zircon U-Pb ID-TIMS ages of the gneiss protoliths in the north and south holes yield 2671.6 ± 2.9 Ma and 2648.5 ± 2.5 Ma, respectively (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S1), which demonstrate the multi-range ages of target rock gneisses in the southern Superior craton31.

Geophysical data across the northeast range

The reflection profile shows key seismic features that, north of the SIC, are characterized by the S1 zone containing pronounced and dense subhorizontal-to-weakly south-dipping reflectors (Fig. 3a and b) . The zone is terminated at the Levack gneiss-SIC contact by a pronounced subvertical F1 structure, which separates the S1 from steeper south-dipping reflectors constituting the S2 and S3 seismic zones. The geometry of these reflectors is concave up, beginning with a ~ 45˚ southern dip at the F1 fault that becomes progressively less steep further south underneath the Onaping Fm, where the geometry changes to a shallow northern dip in relation to the large semi-transparent fold zone between 10 and 12 km distance (Fig. 3a and b). Due to lack of coherent data (low signal-to-noise ratio) in this area (see Materials and Methods), the geometry of the S4, S5 and S6 zones remains speculative. However, gravity modeling using the density of the granophyre (S4) as a contrast line shows that the large scale antiform likely constitutes lithologies with higher densities than the granophyre whereas densities equivalent to or less than the granophyre appear above the large scale fold (Supplementary Fig. S2). The reflector sets of S2 are dense and continuous, whereas in the S3 zone they are more discontinuous (Fig. 3a and b). In contrast, the S4 zone contains less pronounced, more unstructured reflector sets . The dip change in S2, S3 and partly S4, is linked to the prominent large-scale fold centered at ca. 11 km distance and at ca. 1.5 km depth (Fig. 3b). The fold is cut by the F2 event on its southern limb that disconnects the subhorizontal reflectors to the south. Because of fewer internal differences in the seismic zones south of the large fold, individual zones are here inferred based on the geometry of the reflectors on the north side of the fold (Fig. 3b). The MT profile (Fig. 3c) reveals a high conductive feature (C1) along the contact between the S2 and the S3 zones. This feature is intersected by the two drill holes and is entirely surrounded by more resistive crust (R1). In a MT fence diagram (Supplementary Fig. S3), the C1 anomaly displays a NNE-SSW strike length of at least 3 km. Towards the semi-transparent zone further south, the resistivity of the crust decreases (R2). Relative to the C1 conductor, less conductive features (C2 and C3), are centred in the south at shallow crustal level. These are attributed to the graphite-rich mudstone-turbidite facies that constitute much of the infilling Onwatin Fm (Fig. 3c).

Discussion

Crater architecture and large-scale structure

Integration of the surface and deep drill hole geology with the seismic and MT data facilitates new insights into the geological architecture across the eastern part of the North Range of the Sudbury structure (Fig. 4a). We interpret the Levack gneiss north of the SIC to be part of a more extensive footwall complex that continues to the base of the profile (S1 zone; Fig. 4a). The gneiss samples at the base of the drill holes (Fig. 2) show that the gneiss complex extends beneath the SIC, as previously suggested for the central part of the North Range32. Seismologically, the gneiss complex is separated into two different domains by the major F1 fault that detaches the subhorizontal S1 reflectors from the steeper south dipping S2 reflectors underlying the SIC basin (Fig. 4a). Constrained by the deep drill holes, the S2-S3 contact defines the crater floor, which is locally brecciated. Geometrically, the floor follows the S2-S3 concave-up interface where the deepest part of the crater floor is around 3.2 km depth immediately north of the large-scale fold (Fig. 4a). The area from 10 to 13 km has generally a lower signal-to-noise ratio, which places more uncertainty on the geological interpretation of the fold. However, the gravity modelling supports the size, shape and location of the fold observed in the seismic profile. The prominent reflector at the top of the fold we therefore assign to the gabbro-norite (S3 zone; Fig. 4a). As the fold affects both basement gneiss and the SIC, the fold is interpreted to have formed either by crater readjustment or by a later deformation event. The fold, however, predates the development of the F2 fault, because the NW-SE-striking surface fault truncates its forelimbs (Figs. 1a and 4a).

Trough structure formation and mineralization

The large MT C1 zone (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. S3) reveals a clear conductivity anomaly corresponding to the recently intersected Ni-Cu-PGE sulfide mineralization. This occurs within a sublayer norite trough/embayment structure in the crater floor gneiss (Fig. 4a). Currently, an estimate of the total inferred resource of the ore body is 31 Mt comprising a 9 Mt contact mineralization (Fig. 2b) grading at 1.5 wt % Ni, 0.9% Cu, 0.5 g/t Pd and 0.3 g/t Pt, and a 22 Mt footwall gneiss mineralization (Fig. 2c) grading at 3.0 wt% Cu, 0.3 wt% Ni, 1.4 g/t Pd and 1.1 g/t Pt32. This makes it one of the larger ore deposits at Sudbury, specifically in terms of its average Cu grade11, and a significant economic discovery. The geometry of the C1 anomaly continues to the northeast following the SIC-crater floor contact, which indicates a total strike length of the ore body of at least 2.5–3.0 km (Supplementary Fig. S3). The trough structure extends radially in line with the Whistle-Parkin embayment and associated Offset dike. Structurally, therefore, the mineralization most probably represents a continuation of the Whistle-Parkin radial offset (Fig. 1a). With regard to trough formation, it has been suggested that during penetration of the projectile into the crust, the uneven hypervelocity jetting of solid ejecta, melt and vapour scours radial channels into the footwall33–35. The result is a bowl-shaped excavation cavity that is scalloped. However, it is considered unlikely that such primary excavation features would survive modification-stage tectonics, and that is more probable that the trough features were formed by post-impact activation of footwall faults to form graben features, as has been documented at Manicouagan36. Either way, the trough provides a topographic low for the heavy, immiscible metal sulfides to accumulate as they sink out of the melt body11 (Supplementary Fig. S3b).

Crater floor relief and displacement

The high-resolution data presented here has revealed an unprecedented view of the deeply eroded SIC-gneiss contact in the eastern part of the North Range of the Sudbury impact basin. Within a larger complex crater framework, the transect line is located in the outer part of the structural uplift (Fig. 1a). This region typically represents crustal rocks that were structurally elevated following the excavation37. Field documentation28 as well as remote sensing lineament studies15 in the crater floor of the North Range have shown the existence of key concentric structural zones, rich in friction melts (pseudotachylite). These are bounded by key displacement faults that were active during the modification stage of the cratering process. The uplifted Levack gneiss complex immediately north of the SIC likely represents such a zone, which, in addition to being bound by friction melts, also exhibits an enhanced concentration of shock-generated shatter cones28. The northern margin of this uplifted horst-like structure is bounded by the concentric Hess offset dyke (Fig. 1a), which is considered to represent a deep crustal displacement fault that separates the structural uplift to the south (southside upthrow) from the annular trough to the north (northside downthrow;29). The deep-seated F1 fault (Fig. 4a) not only separates the melt sheet to the south from the gneiss to the north, but clearly separates the S1 zone footwall gneiss immediately north of the SIC from the S2 zone gneiss underneath the SIC. The reflector geometry of the latter zone displays a symmetry with the basin floor morphometry (S2-S3 contact), whereas the reflectors in the S1 gneiss are different, having a more subhorizontal geometry (Fig. 4a). If the original melt sheet extended to the proposed location of the innermost components of the collapsed crater rim that is terminating the annular trough to the north (Ring 3, Fig. 1a), then the S1 gneiss potentially should have retained the same basin geometry as the S2 gneiss (if those two footwall domains are stratigraphically in the same crustal level). Because this is not the case, we propose that vertical slip movements were active along the F1 plane between the two gneiss domains. The fault is a deep crustal feature that is observed throughout the entire eight km section (Figs. 3 and 4). Allowing for at least six km of erosion since the impact38, this fault likely facilitated over 14 km of uplift during the crater modification stage. Geometrically, this deep vertical fault plane is distinct from the moderately south-dipping thrust faults developed during post-impact regional deformation, which typically overprint the footwall in the South Range (e.g.18). Consequently, we find it implausible to assign the origin of this fault structure to a post-impact northward thrusting event. We cannot exclude a pre-impact fault origin, which later got reactivated during impact. However, the geometry, nature and position of the F1 fault feature is intriguing.

The formation of deep-rooted displacement faults in basement footwalls is a natural process during the crater modification stage, either by (1) gravitational collapse of the transient cavity via formation of listric faults, or (2) deformation facilitated by vertical displacement in the central uplift of the crater floor and the formation of ring horst-like structures (ring formation). As the former crater process is restricted to the annular trough occurring north of the Hess offset dyke and not to the inner basin floor (Fig. 1a), the latter process is more probable to explain the footwall gneiss immediately north of the F1 fault.

Following the horst-graben dynamic presented by29, the F1 displacement fault would represent the southern margin (the northern being Hess) of a horst-like structure (Fig. 4b).

Such vertical faulting of crystalline crater floor target rocks would be active during footwall adjustments following hypervelocity impact, which has been documented from other large terrestrial impact basins (e.g., Chicxulub). In the 85 km diameter late Triassic Manicouagan impact crater, Canada, field and deep (up to 2 km) drill hole data from the inner basin floor reveal large vertical basement offsets (up to 1000 m) that were active as part of the modification process prior to melt-sheet crystallization39,40.

Structural and scaling comparisons between Sudbury and the similar size Vredefort and Chicxulub impact structures suggest that an inner ring structure could have been in the form of a topographical peak ring that would have been located immediately north of the presently exposed SIC in the North Range41,42. An uplifted topographic high (Fig. 4c) at this location is generally in agreement with the current geology: lower stratigraphic level gneisses are apparent inward of such a structure and higher stratigraphic level Huronian metasediments and greenstone belt lithologies occur northwards (north of Hess; Figs. 1 and 4c). This location is approximately 45 km radius (Fig. 4c) and is very comparable with the more well-preserved peak ring at Chicxulub (~ 40 km radius43).

Further evidence for a peak ring structure at Sudbury within the same circumference as this study comes from the upper chilled contact of the SIC in the southeastern part of the impact basin. A thick ~ 300 m debris deposits with a monomict clast population of Huronian quartzite (up to 100 m in size) is preserved. The source of these clasts were likely derived from a local topographic high in form of a peak ring that emerged above the SIC melt pool44. In addition, a more recent study based on dip direction measurements and paleomagnetic results of the basal SIC contact suggests preservation of a possible remnant of a ring structure in the southern part of the impact basin45.

At Chicxulub, hydrocode collapse models show that the peak ring could have formed when the outward collapse of the central uplift is thrusted on top of the inward collapse of the transient crater rim21. Seismic reflection data from the Chicxulub peak ring structure reveal a prominent and deep inward sloping fault between the annular trough and the structural uplift. This feature has been interpreted as representing a thrust fault between the two inward and outward flow patterns43. Such a thrust plane is difficult to detect at Sudbury but the Hess ring fault at the outer part of the proposed peak ring in this study (Ring 2; Figs. 1a and 4c) occupies a relatively similar position as the thrust plane interpreted at Chicxulub and thus potentially represents such an event. A future extension of the seismic reflection data into the North Range of the Sudbury structure would aid in solving the geometry at depth of the Hess fault and its role in the formation of the ring structure.

We interpret the F1 fault to be part of an important deep basement structure that facilitated formation of the inner uplifted ring related to the horst-like structure proposed by29 and by extension, an indication of the likely position of a topographic ring immediately exterior of the SIC North Range (Fig. 4c). If correct, then the S1 gneiss zone in the northern part of the transect is imaging the deeper roots of such an uplifted structure. This indicates that Sudbury is a peak ring impact structure with similarities to the much better preserved Schrödinger basin on the south side of the Moon (e.g.46). Where the South Range has experienced intense crustal shortening, the melt-sheet and footwall in the North Range likely retained their original position since impact (e.g.,47). Therefore, in a larger-scale crustal model of the Sudbury impact (Fig. 4c), the transect model occupies the northern margin of the inner basin floor of the original topographic ring structure (e.g.42).

Along the ca. 2672 − 2648 million-year-old inner basin floor, the discovery of a large Ni-Cu-PGE deposit in a trough structure demonstrates that impact melt-sheet ore mineralization is facilitated by the morphology of the substrata. In deep crater floor depressions, the lower part of the melt-sheet can pond and gravitationally accumulate economically important metals within the sublayer. The integration of ultra-deep drilling with high-resolution geophysical data has provided additional evidence in favour of Sudbury being a peak ring structure; along with Chicxulub, the two largest known on Earth.

Materials and methods

Diamond drill holes

The two deep diamond drill holes, DDH1417200 (south hole, 3030 m depth), and DDH N-116 K (north holes, 2775 m depth), were drilled in 2020–2021 by Vale and Glencore, respectively. The deep drilling was guided by a multi-disciplinary approach involving the Radio Imaging Method (RIM)49 and the 3D Multigrid Electric Magnetic (MGEM) technique50. These methods can be used to identify potential sulfide bodies. The core logging of the total length of core was carried out by Vale and Glencore and the main lithologies were used in this study to constrain the overall architecture at depth. A total of five core samples of the footwall gneiss from the northern hole in Fig. 1 (hole ID #N-116 K) were textural and mineralogical examined and subsequently sampled from the following depth intervals: 2442–2442.50 m; 2454–2454.50 m; 2455–2455.60 m; 2742–2743 m; 2771–2772 m. Selected core sections of contact and footwall mineralization are shown in Fig. 2 and were recovered from extensive mineralized sections between ca. 2220–2250 m and ca. 2300–2385 m, respectively. From the southern hole in Fig. 1 (hole ID #1417200) mineralogical and textural examination were carried out on selected core sections from the footwall mineralization in ca. 2590–2620 m depth (core sections not shown here). From the deepest parts of the two holes, two footwall gneiss samples were collected for further geochronological work (see Fig. 2 and the Chemical abrasion ID-TIMS U-Pb geochronology section below).

Chemical abrasion ID-TIMS U-Pb geochronology

U-Pb analyses by isotope dilution thermal ionization mass spectrometry (ID-TIMS) were conducted on zircon at the Jack Satterly Geochronology Laboratory (JSGL), University of Toronto, Toronto, Ontario. Heavy mineral concentrates were obtained using conventional techniques (crushing, disk milling, Wilfley table) followed by magnetic and heavy liquid separations. At all stages, stringent cleaning protocols at the JSGL were followed to mitigate against potential laboratory sample cross-contamination. Detailed zircon selection, chemical abrasion pre-treatment techniques modified after51, U and Pb chemical separation and full analytical methods followed those described in52.

A mixed EarthTime 205Pb-235U-235U isotopic spike was added to the dissolution capsules during sample loading. Pb and UO2 were analyzed on a VG354 mass spectrometer using a Daly collector in pulse counting mode. The mass discrimination correction for this detector was constant at 0.07%/atomic mass unit. Thermal mass discrimination corrections were 0.10%/atomic mass unit for Pb and U. Dead time of the Daly system was 15 ns for Pb during the analytical period, monitored using the SRM982 Pb standard53. Mass spectrometer data were reduced using in-house software coded by D.W. Davis. All common Pb in each analysis was attributed to the isotopic composition of the procedural laboratory blank (Supplementary Table S1). Concordia and U-Pb analytical result plotting, age calculations and linear regressions were carried out using the Isoplot 3.71 Add-In for MS Excel of54; uncertainties in the U decay constants, though shown graphically, are not considered in the calculated age errors. All age errors and error ellipses are given at the 2 sigma or 95% level of confidence.

Felsic gneiss − 21RHN0021A (sample interval 2772.0–2772.2 m)

The zircon population from this sample of footwall gneiss from the north hole (DDH N-116 K) consists of predominantly pink grains that are highly cracked and fairly turbid, subhedral in form, irregular, broken, and slightly rounded (Supplementary Fig. S1). Three best quality, clearer, slightly elongate and somewhat sharper single grains were selected for chemical abrasion and analysis. All show a discordancy of ~ 2–3% but are essentially colinear. Free regression of this preliminary data suggests a lower intercept age near 1850 Ma, the canonical, well-constrained age for the Sudbury impact event8,31,55. Anchoring a regression at this date yields an upper intercept age for the felsic gneiss at 2671.6 ± 2.9 Ma (MSWD = 0.36), an age consistent with older components of the regional Levack gneiss [31]. Preliminary observations suggest planar fractures are present in some zircon grains, consistent with the remarks of8 from Levack gneiss samples studied immediately northwest of the SIC.

Felsic gneiss − 22RH050A (sample interval 3027.5–3028.0 m)

Zircon recovered from felsic gneiss sampled at the base of the south hole (DDH 1417200) are morphologically similar to those described above from the north hole basement (Supplementary Fig. S1), and locally also preserve evidence of planar features. Provisional U-Pb isotopic data for four analyzed fractions from this sample are 1–3% discordant and define a colinear array, offset from that of sample 21RHN0021A, with a younger upper intercept age of 2648.5 ± 2.5 Ma (anchored at 1850 Ma; Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. S1). This age is consistent with the timing of late Archean metamorphic component of the Levack gneiss8,31.

Seismic data

The seismic survey was carried out in 2017–2018 as part of a larger Canadian research project (Metal Earth; https://merc.laurentian.ca/research/metal-earth). The data processing of the transect 2D line (Line 182) was carried out by HiSeis (https://hiseis.com/) utilizing forward modelling and 2D refraction tomography. This line was acquired over an area of average elevation change, up to 30 m. The greatest elevation difference combined with strong lateral changes of velocity due to weathering resulted in refraction static corrections of magnitude 40 ms. Receiver spacing of 12 m resulted in 6 m CDP interval and maximum fold of 1600. The specific seismic parameters are listed in the Supplementary Table S2. The spacing between sources was 50 and 25 m, and between receivers the spacing was 25 and 12.5 m. More details on the seismic data acquisition are summarized in56. The processing was aimed to primarily enhance shallow crustal reflections particularly for mineral exploration purposes. The processing workflow includes refraction statics correction, gain recovery, deconvolution, bandpass filtering and noise attenuation algorithms. The static correction shifted the zero time to a final datum of 325 m for all the lines. A two dimensional Fourier-transform-based surface-wave noise attenuation module has been utilized to suppress coherent noise, such as ground roll and converted waves. Pre-stack time migration (PSTM) was applied to aid in handling complex subsurface velocity field. To further enhance resolution, spectral whitening has been incorporated into a pre-stack algorithm. To achieve clearer imaging of steep dips, weights have been allocated to higher offsets. Finally, several processes were applied to the final post-stack migration image such as: bandpass filtering, scaling and depth conversion. These seismic-data processing steps are described in57.

Gravity data

Seismic reflection imaging conducted in the eastern part of the North Range of the Sudbury impact crater reveals what could be interpreted as an antiform-like feature in the southern part of the transect line (Line 182). However, the feature is not well defined on the seismic reflection image because of a lower signal-to-noise ratio at this location. Therefore, we undertook inverse 3D modelling of the gravity data to see if the gravity data is consistent with our seismic interpretation. The data used in the inversion was reduced to give the complete Bouguer anomaly (assuming a density of 2.67 g/cc). The data was a combination of data collected in this study and data from the Geological Survey of Canada, collected on a coarser sampled grid. The VPmg potential-field algorithm developed by58 is used to perform 3D depth to basement geometry gravity inversion. This inversion assumes all layers are homogenous and inverts only for geometrical contacts between layers. The starting model is assumed to be a simple two-layered 3D density model i.e. cover overlying a basement that is constructed within a volume of dimensions of 6 km x 16 km x 8 km and a 300 m cell size. In the present case, the cover is comprised of Whitewater group sediments and the granophyre and was assigned a density contrast of 0 g/cm3. The basement is the higher-density quartz gabbro, norite and Levack gneiss, which were assigned a higher density contrast of 0.1 g/cm3. The Shuttle Radar Topographic Mission (SRTM 90 m) data was used for elevation. This model is then incised into a larger apparent density model to explain any regional trends evident in the data. An initial flat basement surface at 1.5 km depth is assumed as the geometrical contact between the two layers. The surface is then adjusted by the inversion to explain the gravity data. The RMS misfit was reduced from 0.9 mGal to 0.4 mGal. The final inverted surface shows a dome structure (Supplementary Information, Fig. S2) that is consistent with our antiform interpretation (Fig. 4a).

Magnetotelluric data

The magnetotelluric (MT) technique employs naturally occurring electromagnetic signals detected at the Earth’s surface to delineate the electrical resistivity configuration of the subsurface (e.g.59). The MT impedance tensor (Z) establishes a relationship between the recorded electric and magnetic field components at designated frequencies, facilitating the computation of the apparent resistivity and impedance phase [60]. Factors contributing to elevated conductivity encompass interconnected fluids, mineral ores, molten materials, graphite, and sulfide deposits (e.g.,60,61). MT datasets from 240 stations (21 MT and 219 AMT) arranged to provide three-dimensional (3D) coverage are included in this study. Details of MT data acquisition and processing are described in62.

The modular electromagnetic modeling and inversion (ModEM) 3D inversion algorithm63,64 was employed for the inversion process. Employing a finite-difference methodology, the program executes a nonlinear conjugate gradient algorithm that is regularized with model covariance, thereby imposing a penalty for deviations from a predetermined model in order to address the inversion problem. The model mesh is comprised of 104 × 98 × 78 cells along , , z -directions. The dimensions of the cells in the horizontal plane were 200 × 200 m, augmented by 12 padding cells that expanded outwardly by a factor of 1.3. The initial layer has a thickness of 25 m, which increases vertically by a multiplicative factor of 1.2. The initial model was characterized as a homogeneous half-space with a resistivity of 1000 Ωꞏm, and a smoothing parameter valued at 0.3 was determined to be optimal. Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) data, exhibiting a resolution of 90 m, was incorporated into the inversion model, and the static shift was mitigated through the addition of supplementary shallow layers (e.g.65).

At numerous locations, the acquired data exhibit considerable noise and unrealistic characteristics within the AMT “dead band” ranging from 800 Hz to 5 kHz; however, the error estimations associated with these abnormal data points are significantly understated in light of their variability. Consequently, the dataset underwent manual editing to down-weight the influence of all problematic components during the inversion process. The resistivity model was generated exclusively through the inversion of MT impedance (Z), as the incorporation of the vertical field transfer function (tipper) resulted in models with inadequate fit, potentially due to contamination. Impedance measurements were inverted across 18 frequencies spanning from 8,800 Hz to 22.5 Hz. A definitive fixed error threshold of 20% of √|Zxy·Zyx| was applied to the diagonal impedances, along with 5% of |Zxy| for Zxy, and 5% of |Zyx| for Zyx. A global normalized root-mean-square (nRMS) misfit value of 2.76 was achieved for the preferred 3D model. The data fitting was meticulously scrutinized utilizing the total nRMS values for individual stations, alongside the comparison of observed versus fitted impedances and phase tensor responses. The overall fit to the data appears robust, and the model response effectively mirrored the principal characteristics evident in the observed dataset.
