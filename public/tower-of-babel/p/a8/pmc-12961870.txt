Risk factors and clinical characteristics of fulminant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in children: a retrospective case‒control study
Authors: Yixin Ren, Ying Lin, Jihong Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFulminant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (FMPP) represents a life-threatening progression of typical Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) in pediatric patients. This study aimed to identify the risk factors and clinical characteristics associated with FMPP complicated by respiratory failure in children, thereby facilitating early recognition and timely intervention.MethodsA retrospective case–control study was conducted involving 66 children with FMPP and 217 children with non-fulminant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (NFMPP) who were admitted to the Department of Respiratory Medicine and the Intensive Care Unit of the Children’s Hospital Affiliated with Chongqing Medical University between January and December 2023. Cox regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors for FMPP. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance baseline risk factors, followed by a comparative analysis of clinical characteristics between the matched FMPP group (n = 62) and the matched NFMPP group (n = 116).ResultsWheezing, a Mycoplasma pneumoniae load ≥ 1.7 × 10⁷ copies/mL, and pulmonary consolidation were independently associated with FMPP, whereas concomitant Epstein-Barr virus infection and β-lactam treatment for ≥ 3 days were inversely associated. After PSM, children with FMPP more frequently presented with fine crackles and wheezing and had higher rates of atelectasis and neurological complications. Compared with NFMPP cases, FMPP was characterized by increased peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage neutrophil proportions and elevated liver enzyme levels, along with lower fibrinogen, total protein, albumin, and interferon-γ levels. In multivariable analysis, fine crackles, peripheral blood neutrophil percentage, and fibrinogen level remained independently associated with FMPP. Clinically, children with FMPP required more frequent escalation to second-line antimycoplasma therapy, β-lactam antibiotics, and glucocorticoids and experienced longer hospital stays; however, all patients recovered without mortality.ConclusionWheezing, a high Mycoplasma pneumoniae load, and pulmonary consolidation are independent risk factors for FMPP, whereas Epstein-Barr virus coinfection and early β-lactam therapy may mitigate disease progression. Although FMPP is characterized by severe systemic inflammation and multiorgan involvement, prompt and appropriate management can lead to favourable clinical outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Children, Fulminant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia, Respiratory failure, Risk factors, Clinical characteristics

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a significant causative pathogen of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in children [1]. Although the majority of cases of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) are mild and self-limiting, severe cases typically respond well to treatment with favorable outcomes. However, a subset of patients may develop life-threatening complications, including respiratory failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), acute lung injury, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) [2, 3]. A rapidly progressive form of MPP that necessitates life-sustaining interventions has been designated fulminant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (FMPP) [4]. Previous studies have indicated that the incidence of respiratory failure in children with MPP ranges from 1.0% to 1.5% [5–7]. Following the COVID-19 pandemic in 2019 and the subsequent relaxation of nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), the number of reported cases of MPP has been a global resurgence since 2023 [8, 9], accompanied by a notable increase in severe and critical cases.Although a rebound in reported cases has been observed following the COVID-19 pandemic, it remains uncertain whether the actual incidence or prevalence has increased.

Nevertheless, current research on FMPP in children remains limited; it primarily consists of case reports, and the precise clinical characteristics associated with this condition are not yet well defined.Given the potential severity of FMPP and the limited data available, investigating the risk factors and clinical features of this condition in children is crucial. This study aimed to investigate the risk factors and clinical features associated with FMPP in children presenting with respiratory failure as the primary life-threatening manifestation. We conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from pediatric FMPP patients who experienced respiratory failure and were admitted to our hospital in 2023. These findings may provide valuable insights for early recognition and management strategies for critically ill children, facilitating timely interventions to improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2024–227). Since the research utilized only deidentified retrospective data from the hospital’s electronic medical records and imposed no additional risks or interventions on patients, the IRB waived the requirement for informed consent.

Study population

This retrospective case‒control study enrolled pediatric patients diagnosed with MPP who were admitted to the Department of Respiratory Medicine and Intensive Care Unit at Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between January and December 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All participants met the diagnostic criteria for MPP outlined in the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Mycoplasma pneumoniae Pneumonia in Children (2023 Edition), with a disease duration of less than 30 days and an age range from 28 days to 16 years. They met any of the following criteria: (1) A single serum MP-IgM antibody titer ≥ 1:160 (by particle agglutination method); (2) A fourfold or greater increase in the MP-IgM antibody titer in two separate serum samples; (3) Positive MP-DNA or RNA in respiratory specimens (nasopharyngeal aspirates, nasopharyngeal swabs, or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid). FMPP cases were defined by the need for respiratory support due to respiratory failure that occurred either prior to admission or within 24h following emergency evaluation. In contrast, NFMPP patients are characterized by the absence of respiratory failure, thereby eliminating any requirement for respiratory support throughout the entire course of illness. Patients were excluded from this study if they (1) developed other life-threatening complications necessitating additional life-support therapies; (2) had severe underlying conditions that could lead to respiratory failure or life-threatening events (including bronchial foreign body aspiration, status asthmaticus, NYHA class IV heart failure, high-risk pulmonary embolism, etc.); (3) presented with congenital immunodeficiency or malignancy; (4) presented negative initial test results for Mycoplasma pathogens; or (5) had incomplete clinical data or were discharged prematurely.

Relevant definitions

Respiratory failure is defined as the presence of arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) less than 60 mmHg while the patient is breathing ambient air. Respiratory support included both noninvasive methods (continuous/bilevel positive airway pressure [CPAP/BiPAP] or high-flow nasal cannula [HFNC] oxygen therapy) and invasive interventions (endotracheal intubation with mechanical ventilation or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [ECMO]). Conventional oxygen therapy (e.g., nasal cannula, simple face mask) was not considered respiratory support. Other life-support therapies were defined as interventions required for immediately life-threatening organ dysfunction, including but not limited to vasopressor administration (e.g., norepinephrine) for circulatory shock, renal replacement therapy, emergency procedures (e.g., pericardiocentesis, chest drainage), and anticoagulation/thrombolysis for massive thrombosis.

Data collection

This study collected comprehensive clinical data from pediatric patients, including (1) baseline characteristics (age, sex, gestational age, birth weight, comorbidities, etc.); (2) preadmission data (symptoms, disease duration, pathogen detection results, chest imaging findings, and medications); and (3) postadmission data (symptoms and signs, laboratory test results, imaging findings, bronchoscopic findings, treatments and outcomes).

Analytical framework

To enhance causal inference, the study defined the onset of fever or respiratory symptoms as the starting point, with the duration from onset to hospitalization as the follow-up period. Hospitalization time served as the observation cut-off, and respiratory failure was identified as the outcome measure. Preadmission clinical data were collected to identify potential riskfactors. Optimal cut-off values derived from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to dichotomize continuous variables as risk stratification tools. Independent risk factors were determined through univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression (Cox) analyses. Subsequently, propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance the differences in risk factor variables between the FMPP and NFMPP groups. Samples were initially selected from the control group population at a ratio of 1:4 using the random number method. After screening according to unified inclusion criteria, a total of 66 FMPP cases and 217 NFMPP cases were ultimately enrolled (actual ratio 1:3.3). Clinical characteristics were compared postmatching to clarify features associated with FMPP.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed via R (version 4.4.3). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations and were compared via independent t tests; nonnormally distributed data are reported as medians (P25, P75) and were analysed via Mann‒Whitney U tests. Categorical variables are summarized as counts (%) and were compared via the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Optimal cut-offs for continuous variables were determined by ROC curve analysis(maximizingYouden’sindex) aiming to enhance clinical interpretability and to ensure consistency across subsequent analyses. Variable selection was conducted through least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation prioritizing λ.min. PSM was applied at a 1:2 ratio (nearest neighbor matching; calliper = 0.2 × logit[SD]), in an attempt to obtain postmatching balance (standardized mean difference [SMD] < 0.1; FMPP group retention ≥ 90%).The purpose of PSM in this study was to facilitate descriptive comparison of clinical characteristics associated with disease severity.All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 283 pediatric patients were enrolled in this study, including 152 males (53.7%) and 131 females (46.3%). The study enrolled 66 FMPP and 217 NFMPP pediatric patients. In the FMPP group, 39 patients were male (59.1%), while in the NFMPP group, 113 patients were male (52.1%). No statistically significant difference was observed in the proportion of males between the FMPP and NFMPP groups (59.1% vs. 52.1%, P = 0.317). The age of the patients ranged from 2.0 to 179.0 months, with a median age of 53.0 (25.0, 84.0) months.The median age in the FMPP group was [33.0 (15.8, 64.8)] months and that in the NFMPP group was [62.0 (32.0, 88.0)] months.Compared with the NFMPP group (62.0 months), the FMPP group had a significantly younger median age of onset (33.0 months) (P < 0.001). A total of 268 children completed serological testing for Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP), among whom 209 (78.0%) had an IgM antibody titer ≥ 1:160. The IgM positivity rates were 76.8% (43/56) in the FMPP group and 78.3% (166/212) in the NFMPP group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (χ²=0.059, P = 0.808). In addition, MP-DNA PCR testing of respiratory specimens was performed in 282 children, of whom 272 (96.4%) tested positive. The PCR positivity rates were 98.5% (65/66) in the FMPP group and 95.8% (207/216) in the NFMPP group, with no significant difference between groups (χ²=1.039, P = 0.308). After PSM, 62 FMPP cases were successfully matched with 116 NFMPP cases (Fig. 1 for the screening flowchart).

To mitigate the risk of overfitting, in accordance with the 1-in-10 rule [10], we refined the eight candidate variables (initially identified through univariate Cox regression) by conducting LASSO regression analysis (Figs. 2 and 3). This variable selection process resulted in seven statistically significant predictors for subsequent analysis: age, premature birth, wheezing, MP-DNA load, EBV infection, pulmonary consolidation, and β-lactam treatment duration.

These seven variables were subsequently included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis (Table 3). The results indicated that wheezing (HR = 1.987, 95%CI: 1.130~3.493, P = 0.017), an MP-DNA load ≥ 1.7 × 10⁷ copies/mL (HR = 1.785, 95%CI: 1.055~3.020, P = 0.031), and pulmonary consolidation (HR = 1.738, 95%CI: 1.037~2.914, P = 0.036) emerged as independent risk factors for FMPP development. Conversely, concomitant EBV infection (HR = 0.399, 95%CI: 0.185~0.858, P = 0.019) and a duration of β-lactam treatment ≥ 3 days (HR = 0.525, 95%CI: 0.303~0.910, P = 0.022) were identified as being inversely associated with the development of FMPP.

After matching, no significant differences were observed in the baseline characteristics or preadmission data between the two groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 5). The FMPP-matched group presented significantly greater incidences of fine crackles (59.7% vs. 31.9%) and wheezing (48.4% vs. 32.8%) (both P < 0.05). Laboratory tests revealed significantly elevated neutrophil percentage (NEU%), hepatic enzyme levels, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels and decreased fibrinogen (FIB), total protein (TP), and albumin (ALB) levels in the FMPP-matched group (all P < 0.05). In terms of complications, atelectasis (24.2% vs. 12.1%) and neurological complications (14.5% vs. 5.2%) were more prevalent in the FMPP-matched group (both P < 0.05). Subgroup analyses revealed lower interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) levels (0.90 vs. 2.10 pg/mL) and higher bronchoalveolar lavage fluid NEU% (72.0% vs. 66.0%) in FMPP-matched patients (both P < 0.05). Therapeutically, the FMPP-matched group demonstrated significantly higher utilization rates of second-line antimycoplasma agents (38.7% vs. 18.1%), β-lactam antibiotics (62.9% vs. 25.9%), and corticosteroids (45.2% vs. 20.7%) (all P < 0.05), along with significantly prolonged hospitalization durations (9.0 vs. 6.0 days, P < 0.001), although no mortality cases were observed in either group.

Discussion

MPP is a prevalent form of community-acquired pneumonia in children. In recent years, MP has shown widespread prevalence both domestically and internationally, accompanied by an increasing incidence of severe and critical cases. Among these, FMPP presents with an acute onset and poses a life-threatening risk, necessitating urgent attention. This study retrospectively analysed the clinical data of FMPP patients admitted to our hospital in 2023 to investigate their risk factors and clinical characteristics.

Previous research on FMPP in children has consisted primarily of case reports, with its risk factors not yet clearly defined. This study identified independent risk and favorable factors for respiratory failure among MPP patients. The results indicated that wheezing, high MP load, and pulmonary consolidation were independent risk factors for FMPP, while coinfection with EBV and a duration of β-lactam treatment ≥ 3 days may be associated with a reduced risk of FMPP.

Notably, these three factors have been corroborated by previous clinical studies as being associated with more severe manifestations of MPP in pediatric populations. Pathophysiologically, wheezing is closely related to airway hyperresponsiveness and bronchospasm induced by MP infection [11, 12]. A high MP load may trigger a more intense inflammatory response, including excessive activation of neutrophils, macrophages, and T cells, leading to the release of large amounts of proinflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ), resulting in extensive lung tissue damage [13]. Pulmonary consolidation can further exacerbate respiratory failure through impaired gas exchange, reduced lung compliance, and inflammatory spread. These risk factors may synergistically drive disease progression toward FMPP.

Therefore, in clinical practice, children with wheezing, high MP loads, or pulmonary consolidation should be closely monitored for the potential development of respiratory failure, and respiratory function should be assessed early. For patients with a high M. pneumoniae load, the antimycoplasma treatment regimen should be optimized, and second-line drugs (such as doxycycline or levofloxacin) should be considered if necessary to accelerate pathogen clearance. In cases involving children with wheezing or significant pulmonary consolidation, the administration of glucocorticoids or other anti-inflammatory medications may be warranted to mitigate pulmonary inflammation and enhance gas exchange.

Accumulating evidence suggests that the pathogenesis of FMPP is predominantly immune-mediated rather than the result of direct microbial cytotoxicity. Although MP has limited intrinsic virulence, an excessive host immune response triggered by persistent antigenic stimulation plays a central role in disease progression [4, 5]. Excessive neutrophil activation and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation contribute to lung injury in severe MPP [14, 15]. Consistent with these findings, our study observed significantly elevated peripheral and bronchoalveolar neutrophil proportions in children with FMPP. Imbalances in proinflammatory and regulatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α, have been associated with disease severity and extrapulmonary involvement [16]. Meanwhile, T-cell activation and cell-mediated inflammatory damage are key components contributing to the deterioration of MPP [17]. Notably, excessive systemic inflammatory responses may trigger coagulation abnormalities and multiorgan involvement [18]. Previous studies have indicated that diminished or impaired immune function may alleviate lung lesions in individuals infected with MP, including both animal models and human patients [4, 19], which indirectly supports this perspective.

Currently, the specific impact of EBV infection on MPP patients remains unclear; however, it is hypothesized that it is inversely associated with the development of FMPP may be mediated through the modulation of immune function and the induction of a transient state of immunosuppression. Research has shown that during the early stages of acute EBV infection, there is observable clonal expansion of CD8+ T lymphocytes along with a decrease in the CD4+/CD8+ ratio [20–22], accompanied by impaired B-cell functionality and suppressed antibody production [23, 24]. These immunological alterations may partially mitigate the excessive inflammatory response triggered by MP infection. In this study, we observed that the level of IFN-γ in the FMPP group was significantly lower than that in the NFMPP group. Given that IFN-γ serves as a crucial cytokine in Th1-type immune responses, its diminished concentration may reflect an altered status of immune responsiveness in these patients. Furthermore, among the 18 children diagnosed with FMPP who underwent interferon testing for tuberculosis as part of this study, 16 tested negative, whereas two yielded indeterminate results; this finding also suggests potential inhibition of cellular immune function among these children [4]. Additionally, abnormal variations observed in peripheral blood NEU% further imply an imbalance within subsets of immune cells.

However, there is ongoing debate regarding the influence of EBV infection on pediatric patients suffering from MPP. Some studies have reported that coinfection with EBV can lead to prolonged fever duration and increased incidence rates for lung consolidation as well as refractory MPP (RMPP) among affected children [24]. This contradictory finding may be attributed to various factors, including different stages of EBV infection, variations in the viral load, and individual host immune statuses. Future research should aim to elucidate further dynamic changes associated with EBV infections throughout MPP progression and more comprehensively explore their interaction mechanisms with host immune responses. Interestingly, we observed a lower rate of EBV coinfection in FMPP cases, whereas no significant association was found with bacterial coinfection, including pneumococcus. The EBV result may reflect interactions within the respiratory microbiome or host immune modulation. The lack of bacterial coinfection could be influenced by prior β-lactam exposure, which varied in timing relative to illness onset and hospitalization. Additionally, pneumococcal vaccination status was not available, which may have affected susceptibility to bacterial coinfection.

Although the misuse of early β-lactam antibiotics may lead to a delay in the application of antimycoplasma drugs and previous studies have identified this delay as a significant risk factor for FMPP [4, 5, 25], our data indicate that the time from onset to first antimycoplasma drug use is not significantly associated with the occurrence of respiratory failure. In contrast, our study revealed that early short-term use of β-lactam antibiotics can reduce the risk of FMPP, suggesting that these antibiotics may influence disease progression through alternative mechanisms.

The inversely associated effect of β-lactam antibiotics may reflect their regulatory impact on disturbances in the respiratory tract microbiota. Previous research has demonstrated that MP infection can result in airway immune damage or alterations in respiratory tract microbiota composition, thereby increasing susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections [26]. Currently, multiple studies have confirmed that coinfection with bacteria further elevates the risk of severe MPP in children [27, 28]. β-Lactam antibiotics exhibit substantial bactericidal activity against common secondary bacterial pathogens following MP infection (such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae) [29]. In addition, while β-lactam antibiotics do not directly act against MP, antibiotic-mediated perturbations of the host microbiota can alter immune regulation and inflammatory responses. Antibiotic administration has been shown to profoundly affect microbial community composition and diversity, which in turn can influence host immune function through modulation of immune cell activation and cytokine profiles [30]. A double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial published in The Lancet revealed that patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) who were not admitted to intensive care units required only a three-day short course of β-lactam drugs to effectively control symptoms [31], further supporting the rationale behind using β-lactam antibiotics for ≥ 3 days, as observed in this study. Thus, early administration of these antibiotics may confer a benefit in clinical management.

Although bacterial culture positivity rates did not significantly differ between groups (13.6% vs. 11.1%, P = 0.568), this result may have been affected by sampling timing, technical limitations, or sampling site restrictions, leading to potential underestimation of bacterial colonization rates. Future studies should employ more sensitive detection methods and standardized sampling protocols to validate these findings. Additionally, further clinical research is needed to optimize the selection, timing, and duration of β-lactam antibiotic use.

A previous study indicated that FMPP is more prevalent among young, healthy adults, particularly men and smokers [4]. However, in this investigation, we did not observe any correlation between age or sex and the occurrence of FMPP in children. The findings revealed that the vast majority of children with FMPP had no underlying health conditions. The primary clinical manifestations include cough, high fever, dyspnea, and diffuse pulmonary inflammatory infiltration, which aligns with earlier reports [25]. Research evidence suggests that patients with FMPP exhibit significant abnormalities related to pulmonary lesions, systemic inflammatory responses, organ function impairment, and immune regulation [4–6], further corroborating this perspective.

This study revealed that multiorgan damage in children with FMPP affects the liver as well as the coagulation and nervous systems; notably, liver dysfunction is particularly pronounced. In the FMPP group, the levels of LDH and hepatic enzymes were significantly elevated, whereas the levels of TP and ALB were decreased. These results are consistent with those reported by Miyashita et al. [32]. The mechanism underlying liver dysfunction may not be attributed to direct invasion of MP into hepatic tissue but may be closely associated with the body’s immune response. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) represent a class of pattern recognition receptors that are located on the cell surface or within cells and serve as crucial components of the innate immune system. T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain (TIM) proteins constitute another class of immune regulatory molecules expressed on the surface of T cells and play essential roles in mediating T-cell responses. They are involved in regulating T-cell activation, differentiation, and tolerance. Fan Q et al. reported that the levels of TLR2 mRNA and TIM3 protein mRNA were significantly elevated in children with MPP-related hepatitis, which was correlated with ALT levels. These findings suggest that both innate and adaptive immune mechanisms may play significant roles in liver injury associated with MP infection [33].

This study also revealed that the presence of fine crackles in the lungs, peripheral blood NEU%, and FIB levels were independently associated with FMPP in pediatric patients. These findings suggest that airway obstruction, neutrophilic inflammation, and coagulation dysfunction may directly contribute to the pathophysiological processes underlying respiratory failure. Neutrophils can directly eliminate microorganisms through mechanisms such as phagocytosis, degranulation, and the release of antimicrobial peptides. Additionally, they contribute to pathogen clearance by releasing neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [34, 35]. NETs are extracellular reticular structures composed of depolymerized chromatin that serves as a scaffold for various cytoplasmic and granular proteins. These structures have the capacity to capture, neutralize, and kill bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites while potentially preventing the dissemination of pathogens [36]. However, excessive release of NETs may lead to damage to alveolar epithelial cells and endothelial cells, exacerbating lung injury and possibly resulting in vascular occlusion and immune dysregulation [37]. Although the role of neutrophil inflammation in FMPP has been acknowledged to some extent, numerous aspects remain unclear. Future research should aim to elucidate the specific molecular signaling pathways involved in neutrophil inflammation during FMPP pathogenesis.

Notably, the fibrinogen level in the FMPP group (3.61 g/L vs. 4.08 g/L, P = 0.006) was significantly lower than that in the NFMPP group. This discrepancy may be attributed to two primary factors. First, children with FMPP may experience more severe liver function impairment. The liver serves as the principal site for fibrinogen synthesis; thus, when hepatic function is compromised, its synthetic capacity diminishes, resulting in a reduced plasma concentration of fibrinogen. Second, coagulation dysfunction stemming from an inflammatory response could also play a role. Inflammation activates the coagulation cascade, leading to increased consumption of fibrinogen and consequently lowering its levels. Excessive consumption of fibrinogen can serve as an early indicator of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), whereas diminished levels may exacerbate alveolar fibrin deposition and hinder gas exchange [38]. Previous studies have demonstrated a close relationship between D-dimer levels and the severity of lung inflammation following MP infection [39]. As a soluble product derived from fibrin degradation, D-dimer is associated with thrombotic disorders. A review by Koletsky and Weinstein on 11 fatal cases of MPP identified vascular thrombosis in five instances [40], further substantiating the presence of coagulation dysfunction among children with FMPP. However, within this study cohort, only 30.3% (20/66) of the children in the FMPP group presented elevated D-dimer levels; notably, no significant correlation was found between these elevations and respiratory failure occurrence, with thrombosis reported infrequently (in only two cases). Therefore, further investigations into the impact of MP on coagulation function and the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain essential.

In terms of treatment, the FMPP group had a significantly greater rate of switching to second-line antimycoplasma drugs (doxycycline or levofloxacin) than did the NFMPP group (38.7% vs. 18.1%, P = 0.003), indicating a poorer response to first-line therapy. Additionally, β-lactam antibiotic and glucocorticoid use were significantly greater in the FMPP group, likely due to an increased risk of secondary bacterial infection and more intense inflammatory responses. All patients improved and were discharged, with no fatalities, suggesting an overall favorable prognosis for FMPP patients. Future studies should focus on optimizing antibiotic regimens, exploring alternative treatments for nonresponsive patients, and determining the optimal timing, dosage, and duration of glucocorticoid therapy to further improve outcomes and reduce hospitalization time.

Although this study provides valuable insights into the early identification and individualized treatment of FMPP, several limitations should be noted: (1) the single-center retrospective design may introduce selection bias, and despite PSM adjustment, unmeasured confounders may still affect the results; (2) the small sample size and EPV value of 9.43 (slightly below the recommended threshold of 10) may increase the risk of overfitting, although the findings are consistent with clinical practice and prior research, supporting their reliability; (3) the lack of MP resistance gene testing in some patients limits the exploration of macrolide-resistant MP (MRMP) effects; and (4) our study assessed outcomes only during hospitalization, without post-discharge follow-up. As a result, 30-day mortality, readmission, or longer-term complications could not be evaluated; the focus on risk factors and clinical characteristics left treatment and prognosis understudied. Future research should expand sample sizes, improve detection methods, and increase treatment follow-up to optimize clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

This study identified wheezing, high MP load, and pulmonary consolidation as independent risk factors for respiratory failure-type FMPP in children, whereas EBV coinfection and short-term β-lactam antibiotic use were found to reduce the risk of FMPP. These findings provide important evidence for early risk identification of FMPP. FMPP patients exhibit significant abnormalities in pulmonary lesions, systemic inflammatory responses, organ dysfunction, and immune regulation, along with a poor response to first-line antimycoplasma therapy. However, the overall prognosis was favorable. Clinicians should closely monitor high-risk children presenting with wheezing, high MP loads, or pulmonary consolidation, implement early interventions, and optimize management to improve outcomes and reduce hospitalization duration.
