Correlation analysis of breast cancer molecular characteristics and epithelial–mesenchymal transition in circulating tumor cells: Based on clinical cases
Authors: Jiayu Guan, Fuping Li, Xiaoyu Pu, Wenbin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer is a widely occurring malignancy, distinguished by intricate modifications in cellular signaling pathways, with epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) recognized as a pivotal mechanism that propels its pathogenesis. EMT significantly facilitates the invasion and metastasis of tumor cells and profoundly affects patient prognosis. This research aims to clarify the relationship between 3 distinct types of circulating tumor cells (CTCs)—epithelial, hybrid, and mesenchymal—their EMT status, and the associated clinical and pathological features in breast cancer patients. A cohort of 48 breast cancer patients was enrolled, utilizing CanPatrol technology to isolate and classify CTCs from peripheral blood samples. Comprehensive multiplex mRNA in situ detection was employed, utilizing various molecular markers for accurate identification. Statistical analyses, including Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, and Fisher exact test, were conducted to evaluate the correlations between CTC quantities, EMT ratios, and clinical parameters. Our results demonstrated significant differences in mesenchymal-CTC counts across gender and age demographics (P = .008, P = .004), alongside notable discrepancies in EMT ratios correlated with Ki-67 expression levels and breast cancer subtypes (P = .035, P = .048). The EMT ratio was significantly higher in triple-negative breast cancer compared to non-triple-negative variants, with a positive association established between Ki-67 levels and EMT ratios. In conclusion, the EMT ratio of CTCs emerges as a strong predictor of Ki-67 levels and serves as a potential biomarker for triple-negative breast cancer, necessitating further investigation in subsequent studies to enhance prognostic evaluations and therapeutic approaches.

1. Introduction

Breast cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignant neoplasms affecting women globally,[1,2] characterized by considerable heterogeneity that leads to recurrence and metastasis, which are primary contributors to mortality in affected individuals.[3] The “immune-mediated” theory of tumor metastasis highlights the dualistic nature of the immune system, which can either facilitate or hinder metastatic processes, potentially elucidating the ineffectiveness of certain immunotherapies.[4] The “pre-metastatic niche” theory posits that tumor cells can modify the microenvironment of remote organs through the secretion of various factors, thereby facilitating subsequent metastatic spread.[5] Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) have emerged as a promising biomarker, as they are thought to disseminate from breast tumors into the bloodstream, subsequently traveling to distant sites where they can establish secondary growths, thus contributing to metastasis. The process of metastasis involves the separation of CTCs from the original tumor, wherein epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) plays a pivotal role.[6,7] EMT is a biological process that converts epithelial cells into a mesenchymal phenotype, enhancing their migratory, invasive, and stem-like abilities.[8–10] Grasping the EMT status of CTCs is vital for understanding the metastatic behavior of breast cancer. Research has demonstrated that CTCs derived from various molecular subtypes of breast cancer display unique EMT phenotypes, notably, CTCs from triple-negative breast cancer exhibit a pronounced shift toward a mesenchymal characteristic.[11–14] This observation provides new insights into the mechanisms underlying breast cancer dissemination. Additionally, findings suggest that the mixed phenotype observed in the HER2-positive subtype may correlate with resistance to targeted therapies. Integrating conventional chemotherapy with agents that target hybrid-CTCs (H-CTCs) and mesenchymal-CTCs (M-CTCs) could impede their migration and invasion.[15,16] Identifying hybrid or M-CTCs may allow for the modification of treatment strategies, such as the combination of EMT pathway inhibitors with HER2-targeted therapies to improve therapeutic outcomes.

The classification of CTCs in breast cancer patients, alongside the EMT status of these cells, the molecular subtypes of breast cancer, and associated pathological characteristics, underscores the intricate biological complexities inherent in breast cancer. This investigation utilized the CanPatrol platform to conduct an in-depth analysis and classification of CTCs, systematically exploring the relationship between the counts and positivity rates of the 3 CTC types with the clinical features of breast cancer patients, as well as the EMT proportions of CTCs in the blood of individuals with various molecular subtypes of breast cancer. Our objective was to enhance the understanding of breast cancer biology and to lay a robust groundwork for subsequent research into the clinical implications of EMT markers.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patient selection and sample collection

A cohort of 48 breast cancer patients, whose diagnoses were confirmed through pathological evaluation at Shenzhen People’s Hospital between September 2022 and January 2024, was selected (refer to Table 1). CTC detection was conducted as detailed in Table 1. Clinical information such as age, sex, tumor dimensions, tumor stage, the extent of lymph node metastases, and pathological characteristics were systematically gathered for statistical assessment. In this patient population, there was 1 male and 47 females, with ages spanning from 34 to 72 years, yielding a median age of 47 years. Pathological classifications included 2 cases of ductal carcinoma in situ, 34 cases of invasive ductal carcinoma, 10 cases of invasive lobular carcinoma, and 2 cases of invasive carcinoma with special type. Staging revealed 2 cases at stage 0, 23 at stage I, 11 at stage II, 11 at stage III, and 1 at stage IV. The inclusion criteria mandated a breast cancer diagnosis validated by pathology, while exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) patients exhibiting severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; (2) individuals with other malignancies diagnosed within the preceding 5 years; (3) those with a history of hematological disorders; and (4) patients with a prior history of other malignant conditions. Ethical clearance for conducting peripheral blood studies was secured from the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen People’s Hospital (Shenzhen, China; ethics approval no: LL-KY-2024078-01). Prior to enrollment, all participants provided written informed consent, ensuring their confidentiality and anonymity throughout the research process. 2.2. CTC detection

The CanPatrol CTC detection process (Surexam) is delineated as follows (see Fig. 1): Isolation involved discarding the first 2 mL of peripheral blood, followed by the collection of 5 mL of blood into an EDTA tube (Becton Dickinson, Shanghai, China). The analysis was conducted using the CanPatrol system (SurExam Biotech, Guangzhou, China) within 4 hours post-collection. Following the lysis of red blood cells, CTCs were enriched using size-based nanotechnology. Typing and identification were performed via a novel multiplex mRNA in situ analysis, which allows for the localization of specific genes within CTCs. This method achieves high-sensitivity detection of single-copy mRNA through simultaneous hybridization with RNA probes targeting specific genes, coupled with fluorescence signal amplification. By analyzing the fluorescence signals, CTCs are classified into 3 different phenotypes: epithelial-CTCs (E-CTCs), H-CTCs, and M-CTCs, as shown in Figure 2.

2.3. Classification and grouping

Estrogen receptor positivity was defined as having at least 1% of tumor cells expressing the receptor (estrogen receptor [ER] positive ≥ 1%), while progesterone receptor positivity required a similar threshold (PR positive ≥ 1%). A HER2 score of 3+ was classified as positive, whereas a score of 1+ was deemed negative; cases presenting with a HER2 score of 2+ necessitated confirmation via FISH analysis. The Ki-67 labeling index indicated high expression at levels of 30% or above (Ki-67 ≥ 30%) and low expression below this threshold (Ki-67 < 30%). The presence of axillary lymph node metastasis was determined by the involvement of 1 or more lymph nodes, categorized as a positive finding. The EMT ratio was calculated using the formula: (H-CTCs + M-CTCs)/total CTCs. The median EMT ratio was 62%, and the EMT ratio status was defined as positive (>62%) or negative (≤62%).

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables were summarized as median values with corresponding ranges, and for data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed to assess differences between the 2 groups. Categorical variables were represented through percentages and frequencies, with Fisher exact test facilitating comparative analyses. A P-value of <.05 indicates that the findings are deemed statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. CTC types and clinical characteristics

The association among 3 distinct types of CTC counts and the clinical characteristics of breast cancer patients is detailed in Tables 2–4. No statistically significant differences were observed regarding age, gender, tumor size, tumor staging, or axillary lymph node metastasis in relation to E-CTC and H-CTC counts (refer to Tables 2 and 3). However, M-CTC counts exhibited significant variations based on gender and age, with P-values of .008 and .004, respectively (see Table 4). This indicates that M-CTC expression levels are significantly influenced by both gender and age. Notably, there was still a statistical difference in the positivity rate of M-CTC with age (P = .020), and no significant correlation was found between M-CTC counts and tumor size, tumor staging, and axillary lymph node metastasis (Table 4).

4. Discussion

This investigation examined the clinical implications of CTCs and the process of EMT in the peripheral blood of individuals diagnosed with breast cancer, utilizing CanPatrol detection technology. Our primary aim was to assess the relationship between varying types of CTCs and the clinical as well as pathological characteristics of breast cancer. The findings indicated a significant association between M-CTCs and clinical parameters such as sex and age demographics. Specifically, it was observed that male patients exhibited a greater abundance of stromal CTCs compared to their female counterparts, while younger patients presented with a higher incidence of stromal CTCs relative to older patients. This trend may correlate with the increased aggressiveness frequently observed in male and younger breast cancer patients. Such associations suggest that M-CTCs may serve as valuable biomarkers for evaluating the severity of the disease. Additionally, the study revealed that elevated levels of Ki-67 are linked to an increased EMT ratio, with TNBC displaying a higher EMT ratio compared to non-TNBC.

EMT represents a pivotal cellular mechanism wherein epithelial cells undergo a loss of polarity and intercellular connections, thereby transforming into migratory mesenchymal-like cells. In our investigation, we delineated 3 distinct phenotypes of CTCs within the context of breast cancer: the epithelial phenotype (E-CTCs), the mixed epithelial/mesenchymal phenotype (H-CTCs), and the mesenchymal phenotype (M-CTCs). Existing literature indicates that CTCs can exhibit epithelial, mesenchymal, and hybrid characteristics, with M-CTCs being associated with a poorer prognosis.[17,18] This heterogeneity in phenotypes underscores the intricate nature of breast cancer and implies that CTCs may play varied roles in its progression. The elevated EMT ratio observed in TNBC can be partially attributed to the absence of ER, PR, Her-2 expression, which renders TNBC cells resistant to conventional endocrine therapies. Consequently, tumor cells may evolve to enhance their migratory abilities in response to challenging environmental conditions. Moreover, specific signaling pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β pathways, have been implicated in promoting the EMT process and increasing the invasiveness of tumor cells in the context of TNBC.[19,20]

This investigation is subject to several constraints. Firstly, the limited sample size and the design centered on a single location may restrict the extent to which the findings can be considered representative and applicable to broader populations. Secondly, while a variety of technologies for CTC detection exist, each method possesses its own strengths and weaknesses, coupled with an absence of standardized protocols.[21,22] Presently, there is no global agreement on the selection of EMT markers, and variations in methodologies across studies may compromise the comparability of the outcomes. In this research, the CanPatrol CTC detection technology was employed to differentiate between epithelial, mesenchymal, and H-CTCs. Additionally, CTCs were evaluated at fixed time intervals; although such static evaluations yield important insights for clinical application, they also present notable limitations.

The methodologies for detecting CTCs are undergoing continual advancement, with techniques such as CanPatrol, CellSearch, and CytoSorter each presenting unique strengths and limitations. Among these, the CanPatrol method has garnered significant attention within clinical environments due to its remarkable sensitivity and specificity. Research indicates that this technique effectively captures various CTC subtypes and exhibits robust detection capabilities across a range of cancer types. For instance, in patients diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer, the CanPatrol technology achieved a remarkable circulating tumor cell detection rate of 97.5%.[23] Nonetheless, its efficacy in capturing tumor cells characterized by low EpCAM expression is comparatively lower than that of other CTC detection systems, such as the CellSearch platform, which could restrict its applicability in specific patient populations.[24] Furthermore, notable discrepancies exist among different CTC detection methodologies. For example, i-FISH technology has reported a CTC detection rate of up to 80% in samples from oral squamous cell carcinoma, whereas the microfiltration technique only attains a detection rate of 40%.[25] This underscores the importance of selecting the appropriate CTC capture antibodies and methodologies to optimize detection efficiency. The CanPatrol method has consistently demonstrated its potential as a viable option through various studies. A comprehensive comparison between CanPatrol technology and the CellSearch system is detailed in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q726.

In clinical settings, the detection of CTCs is generally conducted at predetermined intervals, which may fail to reflect the dynamic alterations occurring in tumor biology over time. A meta-analysis focusing on patients with ovarian cancer revealed that the presence of positive CTCs correlated with decreased overall survival and progression-free survival; however, these conclusions were derived from static data assessments.[26] Additionally, other research has demonstrated that both the quantity and the characteristics of CTCs can fluctuate as tumors progress through various stages, and understanding these changes is essential for accurately predicting patient responses to therapeutic interventions.[27] Relying on static evaluations of CTCs could result in erroneous interpretations concerning disease advancement and the effectiveness of treatments. For instance, several studies have indicated that the detection of CTCs following chemotherapy is linked to accelerated disease progression, and insights of this nature can only be garnered through continuous, dynamic monitoring.[28]

Currently, effectively implementing this grading system in clinical settings faces challenges, including technical standardization, clinical applicability, and translating laboratory findings into clinical practice. The integration of CTC EMT grading into clinical settings presents various difficulties. Firstly, the protocols for identifying CTC subtypes and their associated biological characteristics remain unstandardized, resulting in notable variances among laboratories that could compromise the consistency and reproducibility of findings.[24] Secondly, the process of translating laboratory results into clinical settings necessitates further investigation, particularly concerning the utilization of EMT grading across diverse breast cancer subtypes and its specific implications for treatment decisions and prognostic outcomes.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, additional investigations into CTCs, especially M-CTCs and their EMT ratios, alongside their clinical and pathological features in breast cancer, may facilitate the development of more targeted and effective personalized treatment strategies for patients with breast cancer.[29,30] This, in turn, has the potential to enhance overall therapeutic results.
