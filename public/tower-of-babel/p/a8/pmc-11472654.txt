Modulation of cortical representations of sensory and contextual information underlies aversive associative learning
Authors: Jiaman Dai, Qian-Quan Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYCortical neurons encode both sensory and contextual information, yet it remains unclear how experiences modulate these cortical representations. Here, we demonstrate that trace eyeblink conditioning (TEC), an aversive associative-learning paradigm linking conditioned (CS) with unconditioned stimuli (US), finely tunes cortical coding at both population and single-neuron levels. Initially, we show that the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) is necessary for TEC acquisition, as evidenced by local muscimol administration. At the population level, TEC enhances activity in a small subset (~20%) of CS- or US-responsive primary neurons (rPNs) while diminishing activity in non-rPNs, including locomotion-tuned or unresponsive PNs. Crucially, TEC learning modulates the encoding of sensory versus contextual information in single rPNs: CS-responsive neurons become less responsive, while US-responsive neurons gain responses to CS. Moreover, we find that the cholinergic pathway, via nicotinic receptors, underlies TEC-induced modulations. These findings suggest that experiences dynamically tune cortical representations through cholinergic pathways.

INTRODUCTION

Early stages of sensory processing in the neocortex involve the integration of information. Traditionally, the sensory neocortex was viewed as extracting physical stimulus features, with higher cortical areas utilizing these signals to generate decision variables, implying hierarchical processing across brain regions.1,2 However, recent research indicates that the superficial layers of the sensory cortex also encode contextual signals, including self-motion, expectation, and attention.3–9 During the initial phases of learning, both sensory and non-sensory information converge in the primary cortex, coinciding with changes in behavior and brain states.3–5,10–17 However, how learning modulates the representation of sensory versus contextual information at the single-neuronal and population levels in the sensory cortex remains unclear.

In reward-based perceptual learning, the initially inactive neurons become responsive to sensory signals, while those carrying sensory information regardless of behavioral outcomes largely remain unchanged.3–5 Conversely, in associative fear-learning tasks, fewer neurons respond to stimulation post-learning, but those that do exhibit enhanced responses.18 Learning-dependent enhancement of top-down contextual inputs appears crucial for associative memory, but not for learning acquisition.11,13 These diverse results across learning paradigms may stem from differences in motivational factors19 and attentional subtypes.20,21 Nevertheless, understanding how learning shapes the balance between neural representations of sensory and contextual information requires further investigation.

Here, employing the trace eyeblink conditioning (TEC) behavioral learning paradigm,22–24 we aimed to elucidate how aversive associative learning modulates cortical neuronal coding of sensory versus contextual information at both population and single-neuronal levels. We conducted concurrent monitoring of animal behavior and neural activities of layer 2/3 primary somatosensory cortex (S1) pyramidal neurons (PNs) in Ai96/CamKII-Cre mice using a two-photon imaging system throughout the TEC learning process. Our findings illuminate the principles underlying TEC-mediated modulation of sensory neuronal coding.

RESULTS

S1 is necessary for TEC acquisition

To investigate how PNs encode the TEC learning, we first trained the awake, head-fixed mice on a running wheel. In our TEC paradigms, whisker deflection (C2 whisker) served as the conditioned stimulus (CS; 60 Hz, 250 ms) and the air puff to the cornea served as the unconditioned stimulus (US; 20 psi, 50 ms), delivered 250 ms after the CS (Figure 1A). We used three different stimulation patterns in one session to isolate learning-induced changes: CS paired with US (16/20 trials, CS-US), CS alone (2/20 trials, CS-only), and US alone (2/20 trials, US-only) (Figure 1A). Meanwhile, we imaged the calcium transients (via GCaMP6s) of PNs through a 3-mm cranial window using a two-photon microscope within the region of interest (ROI) identified with whisker stimulation under intrinsic imaging (Figures 1B and S1A). During imaging, the mice were awake and free to walk on a three-dimensional-printed running wheel.

After repeated pairing of the CS-US over multiple sessions (6.45 ± 0.65 sessions), mice showed a gradual improvement in behavior performance. The contractions of the orbicularis oculi muscle, measured by their electromyography (EMG) amplitude (Figure 1C), increased during a time window from CS onset to US onset (CS-window). The increase was particularly notable in the late part of EMG (late conditioned response [LCR]) but not in the early part of EMG (early conditioned response [ECR]) within the CS-window (Figures 1D and 1E).

In addition, the probability of eyeblink CRs (CR %) progressively increased from 11% ± 5% to 86% ± 4% (Figure 1F), which is consistent with previous reports.22–24 The unconditioned response (UR) following the delivery of air puff (US-window, duration: 0.5 s) remained unchanged after training (Figures S1B and S1C).

To directly test the role of S1 in TEC learning, we inhibited neuronal activity during TEC by local infusion of the GABAA-receptor agonist muscimol into S1. For the treatment group, muscimol was administered from days 1 to 3 (Figures 1G–1I). For the control group, saline was administered from days 1 to 3 (Figures 1G–1I). We found that local muscimol administration led to a reduction in both ECR and LCR compared to the saline group (Figures 1G–1I). Although the effects of muscimol may extend slightly outside of S1 according to the previous reports,13,22 local injection of muscimol effectively inactivated S1. Our results suggest that activity in the barrel cortex is required for TEC acquisition.

Modulation of population neuronal representations by TEC learning

We next examined PN activities at depths from 100 to 250 μm below the pial surface in layer 2/3 of the S1 at three different training stages: (1) habituation stage, where the animals were exposed only to unreinforced CS-only before the TEC training; (2) naive stage, which was the first reinforced session; and (3) expert stage, which was the last session. The average population activities of PNs were modulated by the TEC training (Figures 2A–2C). Surprisingly, the GCaMP6 signals in the CS-window (from CS onset to US onset, CR) increased from the habituation stage to the naive stage (0.36 ± 0.03 vs. 0.47 ± 0.04 Δf/f, p = 6.16 × 10 9; Wilcoxon test), but decreased from the naive stage to the expert stage (0.47 ± 0.04 vs. 0.40 ± 0.04 Δf/f, p = 1.43 × 10−6; Wilcoxon test). Meanwhile, the calcium activities in the US-window (1 s after US onset, UR) decreased from the naive to the expert stage (1.0 ± 0.08 vs. 0.68 ± 0.07 Δf/f, p = 3.70 × 10−14; Wilcoxon test). Additionally, the correlation between calcium signal and locomotion or EMG displayed similar profiles: increased from the habituation stage to the naive stage (locomotion: 0.17 ± 0.009 vs. 0.19 ± 0.007, p = 0.0020; EMG: 0.068 ± 0.004 vs. 0.082 ± 0.003, p = 3.96 × 10−8), but decreased from the naive stage to the expert stage (locomotion: 0.19 ± 0.007 vs. 0.14 ± 0.006, p = 2.15 × 10−6; EMG: 0.082 ± 0.003 vs. 0.038 ± 0.003, p = 4.16 × 10−32; Figures 2D–2G). These changes described above were found to be age and sex independent (Figures S2 and S3).

In addition to analyzing the population activity of PNs, we classified the cells into three subtypes according to their responsiveness to CS, US, or locomotion (see method details and Figure S4 for details; Figure 1H): responsive neurons (type 1, rPNs), locomotion-related neurons (type 2), and unresponsive neurons (type 3). While the percentage of type 1 neurons was lower than 20%, that of type 3 was nearly 50%. This classification revealed that the activities of different cell subtypes changed differently across the learning stages; the percentage of type 1 neurons increased first from habituation to naive, then decreased from naive to expert, while the percentage of type 3 neurons displayed opposite changes to type 1 neurons. There were no significant changes in the composition of type 2 neurons (Figure 2I).

Furthermore, both CR and UR in type 1 neurons increased gradually across the learning stages, while the CR in type 3 neurons increased first from habituation to naive and then both CR and UR decreased dramatically from naive to expert. For type 2 cells, UR decreased dramatically from naive to expert, and CR increased only from habituation to naive (Figures 2J and 2K). Meanwhile, the mean activity of type 1 cells was much higher than that of the other two subtypes, especially type 3 (Figure S5). Lastly, TEC learning-induced changes in the three types of neurons were irrelevant to how the CS and US were delivered, whether as pairs or individually (Figure S6).These results demonstrate that the response profiles of PNs are broadly different between three subsets based on their response properties and are heterogeneously modulated across learning stages. Based on these results, we conclude that learning-induced changes observed at the population level were caused by the enhancement of the responses of small population of rPNs (less than 20%) and the diminishment of the responses of non-rPNs, which include locomotion-related and unresponsive PNs, together account for over 80% of total PN populations.

Behavioral patterns as a signature of TEC learning-induced behavior changes

Locomotion modulates the sensory processing.12,14–17,25 We next investigated the characteristics of locomotion at different stages of TEC training. Interestingly, we found that mice showed an arresting response both after CS and US, especially at the expert stage (Figure 3A). We then categorized the behavioral patterns into three groups: arresting response in the CS-window, arresting response in the US-window, and locomotion in the US-window (indicated by red arrows in Figure 3A). As the learning progressed, the arresting response in the CS-window increased, while the arresting response in the US-window decreased. However, the locomotion in the US-window did not show significant changes (Figure 3B). Thus, these three movement patterns serve as behavioral signatures of different stages of TEC learning.

The locomotion-related neurons were further analyzed based on whether they overlapped with the responsive neurons (overlapped-cells) or not (only-cells). The percentage of overlapped-cells was almost 20%, while the percentage of only-cells was nearly 80% at the three learning stages (Figure 3C). The CR of only-cells increased dramatically from the habituation stage to the naive stage, while the UR of only-cells decreased significantly from the naive to the expert stage (Figures 3D and 3E). However, no significant differences of CR in only-cells were observed between the naive and the expert groups. Meanwhile, there were no significant differences of overlapped-cells in either CR or UR across all learning stages (Figures 3F and 3G). Our results thus suggest that locomotion-tuned neurons, regardless of whether or not they are responsive neurons, do not contribute to the TEC learning.

Modulation of contextual vs. sensory representations in single PNs during TEC learning

Among the type 1 group (rPNs), responses of individual neurons were heterogeneous; these neurons were responsive to either CS or US (e.g., cell 1 and 2 in Figures 1B and S1A), respectively. CS-responsive neurons (CR-neurons) and US-responsive neurons (UR-neurons) presumably encode sensory and putative contextual signal, respectively. We next determined specific response properties of individual neurons by longitudinally tracking them across training stages. To identify the same cells across stages, we calculated the spatial coordinates of each cell located in the merged vascular maps and averaged fluorescence images across sessions (Figure 4A; see also method details). The responsive cells were first detected with positive CS-evoked responses (i.e., CR-neurons) at the habituation stage. We then followed the same CR-neurons at the naive and expert stages. The remaining responsive cells were primarily responsive to the US and thus were defined as UR-neurons.

The trial-by-trial responses of representative CR-neurons and UR-neurons across training stages are shown in Figure 4B. We further characterized the learning-induced dynamics of individual CR- and UR-neurons. First, a decreased CR in CR-neurons and an increased CR in UR-neurons occurred much earlier (from trial, the 14th at naive stage) than did the behavioral changes (EMG and locomotion), supporting the notion that the activities of these neurons likely precede behavior learning.26

Second, UR-neurons were initially unresponsive to CS in the habituation sessions and early trials of the naive stage, but their CR increased in later trials of the naive stage, especially during the expert sessions (Figures 4B and 4C). These results suggest that UR-neurons, which initially encoded only contextual information before TEC, became responsive to both CS and US after TEC training. In contrast, CR-neurons exhibited the opposite response profiles; they responded to CS during the habituation session and early trials of the naive stage, but their CR decreased in the later trials of the naive stage (Figures 4B and 4C). These findings suggest that TEC learning differentially modulates sensory versus contextual-tuned rPNs. While the responses of sensory-tuned neurons (i.e., CR-neurons) decreased, those of contextual-tuned neurons (i.e., UR-neurons) expanded to encode both sensory and contextual information.

Third, the CR of CR-neurons in CS-US trials was almost the same as that in CS-only trials at both naive and expert stages (Figures 4C and S7). In contrast, the activity of UR-neurons in the CS-US trials was the summation of that in the CS-only and US-only trials at both naive and expert stages. These results supporting the idea that that CR- and UR-neurons differentially encode sensory and contextual information and that TEC learning transforms these representations.

Additionally, as the animal gained behavioral learning, indicated by increased EMG activity in the CS-window, CR-neurons’ activities within the CS-window (CR) dramatically and progressively decreased at the expert stage, while the UR-neurons’ CR significantly increased at both the naive and expert stages (Figures 4D and 4E). Meanwhile, the percentage of CR-neurons with significantly decreased CR from the naive to expert stage was larger than the percentage of CR-neurons with increased CR in the same period (Figure 4F; p = 7.36 × 10−9, chi-square test). In contrast, the percentage of UR-neurons whose CR increased was larger than that of UR-neurons whose CR decreased (Figure 4F; p = 7.67 × 10−16, chi-square test).

Lastly, CR-neurons’ CR in CS-only trials was larger than that in US-only trials, and CR decreased dramatically from the naive to the expert stage (Figures 4G and 4H). In contrast, UR-neurons’ CR increased significantly from the naive to expert (Figures 4G and 4H). Meanwhile, the UR of both CR- and UR-neurons in US-only trials showed no significant changes from the naive to the expert stage, although there was a decreasing tendency in CR-neurons and an increasing tendency in UR-neurons. Because there were only two trials of CS- and US-only trials in one session, we additionally analyzed the activities of CR-neurons and UR-neurons in the second session of TEC training in the same mice. We observed no significant differences between the mean activities from two trials and four trials in either the CR-neurons or the UR-neurons (Figure S8). In summary, learning caused CR-neurons to lose their ability to respond to the sensory stimulus. In contrast, UR-neurons, which were initially unresponsive to CS, gained responsiveness to CS after learning, and the activities following US in both CR- and UR-neurons showed no significant changes.

Together, our results suggest that two subtypes of rPNs with distinct response properties—CR-neurons, which encode sensory information, and UR-neurons, which encode contextual signals—are differentially modulated by TEC learning.

Cholinergic modulation of TEC-induced neuronal responses and behavioral changes

What are the mechanisms underlying the TEC-induced modulations? Previous studies have supported a role for acetylcholine (ACh) in mediating learning-related modulations of neural activity.27–29 To examine whether the cholinergic system modulates the activity of the PNs during TEC learning, we first pharmacologically manipulated cholinergic signaling while imaging the calcium transients of PNs across learning stages. The strategy for drug delivery was as follows (Figures 5A–5C): after habituation and the first training session on day 0 (session 0), the nicotinic receptor antagonist mecamylamine (MEC; 5 mg/kg) was delivered systemically once per day, 10 min before training (session 1–session 20), from day 1 to day 4. In the control group, saline was injected from day 1 to day 4 after the first session of training on day 0.

In the MEC-treated group, the LCR remained at a low amplitude from day 1 to day 4. In the control group, the LCR increased gradually with the progression of training. Meanwhile, no significant difference was observed in the ECR either across or within the two groups. To confirm the effect of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) on the LCR, we next locally delivered the MEC to the S1 region with an implanted cannula (Figure S9A). Similar to the effects of systemic administration, local delivery of MEC resulted in a suppression of the LCR. These findings demonstrate that only LCR, which correlated with the learning performance, was modulated by the nicotinic receptors acting locally in the S1 region.

We next examined the roles of muscarinic AChRs in learning by applying the muscarinic antagonist atropine (5 mg/kg) in separate groups of animals (Figure S9B). In contrast to the effects of the nicotinic receptor antagonist, atropine did not significantly impair learning performance. Additionally, optogenetic activation of cholinergic fibers (via ChAT-ChR2 mice) in S1 increased the LCR during TEC training (see method details and Figure S10). Although it has been reported that ChAT-ChR2 mice exhibit severe attention deficits and memory dysfunction,30 we did not observe such deficits. Instead, we observed an improvement in learning performance compared with the control group. These manipulations provide direct evidence that cholinergic activity in S1 is crucial for the learning performance.

Concurrent with the alteration of LCR, cholinergic signaling antagonism also impaired stereotypical behavioral patterns. This resulted in a reduced amplitude of the arresting response in the CS-window and a reduced amplitude of locomotion in the US-window (Figures 5D and 5E). However, no significant changes were found in the arresting response in the US-window.

Lastly, inactivating cholinergic signaling with nAChR antagonist MEC dramatically decreased the average activity of population PNs in both CR and UR, especially reducing the signal amplitude of UR (Figures 5F–5H). We further measured the activity of CR-neurons and UR-neurons separately (Figures 5I and 5J). After treatment with MEC, CR increased in CR-neurons and decreased in UR-neurons. Both CR-neurons and UR-neurons in MEC sessions showed reversed trends compared to their behavior from the naive to expert sessions during TEC learning. Additionally, we compared the activities of CR-neurons and UR-neurons between the saline- and MEC-treated groups in each mouse (Figure S11). We found the same opposite modulation; CR-neurons increased their response in the CS-window, while the CR of UR-neurons decreased in the MEC group compared to those in the saline treatment group. Thus, the characteristic TEC-induced opposite transition of sensory versus contextual-tuned neurons described earlier was entirely abolished by administration of MEC. These results suggest that the activity difference in CR-neurons and UR-neurons may be key to understanding TEC learning performance. To further demonstrate the TEC-induced transformation, we calculated a modulation index (see method details) that measured the absolute difference in calcium transients within the CS-window of CR-neurons and UR-neurons. Notably, the modulation index in each session correlated positively with the mean LCR EMG amplitude in each session (Figure 5K). Additionally, the mean amplitude of arresting response in the CS-window (absolute value) showed a high positive linear relationship with the mean amplitude of LCR in each session (Figure 5L). These results suggest that TEC learning differentially modulated neuronal encoding of the CS (i.e., sensory) and US (i.e., contextual) signals via cholinergic pathways.

DISCUSSION

We trained mice in a TEC task where they learned to close their eyelids in anticipation of an air puff. We quantified task performance using orbicularis oculi muscle EMG and assessed brain state through locomotion and the activities of excitatory neurons in the somatosensory cortex. Additionally, we explored the relationship between neuronal responses and behavior by pharmacologically manipulating the nicotinic receptors. Our findings include (1) at the population level, TEC acquisitions enhanced a small subset (~20%) of CS- or US-rPNs, while diminishing the responses of non-rPNs that were either locomotion-tuned or unresponsive (~80%); (2) the amplitude of arresting response in the CS-window increased with learning performance, whereas those in the US-window decreased; (3) two distinct subtypes of rPNs, CR-neurons presumed to encode sensory information and UR-neurons presumed to encode contextual information, exhibited differential modulation with learning; CR-neurons showed decreased sensory response post-TEC, whereas UR-neurons increased their response to sensory inputs after TEC; and (4) behavioral performance positively correlated with the modulation index, reflecting changes in CR- and UR-neurons. Both behavioral patterns and the modulation index were modulated by cholinergic signaling. These results underscore the critical role of the sensory cortex and cholinergic modulation in sensorimotor learning.

Differential modulation is an efficient strategy for TEC learning

The sensory cortex integrates inputs from multiple sources, each carrying a variety of information essential for establishing effective representations of behaviorally significant stimuli.31 We observed that the locomotion-related neurons were distinct from responsive neurons. As training progressed, a division of labor emerged within the overall population of PNs, leading them to exhibit distinct characteristics at different learning stages.

Importantly, we identified two subtypes of rPNs with distinct properties: CR-neurons and UR-neurons. These were modulated oppositely by TEC learning. CR-neurons decreased their responsiveness to whisker stimulation (i.e., CS), while UR-neurons acquired responses to the CS after TEC. These changes were accompanied by an increased arresting response in the CS-window. Our findings contrast with earlier studies, which reported that responsiveness to the sensory stimulus mostly remained unchanged during learning.3,5 We attribute this difference to the different behavioral paradigms used, especially reward vs. punishment and/or freezing vs. running.32–34 In some perceptual learning paradigms,3,4 mice received two different stimuli to form selective attention (e.g., vertical and horizontal visual stimulation). However, in our TEC paradigm, a single CS (i.e., 60 Hz whisker stimuli) was used.

Regarding the US-evoked responses in a subpopulation of rPNs (i.e., UR-neurons), these changes may result from increased arousal states in the brain. We interpret the activities in UR-neurons as contextual-related information, potentially originating from interneurons or astrocytes in the sensory cortex influenced by heightened arousal states.13,35 The response pattern of UR-neurons during TEC acquisition resembles the reported earlier,3–5 where learning recruits previously unresponsive neurons and enhances their responses. The response profiles of UR-neurons in our study closely resemble those of task-relevant neurons in perceptual learning, displaying enhanced and anticipatory activity during learning.27 Additionally, sensory responses in UR-neurons disappeared when there were no whisker stimuli, and the activity in CS-US trials of UR-neurons was a summation of responses observed in CS-only and US-only trials. These results suggest that UR-neurons have the capability to temporally integrate sensory and contextual signals.

Together, our findings suggest that the role of S1 is initially to distinguish between sensory and contextual inputs based on their different response characteristics, and subsequently, to amplify the differences between these representations during learning. Consequently, the salience of information relayed to downstream areas is enhanced.

Cholinergic signaling underlies TEC induced modulation of neuronal changes

The running speed of the animal often serves as a measure of the arousal state.14,25,36,37 Increased locomotion following the delivery of air puff suggests an increase in the arousal levels. Interestingly, we observed arresting responses immediately after the delivery of CS and US, particularly notable at the expert stage of learning. The arresting response significantly increased in the CS-window but decreased in the US-window from the naive stage to the expert stage, indicating that different mechanisms regulate the arresting responses in these windows. The arresting response in the US-window occurred after the delivery of the air puff, likely representing a startle response. This response reduced from the naive to the expert stage, presumably because animals could close their eyelids before the arrival of the air puff, thereby reducing fear. Conversely, the arresting response in the CS-window might serve as a measurable indicator of active attention to avoid the impending harmful US, consistent with premotor theories.38,39 Additionally, locomotion in the US-window showed a decreased tendency from the naive stage to the expert stage, suggesting that the locomotion after US delivery contributed minimally to learning.

Higher arousal levels are associated with spatiotemporally dynamic patterns of cholinergic modulation in sensory cortex.40 Cholinergic signaling originating from the basal forebrain (BF) rapidly conveys time-locked information to the sensory cortex about the behavioral state of the animal and the occurrence of salient sensory stimuli.29,41 We demonstrated that inactivation of nAChRs decreased the arresting response in the CS-window, but had no effects on the arresting response in the US-window, further confirming their differences. Additionally, inactivation of nAChRs resulted in reduced neuronal activity and locomotion after an air puff, suggesting that both behavioral and neuronal responses were modulated by cholinergic signaling. We observed that the CR activity of CR-neurons increased, whereas CR activity of UR-neurons decreased after treatment with nAChRs antagonists, which was opposite to the changes observed in CR-neurons and UR-neurons from the naive stage to the expert stage. Our results reveal a linear relationship between the modulation index and learning performance. These findings suggest that both the decreased activities of CR-neurons and the increased activities of UR-neurons are necessary for the high learning performance.

Lastly, experiments using electrical stimulation of the BF revealed that ACh could rapidly modulate the neural activity in the visual cortex. Importantly, both the incidence and the polarity of these responses depended on cell type.41,42 The majority of projections from BF to sensory cortex primarily affect interneurons rather than PNs,35,41,43 and only muscarinic receptors, not nicotinic receptors, are widely expressed on PNs.44,45 Our results in S1 showed that blocking muscarinic receptors had no significant influence on learning performance. Together, these findings suggest that the activities of the PNs may be modulated indirectly by cholinergic signaling during learning.

In summary, we conclude that L2/3 PNs in S1 efficiently encode sensory and contextual inputs through opposing modulations. Cholinergic pathways are engaged in the learning-induced modulations, expanding our understanding of mechanisms underlying learning.

Limitations of the study

In the present study, head fixation limited the movement of the mice and might have caused specific arresting responses, which may differ from those in free-moving behavioral paradigms. Furthermore, we did not analyze the activity changes of locomotion-related and unresponsive neurons at the single-neuron level during TEC training, as these neurons are unidentifiable for longitudinal tracking. Therefore, the exact origin of the UR-neurons, whether from locomotion-related or unresponsive neurons, remains unclear. Additionally, it is unclear whether the molecular identities of the CR- and UR-neurons differ.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and the Biosafety Committee of the University of Wyoming. Immune-competent mice of both sexes (age range p60–p300) were used in the experiments described here. Food and water were available ad libitum. Mice were initially housed in groups of 2–5, and after surgery, they were housed individually in a vivarium maintained at 21°C–23°C on a 12-h light/dark cycle. All imaged mice were derived from the crosses of Ai96 [B6; 129S-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm96.1(CAG-GCaMP6s)Hze/J (JAX Stock No: 024106)] with CamKII-Cre (JAX Stock No: 005359). For optogenetics, ChAT-ChR2 (JAX Stock No: 014546) mice were used. Mice expressing the transgenes (Ai96 and cre-specific) or ChAT-ChR2 were identified by PCR, which were outsourced to Transnetyx (transnetyx.com).

METHOD DETAILS

Surgeries

Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane (v/v) and maintained with 0.4 LPM oxygenated 2% isoflurane (v/v) throughout the surgery.46,47 The animals were mounted on a stereotaxic device (NARISHIGE SG-4N) and maintained at 37°C using a heat pad (K&H no.1060). The incision site was treated with 70% isopropyl alcohol and iodine. The scalp was shaved and cleaned, and the exposed skull was allowed to dry. Then, the exposed skull and wound margins were covered by a thin layer of Vetbond. Once dry, a thin layer of dental acrylic was applied around the wound margins, avoiding the region of skull overlying S1 (right hemisphere, 1.6mm posterior and 3.5mm lateral of bregma). A metal head bar was affixed with dental cement caudally to the skull. After implantation, the remainder of the exposed skull were sealed with dental cement. Mice were then recovered on a heating pad. When alert, they were placed back in their home cage. Carprofen was administered daily for 3 days post-surgery. Mice were left to recover for at least 7 days prior to task training.

For recording the differential electromyography (EMG) activity of the ipsilateral orbicularis oculi (OO) muscle, two polyimide-insulated stainless steel wires (125 μm, California Fine Wire) and connecting pins were implanted to the upper eyelid of the left eye.23 A third wire was implanted to the skull near the olfactory bulb as a ground.

For two-photon imaging,48,49 a 3.5 mm circular piece of skull was removed above S1 (right hemisphere, 1.6 mm posterior and 3.5 mm lateral relative to bregma). Once the skull was removed, a sterile 3 mm diameter cover glass was placed directly on the exposed dura and sealed to the surrounding skull with Vetbond.

For in vivo cannula infusion,50 a 1 mm circular craniotomy was drilled above S1 (right hemisphere, 1.6 mm posterior and 3.5 mm lateral relative to bregma).Then a guide cannula (2 mm; C315GMN/SPC; Bilaney) was implanted and secured with Vetbond.

For optogenetics,50 a 1 mm circular craniotomy was drilled above S1 (right hemisphere, 1.6 mm posterior and 3.5 mm lateral relative to bregma). Then an optic fiber (MFC_400/480-0.22-0.5MM_MF1.25_FLT, Doric Lenses, Quebec, Canada) was implanted and secured with Vetbond.

Data acquisition

For intrinsic signal imaging,5,51 the C2 barrel column was located using intrinsic signal imaging under anesthesia. The individual whisker (C2) was inserted into a capillary connected to a piezoelectric device at 8 Hz (1s, with a 10-s gap between pulse trains) controlled by PulsePal via MATLAB. A red light source to illuminate the surface through the cranial window. Reflectance images 300 μm below the brain surface were required with a ×2.7 objective. Vasculature image was then acquired by a 546-nm interference filter and superimposed on the intrinsic signal image.

For two-photon imaging,48,49 two-photon calcium imaging was performed using a resonant/galvo scanning two-photon microscope (Neurolabware, Los Angeles, CA) controlled by Scanbox software (Los Angeles, CA). GCaMP6s fluorescence was excited by an Insight X3 laser (Spectra-physics, Milpitas, USA) setting to 920 nm, focused through a 16×/0.8NA water-immersion objective. Images (512 × 796 pixels, 490 × 630 μm) were acquired with 15.5 Hz frame rate at a depth of 120–250 μm below the pial surface for layer 2/3 imaging. During acquisition, the mouse moved free on a 3D-printed running wheel. Locomotion was recorded by a rotary encoder with its post through the wheel axle, triggered at scanning frame rate. TTLs of the whisker stimulation and air puff were output via the PulsePal to the two-photon system for image alignment.

For EMG acquisition,23,52 the eyelid movement signal was band-pass filtered with 50–500 Hz and the amplified using a 4-channel differential amplifier (model 1700, A-M Systems). The signal was acquired with the 16-bit data acquisition system (Digidata 1322A, Axon Instrument) through an analog input channel sampled at 1000 Hz. TTLs of the whisker stimulation and air puff were output via the PulsePal to the data acquisition system for alignment with the EMG signal.

To deliver the whisker stimulation, an E–650 Piezo Amplifier and piezo, controlled by PulsePul, were used. A solenoid valve (PSV-5, Aalborg) and the driver, controlled by PulsePal, were employed to deliver the air puff to the eye. The control of the two-photon system running and the delivery of whisker stimulation, air puff and laser stimuli were managed by custom-made software in MATLAB (MathWorks).

Behavioral procedures

Prior to training, mice underwent a 3-day habituation period to head restraint, spending 1 h each day with their heads fixed on a wheel. From the 4th day to the 6th day, whisker stimuli (10Hz) were delivered in 4 sessions with 30 trials per session. During this period, two-photon imaging was conducted based on the results of intrinsic signal imaging.22,24 On the 7th day, the whisker stimuli were changed to 60 Hz but in 1 session with 30 trials. This phase is referred to as the ‘Habituation stage during learning (Hab)’ in this article.

After habituation, trace eyeblink conditioning (TEC) was performed. The conditional stimulus (CS) was whisker deflection (60 Hz, 250 ms in duration) followed by an unconditioned stimulus (US) after an interval (250 ms from CS offset to US onset). The US consisted of a periorbital air puff (15 psi, 50 ms in duration) delivered via a plastic needle positioned 5 mm from the cornea. The interval between trials varied randomly from 20 s to 40 s. Each mouse was trained in 5 consecutive sessions per day (20 trials per session) until they learned to maintain eyelid closure from the CS onset to the US offset, as indicated by the EMG processing (Details in data analysis). The first training session was labeled the ‘Naive stage during learning (Naive)’ and the last training session was designated the ‘Expert stage during learning (Exp)’. Within each session of 20 trials, stimuli were presented randomly, with 80% of trials consisting of paired CS and US (CS-US), and 10% each of CS alone (CS-only) and US alone (US-only) trials interspersed randomly. If a mouse showed signs of discomfort during training, it was returned to the home cage for up to 24 h.

Pharmacological and optogenetic manipulation of cholinergic inputs

All drugs or saline were administered starting from the second training session. The drugs or saline were injected at the beginning of each day. After waiting 10 min, TEC training resumed (5 sessions per day). All drugs were dissolved in physiological saline (0.9% sodium chloride). For systemic inactivation of cholinergic inputs, the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor antagonist mecamylamine (5 mg/kg, M9020, Sigma-Aldrich) and the muscarinic acetylcholine receptor antagonist atropine (5 mg/kg, Y000878, Sigma-Aldrich) were injected intraperitoneally (i,p.).27,53 For local inactivation, mecamylamine (0.5 μg/μl) and muscimol (0.1ug/ul, M1523, Sigma-Aldrich) were delivered through the implanted cannula. Pressure injection was performed using a customized device driven by a single axis hydraulic manipulator (NARISHIGE mmo-220A) at a rate of 30–50 nL/min.

For optogenetic activation, the blue light (M470F3, Thorlabs) was delivered from the CS offset to the US onset through the implanted optic fiber. The light delivery was controlled by an LED-driver (LEDD1B, Thorlabs).

Data processing and analysis

The pipeline for processing two-photon images followed previously described methods.48,49 Briefly, movies from the same plane for each session were concatenated and motion corrected. After alignment, a mean image was generated and used to track the same cells for longitudinal imaging. For segmentation, a MATLAB graphical user interface tool (Scanbox, Los Angeles, CA) was employed to define regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to neuronal soma. After segmentation, the calcium transients of each cell were extracted, and estimated spiking was calculated using non-negative temporal deconvolution of the corrected ROI signals with Vanilla algorithm.

For calcium transients’ analysis, the baseline of the signal for each cell was determined by calculating the minimum of the mean values from two different segments of the recording: the first 450 frames and the frames from 451 to 900 (~930 frames in total, corresponding to a 60-s duration before CS onset). The ratio of Δf/f at each time point was then calculated. During each session, the calcium signal was segmented into trials: 30 trials during the Habituation (Hab) stage and 20 trials during the Naive and Expert (Exp) stages. These trials were aligned based on the onset of the conditioned stimulus (CS), with data collected from 1 s before CS onset to 2 s after CS onset.

For cell responses classification (Figure S4), PNs were categorized as ‘responsive’, ‘locomotion-related’, or ‘unresponsive’ based on the responses features. Using estimated spikes, we first determined whether there were spikes (>0) within 1 s after whisker stimulation (1s window). If so, the trial was considered responsive. We then counted the number of responsive trial. If this number of exceeded 10 (60% of the CS-US trials), the cell was classified as ‘responsive neuron’ (Figures S4A and S4B). Locomotion-related cells were identified if their correlation coefficients exceeded 60th percentile of the distribution of all cells’ correlation coefficients distribution in one session. To avoid contamination from the stimulation-evoked responses, the evoked signals/locomotion (10 s time window after CS onset) were excluded from the session analysis (Figure S4C). The overlap of responsive neurons and locomotion-related cells was defined as locomotion-related cells. The remaining cells were classified as ‘unresponsive cells’. Responsive neurons were divided into two types: CS responsive neurons (CR-neurons) and US responsive neurons (UR-neurons), based on the maximum amplitude comparison during CS window (500 ms from CS onset to US onset) and US window (500 ms after US onset).

For longitudinal tracking of the same cells, we consistently located the same imaging plane across learning stages using previously acquired images as references, guided by the vascular map and averaged fluorescence images. After imaging, we merged the vascular map and two-photon image in each session. Then, we selected one cell in all merged images as a reference cell. By calculating the spatial locations relative to the reference cell, we could track the same cells across different sessions.

For locomotion analysis, we first calculated the baseline locomotion by averaging the data 60 s before CS onset (~930 data, 60s duration before CS onset) and selecting the minimum value between two intervals (1–450 frames and 451–900 frames). Subsequently, this baseline was subtracted from the locomotion traces. During each session, the locomotion traces were segmented into 30 or 20 trials (30 trials at Hab stage, 20 trials at Naive and Exp), aligned with the CS onset (1s before CS onset and 2s after CS onset).

For EMG analysis,23,52 we first synchronized the length of the EMG signal with the locomotion or calcium transient by resampling the original EMG data based on the TTLs of CS and US. Next, we calculated the baseline of the EMG (~930 data, 60s before CS onset) by determining the minimum value within two segments (1–450 frames and 451–900 frames). After subtracting this baseline, the EMG traces were segmented into 30 or 20 trials (30 trials at Hab stage, 20 trials at Naive and Exp), aligned with the CS onset (1s before CS onset and 2s after CS onset). The conditioned response (CR) in each trial was defined as the EMG activity that was 3 standard deviations above the baseline, persisting at least 200 ms before US onset. A CR percentage (CR %) greater than 60% within a session was considered to meet the learning criterion. The early CS response (ECR) was the mean amplitude of the EMG from CS onset to CS offset, while the late CS response (LCR) was the average amplitude of EMG from CS offset to US onset. The unconditioned response (UR) was the mean EMG activity during 500 ms period following US.

For linear regression, the modulation index (MI) was calculated using following the equation: MI=CRUR−neurons−CRCR−neurons/CRUR−neurons+CRCR−neurons Where CRUR−neurons is the CR of UR-neurons and CRCR−neurons is the CR of CR-neurons.

Then, linear regression was fitted using MATLAB ‘fitlm’ function. For decay function fitting, the equation was: fx=a1⋅exp−a2⋅x+a3 Where x is the input and a1; a2; a3 are the parameters of the function.

Then the MATLAB function (‘fitnlm’) was used for fitting the data.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All the statistical details are indicated where used. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m.). Statistical analyses were conducted using MATLAB. Parametric tests were used whenever possible to test differences between two or more means. Non-parametric tests were used when data distributions were non-normal. For pairwise comparisons of proportions, Chisquare test was used. Two-tailed tests were used unless otherwise noted. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
