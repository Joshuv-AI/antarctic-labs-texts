Unveiling the Probiotic Properties of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei UFTM 2.9 Through Probiogenomic Analysis
Authors: Bárbara R Fonseca, Yasmin N V Sabino, Beatriz M O Rocha, Vasco A C Azevedo, Bertram Brenig, Siomar C Soares, Leandro N Lemos, Sheila C P D Luquetti, Aline D Paiva, Julliane D Medeiros, Alessandra B F Machado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLactic acid bacteria (LAB) comprise a group of Gram‐positive bacteria with biotechnological applications. LAB, including Lacticaseibacillus spp., are recognized as potential probiotics due to their ability to confer benefits to the host. Here we employ probiogenomic and in vitro analyses to characterize the probiotic potential of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, a LAB that previously demonstrated probiotic properties in vitro. The draft genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 comprises 127 contigs, totaling 3 216 252 base pairs, with a GC content of 46.20%. The bacteria showed metabolic versatility, growing in five carbon sources. A total of 170 genes potentially associated with probiotic characteristics were identified, with functions linked to stress resistance (n = 106), adhesion (n = 12), biosynthesis of vitamins (n = 10), and others. No virulence genes or CRISPR elements were detected, and two phages were identified in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9. Gene clusters encoding bacteriocins were detected and confirmed in vitro. L c. paracasei UFTM 2.9 inhibited all indicator bacteria tested (n = 12), including strains of Listeria innocua, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Escherichia coli. The results indicate the potential use of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 as a probiotic, considering its genetic potential to express traits of interest and survive in the gastrointestinal tract (GIT).Keywords: LAB, Lc. paracasei, probiotics

Introduction

Lc. paracasei is a Gram‐positive Bacillus belonging to the Lactobacillaceae family, ubiquitously present in nature. This bacterium is part of a group of microorganisms known as lactic acid bacteria (LAB), which are catalase‐negative, non‐spore‐forming, and produce lactic acid as the main product of carbohydrate fermentation [1]. LABs have significant biotechnological potential, being used, for example, in the production of fermented dairy products due to their ability to confer organoleptic characteristics and contribute to food preservation through the production of metabolites that extend shelf life [2].

In addition, several specific strains within the genus Lacticaseibacillus have notable application as probiotics, which are microorganisms that, when administered in sufficient quantities, promote the host's well‐being through molecular interactions with the immune and digestive systems [3]. Probiotic effects result from interactions between the microorganisms and the host, wherein the host provides an environment rich in nutrients and metabolites conducive to microbial growth. In return, probiotics play a role in metabolizing carbohydrates and indigestible compounds, producing substances such as short‐chain fatty acids (SCFA) and vitamins. The use of specific microorganisms as probiotics is preceded by in silico, in vitro, and in vivo analyses using experimental models. Probiotics impact host health through various mechanisms, including (i) modulation of the immune system by enhancing immune responses, reducing inflammation, and combating pathogens; (ii) interspecies interactions through bacteriocin production; (iii) production of organic acids and regulation of intestinal pH; (iv) contribution to the composition of the intestinal mucosal barrier; and (v) synthesis of neuromodulatory molecules such as GABA and tryptophan [4].

The classical discovery and characterization of probiotic strains primarily using in vitro analysis is time‐consuming and challenging. In this context, the technological development of genomic sequencing platforms represents a key resource for characterizing new probiotic candidates [5]. Probiogenomics enables the identification of microorganisms and their functional role through genome analysis, supported by in vitro experiments that validate the identified genetic potential. This methodology has been extensively employed to prospect potential new probiotic strains [6, 7].

Considering the evolutionary process of bacteria from the genus Lacticaseibacillus, known for their probiotic potential, it is observed that they share significant genetic information, with small variations potentially influencing the expression of probiotic‐associated traits. Additionally, advancements in sequencing platforms enable access to the genome of potentially probiotic microorganisms, allowing the in silico investigation of these traits and better guidance of in vitro experiments. Therefore, this research aims to characterize the probiotic potential of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, which was previously isolated from cow's milk in Uberaba, Minas Gerais, Brazil, using in silico and in vitro approaches. This bacteria has previously shown inhibitory effects against pathogens in vitro and resistance to acids and bile salts [8].

Material and Methods

Isolation

Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was previously isolated from cow's milk [8] using the De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS; Difco, Sparks, NV, USA) broth. The bacteria were grown for 24 h at 37°C, under a microaerophilic atmosphere, and the stock cultures were maintained in MRS.

The genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, the reference genome of Lc. paracasei deposited in NCBI, as well as genomes of other Lc. paracasei strains and additional bacterial genomes used in this study, were retrieved from the GenBank database (April 2023 to October 2024) and are listed in Table S1.

DNA Extraction

Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was cultivated in MRS broth for 24 h at 37°C. The culture was centrifuged (17 500 × g, 10 min.) (Megafuge 8, Waltham, Thermo Scientific, MA, USA), and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was suspended in 500 µL of bacterial lysis buffer (25% sucrose in TE buffer; 0.5 M EDTA pH 8.0; 1 M Tris pH 8.0; 1.5 mg/mL lysozyme in ultrapure water) to which 5 µL of RNase A solution was added, followed by vortexing and incubation at 37°C for 1 h. Subsequently, the genomic DNA was extracted using the phenol‐chloroform protocol [9]. Agarose gel electrophoresis was performed to verify the integrity of the DNA, and its concentration was quantified using NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific, 107 Wilmington, DE, USA).

Genome Sequencing, Taxonomic Identification, and Functional Annotation

Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 genome was sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq platform (2 × 150 bp) (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The quality of the DNA sequences was assessed using FastQC [10], and the low‐quality reads were removed using Trimmomatic [11] with the following parameters: trailing: 10, leading: 10, slidingwindow: 4:20, and minlen: 50. High‐quality reads were assembled using SPAdes v3.9 [12], by setting the k‐mer sizes to 21, 33, 55, 77, 99, and 127. The quality of the assembled genome was assessed using QUAST [13] and contigs shorter than 500 bp were removed from the final assembly. The genome was annotated using the Pathosystems Resource Integration Center—PATRIC [14]. Taxonomic inferences were made using the 16S rRNA gene, by calculating the average nucleotide identity (ANI) using the BLAST (ANIb) and MUMmer (ANIm) algorithms and the correlation indexes of tetra‐nucleotide signatures (Tetra) using JSpecies [15]. A dendrogram was constructed using ANIb data from Lc. paracasei strains and external groups using Python 3.6. In addition, the functional annotation of the Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 genome was performed using Non‐supervised Orthologous Groups (EggNOG 5.0) [16].

Genomic Island Identification

The genomic island identification in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was performed using the software GIPSy (Genomic Island Prediction Software) with default parameters [17]. The genome of Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. jakobsenii ZN7a‐9 DSM 26046 was used as a closely related organism. The genomic islands overlapping contig endings were excluded, as they may represent false‐positive results. The genomes and the predicted genomic islands were plotted using Blast Ring Image Generator (BRIG) with default parameters [18].

Carbohydrate Growth Assay

To evaluate the growth of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 in diverse carbon sources, the bacteria was cultivated in basal MRS broth consisting of 1.5 g of enzymatic casein, 1.5 g of meat extract, 0.6 g of yeast extract, 0.3 g of ammonium citrate, 0.7 g of sodium acetate, 0.3 g of dipotassium hydrogen phosphate, 0.0075 g of manganese sulfate, and 0.03 g magnesium sulfate supplemented with 2% glucose for 24 h at 37°C. The bacterial cells were washed with sterile saline solution (0.75% NaCl), then the inoculum was adjusted to an initial optical density (OD) at 600 nm of 0.1, measured (GENESYS UV‐Vis, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The bacteria were cultivated in basal MRS medium without glucose supplementation, containing individually added carbohydrates at a final concentration of 2% (w/v): xylose, arabinose, fructose, galactose, rhamnose, inositol, trehalose, lactose, sucrose, and raffinose [19]. The culture was incubated at 37°C for 96 h, and the OD 600 nm was measured at times 0, 6, 12, 24, 30, 36, 48, 54, 60, 72, 78, and 84 h (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA).

Identification of Virulence and Antibiotic Resistance Genes, Plasmids, Phages, and CRISPR‐Cas Systems

The presence of virulence genes and antibiotic resistance genes in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was investigated using ABRIcate [20] against the Virulence Factor database (https://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) and CARD [21]. In addition, the Virulence Finder [22] was used to investigate the presence of virulence genes using a database containing classical pathogens. The search for plasmids was carried out using PlasmidFinder [23]. The plasmid was then assembled using SPAdes version 3.11 [12] and subjected to BLAST analysis against the reference sequence (GenBank accession number: CP000424.1). Only contigs that matched the plasmidial sequence of the database were classified as plasmid‐related. The presence of phage regions was investigated using Phastest [24]. Finally, CRISPRCasFinder [25] was used to verify the presence of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPRs) regions.

Identification of Proteins Potentially Related to Probiosis

In this study, a database was constructed by our team from a literature review that enabled the development of an internal database containing genes associated with the expression of traits considered probiotic [5]. This database was used to search for proteins potentially related to probiotics in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 (Table S2). The database contains 243 sequences, among which it was possible to identify in Lc. paracasei functions associated with adhesion (n = 13), exopolysaccharide biosynthesis (n = 18), biofilm formation (n = 6), antimicrobial activity (n = 2), vitamin biosynthesis (n = 10), acid stress (n = 20), bile tolerance (n = 17), cold stress (n = 4), heat stress (n = 8), osmotic stress (n = 8), starvation (n = 2), stress resistance (n = 41), intestinal persistence (n = 2), immunomodulation (n = 3), organic solvent stress (n = 1), and oxidative stress (n = 15). The identification of proteins in the Lc. paracasei UFTM genome. BLAST analysis of paracasei UFTM 2.9, as well as contigs related to the plasmid, was performed using BLASTx [26] with the following parameters: minimum of 30% amino acid identity and 70% coverage.

In Silico and In Vitro Identification of Antimicrobial Compounds Produced by Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9

The search for putative biosynthetic gene clusters encoding antimicrobial compounds in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was performed using BAGEL4 [27] and antiSMASH [28], and the detected clusters indicate that the predicted antimicrobial compounds may correspond to bacteriocins. The activity of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 against pathogens was evaluated in vitro by growing Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 in MRS medium under microaerophilic conditions in an incubator at 37°C for 24 h. Similarly, the indicator microorganisms were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI; Difco Sparks, Maryland, USA) medium under the same time and temperature conditions and then subjected to a spot‐on‐lawn assay [29]. The bacteria used as indicators of the antagonist activity were Streptococcus mutans, S. agalactiae ATCC 13813, Streptococcus pyogenes ATCC 19615, S. aureus subsp. aureus ATCC 25923, Staphylococcus saprophyticus ATCC 35552, Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC 14990, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212, Enterococcus faecium NCTC 7171, Listeria monocytogenes ATCC 7644, L. monocytogenes ATCC 19112, L. innocua ATCC 33090, E. coli ATCC 25922, and E. coli ATCC 35218.

Results

General Genomic Features of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9

The genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 is composed of 127 contigs, with a total length of 3,216,252 bps and a GC content of 46.20% (Figure 1). The annotated genome resulted in 3528 coding sequences (CDS), of which 1099 are hypothetical proteins, 54 tRNA, and 5 rRNA. The functional annotation performed by EggNOG categorized a total of 2727 proteins, among which 2340 were grouped into 17 categories. The majority of the proteins are associated with carbohydrate transport and metabolism (n = 258), transcription (n = 231), replication, recombination, and repair (n = 176), translation, biogenesis, and ribosome structures (n = 162), and amino acid metabolism (n = 150), among others (Table 1 and Table S3).

Taxonomy

The taxonomy assignment using Lc. paracasei 8700 as the reference genome resulted in 97.81% ANIb and 98.61% ANIm. Additionally, the TETRA analysis resulted in a match of 0.99753 between the evaluated genome and the reference. The TCS results analysis presented a z‐score above 0.99. The ANIb index demonstrates the percentage of similarities among Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, reference genomes of Lc. paracasei, and external groups, as shown in Figure 2.

Nucleotide identity average of Lacticaseibacillus species. Color red indicates the highest genomic similarity, while blue represents the least similar sequences, according to the ANIb index. The index was calculated using the JSpecies software.

Genomic Island Prediction

No pathogenicity/resistance island was identified in the Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 genome. Remarkably, four metabolic islands were identified in contigs 22, 39, 48, and 72, comprising from 18 998 to 67 126 bps. Additionally, two genomic islands of 8690 to 11 736 bp were detected in contigs 43 and 61, respectively (Figure S1). The metabolic islands are predominantly associated with carbohydrate metabolism, while the genomic islands contain proteins related to amino acid metabolism, as shown in Table S4.

Carbohydrate Growth Assay

The growth curve of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was analyzed in several carbon sources. The results revealed that the bacteria reached a high OD 600 nm (> 1) in culture media containing lactose, fructose, and sucrose, and showed a final OD of 0.58 in the presence of galactose. The growth of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was less pronounced (OD 600 nm between 0.259 and 0.358) in the presence of arabinose, inositol, raffinose, and rhamnose. No cellular mass formation was observed in the presence of trehalose or cellobiose as the sole carbon source (Figure 3).

Analysis of Virulence and Antimicrobial Resistance Genes, Phages, CRISPR Regions, and Plasmids in the Genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9

No gene associated with antimicrobial resistance and other virulence factors was identified in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 using the ABRIcate and Virulence Finder software. The Phastest software detected one incomplete and one questionable phage within the genome, comprising 13 842 and 16 392 base pairs, respectively (Table S5). Additionally, CRISPRs were not detected using the CRISPRCasFinder software. PlasmidFinder identified the presence of the replicon repUS74 in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, with 100% coverage and 93.97% identity.

Identification of Probiotic‐Related Proteins

Based on our in‐house database, 170 proteins potentially associated with probiotic functions were identified. The genes were categorized into 16 groups, with stress resistance‐related genes being the most prevalent (n = 106). The genetic capacity for exopolysaccharide production was also evidenced by the presence of 18 associated genes such as rplD, rplE, rpoE, and rpoN, as well as genes involved in adhesion (n = 13), and biofilm formation (n = 6). Genes related to biosynthesis of vitamins (n = 10) were also identified, such as the gene encoding dihydrofolate reductase. Genes associated with immunomodulation (n = 3) and antimicrobial activity (n = 2) were also detected (Figure 4 and Table S6).

Functional annotation of genes associated with probiosis in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9.

Of the proteins identified in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, 14 were in the plasmid‐related contigs. These proteins are involved in stress resistance (n = 7), gut persistence (n = 2), adhesion (n = 1), biosynthesis of exopolysaccharides (n = 2), and biosynthesis of vitamins (n = 2) (Table S7).

Detection of Biosynthetic Gene Clusters Encoding Antimicrobial Compounds and In Vitro Analysis

The analysis performed by BAGEL4 and antiSMASH revealed the identification of five biosynthetic gene clusters encoding bacteriocins (Figure 5). BAGEL4 identified two biosynthetic clusters. The first cluster has an unknown core peptide, in addition to proteins associated with transport and leader cleavage, and immunity (Figure 5A, 1). The second biosynthetic gene cluster encodes carnocin and enterocin production and immunity proteins (Figure 5A, 2). Additionally, the antiSMASH software identified genes associated with salivaricin production in distinct genome regions, carried by additional biosynthetic and regulatory genes, as well as other genes (Figure 5B, 1, 2, 3), indicating the presence of genes involved in signaling, expression, and transport of this peptide.

The synthesis of antimicrobial compounds by Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was assessed in vitro, confirming its inhibitory activity against a range of pathogens (Figure 6). The growth of all evaluated pathogens was inhibited, including Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive bacteria, such as Streptococcus spp., Staphylococcus spp., Enterococcus spp., Listeria spp., and E. coli strains. The largest inhibition halo produced by Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 was observed against E. coli ATCC 3739 and S. saprophyticus ATCC 35552, while the smaller inhibition zone was observed against S. mutans, S. pyogenes ATCC 19615, and E. faecalis ATCC 29212 (Table S8).

Discussion

Advancements in genetic sequencing have broadened the scope of knowledge about probiotics, allowing a fast screening of potential new probiotic strains and revealing new functions and mechanisms for this beneficial bacterial group. This study used a probiogenomic approach to characterize the probiotic potential of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9. The results showed that the bacterium harbors genes related to multiple probiotic‐related mechanisms, and some beneficial properties were confirmed in vitro.

The functional annotation of the Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 genome revealed a high number of genes associated with carbohydrate metabolism, including ycsE, galA, ptcB, celB, celC, lacA, lacB, lacF, galK, rafA, and mtlA, which are precursors for the PTS system of fructose, lactose, galactose, and cellobiose. Similarly, the genes plsC, wecA, gpmB, and manA identified in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 are also associated with carbohydrate utilization and were also detected in Lactiplantibacillus plantarum JS21 [30], L. plantarum [31], Bifidobacterium longum NCC2705 [32], and Lactobacillus reuteri 100–23 [33]. Additionally, the genes recX, ribF, rplD, rplE, and rpoZ, annotated in the Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 genome, are categorized in the literature as precursors for exopolysaccharide synthesis, essential molecules in the processes of microbial adhesion to the intestinal mucosa, protection against acidification caused by gastrointestinal conditions, and the formation of a protective barrier [34].

In silico analyses also revealed the presence of four metabolic islands in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, highlighting the presence of several genes related to carbohydrate metabolism, such as lacF and sorC, including genes for lactate assimilation (e.g., lutA, lutB, and lutC), in addition to genes associated with the biosynthesis of vitamins, such as ribZ. The presence of metabolic islands in the Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 genome may indicate the acquisition of adaptive traits, providing an ecological and competitive advantage to the bacteria. Corroborating with the genomic findings, the in vitro growth of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 in different carbohydrates was demonstrated, with the bacterium reaching a higher OD in the presence of galactose, fructose, and sucrose. These results are consistent with Tarrah and collaborators, who analyzed the complete sequences of enzymes responsible for the hydrolysis of carbohydrates in the genome of Lc. paracasei DTA72, verifying the strain's ability to grow in the presence of fructose, glucose, lactose, and galactose. Additionally, Pei and collaborators analyzed 121 isolates of Lc. paracasei and found high utilization of fructose and trehalose, along with low utilization of xylose and arabinose. The authors attributed the differential carbohydrate use among isolates to the environments from which the microorganisms were isolated, highlighting the adaptive capacity of Lc. paracasei strains.

In addition to revealing a diverse metabolic potential, the in‐house database gathering genes associated with probiotic characteristics enabled the identification of 170 genes potentially related to probiotic activity in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9. Among other features, the proteins identified in the genome indicate the potential of the strain to produce exopolysaccharides (n = 18), adhere to surfaces (n = 13), and form biofilms (n = 6), which are pivotal traits for probiotic persistence in the host [34]. Probiotics also have to survive through the variable and hostile conditions of the GIT. In this sense, genes related to resistance to bile salts (e.g., luxS, msrC, mutT, nagB, nhaC), oxidative stress (e.g., dnaJ, dnaK, dps, eno2, epsA), osmotic stress (e.g., ctr, cydD, cysE, ddl), and temperature variation (e.g., clpX, cobC, copa, cpdA) were identified in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, indicating the potential viability of the strain in the GIT. The resistance of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 to bile salts and low pH was also previously demonstrated in vitro [8].

Probiotic effects also involve interactions between microorganisms and the host, in which the microbial growth can lead to the production of beneficial substances. In this context, the presence of dihydrofolate reductase, NAD‐dependent protein deacetylase, and D‐alanyl carrier protein ligase, genes associated with the biosynthesis of vitamins B9 (folic acid), B3 (niacin), and the formation of surface peptides and SCFA, respectively, was observed in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, reinforcing its potential probiotic capacity. Besides providing essential vitamins to the host, these genes stabilize the resident microbiota and modulate the recruitment of macrophages and neutrophils, as indicated by previous studies conducted on the same species [35]. Genes involved in the synthesis of B‐complex vitamins were also identified in other strains of Lc. paracasei, such as Lc. paracasei ZFM 54, isolated from infant feces, highlighting the microorganism's capacity to provide essential micronutrients to the host [36].

Another essential trait of probiotics that beneficially impacts the host's health is their ability to inhibit the growth of pathogens, maintain a balanced microbial community in the GIT, and prevent infections. In silico analyses of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 indicated the presence of biosynthetic gene clusters encoding salivaricin (class I bacteriocin), carnocin (class II bacteriocin—lantibiotic), and enterocin (class IIa bacteriocin—pediocin), all known for their activity against Gram‐positive pathogens, such as Listeria spp. and Streptococcus spp. [37]. Our in vitro results supported the in silico findings, showing that Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 presents a broad inhibitory potential against pathogens responsible for foodborne infections and dysbiosis. In addition to inhibiting Gram‐positive pathogens, Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 inhibited the growth of two E. coli strains. The ability of LAB to express different types of bacteriocins depending on environmental factors and the specific pathogens encountered can also be demonstrated [38]. To identify new food preservatives, the authors isolated an antimicrobial metabolite from Lc. paracasei ZHANG, classified as a class II bacteriocin, which was capable of inhibiting S. aureus and L. monocytogenes. Additionally, the activity of Lc. paracasei CNCM I‐5369 against E. coli strains was previously demonstrated [23, 39].

When characterizing potentially new probiotic strains, it is crucial to assess the presence of mobile genetic elements and virulence factors to determine whether the microorganism could cause any infections or diseases in the host. Additionally, this evaluation can help uncover its effectiveness in promoting the host's health. The absence of virulence factors in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 analyzed in this study corroborates with previous findings in the literature suggesting the biosafety of this bacterial species [40]. As part of biosafety considerations, a specific phage search was conducted in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, identifying two phages classified as questionable and incomplete. It has been demonstrated in the literature that phage editing regions in Lc. paracasei genomes are questionable or inconsistent [41], mainly attributed to the different sources of microbial isolation, consistent with the results of this research. Furthermore, CRISPR elements were not detected.

A plasmid replicon was identified in the genome of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9. This plasmid harbors genes potentially associated with probiosis, indicating a possible acquisition of beneficial traits in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 throughout the evolution of this bacterium. The identified genes are associated with stress response (osmV and copA), carbohydrate transport (PTS system) and metabolism (treA and malP), and biosynthesis of vitamins (btuD and ytrB). Plasmids have been extensively described in lactobacilli, carrying genes that confer niche‐specific traits, and improving bacterial survival in competitive environments [42]. In addition, the presence of these genes in Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 may enhance the technological and probiotic properties of this strain.

Conclusion

The results obtained indicate the probiotic potential of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9, considering the identification of genes associated with probiotic functions, broad pathogen inhibition capacity, and the absence of biosafety‐compromising factors, along with several genetic similarities with other probiotic microorganisms categorized in the literature. Further studies are warranted to verify the safety and beneficial effects of Lc. paracasei UFTM 2.9 in vivo.
