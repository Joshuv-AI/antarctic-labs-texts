Indirect modeling of derived outcomes: Are minor prediction discrepancies a cause for concern?
Authors: John P Prybylski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is often a goal of model development to predict data from which a variety of outcomes can be derived, such as threshold‐based categorization or change from baseline (CFB) transformations. This approach can improve power or support multiple decisions. Because these derivations are indirectly predicted from the model, they are valuable tests for misspecification when used in visual or numeric predictive checks (V/NPCs). However, derived outcome V/NPCs (especially if primary or key secondary) are often overly scrutinized and held to an uncommon standard when comparing model predictions to point estimates, even if by conventional standards both the directly and indirectly modeled data are captured well. Here, simulations of directly modeled data were used to determine where apparent issues in V/NPCs of derived outcomes are expected. Two types of datasets were simulated: (1) a simple pre–post study and (2) pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic data from a dose‐ranging study. A psoriasis exposure–response model case study was also assessed. V/NPCs were generated on the raw data, CFB data, and placebo‐corrected CFB (dCFB) data, and binned summary statistics of the observed data for each trial were graded as being strongly or weakly supportive of a predictive model (within the interquartile range or the 95% central distribution of all simulated trials, respectively). In all cases, the strength of support in direct data V/NPCs was minimally related to that in derived outcome V/NPCs. There are myriad benefits to modeling the underlying data of a derived measure, and these results support caution in discarding adequate models based on overly strict derived measure predictive checks.

INTRODUCTION

The utility of a model to inform decisions in drug development depends mainly upon the ability of the model to predict the outcome of interest. 1 However, over the course of a development lifecycle, the outcome of interest may shift, become re‐contextualized, or even change entirely, and thus a model that can adapt to the evolving needs of an organization sponsoring development, or support decisions with adequate power using a smaller sample size, will be the most useful. Often, this can be achieved by modeling an observable process that is used to determine various outcomes, for example, modeling absolute neutrophil count to report change from baseline or different degrees of neutropenia, potentially using different scales 2 ; and modeling each component of the Mayo Clinical Score for ulcerative colitis so the full score, partial score, or modified score (or responders by some subset of achievement) can be derived. 3 In these cases, all derived values from the model should typically be tested with visual and numeric predictive checks (V/NPCs) before the model is considered adequate for predicting these derived values, but once it has passed these tests by standard criteria, any relevant estimands from the model should be accepted. 4

Unfortunately, it is generally not the case that robust models with valuable flexibility, statistical rigor, and power are accepted by stakeholders without exhaustive defense, particularly when an indirectly modeled, derived value is the outcome of interest. A model that precisely captures the observed data with good predictive capacity may not exactly match a derived outcome when looking only at central tendency of observed summary statistics or point estimates relative to the distributions of predictions; as long as predictions are consistent with the data within acceptable margins of error, few analysts will see that as a sign of model misspecification, and indeed to do so would likely lead to a time‐consuming fishing expedition. Simpler models where the outcome of interest is the dependent variable will typically lead to more agreement between the central tendency of predictions and the observed data, so an organization that favors these central tendencies (even if misleading or inconsistent) will indirectly discourage innovation. Modeling only the outcome of interest also ignores the underlying data from which the outcome of interest is derived leading to censoring or incomplete integration of all available information.

While it is appropriate to defend the predictions from a model with strong arguments, it is not widely recognized how often good models of observed data (i.e., observations highly central with simulations) appear to be poorer predictors of derived data. When predictions appear to be poor, the appropriate conclusion is either that the model is misspecified or that the appearance is the result of random chance. In the present analysis, this problem is examined through illustrative simulated examples from known models so misspecification is not possible. The aim of this work is thus to identify the relationship between apparent quality of V/NPCs for models across levels of derivation from directly modeled data, with a focus on quality assessments that use stricter criteria than suggested by conventions.

METHODS

Outcome assessment

Each assessment was essentially a clinical trial simulation (CTS); each trial had a null response arm (referred to as the “placebo” arm, although no placebo effect is simulated) and several arms with expected response (levels depending on scenario). The direct observation within each scenario is some continuous value with a nonzero baseline measurement. The first level of derivation is an individual change from baseline (CFB), and a secondary derivation is a “placebo‐corrected” arm‐level change from baseline (dCFB). The former is the difference between baseline and each later observation, while the latter is the trial arm‐median CFB, subtracting the CFB in a placebo arm of the trial.

The activities of model appraisal were simulated where each trial dataset was assessed in the context of all of the trial datasets as if all those datasets were generated in a V/NPC of the model fitted to the index trial; that is, if 1000 trials were simulated, Trial 1 was assessed as a member of the 1000 trial distributions. Explicitly, the dCFB and the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of the observed data and CFB data at each timepoint were determined for all trials. 5 For each of these summary statistics, an empirical distribution calculated from the simulations could be determined, which would then be used to assess the relationship of each summary statistic to the central tendency of that statistic (i.e., the relation of that summary to the center of the predicted distribution of the summary). Arbitrarily, each summary statistic was classified as “weakly” or “strongly” supporting a predictive model; strength was judged as the proximity of an observed quantile to the center of the distribution from simulations, so “strong” support was an observed quantile within the center 50% of simulated (25th–75th percentile) and “weak” support was within the center 95% of simulations (2.5th–97.5th percentiles). The number of observations, CFBs, and dCFBs within these thresholds per trial and whether the number of supportive points in observed data or CFB data was predictive of support in dCFB data were analyzed. The depth of analysis varied by scenario.

Pre–post simulation

To examine the simplest case where the probabilistic effects could be tested, a simulation was developed from samples of uncorrelated, normally distributed, random numbers. The CTS for this scenario was based on 10,000 trials, with a range of sample sizes from 10 to 300. All baseline measurements were simulated from Nμσ2, and a single additional measurement was simulated from Nμ·1−Xσ2, where X is a percent reduction. There were four simulated study arms, each with an arm‐level reduction of 0%, 5%, 10%, and 20%. To minimize complexity, there was no correlation between measurements for the same individual, simulated observations were unbounded, and no trial‐level variability was considered (all trials used the same μ and σ, 200 and 30, respectively).

Trials were categorized by the percentage of all observations strongly supportive of a predictive model (central 50% of simulations). Because of the high number of simulated trials, the threshold of 70% of points could be used, so trials with greater than this threshold in data, CFB, or dCFB were labeled as being overall strongly supportive of models for either of these outcomes. The relationship between trials strongly supporting a model for dCFB and for data or CFB was of particular interest; whether or not this relationship is improved by sample size was also considered.

Pharmacokinetic/Pharmacodynamic simulation

A hypothetical model for the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PKPD) activity of a drug on a measurable target was developed. The drug PK was represented by a two‐compartment structural model with first‐order absorption. The target was represented as responding to the drug by a Type I indirect response model (inhibition on synthesis 6 ). Complexity was added to the modeled target by having interindividual variability on both the baseline level and degradation rate, a high proportional residual variability, and a relatively high lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) leading to a large number of simulated below quantification limit (BLQ) observations. This model was selected since it should be identifiable with a sequential PKPD model approach using M3 to handle BLQ data, 7 , 8 but may have typical issues common in fitting these models with early efficacy studies; the parameterized noise within the model was also expected to generate widely distributed primary and secondary derived values. Code for the model using the R package mrgsolve 9 is in the Supplementary Materials.

For 1000 simulated trials with 50 subjects per arm, observations were made at baseline, several times throughout dosing, and then again in a follow‐up after the dosing period ended. Calculations of CFB that included a BLQ observation would replace that value with half of the LLOQ. Special statistical or clinical considerations like parameter uncertainty, attrition, sample loss, or unmeasured covariates were not in scope for the analysis and were not simulated. While each of these would provide more variability, it was expected that the parameterized noise was sufficient for the present analysis.

Two types of analyses were performed with the model, the first of which was based only on analyzing each single trial data in relation to all other simulations. This analysis considered the relationship between the percentages of strongly or weakly supporting values in the data, CFB and dCFB. For part of the analysis, a VPC exercise is simulated and the top 2.5% of trials with strongly supportive data of a predictive model were selected, then the distribution of those trial data in VPCs for CFB and dCFB is assessed. For this VPC analysis, the proportion of summaries graded as strongly and weakly supportive was considered, in which it is expected that 95% and 50% of values, respectively, will align (overall, given the definitions of these support levels); over‐ and under‐prediction grades were also defined as observed summary values below or above the interquartile ranges of predicted summary values, respectively, thus each has an expected distribution of 25%.

The second type of analysis using the PKPD model also assessed the identifiability of the model. A subset of 300 simulated trials was used to fit the model, which used the true parameter values as initial estimates. The resulting model fits were appraised by ability to minimize successfully, estimating covariance, displaying acceptable shrinkage, and adequate conditioning; these features were selected as they are computationally easy to determine and would be among the key numerical facets an analyst would consider for deciding if a model is appropriate. Model fits that passed these criteria would then undergo similar assessment as in the simulation‐only analysis, but the 1000 simulations generating the reference distributions used the updated parameter values from the fit for each dataset. This refitting analysis was designed to test if the previous results would still hold if the comparator simulations were not generated from the same model as the test data, even if the model still appears to predict the data.

There was no intentional attempt to have the PKPD model match the pre–post simulation (e.g., parameters were not selected to create comparable μ or σ of baselines or percent reduction). While the outcomes assessments were the same for each and similarities can be observed, the pre–post and PKPD simulation assessments were separate methods of considering the problem.

Case example: Psoriasis exposure–response

A model to predict Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) in response to an average drug exposure was recently published. 10 The model used zero‐inflated beta distributions to predict PASI response and then derived PASI threshold responses (those based on percent change from baseline improvement, such as the 50% CFB PASI50). In the interest of simplicity and not using actual clinical data, a trial design was simulated using daily dosing with placebo or 10, 30, and 60 mg of the modeled drug, randomized 2:1:1:2 with 180 patients. The published (“true”) model was used, and a misspecified version of that model was also created. The misspecified model did not use zero inflation for the beta distribution, increased the 50% maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) parameter to 100 ng/mL (it is 53 ng/mL in the true model), and randomly changed the other parameters by a small amount. The simulation script for the true model is available as Supplementary Material.

Using the true and misspecified models, 100,000 trials were simulated. Predictions in each model for PASI raw data, PASI75, placebo‐corrected PASI75 (dPASI75), and dPASI75 at Week 12 were scored by how many binned summaries of the true model data (raw and derived) were within the weakly predictive ranges. For each trial and derivation level, the percentage of summaries within the supportive range for the true model in comparison to the misspecified model was examined.

Software

Simulations and final analyses were all performed in R version 4.2.1. 11 Fitting was done in NONMEM version 7.5.0, 12 with Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM version 5.3.0 13 for running and summarizing a large number of model fits. The NMTRAN control file for one of these model fits is included in Supplementary Material.

It was the case that primary and secondary derived end points being strongly supportive was more likely when the end points one step earlier in the derivation were supportive: strongly supportive raw data were more likely to have strongly supportive CFB data, and likewise supportive CFB data were more likely to have supportive dCFB data. DISCUSSION

The central tendency of the distribution of predicted summaries relative to observed summary statistics is often used to judge the quality of V/NPCs. This analysis demonstrated that V/NPCs for directly or indirectly modeled data vary in their apparent quality by this metric, and there is only a marginal relationship between V/NPC quality and level of derivation for a given trial dataset. The weakness of the relationship or the presence of any relationship may be surprising for various reasons. It is reasonable to expect that well‐predicted raw data observations will be equally well predicted when transformed in a way that combines observations (such as in CFB), but the loss of apparent quality for predictions should also be expected given that the noise of individual measurements is also being combined. As more noise is introduced to those individual data points being used in the computation of derived outcomes, the less relationship centrality of the observed raw data has to the derived outcome centrality (relative to predictions).

Labeling in this analysis intentionally asserts falsely that summary statistics of data more strongly support a model the more centrally aligned those summaries are to comparable summaries from simulations. While indeed there are conventional thresholds from which these summaries should not deviate too far, it is in fact inappropriate to think of it as a continuum favoring centrality, as demonstrated by the results of the analysis. Because it is hypothesized that the model represents the underlying process generating the data, it should thus be expected that half of the summary statistics of binned observations will be within the interquartile range of simulations, 95% will be within the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, and then 5% will be outside. This is true regardless of transformations made to the observed and simulated data, provided they are consistent. More bins, more transformations, more summaries, and more arms will all increase the probability of observing at least one summary statistic outside the supportive range as a result of the multiple comparisons problem. Therefore, aspiration for a model where most of the bins have summaries “strongly” supporting a given model will result in adequate models being discarded more often; that is, the “outcome of interest” for a V/NPC diagnostic is maximal achievement of “weak” support, not the continuous measure of support centrality.

The main examples under investigation in the present analysis were relatively simple and ultimately dictated by normal or lognormal distributions. Skewed distributions, time‐dependent variability, and other probabilistic phenomena can be captured by modeling, so a model that ignores these when they are present would be misspecified. A model misspecified like this could easily appear to support some appropriately binned form of the raw data by V/NPCs but have a trend of poorer predictions in derived outcomes. The goal of this work is not to suggest that all models that appear to predict raw data well are valid for predicting everything that emerges from those data. However, if data and derived values satisfy a priori or conventional thresholds for quality V/NPCs, analysts and stakeholders should not overburden themselves simply because the model does not satisfy stricter post hoc criteria (e.g., central tendency of observed outcomes measures relative to simulations). Indeed, fixing a perceived issue like this encourages bad habits like fitting a change from baseline model dependent on baseline, 14 or forces analysts to prefer simpler exposure–response models that may be flawed in other ways. 15

The psoriasis case in this analysis provides an interesting example where raw data may be predicted incorrectly (with the misspecified model) but still provide similar predictive capacity for a derived outcome that a more refined model would also provide. In this case, the published model 10 would be able to predict PASI50, PASI90, and (because of the zero inflation absent in the misspecified model) PASI100 with similar predictive capacities, which may not be the case for the misspecified model without additional verification. This case illustrates that predictive capacity for a derived outcome should not dictate the assessment of a model adequacy, despite it being important to the overall assessment.

There are many situations where the outcomes of interest for drug development decisions are indirectly predicted by a model. The PASI example is one of many common CFB measures. The choice to examine CFB measures as a derived measurement in this analysis was not arbitrary; in the assessment of PKPD and exposure–response models, it is often a de facto outcome of interest. Myelosuppression was previously discussed in regard to neutropenia predictions from directly modeled neutrophils; this is a type of derived outcome that is not a CFB measurement but rather a grade applied to each observed neutrophil count. Using topotecan as an example from when modeling neutrophils (and other cell counts) was being revolutionized in pharmacometrics, 16 neutropenia could be predicted by a longitudinal‐ordered categorical model 17 or a semi‐mechanistic model of neutrophil count. 18 Both topotecan models have similar number of parameters and can support similar decisions regarding time courses of adverse events, but only the semi‐mechanistic model can predict nadir characteristics and is insensitive to the neutropenia grading scale in use; thus, the semi‐mechanistic model can implicitly support more decisions, but will also require more V/NPCs, risking multiple comparisons errors in assessment of model quality. Noncompartmental analysis parameters are another example of derived values from a set of complex interrogations of the predicted and observed data and are extremely valuable for V/NPCs of a population PK model. 19 Depending on the PK outcome of interest (e.g., maximum concentration, which can be useful to define safety margins), appearance of over‐ or under‐prediction from judging the narrow proximity of observations to the center of simulation distributions could be highly problematic, even if PK time courses are predicted well. It is widely recognized that population PK modeling is typically able to detect covariate effects with greater power (per sample size) than conventional statistical tests of derived exposure metrics, and thus careful assessment of apparent prediction error for derived data is needed.

Frequently concerns arise when a derived outcome is predicted with a bias, even if the raw data are predicted well. It is true that bias and significant trends can arise by random chance, but require more scrutiny than single timepoints or nonsignificant trends. In these cases, suspecting model misspecification would be appropriate and the decision to accept the bias should be made with caution. However, methodology for generating the V/NPCs should also be scrutinized as a cause of the apparent biases and trends, including the need for prediction–correction, or misinterpreting a controlled simulation result as an NPC. 4 , 5

CONCLUSIONS

These results have findings relevant to both modeling strategy and diagnostics. From a strategic perspective, the loss of information when continuous data are converted to categorical data must be considered (e.g., a change from baseline in PASI score of −76% compared to −74% is negligible but makes the former a responder and the latter a nonresponder to treatment). To achieve similar accuracy or statistical power in a study design, the loss of information would need to be compensated by a larger sample size. From a modeling perspective, the full information should be used: the model should be built on the continuous measure, and the composite, bounded or other underlying numeric properties of the data respected, with any derived outcomes calculated in a subsequent step.

In diagnostics, it should be expected that indirectly predicted, derived outcomes will have observations that deviate from the central tendency of simulations even if observations for the directly modeled data are closely aligned with the central tendency; complementary to this, observations for directly modeled data that deviate from the central tendency may have highly central derived data. This is an inevitable result of probability (spending alpha) and should not be seen as necessarily indicating model misspecification. Each summary of data with an associated model is hypothesized to fall within a modeled distribution, and thus the more times that distribution is sampled, the more likely it is to observe at least one value far off‐center. Further work could be done to distinguish concerning bias from expected noise in V/NPCs to relieve concerns when derived observations deviate from predictions.
