Dose–response effects of resistance training on physical function in frail older Chinese adults: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiaoxing Lai, Hongwei Zhu, Zhao Wu, Baoyu Chen, Qian Jiang, Hongdi Du, Xiaopeng Huo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective exercise for the frail elderly has been found to contribute to healthy aging; the corresponding relationship between intensity and volume of exercise and health effects remains unclear. The present study aimed to investigate the dose–response effects of resistance training on muscle strength and physical fitness in frail older adults.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, participants were randomized into seven groups: moderate‐volume low‐intensity, moderate‐volume moderate‐intensity, moderate‐volume high‐intensity, high‐volume low‐intensity, high‐volume moderate‐intensity, high‐volume high‐intensity and routine care, receiving 12 weeks of resistance training of different intensities and volumes of exercise. The outcomes were muscle strength (assessed by ergonomics force gauges) and physical fitness function (assessed by the 6‐min walking test [6MWT], the 30‐s sit‐to‐stand test [30sSTST] and the 8‐foot up‐and‐go test [8‐FUGT]) before and at 6 and 12 weeks of intervention.ResultsA total of 161 participants completed the exercise intervention. There were no significant differences in age, sex, height, body weight and body mass index among the seven groups. The exercise volume of resistance training showed linear relationships with muscle strength of the lower limbs, 30sSTST and 6MWT results and a non‐linear relationship with 8‐FUGT. Resistance training intensity was found to have a linear relationship with muscle strength of the lower limbs and 6MWT and non‐linear relationships with 30sSTST and 8‐FUGT. The mixed linear model analysis revealed that the lower limb muscle strength differed significantly before and during the intervention (W = 8571.5, P adj < 0.001), before and after the intervention (W = 6968, P adj = 0.001) and during and after the intervention (W = 2834.5, P adj < 0.001); that the 6MWT performance differed during and after the intervention (W = 3184, P adj < 0.001); and that the 30sSTST was different between before and during the intervention (W = 2350.5, P adj = 0.012) and between during and after the intervention (W = 2290.5, P adj = 0.045).ConclusionsResistance training was found to be associated with muscle strength and physical fitness in frail older adults in a dose‐dependent manner. High‐intensity resistance training could be more effective for improving the muscle strength of frail older adults, and the improvement of 6MWT performance was even higher. High‐volume resistance training significantly improved muscle strength, with even greater improvement in the 30sSTST and 6MWT performances. Both the intensity and volume of exercise were found to greatly value physical function in frail older adults. Low–moderate‐intensity resistance training and low–moderate‐volume resistance training also had some advantages in terms of safety, efficacy and acceptance in elderly with frailty.Keywords: dose–response, frailty, muscle strength, older adults, physical fitness, resistance training, volume and intensity

Introduction

Frailty can be defined as declined physiological reserve capacity and elevated disease susceptibility among older adults, significantly increasing their incidence of disability, morbidity and mortality. 1 , 2 It may involve the loss of muscle mass and strength, reduced energy and exercise tolerance, cognitive impairment and decreased physiological reserve leading to poor health outcomes and reduced ability to recover from acute stresses, diseases or therapies. 1 , 2 Effective interventions can improve these outcomes or potentially reverse the physical condition of the elderly. 1 , 2 Exercise is one of the most significant interventions to delay frailty. 3 Therefore, engaging in effective exercises is an important intervention that can promote healthy aging in the older population.

A multi‐component exercise that includes resistance training, balance training, aerobic training and other training is recommended by the American Sports Medical Association for older adults. 4 Previous studies demonstrated that resistance training could significantly elevate IGF‐1 (insulin‐like growth factor 1) and IGF‐1R (insulin‐like growth factor 1 receptor) levels and has a stronger effect on preventing sarcopenia than aerobic exercises. 5 , 6 In addition, resistance training could enhance the synthesis of skeletal muscle proteins, activate skeletal muscle satellite cells, increase the cross‐sectional area of types I and II muscle fibres, promote adaptive modifications of the endocrine system and consequently enhance the strength of skeletal muscles. 7 , 8 , 9 The dose related to physical activities refers not only to the total energy consumption and the duration of physical activities but also to various factors relevant to physical activity, such as intensity, volume and frequency of exercises. Investigating the relationship between single‐factor changes in exercise dosage and health benefits could help establish the exact doses leading to health benefits.

It has been reported that even the lowest intensity exercises can reduce the mortality rate in frail older adults. A previous meta‐analysis on the effects of resistance training on the circumference of the quadriceps femoris and flexion strength of the knee joint showed that low‐intensity resistance training and pressure training could enhance the muscle strength and cross‐sectional area of muscle and are suitable for older adults. 10 , 11 , 12 Resistance training in a dose of 39 times over 7 weeks predicted the changes in lean body mass in older adults without involving other training variables. 13 Previous studies demonstrated dose–response relationships between physical exercise dose and health, and exercise could effectively reduce the risk of various diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, colorectal cancer and breast cancer. 14 , 15 However, further investigations are still needed to explore the dose–response relationship between exercise doses and health benefits in the frail older adult cohort.

Recent studies on physical activities and health benefits have shifted from epidemiological surveys to randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Epidemiological surveys can summarize the overall relationship between total physical exercise dose and health benefits, but they cannot deduce the relationship between a single exercise factor, such as intensity, volume, frequency and health benefits. Therefore, experimental studies are required to explore the relationship between exercise dose and health benefits caused by single‐factor changes. In the present study, older adult participants were divided into seven groups according to the volume and intensity of exercise to explore the dose–response relationship between resistance training and muscle strength and physical fitness in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single‐centre randomized controlled study included frail older adults enrolled at a pension agency in Chaoyang District, Beijing, between June and December 2021. The study was conducted and reported according to the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines. The participants were recruited through health education and propaganda posters on the bulletin board of the pension agency. Each eligible participant was fully informed of the study's aims, processes and potential risks. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking Union Medical College Hospital (No. JS‐2264), and all participants signed the informed consent form. The clinical registration number is ChiCTR2000031099.

The inclusion criteria were the following: (1) ≥60 years; (2) volunteered to participate in this study and signed the informed consent form; and (3) met the Fried phenotype criteria for frailty (meet three or more of the five parameters of the Fried frailty phenotype). 16 The exclusion criteria were the following: (1) cognitive impairment (Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE] score < 27 points) and could not cooperate and complete the study; (2) unstable cardiovascular diseases, neuromuscular diseases, musculoskeletal deformity, symptomatic rheumatoid arthritis (RA) or deep venous thrombosis (DVT); (3) regular moderate or intense physical activities (no more than three times per week and no longer than 30 min per exercise); (4) surgery within 1 month; (5) participated in other studies of exercise interventions concurrently; and (6) unsuitable to participate in the research (assessed by Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire [PAR‐Q], ‘Yes’ to at least one of the seven questions of PAR‐Q). In addition, individuals were considered as having dropped out of this study if they (1) quit the study spontaneously; (2) showed poor compliance (<80% of the scheduled exercise times) and did not participate in the training for more than 2 weeks; and (3) showed poor physical conditions that were not suitable for participating in exercises and could not meet the required exercise intensity for more than 3 weeks during the training. For participants who dropped out of the study, the researchers enquired and recorded the reasons and time for quitting.

Sample size

The sample size of this study was estimated according to the following equation 17 :

According to the sample size estimation of randomized block design, the Q value was 4.4. The primary outcome was quadriceps muscle strength. The error of mean square (mS e ) was 9.2 kg from the preliminary experiment, the difference between groups (D) was 5.7 kg, α was 0.05, β was 0.05 and at least 18 patients were to be included in each group. As the dropout rate was estimated at <20%, 23 participants were finally included in each group, and the total sample size of this study was 161.

Randomization

A black‐box method was used for randomization. The process was as follows: Seven sealed envelopes were prepared, each containing a piece of paper. Envelopes were marked as routine care, moderate‐volume low‐intensity, moderate‐volume moderate‐intensity, moderate‐volume high‐intensity, high‐volume low‐intensity, high‐volume moderate‐intensity and high‐volume high‐intensity. A third party (not the researcher) who did not know the envelope's content wrote the numbers from 1 to 7 on the envelopes. The researchers opened the envelopes of the participants and divided them into seven groups according to the markings on the inside of the envelopes.

Intervention

Previous studies on the reduction of skeletal muscles, regimens of resistance training in older adults and exercises with a resistance band 18 , 19 provided theoretical evidence for designing the resistance band exercise strategy. Resistance bands are compact, portable tools that can be used for functional training with a low exercise risk. In order to improve the scope and feasibility of the training strategy, the Thera‐band resistance band was used for resistance training in the present study. The training strategy mainly involved engaging and using the joint muscles while seated on a chair. According to the Guidelines for Exercise and Physical Activity for Americans (Second Edition) issued by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), 20 , 21 a moderate exercise volume was applied for the three sets of resistance training, and a high exercise volume was applied for five sets. Based on the strength of the resistance band, yellow resistance bands (3.0 lb) were considered low intensity, green resistance bands (4.6 lb) were considered moderate intensity and blue resistance bands (5.8 lb) were considered high intensity. The frequency of exercise was three times per week, and the duration of the exercise intervention was 12 weeks. The participants were carefully screened to meet the research needs (325 individuals screened and 161 enrolled), and all mastered the exercise method before the intervention.

The participants were randomized into seven groups (Table 1 ). The exercise mode was resistance training of the knee extensor of the lower limbs. Briefly, a knot was tied in the resistance band, which was then fixed to a stable object (such as a chair) at the height level of the ankle joint. The participants were then asked to sit on the chair, placing both feet into the band loop. Next, they were asked to place both hands on the upper thigh, inhale and while exhaling, lift one leg upwards at the knee (pulling the resistance band) and hold the leg in this position for 2 s. It was not necessary for the participant to lift the leg all the way up to have it horizontal to the floor; it depended on the participant's physical ability. One complete movement lasted for 6–8 s, and the exercise was performed with eight repetitions per group. The participants were allowed to rest for 1–2 min between two groups of exercises. The participants in the routine care group received one exercise‐related health education lesson per week but no specific physical training interventions. The participants were asked to wear sportswear and sneakers during the exercise, and ideally, the exercise had to be performed 1–2 h after eating and not on an empty stomach. The participants were asked to stop the exercises immediately if the following symptoms appeared: pain or discomfort, dizziness, nausea or muscle spasm. The training was supervised by professionals to prevent accidents such as falls. Heart rate and blood pressure variations were monitored before and after training. The subjects were followed up every 2 weeks by the researchers to check the completion of the exercise intervention and during the 12‐week intervention.

Participants' basic characteristics, including age, sex, height, body weight and body mass index (BMI), were also collected.

Outcomes

Muscle strength and physical fitness were measured and recorded for all participants before (on recruitment), during (6 weeks of intervention) and after (12 weeks of intervention) the intervention.

Muscle strength assessment

A portable digital myosthenometer (Series EKE‐500; MARK‐10 Corporation, USA) certified by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) of the United States was used to quantify the muscle strength of the lower limbs (kilograms). The instrument made ergonomics analysis simple and accurate, ideal for strength assessment. The quadriceps femoris is the major lower limb muscle that maintains the knee joint's stability and is vital for walking. Therefore, in this study, the quadriceps femoris muscle strength was measured. The participants were placed in a sitting position with both feet resting on the floor and the knee joint bent at 90°. For the measurement, the participants were required to stretch their knees, and the investigators held the myosthenometer 2 cm below the participant's knee and obtained the muscle strength of the quadriceps femoris. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the average value was calculated. The evaluators had received standardized training and strict examination for measurement methods to ensure the accuracy of the measurement results.

Physical fitness

Physical fitness was examined using the senior fitness test (SFT) developed and improved by Rikli and Jones. 22 As this study mainly focused on lower limb resistance training, the 6‐min walking test (6MWT), the 30‐s sit‐to‐stand test (30sSTST) and the 8‐foot up‐and‐go test (8‐FUGT) were used to reflect lower limb physical function. The 6MWT is a practical test that has gained importance in evaluating the functional status and aerobic endurance of the elderly. The 6MWT was performed along a 30‐m corridor. The participants received an explanation of what was required in the test and were told to walk as far as possible in 6 min without running. After the start signal, the tested participant walked as fast as possible, and the distance walked within 6 min was recorded (under the conditions of being physically comfortable). The 30sSTST was used to assess lower limb muscle strength indirectly. For the 30sSTST, the participants sat in a chair with both feet resting on the floor. After hearing the start signal, the participants stood up and then returned to the sitting position. The total number of sit‐to‐stand completed within 30 s was recorded. The 8‐FUGT was used widely to assess the risk of falls and evaluate balance, gait and strength. For the 8‐FUGT, the participants were sitting in a chair. After hearing the start signal, the participants stood up, walked as fast as possible to the marker 8 feet away and then walked back to sit in the chair. The time (seconds) that the participants required to finish this process was recorded. Before the formal tests, the researcher showed the participants how to do the test correctly, after which the participants did a pre‐test to familiarize themselves with the tests.

Statistical analysis

All the relevant data were archived for intention‐to‐treat (ITT) analysis to reduce the bias in outcomes. R 3.6.3 was used for statistical analysis. As the data showed a skewed distribution, they were described using medians and quartiles. The mixed linear model was used to evaluate the main effect (time and group) and interaction effects of the longitudinal repeated measurements. The Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test was performed for simple effect analysis. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was performed for pairwise comparisons between different groups at the same time point and in the same group at different time points. The Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test was used to compare data at different time points. The Bonferroni test was used to adjust the significance of multiple comparison analyses. Two‐sided P‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 161 older adults who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in this study. The participants were randomized into six intervention groups and the routine care group, with 23 subjects per group (Figure 1 ). None of the participants dropped out during the intervention. There were no significant differences in age, sex, height, body weight, BMI, 30sSTST, 6MWT and 8‐FUGT performances among the seven groups (P > 0.05; Table 2 ).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that muscle strength and 6MWT performance increased significantly more after high‐intensity resistance training than after low‐intensity training. Our results also showed that high‐volume resistance training significantly improved muscle strength among frail older adults, with even greater improvement in the 30sSTST and 6MWT performances. These results indicate a dose–response relationship between the intensity of training and physical function improvements and between the training volume and physical functional performance.

The present study revealed that resistance training could improve muscle strength among frail older adult participants, which is consistent with previous studies. Indeed, a study on lower limb strength in older male adults showed that resistance training with small devices could enhance lower limb muscle strength and stabilize core zone strength. 23 Furthermore, another study showed that resistance training could increase muscle size and strength in older adults. 24 In light of the study results and physiological features of the muscles of older adults, 25 , 26 we postulate that resistance training of the lower limbs might improve muscle strength in frail older adults for the following reasons. First, resistance training of the knee extensor was performed for functional training of the lower limb muscles, consequently improving muscle output. Considering the overall motion profile, the use of resistance bands might enhance active force generation within the quadriceps femoris, gluteus maximus, anterior tibial muscle, gastrocnemius muscle and hamstring muscles, maintain unstable motion of physical gesture to the highest level and lasting for 1–3 s, completing the peak contraction of the muscles, maximize the enhancement of muscle strength and build core strength by maintaining the physical gesture. The lower limbs' muscle strength represents the lower limbs' motion capability and has an important role in human movements. Reducing lower limb muscle strength harms older adults as it increases their risk of falling and reduces their mobility and self‐care ability. 27 Therefore, improving lower limb muscle strength is especially important for frail older adults to maintain mobility.

Physical fitness refers to the ability to safely and independently perform daily activities without experiencing physical fatigue. 28 The 6MWT is used to evaluate cardiopulmonary function, the 30sSTST is used to evaluate lower limb strength and the 8‐FUGT is used to evaluate balance, gait and strength. This study demonstrated that resistance training could significantly improve the physical fitness of frail older adults, which is supported by the literature. A previous study demonstrated that moderate‐ or high‐intensity resistance training could improve physical function in frail older adults. 29 A meta‐analysis of knee arthroplasty patients showed that progressive resistance training for 12 weeks using a resistance band significantly improved lower limb motor control and function. 30 Physical fitness tests such as the timed foot up‐and‐go test, repeated sitting up and step speed not only reflect the muscle endurance of lower limbs but also are associated with the abilities of motion, balance and self‐caring, as well as with medical expenditure. 31 , 32 In the 8‐FUGT, subjects are made to change position from sitting to standing, moving and turning, and standing to sitting. The change from the standing to the sitting position requires the participation of muscle groups responsible for the extension of the hip and knee joints while maintaining physical gesture and control capability. Lower limb muscle strength is also required for moving and turning. Several factors influence the walking capability of older adults, including strength and endurance of lower limbs, ability to balance, joint mobility, and regulatory and control capability of the central nervous system (CNS). Resistance training using a resistance band could activate deep muscle sensory receptors, engage more muscle groups to participate in activities, improve the neurological control of muscles, correct muscle balance, integrate sensory and motion capabilities to promote stability and enhance coordinated exercises and aerobic exercise endurance. 33

This study evaluated the effects of resistance training of three different volumes of exercise on muscle force and physical fitness among frail older adults. The findings showed that high‐volume resistance training significantly improved muscle strength among frail older adults, with even greater improvement in the 30sSTST and 6MWT performances. In older adults, Galvão and Taafe showed that both low‐ and high‐volume resistance exercise protocols improved neuromuscular parameters and physical performance, but greater gains in muscle strength and endurance were observed after high‐volume resistance exercise. 34

The findings of this study also indicated that the resistance training dose exhibited a linear relationship with lower limb muscle strength and performances in the 30sSTST and 6MWT, indicating that higher resistance training doses could improve muscle strength and physical fitness. In addition, older adults could benefit from high‐volume resistance training; a meta‐analysis showed that in older adults, resistance training volume predicted the changes in lean body mass without involving other training variables. 13

The dose–response effects of physical activities on health refer to the causal relationship between the increases in physical activities (exercise dosage) on the variations of specific health indicators (risk factors, diseases, organ functions, organ structures, system functions and quality of life). Previous studies demonstrated that the synthesis of muscle protein and intracellular anabolism pathway could influence the elevation of exercise volume. 35 Although these mechanisms are still unclear, higher exercise volumes could promote proteins' synthesis following exercise recovery. Also, the volume of exercise is an important factor that influences the intracellular processes of muscle anabolism. The literature demonstrated a reversed U‐shaped relationship between total exercise volume and physiological responses, and the exercise effects stop after a certain threshold is reached. 15 The exercise volume is the most easily alterable variable influencing major muscle function, which has important effects on muscle hypertrophy and health. 36 Even in cases where individuals cannot or will not undergo a relatively high intensity of resistance training, training with a relatively high exercise volume still brings significant health benefits.

This study investigated the effects of resistance training of four different levels of intensity (low‐intensity, moderate‐intensity, high‐intensity and routine care groups) on muscle strength and physical fitness in frail older adults, showing that high‐intensity resistance training could better improve the muscle strength of frail older adults, and the improvement of 6MWT performance was even higher. Our findings also showed that exercise intensity of resistance training was linearly correlated to muscle strength and 6MWT performance of frail older adults, indicating that higher intensities of resistance training could improve muscle strength and endurance to a certain extent. Previous studies have suggested that high‐intensity resistance training is more effective than moderate‐ and low‐intensity protocols to improve muscle strength. 37 Appropriate weight‐bearing resistance training could reduce protein synthesis resistance in older adults and help maintain the protein level in skeletal muscles. 7 , 8 Protein loss induced by reduced protein synthesis and elevated catabolism has been considered important mechanisms underlying frailty. 2 , 6 , 26 Relatively high‐intensity strength training could enhance the protein synthesis of skeletal muscles, reduce the apoptotic signals of aging skeletal muscles, prevent myocyte apoptosis that worsens muscle loss and reverse the reduction of skeletal muscle mass and strength in older adults. A study that analysed resistance training and neurological adaption showed that high‐intensity exercises effectively enhanced strength, and the possible pathways could include enhancement of excitability of motor neurons through reflection and hyper‐polarization, the elevation of muscle activation via powerful nerve impulses, increase in synchronization of motor unit activities and enhancement of muscle cooperation during activities. 38 The results of this study did not demonstrate greater improvements in 30sSTST and 8‐FUGT performances after resistance exercise compared with 6MWT performance. The 30sSTST briefly reflected muscle strength and endurance. Our results demonstrated a nearly reversed V‐shaped relationship between exercise intensity and 30sSTST performance, and the exercise effects were no longer observed after a certain threshold was reached. During the 8‐FUGT process, the subjects went through the standing reaction stage, the walking acceleration stage and the deceleration stage, which fully reflected their comprehensive ability of dynamic balance and movement. In the present study, resistance training did not benefit frail older adults' balance and body control.

The present study has some limitations. First, this was a single‐centre study with a limited sample size, which might lead to statistical bias. In addition, this study mainly focused on the effects of resistance training on physical function in frail older adults, while other factors that may have contributed to the improvements in frail elderly, such as psychological factors and cognitive function, were not considered. Finally, although the frailty phenotype, also known as the physical frailty model, is widely accepted in clinic and research, frailty is related to other health domains such as nutrition, psychology, cognition and sociological characteristics.

However, this is the first study that focused on the resistance training dose–effect relationship aiming to explore the appropriate volume and intensity of exercise resistance training for improving muscle strength and physical fitness in frail elderly in China, revealing that exercise intervention is beneficial for frail elderly.

In conclusion, these results indicated a strong dose–response relationship between the intensity of training and physical function improvements (muscle strength and 6MWT performance) and between the volume of training and physical functional performance (muscle strength, 30sSTST and 6MWT performances). The magnitude of the strength gained after high‐intensity or high‐volume resistance training explained much of the exercise's effect on functional performance. Low–moderate‐intensity resistance training and low–moderate‐volume resistance training appeared to be effective in improving functional performance from a physiological perspective. They also appeared to offer some advantages in terms of safety, efficacy and acceptance in frail elderly persons. These findings might be used as a valuable reference for specific recommendations on physical activities, safety and effective physical exercise plans, thus helping to promote healthy aging in the older population. In addition, these findings may affect how resistance exercise is prescribed to frail older adults. Nevertheless, studies with large sample sizes from multiple centres are needed to further investigate the dose–response relationship between exercise and health in older adults.
