Injury fear in mountaineering accidents: a study of Turkish mountaineers’ experiences
Authors: Erdoğan Ekinci, Fatih Mehmet Parlar, Özge Ercan, Nedim Tekin, Begüm Yaşar Sönmez, Ümit Doğan Üstün
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimMountaineering, which involves high levels of risk and challenge, is an extreme sports activity with a deep history, requiring superior physical fitness, mental strength and technical knowledge. The high level of risk inherent in mountaineering also brings with it injuries, which negatively affect athletes’ performance and lead to traumatic experiences that cause anxiety. This research aims to examine in depth the injury concerns of licensed mountaineers.MethodThe present research conducted as a qualitative study with the phenomenological approach and the data were obtained through in-depth interviews conducted face-to-face. In the interviews conducted with 12 professional mountaineers selected with the criterion sampling method, an interview form consisting of semi-structured questions developed by the research team based on Lazarus’ Cognitive-Motivational-Relational Theory was used. The obtained data were analyzed with the content analysis technique.ResultsThe research results reveal that the injury concerns of mountaineers are related to the themes of risk management, psychological resilience, mental strengthening and strategic thinking in the cognitive dimension; amotivation, passion and taking precautions in the motivational dimension; and resistance, environmental support, environmental pressure, developing self-awareness and persuasion in the relational dimension.ConclusionThese results show that the performance of athletes in the Cognitive-Motivational-Relational dimensions is affected by injury fear. Therefore, it shows that injury fear is a critical factor that must be taken into consideration and managed in order to ensure sustainability in mountaineering.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01292-2.

Introduction

Mountaineering is a form of extreme sports that is both physically and mentally demanding and necessitates sophisticated skills because of its deep-rooted history [1]. Currently, millions of individuals participate in a variety of activities, including mountaineering, in the mountains [2]. In developed countries, individuals have a greater amount of leisure time, which enables them to engage in a greater variety of activities, thereby enhancing safety standards and attracting a broader audience interested in mountaineering [3]. Mountaineering, in addition to consciously fortifying the body through physical activity processes, also induces an effect that tests physical limitations. It is crucial for individuals to establish social interaction, communicate their experiences, and comprehend one another in order to survive in challenging mountainous environments [4]. Nevertheless, mountaineers may be subjected to hazardous and life-threatening circumstances while climbing [5].

One could argue that the hazards of mountaineering are associated with its occurrence in a natural environment. The health of the athlete can be impacted by risk factors, particularly low pressure and hypoxia, in this branch, where unusual situations in natural environments can be encountered [6]. The activity of mountaineering is exceedingly hazardous due to numerous risk factors, including the potential for avalanches, mountain sickness, sudden weather events, and the passage through deep crevices in the mountains [7]. Consequently, athletes who engage in mountaineering activities are at a significant risk of experiencing accidents [8]. Mountaineering accidents occur at varying intervals during the activity, as a result of a variety of factors or a combination of factors that contribute to these reasons [9, 10]. In the mountain environment, the prevention of injuries or the ability to survive them with damage is contingent upon factors such as physical conditions, limited access to emergency medical facilities, and harsh weather conditions. Morbidity and mortality rates are elevated by climbing-related injuries [11]. A high altitude exposure, particularly changes in reasoning, emotional processes, and motor skill functions, can result in mood and performance disorders when traveling or climbing in mountainous areas [12].

The risk of certain mental health problems, such as high altitude, transient psychosis, and post-traumatic stress disorder, may be elevated by the individual characteristics of mountain climbing, despite the generally low prevalence of mental health problems in mountaineers [13]. In this context, mountaineering is a significant activity that induces physical, cognitive, and emotional discomfort, as well as the development of a variety of anxiety states [4]. Mountaineers frequently experience feelings of confusion and conflict as a result of their voluntary participation in hazardous environments that are replete with life-threatening obstacles [14]. Nevertheless, mountaineers who are motivated by intense arousal, anxiety, and momentary pleasure may opt to ascend mountains solely to experience these emotions [15], which exacerbates the likelihood of accidents and injuries. The psychological state of anxiety is one of the causes of injuries in mountaineering accidents [16]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that athletes with elevated anxiety levels are more susceptible to injury than those with lower anxiety levels [17–20]. Anxiety, particularly the type that arises as a consequence of sports injuries, is broadly defined as a vague state of fear or anxiety that is present in various sports [21]. It is also recognized that anxiety can be effective in the prevention, occurrence, rehabilitation, and return to sports of sports injuries [22]. Despite the widespread belief that physical preparation and conditioning can prevent the majority of sports injuries, psychological factors are also at play in the development of these injuries [23].

The participants place a significant value on numerous environmental, personal, and psychological factors in the mountains [24]. Post-traumatic emotional states, including anxiety, stress, and fear, may result from accidents and injuries that occur during mountaineering activities [25, 26]. In order to comprehend the impact of the hazardous nature of mountaineering and the stressful environment on the emotional states of individuals, it is crucial to investigate the manner in which emotions emerge as a psychological response. Emotions are the emotional states that arise as a result of an individual’s assessment of the significance of an event in relation to their overall well-being [27]. Lazarus [28] contended that emotions are the result of a specific stimulus and that it is challenging to elucidate an individual’s general identity and their emotional responses to specific events. The Cognitive-Motivational-Relational Theory (CMRT), which was developed by Lazarus, is a theory that is frequently employed in research on the emotional states of athletes. It is contended by this theory that cognitive evaluations and fundamental relational themes are critical in the development of athletes’ emotions [29]. In order to establish the cognitive-motivational-relational characteristics of all emotions in a sports environment, Lazarus [30] categorized the emotions that an athlete reveals during an activity as positive (relief, happiness, pride) and negative (anger, anxiety, guilt, shame). It is widely recognized that negative emotions are not always detrimental to sports activities, and positive emotions are not always advantageous [31]. CMRT, developed by Lazarus, was founded upon two explicit objectives. These are: to integrate the Process-Oriented Theory of Stress, Appraisal, and Coping developed by Folkman et al. [32], and to handle the results of past psychological studies on emotion and appraisal, along with their evaluations, independently of unrelated components such as causal attributions and action tendencies [33]. CMRT includes a perspective in which individuals and environments mutually and dynamically determine each other, and in this context, emotions mediated by appraisal establish the causal connection that links them [30]. In this regard, they named the cognitive representation of the person–environment relationship underlying each emotion as relational meaning, and they defined it at two levels of analysis. The first level is the definition of specific judgments individuals make when evaluating relational harms and benefits. They identified six appraisal components; two of these are related to primary appraisal, and the remaining four are associated with secondary appraisal [34].

It can be said that experiences such as anxiety, fear, risk, etc. that occur in mountaineering activities are compatible with the theoretical process revealed by CMRT with the reaction of individuals to their emotional states. Lazarus [30] defines the reactions of individuals to the situations that arise in their environment as cognitive, motivational and relational. In this respect, CMRT is the process of interpreting how meaningful the emotional reactions of the individual in uncertain and stressful environments are for the individual and how he/she copes with this situation [33]. In this context, mountaineering is an important type of sport that involves high risks, unexpected conditions, requires physical and psychological strength [35], and elicits different emotional states in climbers [36]. In these challenging environments, how mountaineers react to the threats they face, their coping strategies against these threats and the resulting emotions can be evaluated on the basis of CMRT [37]. In this context, the research is very suitable to be examined in terms of the evaluation processes of CMRT in determining the emotional experiences that emerge in mountaineering. In addition, it is important in terms of explaining how the cognitive processes in the CMRT emerge with emotional states such as anxiety, fear, and ricochet. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the impact of anxiety on the decision-making processes, perceptions of safety, and social interactions of athletes in the context of high-risk sports, such as mountaineering. An in-depth analysis of the factors that influence the anxiety levels of mountaineers in relation to accident concerns has the potential to facilitate the development of coping strategies that can be used to manage the anxiety levels of mountaineers after accidents. It is believed that this information can be beneficial in assisting individuals who are currently or plan to participate in mountaineering sports in managing potential risks.

This study makes a significant contribution to the literature by analyzing emotional experiences in high-risk outdoor sports such as mountaineering, particularly in terms of anxiety levels, risk perception, and responses to traumatic situations. While existing studies generally explain mountaineering accidents through physiological, technical, or environmental factors, this research examines the determining role of psychological processes in the occurrence of accidents and in the behavior of mountaineers. In this context, the study is unique in that it addresses the role of emotions in mountaineering within a theoretical framework, focuses on cognitive appraisal processes, and offers an original perspective aimed at understanding the psychological factors that may lead to accidents. In summary, this study was undertaken to examine the anxiety levels, stress factors, and coping strategies that are employed in mountaineering. Accordingly, the aim of this study is to examine the emotional experiences of Turkish mountaineers in high-risk mountain environments particularly their levels of anxiety, perceptions of risk, and responses to these situations within the framework of Lazarus’ CMRT, and to explore the relationship between these processes and injury-related anxiety.

Accordingly, the main research question of this study is: “What are the injury-related fear experiences of Turkish mountaineers during mountaineering accidents?” The following sub-questions have been formulated to support this primary question:

What types of emotional experiences do mountaineers have in high-risk climbing environments?How does anxiety experienced during mountaineering reflect on individuals’ experiences and decision-making processes?How do mountaineers assess the risks present in mountain environments, and how do these assessments shape their emotional responses?

Method

The study favored qualitative research methods due to their capacity to capture perceptions and phenomena in their natural and authentic contexts [38, 39]. Phenomenology, while familiar, provides a robust framework for research aimed at exploring phenomena whose complete significance remains elusive, was employed as the study design [39].

Research sample

The study employed criterion sampling, a purposeful sampling method that facilitates an in-depth examination of situations believed to contain rich information. The criterion sampling method involves examining all instances that fulfill a specified set of predetermined criteria [39]. The criteria for participant selection in this study included: possession of a mountaineering license, a minimum of 5 years of climbing experience, and having ascended at least 10 peaksAthletes meeting these criteria were contacted, and interviews were scheduled with those who consented to participate in the study voluntarily. According to Patton [40] determining sample size in qualitative research is not subject to strict rules; it exhibits flexibility depending on key factors such as the purposeful sampling strategy chosen in line with the research purpose, the quality of the data obtained, and the level of information saturation achieved. The study by Guest et al. [41] provides an important reference point regarding the principle of information saturation, which underlies this approach. This study empirically demonstrated that thematic saturation in qualitative research can generally be achieved with a relatively small number of participants, such as 6 to 12 interviews. This finding supports why, in the qualitative paradigm, smaller samples that provide rich and in-depth data are considered sufficient and meaningful, rather than large samples.

Data collection tool

The study’s data collection tool comprises two components. The initial section presents data regarding participants’ age, gender, discipline, years of experience, number of summits, and injuries. In the second part, a semi-structured interview form was utilized, created by the research team in accordance with Lazarus’ Cognitive-Motivational-Relational Theory [28]. The form comprises a total of six questions, with two questions allocated to each dimension. The data collection tool was particularly designed for this study and has not been utilized in any prior research. To ensure the internal validity of the measurement tool, feedback was solicited from an expert in qualitative research and an experienced mountaineer, followed by a pilot interview with an athlete to finalize the data collection instrument.

Validity and reliability

To ensure the validity and reliability of the research data, the techniques of “Credibility,” “Transferability,” “Consistency,” and “Confirmability,” commonly employed in qualitative research, were utilized as a foundation [38, 39, 42]. To enhance the study’s credibility, techniques such as expert review and participant confirmation were employed. The data obtained were analyzed by experts proficient in qualitative research methods, and the accuracy and consistency of the comments were assessed. The interview transcripts were provided to the participants for validation of the accuracy of the data in representing their experiences. To ensure data transferability, participants with direct relevance to the research topic and substantial knowledge were selected through purposeful sampling. The objective was to develop a comprehensive data set that would enhance the applicability of the findings in analogous contexts. To evaluate data consistency, intercoder reliability was determined using the Miles & Huberman [43] formula “Consensus/(Consensus + Disagreement)x100” a method commonly employed in qualitative research. The analysis yielded an agreement of 87%. To ensure the confirmability of the findings, the researchers systematically archived the raw data obtained from the interviews and the analysis notes. The archiving process facilitates the transparent presentation of the evidence underlying the findings, enabling evaluation by other researchers.

Data collection procedure

Data were obtained from licensed mountaineers via semi-structured interviews conducted in person by the research team. The researchers conducting the interviews possessed training in qualitative research methods, particularly in interview techniques, and had experience in managing the face-to-face interview process. The interviews were conducted privately with each participant in a quiet, distraction-free office environment to ensure comfort. During the interviews, a voice recorder was utilized with the explicit consent of the volunteers to ensure practicality, as well as the archiving and confirmability of the raw data. Participants were guaranteed that the audio recordings would be accessed solely by the research team and that their identities would remain confidential. Prior to the commencement of the interviews, participants received comprehensive information regarding the study’s purpose, scope, and anticipated duration. They were informed of the voluntary nature of their participation, and each participant signed an informed consent/assent form. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without the necessity of providing a rationale. The data collection phase spanned a total of nine days. An interview protocol was employed to standardize the interviews, with all researchers adhering to this protocol. The research team promptly converted the audio recordings into plain text within a computer environment, preparing them for content analysis. During transcription, measures were implemented to guarantee that the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, preserving the integrity of the participants’ statements. Upon completion of the transcriptions, anonymization was conducted to safeguard the identities of the participants.

Data analyses

The analysis of the data employed the technique of content analysis. Content analysis involves the systematic summarization, classification, comparison, and qualitative explanation of texts or other content types through scientific methods [44]. The data collected via face-to-face interviews were manually analyzed by the research team using Microsoft Excel, resulting in the identification of sub-themes and codes. The coding team comprised researchers with prior experience in qualitative research, proficiency in qualitative data analysis, and a record of academic publications utilizing this methodology. To enhance the reliability of the coding process, a field expert, independent of the research, matched codes and themes for the reliability analysis following the final coding. The coding utilized pre-determined dimensions based on Lazarus’s Cognitive-Motivational-Relational theory, which served as a model for the research. Subsequently, the analysis was enhanced by incorporating additional sub-themes and codes derived from the data. The analysis of themes and codes considered both the frequency and significance of participants’ statements.

Ethics

The Ethics Committee of the Artvin Çoruh Univerity approved the data collection tool and research protocol utilized in this study. The study included volunteers, who received comprehensive information regarding the purpose, process, risks, and benefits prior to data collection. An informed consent form was subsequently read and signed. All collected data are securely stored and accessible solely by the research team. The publication of the research results did not include any information that could disclose the identity of the participants. The researchers commit to adhering to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the Personal Data Protection Act.

Risk management

The fear related to injury experienced by mountaineers during ascents is associated with increased risks and diminished performance. Cognitive processes significantly influence emotional reactions.

“Climbing a mountain requires a calm mindset, as anxiety and intrusive thoughts can negatively impact both the individual and the overall climbing experience. Psychology plays a crucial role in assessing potential injury risks based on the difficulty of the route.” (K2).

“Climbing a mountain requires a calm mindset, as anxiety and intrusive thoughts can negatively impact both the individual and the overall climbing experience. Psychology plays a crucial role in assessing potential injury risks based on the difficulty of the route.” (K2).

A participant noted that risk analysis and mitigation constitute a strategic approach. The endeavor to eliminate risks entirely and the choice to retreat when warranted indicate that the mountaineers operate with deliberation and strategic consideration. The assertion is made that retreating is not indicative of weakness, but rather a strategic approach.

“Mountaineering is governed by specific rules. In assessing risk, the initial focus is on determining the feasibility of eliminating the risk entirely. If we are unable to proceed, we will retreat if the risk is significant.” (K4).

“Mountaineering is governed by specific rules. In assessing risk, the initial focus is on determining the feasibility of eliminating the risk entirely. If we are unable to proceed, we will retreat if the risk is significant.” (K4).

Measures implemented to mitigate injury risk reflect a cognitive assessment process. Preventive behavior underscores the importance of safety at each stage, highlighting the necessity of technical knowledge and experience.

“We implement all necessary measures to prevent potential injuries. We ascend with the expectation of achieving optimal performance through the most dependable methods.” (K12).

“We implement all necessary measures to prevent potential injuries. We ascend with the expectation of achieving optimal performance through the most dependable methods.” (K12).

Risk management is regarded as an essential component of mountaineering. Risk analysis incorporating cognitive processes encompasses various dimensions, including psychological, analytical, and technical thinking. Mountaineers engage in deliberate risk analysis, implement precautions, and withdraw when deemed necessary.

Psychological resilience

The significance of both physical and psychological preparation for high-risk sports such as mountaineering is highlighted. Assessing psychological readiness prior to engaging in the activity demonstrates the relationship between cognitive processes and subsequent actions.

“Participation in an activity is inappropriate if one’s mental state or psychological readiness is lacking. To engage in the activity, it is insufficient to possess good physical condition; a positive mental state is also essential.” (K8).

“Participation in an activity is inappropriate if one’s mental state or psychological readiness is lacking. To engage in the activity, it is insufficient to possess good physical condition; a positive mental state is also essential.” (K8).

Self-awareness is a crucial factor in the psychological evaluation of oneself. Understanding one’s own limits and abilities facilitates more effective risk management. Furthermore, psychological preparation is observed to influence motivation.

“In mountaineering, it is essential to prepare psychologically and possess a deep self-awareness.” (K5).

“In mountaineering, it is essential to prepare psychologically and possess a deep self-awareness.” (K5).

Participants engage in cognitive evaluation through the recollection of their past experiences. Their positive self-talk enhances motivation by leveraging strength from prior successful experiences.

“For instance, I often reflect on this during walks or at work when I encounter a critical juncture; a fall could result in disability or death. I reflect on my current actions and affirm my capability to accomplish this task. I have encountered a similar situation previously and successfully navigated it; therefore, I assert my capability to do so again.” (K1).

“For instance, I often reflect on this during walks or at work when I encounter a critical juncture; a fall could result in disability or death. I reflect on my current actions and affirm my capability to accomplish this task. I have encountered a similar situation previously and successfully navigated it; therefore, I assert my capability to do so again.” (K1).

The mountaineers effectively manage challenging conditions through psychological preparation and self-awareness. The integration of prior experiences with cognitive processes enhances motivation. Psychological resilience is essential for survival and success in mountaineering. Enhancing both physical and mental strength is essential during the preparation process. Furthermore, the development of self-confidence, self-awareness, and the ability to sustain motivation during challenging circumstances assist individuals in managing risky situations.

Mental strengthening

The fear regarding injury prompts mountaineers to adopt more cautious and serious strategies. Conscious management of fear, rather than its complete eradication, enhances safety.

“The concern of injury prompts a more serious consideration of the situation. I rarely forget the sensation of that fear of injury. In critical situations, this thought frequently occupies my mind.” (K1).

“The concern of injury prompts a more serious consideration of the situation. I rarely forget the sensation of that fear of injury. In critical situations, this thought frequently occupies my mind.” (K1).

Past successes and experiences contribute to self-confidence in facing future challenges, while teamwork serves a supportive psychological function. The participant articulated this scenario, which enhances motivation, in the following manner:

“Past experiences provide me with the greatest benefits. My continued survival and ability to engage in perilous and challenging activities indicate that progress is being made, and I am motivated by the expectation of future success. The team is crucial, as the individuals with whom we collaborate hold significant value.” (K6).

“Past experiences provide me with the greatest benefits. My continued survival and ability to engage in perilous and challenging activities indicate that progress is being made, and I am motivated by the expectation of future success. The team is crucial, as the individuals with whom we collaborate hold significant value.” (K6).

Team members who value and exchange each other’s ideas foster a trusting environment during mountaineering activities, which enhances participants’ preparedness and alleviates fear.

“During climbing activities, I value the perspectives of my peers present. Given the potential risks involved, it is imperative that we implement appropriate precautions. Consequently, I experience minimal anxiety. I experience minimal emotional anxiety; I approach the climb well-prepared and confident in my abilities.” (K7).

“During climbing activities, I value the perspectives of my peers present. Given the potential risks involved, it is imperative that we implement appropriate precautions. Consequently, I experience minimal anxiety. I experience minimal emotional anxiety; I approach the climb well-prepared and confident in my abilities.” (K7).

Mental strength encompasses strategies employed by mountaineer to address challenges, regulate anxiety, and derive lessons from adversities. It seems to enhance long-term psychological resilience through the application of team dynamics.

Strategic thingking

Trusting the team during mountaineering activities enhances psychological comfort in strategic decision-making. Mountaineering, often perceived as an individual sport, is most safely conducted within a robust team framework.

Mountaineering occurs in extreme conditions; therefore, a stronger team and trusted companions enhance comfort during the climb. “If an incident occurs here, you state, they can transport me away.” (K5).

Mountaineering occurs in extreme conditions; therefore, a stronger team and trusted companions enhance comfort during the climb. “If an incident occurs here, you state, they can transport me away.” (K5).

Strategic thinking influences both individual performance and team dynamics. Understanding the team’s competencies regarding their roles and responsibilities in the event of potential risks, including identifying individuals capable of managing injuries, is essential for forming an effective team. This should be integrated with the leader’s strategic thinking and planning capabilities.

“The composition of the climbing team is significant; while we assess injuries on an individual basis, it is essential to consider whether the team can accommodate an injured member. As the team leader, can your team support you? These factors must also be considered.” (K2).

“The composition of the climbing team is significant; while we assess injuries on an individual basis, it is essential to consider whether the team can accommodate an injured member. As the team leader, can your team support you? These factors must also be considered.” (K2).

Strategic thinking enables the mountaineers to make decisions that prioritize safety and efficiency for both himself and his team. In high-risk sports like mountaineering, individuals must depend on both their personal skills and the strength and organization of their team. An effective strategic thinking process necessitates individual preparation alongside a thorough analysis of team dynamics.

Amotivation

Awareness of risks may lead to demotivation and fear. The potential for minor errors to result in significant harm and repercussions is a scenario that is frequently evaluated and can occasionally influence motivation. A participant stated the following regarding this subject.

“Mountaineering is a sport that inherently involves significant risks. The structure of this sport is markedly distinct from that of others. Your life is currently in a state of direct threat. An insignificant error may yield remarkable outcomes. Consequently, we have several concerns.” (K6).

“Mountaineering is a sport that inherently involves significant risks. The structure of this sport is markedly distinct from that of others. Your life is currently in a state of direct threat. An insignificant error may yield remarkable outcomes. Consequently, we have several concerns.” (K6).

Mountaineering is characterized by a significant criterion that sets it apart from other sports disciplines. The decline in an athlete’s performance can lead to a lack of athletic success, and the inability to secure a medal may result in critical situations. In mountaineering, even a minor error, negligence, or mishap can lead to life-threatening consequences. Psychological issues following an accident can lead to significant declines in motivation for both the individual and their team members.

“A friend experienced an accident. At that time, we found ourselves in a psychologically challenging situation, leading us to suspend the activity for that day and defer it to the following day. I reflected on my presence in this situation following the accident.” (K8).

“A friend experienced an accident. At that time, we found ourselves in a psychologically challenging situation, leading us to suspend the activity for that day and defer it to the following day. I reflected on my presence in this situation following the accident.” (K8).

Mountaineers may encounter fear stemming from the inherent risks of the sport, potentially diminishing their motivation. Moreover, traumatic events, including accidents, can influence individuals’ motivation.

Passion

The risks associated with mountaineering are often outweighed by the enjoyment and significance it provides. Anxiety and fear are inherent in the process; however, they do not hinder one’s ability to persist in climbing.

“The presence of fear and worry does not inhibit my progress. The numerous advantages suggest that this activity transcends the definition of a sport, categorizing it as something distinctly different. The struggle for survival serves as a primary motivation for pleasure.” (K10).

“The presence of fear and worry does not inhibit my progress. The numerous advantages suggest that this activity transcends the definition of a sport, categorizing it as something distinctly different. The struggle for survival serves as a primary motivation for pleasure.” (K10).

Risk is inherent in the sport, and mountaineers acknowledge this fact. Mountaineers assert that the risks associated with their activity are comparable to those encountered in everyday life, despite the presence of hazardous situations in other sports.

“You acknowledge this from the outset. Other sports also present significant risks. Is there a growing concern among individuals regarding the risk of a dumbbell falling on them in the gym? No.” (K12).

“You acknowledge this from the outset. Other sports also present significant risks. Is there a growing concern among individuals regarding the risk of a dumbbell falling on them in the gym? No.” (K12).

Mountaineers perceive this sport as more than merely a physical endeavor. Due to their affinity for nature, individuals integrate and admire it, thereby accepting mountaineering as an integral aspect, notwithstanding the associated risks. It is evident that they possess a significant attachment to this sport.

“Every sport entails inherent risks. Riding a bike involves inherent risks. Mountaineering presents inherent risks. We engage in sports while acknowledging the associated risk of injury. The admiration and affection for nature that drives us is something I cannot give up.” (K7).

“Every sport entails inherent risks. Riding a bike involves inherent risks. Mountaineering presents inherent risks. We engage in sports while acknowledging the associated risk of injury. The admiration and affection for nature that drives us is something I cannot give up.” (K7).

Passion significantly influences mountaineers’ commitment to the sport and sustains their motivation in the face of inherent risks. This commitment is underpinned by factors including intrinsic motivation, the normalization of risk perception, and an appreciation for nature. Mountaineering serves as both a sport and a lifestyle, representing a quest for personal meaning.

Taking precautions

Understanding the physical and psychological capabilities of mountaineers and responding appropriately to their competencies maintains their motivation consistently. The precautions implemented prior to the activity mitigate concerns related to injury or accident, thereby sustaining motivation levels.

“If a route poses a risk of injury or accident, I avoid it. I avoid routes that exceed my capabilities.” (K1).

“If a route poses a risk of injury or accident, I avoid it. I avoid routes that exceed my capabilities.” (K1).

Modifying or discarding a pre-established route when required enhances safety in the activity. Adaptation and flexibility are critically important for healthy mountaineering activities.

“One should avoid entering a situation where the outcome is unclear, as changes along the way may lead to discouragement. It is necessary to progress regardless. Modifying the route and adjusting the target contribute to overall health.” (K10).

“One should avoid entering a situation where the outcome is unclear, as changes along the way may lead to discouragement. It is necessary to progress regardless. Modifying the route and adjusting the target contribute to overall health.” (K10).

The significance of retreat is emphasized, as is the significance of determination. Risk assessment and survival awareness are fundamentally linked.

“In the event of an injury, concerns may arise regarding the ability to return to the field subsequently. Mountaineering can test one’s limits and present extreme challenges; however, if safety is compromised and there is a risk of injury or death, it is imperative to retreat.” (K2).

“In the event of an injury, concerns may arise regarding the ability to return to the field subsequently. Mountaineering can test one’s limits and present extreme challenges; however, if safety is compromised and there is a risk of injury or death, it is imperative to retreat.” (K2).

Deliberate choices made by mountaineers enhance their survival probabilities by mitigating unnecessary risks. Self-awareness, adaptability, and the capacity to withdraw when required are essential strategies in this process. Mountaineering involves not only courage but also the necessity for deliberate risk management.

Resistance

Rather than disregarding the families’ concerns, it is essential to actively address them. The focus is on reducing anxiety and fostering trust through the dissemination of information. The ability to remain unaffected by the negative thoughts of individuals possessing incomplete or incorrect information demonstrates the resistance of mountaineers in the face of external influences.

“Families often express disapproval. However, if I complete all the required tasks, it does not impact me. When inquiring about potential outcomes, rather than responding with a definitive no, the precautions I implement and the persuasive discussions I engage in also serve to alleviate their concerns. The negative opinions of individuals lacking knowledge in mountaineering do not significantly impact me.” (K12).

“Families often express disapproval. However, if I complete all the required tasks, it does not impact me. When inquiring about potential outcomes, rather than responding with a definitive no, the precautions I implement and the persuasive discussions I engage in also serve to alleviate their concerns. The negative opinions of individuals lacking knowledge in mountaineering do not significantly impact me.” (K12).

Participants contend that risk is intrinsic to mountaineering, leading to the adoption of precautions as a fundamental reflex. When individuals believe that risk perception is subject to change, they remain unaffected by the negative emotions present in the environment.

“It does not have an impact on me. Numerous incidents are encountered. Recognizing a danger necessitates the implementation of precautions. While driving, one considers the approach to an intersection, reduces acceleration, and contemplates various scenarios in anticipation of potential vehicle encounters. We understand the potential risks associated with mountainous environments.” (K11).

“It does not have an impact on me. Numerous incidents are encountered. Recognizing a danger necessitates the implementation of precautions. While driving, one considers the approach to an intersection, reduces acceleration, and contemplates various scenarios in anticipation of potential vehicle encounters. We understand the potential risks associated with mountainous environments.” (K11).

External stimuli are shown to have no impact on an individual’s motivation. Motivation is characterized as an entirely internal process, with individuals making decisions independently of external influences.

“Mountaineering is perceived as hazardous, prompting warnings for caution and similar advisories. This situation does not compel you to retreat. This does not concern me. My motivation is inherently self-derived.” (K7).

“Mountaineering is perceived as hazardous, prompting warnings for caution and similar advisories. This situation does not compel you to retreat. This does not concern me. My motivation is inherently self-derived.” (K7).

Mountaineers’ responses to negative comments, concerns, and environmental pressures are observed. They are capable of making decisions independently of external influences, evaluating risks within a logical framework, and maintaining resilience due to their intrinsic motivation.

Environmental factors

Family support is recognized as a significant factor that enhances an individual’s psychological resilience. The family, while concerned, remains supportive rather than obstructive. This support enhances the individual’s strength and facilitates the confident implementation of their decisions.

“My family is concerned about me, yet they do not impose restrictions. Their support enhances my strength.” (K10).

“My family is concerned about me, yet they do not impose restrictions. Their support enhances my strength.” (K10).

Some participants convert criticism into motivation. Criticism and societal prejudices can serve to enhance motivation in certain individuals.

“It increases my motivation. Mountaineering presents challenges that are difficult to comprehend. However, their criticism and remarks such as “you’ll fall and die, what are you doing in the mountains with so many people” did not have a detrimental impact on me. Conversely, it increased my ambition.” (K3).

“It increases my motivation. Mountaineering presents challenges that are difficult to comprehend. However, their criticism and remarks such as “you’ll fall and die, what are you doing in the mountains with so many people” did not have a detrimental impact on me. Conversely, it increased my ambition.” (K3).

Participants who listen to the experiences of others demonstrate increased caution and the ability to learn from those lessons rather than resorting to resignation. Emotional responses, such as “shuddering” and “feeling sad,” serve as stimuli that prompt the climber to engage in conscious action.

“Individuals often experience discomfort and sadness upon hearing negative information. One learns from this situation and endeavors to act with greater awareness.” (K10).

“Individuals often experience discomfort and sadness upon hearing negative information. One learns from this situation and endeavors to act with greater awareness.” (K10).

Family support serves as a significant factor that enhances the strength of participants. Criticism appears to enhance motivation among certain participants. Negative experiences and narratives do not deter mountaineers from their pursuits; rather, they enhance their awareness and caution.

Social pressure

Mountaineers mitigate external pressure by prioritizing safety precautions.

“We provide reassurance, indicating that there are no issues and that security measures are being implemented. In this regard, it does not provide any positive or negative contribution.” (K7).

“We provide reassurance, indicating that there are no issues and that security measures are being implemented. In this regard, it does not provide any positive or negative contribution.” (K7).

Peer pressure is observed to have an initial negative impact; however, mountaineers develop strategies to mitigate this pressure. These strategies include communicating with their families about their activities in a more light-hearted manner, employing softening expressions, concealing the activity, or providing alternative explanations.

“This situation has a detrimental impact on us; however, we have adapted to it over time. What are the next steps? We visit the mountains for camping rather than climbing. To alleviate concerns among our relatives and elders.” (K2).

“This situation has a detrimental impact on us; however, we have adapted to it over time. What are the next steps? We visit the mountains for camping rather than climbing. To alleviate concerns among our relatives and elders.” (K2).

Family support is contingent upon the mountaineer’s confidence in personal control and the precautions taken, rather than direct intervention. The mountaineer’s experience significantly influences the family’s adaptation process.

“Maintaining control on the mountain enhances safety. My family is accustomed to my mountaineering activities, which I have engaged in for several years. My mother occasionally makes remarks. It is understood that my confidence in my actions will prevent any issues.” (K11).

“Maintaining control on the mountain enhances safety. My family is accustomed to my mountaineering activities, which I have engaged in for several years. My mother occasionally makes remarks. It is understood that my confidence in my actions will prevent any issues.” (K11).

Mountaineers employ various strategies to manage fear and social pressure, including informing, softening the situation, developing habits, and framing the context. Social pressure diminishes over time as familiarity and experience with the environment increase. Activities described using less intense terminology mitigate the situation; both experience and self-reliance appear to possess a capacity for reducing pressure.

Self-awareness

When contemplating their obligations to their families, the concerns of their families persist in their thoughts. Nonetheless, it is underscored that this differs from broader environmental issues and is directly pertinent to their individual areas of responsibility.

“If I were to fall from this precarious location and sustain injuries, I wonder who would heed my mother’s concerns… such thoughts arise, yet not those pertaining to the external environment.” Only the most pertinent words regarding the risks I may encounter owing to our responsibilities are recalled; the others normally do not surface in my thoughts.” (K3).

“If I were to fall from this precarious location and sustain injuries, I wonder who would heed my mother’s concerns… such thoughts arise, yet not those pertaining to the external environment.” Only the most pertinent words regarding the risks I may encounter owing to our responsibilities are recalled; the others normally do not surface in my thoughts.” (K3).

Negative external remarks are counterbalanced by the enhancement of individual self-awareness. A mountaineer aware of his own abilities disregards the unfavorable judgments of others. They even choose not to disclose their mountaineering experiences. One participant articulated it as follows.

“Individuals unfamiliar with my personal capabilities and experience, who lack knowledge of my potential, criticize me. They assert that you will sustain injuries or experience an accident. I do not disclose to everyone the specifics of our activities, such as our ascent or the accomplishments we achieved. I typically refrain from disclosing my actions to anyone who have not undertaken this profession. Because they can occasionally have adverse effects on individuals.” (K1).

“Individuals unfamiliar with my personal capabilities and experience, who lack knowledge of my potential, criticize me. They assert that you will sustain injuries or experience an accident. I do not disclose to everyone the specifics of our activities, such as our ascent or the accomplishments we achieved. I typically refrain from disclosing my actions to anyone who have not undertaken this profession. Because they can occasionally have adverse effects on individuals.” (K1).

Self-awareness enables individuals to identify their limitations and comprehend their management of external influences. Family responsibilities and an understanding of their own capacities enhance self-awareness. Individuals also learn to disregard negative opinions from others and choose to share their experiences solely with those who can comprehend them.

Persuasion

The experiences of others should be considered, as they serve as a reference to prevent errors in analogous situations. Personal experiences and those of others facilitate the persuasion process by enhancing knowledge transfer and awareness of risks.

“It is essential to consider their experiences; thus, when encountering a similar situation, one should reflect and communicate to oneself and peers the potential consequences of such actions. It is essential to consider a wide range of experiences. This enhances your awareness.” (K9).

“It is essential to consider their experiences; thus, when encountering a similar situation, one should reflect and communicate to oneself and peers the potential consequences of such actions. It is essential to consider a wide range of experiences. This enhances your awareness.” (K9).

Learning from mistakes establishes a balance between risk factors and necessary precautions within the framework of cause-effect relationships. Error analyses not only promote future caution but also serve as a persuasion strategy by raising awareness of the issue among others.

“It is essential to recognize areas requiring increased caution and to acknowledge the potential for flexible or open errors in the event of injury. It is essential to focus on this aspect during the process. This action is necessary. This indicates an increase in our foresight to implement additional precautions.” (K3).

“It is essential to recognize areas requiring increased caution and to acknowledge the potential for flexible or open errors in the event of injury. It is essential to focus on this aspect during the process. This action is necessary. This indicates an increase in our foresight to implement additional precautions.” (K3).

Mountaineers can persuade themselves and others by leveraging both personal and shared experiences. Elements such as experience sharing, awareness development, and foresight acquisition are significant in this process.

Discussion

This study conducted a thorough examination of injury fear in professional mountaineering. This section discusses the findings from the study conducted within the framework of Lazarus’ Cognitive-Motivational-Relational theory, aligning with the main theme and sub-themes.

The cognitive dimension reveals significant findings regarding the impact of injury fear on cognitive processes, particularly in domains such as risk management, psychological resilience, mental strength, and strategic thinking. Data derived from participants’ experiences indicate that mountaineering functions as both a physical activity and a catalyst for diverse cognitive processes, significantly influencing individuals’ thinking styles, decision-making mechanisms, and problem-solving capabilities.

The study reveals that mountaineers acquire the ability to analyze, evaluate, and manage risks comprehensively. Participants underscored the importance of identifying potential hazards proactively, assessing possible scenarios, and implementing appropriate precautions. This suggests that the uncertainties and dangers associated with mountain climbing activate cognitive adaptation processes and enhance risk-focused thinking skills in individuals. In various nature sports, such as mountain climbing, participants must possess knowledge of risk management and emergency response protocols [45]. Participants indicated that, alongside risk minimization strategies, the decision to retreat when necessary is not indicative of weakness, but rather a deliberate and calculated approach. In extreme risk or dangerous situations, individuals may experience various negative emotions, particularly fear and anxiety [46]. Risk tolerance in mountaineering is influenced by personal and environmental factors, with individuals evaluating both their own capabilities and the surrounding conditions when assessing risks. This finding highlights that risk management in mountaineering necessitates both technical skills and cognitive flexibility. Identifying and analyzing risks in advance is crucial in high-risk environments like mountaineering [47–49].

The mental toughness of mountaineers is a crucial factor influencing both climbing success and safety within the cognitive dimension. Participants indicated that evaluating their psychological readiness and recognizing their emotional state prior to engaging in the activity significantly influences their performance and risk perception. Understanding one’s own limitations and capabilities enhances self-confidence and bolsters the ability to manage challenging situations. Mental toughness is particularly crucial for climbers operating in mountaineering environments characterized by extremely challenging conditions [50, 51]. Furthermore, recalling past experiences and engaging in positive self-talk enhances motivation and aids in managing fear. This finding indicates that psychological preparation is equally important as physical preparation in mountaineering, and that mental strength enables individuals to navigate challenges and manage risky situations effectively. Allen-Collinson et al. [4] highlight the importance of mental resilience in managing challenges. Crust et al. [52] assert that it influences preparation processes, social and interpersonal behaviors, as well as individual behaviors among climbers exhibiting strong resilience.

The characteristics of mental strength in mountaineering illustrate the strategies athletes employ in climbing and performance management. Participants indicated that injury anxiety prompts increased caution and fosters a more serious approach to the event. Consciously controlling and managing anxiety, rather than attempting to eliminate it entirely, enhances safety and bolsters the ability to handle risky situations. Given the risks associated with mountaineering and the potential for injury or fatality, it is evident that a high degree of physical endurance and a robust mental disposition are essential for the successful completion of a climb [53]. Previous success and experience have been demonstrated to contribute to self-confidence in facing future challenges. This finding indicates that cognitive enhancement in mountaineering encompasses various strategies, including managing anxiety, leveraging past experiences, and boosting self-confidence. According to Allen-Collinson et al. [4], mentally stronger climbers exhibit characteristics such as meticulous preparation, elevated work tempo, swift recovery from setbacks, commitment, and resilience.

The final sub-theme that distinctly emerged within the cognitive dimension pertained to strategic thinking. Participants highlighted that mountaineering is conducted most safely when undertaken with an appropriate team. Strategic thinking necessitates the evaluation of both individual competencies and the cohesion and structure of the team [54]. Understanding the team’s competencies regarding their roles and responsibilities in potential risk scenarios, such as identifying who is capable of transporting an injured individual, is essential for effective team formation, particularly when integrated with the leader’s strategic thinking and planning abilities. This finding indicates that strategic thinking in mountaineering encompasses not only route selection and technical skills but also team dynamics and leadership abilities. This argument posits that strategic thinking skills are essential across all aspects of life, as they significantly influence decision-making, exemplified by activities like mountaineering, which can have critical outcomes [55].

The motivational theme encompasses the subthemes of injury fear, which include amotivation, passion, and precautionary measures.

Recognition of risks has demonstrated its potential as a significant factor in demotivating climbers. Participants indicated that potential dangers induce fear and may even lead to considerations of non-participation in the activity. The potential for minor errors to result in significant repercussions is a persistent source of fear for mountaineers, potentially undermining their motivation. Weather events, including fog, rain, and storms [56], along with man-made structures [57], low oxygen levels [58], and extreme cold [59], diminish mountaineers’ motivation during the activity. Traumatic events, particularly accidents, can significantly disrupt individual motivation and provoke existential inquiries such as “What am I doing here?” This situation illustrates that risk perception in mountaineering exerts a bidirectional influence on motivation. Awareness of risks enhances safety through the promotion of precautions; however, it may also detrimentally impact performance by inducing demotivation and fear. This situation illustrates that risk perception influences the relationship between motivation and performance in high-risk sports [60].

The study identifies passion as a significant motivational subtheme. The risks associated with mountaineering are often considered to be outweighed by the enjoyment and significance it provides. Participants indicated that anxiety and fear are constant factors; however, these emotions do not hinder their climbing activities, as they acknowledge them as integral to the process. Factors including passion for mountain climbing, survival challenges, and spiritual well-being counterbalance the adverse effects of risks on motivation, thereby ensuring that mountaineers sustain their commitment to the sport. High mountains, canyons, glaciers, waterfalls, and elevated passes represent significant aesthetic features of nature that promote engagement with mountainous landscapes and environmental observation [61]. Participants perceive mountaineering not merely as a physical activity, but as an essential aspect of their lives, embracing the associated risks due to their profound appreciation for nature and their sense of unity with it. This finding indicates that passion for mountaineering is influenced by factors including intrinsic motivation, the normalization of risk perception, and an appreciation for nature. This situation highlights that mountaineering has evolved into both a sport and a lifestyle, serving as a quest for personal meaning. Galiakbarov et al. [62] identified several internal and external motivations driving mountaineers’ consistent climbing activities, including the desire to reconnect with the natural environment of the mountains, the need to recall memories of past climbs, and the opportunity for self-actualization.

A further sub-theme identified in the motivational dimension pertains to precaution-taking behaviors. Participants indicated that elevated motivation correlates with enhanced safety and necessitates effective risk management. Motivation can guide mountaineers toward new routes, compel them to collaborate with more experienced teams, and facilitate the acquisition of knowledge regarding risks. Conversely, excessive motivation may result in carelessness and the making of risky decisions. Mountaineers must effectively manage their motivation while prioritizing risk assessment and survival awareness. The research indicated that mountaineers enhance their survival probabilities through deliberate decision-making and the avoidance of superfluous risks. It is noted that individuals may be predisposed to forgo their personal objectives to assist others facing challenges. In this process, self-awareness, adaptability, and the capacity to withdraw when needed emerge as significant strategies. This finding highlights that mountaineering involves not only courage but also necessitates deliberate risk management. Crust et al. [52] noted that mountaineers typically exhibit competitiveness, discipline, and a focus on achievement, while also possessing the capacity for self-regulation. Harirchi et al. [63] highlighted the necessity for mountaineers to utilize suitable clothing and equipment as a precaution in high-altitude and winter climbing, to be accompanied by an experienced guide, and to undergo training to manage cold weather conditions. Knott [64] noted that a primary challenge in mountaineering lies in the ability of both novice and seasoned climbing enthusiasts to accurately evaluate their own skills and limitations, as well as to identify the additional skills and practical training required for improvement.

Sub-themes that emerged in the relational dimension were resistance, environmental factors, social pressure, self-awareness and persuasion.

The findings indicated that mountaineers demonstrated considerable resilience to negative feedback, concerns, and environmental pressures. Participants indicated that they attempted to address their families’ concerns, yet remained unaffected by the negative perceptions of individuals possessing incomplete or inaccurate information. The participants contended that risks are intrinsic to mountain climbing, asserting that precautionary measures have become a fundamental reflex, and noted that external warnings do not influence their motivation. This situation illustrates that the motivation of mountaineers is an entirely internal process, allowing individuals to make decisions independently of external influences. Mountain climbing is characterized as an endeavor by individuals to achieve liberation from the external pressures imposed by their cultural and physical surroundings [65]. Intrinsic motivation serves as a significant driving force for individuals engaging in challenging and perilous activities, providing a protective barrier against external pressures [66].

The sub-theme of environmental factors within the relational dimension indicates that family support significantly enhances the psychological resilience of mountain climbers. Participants indicated that their families’ supportive, rather than obstructive, attitude—despite any concerns—enhanced their sense of strength and confidence in executing their decisions. Mountaineers who converted criticism into motivation and drew lessons from others’ experiences indicated that environmental support enhanced their ability to manage challenges. Family support is identified as a significant factor in this process. Another study highlighting the significance of family support corroborates our research [67]. A further study [68] indicates that individuals employ various strategies to mitigate pressures from family and the environment. They employed strategies including the dissemination of information, fostering trust, sharing their experiences, or presenting them in a more subdued manner. This finding highlights the significant role of social support in safeguarding individuals’ psychological well-being and enhancing performance in stressful and risky contexts [69].

The sub-theme of social pressure illustrates that mountaineers employ various strategies to navigate social pressures. Participants indicated that they mitigate the concerns of others by highlighting the safety measures they implement and concealing their activities. Family support is correlated with the mountaineers’ confidence in the measures implemented and their personal control, rather than involving direct intervention. Humagain and Singleton [70] highlight that an individual’s decision to engage in mountaineering is significantly influenced by the attitudes of family and friends, as well as the support or opposition from this close circle, which can impact the decision-making process. With time, the social environment becomes familiar, and the mountaineers’ experiences grow, leading to a reduction in fear. This finding aligns with social psychology theories indicating that social norms and expectations influence individual behavior, prompting the development of various adaptation strategies to manage these expectations [71].

The sub-theme of self-awareness, identified within the relational dimension, illustrates the impact of environmental relationships on mountaineers. Participants indicate that feedback from their environment concerning mountaineering activities enhances their knowledge and self-awareness, which in turn facilitates the adoption of new precautions informed by others’ experiences, thereby mitigating risks. Environmental stimuli serve as a significant data source for mountaineers in addressing their injury concerns. Mountaineers face challenges in nature and relate their experiences to the characteristics of these challenges. Acknowledging their limited capacity to influence these factors, they come to understand their personal limits and capabilities [72]. To address the challenges faced in mountaineering, it is essential to enhance psychological health through the development of self-awareness, given that mountaineering comprises 20% physical exertion and 80% mental engagement [73].

The final sub-theme that emerged in the relational dimension is persuasion. Participants assert that the communication environment must effectively inform and persuade them to mitigate or eradicate their biases and apprehensions regarding potential injury risks. The findings indicate that continuous mountaineering has resulted in the environment gradually accommodating the associated risks over time. This situation indicates that the injury concerns of mountaineers must be addressed at a relational level. Mountaineers engage with nature and their surroundings through adventurous experiences, temporary solitude, and personal freedom, influencing their perceptions of social values [74]. The mountaineering experience fosters a profound sense of satisfaction, enabling individuals to transcend their limitations and connect with an ineffable and enduring force. This process enhances individual consciousness and awareness, fostering the courage and self-confidence necessary for making positive contributions to society [75].

The evaluation of research findings indicates that injury fear in professional mountaineering is a complex process influenced by social and cultural contexts, rather than merely an individual experience. This understanding provides a significant foundation for future research.

Conclusion

This study analyzes the cognitive, motivational, and relational effects of injury fear in mountaineering. The findings derived from Lazarus’ Cognitive-Motivational-Relational Theory critically examine concepts including risk management, psychological resilience, and strategic thinking in mountaineering, while highlighting their influence on mountaineers’ motivations, risk perceptions, and interactions with the environment. Injury fear in mountaineering significantly influences individual performance and group dynamics. This fear fosters the development of mental flexibility, strategic planning, and risk management skills, while also significantly influencing motivation and group interactions. The findings indicate that both physical and cognitive-psychological factors must be considered in mountaineering training and preparation processes.

Limitations

This study represents a significant advancement in comprehending injury concerns among Turkish climbers; however, it is not without limitations. The identified limitations offer important insights for future research endeavors. This study represents a significant initial effort to explore injury concerns among Turkish climbers; however, the limited sample size of 12 professional climbers and the use of semi-structured interviews constrain the generalizability of the findings. Research employing a larger sample that encompasses various experience levels, disciplines, geographic regions, and diverse data collection methods (such as observation, surveys, and focus groups) could enhance the breadth and reliability of the results. Furthermore, contextual factors, including environmental and personal elements, are insufficiently addressed, representing an additional limitation.

Recommendations

Future research should complement qualitative findings with quantitative data through mixed methods, assess the efficacy of psychological intervention programs, conduct cultural comparisons, and objectively measure injury concerns by utilizing physiological data collected via wearable technologies.
