Alcohol-induced oxidative stress triggers mitochondrial Ca2+ release through the permeability transition pore
Authors: Kwangwoo Lee, Jiyoon Lee, Jihyo Byun, Eunmi Lee, Pilhan Kim, Won Kim, Won-Il Jeong
Published: 2026

A chronic-plus-binge ethanol (EtOH) mouse model is employed to investigate the molecular mechanisms of alcoholassociated steatohepatitis (ASH) [1,2]. In this model, alcoholinduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and calcium (Ca²+) fluctuations in mitochondria drive glutamate release from vesicular glutamate transporter 3 (VGLUT3) granules, activating metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5) in Kupffer cells via pseudosynapse formation between ballooned hepatocytes and Kupffer cells, which leads to hepatocyte death and neutrophil infiltration [2]. However, the underlying mechanism of Ca2+ release from mitochondria post binge is still obscure despite of elevated mitochondrial Ca2+ levels of rat and mouse hepatocytes by chronic alcohol consumption [3,4]. Additionally, high mitochondrial Ca2+ levels coupled to the ROS production activates mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) in the mitochondrial membrane [5], releasing Ca2+ into the cytoplasm. Therefore, we explored whether binge drinking following chronic alcohol exposure exacerbates mitochondrial ROS production and the resultant Ca2+ release through mPTP in ASH models.

To elucidate the mechanism underlying mitochondrial Ca2+ release in chronic plus binge EtOH drinking model (Supplementary Fig. 1A), we have re-analyzed previously generated bulk RNA-sequencing data [2] and performed quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses using isolated hepatocytes (HEPs) from EtOH-fed mouse livers. These analyses revealed increased expression of mPTP and alcohol metabolizing enzyme-related genes following binge drinking for 1 hour (E + 1B) (Fig. 1A, 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1B), suggesting ROS-mediated upregulation of the mPTP. To validate these observations in vitro, isolated mouse HEPs were exposed with 20 mM EtOH for multiple times. qRTPCR analyses showed that alcohol metabolism-related genes (Cyp2e1, Aldh2, and Adh1) were upregulated at early time points, whereas ROS-mediated (Trp53 and Nfe2l2), mPTP-related (Ppif, Vdac2, Vdac3, and Slc25a4) and glu-tamate production-related (Gls2, Aldh4a1, Glul, and Slc17a8) genes were induced at later time points. These results suggest that oxidative stress generated during alcohol metabolism may contribute to the upregulation of mPTPand glutamate production-associated genes (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Next, we examined whether mitochondrial ROS production in EtOH-exposed HEPs induces mitochondrial Ca2+ leakage in vitro. When primary mouse HEPs were exposed to high-dose EtOH (100 mM), we observed a marked increase in MitoSOX fluorescence, indicative of mitochondrial ROS [6], at 15 minutes after EtOH treatment, along with leakage of accumulated Calcein AM [7] from mitochondria beginning at 4 minutes (Fig. 1D). However, the fluorescence of Calcein AM recovered after 30 minutes (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 1D). These data suggested that mitochondrial ROS production under high-dose EtOH exposure may induce Ca²+ leakage through transient mPTP opening. To further test this relationship, HEPs were first treated with 20 mM EtOH for 12 hours to induce mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation, followed by an additional exposure to 100 mM EtOH for up to 12 hours. Rhod-2 AM staining, a fluorescent mitochondrial Ca2+ indicator [8], showed that 20 mM EtOH increased mitochondrial Ca2+ levels, whereas subsequent high-dose EtOH exposure markedly reduced these levels, consequently leading to increased glutamate release into the cultured media (Fig. 1E).

To validate these findings in vivo, mice were fed a liquid EtOH diets for 2 weeks and then subjected to single binge drinking. Using these mice, intravital liver imaging revealed that the Rhod-2 AM fluorescence intensity was elevated in HEPs of 2 week-EtOH-fed mice, while binge drinking immediately decreased intramitochondrial Ca2+ levels within 10 minutes (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Video 1). However, in vivo and in vitro pre-treatments with Mito-TEMPO (a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant) [9] and Cyclosporine A (an inhibitor of mPTP targeting cyclophilin D) [10] preserved Rhod-2 AM fluorescence and decreased the expression of Ppif and Cyp2e1 in EtOH-exposed HEPs, respectively (Fig. 1G, 1H, Supplementary Fig. 1E-I, Supplementary Videos 2, 3), indicating a critical link between mitochondrial ROS production and mPTP-induced Ca2+ release in EtOH-exposed HEPs.

To explore similar molecular mechanisms in human HEPs, we tested the expression levels of alcohol metabolism, ROS- and glutamate-related genes and proteins using several hepatoma cell lines (Fig. 1I, Supplementary Fig. 1J). Among four different cell lines, SNU475 cells specifically expressed alcohol metabolizing enzymes (CYP2E1, ALDH2), its regulatory factors (TP53, NFE2L2), and glutamate-related (ALDH4A1, SLC7A11) genes or proteins compared to those of other hepatoma cell lines lacking CYP2E1 expression or having mutation in p53. However, SLC17A8 (encoding VGLUT3) expression was higher in Huh7 cells compared to other cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 1J), whereas SLC7A11 (encoding xCT) was expressed in all cell lines, suggesting that glutamate can be excreted via VGLUT3 and xCT in response to ROS production [2,11]. Therefore, SNU475 hepatoma cell was selected for EtOH experiments and treated with 20 mM EtOH for 12 hours. In lines with mouse findings, 20 mM EtOH treatment successfully increased mitochondrial Ca2+ levels and upregulated mPTPrelated, ROS-mediated and glutamate production-related genes in SNU475 hepatoma cells (Fig. 1J, 1K, Supplementary Fig. 1K). Then, these cells were treated with additional 100 mM EtOH, leading to decreased mitochondrial Ca2+ levels and increased glutamate levels the in the cultured media (Fig. 1L, Supplementary Fig. 1L).

Although chronic alcohol consumption primarily induces hepatic steatosis, the mechanisms driving its progression to hepatitis remain unclear. The chronic-plus-binge model is currently the only experimental animal model that reliably produces ASH by upregulating CXCL1 to promote neutrophil migration and subsequent liver injury [1]. Our recent work demonstrated that cytoplasmic transport of Ca2+ from mitochondria initiates hepatic glutamate release in perivenous HEP, which subsequently activates mGluR5 in Kupffer cells. This signaling induces ROS production and CXCL1 expression in Kupffer cells through the formation of a metabolic synapse between HEPs and Kupffer cells [2]. Yet, the mechanism by which mitochondria release their accumulated Ca2+ into the cytosol had not been defined. The present study clarifies this mechanism. In HEPs stimulated for alcohol metabolism, acute high-dose EtOH exposure induces mitochondrial ROS production, transiently opening the mPTP and rapidly releasing mitochondrial Ca2+ into the cytoplasm. This promotes VGLUT3+ granules to dock and fuse with the plasma membrane via the Ca2+-dependent soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor-attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complex, leading to hepatic glutamate excretion (Fig. 1M).

Although mitochondria are not a major organ of Ca2+ storage, they involve the modulation of the Ca2+ signal in cells by receiving or releasing Ca2+ according to the needs of the cell [2,5]. Mitochondria can accumulate Ca2+ through the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (MCU) channel complex and the mitochondria-associated ER membrane Ca2+-channeling complex. Conversely, Ca2+ efflux from the mitochondria is principally mediated by NCLX (Na+/Ca2+ exchanger) and LETM1 (Ca2+/H+ exchanger); however, under pathological or specific conditions, the mPTP serves as an alternative route for Ca2+ release [5]. In our previous report, we identified only upregulated MCU-related genes in HEPs for Ca2+ storage after 2-week EtOH intake [2] and the present study showed specific changes of gene expression associated with the mPTP (Fig. 1A-C). This change may involve EtOH-mediated Nfe2l2 upregulation (Supplementary Fig. 1C), but further study is needed. The opening of the mPTP marks a critical event in disease pathogenesis: transient opening can protects cells from oxidative stress through Ca2+ efflux, whereas sustained opening disrupt ATP synthesis, induce mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately trigger cell death [5]. Additionally, the mPTP opening may induce osmotic changes and massive swelling of the mitochondria, remodeling of the mitochondrial cristae, and activation of some proteins in the mitochondrial membrane, consequently releasing proapoptotic proteins [12]. These features are representative findings in HEPs of chronic alcohol-fed mice. However, apoptotic HEPs are seldom observed after high-dose EtOH exposure in vitro, implying that HEPs may possess strong antioxidant defense or that non-parenchymal cells significantly contribute to the mechanisms underlying HEP death. This phenomenon requires further study. Nevertheless, our study showed that chronic low dose of alcohol treatment increased the contents of mitochondrial Ca2+ in HEPs and additional high-dose of EtOH exposure successfully released mitochondrial Ca2+ into cytosol through the mPTP, suggesting useful investigation techniques related to Ca2+ accumulation and mPTP opening studies using primary mouse HEPs and SNU475 cells.

Interestingly, in neurons, Ca2+ serves as a key mediator that converts electrical signals into chemical signals. At nerve terminals, voltage-gated calcium channels open in response to action potentials, and the resulting influx of Ca2+ triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the cell membrane, releasing glutamate into the synaptic cleft to transmit sig-nals [13]. The regulation of Ca²+ homeostasis by hormonal signals is vital in HEPs, as in neurons. This signaling axis not only orchestrates basal metabolic and detoxification processes but also appears to serve as a central mediator in ethanol-induced hepatocellular injury [14]. Our previous study identified a mechanism in which cytosolic Ca2+ fluctuations, together with the SNARE complex, facilitate the secretion of VGLUT3+ granules enriched with glutamate from HEPs [2]. Another study reported the involvement of the SNARE complex in very-low-density lipoprotein transport vesicle-Golgi fusion in the liver [15]. Nevertheless, the release of specific vesicles containing glutamate or other substances, from HEPs via Ca2+ and the SNARE complex remains poorly understood. Therefore, comprehensive investigations into the synthesis, storage, secretion, receptor interactions, and uptake of neurotransmitter-like substances (referred to as hepatotransmitter) in HEPs are expected to significantly advance our understanding of, and capacity to address, a wide range of diseases beyond ASH.
