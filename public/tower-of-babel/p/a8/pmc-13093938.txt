Corneal densitometry and its association with cumulative dissipated energy after phacoemulsification
Authors: Utku Furuncuoglu, Isil Uslubas, Muge Toprak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate corneal densitometry after cataract surgery and its association with cumulative dissipated energy (CDE).MethodsNinety-nine eyes of 74 patients who underwent uncomplicated phacoemulsification with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation surgery were included in this retrospective study. Examinations were conducted at baseline and 1 and 3 months after phacoemulsification. Corneal densitometry (CD) and 10-mm corneal volume (CV) measurements were obtained from corneal topography (Pentacam, Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany).ResultsThe mean CV values increased significantly at the 1 and 3 months after surgery compared to baseline value (p<0.05). The mean CD values increased significantly at 1 month after surgery compared to baseline level for total diameter and all corneal annular zones except zone 2 and zone 3 in the anterior corneal layer (p<0.05). There was a positive correlation between CDE and the change in CD values in zone 3, zone 4 and total diameter in both the anterior and central corneal layers from baseline to 1 month after surgery (r=0.221, r=0.235, r=0.224, r=0.203, r=0.246, r=0.224; for all p<0.05, respectively).ConclusionsCorneal densitometry increased at different depths and layers of the cornea at 1 month after surgery, but returned to baseline values at 3 months after surgery. No correlation was found between CDE and change in CV values from baseline to 1 and 3 months after surgery. There was a positive correlation between CDE and the change in CD values in the peripheral 6-12 mm zone and total diameter within the anterior and central layers of cornea. The lack of endothelial cell count was a significant limitation of our study and limits the ability to differentiate between transient corneal edema and permanent endothelial cell loss as possible causes of postoperative densitometric changes.Keywords: Corneal volume, Cumulative dissipated energy, Densitometry, Pentacam

Introduction

Cataract surgery is one of the most commonly performed surgical procedures worldwide, utilizing various techniques to remove the opacified lens and restore vision [1]. A crucial factor in the success of this surgery is the management of cumulative dissipated energy (CDE), which refers to the total amount of energy delivered to the eye during the procedure [2]. The Centurion system (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX) measures the amount of CDE and indicates it at the end of the operation.

Cumulative dissipated energy is a measurement that quantifies the energy expended in surgical procedures, particularly during phacoemulsification in cataract surgery. The mechanisms of energy dissipation involve the conversion of ultrasound energy into mechanical vibrations that emulsify the cataractous lens. During this process, energy is transmitted to surrounding tissues, leading to potential thermal and physical damage. Studies indicate that higher levels of CDE can cause significantly increased tissue temperature and stress, necessitating careful monitoring to enhance patient safety [2, 3]. Understanding CDE is vital as it has direct implications for ocular health post-surgery [4, 5].

Optical density is a complex property of materials that can cause the refraction effect, a description of the shading ability of materials, and slow down the speed at which light propagates [6]. The cornea’s optical density value, corneal densitometry (CD), is a biological characteristic that can be utilized as a key gauge of corneal transparency and a reflection of the tissue’s health [7, 8].

High CDE values have been associated with postoperative corneal changes [9]. However, the specific relationship between CDE and CD across different corneal regions remains unclear [6]. This study investigates whether CDE value is associated with CD and 10-mm corneal volume (CV) and the effect of phacoemulsification surgery on densitometry in different corneal regions.

Materials and methods

A single-center retrospective study was conducted from October 2023 to October 2024 at the Ophthalmology Department, Kocaeli City Hospital in Kocaeli, Turkey. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of Kocaeli City Hospital (2024 − 147). Informed consent was not required because this study was a retrospective chart review with no risk to any of the patients, according to institutional review board approval.

Subjects and data

We reviewed 74 medical charts of patients who underwent cataract surgery in our clinic. Eyes that underwent uncomplicated phacoemulsification with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation surgery were included in the study. Clinical data collected included age, gender, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), anterior segment examination with a slit lamp biomicroscope and dilated fundus examination. All patients had grade 3 or greater nuclear opacification according to the Lens Opacities Classification System III (LOCS III). Systemic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus (DM) were noted. Intraoperative surgical details including CDE values were reviewed. Corneal densitometry in different corneal regions and the CV contained within 10-mm diameter discs were obtained from corneal topography (Pentacam, Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany) performed preoperatively and at 1 and 3 months after surgery. Only outputs with adequate image quality, defined by the quality specification (QS) being “OK”, were used for analysis. This exclusion is unlikely to have introduced selection bias because the QS is an automatic, device-generated parameter based solely on image acquisition standards rather than patient characteristics. All measurements are performed by a single experienced operator. The Pentacam software (Version 1.20r36) automatically calculated all parameters. Zonal CD values were automatically assessed in four concentric annular zones centered on the corneal apex (zone 1: 0–2 mm, zone 2: 2–6 mm, zone 3: 6–10 mm, zone 4: 10–12 mm, and total diameter) and at different depths (anterior layer: superficial 120 μm; central layer: from 120 μm to the final 60 μm; posterior layer: innermost 60 μm; total corneal thickness). In the Scheimpflug image, the CD results were expressed in grayscale units and are shown as the pixel luminance per unit volume. The measurements varied depending on how much light the cornea was backscattering, from 0 (maximum transparency) to 100 (totally opaque cornea). Patients with previous intraocular surgery, ocular surface disorder, glaucoma, keratoconus, active or inactive uveitis, corneal dystrophies or scarring were excluded.

Surgical procedure

Prior to the procedure, the eyelid skin was disinfected with a 10% povidone-iodine, while the cornea and conjunctiva were disinfected with a 5% povidone-iodine for approximately 3 min and then rinsed out with balanced salt solution. All cataract surgeries were performed through a temporal 2.2 mm clear corneal incision by the same surgeon (U.F.) using the same phacoemulsifier machine (Centurion Vision System, Alcon, Geneva, Switzerland) with the same settings under topical anesthesia. Following continued curvilinear capsulorhexis, hydrodissection and hydrodelineation were performed. The nucleus was aspirated using the stop and chop technique. Following cortical aspiration, an IOL was implanted. At the end of each operation, CDE was recorded. Moxifloxacin for 1 week and prednisolon acetate with a tapering regimen for 1 month, eye drops were given to all patients postoperatively. No nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) were prescribed before or after surgery.

Statistical analysis

A sample size calculated using G*Power Software 3.1. The primary outcome was the change in CD from baseline to postoperative month 1. Based on an expected moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.40), α = 0.05, and power (1–β) = 0.90, a minimum of 78 eyes was required. Mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum value, frequency and percentage were used for descriptive statistics. The distribution of variables is measured by the Kolmogorov Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Paired samples t test and wilcoxon test were used for the repeated measurement analysis. Spearman correlation analysis was used in the correlation analysis. To reduce the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied by dividing the significance threshold (α = 0.05) by the number of comparisons performed within each corneal layer and zone (5 zones → adjusted α = 0.05/5 = 0.01). Accordingly, p-values < 0.01 were considered statistically significant within each layer.

Fourty-eight patients were female, and 26 patients were male. The mean age was 67.9 ± 7.7 years. Surgery was performed on the right eye in 50.5% of cases. Twenty-one patients had DM. Discussion

In our study, we investigated CD changes after phacoemulsification and its relationship with CDE. We found that CD values ​​at the anterior corneal apex increased significantly at 1 month after surgery compared to baseline. ​​In the deep layers after the most anterior layer, CD values increased significantly at 1 month after surgery compared to baseline in only peripheric zone 4. However, we found that CD values decreased to baseline for zone 1, zone 2, and zone 3 in all corneal layers at the 3 months after surgery. Also, we found that there is a positive correlation between CDE and change in CD values from baseline to 1 month after surgery in the peripheral 6–12 mm zone and total diameter in both the anterior and central corneal layers.

It has been known and researched for many years that cataract surgery can affect corneal health. Various parameters are used to evaluate corneal health after surgery. Previous studies have demonstrated that CD is associated with corneal transparency and health [6, 10, 11]. A study evaluating CD after cataract surgery reported that CD significantly increased at 1 month after surgery in the anterior and central layer for zone 1 [12]. Ishikawa et al. [13] reported that CD increased significantly on day 3 after surgery and returned to normal value on day 7 in the anterior layer of zone 1. These studies found no significant difference in CD values for zone 1 in the central and posterior layers. Liu et al. [14] reported that CD values were higher in month 1 and returned to normal in month 3 after surgery in the anterior layer for zone 1. They emphasized that CD values decreased to normal in the central and posterior layer for zone 1 on day 7 after surgery, while it took 3 months in the anterior layer. The above studies are consistent with our results and indicated that epithelial edema regresses more slowly than stromal and endothelial edema. Because Ishikawa et al. [13] used a different concentration of povidone-iodine as 1% for conjunctival and corneal disinfection in the surgery, CD values ​​may have returned to normal in the anterior layer for zone 1 earlier than we reported. 5% povidone-iodine may damage the cornea epithelium and 10% povidone-iodine used as a presurgical skin antiseptic may also be toxic to the cornea [15, 16]. Topical anesthesia may also be toxic to the corneal epithelium. These above data may explain why the anterior layer heals slower than the central and posterior layers.

Interestingly, we found that the CD value was higher at 1 month after surgery compared to the baseline only in zone 4, and was not higher in zone 1 in the central and posterior layers, in contrast to the anterior layer. The significance did not exist for posterior layer after bonferroni correction. The variation in CD across different zones and layers may reflect both structural and procedural factors. The anterior layer, which includes the epithelium and superficial stroma, is more sensitive to surgical trauma, topical anesthesia, and povidone-iodine exposure, which may lead to more pronounced short-term increases in light scatter. In contrast, deeper stromal and endothelial regions might be expected to be less affected by transient edema. The peripheral zone may exhibit greater densitometric changes due to the main corneal incision and subsequent wound healing. These results supports that CD can be used as an indicator for corneal wound healing. However, as reported in a previous study, peripheral CDE values ​​should be evaluated carefully in the clinic due to their low reproducibility and reliability [17].

In our study, there was no significant difference in the mean CDE values between patients with and without diabetes. Leda et al. [9] reported similar results to our study. In contrast, Bui et al. [18] reported that DM is associated with higher perioperative CDE values. These differences may be due to the operation time. It’s known that the small pupil size is more common in diabetic patients [19]. This may cause an increase in the operation time and CDE. We found that the mean baseline CD values were higher in the peripheral 6–12 mm zone in the anterior, central corneal layer and total corneal thickness in the diabetic group. Conversely, Ramm et al. [20] reported that in the diabetic group, CD of nearly all corneal zones and layers were lower than non-diabetic group. Yilmaz et al. [21] reported that CD values were higher for zone 1 in all corneal layers and for zone 3 in the anterior and central corneal layers in the diabetic group. It is known that hyperglycemia causes changes in stromal morphology and central corneal density by inducing collagen cross-links, leading to the development of stromal and epithelial edema and loss of corneal transparency [22]. Therefore, high CDE values ​​appear to be a natural consequence of the disease. However, since we did not have HbA1c measurements, we were unable to perform further subgroup analyses evaluating the relationship between glycemic control and CD. We showed that CD values increased at 3 months after surgery compared to baseline for zone 4 in the total corneal thickness in the diabetic group contrast to the non-diabetic group. Considering that surgical incisions are made in zone 4, this may be related to the prolonged wound healing in diabetes. It should be kept in mind that the type of diabetes, its regulation and duration are effective in all these parameters.

We found that CDE is associated with higher CD values at peripheral zones in the anterior and central layers and total corneal thickness. No significant association was found between CDE and CD values in the 6 mm diameter central area in the anterior and central layers of the cornea at 1 month after surgery. For the posterior layer, there was a positive correlation only in zone 4. However, CDE had no effect on month 3 after surgery CD values in all corneal layers and depths. This finding suggests that at least CDE value in the current study, may not impair corneal transparency in the long term. However, the weak correlations between CDE and changes in CD (r values ​​ranged from approximately 0.20 to 0.27) indicated that CDE explained less than 7% of the variance in densitometry measurements. Therefore, while higher CDE leads to increased CD in the early postoperative period, particularly in peripheral corneal regions, it limits the ability to predict changes in corneal transparency at the individual patient level. These findings suggest that changes in CD after phacoemulsification are likely multifactorial and cannot be explained by CDE alone. To the best our knowledge, this is the first report evaluating the association between CDE and CD values.

Corneal volume could be used to assess corneal health after phacoemulsification. Previous studies reported that CV increases after phacoemulsification [23–25]. Suzuki et al. [26] reported that the 10-mm CV was significantly higher at 1 and 3 months after phacoemulsification. Similar results in our study suggest that corneal healing takes a long time after phacoemulsification. Causes of CV increase may include corneal stromal edema or endothelial damage after phacoemulsification. On the other hand, no significant association was found between CDE and change in 10-mm CV values from baseline to 1 and 3 months after surgery.

A major limitation of this study is that endothelial cell counting was not performed. Although corneal endothelial cell damage is well known after phacoemulsification, we could not evaluate its relationship with densitometry. Hypothetically, low endothelial count with higher peroperative CDE may be associated with higher CD after surgery. The lack of endothelial cell data prevents us from distinguishing whether the observed postoperative CD increases represent transient edema, or subtle endothelial compromise. Therefore, the biological mechanism underlying CDE related CD changes cannot be definitively established. This could be the subject of further study. Another limitation is that changed anterior chamber parameters after cataract surgery may affect CD, but we could not analyse this. A study analysing anterior chamber parameters with CD may be considered in the future. We believe that this study, which evaluates the effects of phacoemulsification on the cornea in different layers and depths together with its association with CDE, sheds light on an important area.

In conclusion, we found that CD values increased at different depths and layers of the cornea at 1 month after surgery, but returned to baseline ​​at 3 months after surgery. No correlation was found between CDE and change in CV values from baseline to 1 and 3 months after surgery. 10-mm CV increased at 1 and 3 months after surgery. These findings suggest that both CD and CV can be used in the clinical follow-up of corneal healing. The increase in CD after surgery, especially in the anterior corneal layer, in the central 0–2 mm cornea and in the total corneal diameter, suggests that it can be used in the follow-up of postoperative epithelial edema. Further comprehensive studies are necessary to better understand these indicators. Since high peroperative CDE value ​​is associated with high CD value ​​in total corneal diameter after phacoemulsification, it is important that the CDE value is as low as possible in cataract surgery, especially in patients with corneal disease. It is important to emphasize that, although the association between CDE and postoperative CD was statistically significant, the effect sizes were weak and probably not clinically relevant. Therefore, these results should not be interpreted as strong evidence that CDE significantly affects postoperative corneal clarity and they have limited clinical predictive value for individual patients. Currently, there is no consensus regarding a specific threshold that defines a clinically significant change in CD after cataract surgery. Corneal densitometry values are best interpreted as relative changes over time rather than absolute values. We suggest the clinical use of serial CD measurements and comparison with the fellow eye, particularly in cases where visual acuity does not improve after cataract surgery and the reason cannot be determined by biomicroscopic examination. We believe it provides an indicator of subtle corneal changes in ambiguous situations.
