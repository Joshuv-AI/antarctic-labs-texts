Disulfide-stabilized trimeric hemagglutinin ectodomains provide enhanced heterologous influenza protection
Authors: De-Jian Liu, Xiu-Qin Zhong, Yan-Xia Ru, Shi-Long Zhao, Cui-Cui Liu, Yi-Bo Tang, Xuan Wu, Yi-Shuai Zhang, Hui-Hui Zhang, Jia-Yue She, Mu-Yang Wan, Yao-Wang Li, He-Ping Zheng, Lei Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza virus infection poses a continual menace to public health. Here, we developed soluble trimeric HA ectodomain vaccines by establishing interprotomer disulfide bonds in the stem region, which effectively preserve the native antigenicity of stem epitopes. The stable trimeric H1 ectodomain proteins exhibited higher thermal stabilities in comparison with unmodified HAs and showed strong binding activities towards a panel of anti-stem cross-reactive antibodies that recognize either interprotomer or intraprotomer epitopes. Negative stain transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed the stable trimer architecture of the interprotomer disulfide-stapled WA11#5, NC99#2, and FLD#1 proteins as well as the irregular aggregation of unmodified HA molecules. Immunizations of mice with those trimeric HA ectodomain vaccines formulated with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant elicited significantly more potent cross-neutralizing antibody responses and offered broader immuno-protection against lethal infections with heterologous influenza strains compared to unmodified HA proteins. Additionally, the findings of our study indicate that elevated levels of HA stem-specific antibody responses correlate with strengthened cross-protections. Our design strategy has proven effective in trimerizing HA ectodomains derived from both influenza A and B viruses, thereby providing a valuable reference for designing future influenza HA immunogens.

Introduction

Vaccination for the general public is a proven and effective measure in preventing symptomatic influenza infections. Currently, hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) antibody titre is commonly used as surrogate marker for serological protection provided by seasonal influenza vaccines [1–3]. However, due to frequent antigenic drift occurring in the HA head domain, seasonal influenza viruses can occasionally evade the established immune response and cause symptomatic infection [4–6]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that B cell epitopes located in the HA stem exhibit higher levels of conservation [7,8]. There has already been an increasing recognition of the crucial role played by HA stem-specific immunity in conferring cross-protection [9–11]. An effective and practical design strategy that can steer humoral immune response towards production of HA stem-specific antibodies would expedite the development of a universal influenza vaccine.

The objective of this study is to develop disulfide-stabilized soluble trimeric HA immunogens that maintain native antigenicity, while also demonstrating an enhanced capacity of eliciting stem-specific antibody responses and greater cross-protection potential. When the transmembrane and cytosolic domains are removed, the HA ectodomain of many strains fails to assemble into a stable homotrimer architecture as observed in the full-length HA, which most likely hamper the formation of protective B cell epitopes [12], particularly those featured by the interprotomer. The successful isolation and characterization of human cross-protective monoclonal antibodies, such as CT149 and 3I14, underscore the importance of preserving the native interprotomer conformation of the HA stem for inducing cross-protection [13,14]. Conventional approaches for stabilizing the trimerization of HA ectodomains involve fusing an exogenous trimerization domain to the C-terminus of HA ectodomain [15–18]. It was previously reported that the foldon motif effectively trimerized HK68 H3 ectodomains, whereas a negative-stain 2-D classification analysis revealed that the stem region still exhibited obvious dissociation [16]. Furthermore, the inclusion of exogenous motifs carrying non-specific B cell epitopes may lead to a potential reduction in influenza antigen-specific immunity as a consequence of interclonal competitions among B cells targeting different epitopes within the same antigen. Moreover, distinct B cell epitopes can induce antibody responses utilizing the same germline gene encoding immunoglobulin variable fragment [11,19,20]. The aforementioned aspects emphasize the advantages of scaffold-free design strategy.

Many innovative approaches have been explored to improve influenza vaccine protection efficacy, involving repetitive display, delivery vehicles, adjuvants, and even boost immunization regimens [18,21–23]. The primary function of these strategies is to enhance the overall immunogenicity of vaccines and basically strengthen the antibody responses targeting immuno-dominant but highly variable epitopes. We propose a scaffold-free design approach to maintain and stabilize the native antigenic conformation of HA ectodomains by building interprotomer disulfide bridges. It is expected that the HA ectodomain trimers designed based on this strategy will essentially improve the induction efficiency and protectiveness of stem antibodies, thereby enhancing the cross-protection efficacy.

Results

Design and characterization of disulfide-stabilized HA proteins

We identified some pairs of specific amino acid sites to be mutated into cysteines in order to introduce interprotomer disulfide bonds. Two amino acids from each pair are positioned on each side of the protomer interface, with their Cα-Cβ axes oriented towards each other and a suitable distance between the Cα atoms. Additionally, the backbone conformations of the cysteine substitutes were assessed according to the Ramachandran plot. After comprehensive analyses of the HA protein structures, we simulated and visualized the putative cysteine pairs and disulfide bonds thereof that satisfy the aforementioned criteria. In the H1 sequences from the strains of A/Washington/05/2011 (WA11) and A/New Caledonia/20/1999 (NC99), pairs of cysteine mutations include K68C/R76C in WA11#1 and NC99#1, L20C/K47C in WA11#2, L20C/G47C in NC99#2, G4C/N117C in WA11#3 and NC99#3, inserting a tri-peptide G8-C8a-F9 and K121C in WA11#4 and NC99#4, inserting a quadri-peptide G8-C8a-F9-G8b and K121C in WA11#5 and NC99#5, N124C/G134C in WA11#6, and K58C/E97C in NC99#6. In the HA sequence from the influenza B virus strain of B/Florida/4/2006 (FLD), pairs of cysteine mutations include I6C/A117C in FLD#1, inserting a tri-peptide S67-C67a-G68 and E79C in FLD#2, and K124C/N135C in FLD#3 (Figure 1). All modified and unmodified HA ectodomain proteins were effectively expressed in Sf9 cell culture and purified by means of hexa-His tag purification. Subsequently, molecular characterizations were conducted to investigate whether these mutant proteins form trimers and possess covalent junctions between protomers. The size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) profiles showed that the modified HA proteins WA11#5, NC99#2, and FLD#1 exhibited substantially elevated proportions of trimer compared with other HA variants derived from WA11, NC99, and FLD, respectively (Figure 2 A; Figure S 1; Table S 1). Western blot analyses of these trimeric HA proteins under reducing and non-reducing conditions reveal that the introduced cysteine pairs spontaneously form disulfide bonds that connect HA protomers while the unmodified WA11wt, NC99wt, and FLDwt constructs scarcely form trimers (Figure 2 B). Consistent with immunoblotting data, the negative stain TEM images demonstrated the largely irregular aggregation of unmodified HA molecules (Figure 2 C – E). SEC-purified trimer proteins from WA11#5, NC99#2, and FLD#1 were determined to be homogeneous, stable, and structurally symmetrical homotrimers, as presented in the two-dimentional (2D) class averages (Figure 2 C – E). Higher mid-point transition temperatures (Tm) were detected in WA11#5, NC99#2, and FLD#1 when compared with their corresponding unmodified HAs (Figure 2 F – H), indicating that the interprotomer disulfide bonds notably enhance thermal stability. WA11wt is relatively structurally stable. Among all unmodified HAs, WA11wt presented a later transition, with a Tm of 56.94 °C that is close to the Tm1 of WA11#5, at which the dimer interfaces were dissociated (Figure 2 F). Antigenicity assessment of HA proteins

The antigenicity of HA proteins was assessed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). A panel of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) specific to the HA stem, including CT149, 3I14, CR9114, CR6261, and FISW84, were purified by SEC and characterized by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Figure S 2 A – F). The HA stem-specific nanobody, SD83, was utilized for binding of HA antigens derived from the influenza B FLD strain. It is noteworthy that CT149 and 3I14 recognize interprotomer B cell epitopes, while other mAbs and SD83 recognize intraprotomer epitopes. ELISA results showed that WA11#4 and WA11#5 exhibited stronger binding activities to CT149 and 3I14 compared with other WA11 HA proteins (Figure 3 A). Among NC99 HA protein mutants, NC99#2 showed the strongest binding activities to CT149 and 3I14 (Figure 3 B). The helix-A, a crucial structure in the HA stem responsible for inducing cross-reactive antibody responses, has relatively higher temperature factor in numerous atomic-resolution HA protein structures [24,25], implying its structural instability. We further assessed the heat stability of conformational epitopes recognized by the tested mAbs. The results showed that the binding activities of WA11#4, WA11#5, and NC99#2 towards both CR9114 and CR6261 decreased as temperature increased during protein treatment (Figure S 3), indicating that the hydrophobic pocket structure containing the helix-A in these HA proteins is sensitive to temperature changes. In contrast, the conformation of the H1 membrane-proximal region, where FISW84 binds, exhibited remarkable resistance to temperature fluctuations ranging from 4 to 60 °C (Figure S 3).

Disulfide-stabilized HA ectodomain trimers enhanced cross-reactive igG responses

The mice immunization schedules developed for assessing the immunogenicity of HA proteins was depicted in Figure S 4 and Table S 2. The homologous prime-boost vaccinations with all tested HA proteins effectively elicited specific IgG responses in mice (Figure 4). The disulfide-stabilized H1 ectodomain trimers, such as WA11#5 and NC99#2, possessed superior capacities of inducing cross-reactive antibody responses compared with unmodified HAs. This was demonstrated by the elevated levels of serum IgG titre specific to genetically distant heterologous HAs and the miniH1 #4900 in immunization groups of WA11#5 and NC99#2 (Figure 4; Figure S 2 G and H). The miniH1 #4900 is a stabilized trimeric H1 stem protein and serves as a reliable antigen for the titration of H1-specific antibodies [24]. The WA11 H1 is genetically closer to the H1 from the strains of A/reassortant/NYMC X-179A (X-179A, H1N1), A/Sichuan/1/2009 (SC09, H1N1), and A/Wuling/swl11131/2023 (WL23, H1N1) than the H1 from the strains of A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8, H1N1) and A/New Jersey/8/1976 (NJ76, H1N1); therefore, the immune sera induced by WA11 H1 demonstrated higher IgG titres against the H1 derived from X-179A, SC09, and WL23 (Figure 4). Among all WA11 groups, WA11#5 immune sera showed the strongest levels of binding activities to all inactivated H1N1 viruses in the tests (Figure 4). Immunizations with NC99#2 conferred significantly stronger IgG responses against all inactivated H1N1 viruses than NC99wt (Figure 4). Whereas all H1-elicited immune sera showed remarkably decreased heterosubtypic binding activities to the H9 from the strain of A/Chicken/Jiangsu/7/2002 H9N2, the H9-specific IgG titres of WA11#5 and NC99#2 groups were slightly higher than those of WA11wt and NC99wt groups, respectively. Further titration of miniH1-specific IgG reveals that the stronger H1 stem-specific IgG response is closely related to the broader serum cross-reactivities. In addition, the binding assays with WA11 H1 immune sera showed that IgG1 was the predominant subclass in the X-179A-specific IgG response, whereas a balanced IgG1/IgG2a ratio was observed when the IgG responses were titrated against PR8 and miniH1 as well (Figure S 5). Due to the unavailability of a mAb for verifying interprotomer antigenic conformation of the influenza B HA mutant proteins, hence we opted to express a foldon-stabilized FLD HA trimer protein serving as a control immunogen in vaccination experiments. Additionally, a previous negative stain TEM analysis revealed that the native quaternary structure of HA stem domain cannot be restored by fusing a foldon trimerization domain at the C-terminus of HA ectodomain [16]. Here we compared the immunogenicity and cross-protection efficacy between the disulfide-stabilized HA trimer and foldon-stabilized HA trimer. The FLD#1 vaccine elicited higher IgG response against the HA from the strain of B/Anhui-Tunxi/1528/2014 (B/AH14) than the FLDwt vaccine. The FLD#1 and the FLD-foldon induced comparable IgG responses against either the heterologous HA from the strain of B/Ibaraki/2/1985 (B/Iba85) or the homologous HA from the strain of B/AH14, while the miniH1-specific IgG titres of the FLD#1 group were significantly higher than that of the FLD-foldon group (Figure 4). Titration of IgG subclasses against the B/Iba85 HA showed that the FLD#1 vaccine elicited comparable IgG1 and IgG2a responses (Figure S 5).

Disulfide-stabilized HA ectodomain trimers conferred complete protections against influenza infections

Three weeks post the second immunization, mice were intranasally challenged with 4 × 50% mouse lethal dose (mLD50) of mouse-adapted H1N1 strain PR8, X-179A, or 6 × mLD50 influenza B virus strain Iba85 (Figure S 4; Table S 2). The challenge experiment results showed that only mice immunized with WA11#5 or NC99#2 were fully protected against lethal influenza infection with PR8 and X-179A (Figure 5 A – H). Complete protection was observed only in the FLD#1 group after the Iba85 challenge (Figure 5 I and J). Furthermore, FLD#1 immunization significantly reduced lung virus titres at day 4 post infection compared to other FLD groups (Figure 5 K), resulting in a 411-fold reduction in lung virus titres compared to the FLDwt group. Discussion

Influenza HA-based vaccines predominantly induce neutralizing antibody response against the HA head; however, this type of response generally does not provide universal immuno-protection [26–29]. This drives us to contemplate a viable approach for designing novel HA immunogens capable of enhancing the antibody response towards the stem region. In this study, we have successfully constructed scaffold-free soluble trimeric HA proteins derived from HAs of influenza A and B viruses, which maintain the overall antigenic conformation intact. The trimeric HA ectodomain offer many advantages over the unmodified HA ectodomain in terms of enhanced conformational stability, strengthened immunogenicity and improved cross-protection efficacy. Our findings also reveal that stable and native antigenic conformation of the HA stem domain correlates with efficient induction of cross-protective antibodies. Additionally, we have extensively investigated structural properties of the amino acid residues of interest in the HA models and strived to create design criteria for constructing interprotomer disulfide bonds, which evidently and effectively narrows down the selection scope of potential cysteine mutation sites, as shown in Figure 1.

A prerequisite for a protective HA immunogen lies in ensuring that modified proteins preserve the native antigenic conformational epitopes, particularly those in the stem region accountable for inducing cross-protective antibodies [30–33]. In recent years, there has been numerous reports from various research groups demonstrating the stabilizing functions of the non-native disulfide bridges [24,34–41]. We have successfully designed the disulfide-stabilized H1 ectodomain trimers, WA11#5 and NC99#2, which preserve both interprotomer and intraprotomer B cell epitopes in the stem region (Figure 3 A and B). Fusing a heterologous trimerization domain is likely a suboptimal choice for preserving the native overall conformation of the HA ectodomain. It was recently discovered that T4 foldon-trimerized HK68 HA exhibited splaying in the membrane-proximal region compared with the viral HA [16]. Objectively, our comparative analyses reveal that FLD#1 is indeed superior over FLD-foldon in terms of the stem-specific antibody response and protection efficacy (Figure 4; Figure 5 I and J). The highly trimerized HA constructs exhibiting considerable efficacy in inducing stem-specific cross-protective antibodies also establish a solid foundation for carrying out another HA head transplantation design strategy [42–44]. A soluble chimeric HA can be constructed through recombinant fusion, in which the head domain of annually predicted HA replaces the head domain of a developed HA construct in this study, such as WA11#5, NC99#2, or FLD#1. Such a chimeric HA protein is expected to improve serum HAI protectiveness while effectively conferring cross-protection. Moreover, the design of an HA stem protein can also draw on the identified interprotomer cysteine-bridges within the HA stem domain.

The results of our study demonstrate that the presence of serum cross-neutralizing activity rather than serum HAI activity closely correlates with effective heterologous protection. This is consistent with the previous findings regarding immuno-protection mechanisms in HA-based vaccination projects [18,45,46]. Additionally, many studies have revealed that HA stem-specific antibodies cross-protect mice against influenza infections through antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cell-mediated phagocytosis [47,48]. BALB/c mouse IgG2a exhibits more efficient than IgG1 in these Fc-mediated effector functions, capable of interacting with all activating FcγRI, FcγRIII, and FcγRIV [47]. Serological analysis results demonstrated a significant HA stem-specific IgG2a response following the vaccination with HA trimer (Figure S 5), signifying that the IgG2a-dependent cell-mediated effector functions most likely contribute to the protection conferred by the HA vaccines.

Objectively, this study has some drawbacks and limitations. The metastable HA has the potential to expose specific conserved B cell epitopes at the interface cleft between HA protomers [49]. However, these epitopes would be concealed in the disulfide-stabilized trimer structure, potentially compromising the protective antibody response in certain individuals. Additionally, there is a scarcity of available antibody tools for assessing the antigenicity of influenza B HA. Thus, we cannot provide more experimental evidence from the antigenicity aspect to elucidate the reasons why FLD#1 induced stronger cross-neutralizing protection than FLDwt and FLD-foldon. In the future investigation, it would be intriguing to compare cross-protection efficacy provided by a trivalent HA vaccine incorporating WA11#5, FLD#1, and an H3 immunogen, with a trivalent seasonal influenza vaccine.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All mice experiments were performed in strict accordance with the animal welfare requirements of Hunan University. All mice experiment protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee from the College of Biology, Hunan University (Ethics File Code: HNUBIO202202001). All BALB/c mice infection experiments using mouse-adapted influenza A and B viruses were performed in the animal facility that meets the biosafety level 2 requirement at Hunan Normal University. The used influenza A and B viruses for mice infections include the PR8 H1N1 (HA encoding gene sequence is deposited in GenBank database under the accession number AYA81842.1), the X-179A (a classical reassortant infectious virus incorporating gene segments of HA, NA and PB1 derived from A/California/7/2009 pdm09 H1N1 and gene segments of other five internal antigens derived from PR8 H1N1; HA encoding gene sequence is deposited in GenBank database under the accession number ADE29095.1), and the influenza B virus strain B/Iba85 (Victoria lineage) [50].

Virus strains and cell lines

The mouse-adapted influenza virus strains PR8, X-179A, A/Guizhou/54/1989 (GZ89, H3N2; HA encoding gene sequence is deposited in GenBank database under the accession number BAA01026), JS02 H9N2 (HA encoding gene sequence is deposited in GenBank database under the accession number ACI89433) [51], and B/Iba85 were kindly gifted by Dr. Hai-Yan Chang from College of Life Science and Technology, Hunan Normal University, Changsha, and Dr. Ze Chen from Shanghai Institute of Biological Products, Shanghai. The mouse-adapted influenza A virus strains of NJ76 (H1N1; ATCC VR-897) and A/Hong Kong/8/1968 (HK68, H3N2; ATCC VR-1679) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The influenza virus strains SC09 H1N1 (HA encoding gene sequence is deposited in GenBank database under the accession number ACR32986) and B/AH14 (Yamagata lineage) [52] were kindly gifted by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The influenza A virus strain WL23 H1N1 (GISAID ID: EPI3205159) was gifted by Hunan Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention. All influenza viruses were propagated in 10-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs. The median tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) and mLD50 of all virus strains were determined by inoculating Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cell culture and six to eight-week-old female BALB/c mice, respectively. The MDCK cell line and the Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 insect cell line were purchased from ATCC. MDCK cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; catalog number (Cat.#): C11965500BT, Gibco, U.S.) supplemented with 100 μg/ml streptomycin, 100 U/ml penicillin (Cat.#: P1400, Solarbio, China), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat.#:10270-106, Gibco, U.S.). Sf9 insect cells were cultured in SF-SFM medium (Cat.#: SF10111-2, Suzhou Womei Biology Co., Ltd., China) supplemented with 100 μg/ml streptomycin and 100 U/ml penicillin.

Construction of soluble hemagglutinin proteins

The amino acid sequences of the HA ectodomains derived from the H1N1 strain A/Washington/05/2011 (WA11, GenBank Protein Accession: AGK72598.1, amino acids D1 – Q512) isolated post-2009 pandemic, the H1N1 strain A/New Caledonia/20/1999 (NC99, GenBank Protein Accession: AFO65027.1, amino acids D1 – Q511) isolated pre-2009 pandemic, and the influenza B strain B/Florida/4/2006 (FLD, GenBank Protein Accession: ACA33493.1, amino acids D1 – A521) from Yamagata-lineage were presented in Table S 3. All recombinant HA proteins carry an N-terminal melittin signal peptide and a C-terminal hexa-His tag. Trimerized FLD-foldon protein contains a C-terminal T4 foldon trimerization motif. The codon usage of genes encoding HA ectodomains and miniH1 [24] was optimized to enhance protein expression in Sf9 insect cells. To retain and stabilize the pre-fusion state of HA, the protease cleavage site in all HA proteins was removed by single mutation R329Q [53].

We used PyMOL and Coot software for visualization of HA structure models (PDB ID: 4LXV, 5UGY, and 4M40). Utilizing structural information, we identified several positions for introducing interprotomer cysteine bonds in the HAs from WA11, NC99, and FLD strains [54,55]. The proposed amino acid substitutions are as follows: WA11#1 (HA2 K68C, HA2 R76C), WA11#2 (HA1 L20C, HA2 K47C), WA11#3 (HA2 G4C, HA2 N117C), WA11#4 (insert C8a in between G8 and F9 in HA2, HA2 K121C), WA11#5 (insert C8aG8b in between G8 and F9 in HA2, HA2 K121C), WA11#6 (HA2 N124C, HA2 G134C), NC99#1 (HA2 K68C, HA2 R76C), NC99#2 (HA1 L20C, HA2 G47C), NC99#3 (HA2 G4C, HA2 N117C), NC99#4 (insert C8a in between G8 and F9 in HA2, HA2 K121C), NC99#5 (insert C8aG8b in between G8 and F9 in HA2, HA2 K121C), NC99#6 (HA2 K58C, HA2 E97C), FLD#1 (HA2 I6C, HA2 A117C), FLD#2 (insert C67a in between S67 and G68 in HA2, HA2 E79C), and FLD#3 (HA2 K124C, HA2 N135C).

Expression and purification of recombinant HAs and mAbs

The recombinant baculovirus expression system was employed for the secretory expression of recombinant proteins [56]. Recombinant HA proteins were purified via metal affinity chromatography using Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow resin (Cat.#: 17371202, Cytiva, U.S.). The HA trimer proteins were further purified by SEC using the Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cat.#: 28990944, Cytiva, U.S.) on ÄKTA pure 25 L1 system (Cat.#: 29018225, Cytiva, U.S.).

The genes encoding the light and heavy chains of HA stem-specific human mAbs CR9114 [11], 3I14 [57], CT149 [13], CR6261 [9], or FISW84 [58] were cloned into the pFastBac Dual plasmid vector, under the control of the p10 promoter and polyhedrin promoter, respectively. The light and heavy chains have the GP64 signal peptide and the melittin signal peptide at their N-terminals, respectively. The recombinant nanobody SD83 [59] carries the N-terminal melittin signal peptide and the C-terminal FLAG-tag. The gene encoding the recombinant nanobody SD83 was cloned into pFastBac plasmid vector for expression in Sf9 insect cells. The mAbs were purified using protein A resin (Cat.#: SA015025, SMART, Changzhou) and the recombinant SD83 was purified using anti-FLAG affinity resin (Cat.#: SM009005, SMART, Changzhou). The mAbs and SD83 underwent further purification and analysis by SEC using the Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column.

Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining and western blotting

The recombinant HA proteins, miniH1, and mAbs were separated in SDS-PAGE, and the gels were stained with 0.25% Coomassie brilliant blue dye R-250 solution (Cat.#: C8430, Solarbio, China). In a western blot immunoassay, following SDS-PAGE separation, the proteins were transferred from polyacrylamide gel onto a methanol-activated polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The blotted PVDF membrane was blocked in 10% skim milk dissolved in 0.5% Tween 20-containing Tris-buffered saline (TBST), at room temperature for 2 h, and then probed with anti-hexa-His tag mouse IgG mAb (Cat.#: 105327-MM02T-H, Sino Biological Inc., U.S.). The PVDF membranes were washed with TBST three times at room temperature, and were incubated with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Proteintech Group, Inc., U.S.) diluted with 5% skim milk in TBST, at room temperature for 1 h. The binding was visualized using the ClarityTM Western ECL Substrate (Cat.#: 1705061, Bio-Rad, U.S.).

Negative stain transmission electron microscopy analysis

To analyze the morphology of All modified and unmodified HA ectodomain proteins, negatively stained protein samples were visualized using a 120 kV TEM (Thermofisher Scientific Company). For negative staining experiment, HA proteins of WA11wt, NC99wt, and FLDwt were affinity-purified, and HA trimer proteins of WA11#5, NC99#2, and FLD#1 were further purified by SEC. For each sample, 5 µl of protein solution (0.02 mg/ml) was applied to a glow-discharged carbon-coated grid. After 60 s, the excess solution was blotted up with a filter paper, followed by staining with 2% w/v uranyl acetate. Briefly, 4 µl of uranyl acetate was added to the protein sample for a 10 s incubation, and then was removed. This process was repeated twice. Until the grid was air dried, the negatively stained samples were detected using a 120 kV TEM and micrographs were collected using a ceta 16M camera with a magnification of 73,000 ×. Then, the collected micrographs were processed by using cryoSPARC4.4 software. In the 2D class averaging process, single particles selected from TEM data were aligned relative to each other and computationally classified based on similarities.

Differential scanning fluorimetry

The thermal stabilities of recombinant proteins were studied by utilizing the Applied Biosystems 7500 rapid real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR system (Thermo Fisher). For thermal stability analysis, HA proteins of WA11wt, NC99wt, and FLDwt were affinity-purified, and HA trimer proteins of WA11#5, NC99#2, and FLD#1 were further purified by SEC. DSF assays were performed in 20 μl volume system. The temperature scans were conducted between 25 and 99 °C, at a heating rate of 1 °C/min. The test samples containing proteins at a final concentration of ∼ 0.1 mg/ml and appropriately diluted SYPROTM Orange dye (Cat.#: S6651, Thermo Fisher, U.S.) were thoroughly mixed by centrifugation and measured using DSF. The PBS was used as the negative control sample.

Immunization and viral challenge experiments

Specific-pathogen-free six-to-eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd, Changsha, China. Vaccines were formulated with 10 μg HA protein in 50 μl of sterile PBS adjuvanted with 50 μl of incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (IFA, Cat.#: F5506, Sigma, U.S.). Mice were intraperitoneally immunized twice, at a three-week interval (Figure S4, Table S2), and submandibular blood collections were performed one day before the prime immunization and two weeks after the last immunization. The blood samples were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min and centrifuged at 4,000 × g at room temperature for 5 min, then serum samples were collected and stored at −20 °C for later use. In challenge experiments, mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital and intranasally infected with 4 × mLD50 H1N1 virus strain PR8 or X-179A, or 6 × mLD50 influenza B virus strain Iba85. Body weight changes and survival rates were monitored daily for 14 days post-infection. Weight loss of over 30% was used as the experimental endpoint at which mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation.

ELISA

The binding activities of recombinant HAs to the mAbs CT149, 3I14, CR9114, CR6261, FISW84, and the nanobody SD83, were assessed using ELISA. In brief, 96-well flat-bottom immunoplates were coated with 200 ng/well HA proteins in 50 μl of 0.05 M carbonate bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) at 4 °C overnight. The immunoplates were washed three times with 300 μl of PBS containing 0.5% Tween 20 (PBST) and blocked with 300 μl of 10% skim milk in PBST at room temperature for 2 h. After incubation with three-fold serial dilutions of mAbs or SD83 at room temperature for 1 h, the immunoplates were washed three times with PBST, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Cat.#: SA00001-17, Proteintech Group, Inc., U.S.) or HRP-conjugated anti-FLAG tag IgG (Cat.#: HRP-66008, Proteintech Group, Inc., U.S.) at room temperature for 1 h. After the immunoplates were washed three times with PBST, the 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Cat.#: PR1200, Solarbio, China) was used for colour development and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Cat.#: DLJ-200, DLJ Bio-Tech, Jiangsu, China).

For titrating serum antibodies specific to influenza viruses and miniH1, 500 ng per well formalin-inactivated PR8, X-179A, SC09, WL23, NJ76, JS02, B/AH14, or B/Iba85 viruses or 200 ng per well miniH1 protein was used to coat 96-well immunoplates. Three-fold serial dilutions of mouse serum samples with an initial 100 dilution factor were added for binding antigens. Bound IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a antibodies were detected by HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Cat.#: SA00001-1, Proteintech Group, Inc., U.S.), HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG1 antibody (Cat.#: SA00012-1, Proteintech Group, Inc., U.S.), and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG2a antibody (Cat.#: SA00012-2, Proteintech Group, Inc., U.S.), respectively.

Heat stability of the disulfide stabilized HA proteins was evaluated by using ELISA. Briefly, HA proteins in sterile PBS solution at a concentration of 1 mg/ml were heat-treated at 4, 20 °C, or 37 °C for 36 h, or 60 °C for 30 min, before coating immunoplates. Bovine serum albumin protein in PBS at a concentration of 1 mg/ml was used as a negative antigen control for rulling out nonspecific binding of mAbs. The antigen-coated immunoplates were incubated with serially diluted HA stem-specific mAbs at room temperature for 1 h. The incubation of X-179A-specific mice hyperimmune sera was a positive control group. Bound mAbs and mouse serum antibodies were detected by HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG antibody (Cat.#: SA00001-17, Proteintech Group, Inc., U.S.) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Cat.#: SA00001-1, Proteintech Group, Inc., U.S.), respectively.

Determination of influenza virus titres from mouse lungs

Four mice from each FLD HA immunization group were sacrified on the 4th day post infection with 0.2 × mLD50 Iba85 virus for lung virus titration. Mouse lungs were excised and homogenized in 10% (w:v) serum-free DMEM medium. Lung homogenates were cleared from tissue debris by centrifugation at 10,000 × g, 4 °C, for 10 min. MDCK cells were seeded at 2 × 104 cells per well in 96-well cell culture plates and cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2, overnight. On the second day, MDCK cell cultures were infected with 100 μl of ten-fold serial dilutions of the cleared supernatant from lung homogenate samples for 1 h. After washing three times with sterile PBS, the infected MDCK cells were cultured in 100 μl serum-free DMEM medium supplemented with 2 μg/ml of TPCK-treated trypsin (Cat.#: 4370285, Sigma, U.S.), 0.1 mM MEM non-essential amino acid (Cat.#: 11140-050, Gibco, U.S.), 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and 100 U/ml penicillin (Cat.#: P1400, Solarbio, China) at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for three days. The presence of virus in the supernatant was assayed by measuring hemagglutination activity in the supernatant. The TCID50/ml values were calculated using the Reed and Munch method [60]. All the infection experiments were performed in a BSL-2 laboratory from Hunan University.

Hemagglutination inhibition assay

To remove non-specific agglutination inhibitors from mouse serum samples, serum samples were incubated with three volumes of receptor-destroying enzyme solution (RDE, Cat No. 340122, Denka Seiken, Japan) at 37 °C for 16 h, followed by a 30 min heat-inactivation at 56 °C and further dilution to 1:8 with PBS. Twenty-five μl of two-fold serially diluted RDE-treated sera (starting from 1:8) were incubated with 25 μl of 4 HA units of influenza virus at room temperature for 1 h. A 50-μl volume of 1% rooster red blood cell suspension was added, and the reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for a further 30 min or until cells in the red blood cell control wells had fully settled. Hemagglutination activity was determined by numerating the wells where red blood cells clotted. HAI titre was the reciprocal of the highest dilution of serum that completely prevented hemagglutination.

Microneutralization assay

The serum samples were pretreated by heat inactivation at 56 °C for 30 min. Fifty μl of serum-free DMEM medium containing 100 × TCID50 influenza virus was incubated at room temperature for 1 h with 50 μl of two-fold serially diluted serum samples (starting from 1:10). The mixture was added to MDCK cell culture, which had been grown to 80% confluence in 96-well plates, and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 1 h. After washing with sterile PBS three times, 100 μl of DMEM medium supplemented with 2 μg/ml of TPCK-treated trypsin (Cat.#: 4370285, Sigma, U.S.), 0.1 mM MEM non-essential amino acid (Cat.#: 11140-050, Gibco, U.S.), 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and 100 U/ml penicillin (Cat.#: P1400, Solarbio, China) was added to the cell culture, and cells were grown at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for three days to allow time for several rounds of the full viral life cycle. Finally, the supernatant is used in a hemagglutination assay with red blood cells to determine the presence of virus and the degree of neutralization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 software. An unpaired t-test was used to determine if there is a significant difference between test groups in serological assays. Statistical analyses of survival rates between mouse challenge groups were performed using a log-rank test. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In figures, asterisks *, **, ***, ****, and N.S. represent p-values less than 0.05, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001, and not significant, respectively.
