Analysis of Mucosal Wave and Vibratory Patterns in Vocally Healthy Children and Adults
Authors: Bernhard Jakubass, Hamide Ghaemi, Maggie Lynn Stall, Dimitar D. Deliyski, Rita R. Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo conduct a systematic investigation and comparison of the mucosal wave of vocal fold oscillations, the longitudinal anterior–posterior (AP) vibratory patterns of opening and closing, and posterior glottal gap in vocally healthy children (girls and boys) and adults (women and men).MethodsHigh‐speed videoendoscopy (HSV) recordings of the sustained phonations of 40 subjects (10 men, 10 women, 10 boys, and 10 girls) were analyzed. For each recording, three sections of stable sustained phonation were selected. Two experts rated independently the anterior and posterior commissure points, the mucosal wave, and the AP vibratory patterns during the opening and closing phases of vocal fold vibration. A third rater mediated a consensus. Statistical significance between participant groups was evaluated using Pearson's chi‐square test and post hoc Mann–Whitney U tests.ResultsMucosal wave amplitude was highest in children, whereas adult males exhibited lower amplitudes (p < 0.05). Posterior‐to‐anterior (p2a) opening patterns dominated in all groups, especially among children and females, while men showed more variation (p < 0.001). Anterior‐to‐posterior (a2p) closing pattern was most common in girls and females, whereas simultaneous (sim) closure was predominant in boys (p < 0.01). Posterior glottal gap was more frequently visible in children and females, while most adult males showed no visible gap (p < 0.001).ConclusionThis study identified age‐ and gender‐related differences in vocal fold vibration. The findings offer key reference data for age‐appropriate clinical visual‐perceptual voice assessment.

Introduction

High‐speed videoendoscopy (HSV) is a novel technique for assessing vocal fold (VF) vibratory behaviors. HSV records the complete vibratory cycle at frame rates of several thousands of frames per second (fps), in contrast to stroboscopy, which records at 30 fps while simulating the intra‐cycle periodicity, assuming regularity in VF vibration. The high temporal resolution of HSV provides information about both normal and pathological laryngeal function by enabling the detection of intermittent vibratory abnormalities [1, 2]. HSV facilitates the evaluation of mucosal wave and vibratory patterns irrespective of the degree of dysphonia and consequently provides a comprehensive understanding of how these features change with age, gender, and medical conditions [2, 3, 4, 5, 6].

The information obtained from HSV offers insights into important clinical features. Vocal clarity and intensity are directly correlated with amplitude, which measures the lateral displacement of the VFs during vibration [7, 8, 9]. Anatomical and physiological elements such as VF length and tension affect the fundamental frequency, which is the rate of vibratory cycles [10]. The mucosal wave, a crucial aspect of typical phonation, is the wave‐like movement of the surface layers of the VFs and is essential for both vocal health and effective voice production [11, 12]. Additional factors that reveal laryngeal coordination and overall vibratory efficiency include vibratory symmetry, periodicity, and glottal closure [1, 7, 11, 13, 14]. Since HSV quantifies VF vibrations with high temporal resolution, it is especially useful for analyzing irregular vibrations and intermittent variations in mucosal wave motion, unlike traditional stroboscopic imaging [1].

Because developmental variations in laryngeal shape and function significantly influence VF biomechanics, HSV has proven especially useful in research involving pediatric populations [15]. Research has shown that pediatric larynges have distinct vibratory properties compared to adults, such as lower vibratory amplitude and unique glottal closure patterns [1, 9, 11, 16], due to variations in VF structure and developmental architecture [17, 18, 19]. Research using HSV to examine vibratory characteristics has evolved chronologically. Initial studies by Patel et al. established baseline differences between adults and children, demonstrating substantial differences in vibratory patterns between children and adults [1, 9, 11, 16]. Compared to adults, children exhibit a longer opening phase, a more prominent posterior glottal gap, and greater prevalence of the presence of a posterior glottal gap during phonation [16]. By quantitatively analyzing phonovibrograms, Döllinger et al. reported notable differences in the anterior–posterior (AP) spatiotemporal pattern of opening and closing between children and adults, underlining the need for further exploration of pediatric VF behavior [10]. They showed that the closing was more abrupt and shutter‐like in children through quantitative phonovibrogram analysis [10]. Subsequently, Patel et al. expanded this work through spatiotemporal analysis and structured light laser HSV endoscopy, revealing notable variations in vibratory amplitude and cycle regularity [7, 20]. All of these studies rely on quantitative analysis tools. However, in clinical practice, visual‐perceptual assessment of vocal fold dynamics remains the standard approach. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have investigated differences in anterior–posterior modes of vibration using visual‐perceptual analysis in children. For instance, Orlikoff et al. demonstrated substantial differences in the longitudinal phase of VF vibration between adult males and females, with males showing distinct phase lag characteristics; however, comparable data on children remain unclear [21].

Another critical aspect of VF vibration is the mucosal wave, which reflects the biomechanical properties of the VFs during oscillation. These waves, visible during high‐speed imaging or stroboscopic examination, provide essential insights into VF health and function. In adults, the mucosal wave is well characterized, with differences documented between males and females in amplitude and propagation [3]. However, in children, whose laryngeal structures are still developing, the biomechanics of the mucosal wave remain understudied. To the best of our knowledge, none of the above studies have investigated mucosal wave differences between children and adults.

The current study aims to address these gaps in the literature by investigating differences in mucosal wave and vibratory patterns between vocally healthy adults and children. This research intends to find mucosal wave differences and vibratory patterns of VF opening, closing, along with the presence of a posterior gap while identifying developmental patterns through visual‐perceptual analyses of vocal fold biomechanics.

Materials and Methods

Participants

A total of 40 participants were recruited at the Vocal Physiology and Imaging Laboratory, Indiana University (IU), after signing appropriate consent/assent forms approved by the IU Institutional Review Board. The study included 20 adults and 20 children as follows: 10 men aged 19–45 years (Mean = 27.2, SD = 9.3), 10 women aged 19–40 years (Mean = 26.1, SD = 7.2), 10 boys aged 5–11 years (Mean = 8.0, SD = 2.1), and 10 girls aged 6–10 years (Mean = 8.4, SD = 1.3), all without any voice disorders. Participants were included if they had a normal overall voice rating on the GRBAS scale [22], and a negative history of voice pathology, active upper respiratory tract infection, and laryngeal or VF surgery. Adults had a negative history of smoking, and female participants were not pregnant at the time of the study. Additional exclusion criteria were any neurological, respiratory, or systemic disorders affecting voice production, as well as auditory or visual deficits that could interfere with participation.

Data Collection

High‐speed videoendoscopic recordings were captured at 4000 fps with a spatial resolution of 512 × 256 pixels (PENTAX, Model 9710, Montvale, New Jersey). The recording duration was 4 s. The participants were asked to sustain/i:/at self‐selected habitual pitch and loudness. Endoscopic recordings were obtained using a standard 10‐mm rigid endoscope without the application of a topical anesthetic to the oral mucosa.

Video Analysis

The analysis of the HSV recordings was done in three steps: identification of the anterior and posterior commissures, investigation of the posterior glottal gap, the opening and closing patterns, and evaluation of the mucosal wave. For each video recording, the analysis was performed on three sections of stable phonation, further defined as tokens. Each token had a duration of 62.5 ms (250 frames). All analysis tasks were performed by two expert raters independently using custom MATLAB (version 2024b, MathWorks Inc., Natick, Massachusetts) scripts (one expert in clinical evaluation of voice and one expert in voice analysis engineering). Disagreement between the two raters was resolved by discussion with a third (senior expert) rater.

Anterior and Posterior Commissures Determination

The two raters determined the positions of the anterior and posterior commissures using a custom‐built MATLAB graphical user interface (Figure 1).

Graphical User Interface (GUI) for the rating of the anterior and posterior commissure positions. Both commissures and the connecting anteriorposterior line (AP line) are highlighted in green.

Mucosal Wave Evaluation

The lateral movement of the mucosa of the VF was examined from the view of the entire VF to visually rate mucosal waves. The extent of the mucosal wave was visually rated in 25% increments from the anterior–posterior line (0%, defining the glottal midline) towards the lateral VF edges (100%), separately for the left and right VFs [12]. Vertical anchor lines were placed at 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% to guide the lateral movement ratings (Figure 2).

Based on the manual adjustable position of the line for 100% vocal fold (VF) width (brown) the positions of the 25% (orange), 50% (blue), and 75% (purple) VF width lines are adjusted automatically to assist with the mucosal wave rating. The colors match the plot in Figure 4.

Vibration Pattern and Posterior Glottal Gap Analysis

Digital kymograms (DKG) were derived at five equally spaced locations along the posteroanterior axis of the VFs, namely, at 30%, 42.5%, 55%, 67.5%, and 80% of the anteriorposterior distance, with the most posterior DKG derived at 30% and the most anterior DKG at 80% (Figure 3). This design was chosen based on previous research by Orlikoff et al. and adjusted to the current data set [21]. For the longitudinal phase difference, it was evaluated where the initiation of tissue separation (opening) and the first tissue contact (closing) occurred. The rating categories for the opening and closing patterns are as follows: anterior to posterior (a2p), posterior to anterior (p2a), starting at both ends and progressing towards the middle (ends), starting in the middle and progressing towards both ends (mid), and simultaneous (sim) (Figure 3). The posterior glottal gap was rated by the most anterior kymogram line that showed a permanent gap between the VFs. In the case of visible vocal fold contact in all DKGs, a distinction was made whether a posterior glottal gap smaller than 30% was visible in the HSV (< 30%) or non‐visible at all (NV).

Statistical Analysis

The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was used to evaluate agreement on numeric ratings (mucosal wave) between the two raters. Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and outcome variables. The statistical significance of the differences between participant groups was evaluated using Pearson's chi‐square test and post hoc Mann–Whitney U tests. In cases of statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), the effect size was computed with Cohen's d for the numeric measures. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Inter‐Rater Reliability

The inter‐rater reliability across the entire dataset for the commissure ratings was 11.5 pixels (posterior) and 10.6 pixels (anterior). For the left VF, the ICC value was 0.706 (95% Confidence Interval = [0.490, 0.820], F value = 7.216, p value < 0.001) and 0.636 for the right VF (95% Confidence Interval = [0.410, 0.770], F value = 5.460, p value < 0.001), representing good agreement for both sides. For the non‐numeric ratings (vibratory pattern and posterior glottal gap), the agreement between the two raters was measured using Cohen's Kappa. For the opening patterns, Cohen's Kappa was 0.399; for closing patterns, 0.270; and for the posterior glottal gap ratings, 0.528, representing fair to moderate agreement between the two raters.

Mucosal Wave Evaluation

In children's groups, the girls primarily demonstrated 100% mucosal wave amplitude on both vocal folds (66.7% left; 63.3% right), followed by 75% amplitude (23.3% left, 36.7% right) and 50% (10.0% left, 0.0% right). The boys exhibited a more variable distribution, with the highest frequencies at 100% (46.7% both sides) and 75% (40.0% both sides) amplitudes. The lower amplitude categories (25% and 50%) were minimally observed among boys (6.7% both categories, both sides) (Figure 4).

Qualitatively, among vocally healthy adults, different patterns of mucosal wave amplitude were observed between females and males. Most women demonstrated a 75% mucosal wave amplitude (63.3% left, 46.7% right), followed by 50% (30.0% left, 40.0% right) and 100% (6.7% left, 13.3% right). Men, in contrast, showed the highest occurrence at 50% amplitude (56.7% left, 53.3% right), with lower frequencies at 75% (33.3% both sides) and 100% (10.0% left, 13.3% right).

Age‐related differences were more prominent than gender‐based ones. Both right and left mucosal waves significantly differed between girls and women (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.2 and 1.1), girls and men (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.4), and boys and men (p = 0.012 and 0.004, Cohen's d = 0.9 and 1.0), reflecting large effect sizes in most comparisons. No significant differences were found between women and men or between girls and boys for left and right mucosal waves (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Opening Pattern Analysis

Among children, girls predominantly (90.0%) demonstrated (p2a) openings (90.0%), while 10.0% exhibited sim openings. The boys also showed a high frequency of p2a opening (80.0%), with smaller proportions exhibiting a2p (3.3%), ends (3.3%), and sim (13.3%) (Figure 5).

An analysis of anterior–posterior vibratory opening patterns revealed that the women's group exclusively exhibited p2a opening patterns (100%), with no instances of other patterns. In the men's group, the p2a pattern was the most frequent (46.7%), followed by a2p (33.3%) and mid (20.0%) (Figure 5).

With all other groups predominantly showing an a2p opening pattern, only the men's group was statistically significantly different (p < 0.001) from (all) other groups, by showing more variety in opening patterns (Table 1).

Closing Pattern Analysis

Among children, the girls predominantly exhibited a2p closure (70.0%), with sim closure observed in 23.3% of cases and mid closure in 6.7%. The boys mainly demonstrated sim closure (60.0%), followed by a2p closure (23.3%), mid closure (13.3%), and p2a closure (3.3%) (Figure 6).

In terms of anterior–posterior vibratory closing patterns, a2p closure was the most prevalent pattern among women (56.7%), followed by mid closure (40.0%). The sim pattern was observed in only 3.3% of instances. Among men, p2a closure was the most common (40.0%), while a2p and sim closure each occurred in 20.0% of cases, with mid closure also noted in 20.0% (Figure 6).

The difference between the groups was more related to gender than age, with statistically significant differences between women and men (p = 0.001), as well as between women and boys (p = 0.001), and girls and boys (p = 0.007), (Table 1).

Posterior Glottal Gap Assessment

Posterior glottal analysis revealed distinct patterns across groups. In the adult population, the women demonstrated a measurable posterior glottal gap during the closed phase of sustained phonation, with the majority showing a 42.5% gap (43.3%) and smaller proportions showing gaps of < 30% (20.0%), NV (20.0%), and 30% (16.7%). In contrast, most men (83.3%) exhibited a non‐visible glottal gap (NV), with only minor proportions demonstrating gaps of 30% (13.3%) and < 30% (3.3%) (Figure 7).

In the pediatric population, the girls exhibited posterior gaps, predominantly at 42.5% (33.3%), followed by NV (26.7%), 30% (20.0%), < 30% (13.3%), and 55% (6.7%). Boys demonstrated the largest gaps at 30% (36.7%), followed by 42.5% (30.0%), < 30% (26.7%), and NV (6.7%) (Figure 7).

Discussion

The present study provides a detailed, systematic, visual‐perceptual evaluation of mucosal wave, anterior‐posterior vibratory behaviors, and posterior gap in vocally healthy children and adults using HSV. Our findings reveal age and sex‐specific differences in the extent of mucosal wave propagation, the directionality of vibratory opening and closing along the anterior‐posterior axis, and glottal closure configuration. Notably, the mucosal wave excursions were greater in children than in adults, with girls exhibiting the most extensive wave propagation, while women also showed larger amplitudes than men. The opening pattern of vocal fold vibration was predominantly posterior to anterior (p2a), particularly in children and females. Closing patterns varied by group, with girls and women showing a higher prevalence of anterior to posterior (a2p) closure, while boys mostly showed simultaneous (sim) closure. The posterior glottal gap was most frequently observed in children and females.

Mucosal Wave Characteristics

Mucosal wave propagation is a hallmark of healthy phonation, and in our study, children, especially girls, demonstrated larger mucosal waves compared to adults. More than 60% of girls exhibited lateral mucosal wave excursions covering the entire medial surface (rated as 100%) of both vocal folds, while adult men most frequently showed only partial propagation, with the mucosal wave covering approximately 50% of the fold width. Our findings indicate higher pliability in pediatric VF tissue. Also, Patel et al. found indications that the VF tissue in children is more flexible than in adults [1]. The richly elastic cover layers in children facilitate broader wave propagation, which is gradually attenuated as collagen density increases with age [17, 18, 23].

Longitudinal AP Vibratory Pattern Differences

In our dataset, the posterior to anterior (p2a) opening pattern dominated across all four groups, particularly in women (100%) and girls (90%). This mirrors earlier findings by Orlikoff et al. who demonstrated that especially healthy adult female speakers often show posterior to anterior (p2a) separation during phonation [21]. The findings from female speakers are also similar to a quantitative analysis of anterior–posterior vibratory patterns using phonovibrograms by Döllinger et al. [10] This sequence may facilitate efficient airflow initiation in speakers with smaller or more pliable glottal structures, which is characteristic of children and adult females.

In contrast, closure patterns diverged significantly by age and sex. The anterior to posterior (a2p) closure was predominant among girls (70.0%) and women (56.7%). Orlikoff et al. even reported 100% anterior to posterior (a2p) for women in a comfortable and breathy voice [21], while boys in our study exhibited sim closure most frequently (60.0%). The variability in closing patterns, particularly among boys, reflects less stable vibratory control during glottal termination, which is consistent with previous phonovibrogram‐based findings from Döllinger et al. indicating increased temporal variability and reduced control in pediatric laryngeal motion [10]. Moreover, Döllinger et al. reported that children, regardless of sex, demonstrated mostly a synchronous vocal fold closure pattern [10]. The simultaneous (sim) closure may represent a transitional phase in vibratory maturation, preceding the development of more directionally consistent patterns seen in adults.

Posterior Glottal Gap

A critical finding of this study is the disproportionately high prevalence of posterior glottal gap among children and adult females, contrasted with its relative absence in adult males. This is in agreement with Patel et al. who reported posterior gap configurations in 85% of girls, 68% of boys, 75% of women, and 54% of men during modal phonation [16]. Also, Orlikoff et al. reported more and larger posterior gaps for women than men during comfortable and breathy voicing [21]. Also, Linville, as well as Biever and Bless reported similar findings [24, 25]. The consistency of this configuration in females and prepubertal children, even in the absence of pathology, has been attributed to anatomical and histological differences. Specifically, the cartilaginous glottis is relatively larger in children and women, leading to incomplete approximation of the posterior vocal folds during phonation. Additionally, females show slower elongation of the membranous vocal folds during development, which may delay the achievement of full posterior contact [16].

Importantly, the posterior glottal gap in children should not be misinterpreted as glottal insufficiency or vocal fold paralysis. Instead, it reflects a normative developmental trait, underlining the necessity for pediatric‐specific diagnostic criteria. Our visual‐perceptual observations substantiate prior high‐speed imaging and glottographic findings and suggest that the presence of a posterior glottal gap in children is not a pathological marker but rather a normal feature of immature vocal fold anatomy and biomechanics [26].

Clinical Implications

The normative data presented here provide a benchmark for distinguishing healthy pediatric laryngeal physiology from pathology. The results highlight important age‐ and sex‐related differences in vocal fold vibratory characteristics, underscoring the need for clinicians to apply age and sex‐specific interpretations during laryngeal imaging. The findings emphasize that certain features often considered abnormal in adults, such as extensive mucosal waves, posterior‐to‐anterior (p2a) opening patterns, simultaneous (sim) closure, or posterior glottal gaps, may be completely normal in children, rather than indicators of pathology. Similarly, the predominance of anterior‐to‐posterior closure patterns in females reflects physiological differences that must be considered during assessment. Misinterpretation of these developmental and sex‐specific traits could lead to overdiagnosis or inappropriate intervention. Together, these data emphasize that applying a single normative framework across all patient groups risks overdiagnosis, particularly in women and children, and highlight the importance of age‐ and sex‐specific reference values when planning treatment, counseling patients, and monitoring outcomes.

From a diagnostic perspective, recognizing the normative vibratory profiles in pediatric populations will improve accuracy in differentiating voice disorders from developmental variants. It also highlights the limitations of relying solely on adult‐derived assessment criteria or tools like videostroboscopy, which lack the temporal resolution needed to capture pediatric vibratory nuances. Integrating HSV into pediatric voice clinics can offer more precise identification of deviant vibratory behaviors. Although stroboscopy is the preferred assessment tool for clinical evaluation, it is limited for evaluating pediatric vocal fold vibration motion [27]. As it relies on periodic vibration to generate a composite image, its utility is limited in children, who have high fundamental frequencies and often present with a reduced ability to sustain stable phonation during endoscopic laryngeal examination [16, 28]. Several studies, including the present one, have demonstrated both qualitative and quantitative differences in vibratory motion in children using high‐speed videoendoscopy [1, 7, 10, 11, 16, 29, 30, 31]. However, high‐speed videoendoscopy is not yet widely integrated into routine pediatric voice clinics due to factors such as the lack of a compact, clinically ready setup in the United States and demands of data processing and interpretation. As normative pediatric datasets expand, and automated analysis becomes more clinically accessible, high‐speed video endoscopy holds promise as a valuable complement to stroboscopy, offering supporting data that can strengthen diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning for children with voice disorders. In the meantime, findings from the current study provide valuable evidence to guide the interpretation of videostroboscopy in children, helping clinicians make more informed assessments and treatment decisions. This trajectory mirrors the role of early high‐speed film studies by Hans von Leden and Paul Moore, which laid the groundwork for the widespread adoption of videostroboscopy in clinical practice [32, 33, 34].

Future Directions

Findings of this study underscore developmental differences in the physiological mechanisms underlying vibratory motion in children. Future studies should evaluate the three‐dimensional changes in vibratory physiology by combining high‐speed videoendoscopy with laser imaging and computational modeling [8, 9, 20, 31, 35, 36, 37, 38]. Longitudinal studies are also needed to map developmental changes in vibratory function across the lifespan, which would enhance understanding of how vocal fold vibrations evolve from childhood to adulthood. Finally, extending this research to encompass a range of pediatric voice pathologies will provide more precise, clinically relevant information to guide assessment and treatment.

Limitations

Despite the novel insights offered, the study sample size (N = 40, n = 10 per group) limits the statistical power to generalize findings across broader populations. Stratifying children by narrower age bands could also uncover more nuanced developmental trends. Extending the study to other clinical centers could not only increase the overall sample size but also broaden the ability to generalize. Although the frame rate of 4000 fps has been shown to be adequate for visual ratings, some vibratory features, especially in children, could benefit from increased precision with higher frame rates [39].

Conclusion

This study establishes normative patterns of vocal fold vibratory behavior and mucosal wave characteristics across age and gender groups, revealing significant developmental and sex‐related differences in laryngeal biomechanics. The findings demonstrate that children and females exhibit distinct vibratory signatures characterized by increased mucosal wave amplitudes, predominantly posterior‐to‐anterior opening patterns, and enhanced posterior glottal gaps. These results provide essential baseline data for developing age‐appropriate clinical assessment protocols and contribute to our understanding of normal vocal fold development. The systematic characterization of these findings represents a significant advancement in pediatric voice research and establishes a foundation for future investigations of developmental voice disorders.
