The moderating effect of gender on the relationship between digital intelligence and digital amnesia
Authors: S James Robert, S Kadhiravan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital amnesia refers to the phenomenon where people tend to forget information that they store digitally, and relying heavily on digital devices to remember the information. The ease of access to digital devices may encourage digital dependence, which could lead to digital amnesia. This study provides a preliminary understanding of the association between digital intelligence and digital amnesia among college students and the moderating role of gender in the relationship. This cross-sectional study has employed a stratified random sampling technique to recruit 1265 students via the survey method. The results revealed a significant negative association between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. Findings also indicated that males reported having higher level of digital amnesia, and females reported higher level of digital intelligence. Furthermore, gender played a significant moderator role between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. Overall, this study has provided a novel finding of the moderating role of gender in relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence in the Indian context. Furthermore, the scores of digital amnesia in this study raise a concern over the effectiveness of current sex education in India. The scores may underscore the need for educational initiatives that address the adverse effects of digital amnesia and emphasize the importance of promoting digital intelligence.Keywords: Digital amnesia, Digital intelligence, Gender, Moderation, College students

Introduction

The influence of digital technologies has been reshaping the global landscape of education for a significant period, becoming so deeply integrated into daily life that distancing ourselves from these changes is no longer feasible [7]. The adoption and use of digital technologies such as smartphones, social media, cloud-based systems, robots, and artificial intelligence have increasingly reshaped human perceptions, actions, and environments, and have been associated with a myriad benefits for individuals, organizations, and societies. The internet as a huge network has both benefits and drawbacks. It contains millions of private, public, academic, business, and government channels from local to global scope, with dramatic effects on human life playing a significant role in people’s behaviour and mentality (Young 2004). The greater accessibility of digital technology induced and increased the dependency markedly. Despite the conspicuous benefits, the rise of digital technologies revealed a number of dark sides with grave impacts across multiple domains. Concerns regarding the potential negative impact of digitalization on memory are well-founded. Sparrow et al. [27] conducted one of the earliest studies suggesting that internet use may have a detrimental impact on memory. Their findings, widely cited in scientific literature (over 2150 citations as of February 8, 2025) and discussed in popular media, raised concerns about the cognitive consequences of digital dependence. Subsequently, many research articles have increasingly highlighted the cognitive costs associated with excessive digital engagement. For instance, a study by Sharifian and Zahodne [26] revealed a significant association between high social media usage and increased everyday memory failures. This suggests that the cognitive overload induced by continuous digital interaction may interfere with memory consolidation and retrieval processes. Such findings emphasize the need for a balanced approach to technology use to mitigate its potential cognitive drawbacks.

The internet especially, Wikipedia, ChatGPT and Google search are considered as an extension of human brain or termed as ‘online brain’. Many people struggled to recall simple information since they entrust to their devices, including the phone numbers of partners and parents. With information available at the click of a button, individuals may increasingly fail to commit or process that information in memory [4]. Students, nowadays have poor grasping as well as internalizing problems in online learning. These factors could have been collectively contributed to the emergence of digital amnesia. Students with over-reliant on digital devices would rely excessively on internet through various online activities such as Instagram, Facebook, Twitter, Snapchat which would distract them from focusing on their academics. This tendency would weaken their cognitive processes in turn affects their ability to recall information. These constant distractions and multitasking associated with excessive use of digital devices can further impair concentration, hindering the consolidation of memories which could pave path for digital amnesia.

Theoretical background

In the digital era, the way individuals process, store, and retrieve information has drastically changed. The phenomenon of digital amnesia, referring to the tendency to forget information readily accessible through digital devices, is theoretically supported by Cognitive Load Theory (CLT; [29]). CLT explains how the limitations of working memory influence learning and information processing. According to CLT, working memory can only hold a small amount of information at a time, with a maximum duration of about 20 s [17]. When a task demands more mental effort than working memory can handle, it leads to cognitive overload, which makes learning less effective and weakens memory retention. In the context of digital device usage, the constant flow of notifications, multitasking, and easy access to information at the click of a button can overload working memory with extraneous demands, leaving fewer resources for learning. This overload can interfere with the germane cognitive load needed for processing and storing information in long-term memory [17].

In the context of college students, who are constantly engaging with online learning platforms, social media, and entertainment, the overload of information is likely to disrupt optimal cognitive functioning, increasing the risk of digital amnesia [9]. Digital intelligence, the ability to use digital tools efficiently and responsibly, can help manage cognitive load by minimizing distractions, organizing information effectively, and prioritizing essential tasks, thereby supporting memory performance. Individuals with higher digital intelligence may employ strategies such as managing distractions, organizing digital information, and prioritizing essential tasks, thereby reducing unnecessary cognitive load and improving memory performance.

Digital amnesia in India

Despite digital amnesia is becoming increasingly significant in our digital age, research on this phenomenon remains relatively scarce, particularly within the Indian context. The majority of people in India agreed that they use the internet as an online extension of their brain [28]. Savarimuthu and Subramanian [24] highlighted that youth differed significantly in their digital amnesia based on their demographic categories such as gender and family type. Robert et al. [23] developed a scale to assess digital amnesia among Indian college students. Furthermore, the it was emphasized that the physical and psychological impairments associated with digital amnesia are not generic, but rather vary depending on the specific digital device involved [23].

As of 2023, India’s smartphone penetration rate was close to 71% [25]. The increasing reliance on gadgets in India has made digital amnesia an alarming concern, particularly among students. The widespread adoption of smartphones and cloud storage applications has led to a significant shift in cognitive functioning, where individuals increasingly depend on digital devices to remember even basic information such as phone numbers, passwords, and events. This over-reliance on digital devices is further exacerbated by the rapid expansion of AI-driven personal assistants and cloud-based information repositories. The rapid evolution of India’s digital landscape, fueled by increased internet accessibility, widespread social media engagement, and the growing work-from-home culture, has significantly contributed to the rising prevalence of digital amnesia. Indian students, in particular, are highly vulnerable due to their extensive engagement with online education, gaming, and social networking [22]. This phenomenon not only affects memory but also influences emotional well-being, sleep patterns, and overall mental health. The need for digital intelligence and cognitive awareness programs is critical to mitigate the long-term implications of digital amnesia in India’s fast-growing technological ecosystem.

Digital amnesia and digital intelligence

Digital intelligence plays a pivotal role in the 21st century as an emerging form of intelligence shaped by continuous human interaction with information and communication technologies. Digital intelligence is defined as a set of social, emotional, and cognitive abilities that allow individuals to meet challenges and adapt to the requirements of digital life [18]. It encompasses a range of competencies, including digital literacy, digital safety, digital communication, digital emotional intelligence, digital safety, and the ability to balance technology use with cognitive well-being. Scholars have argued that recognizing and nurturing this intelligence has the potential to broaden the horizons of teaching and learning, fostering more effective educational practices suited to contemporary digital environments [7]. Moreover, digital intelligence is not limited to academic contexts alone; it significantly influences various dimensions of personal and professional life, shaping individuals’ abilities to engage meaningfully, responsibly, and safely within digital ecosystems [5]. As individuals navigate an increasingly digitalized world, digital intelligence serves as a crucial factor in managing and utilizing digital devices effectively, potentially mitigating memory lapses and enhancing information retention.

Digital intelligence represents the evolving cognitive framework, especially prominent among younger generations, shaped by the rapidly expanding digital landscape. However, excessive reliance on digital devices, without actively engaging cognitive processes, can contribute to digital amnesia. Relying on search engines, cloud storage, and AI assistants for memory reduces the need to actively recall information, which can weaken our natural memory abilities. This increasing dependence is especially concerning for younger generations who have grown up in a digital world, as it may have long-term effects on their cognitive development. Despite its significance, research on the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence remains limited. While studies have explored digital amnesia as a phenomenon, there is still a lack of comprehensive investigation into its predictors, underlying mechanisms, and potential interventions. Studies highlighted that in-depth research with appropriate psychometric tools would be helpful in assessing digital amnesia across the population, especially among youth [23]. The factors that lead to digital amnesia and its possible causes are not yet fully understood in the literature. Digital Intelligence has recently gained attention as a protective factor in managing the risks associated with digital engagement. Students with higher digital intelligence are better equipped to balance digital consumption with cognitive well-being, adopting strategies to prevent cognitive overload and foster mindful technology use [15, 19]. Studies have shown that digital intelligence promotes digital well-being, mitigates the negative effects of excessive technology use, and supports academic achievement [13, 34]. Therefore, studying the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence is crucial to gaining deeper insights into this phenomenon.

Gender differences in digital amnesia

The consequences of digital amnesia may vary depending on individual thresholds. Some individuals tend to be more susceptible to the effects of digital amnesia than others. Studies highlighted that digital amnesia varies by gender, with men often experiencing higher levels than women [4, 12, 24]. Males were found to use gadgets more frequently than females during the pandemic [20]. This heightened dependence may make them more susceptible to digital amnesia, as frequent digital access reduces cognitive effort for memory retention. Interestingly, a study by Swaminathan [28] revealed that female students are increasingly relying on gadgets and the internet as their primary cognitive source.

Gender as a moderator

It is crucial to consider the moderating role of gender in exploring the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. According to Gender Schema Theory [2], gender-related norms and expectations influence how individuals interact with technology and process information. Research has shown gender-based variations in technology use patterns, multitasking tendencies, and cognitive approaches [3, 33]. These variations could influence how digital intelligence affects the experience of digital amnesia, making it essential to explore gender as a potential moderator. The degree of digital amnesia experienced may vary depending on the gender of the individual and level of digital intelligence. Men and women, for instance, might use digital devices in different manners, which could have distinct effects on memory and reliance on digital resources. This difference may indicate that one gender may benefit more than the other from high levels of digital intelligence in terms of lessening digital amnesia. Gaining a better understanding of these gender disparities can help to develop more specialized strategies for managing digital dependency and improving cognitive function across gender groups. It can also shed light on the interactions between digital amnesia and digital intelligence (Fig. 1).

The present study

This study aims to contribute to the existing literature on digital amnesia and digital intelligence by exploring their interrelationship, an area that has not been extensively investigated. To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically examine the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. Current research on gender differences in digital amnesia and digital intelligence is limited, with only one study reporting gender-related variations in digital amnesia [22]. Additionally, the moderating effect of gender on the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence remains underexplored. Therefore, this study advances the field by analyzing how gender moderates the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence among college students in India. The research addresses the following questions:

Is there a relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence?

Is there a relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence?

Does digital intelligence influence digital amnesia?

Does digital intelligence influence digital amnesia?

Does gender moderate the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence?

Method

Cross-sectional study was conducted to investigate the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. Stratified random sampling technique was utilized for the data collection.

Participants

Undergraduate students studying in different colleges in Salem district, Tamil Nadu, India were recruited for the study. A total of 1265 students (51.5% males; 48.5% females) completed the survey.

Measures

Digital Amnesia Scale (DAS)

The DAS was developed by Robert et al. [23] that measures the digital amnesia among college students. DAS has 20 statements with the score of 0 (Never) to 4 (Always). There are three factors of DAS such as digital distraction, digital dependency, and digital detox. The Cronbach alpha value of these factors was found to be 0.92, 0.89, and 0.81. A composite reliability of more than 0.70 during the convergent validity test indicated that the convergent validity was satisfactory.

Digital Intelligence Quotient Questionnaire (DIQ)

The DIQ was developed by Na-Nan et al. [14] that measures the digital intelligence. This questionnaire has 33 items with the score of 1 (Strongly Disagree) and 5 (Strongly Agree). There are eight various dimensions such as digital identity, digital use, digital safety, digital security, digital emotional intelligence, digital communication, digital literacy and digital rights. The Cronbach alpha value for overall digital intelligence was 0.88. This scale has acceptable convergent and divergent validity.

Procedure

Stratified random sampling was used collect the data. Permission was granted by the principals/authorities of 14 undergraduate Arts and Science colleges after consultation. The DAS and DIQ questionnaires were distributed, and required data was provided by students who consented to participate in the research. A total of 1450 participants were initially collected. However, 185 participants were removed from the final analysis after data screening. Finally, 1265 participants were included for analysis. Age of the participants ranged between 17 and 24 years, with a mean age of 18.78 years (SD = 1.55).

Statistical analysis

Jamovi 2.4.14 was used to process and analyze the data. Pearson’s correlation test was employed to investigate the relationship between variables. Independent sample t-test was employed to find out differences in digital amnesia. Simple linear regression analysis was utilized to assess the influence of digital intelligence on digital amnesia.

Moderating effect of gender

Hayes’s [10] SPSS Macro PROCESS analysis Model 1 was used to examine the moderating effect of gender in the association between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. The overall model predicting digital amnesia was statistically significant (F (3, 1261) = 5.02, p = 0.002, R2 = 0.017), indicating that the predictors explain 17% of the variance in digital amnesia. The addition of the interaction term (gender × digital intelligence) significantly improved the model (ΔR2 = 0.042, p = 0.011), confirming that gender moderates the effect of digital intelligence on digital amnesia. Digital intelligence was significantly and negatively associated with digital amnesia (β = −0.36, t (1261) = −3.75, p < 0.001), meaning that higher levels of digital intelligence corresponded with lower digital amnesia. However, gender was not directly linked to digital amnesia (β = 0.53, t (1261) = 1.62, p = 0.108). The interaction effect was significant (β = −0.30, t (1261) = −2.56, p = 0.011), indicating that the negative impact of digital intelligence on digital amnesia was stronger for males than for females.

Figure 2 illustrates the moderation effect of gender in the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. At lower levels of digital intelligence, males (β = −0.36, t (1261) = −3.75, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.17, 0.54]) exhibited higher level of digital amnesia than females (β = −0.05, t (1261) = −0.75, p = 0.46, 95% CI [− 0.09, 0.19]). However, as digital intelligence increased, digital amnesia of males declined more sharply compared to females. This suggests that males experience a stronger negative association between digital amnesia and digital intelligence than females.

Discussion

The present study examines the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence among college students in India, with a specific focus on the moderating role of gender in this relationship. This is a pioneering research to study the moderating effect of gender between these variables. Additionally, the study investigates the influence of digital intelligence on digital amnesia.

Digital intelligence and its various components including digital identity, digital use, digital safety, digital security, digital emotional intelligence, digital communication, digital literacy, and digital rights were negatively correlated with digital distraction, digital dependency, and overall digital amnesia. In contrast, these components were positively associated with digital detox.

Digital identity refers to the way individuals present themselves online, including their personal information, social media profiles, and digital footprint. A competent digital identity can reduce digital distractions and dependency by promoting responsible online behaviour, which helps mitigate digital amnesia. Conversely, a strong digital identity may encourage a well-organized approach to digital engagement, positively correlating with digital detox. Digital use encompasses the manner and extent to which individuals effectively engage with digital devices and online platforms. Digital amnesia can be avoided by practicing balanced digital use, which lessens the tendency to rely too much on digital resources. A positive perspective on digital detox is fostered by responsible digital use, which is also associated with sound online behaviours. Digital safety involves the measures taken to protect oneself from online risks, including privacy concerns, cyberbullying, and exposure to harmful content [6]. Being aware of and practicing digital safety fosters more controlled and intentional use of digital tools, which helps reduce digital distraction and dependency. These safe digital practices encourage more mindful engagement with technology, increasing the likelihood of embracing digital detox.

Digital security involves safeguarding digital devices and data from unauthorized access, cyber-attacks, and other potential security threats [30]. A focus on digital security may encourage users to be more vigilant and deliberate in their online activities, minimizing distractions and dependency on digital tools. Security-conscious individuals are more likely to engage in digital detox practices, as they tend to be more aware of the potential risks of overuse. Digital emotional intelligence is defined as the integration of emotional intelligence and digital competence, encompassing the ability to understand, manage, and express emotions effectively within digital environments, while also navigating and utilizing digital tools responsibly and ethically [1]. People with high digital emotional intelligence are better equipped to manage negative online interactions, which helps reduce stress and dependence on digital platforms. This, in turn, minimizes distractions and lowers the risk of digital amnesia. Such individuals are more likely to pursue a healthy balance in their digital lives, leading to a positive correlation with digital detox practices. Digital communication involves the effective utilization of the online platforms such as emails, social media and other applications to facilitate meaningful and productive interactions [11]. Effective and meaningful digital communication can prevent unnecessary distractions and overreliance on digital tools, thereby reducing digital amnesia. People who prioritize clear and purposeful communication are more inclined to practice digital detox, seeking a balance in their digital interactions. Digital literacy is an essential requirement for every individual to survive in the digital society [21].

Digital literacy refers to the assortment of cognitive-thinking strategies that consumers of digital information utilize [8]. Other terms used alongside or sometimes synonymously with digital literacy include: internet literacy, multi-literacy, information literacy, information communication technologies (ICT) literacy, and computer literacy [16]. People with high digital literacy are better equipped to navigate the digital world efficiently, which helps minimize distractions and dependency. They are more adept at managing their digital presence, supporting their efforts toward digital detox and reducing the risk of digital amnesia. Digital rights refer to the understanding of one’s entitlements and participation rights on the Internet, including licensed rights to use digital content and specialized software [7]. Knowledge of digital rights helps individuals recognize their ability to control and limit their digital interactions. This awareness can reduce the tendency to rely excessively on digital platforms, as users are more likely to set boundaries and manage their usage effectively. When individuals are aware of their rights related to digital content and software, they are less likely to engage in unauthorized or compulsive use of digital resources. This can help decrease dependency on digital tools and platforms. Recognizing digital rights helps individuals take control of their digital usage and make conscious decisions about their online presence. This empowerment supports efforts toward digital detox by encouraging users to create and maintain a healthy balance between digital and offline activities.

Further, higher level of self-reported digital intelligence was significantly associated with lower scores in digital distraction, digital dependency, and overall digital amnesia, and with higher digital detox scores. Digital intelligence involves the ability to effectively navigate, manage, and use digital technology and information [18]. Students with high digital intelligence may minimize these distractions by adopting to digital landscape effectively. They may develop healthier digital habits, set limits on screen time, and prioritize offline activities. By balancing their online and offline lives, they can prevent or reduce digital dependency. Digital distraction occurs when an individual’s focus is diverted by digital devices, like smartphones, laptops and tablets, etc. Digital dependency refers to the over-reliance on digital devices and the internet, often to the point where it impairs the daily life of an individual [31]. Therefore, individuals with higher digital intelligence would experience fewer instances of digital distraction and digital dependency. Digital detox is the intentional abstention from digital gadgets for a predetermined amount of time in order to eliminate dependence. Digital intelligence could promote the ability to recognize when a person needs to take a break from digital consumption and the right ways to detoxification.

Furthermore, the moderation analysis indicate that participants who reported higher level of digital intelligence reported experiencing less digital amnesia. However, gender was not directly linked to digital amnesia. Importantly, the significant interaction effect demonstrates that gender moderates the relationship between digital intelligence and digital amnesia, with males showing a stronger negative association compared to females. These findings align with previous literature suggesting that males and females differ in their cognitive adaptation to digital environments. The steeper decline in digital amnesia among males as digital intelligence increases may indicate that males rely more on digital intelligence as a compensatory mechanism for managing digital information. Conversely, females demonstrated a more stable pattern, potentially reflecting different cognitive strategies or a lower susceptibility to digital amnesia. This finding supports the notion that males may be more vulnerable to digital amnesia at lower levels of digital intelligence, whereas females maintain more consistent cognitive processing regardless of their digital intelligence levels. The findings contribute to the growing body of literature on digital cognition by emphasizing the importance of considering gender differences in digital intelligence and its cognitive consequences.

Conclusion

This study was among the first to investigate the moderating effect of gender in the association between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. The findings suggest that enhancing digital intelligence is key to mitigating the adverse effects of digital amnesia in young people. Colleges might consider developing tailored interventions to address memory-related issues in digital environments, with a focus on gender differences in digital amnesia. By educating youth on the potential risks of digital amnesia and the value of digital intelligence, they can be empowered to make more informed decisions. Ongoing research is essential to ensure interventions remain relevant in an ever-changing digital world. Future studies should further explore gender-specific cognitive strategies in digital environments to develop more effective interventions for digital memory management.

Limitations

The study was conducted with college students from a particular city in India, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or populations. Cultural, social, and educational differences in other areas might influence the relationship between digital amnesia, digital intelligence, and gender. Since it is a cross-sectional study, it only captures data at one point in time, which limits the ability to determine causality or observe changes over time in the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence. In addition, the present study relied on self-reported measures of digital intelligence and digital amnesia, which reflect participants’ perceptions rather than objectively measured competences or behaviours [32]. While the study found a moderating effect of gender, there may be other unexamined factors (e.g., technology access, digital literacy) that also influence the relationship between digital amnesia and digital intelligence, which were not accounted for in the study.

Moreover, establishing causality in studies of digital technologies poses particular challenges. Digital tools and platforms are typically integrated into daily life gradually, and their influence tends to accumulate over time rather than appearing at a single, identifiable moment. Patterns of adoption also differ substantially across social and demographic groups, which can lead to varied and sometimes uneven effects. Moreover, these shifts in digital engagement occur alongside other widespread economic, cultural, and societal changes, making it difficult to isolate the specific contribution of digitalisation from other simultaneous developments. In light of these complexities, our findings should be interpreted with caution. They reflect associations observed in the data rather than definitive causal pathways. Additionally, as this study relied on self-reported measures of digital intelligence and digital amnesia, the results represent participants’ perceptions and may not fully capture their actual behaviours or competencies.
