Five Years After Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation: A Prospective Cohort Study of Health-Related Quality of Life and Patient Outcomes
Authors: Floris E. J. Jolink, Marisa Onrust, Dinis Dos Reis Miranda, Loes Mandigers, Thijs Delnoij, Jacinta J. Maas, S. Jorinde Raasveld, Alexander P. J. Vlaar, Dirk W. Donker, Erik Scholten, Jesse de Metz, Judith van den Brule, Marijn Kuijpers, Karin M. Vermeulen, Peter H. J. van der Voort, Walter M. van den Bergh, Annemieke Oude Lansink-Hartgring, Joep M. Droogh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To report on 5-year survival, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and occupational status of patients with respiratory or cardiac failure, who were supported with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).Design:A prospective multicenter observational cohort follow-up study.Setting:ICUs of ten ECMO centers in the Netherlands, representing more than 90% of all patients supported with ECMO in the Netherlands.Patients:Patients supported with ECMO between August 2017 and July 2019.Interventions:None.Measurements and Main Results:Survival was assessed by consulting the Dutch municipal records database. All 5-year survivors received a questionnaire. HRQoL was assessed using the standardized EuroQol 5D Five Levels instrument, and occupational status was assessed using the standardized Institute for Medical Technology Assessment Productivity Cost Questionnaire. Of the initial 428 participants, 230 (54%) survived the hospital admission, 213 (50%) survived the first year, and 155 (36%) survived 5 years. Five-year survival rates were 42% for patients receiving ECMO for respiratory support, 39% for cardiac support, and 25% for extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation. The response rate to the questionnaire was 72%. HRQoL was rated satisfactory at 5 years, with a median EuroQol index of 0.82 (interquartile range, 0.73–0.98). Five-year survivors reported slight-to-moderate impairment of mobility (39%), impairment of usual activities (44%), and pain or discomfort (60%). After 5 years, the majority of patients were either employed (41%), retired (31%), or permanently declared unfit for work (26%).Conclusions:Long-term outcome in patients supported with ECMO is good. Five-year survivors reported that HRQoL was satisfactory despite some impairment in day-to-day functioning.

KEY POINTS

Question: To investigate and report on 5-year survival, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and occupational status of patients with respiratory or cardiac failure patients, who were supported with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).

Findings: This prospective multicenter follow-up study showed that the majority of mortality occurs during the initial hospital admission or within the first year following ECMO initiation, but thereafter it stabilized. Five-year survivors reported that HRQoL was satisfactory despite some impairment in day-to-day functioning.

Meaning: The presented results on long-term outcomes may aid in shared decision-making and justify the utilization of ECMO as a resource-intensive, but life-saving intervention.

In the last decades, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has shown to be a promising technique for supporting critically ill patients with respiratory or cardiac failure in the ICU. The worldwide number of ECMO runs in adult patients is increasing rapidly (1). ECMO is a resource-intensive, complex, and invasive form of life support, and despite its utilization, it still accompanies a high mortality rate. Survival until hospital discharge depends on the underlying illness and mode of ECMO support. Due to an aging population with increasing comorbidities, there is a growing urgency for the sustainable use of limited healthcare resources. Therefore, given the limited data on long-term outcomes and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), the justification for ECMO may be a subject of debate (1, 2).

Several randomized controlled trials have presented results suggesting a survival benefit with the use of ECMO (3–6). In these trials, follow-up mostly continued for up to 6 months and HRQoL was considered a secondary outcome parameter. As definitions of “long term” and HRQoL assessment tools differ widely in literature, interstudy comparability is limited. Hence, the divergent finding in HRQoL may be attributed to how and when HRQoL was measured.

With the benefit of ECMO being debated, the need for results on long-term survival and HRQoL has become increasingly relevant. In current literature, ECMO survivors reported a reduced HRQoL when compared with the general population, but similar HRQoL compared with certain subgroups such as “conventionally treated” acute respiratory distress syndrome survivors or acute myocardial infarction survivors (7–10). Evidence from these studies suggests that long-term survival is acceptable, although usually defined as 6 months.

Therefore, we conducted a long-term follow-up of the Cost-Effectiveness of ECMO Study (CEES) (2) cohort to report on 5-year survival, HRQoL, and occupational status of patients who were supported with extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR), respiratory, or cardiac ECMO.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Setting

The current study is a follow-up study of a previously conducted and published prospective multicenter observational cohort study on survival, HRQoL, and CEES (2). The study was performed in ten university or major nonuniversity hospitals in the Netherlands and included patients who received ECMO support between August 2017 and July 2019. The trial was registered on July 19, 2016 (ClinicalTrials identifier NCT02837419). The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement checklist is available in Table S1 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H816).

Participants and Recruitment

In the CEES, all consecutive patients supported with ECMO in all participating centers were included. Patients only supported with ECMO for perioperative or procedural support were excluded. For the current follow-up study, the national population registry of the Netherlands was consulted to determine survival status. ECMO survivors received an information letter about the purpose of the study, a questionnaire, and a prepaid return envelope.

Data Collection

Demographic data such as age, reason for admission, mode of ECMO, and length of ICU and hospital stay were retrieved from the database of the previously conducted study.

HRQoL was assessed using the EuroQol 5D Five Levels (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire, which was introduced by the EuroQol Group in 2009 (11). The questionnaire comprised five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension had five levels of severity. The five dimensions could be combined into the EuroQol index (EQ-index) score (0–1) that summarized the patient’s HRQoL status. Furthermore, the EQ-5D-5L tool included a Visual Analog Scale (EQ-VAS) ranging from 0 to 100 for patients to reflect on their currently perceived HRQoL: “the worst health imaginable” was scored as 0 and “the best health imaginable” as 100. The EQ-5D-5L is a widely used and standardized instrument validated for many countries and in different populations (12).

Occupational status was assessed using the Institute for Medical Technology Assessment Productivity Cost Questionnaire (IPCQ) (13). This is a standardized instrument for the assessment of health-related productivity losses. The questionnaire contained questions about participation in paid and unpaid work. Data collection and management were performed using the secured web-based data management system Research Electronic Data Capture (Version 13.7.19; Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN).

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the studied population. Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) or mean (± sd), depending on whether they followed a normal distribution (Table S2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H816). Categorical variables were shown as numbers and percentages. A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used for the 5-year follow-up period. To provide context for the EQ-5D-5L outcomes of 5-year survivors, we planned to compare the HRQoL results of survivors to the general population using a Belgian normative dataset (due to the lack of a Dutch dataset). The quality of life reported in this normative dataset was consistent with that reported in other European countries and the Dutch and Belgian populations share a similar standard of care (12). Data analyses were done using the IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and R (Version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), using the RStudio interface (Version 2023.6.1.524; Posit Software, Boston, MA). Details on the statistical analysis were disclosed in the Online Supplement (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H816).

Ethical Approval

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the 1975 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The initial study was assessed and approved by the institutional review board of the University Medical Center Groningen (protocol number METc 2017/196, April 6, 2017, The Dutch ECLS group, multi-center-study of efficacy and cost-effectiveness of ECLS therapy). The trial is registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov/ (NCT02837419). To perform the long-term follow-up, an amendment to the initial study was submitted and approved (protocol number METc 2017/196, June 1, 2022, Amendment to The Dutch ECLS group, multicenter-study of efficacy and cost-effectiveness of ECLS therapy). Furthermore, the protocol was approved by the ethical committees of the other participating hospitals. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives.

RESULTS

Participants

Of the initial 428 included participants, 230 (54%) survived the hospital admission, 213 (50%) were alive after 1 year, and 155 (36%) were alive after 5 years. As such, of the ECMO patients surviving to hospital discharge, 92% were alive after 1 year and 67% were alive after 5 years. Compared with the baseline cohort, the 5-year survivors were typically younger at ECMO initiation and had a lower Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation IV score at ICU admission. The baseline cohort consisted of 34% women, while the 5-year survivors comprised 36% women. Compared with the baseline cohort, 5-year survivors had spent a longer duration on ECMO, in the ICU, and in the hospital. Study Results

A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 2) showed that the majority of mortality after ECMO initiation occurred during the initial hospital admission or within the first year. Patients who survived this critical period were likely to achieve long-term survival (Online Supplement, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H816). Five-year survival was 42% in patients who received ECMO for respiratory support, 39% for cardiac support, and 25% for ECPR.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves. This Kaplan-Meier curve illustrates survival probabilities for patients supported with different extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) modes. Survival is highlighted at 6 mo, 1 yr, and 5 yr for respiratory ECMO (blue), cardiac ECMO (orange), and extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR; green).

Five-year HRQoL was rated similar to 1-year HRQoL, with a median EQ-index value of 0.82 (IQR, 0.73–0.98) and a median EQ-VAS value of 75/100 (IQR, 65–85). Five-year survivors reported slight-to-moderate impairment of mobility (39%), impairment of usual activities (44%), and pain or discomfort (60%). Two (2%) of the 5-year survivors reported extreme difficulties with self-care, whereas 85% reported no issues in this domain. Overall, the percentage of participants reporting any form of impairment in the various HRQoL domains decreased slightly over the past 5 years, with the exception of the domain “anxiety or depression.” The number of respondents reporting issues in this domain has remained virtually unchanged over the years (31%). DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is one of few observational studies presenting 5-year perspectives on patient outcomes, HRQoL, and occupational status after ECMO support. Our results show that survival rates stabilized over time, and despite high mortality in the first year, more than one-third of patients remained alive 5 years after ECMO support. Despite slight-to-moderate impairment of mobility, impairment of usual activities, and pain or discomfort; HRQoL was rated satisfactory after 5 years, showing values approaching that of the Belgian general population. The majority of patients returned to employment; however, the degree to which they did so varied.

Our findings aligned with previous studies, showing similar Kaplan-Meier survival curves for ECMO-supported patients. The 5-year survival rate for respiratory ECMO in our cohort was 42%, consistent with reported rates of 30–48% (14–17). For patients with cardiogenic shock (both surgical and nonsurgical), 5-year survival was 39%, exceeding the 33% reported for comparable cohorts (16) and the 22–32% reported specifically for post-cardiotomy patients (18–20). The higher survival in our cohort may reflect differences in patient selection, inclusion of nonsurgical cardiogenic shock, and successful bridging to heart transplantation or mechanical circulatory support. We also distinguished survival outcomes between venoarterial ECMO and ECPR, with the latter showing worse outcomes. Our 25% 5-year survival rate for ECPR patients aligned with reported rates of 21–24% (8, 21, 22). Long-term data on ECPR was scarce, necessitating comparisons with shorter follow-up studies.

Our cohort showed a longer ECMO support duration and ICU length of stay in survivors. We hypothesize that in ICU patients for whom ECMO was ultimately deemed futile, support was withdrawn, with a palliative intent. As a result, death often occurred shortly after ECMO decannulation. This could have resulting in the shorter length of stay and shorter durations of ECMO support.

To provide context for the EQ-5D-5L outcomes of our 5-year survivors, we compared survivors to the general population. In the general population, the mean EQ-index value for both men and women in the age group of our 5-year survivors was reported to be 0.83 (0.81–0.85), and the mean EQ-VAS value was reported to be 76 (75–77) (12). While fewer survivors reported impairments in HRQoL domains at 5-year follow-up compared with 1-year follow-up, these issues remained more prevalent than in the general population.

While the Netherlands does not have ECMO-specific rehabilitation programs, ICU patients typically receive multidisciplinary post-ICU care that may include physical therapy, occupational therapy, psychologic support, and social work. This is mostly initiated by the general practitioner or as part of aftercare programs following a transplantation or surgery. A routine follow-up of these patients is essential (23).

A slight increase in EQ-5D index and VAS scores was observed between the 1- and 5-year follow-up. One possible explanation is that patients with greater impairments in HRQoL may have been more likely to die during the long-term follow-up period. However, given the relatively low number of deaths and the small magnitude of change in EQ-5D scores, we believe it is important to interpret these findings with caution. Additionally, the EQ-5D domains may capture aspects of functioning that improve only gradually over a longer period of time.

Based on our HRQoL assessment, we concluded that ECMO survivors experienced a satisfactory quality of life, 5 years after ECMO. Comparing our HRQoL outcomes to the literature was challenging due to the use of different assessment tools, such as the EQ-5D-5L, the 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey, and the St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire. Studies utilizing the EQ-5D-5L showed consistent results, all reporting slight-to-moderate problems with mobility, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression among ECMO survivors (8, 16, 18, 24). In the literature, the reported EQ-index and EQ-VAS are similar to those found in this study (8, 16). When including studies utilizing alternative HRQoL assessment tools, the overall consensus in the literature suggested that ECMO survivors generally reported an acceptable HRQoL despite experiencing persistent, slight-to-moderate physical and mental impairments (25). Interestingly, although most studies reported that few survivors regretted having undergone ECMO, one study did identify notably poor HRQoL outcomes, particularly in regard to social functioning (26). Additionally, post-traumatic stress symptoms appear to be relatively common among ECMO survivors, with reported prevalence ranging from 5% to 30% (15, 16, 27).

In our study, approximately 40% of patients successfully returned to paid employment, while 25% were unable to return to work, and the remainder transitioned into retirement. Given the mean age of 55 years in our cohort and the retirement age of 67 years in the Netherlands, this could translate into a potential decade of occupational productivity, which could benefit both societal economic gain and individual quality of life. Return-to-work rates in the literature varied widely. In older post-cardiotomy populations, only 17% of patients returned to paid employment (25). After cardiogenic shock or cardiac arrest, rates ranged from 33% to 39% (28, 29). For respiratory failure survivors, reported return-to-work rates were notably higher, approaching 75%, but these values were heavily influenced by the younger age of the studied population (median age of 38 yr) (15).

This study had a follow-up duration of 5 years, with a multicenter observational design, reporting on different categories of patients supported with ECMO. The response rate to our HRQoL questionnaire was 72% which matched or improved on previous studies in this field (16, 30).

However, our study had several limitations. First, we were unable to obtain an address of ten 5-year survivors and, therefore, could not invite them to participate in the questionnaire, which could cause a selection bias. Second, inherent to the design of this study, there was also some potential for nonresponse bias, where patients with poor functional outcomes might have been less likely to respond to our written HRQoL questionnaire, leading to overly optimistic results. However, given the high response rate, we expected this bias to be minimal. Third, due to logistical reasons, not all participants received the questionnaire precisely 5 years after ECMO support. In a minority of cases, the questionnaire was sent between 4.5 and 5 years. We did not expect this to impact the outcome. Last, while numerous studies have explored predictors of mortality in ECMO patients, tools and prediction models of a “good outcome”—such as meaningful functional recovery with satisfactory HRQoL or the return to independence—remained scarce and were likely highly personal.

CONCLUSIONS

Patients supported with ECMO are known to have high early mortality, but our study shows that survival stabilizes over time, with over one-third of patients surviving up to 5 years. Patients overcoming the critical period after ECMO decannulation and recovery from critical illness were likely to achieve long-term survival, suggesting that studies presenting 1-year follow-up may reliably estimate long-term outcomes. Despite some impairments in daily functioning, HRQoL was satisfactory and approached that of the general population. Almost three-quarters of patients returned to work or retirement. These findings support the utilization of ECMO as a resource-intensive, but life-saving intervention and provide insights for shared decision-making.

IMPLICATIONS FOR CLINICAL PRACTICE AND FUTURE RESEARCH

The results of this study provide important insights for various stakeholders. For critical care clinicians, patients, and their relatives, the presented findings of long-term outcomes and HRQoL offer perspective and may aid in shared decision-making and advanced care planning. For critical care researchers, the results of this prospective multicenter cohort study allow a comparison of mortality rates, HRQoL, and return-to-work outcomes. The stabilization of survival rates after the first year suggests that 1-year follow-up may provide a reliable estimation of long-term outcomes. For hospitals, public health agencies, and government agencies, the finding that high investments in the initial hospital admission translate into long-term benefits justifies the utilization of ECMO as a life-saving intervention, as many survivors experience a satisfactory quality of life and the potential to return to work.
