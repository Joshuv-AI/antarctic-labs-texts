EBV Genome Variations and Association With Diseases
Authors: Xueer Lin, Jingtong Liang, Qiuting Zhang, Qinyao Huang, Xinyu Zhang, Miao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Epstein‐Barr virus (EBV) is associated with a range of diseases, including malignancies and autoimmune disorders. Driven by advances in deep sequencing technologies, recent studies have systematically characterized EBV genomic landscapes in diverse clinical specimens, uncovering distinct geographic patterns in strain distribution. However, a comprehensive understanding of how EBV genomic variation contributes to disease pathogenesis remains incomplete. This review aims to consolidate current knowledge on EBV genomic variation and its role in disease development. First, we delineate the evolutionary origins of EBV genetic variations, focusing on key factors such as replication errors, recombination, and immune‐driven selection. Next, we summarize the association between EBV subtypes and diseases, with evidence linking specific genetic variations to malignancies, infectious mononucleosis, and multiple sclerosis. At the mechanistic level, EBV genomic variation may influence pathogenesis through three interconnected mechanisms: viral functional changes, viral–host interactions, and molecular mimicry. Finally, this review explores how genomic insights into EBV can inform clinical applications, including early diagnostic biomarkers and next‐generation therapies targeting lineage‐specific variations, such as mRNA vaccines and small molecules that disrupt latency. These advances highlight the critical role of EBV genomics in understanding disease mechanisms and developing precision interventions to address the global burden of EBV‐related diseases.

Introduction

EBV Genome

The Epstein‐Barr Virus (EBV), a member of the human gamma‐herpesvirus family, was the first human oncogenic virus to be identified, discovered in cells from African Burkitt's lymphoma in 1964 [1, 2]. EBV has a linear double‐stranded DNA genome of approximately 172 kb, encoding about 80 open reading frames (ORFs) and 46 non‐coding RNAs. In cells, EBV can choose between two alternative states, latent or lytic, by coordinately expressing genes. EBV latency genes are comprised of six Epstein‐Barr nuclear antigens (EBNA 1, 2, 3 A, 3B, 3 C, and the EBNA leader protein), three latent membrane proteins (LMP1, LMP2A, and LMP2B), and multiple non‐coding RNAs, including EBV‐encoded small RNAs (EBERs) and BART and BHRF1 microRNAs (miRNAs) [3, 4]. They are expressed during latent infection to help promote survival of the host cell and persistence of the viral genome. Conversely, during the lytic cycle, EBV can express a set of lytic genes leading to virus replication, such as viral transcription factors (e.g., BZLF1), and viral DNA polymerase (BALF5) [5].

EBV Infection and Persistence

EBV transmission primarily occurs through saliva, and the initial infection is often asymptomatic, usually occurring in infancy or childhood [6]. After transferring across the mucosal epithelium from the saliva, the virus infects B cells in secondary lymphoid tissues such as the tonsils [7]. Following primary infection, EBV establishes latency in memory B cells, from which sporadic reactivations may occur [8]. Such EBV infection maintains lifelong persistence in its host through a biphasic life cycle. During the latent phase, viral antigens are mostly down‐regulated, and the EBV genome is maintained as episomes in the host nucleus to evade immune surveillance and be passively replicated through host cell division. Under certain conditions, including B‐cell receptor (BCR) activation and plasma cell differentiation, EBV can reactivate into the lytic cycle and produce infectious virions [4, 9]. Cell‐free virions, as well as virion‐carrying B cells, can infect epithelial cells from the basolateral side [10]. In epithelial cells, lytic replication occurs more efficiently, shedding infectious virions into saliva for transmission [11, 12]. Oncogenesis involves both viral cycles, indicating a more intricate mechanism of gene regulation. Latent infection, driven by viral oncoproteins, is the principal mechanism, while emerging evidence indicates that viral genes associated with the lytic cycle may also contribute to malignant transformation [13].

EBV‐Associated Diseases

Although host immune responses generally control EBV infection in an asymptomatic state, under certain conditions, EBV can contribute to the development of a spectrum of diseases, including multiple malignancies, autoimmune pathologies, and acute clinical syndromes.

EBV‐associated lymphoid malignancies are characterized by abnormal proliferation of infected lymphocytes. In B cells, EBV expresses restricted patterns of latent proteins that promote cell proliferation and support long‐term viral persistence. However, in individuals with impaired virus‐specific immune control or dysregulated viral life cycle, EBV can drive uncontrolled B cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis, facilitating their transformation into lymphoma cells [14]. These processes contribute to the pathogenesis of Burkitt lymphoma (BL), Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), and specific subsets of diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [15]. In rare cases, the viral oncogenic potential extends to lymphoid malignancies of T/NK‐cell origin, including extranodal NK/T‐cell lymphoma (nasal type) and aggressive NK‐cell leukemia [16].

EBV also exhibits a striking tropism for epithelial cells and shows strong etiological links with undifferentiated nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) and EBV‐associated gastric carcinoma [17, 18]. In oropharyngeal epithelial cells, EBV normally exists in a lytic state. However, EBV shows increased lytic activity in the precancerous stage, as indicated by elevated EBV‐specific IgA and IgG levels prior to NPC diagnosis, and successfully establishes persistent latent infection in pre‐malignant epithelial cells as an early event during NPC tumorigenesis [19, 20]. Viral gene products, including EBV proteins and non‐coding RNAs, cooperate with host genetic factors to promote tumorigenesis by driving malignant proliferation, facilitating immune evasion, inducing host genomic instability, and remodeling the tumor microenvironment [7, 21].

Beyond virus‐driven malignancies, dysregulated immune responses to EBV infection can also cause immune pathologies [22]. Infectious mononucleosis (IM), for example, arises from an exaggerated T‐cell response during primary EBV infection and occurs predominantly in adolescents and young adults [23]. Notably, IM is a recognized risk factor for the subsequent development of EBV‐positive HL and multiple sclerosis (MS) [24, 25]. In addition, EBV infection has been implicated in autoimmune dysregulation, particularly in MS and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain EBV‐driven autoimmunity, including molecular mimicry, B‐cell reprogramming, and dysregulated EBV infection [26].

EBV Genome Variation

While EBV infection is nearly universal, the geographic distribution of EBV‐associated diseases—such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma in South China, Burkitt lymphoma in sub‐Saharan Africa, and multiple sclerosis in northern Europe—reveals striking disparities [15, 27, 28, 29]. These patterns reflect a complex interplay of host genetics, environmental factors, and the evolutionary divergence of EBV itself. Strain‐specific genetic variation has been linked to regional disease patterns, exemplified by two non‐synonymous variants in the BALF2 gene that together are linked to over 80% of nasopharyngeal carcinomas in endemic regions [30, 31, 32, 33], as well as the differential distribution of type 1/2 EBV strains linked to lymphomagenesis [34, 35, 36, 37]. These findings highlight that viral evolution contributes to regional disease patterns, emphasizing the importance of incorporating EBV genome diversity as a key factor in understanding disease etiology and global disparities.

Over millennia, EBV has diversified into phylogenetically distinct strains, adapting to specific human populations. Early insights into EBV genomic variation were derived from a limited number of well‐characterized laboratory strains and disease‐associated isolates, which laid the foundation for understanding functional heterogeneity in EBV biology and genome‐level diversity (Table 1) [38, 41, 43, 45, 48, 51, 53, 55, 58, 60, 62, 64, 66]. Since the first complete EBV genome, B95‐8, was sequenced in 1984 [39], numerous sequence variations have been identified within single genes or short DNA segments across the EBV genome. Notably, polymorphisms in EBNA2 and the EBNA3 gene cluster (EBNA3A, EBNA3B, and EBNA3C) together contribute to the majority of EBV genomic diversity. Based on these differences, EBV is classified into two major subtypes, type 1 (EBV‐1) and type 2 (EBV‐2), representing two deeply diverged viral lineages [42, 67]. Type 1 EBV, which displays a stronger capacity to transform B cells [37], is the dominant strain globally, while type 2 is more prevalent in certain regions, such as sub‐Saharan Africa and Papua New Guinea [35]. Given the strong association between EBV genetic variations and diseases, a comprehensive investigation of these variations is essential. This review focuses on EBV genome variations and their associations with different diseases, exploring their role in disease pathogenesis and progression. By summarizing these relationships, we aim to provide insights into the mechanisms of EBV‐related diseases and offer a basis for future applications in diagnostics and targeted therapies.

EBV Sequence Variation: Molecular Origins and Selection

Replication Errors

EBV replication relies on the host cell's DNA polymerase, which, despite its high fidelity, is not entirely error‐free. Studies estimate EBV's mutation rate at approximately 6.4 × 10⁻⁸ mutations per site per year, a rate comparable to that of other herpesviruses, such as HSV‐1 (3.5 × 10⁻⁸ mutations per site per year) [68].

Although the host mismatch repair system mitigates the majority of replication errors, a fraction of these errors persist and accumulate within the viral genome. These mutations primarily appear as single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and short insertions or deletions (InDels). Genomic analyzes reveal considerable variation in mutation rates across different regions of the EBV genome. Latency‐associated genes exhibit the highest sequence variability, whereas genes encoding structural proteins remain more conserved [69].

APOBEC‐Editing

The APOBEC3 family of cytidine deaminases plays a key role in antiviral defense by inducing C → U deaminations, leading to G → A and C → T mutations in viral genomes. APOBEC3 preferentially targets TpC motifs in single‐stranded DNA, and hypermutated EBV sequences detected in infected B‐cell lines suggest that this enzyme contributes to EBV genome diversification [70].

To counteract this, EBV encodes BORF2, a protein that binds to and inhibits APOBEC3B, preventing excessive mutagenesis and maintaining genome stability. BORF2 relocates APOBEC3B from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, restricting its access to viral DNA [71]. This dynamic interaction between APOBEC3‐driven mutations and EBV's defense mechanisms illustrates an ongoing evolutionary arms race, shaping the virus's adaptation and persistence in human populations [70, 71].

EBV Recombination

Recombination serves as a major driver of EBV genomic diversity, facilitating adaptation to diverse host immune environments and contributing to viral persistence. Multiple sequencing studies have identified natural type 1/type 2 EBV recombinants, arising through both intratypic (within the same viral type) and the relatively rare intertypic (between type 1 and type 2) recombination events. Both forms may lead to extensive genetic exchange that may enhance viral fitness [69]. Genome‐wide analyzes further confirm that recombination events are non‐randomly distributed and are selectively constrained in key regulatory and latency‐associated genes, such as EBNA2, the EBNA3 family, BZLF1, and BRLF1, which are essential for maintaining latency and triggering lytic reactivation [72], suggesting a conserved viral strategy to preserve essential viral functions.

The impact of recombination on EBV‐associated diseases is particularly evident in NPC, where distinct recombinant strains are frequently found in high‐risk populations. Zhang et al. further demonstrated that a recombinant EBV strain linked to NPC in South China emerged through Out‐of‐Africa migration and subsequent clonal expansion [68], underscoring the role of recombination in regional strain adaptation and disease prevalence. The presence of geographically distinct recombinant strains suggests that recombination is an evolutionary mechanism that enables EBV to fine‐tune its genetic composition in response to host immune pressures and environmental factors.

Immune‐Driven Selection

Population‐specific HLA haplotypes are thought to exert cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)‐mediated immune pressure, driving strain variation in immunodominant epitopes of highly immunogenic proteins. Such immune‐driven selection occurs primarily in latency‐associated genes, such as EBNA3A, EBNA3B, and EBNA3C [69]. A well‐characterized example is the HLA‐A11 allele, which elicits strong CTL responses against A11‐restricted epitopes within EBNA3B (e.g., IVTDFSVIK/AVFDRKSDAK). These epitopes are conserved among most type 1 strains in Caucasian and central African populations. In contrast, EBV strains prevalent in southern Chinese populations, where more than 50% of individuals carry the HLA‐A11 allele, harbor multiple mutations within these epitopes that impair HLA binding and CTL recognition, facilitating immune evasion [73, 74, 75, 76]. Notably, the high linkage disequilibrium observed in EBV genes containing T‐cell epitopes further suggests that host immune selection has shaped viral genetic diversity by preserving beneficial escape mutations [77]. Collectively, these findings underscore the ongoing adaptive evolution of EBV under immune pressure, shaping its genetic landscape to optimize persistence and immune evasion in human populations.

Association Between EBV Genome Variations and Diseases

EBV Variations in Malignant Diseases

Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma

A large‐scale, genome‐wide association study in southern Chinese cohorts identified three non‐synonymous variants within BALF2 (NC_007605.1: V700L [162215 C > A], I613V [162476 T > C], V317M [163364 C > T]) that strongly associated with the risk of NPC [31]. Individuals infected with the CCT haplotype have an 11‐fold increased risk for developing NPC compared to carriers of the low‐risk haplotype ATC. Notably, these high‐risk haplotypes (CCT/CCC) are highly enriched in cases (~ 93%), accounting for 83% of NPC risk in this population [31].

Notably, the signal likely reflects lineage‐level rather than single‐site effects: clustered variants across EBER, BNRF1, and RPMS1/BART show strong linkage disequilibrium with BALF2 markers, collectively defining a coherent high‐risk subtype/lineage [32, 33, 78]. Beyond the initial discovery, studies of NPC cases in the US have validated strong associations with BALF2 high‐risk substitutions (e.g., 162476 T > C and 163364 C > T), reinforcing the existence of a bona fide high‐risk lineage for NPC [79]. However, the mechanistic basis for how this high‐risk lineage contributes to NPC pathogenesis remains poorly defined.

Geographical heterogeneity remains an open question. Southeast Asia (e.g., Malaysia and Indonesia) experiences high NPC incidence, yet whether distinct regional high‐risk lineages exist remains unclear. Outside southern China (e.g., in Japan), BALF2 high‐risk loci appear less prevalent, consistent with population structure and regional viral diversity that may underlie geographic variation in NPC risk [28].

Moreover, host‐virus interaction studies indicate that NPC susceptibility attributable to HLA polymorphisms depends on infection with high‐risk EBV subtypes [80]. How particular viral lineages and host genetic factors cooperate to drive carcinogenesis, however, remains to be elucidated.

Lymphoma

EBV infection has been linked to several B‐cell lymphomas, including Burkitt lymphoma (BL), diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL), and Hodgkin's Lymphoma (HL). EBV genomes from lymphoid malignancies show distinct variation patterns compared with those from epithelial malignancies, with intragenic deletions, particularly long deletions, being a distinctive feature of lymphoid malignancies [72].

The link between Burkitt lymphoma and EBV has long been recognized. EBV positivity is observed in more than 90% of BLs in sub‐Saharan Africa (known as “endemic” BLs), where Plasmodium falciparum malaria is prevalent, and up to 30% in other parts of the world (“sporadic” BLs) [81]. BL develops through the combined action of a genetic accident leading to uncontrolled expression of the c‐myc oncogene and infection with EBV. While the chromosomal translocation leading to c‐MYC gene deregulation in BL is well established, the role of EBV in BL pathogenesis continues to be elucidated [82]. Whole‐genome sequencing of 101 BL samples demonstrated that EBV infection was associated with higher mutation load, with type 1 EBV exhibiting a higher mutational burden than type 2 EBV, suggesting that EBV, particularly EBV type 1, supports BL oncogenesis [83]. Furthermore, EBNA2‐deleted EBV strains, which sustain a Wp‐restricted latency, were found in a subset of endemic Burkitt lymphomas [84]. Since Wp drives expression of the EBNA1, EBNA‐LP, EBNA3A/B/C, and BHRF1 genes, loss of EBNA2 expression in an EBV‐infected tumor progenitor cell may be selected for BL due to its marked apoptosis resistance [84, 85, 86].

Beyond BL, specific EBV genomic deletions are linked to other B‐cell lymphomas. For instance, a 30‐base pair deletion in the C‐terminal region of the EBV LMP1 gene has been reported in HL cases, which suggests enhanced transforming properties [87, 88, 89]. However, this variant was not found to be significantly enriched among EBV + HL [90, 91]. Moreover, research has revealed that EBV with deletions in EBNA3B exhibits increased oncogenicity, driving monomorphic DLBCL in humanized mice, while recurrent EBNA3B mutations in human lymphomas underscore its function as a viral tumor suppressor [92]. Additionally, EBNA2‐deleted EBV variants can induce HL‐like and DLBCL‐like lymphomas in humanized mice, though their prevalence in human HL or DLBCL requires further investigation [44].

Although EBV is well‐established as a B‐cell‐tropic virus, it is also associated with some cancers and nonmalignant lymphoproliferative diseases involving T or natural killer (NK) cells. Infection of T cells by type 2 EBV has been demonstrated both in vivo (in healthy Kenyan children) and in vitro [93, 94]. Moreover, the rare occasions in which EBV gains access into the T‐ or NK‐lineage cells can lead to serious consequences, like Chronic active EBV infection (CAEBV) and NK/T‐cell lymphoma (NKTCL). Several studies described frequent deletions within the EBV genome associated with T or NK cell diseases [95, 96, 97]. Approximately 43% of NKTCLs and 35% of CAEBV lymphocytes harbored frequent intragenic deletions that impair BART miRNA expression, resulting in upregulation of two immediate early genes, BZLF1 and BRLF1, and the promotion of lymphomagenesis in host cells [97]. While genome sequencing identified EBV variant hot spots in genes like EBNA and LMP‐1/2 in NKTCLs [98, 99], a broader geographic analysis demonstrated that these variants are geographically common in East Asia, indicating that geography, not disease type, is the primary driver of EBV genome variation [99].

Gastric Carcinoma

Gastric cancer (GC) accounts for over one million annual cases globally, with approximately 10% demonstrating EBV association. Unlike EBV‐driven NPC with distinct geographic clustering, EBV‐positive GC lacks regional endemicity, suggesting distinct oncogenic mechanisms.

EBV‐positive GC exhibits unique pathological hallmarks, most notably dense lymphocytic infiltration reflecting host antiviral immune responses. This tumor microenvironment differs markedly from EBV‐negative GC, which typically lacks such immune cell recruitment [100]. Furthermore, EBV‐associated GC is characterized by a pronounced CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP), involving hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters [101, 102, 103]. Viral induction of host DNA methylation disrupts apoptosis, cell cycle control, and immune evasion pathways, while simultaneously maintaining EBV latency through epigenetic silencing of immunogenic viral genes [104]. EBV persists in gastric epithelium through tightly regulated latency programs (primarily Type I/II), leveraging chromatin remodeling and CpG methylation to balance viral persistence and oncogenesis. By methylating both viral and host genomes, EBV silences tumor suppressors (e.g., CDKN2A) and limits expression of immunogenic latent proteins (e.g., LMPs), thereby evading immune detection [104]. This epigenomic hijacking creates a permissive niche for malignant transformation, where sustained viral latency coexists with progressive genomic instability.

Sequence analysis of GC reveals the absence of p53 mutations, frequent mutations in the PI3K pathway, and amplification of the PDL1/PDL2 loci. Notably, EBV‐positive GC shows a better prognosis compared to EBV‐negative GC, with significantly improved responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors [105]. These findings highlight the importance of screening all GCs for EBV presence, as it may provide valuable prognostic information and guide therapeutic decisions.

Other Malignancies (PTLD/pLELC)

Post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorders (PTLDs), characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of lymphocytes in the context of post‐transplant immunosuppression, are a major malignant complication of organ and tissue transplantation [106, 107]. In transplant recipients, EBV reactivations are a major concern due to T‐cell inhibition and a consequent lack of T‐cell modulation on B‐cell proliferation caused by immunosuppression. EBV reactivations in transplant recipients may lead to the development of life‐threatening EBV + PTLD [108]. EBV strains carrying LMP‐1 GGDPHLPTL and GGDPPLPTL variants show a potent inhibition of NKG2A + NK and T cells, thereby enabling the immune evasion and reactivation of these EBV strains. The combination of infection with these EBV strains and inefficient immune response of the host is associated with a particularly high risk for EBV + PTLD in transplant recipients [109]. Additionally, whole‐genome sequencing studies discovered amino acid changes within several genes, including EBNA3C, associated with PTLD [110, 111]. Considering that EBNA3C is an important target of the T cell response during latent infection, with as many as 4% of CD8 + T cells specific for an immunodominant peptide of EBNA3C [112], these data suggest that EBV variations amongst vital genes may impact the host immune response to EBV and potentially contribute to B‐cell transformation and development of lymphomas, providing new information for the pathogenesis of EBV‐positive PTLD.

Pulmonary lymphoepithelioma‐like carcinoma (pLELC) is a rare subtype of primary non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), closely related to EBV infection [113, 114]. Compared to other EBV‐associated diseases, pLELC is considered to be more similar to undifferentiated NPC, not only in terms of morphology but also in the genetic similarity of tumor cells and EBV strains derived from these two diseases [115, 116]. Specifically, a similar abundance of EBV sequences was found between pLELC and NPC [115]. In addition, several previously reported NPC‐associated EBV genomic variations were also present in the EBV strains from pLELC, such as the variations within the EBER regions, the genes BNRF1 (A5399G), BVRF2 (A137316C), and BALF2 (C163364T) [99, 116].

EBV Variations in Benign Diseases

Autoimmune Diseases

The connection between specific EBV strains and autoimmune diseases remains largely inconclusive [117]. Unlike EBV‐associated malignancies, where viral presence is more pronounced, the frequency of EBV‐positive B cells in autoimmune disorders is in general lower. This limited abundance presents technical challenges for sequencing viral strains in these conditions, making it difficult to fully characterize EBV genomic variations and their potential role in autoimmunity.

Multiple Sclerosis

Among the autoimmune diseases epidemiologically linked to EBV, MS exhibits the strongest association. Nearly all MS patients exhibit EBV seropositivity, whereas the infection rate in the general population is approximately 85%–95% [118]. Compelling evidence highlights a strong association between EBV and MS pathogenesis, as demonstrated by a longitudinal study of over 10 million US military personnel that revealed a 32‐fold increased MS risk following EBV seroconversion [119]. Furthermore, EBV DNA was detected in 72.7% of patients during clinical exacerbations, whereas no viral DNA was observed in stable disease states [120]. Elevated titers of EBNA1‐specific antibodies and symptomatic infectious mononucleosis (IM) are independent risk factors for MS, suggesting that EBV reactivation occurs more frequently in MS patients [121, 122, 123]. Moreover, the pathophysiological link between EBV infection and MS may be mediated through several interconnected mechanisms. These include EBV‐induced expansion of neuroinvasive B cells that recruit T cells to the central nervous system (CNS) [124], as well as B cell dysfunction, immune evasion, and molecular mimicry, which collectively lead to an autoimmune response against the CNS [125]. Moreover, FOXP1 expression was significantly elevated in LCLs from active MS patients, where it was found to induce EBV lytic program genes, further implicating EBV reactivation in MS pathogenesis [125].

Beyond EBV infection itself, specific EBV genetic variants may play a crucial role in MS pathogenesis. Specifically, Focused sequencing analyzes of EBV strains isolated from the blood of MS patients identified a specific LMP1 variant that impairs NKG2A + NK cell‐mediated immune regulation, thereby reducing the clearance of autoreactive CD8 + T cells and contributing to MS pathogenesis [126]. In another study, sequencing of EBV genomes from spontaneous LCLs derived from MS patients and healthy individuals revealed greater genetic heterogeneity in EBV isolates from patients with active MS, particularly within the EBNA3 and oriP loci. As oriP contains multiple EBNA1‐binding sites required for episomal maintenance, sequence variations in oriP may compromise EBNA1‐DNA binding, destabilize latency, and consequently promote increased lytic activation and virus replication in host cells [125]. In another study, MS patients harbored specific EBNA2 gene variants associated with increased disease risk, reinforcing the role of viral genetic factors in MS susceptibility [127]. While preliminary evidence supports associations between specific EBV variations and autoimmune diseases, the functional consequences of EBV genetic heterogeneity for disease susceptibility remain largely unresolved and represent an important focus for future investigation.

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterized by the production of autoantibodies, immune dysregulation, and multi‐organ involvement. Growing evidence suggests a strong association between SLE and EBV infection, with patients exhibiting significantly elevated EBV loads in their blood [128] and an increased number of EBV‐infected B cells correlating with disease flares [129, 130]. Additionally, serological evidence further supports the role of EBV infection and lytic reactivation in SLE development [130, 131, 132, 133]. Serological markers of EBV infection, including VCA‐specific IgG and EA‐specific IgG, are significantly higher in SLE patients compared to healthy individuals [130]. These immune responses to EBV have been associated with greater disease activity and flare‐ups, whereas antibody levels against other herpesviruses, such as cytomegalovirus (CMV) and herpes simplex virus (HSV), remain comparable between SLE patients and controls [133, 134]. Importantly, elevated levels of EA‐specific IgG, indicative of EBV reactivation, have been detected prior to disease transition in at‐risk individuals and are associated with the subsequent transition from preclinical to clinical SLE [130]. Mechanistically, recent studies have shown that EBV infection, via EBNA2‐mediated transcriptional and epigenetic regulation, can reprogram autoreactive B cells into antigen‐presenting cells, which consequently activate autoreactive T cells and trigger pathogenic autoimmunity in SLE [135].

Sjögren Syndrome

Sjögren syndrome (SS) has long been suspected to have a connection with EBV infection, as supported by the unusually elevated levels of EBV loads and anti‐EBV antibodies in SS patients. EBV DNA has been detected at significantly elevated levels in the lacrimal and salivary glands of SS patients, indicating that these glands might be critical sites for EBV latency and reactivation [136]. The presence of antibodies to the EBV early antigen diffuse (anti‐EA‐D), a marker of EBV reactivation, is significantly higher in Sjögren syndrome patients than in healthy controls [137, 138]. Further immunological evidence indicated that EBV infection and reactivation may contribute to the generation of self‐reactive B cells. A recent case report documented IgM‐to‐IgG class switching and the increasing levels of IgG anti‐Ro60/anti‐Ro52 autoantibodies during the course of acute EBV infection in a patient who subsequently developed Sjögren's disease, suggesting that EBV infection may drive the generation of autoantibodies in SS [139].

In addition, EBV infection appears to play a role in the activation of autoreactive B cells within ectopic lymphoid structures in Sjögren syndrome salivary glands, facilitating local autoantibody production against SS‐specific antigens [140]. This dysregulated immune response may be driven by EBV latent and lytic infection, which alters B cell differentiation and survival, further fueling the autoimmune cascade characteristic of Sjögren syndrome. Taken together, these immunological and virological findings suggest a potential role of the EBV infection in the pathogenesis of SS.

Infectious Mononucleosis

EBV is the primary cause of infectious mononucleosis (IM), a self‐limiting lymphoproliferative disease. Genetic variations within EBV influence the development and severity of IM, with key genes such as BZLF1, EBNA1, and LMP1 affecting viral lytic activity, immune evasion, and disease outcomes.

BZLF1 encodes the Zta protein, which is essential for transitioning EBV from latency to the lytic phase. Studies on BZLF1 polymorphisms in IM consistently identify Zp‐P and Zp‐V3 as the predominant variants [141, 142, 143], with patients maintaining the same Zp variant throughout infection, suggesting a stable viral profile [141]. Beyond BZLF1, variations in EBNA1 and EBNA2 have also been linked to IM. The P‐thr EBNA1 variant is most common among Croatian IM patients, while the P‐ala variant is significantly associated with the China1 LMP1 strain [144], indicating possible interactions between viral genes. In EBNA2, EBV type 1 dominates IM cases in Europe, with a Leu212 insertion frequently observed in type 1 isolates [144].

Variability in LMP1 also appears to play a role in IM pathogenesis. The 30‐bp deletion variant (LMP1‐del30) has been identified in 66% of IM cases, suggesting a potential role in viral persistence [145]. Studies in Croatian and Argentinian IM patients indicate that B95‐8‐related variants are predominant, with co‐infections linked to altered immune responses [146, 147]. Additionally, research correlating EBNA1, EBNA2, and LMP1 polymorphisms with clinical markers suggests that specific variant combinations are significantly associated with elevated liver enzyme levels, highlighting their potential role in disease severity [148].

Mechanisms of EBV Variations Contributing to Diseases

Understanding how viral genetic variation contributes to the diverse spectrum of EBV‐associated diseases requires integrating both the molecular mechanisms and the functional phenotypes associated with distinct EBV variants (Table 2). Collectively, current evidence supports multiple mechanisms by which EBV genetic variations may promote disease progression. These include viral functional alterations affecting oncogenic transformation and the latency‐to‐lytic switch, viral–host interactions involved in immune evasion and modulation of host signaling pathways, as well as molecular mimicry implicated in autoimmune diseases.

Viral Functional Changes

EBV Variations Affecting Oncogenic Transformation

EBV can transform resting B cells and is implicated in the development of B‐cell lymphomas, with viral genetic variations playing a crucial role in modulating its oncogenic potential. A major source of polymorphisms among EBV strains is the sequence divergence within EBNA2, which constitutes the major genetic difference between type 1 and type 2 EBV and largely determines their capacity to transform resting human B cells into immortalized lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) in vitro [37, 149]. In addition, EBNA2 deletions are frequently observed in EBV strains derived from endemic BL cell lines, such as P3HR1. These EBNA2‐deleted EBV variants support a Wp‐restricted latency program in BL cells and are associated with increased resistance to apoptosis compared with conventional EBV‐positive BL cell lines [84, 86].

Beyond strain‐level variation, mutations in specific viral genes further modulate EBV oncogenicity. For instance, EBNA3B‐deficient EBV strains exhibit enhanced B‐cell transformation in vitro and more aggressive proliferation in vivo [92]. The frequent detection of EBNA3B mutations in EBV‐positive lymphomas suggests that loss of this gene may confer a selective advantage during malignant progression. Similarly, genetic polymorphisms in LMP1, a critical EBV oncogene, are closely associated with increased oncogenic potential. The 30 bp deletion variant of LMP1, commonly found in NPC, has been linked to enhanced lymphocyte transformation and tumorigenicity [152]. Additionally, sequence variation in EBER2 may also contribute to EBV‐driven oncogenesis. A 4‐bp deletion in EBER2 has been identified as a high‐risk variant in Hong Kong NPC cases [32]. Experimental evidence indicates that EBER2 promotes B‐cell proliferation by enhancing the expression of the deubiquitinase UCHL1, cyclin B1, and Aurora kinases, an effect also observed in epithelial cells [56]. Moreover, a specific EBER2 variant has been shown to promote the expansion of latently infected B cells by inhibiting plasma cell differentiation, potentially facilitating viral persistence and increasing tumorigenicity [153]. Together, variations in EBV‐encoded oncoproteins and ncRNAs may fine‐tune cell growth and cell cycle, contributing to the progression of EBV‐mediated malignant transformation.

EBV Variations Influencing Latency‐Lytic Switch

The EBV latent‐to‐lytic switch occurs sporadically in healthy infected individuals and represents an intrinsic viral strategy to generate virus progeny for transmission while expanding the pool of infected cells. Accumulating evidence indicates that EBV reactivation also plays important roles in EBV‐mediated tumorigenesis and autoimmunity. Serology studies suggest that elevated markers of EBV lytic activity often precede the development of EBV‐associated diseases, particularly NPC, implicating lytic replication as a contributing factor in oncogenesis [130, 154]. Mechanistically, lytic reactivation has been proposed to promote tumorigenesis by conditioning the tumor microenvironment, facilitating immune escape, and inducing host genomic instability [7, 22, 155]. In parallel, aberrant lytic reactivation in B cells can also exert immunomodulatory effects that may contribute to autoimmunity [26, 125, 140].

Genetic polymorphisms in key viral regulatory genes play an important role in determining EBV's propensity to switch between latency and lytic reactivation. The immediate‐early gene BZLF1 encodes the transcriptional activator Zta, which initiates the lytic transcriptional cascade and thereby governs the latency‐to‐lytic transition. Polymorphisms within both the BZLF1 coding region and its promoter modulate lytic gene expression and viral DNA replication efficiency. A well‐characterized example is the BZLF1 Zp‐V3 promoter variant, which introduces an NFAT‐binding site, making EBV more responsive to NFATc1‐inducing stimuli, such as BCR activation, resulting in enhanced lytic reactivation [151]. Consistent with this, although type 2 EBV transforms B cells less efficiently than type 1 EBV, it exhibits higher inducible lytic gene expression, largely attributable to increased NFATc1/NFATc2 signaling [156]. In addition, variation in viral components involved in repression of lytic gene expression also influences the stability of latent infection. For example, the NPC‐derived V‐val EBNA1 variant exhibits enhanced transcription activation properties, which have been linked to impaired episome maintenance and compromised repression of lytic gene expression [150]. Moreover, recurrent deletions within BART miRNAs, which are significantly enriched in EBV‐associated lymphoid malignancies—particularly DLBCL—may result in activation of the lytic program, as these deletions preferentially affect miRNA clusters that negatively regulate the immediate‐early genes, such as BZLF1 and BRLF1 [72, 157].

Specific polymorphisms also modulate lytic activity in a strain‐dependent way. The NPC‐derived M81 strain, which is prevalent in endemic regions, exhibits spontaneous and high‐level lytic replication in LCLs. Polymorphisms within the BZLF1 gene have been implicated as partial contributors to this phenotype [55, 158]. In addition, EBER2 variants unique to M81 have been shown to upregulate CXCL8 expression through activation of the Toll‐like receptor 7 (TLR7) pathway, thereby promoting lytic viral reactivation and inducing chronic inflammation in infected cells [57]. These strain‐specific EBER2 functions may contribute to the elevated incidence of NPC in Southern China.

Viral–Host Interactions

Immune Evasion

EBV has evolved multiple mechanisms to evade host immune surveillance, enabling persistent infection and contributing to the development of EBV‐associated diseases. These mechanisms primarily involve mutations in viral epitopes that facilitate escape from T‐cell recognition, disruption of antigen presentation, and modulation of innate immune responses.

EBV evades CTL‐mediated immunity by altering T‐cell epitopes, particularly within latency‐associated genes such as EBNA3A, EBNA3B, EBNA3C, and LMP1 [69, 74, 159, 160]. These mutations impair peptide binding to host HLA molecules, reducing antigen presentation and CTL recognition. A well‐documented example is the K → T mutation at position 424 of EBNA3B, identified in populations with high HLA‐A11 prevalence, which disrupts peptide–HLA interactions and may facilitate immune escape in HLA‐A11 carriers [73, 75].

Beyond epitope mutations, some EBV variants directly interfere with cytokine/chemokine production and antigen presentation. For instance, B cells infected with an EBNA3B‐deficient EBV exhibit reduced CXCL9 and CXCL10 expression, limiting T‐cell recruitment and facilitating immune evasion [92]. Recent evidence indicates that EBV can promote T cell infiltration into the CNS and contribute to the pathogenesis of MS through EBNA3B‐dependent production of CXCL9 by CNS‐homing B cells [124]. In addition, BNLF2a inhibits the transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP), reducing HLA class I surface expression, limiting CTL recognition [161]. Moreover, EBV encodes a virus interleukin‐10 (vIL‐10) homolog through the BCRF1 gene, which perturbs host immune responses by inhibiting antiviral inflammatory cytokine production and downregulating TAP [162, 163].

In addition, EBV variants modulate NK cell activity by interacting with HLA‐E. Certain LMP1 polymorphisms generate peptides with high affinity for HLA‐E, engaging the NKG2A inhibitory receptor on NK cells and suppressing their cytotoxic function [126, 164]. This mechanism is frequently associated with PTLD and MS, particularly in individuals carrying high‐expressing HLA‐E alleles.

Beyond direct immune evasion, EBV's impact on host immunity is influenced by genetic susceptibility. A population‐based case‐control study in NPC‐endemic southern China revealed that HLA genes and high‐risk EBV variants have joint effects on NPC risk [80]. These interactions account for a substantial proportion of genetic susceptibility, emphasizing the importance of viral–host interactions in disease pathogenesis.

Regulation of Host Cell Signaling Pathways

EBV genetic variants exhibit distinct functional differences in modulating host signaling pathways, influencing viral persistence, immune evasion, and oncogenesis. LMP1, a key oncogenic protein, has multiple variants that differentially regulate host signaling, affecting their pathogenicity and tumorigenic potential. For example, an LMP1 isoform identified in Chinese NPC patients carries a 10‐amino‐acid deletion in the CTAR2 region [165]. The CAO LMP1 isoform has demonstrated greater oncogenic potential than the B95.8 prototype in human epithelial cell lines, with distinct differences in NF‐κB and AP‐1 activation [166, 167]. Additionally, certain LMP1 variants, such as G212S and S366T, sustain activation of the ERK/MAPK signaling pathway, upregulating c‐Fos expression and promoting cell proliferation and malignant transformation [168]. Several LMP1 variants also activate the CTAR1‐dependent PI3K pathway, inducing Akt phosphorylation, potentially through TRAF‐mediated interactions [169].

Beyond LMP1, variants of EBNA3B and EBNA3C also modulate host signaling pathways, affecting viral persistence and immune evasion. Deletion variants of EBNA3B upregulate CXCR4 expression, enhancing B‐cell migration and potentially altering EBV dissemination [170]. Meanwhile, reduced EBNA3C expression leads to increased Jagged1 levels, activating Notch signaling, which may influence immune interactions rather than B‐cell proliferation [170]. These findings illustrate how EBV genetic variations reshape critical host cell signaling pathways, influencing cellular behavior and contributing to disease progression.

Molecular Mimicry

Molecular mimicry occurs when pathogen‐derived antigens share structural or sequence similarities with host self‐antigens, leading to immune cross‐reactivity and potentially triggering autoimmune diseases. Studies have demonstrated that EBV utilizes molecular mimicry to contribute to the development of autoimmune diseases. Specifically, the EBNA1 exhibits sequence and structural homology with several central nervous system (CNS) antigens, including GlialCAM, αB‐crystallin (CRYAB), myelin basic protein (MBP), and neuronal protein Anoctamin 2 (ANO2) [171, 172, 173]. This similarity can lead to the production of cross‐reactive autoantibodies that mistakenly target both EBNA1 and CNS components. In MS patients, EBNA1‐specific autoantibodies have been detected in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), contributing to oligoclonal bands—antibody clusters produced by clonal B‐cell‐derived plasma cells in the CNS, a hallmark of MS [173, 174, 175, 176, 177]. Notably, a specific region within EBNA1 (amino acids 385‐420) has been linked to elevated antibody levels in MS patients, showing the strongest association with disease risk compared to other regions of the protein [178]. Further supporting this association, an animal model study demonstrated that injection of a peptide fragment spanning amino acids 395‐420 of EBNA1 into mice with experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) significantly exacerbated CNS inflammation and demyelination [173]. Antibodies targeting this segment have been found to cross‐react with αB‐crystallin and GlialCAM. Additionally, BRRF2‐specific antibodies have been found to cross‐react with mitochondrial proteins, further reinforcing EBV's role in autoimmunity [179].

Clinical Implications and Future Perspectives of EBV

Diagnosis

The evolution of EBV‐specific diagnostic modalities has revolutionized clinical detection across disease states. In NPC, particularly endemic undifferentiated subtypes, in situ hybridization for EBV‐encoded small RNAs (EBERs) remains the diagnostic cornerstone for confirming viral tropism within malignant epithelium [180]. Serological profiling—notably elevated IgA titers against viral capsid antigen (VCA) and early antigen (EA)—provides critical risk stratification in NPC screening programs, particularly within high‐incidence populations [181]. The advent of plasma EBV DNA quantification via qPCR has further transformed clinical paradigms, offering a dynamic biomarker for diagnosis, therapeutic monitoring, and early relapse detection [17]. Beyond NPC, plasma EBV DNA load complements histopathological assessment in EBV‐associated gastric carcinomas and lymphoproliferative disorders, demonstrating cross‐cancer utility [182].

Emerging evidence extends EBV's diagnostic relevance to autoimmune pathogenesis, where elevated anti‐EBNA1 antibodies correlate with multiple sclerosis (MS) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) progression, suggesting viral serology as a potential sentinel for immune dysregulation [119]. Crucially, EBV's genetic heterogeneity—marked by distinct subtype distributions across geographical region [31, 68]—presents opportunities for precision diagnostics. Recent work identifies EBNA1 sequence variants as molecular determinants of NPC susceptibility, with variant‐specific IgA serology achieving superior discriminative capacity compared to conventional assay [183]. Integrating EBNA1‐variant IgA with VCA‐IgA enhanced screening sensitivity to 92% (specificity ≥ 85%), advocating for geographically tailored surveillance protocols.

A paradigm‐shifting approach combines EBV genetic variants, host HLA polymorphisms, and epidemiological factors into a composite risk score (CRS) for NPC prediction [184]. In southern Chinese cohorts, the highest‐risk CRS decile exhibited a 21‐fold elevated NPC incidence versus the lowest quintile. When synergized with standard EBV serology, CRS boosted positive predictive value from 5% to > 40%, illustrating how multidimensional biomarker integration could redefine early detection frameworks.

Prognostics

EBV's prognostic implications exhibit striking context‐dependence. In NPC, EBV‐positive tumors—particularly radiation‐sensitive undifferentiated subtypes—correlate with favorable therapeutic responses and survival outcomes [180]. EBV‐associated gastric carcinomas demonstrate a unique molecular landscape characterized by dense lymphocytic infiltration and PIK3CA mutations, conferring survival advantages over EBV‐negative counterparts [182]. Conversely, EBV positivity portends adverse prognosis in diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL), delineating a distinct biological entity with therapeutic implications [185].

Notably, EBV seroconversion patterns are emerging as sentinels for autoimmune pathogenesis. Longitudinal studies reveal escalating anti‐EBV antibody titers years before clinical onset of multiple sclerosis, positioning EBV serology as a potential pre‐symptomatic prognostic tool [186, 187]. These collective insights underscore EBV's dual role as a molecular timekeeper—informing both disease trajectories and therapeutic vulnerabilities across oncological and immunological spectra.

Therapeutic Strategies

Adoptive T‐Cell Therapy

Therapeutic approaches targeting EBV are advancing rapidly, leveraging the virus's unique biology. In EBV‐positive lymphoproliferative disorders, adoptive transfer of EBV‐specific CTLs has demonstrated durable remissions in immunosuppressed patients, including those with post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorders (PTLD) [188]. Common targeted EBV platforms are LMP1, LMP2, and EBNA1 [188]. Tabelecleucel, the first off‐the‐shelf, allogeneic, EBV‐specific T‐cell immunotherapy, provides clinical benefit in patients with relapsed or refractory EBV‐positive post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disease [189].

Small Molecules Targeting EBV

BXLF1 and BGLF1

EBV‐positive tumor cells primarily express a limited repertoire of EBV‐encoded latent genes, with only a small subset of tumor cells expressing lytic genes. A promising therapeutic approach is to induce the lytic cycle, driving the expression of viral proteins that are vulnerable to small‐molecule inhibitors. This “kick and kill” strategy triggers EBV lytic replication, rendering the virus susceptible to inhibitors targeting lytic enzymes, particularly viral kinases and DNA polymerases [190]. Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors play a key role in this process by derepressing the promoters of BZLF1 and BRLF1, initiating the latent‐to‐lytic transition. The lytic genes BXLF1 and BGLF4 encode viral thymidine kinase and protein kinase, respectively, which phosphorylate nucleoside analogs into their active, cytotoxic forms. These activated analogs lead to DNA chain termination in both viral and host cells, ultimately inducing tumor cell death. Several clinical trials are still underway (e.g., NCT05011058).

EBNA1

EBNA1, a viral‐encoded regulatory protein critical for the replication, maintenance, and survival of EBV during latency, is the only protein that is expressed in all oncogenic forms of latency and in all EBV‐positive tumors. Thus, EBNA1 is considered an attractive target for therapeutic intervention. One EBNA1 inhibitor (VK‐2019) has progressed to clinical studies for the treatment of patients with advanced NPC and demonstrated safety and efficacy [191].

Checkpoint Inhibitors

Targeting immune checkpoint inhibitors represents a promising therapeutic strategy, either as monotherapy or in combination with other antiviral treatments. PD‐L1 expression is frequently observed in EBV‐associated gastric carcinoma (EBVaGC), as confirmed by immunohistochemistry [192], and EBVaGC has demonstrated responsiveness to the anti‐PD‐L1 antibody avelumab [193].

In EBV‐positive NPC, higher levels of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes, particularly CD8 + T cells, are observed compared to EBV‐negative NPC. These CD8+ cells are associated with elevated PD‐L1 expression within tumors [194]. Combining PD‐1/PD‐L1 checkpoint inhibitors with chemotherapeutic agents, such as cisplatin, may enhance tumor immunogenicity and reduce the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes [195]. The PD‐1 inhibitor toripalimab has shown a manageable safety profile and durable clinical responses in patients with chemorefractory metastatic NPC [195].

In Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), nivolumab (a PD‐1 inhibitor) and pembrolizumab (a PD‐L1 inhibitor) have demonstrated significant efficacy in relapsed or refractory cases [196, 197], leading to their accelerated FDA approval for this indication.

Vaccine Development

Clinical Trials of EBV Vaccines

Currently, several prophylactic EBV vaccines are in early‐phase clinical development. EBV lytic glycoproteins gp350, gH/gL, gB, and gp42, which cooperate to mediate the attachment and fusion of the virus to its target cells, are primarily candidates in prophylactic subunit vaccines to induce virus‐neutralizing antibodies [198, 199]. The National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) has three ongoing clinical studies of EBV nanoparticle‐based vaccines, including two gp350‐ferritin vaccines (NCT04645147 and NCT05683834) and a gH/gL/gp42‐ferritin nanoparticle vaccine (NCT06908096). These trials assess safety and immunogenicity in healthy adults with or without EBV infection. In addition, Moderna has developed two EBV vaccines. mRNA‐1189, encoding gH, gL, gp42, and gp220, is being evaluated in the Phase 1/2 Eclipse Trial in 12‐ to 30‐year‐old healthy adults (NCT05164094). mRNA‐1195 is undergoing Phase 1 evaluation in EBV‐seropositive adults aged 18–55 years (NCT05831111). A Phase 2 trial of mRNA‐1195 is currently underway to investigate the safety and effectiveness of preventing MS relapses in 18‐ to 55‐year‐old adults (NCT06735248).

Therapeutic EBV vaccines aim to boost EBV‐specific T cells capable of targeting infected or malignant cells. Most therapeutic vaccines focus on the latent antigens EBNA1, LMP1, and LMP2, as these proteins play essential roles in cellular transformation [198, 200]. Lytic proteins such as BZLF1, which initiates the transition from latency to the lytic cycle, are also potential target antigens for vaccine development [201]. A variety of vaccine platforms are currently being explored, including recombinant viral vectors, synthetic EBV peptides, and mRNA‐based therapeutic vaccines [198, 202]. Among them, WGc‐043, an mRNA vaccine targeting EBV‐positive tumor‐associated antigens, is being evaluated in a Phase 1 clinical trial (NCT05714748). Early clinical observations have suggested encouraging signals of disease control in EBV‐positive recurrent or metastatic NPC patients exposed to WGc‐043 [203].

Challenges

The growing understanding of EBV genomic variations and their disease links highlights the need for future vaccines to incorporate the genetic diversity of EBV subtypes. The development of vaccines targeting EBV subtypes represents a promising yet challenging frontier in immunology and virology. EBV is classified into two major subtypes, EBV‐1 and EBV‐2, which differ mainly in the sequences of the genes of EBNA2, EBNA‐3A, EBNA‐3B, and EBNA‐3C [204]. EBV‐1 is the globally predominant subtype, while EBV‐2, though less common worldwide, is more prevalent in certain regions such as sub‐Saharan Africa [205].

Current vaccine candidates targeting EBV‐1 often focus on key proteins, including the latent proteins EBNA1 and EBNA3, the lytic glycoproteins gp350, gH/gL, gB, and gp42, as well as the immediate‐early lytic proteins BZLF1 and BRLF1 [206, 207, 208, 209, 210]. Vaccine development for EBV‐2 has lagged behind EBV‐1, partly due to its lower prevalence and the complexity of its antigenic differences. Key antigens for EBV‐2 include EBNA‐2 and EBNA‐3 proteins, which differ significantly from their EBV‐1 counterparts. These differences can be exploited to design subtype‐specific vaccines that elicit targeted immune responses [204]. LMP‐1, an oncogenic protein expressed during latency, is another potential target for EBV‐2 vaccines [211]. However, its variability and role in immune evasion pose challenges for vaccine design.

Opportunities in EBV Subtype‐Specific Vaccine Development

mRNA Vaccine Technology

The success of mRNA vaccines against SARS‐CoV‐2 has revolutionized vaccine development and opened new possibilities for EBV vaccines. mRNA platforms offer the flexibility to rapidly encode subtype‐specific antigens, enabling the development of tailored vaccines for both EBV‐1 and EBV‐2. This technology also allows for quick adaptation to emerging viral variants or regional subtype prevalence.

Structural Vaccinology

Advances in structural biology have provided unprecedented insights into the molecular architecture of viral proteins. By leveraging these tools, researchers can design immunogens that precisely target subtype‐specific epitopes, enhancing the specificity and efficacy of EBV vaccines.

Multivalent Vaccines

Combining antigens from both EBV‐1 and EBV‐2 into a single vaccine could provide broad protection, particularly in regions where both subtypes co‐circulate. A multivalent approach would address the challenges posed by subtype diversity and ensure comprehensive immune coverage.

Regional‐Specific Vaccine Strategies

Given the higher prevalence of EBV‐2 in regions like sub‐Saharan Africa, tailored vaccine strategies could be developed to address the unique epidemiological and immunological needs of these populations. This approach would involve prioritizing EBV‐2‐specific antigens and leveraging local healthcare infrastructure for vaccine deployment.

Conclusion

The study of EBV genome variations has deepened our understanding of its role in multiple diseases, particularly malignancies and autoimmune disorders. Advances in sequencing technologies have facilitated the identification of pathogenic EBV variants associated with specific diseases, shedding light on the mechanisms through which EBV contributes to pathogenesis. Intriguingly, despite the near ubiquity of EBV infection worldwide, only a minority of carriers develop EBV‐associated diseases, highlighting the complex interplay between virus, host immunity, and environmental factors. From an evolutionary perspective, this apparent paradox is not surprising. EBV has co‐evolved with host immunity since the out‐of‐Africa migration and has developed highly sophisticated strategies to establish a cellular environment that supports viral persistence, immune evasion, and efficient transmission—typically with minimal harm to the host. EBV‐associated diseases are therefore more plausibly viewed as incidental by‐products of long‐term persistence and chronic virus–host interactions, rather than adaptive traits selected for viral fitness.

Nevertheless, the functional consequences of most EBV genomic variants enriched in EBV‐associated diseases remain poorly understood. How these mutations interact with host immunity, particularly in the context of HLA diversity and immune surveillance, requires further investigation. A deeper understanding of how specific EBV mutations alter viral–host interactions and immune evasion would be critical for elucidating EBV's contributions to diverse diseases and for explaining the marked geographic disparities observed in certain EBV‐associated diseases. Large‐scale viral genomic analyzes, combined with host genetic studies, are critical for disentangling EBV–host interactions for guiding the development of improved diagnostics and therapeutics against EBV‐associated diseases.
