Addressing Frailty in Cardiology
Authors: Jie Jun Wong, Rilong Hong, Terence Ing Wei Ong, Jae-Young Lim, Tingting Yan, Duk-Woo Park, Gabrielle HN Tan, Hongzhou Zhang, Thu Thi Hoai Nguyen, Huong Thi Thu Nguyen, Yanhong Dong, William KF Kong, Shang Li, Jack WC Tan, Huyen Thi Thanh Vu, Angela S Koh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreventing frailty is crucial for improving outcomes in aging populations at heightened cardiovascular risk, yet implementation in real-world practice remains challenging. The authors previously reported low use of frailty strategies among cardiologists in Asia.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to explore the barriers to frailty implementation among cardiologists, other physicians, and nurses.MethodsA multinational web-based survey was conducted targeting health care communities across Asia.ResultsAmong 238 respondents (median age 34.0 years; IQR: 12 years; 63.4% women), 172 were doctors, 57 nurses, and 8 allied health professionals. Among doctors, 59 were cardiologists representing various subspecialties, including critical care cardiology, interventional cardiology, and general cardiology. Among all respondents, one-quarter lacked confidence in identifying (22.7%) or treating (28.2%) frailty, and many were uncertain of the screening tools (34.9%) and subsequent steps (37.8%). Other barriers include inadequate multidisciplinary support (21%), communication difficulties (45.8%), resource limitations (52.9%), patient and caregiver unreceptiveness (21.8%), and insufficient community care collaborations (39.5%). Compared with other physicians, cardiologists reported being too busy (P = 0.041) and having less awareness of screening selection (P = 0.018) and appropriate assessment tools (P = 0.008). Nurses experienced fewer interdisciplinary communication difficulties (P = 0.003) than cardiologists. There were no significant differences in understanding, support received, and patients’ and caregivers’ receptiveness toward frailty interventions between nurses and cardiologists.ConclusionsDistinct barriers to frailty implementation exist at multiple levels, including gaps in provider expertise, insufficient support, and resource constraints that limit adequate frailty care provision. To address the diverse needs of stakeholders, a comprehensive implementation strategy leveraging interdisciplinary cardiogeriatric team resources and involving nurses could improve frailty care in cardiology.Key Words: barriers, cardiology, frailty, implementation, policy

Over the past decade, frailty has garnered increasing attention because of the growing burden of global population aging and its significant implications for clinical practice.1 The presence of frailty alters disease trajectories, increasing mortality2 and reducing the effectiveness of treatments,3 leading some to consider frailty as a new vital sign.4 The prevention of frailty is particularly important in the field of cardiology.5 Frailty shares a bidirectional relationship with cardiovascular disease, and both conditions share several common risk factors and underlying biological mechanisms.6 A robust cardiovascular system is vital for maintaining physical and cognitive resilience;7 however, frailty remains common among older adults with cardiovascular diseases.8 Addressing frailty promptly, through early detection and prevention in this population, is crucial for ensuring healthy aging and alleviating the global burden of cardiovascular disease.9

Although it is widely acknowledged that contemporary cardiovascular practice should identify patients who are frail and can benefit from frailty interventions, several critical implementation gaps remain. Frailty is a multidimensional syndrome that requires a comprehensive approach for effective prevention measures.10,11 This strategy involves engaging various stakeholders and addressing different levels within the health care delivery system.12 The emergence of various assessment tools has led to inconsistencies in research,13 which hampers both reproducibility and comparability; this heterogeneity limits the ability to draw consistent conclusions across studies.9 The multitude of definitions of frailty has posed significant challenges for research.14 Frailty can result from physical, cognitive, nutritional, and psychosocial vulnerabilities; however, there is currently no comprehensive definition that adequately encompasses all aspects of these domains.9 Furthermore, many of these components are not readily addressed in cardiovascular practice alone.9 Implementing frailty scales can be resource intensive and time consuming for proper measurement;15 consequently, many clinicians rely on single-item measures of frailty.13 However, these measures typically serve as surrogates or focus narrowly on a single aspect, which does not adequately capture frailty as a broader construct.9

Effective and evidence-based implementation strategies for preventing frailty in cardiology are urgently needed.16,17 In 2022, the European Society of Cardiology published a consensus paper highlighting the impact of frailty in cardiology.5 This aim was to provide clinicians with a comprehensive overview of frailty assessments and to offer strategies for effectively addressing frailty in clinical practice. The paper included recommendations for frailty screening and measures to enhance the detection of frailty, along with solutions for managing frailty in patients at risk for cardiovascular disease.5 However, a follow-up survey of cardiologists conducted 1 year after publication revealed a lack of knowledge and inadequate practices regarding frailty.18 The survey highlighted gaps in clinical cardiology practice, indicating that more efforts are desperately needed to improve the integration of frailty measures.

Recognizing these critical implementation gaps, we designed this survey to understand the unique obstacles facing various stakeholders in limiting the effective implementation of frailty strategies in their clinical practice. We further examined the challenges particular to cardiologists compared with other specialties and health care providers, including nurses, to identify the specific needs in cardiology that may require a tailored approach.

Methods

Survey setup and validation

This study was an investigator-initiated survey conceived, optimized, revised, and approved by the authors. The development of the study was guided by the content of the 2022 European consensus statement on frailty in cardiology5 and other publications discussing barriers to implementing frailty interventions.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 The survey questions were designed to explore various levels of implementation barriers from different perspectives, namely health care provider factors, guideline and workflow factors, multidisciplinary team factors, patient and caregiver factors, institutional factors, and resource factors.

The survey questions were collaboratively designed by the study team, drawing on existing questionnaires used in noncardiology contexts, as well as insights from the European frailty in cardiology consensus. The development process was informed by a comprehensive analysis of barriers identified in previous studies outside the cardiology domain. Several key themes emerged from this analysis, which included individual health care provider factors, guideline and workflow considerations, multidisciplinary team dynamics, patient and caregiver influences, resource availability, and institutional factors. Individual health care provider factors encompassed motivations24 and perceptions of the relevance of frailty,18 openness to further education,21 and confidence in recognizing and addressing frailty.21 Guideline and workflow factors involve establishing clear objectives for screening22 and implementing effective systems for selecting appropriate tools,23 identifying at-risk patients,25 and determining the necessary actions following screening.23 Multidisciplinary team factors focus on issues related to accessibility and communication among interdisciplinary teams.26 Patient and caregiver factors included receptiveness and education on frailty. Resource availability pertained to the allocation of time,25 necessary staffing, and alternative solutions, such as digital health technologies.27 Institutional factors refer to the perceptions held by the institution23 and its collaborative efforts with community care.26

The final survey material comprised 29 individual questions and is presented in Supplemental Table 1. Respondents were asked about their experiences and perceptions of frailty implementation strategies in their practices. The responses were evaluated using a self-reported 4-point Likert scale, which included the following standardized options: “strongly agree,” “agree,” “disagree,” and “strongly disagree.” We reviewed the survey questions and responses. The survey was initially developed and validated in English. To ensure accuracy before publication, it underwent translation with the assistance of coauthors who are native speakers. A back-translation process was later conducted to ensure that the survey questions preserved their intended meaning and significance. The study protocol received approval from the SingHealth Institutional Review Board (CIRB 2023/2086).

Survey distribution

After validation, the survey was published on SurveyMonkey (Momentive). The survey links were shared electronically via e-mail with the mailing lists of various cardiac societies in Asia and posted on several social networks. The survey was available on the web platform for 8 months, from December 2023 to July 2024.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as frequency (percentage), while continuous data are presented as median (IQR). Chi-square tests and Fisher exact tests were used to compare categorical variables between and within groups. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between groups. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed to compare responses from cardiologists (“cardiologists”) with those from other specialists (“physicians”) and nurses (“nurses”). A 95% confidence level was used for the statistical analyses. A 2-tailed P value of ≤0.050 was used to denote statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS Statistics for Windows version 29 (IBM).

Results

Participant characteristics

In total, 238 health care professionals (median age 34.0 years; IQR: 12.0; 63.4% women) responded to the survey (Table 1). A total of 172 doctors, 57 nurses, and 8 allied health professionals (AHPs) responded. Eleven medical students were excluded from the final analysis. Physicians were represented by a broad range of specialties, including geriatricians (n = 29), internal medicine physicians (n = 18), and general surgeons (n = 16). Among the 59 cardiologists, there were diverse representations of subspecialties, including critical care cardiology (n = 15), interventional cardiology (n = 12), and general cardiology (n = 20). Most respondents practiced in Asia-Pacific countries, including China, Malaysia, Singapore, Vietnam, and South Korea (Supplemental Table 2). Nearly all respondents (90.3%) participated in inpatient care, while more than one-half (57.1%) were involved in outpatient care.

Overall responses

Health care provider factors

Most respondents agreed that frailty is important in providing care for their patients (93.3%) (Supplemental Table 2). However, about one-quarter of respondents (22.7%) believed that they were not confident in identifying frailty, and about one-quarter (28.2%) believed that they were not confident in treating frailty in their patients with frailty. Most respondents believed that receiving more education and training on frailty would benefit them (95.4%).

Guideline and workflow factors

About one-quarter of respondents (23.1%) did not know which patients required frailty screening. About one-third of respondents were unaware of the choice of frailty screening tools (34.9%) or the necessary steps to take after performing frailty screening (37.8%). When implemented, some respondents believed that the goals of the frailty interventions were unclear (19.3%).

Multidisciplinary team factors

Most respondents found multidisciplinary teams helpful when managing frailty in their practice (96.6%). However, they believed that the primary (main) physician should still take the leading role in managing frailty among their patients (91.2%). However, many respondents reported experiencing communication difficulties with members of multidisciplinary teams (45.8%) or were unable to readily get support from interdisciplinary teams when managing frailty (21.0%)

Patient and caregiver factors

Regarding patients and their caregivers (P&Cs), some respondents found P&Cs unreceptive to frailty screening and intervention measures (21.8%). The majority of respondents (93.8%) believed that more awareness and education measures were needed for P&Cs regarding frailty.

Institutional factors

When asked about their institutions’ practices, many respondents perceived that their institutions performed inadequately or very poorly in managing frailty (39.9%). Some respondents opined that their institutions do not promote good frailty practices (21.0%). About one-third of respondents (39.5%) believed that collaborations between their institution and community care services were inadequate.

Resources factors

Regarding resources available, many respondents reported being too busy in their practices to manage frailty (50.8%) and believed that frailty assessments consume too much time and resources (47.1%). About one-third of respondents (36.1%) believed that their institutions had insufficient resources to manage frailty. When asked about alternatives to traditional health care provision for managing frailty, most respondents (92.0%) believed that digital health technology or remote monitoring capabilities would be helpful in their practice.

Cardiologists and other physicians

Compared with physicians outside of cardiology, there were no significant differences between cardiologists regarding health care provider factors, multidisciplinary team factors, patient and caregiver factors, or institutional factors (Table 2). However, cardiologists were less likely to be aware of which patients required frailty screening (74.6% vs 88.6%; P = 0.018) and which frailty screening tool to use (61.0% vs 79.8%; P = 0.008). Regarding resource factors, cardiologists were more likely to strongly agree that they were too busy to manage frailty in their practice (22% vs 10.5%; P = 0.041).

Discussion

In this multinational survey on frailty, we highlighted the gaps in frailty implementation reported by 238 health care providers, including cardiologists, other physicians, nurses, and AHPs, within Asian health care communities (Central Illustration). We outline the specific challenges faced at various levels of the health care system, including individual providers, multidisciplinary teams, health care institutions, and P&Cs, and illustrate the limitations in available resources, guidelines, and workflows. The findings of this survey emphasize the need for concerted and multifaceted efforts to improve frailty implementation across global health care systems and in cardiology.

Frailty Survey Responses (Selected) and Proposed Implementation Strategies for Cardiology

In this multinational frailty survey, we identified gaps in the implementation of care reported by 238 health care providers, including cardiologists, other physicians, nurses, and allied health professionals. When faced with frailty in patients, cardiologists may encounter specific barriers and challenges that differ from those faced by other physicians and nurses. Therefore, a tailored response that addresses the unique needs of various health care providers is essential. Additionally, it is crucial to identify obstacles at every level of the frailty care continuum to tackle the implementation barriers related to frailty. Overcoming barriers to frailty implementation

Over the past decade, there has been a growing recognition of the importance of frailty in our health care models.1 This trend, driven partly by global population aging, has led to a significant increase in research on frailty;28 the links between frailty and morbidity across various diseases and health states are now well established,29 and numerous frailty assessment tools have been developed and validated.30 However, the practical implementation of frailty measures in clinical settings has proved challenging23,31 because of several issues, including a lack of clear consensus on definitions,21 the appropriate tools, and the necessary actions following detection.32 Resource limitations and the demands of burdened health care systems hinder the effective integration of frailty concepts into practice,32 making it seemingly impossible without a significant redesign of the existing health care provision models.33 For this reason, research into practical strategies for implementing frailty measures has become increasingly important.12 For instance, targeted implementation measures focusing on the specific needs of different health care provider groups and at-risk patients can be more cost effective than a general untargeted approach to frailty screening.19 Our previous work on cardiologists revealed that interventional cardiologists were less engaged in multidisciplinary team care compared with noninterventional cardiologists.18 At the same time, heart failure specialists were more attuned to identifying preprocedural frailty and polypharmacy. Creating frailty implementation strategies tailored to specific health care provider groups helps bridge gaps in clinical practice.

Asia faces distinct challenges in managing frailty, with its prevalence believed to be higher in Asian countries compared with Western nations.34 In socioeconomically disadvantaged and indigenous communities, the prevalence of frailty can exceed 50%,35,36 but this prevalence is thought to be underestimated because of a large number of nonresponses among older adults.37 The health care costs associated with frailty are particularly concerning in the Asia-Pacific region, where older adults with significant health care needs often struggle to access publicly funded services.38 Cultural attitudes toward aging and frailty may negatively affect self-perceptions among older Asian adults, which can hinder health-seeking behaviors.39 Furthermore, low attendance rates for postdischarge rehabilitation are common in many Asian countries.40 To effectively address frailty in this region, strategies that differ from Western health care models may be necessary.

Frailty is a complex, multidimensional syndrome that requires a comprehensive and multidisciplinary approach.10 To effectively implement frailty measures, layered strategies involving various stakeholders and policies are essential for successful integration into routine clinical practice.11 Beyond individual health care providers, gaps at the institutional21 and patient-caregiver22 levels, as well as systemic issues relating to inadequate resources and workflows21 can significantly hinder successful execution. Identifying and addressing barriers at every level of the frailty care continuum is crucial, and a coordinated strategy to enhance frailty care is vital.31 Improving knowledge and awareness is a key aspect of this approach for individual health care providers.18 Although most participants acknowledged the importance of addressing frailty in this survey, many expressed a lack of confidence in identifying or managing it. Many respondents indicated they would benefit from additional education and training on frailty.

Beyond the individual providers, establishing clear organizational workflows is also vital to ensure the sustainability of frailty care.23 Some survey respondents believed that the goals of frailty implementation were often unclear; many were unaware of the patient selection criteria, necessary frailty tools, and the steps to take after screening. Implementing automated clinical pathways for at-risk individuals could enhance the adoption of frailty measures.41 Raising awareness and educating P&Cs is crucial in encouraging participation in frailty initiatives and facilitating continuity of community care.42 It is also essential to understand the motivations behind health-seeking behaviors among older adults.43 Negative self-perceptions of frailty among older adults are a recognized barrier to seeking care,22 making it necessary to shift this mindset. Some respondents reported that P&Cs were often unresponsive to frailty measures, and the majority agreed that more education on frailty was necessary for them.

It is also essential to consider the practical aspects of implementing change, and the real-world needs of health care providers on the ground are equally important.44 In this survey, many respondents reported feeling too busy to address frailty and indicated that screening and assessments consume excessive time and resources. Many respondents believed that their institutions lacked the resources to manage frailty effectively. To scale up the integration of frailty care, engaging staff in prioritizing high-impact implementation items and identifying simple, cost-effective processes that reduce work loads are necessary to ease pressures on overstretched health care systems.45 One approach to reducing the burden of implementation has been to use disease codes from electronic hospital records46; however, this method does not fully capture disease severity and overlooks important aspects of frailty, such as weakness and functional dependency in daily living. Variations in documentation and coding of diagnoses could lead to measurement errors. Leveraging community care resources can help alleviate the burden on hospitals.47 However, many respondents believed that the community care collaborations from their institutions were inadequate. Exploring alternative health care delivery methods, such as digital health technology or remote monitoring,48 and using automated assessments and rehabilitation guided by robotics49 or virtual frailty wards50 could alleviate staffing stresses and improve the efficiency of frailty measures.

Determining who should lead to the management of frailty can often be unclear. In this survey, the majority of respondents indicated that the primary (main) physician should take on the role of the frailty leader. However, the constraints of insufficient time, resources, and expertise may hinder the effectiveness of this approach. In some institutions, physicians and nurses from geriatrics and general medicine share leadership in frailty management across various specialties.23,51 In other cases, certain health care professionals are specifically trained to serve as local frailty leaders.51 At times, there are no assigned roles for frailty leaders, which can result in fragmented care for frail patients. The European frailty consensus recommends that a specialized frailty team provide a structured and integrated management plan tailored to the unique needs of frail patients.5 Indeed, frailty intervention teams have been shown to improve the self-rated quality of life for patients and reduce the level of caregiver burden for their caregivers.52 In health care systems in which there is a shortage of geriatricians, the responsibility of frailty leadership may fall to the main physician. To better support these individuals, the integration of clinical workflows, proactive multidisciplinary teams, and specialty nurses will be even more crucial.

Frailty in cardiology

Frailty is a significant concern in cardiology. Frailty is prevalent in older adults with cardiovascular disease and is a robust, independent predictor of cardiovascular mortality,53,54 and its presence diminishes the effectiveness of treatments3 and contributes to poorer functional outcomes. Increasingly, older patients are undergoing invasive cardiovascular procedures,55 making it essential for clinicians to identify frailty early. In one study, more than 30% of older patients with acute coronary syndrome had frailty, which was linked to a higher risk for adverse outcomes.54 Recognizing frailty will facilitate shared decision making and tailor treatment strategies to align with treatment goals and the values and preferences of patients.56 The European consensus on frailty in cardiology5 was intended to raise awareness about the issue and provided recommendations for screening and interventions. These recommendations included identifying at-risk patient groups, suggesting examples of screening workflows, and offering guidance on forming frailty care teams.5 In a follow-up survey conducted 1 year after the consensus’s publication,18 we identified gaps in the self-reported knowledge and practices of frailty among cardiologists. For instance, more than one-half of the surveyed cardiologists had never or rarely screened their older patients for frailty before undergoing invasive procedures or during acute cardiovascular disease, which contradicts the consensus recommendations. Alarmingly, more than one-half had never or rarely used cardiac rehabilitation or engaged in multidisciplinary team care to address frailty. Identifying the gaps specific to each stakeholder, such as providers of cardiovascular care, to reduce the barriers to implementation57 and improve frailty management in cardiology.

Although significant emphasis has been placed on increasing frailty detection and prevention measures in cardiology, many cardiologists in this survey, despite recognizing the importance of addressing frailty, reported that they were too busy to do so. Some have found that frailty measures consume excessive time and resources or noted that there are insufficient institutional resources. Many cardiologists were either not confident in or unaware of the appropriate tools or steps required after screening. Yet many cardiologists report experiencing difficulties obtaining support from or communicating effectively with multidisciplinary teams. Therefore, leveraging alternative resources outside of cardiology may be necessary to enhance frailty measures within cardiology.

Although cardiologists and physicians both acknowledged the significance of frailty measures, fewer cardiologists reported knowing which patients required screening and which frailty tools were appropriate. Cardiologists were also more likely to report being too busy to manage frailty, suggesting that limitations in time and work load may hinder the effectiveness of frailty training for those overwhelmed by their clinical work load58 or who have additional responsibilities, such as performing interventional procedures.18 Exploring alternative resources to navigate resource constraints could enhance clinical frailty practice in real-world settings. From a practical perspective, delegating frailty roles to other health care teams,59 establishing specialist frailty teams with cardiogeriatrics expertise,60 and implementing automated pathways61 may improve the adoption of frailty measures disrupting clinical workflows in this context.

In addition to physicians, nurses can play a crucial role in implementing effective measures to address frailty.62,63 In this survey, nurses were less likely to report communication barriers with multidisciplinary teams and were less likely to feel too busy to manage frailty. Nurses often spend more time at the bedside with patients,64 offering opportunities to identify frail individuals and initiate referrals for at-risk patients into frailty workflows.65 Nursing-led interventions can also improve the physical and mental well-being of older adults who are determined to be prefrail or frail.66 It is essential to recognize that relying solely on visual assessments, often referred to as the “eyeball test,” is not an adequate method for evaluating frailty.67 Proper training in frailty is essential. However, the most effective way to increase nursing-led participation in frailty implementation remains uncertain.68 Involving advanced nurse practitioners with specialized training in frailty may enhance the adoption of frailty pathways and contribute to their successful implementation.69

To overcome existing barriers, education and training are crucial for enhancing frailty assessments in clinical practice. A systematic review of 9 frailty education programs showed that multidisciplinary programs specifically designed for clinical application and using flexible delivery methods, such as a combination of e-learning and in-person workshops, resulted in the most significant improvements in knowledge and self-reported competence.70 To efficiently address staffing time constraints, several effective strategies can be implemented, including offering online self-paced modules on foundational knowledge (eg, recognizing frailty, applying basic screening tools),71 providing training during regular clinical meetings or lunch-and-learn sessions,72 and offering on-the-job mentoring or case-based discussions.73 Establishing a small group of frailty champions (eg, advanced nursing practitioners, AHPs, geriatric cardiologists) can help disseminate knowledge and serve as contact points for others.74 Training staff members to integrate frailty assessments into routine tasks, such as gait speed checks during vital signs collection and frailty screening tools into electronic medical records, admission assessments, or annual checkups, can enhance the number of time points and consistency of assessments.75 Actively involving patients and caregivers in the design process may enhance the relevance and acceptance of tailored initiatives.76

Using digital health solutions to manage frailty can effectively address current resource limitations. Some applications include mobile and web-based applications with offline functionality, such as text message–based frailty screening questionnaires or progressive web apps, which can enhance accessibility for health care providers and patients to frailty screening tools.77,78 Integrating frailty assessment tools into electronic health record systems can streamline the process of frailty assessment.79 Features such as autopopulating frailty risk scores from existing data can minimize duplicate work, reduce training time, and alleviate clinician work load.79 Using artificial intelligence, chatbots, wearable technology, telemedicine, or home monitoring kits for frail patients with recurrent hospitalizations enables timely remote check-ins, allowing health care providers to focus on at-risk cases and avert and manage health deterioration.80,81 Collaboration with community organizations, technology firms, and academic institutions can leverage existing infrastructure and funding opportunities.82 Involving patients and caregivers in the solution design process, along with offering clear, role-specific training in digital literacy, can encourage the adoption of new technologies and reduce resistance to them.83

Study limitations

First, this self-reported survey reflects the respondents’ perceptions rather than actual implementation practices, which may introduce reporting biases inherent in survey designs. Although there is a tendency for respondents to reply favorably,84 the findings are still relevant, as they reveal significant gaps in frailty implementation that can help enhance clinical practice. Identifying barriers does not guarantee that addressing them will improve implementation; further research using trial designs is essential to validate the effectiveness of these interventions.

Second, frailty assessment tools vary in terms of resource intensity, and responses may differ depending on the prevailing assessment method used. The Clinical Frailty Scale and the FRAIL scale are quick scoring systems that require minimal training, while the Fried Frailty Phenotype requires more resources.85,86 Our findings suggest that the choice of frailty assessment should be made on the basis of time and resource considerations of each health care setting.

Third, most respondents in the study were from Asian countries, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions outside of Asia because of differences in health care systems.87 Even within Asia, the majority of survey responses were received from China, Malaysia, Singapore, Vietnam, and South Korea. As a result, the findings may not fully represent the entire Asian continent; they primarily reflect the perspectives of East Asian and Southeast Asian countries and cannot be generalized to the health care environments of much of southern, central, and western Asia. The responses originate mainly from 5 middle- to high-income countries with relatively advanced or rapidly developing health care systems.88 Differences in health burdens, governance of health care policies, and access to resources, as well as existing gaps in technology and infrastructure, are likely to affect health priorities across various regions significantly.89 Moreover, variations in cultural accessibility, including the influence of health beliefs, may further intensify these disparities.90 Nevertheless, considering the high prevalence of frailty87 and cardiovascular disease91 in Asian communities, the findings of this survey are still relevant for reducing the global burden of frailty.

Fourth, despite the best efforts to maintain dedicated mailing lists, misclassification of respondents could still occur with an open-access survey. Furthermore, as an electronically distributed survey, this survey is prone to biases such as sampling bias, demand characteristics, and acquiescence bias. However, our findings align with other non–cardiovascular disease studies reporting gaps in frailty implementation at multiple levels and among different groups of health care providers; hence, we believe the results to be representative.

Fifth, the survey questions were quantitative and focused rather than qualitative. As a result, the respondents may have experienced other barriers and gaps that the survey did not capture. However, during the design of this survey, we drew on prior literature that identified gaps using qualitative methods, which were incorporated into this study.

Sixth, there was a low representation of AHPs, which limited our ability to conduct subgroup analyses on the barriers faced by AHPs because of their small number. Additionally, the respondents outside of cardiology included those from primarily procedural fields (eg, general surgery), nonprocedural fields, and those that encompass both (eg, emergency medicine, anesthesiology). We acknowledge that AHPs likely face unique implementation challenges.92 Similarly, surgical specialists and nurses in surgical care will face distinct challenges and needs compared with those in nonsurgical specialties.23 Larger studies with better representation are needed for greater statistical power for subgroup analyses.

Despite these limitations, the survey’s findings are significant, as they highlight the need for specific interventions targeted at each health care provider group.

Conclusions

Barriers to frailty implementations exist at multiple levels and among different groups of health care providers. Gaps in the expertise of individual providers, the roles of multidisciplinary teams and health care institutions, and limitations relating to patients, caregivers, guidelines, workflows, and resources can collectively pose challenges. Adopting a multifaceted approach that caters to the needs of different stakeholder groups may be necessary to ensure successful implementation. In addressing frailty within cardiology, leveraging multidisciplinary resources with specific expertise in cardiogeriatric care and involving other health care team members, such as nurses, may enhance collaborative effects. This approach may promote frailty assessments and interventions, ultimately reducing the burden of frailty in cardiology. Further research is essential, particularly through implementation trial designs, to validate the effectiveness of these strategies.
