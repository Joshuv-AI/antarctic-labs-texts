BCL-2/BCL-xL inhibitor pelcitoclax with osimertinib for EGFR-mutated advanced non-small-cell lung cancer: a phase 1b trial
Authors: Yuxiang Ma, Chunjiao Wu, Yan-Qiu Zhao, Jiuwei Cui, Yan Huang, Wenfeng Fang, Yunpeng Yang, Yuanyuan Zhao, Chen Yang, Wentao Pan, Dengkun Xiong, Lichuang Men, Hengbang Wang, Dajun Yang, Yifan Zhai, Li Zhang, Hongyun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvercoming tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) resistance improves survival in advanced epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). We therefore examined the safety and preliminary efficacy of pelcitoclax, a B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2)/BCL-extra-large (BCL-xL) inhibitor, and osimertinib in patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC.MethodsEnrolled patients included Cohort 1 (previously progressed after third-generation TKI treatment+chemotherapy), Cohort 2 (previously progressed after first-generation or second-generation TKI treatment+chemotherapy), and Cohort 3 (TKI treatment-naive±prior chemotherapy). Patients received intravenous pelcitoclax (160 mg in dose-expansion and either 160 or 240 mg in dose-escalation) weekly and oral osimertinib 80 mg daily. Primary endpoints were safety and recommended phase 2 dose (dose-escalation), objective response rate (ORR) and safety (dose-expansion).Results64 patients were enrolled (13, dose-escalation; 51, dose-expansion): 29 patients in Cohort 1, 8 in Cohort 2, and 27 in Cohort 3. One dose-limiting toxicity occurred at pelcitoclax 240 mg, and the recommended phase 2 dose was 160 mg plus osimertinib 80 mg. The ORR was 10.7% and median progression-free survival (mPFS) 2.7 months in patients previously progressed after third-generation TKI treatment+chemotherapy (Cohort 1) and 80.8% and 16.4 months in TKI treatment-naïve patients±prior chemotherapy (Cohort 3). mPFS was longer in Cohort 1 patients with high expression of BCL-xL (4.2 vs 2.7 months, p=0.058).ConclusionsPelcitoclax combined with osimertinib showed promising safety and antitumor activity in EGFR-mutated NSCLC.Trial registration numberNCT04001777.Keywords: Lung Cancer

Background

The third-generation epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR TKI) osimertinib has enhanced the treatment of patients with EGFR-mutated advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), whether untreated with or resistant to first-generation or second-generation TKIs.1 2 However, tumor progression and resistance are inevitable with osimertinib therapy, underscoring the need to identify effective treatment strategies to prolong progression-free survival (PFS) in these patients.3 4 Similarly, TP53 mutations have been associated with poor prognosis in patients with NSCLC treated with TKIs. In the FLAURA2 study, the PFS benefit of osimertinib plus chemotherapy versus osimertinib monotherapy was similar in patients with or without TP53 alterations.5 At present, osimertinib plus chemotherapy is approved for first-line treatment of EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC in China. In the MARIPOSA study, the incidence of acquired TP53 resistance mutations was numerically lower in patients treated with amivantamab plus lazertinib versus osimertinib monotherapy.6 Based on this, amivantamab plus lazertinib is approved as first-line treatment for EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC in the USA. In China, patients who have NSCLC with TP53 mutations lack chemotherapeutic options. Exploring effective combination therapies to overcome osimertinib resistance or delay its progression in first-line settings is still an urgent need in patients with advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC.

A potentially rational strategy to potentiate therapeutic activity against malignancies is to combine osimertinib with proapoptotic drugs.7 As the main regulators of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2) family proteins such as BCL-2, BCL-extra-large (BCL-xL), and myeloid cell leukemia-1 (MCL-1) are abnormally expressed in tumorigenesis and may underlie the development of resistance to anticancer agents, making them attractive therapeutic targets.8,10 The investigational BCL-2 and BCL-xL inhibitor pelcitoclax (APG-1252) has shown encouraging antitumor effects in cellular models of acute myeloid leukemia and gastric cancer.11 12 In a preclinical study, pelcitoclax alone decreased survival and induced apoptosis of NSCLC cell lines with low MCL-1 expression.13 Pelcitoclax combined with osimertinib synergistically reduced survival of EGFR-mutated NSCLC cell lines, including those resistant to osimertinib, thus overcoming acquired resistance of osimertinib against NSCLC in vitro.13 These data support the hypothesis that BCL-2 protein family inhibitors are potential agents to overcome acquired resistance to third-generation EGFR TKIs and prolong survival of EGFR TKI-naive patients.

The objective of this phase 1b trial was to evaluate the safety, recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D), and preliminary efficacy of pelcitoclax combined with osimertinib in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC.

Methods

Study design

This single-arm, multicenter, phase 1b trial included two stages. The dose-finding stage explored the safety and tolerability of pelcitoclax combined with osimertinib with the primary endpoints of maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and/or RP2D of pelcitoclax. Following this, a dose-expansion stage evaluated the efficacy (objective response rate (ORR)) and safety of pelcitoclax at RP2D combined with osimertinib. Secondary endpoints of both stages included evaluation of the pharmacokinetic characteristics of pelcitoclax when combined with osimertinib, median PFS (mPFS), duration of response, and overall survival. The dose-finding stage was conducted with a “3+3 design”14 and enrolled patients with disease refractory to first-line treatment with EGFR TKIs and ≥1 line of platinum-based chemotherapy. Pelcitoclax was initially administered intravenously at a dose of 240 mg, which was determined according to phase 1 clinical trials (internal study numbers (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers) APG-1252-CH-001 (NCT03387332) and APG-1252-US-001 (NCT03080311)). Subsequent doses were adjusted up (to 320 or 400 mg) or down (to 160 or 80 mg) according to evidence-related to dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs). In the dose-expansion stage, patients received pelcitoclax at the RP2D established during the dose-finding stage.

Participants

Patients aged ≥18 years with pathologically confirmed advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC were eligible. Inclusion criteria were ≥1 measurable lesion based on Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST; V.1.1),15 Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–1, adequate bone marrow reserve and organ function, and no brain metastases or brain metastasis in stable condition for 28 days after treatment. Exclusion criteria were clinically active or previous interstitial lung disease, resting QTc interval >470 ms, congenital or family history of long QT syndrome, and previous treatment with BCL-2/BCL-xL-targeted inhibitors.

Three cohorts in the dose-expansion period were enrolled: Cohort 1 included patients who had previously progressed on third-generation TKIs and platinum-based chemotherapy; Cohort 2, patients who had previously progressed on first-generation or second-generation TKIs and chemotherapy; and Cohort 3, patients who were EGFR TKI treatment-naive, with or without prior chemotherapy. Once an objective response (confirmed complete or partial response (CR or PR) assessed according to RECIST V.1.1 criteria) occurred in the first 10 evaluable patients in Cohort 1, expansion of Cohorts 2 and 3 began synchronously.

Procedures

Pelcitoclax was administered by intravenous infusion for 30 min, once weekly, combined with osimertinib 80 mg administered orally once daily. When both drugs were given on the same day, osimertinib was administered first, followed by intravenous infusion of pelcitoclax within 1 hour. Patients could be discontinued from the study for disease progression, permanent drug discontinuation due to intolerable drug-related adverse events (AEs), or investigators’ decision that the patient would not continue to benefit from the study, whichever occurred first.

Outcomes

Safety and tolerability were defined by the incidences of AEs and serious AEs. Investigators assessed and graded AEs according to National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events V.5.0. During the dose-finding stage, DLTs were evaluated at each dose level based on drug-related AEs occurring within 21 days (one cycle) after administration of the first dose. DLTs included all non-hematologic and hematologic AEs.

Tumor responses—CR, PR, stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD)—were assessed according to RECIST V.1.1. Efficacy was defined by the objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR; defined as the proportion of patients achieving an optimal overall response (CR, PR, or SD)). CT or MRI was performed at baseline and every two cycles (6 weeks). MRI was used to assess the efficacy of pelcitoclax-osimertinib in brain metastases.

Pharmacokinetics

After administration of pelcitoclax and osimertinib, plasma samples were collected during the dose-finding stage on Cycle 1 Day 1 and Cycle 1 Day 8 (predose, 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 hour; and 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 hours postdose). In the dose-expansion stage, plasma was collected on Cycle 1 Day 1, Cycle 1 Day 8, and Cycle 1 Day 15 (predose and 0.5 hour; and 1, 4, and 6–8 hours postdose). Plasma concentrations were determined by validated liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry, with a pelcitoclax detection curve range of 10–10,000 ng/mL. Non-compartmental analysis was conducted to calculate pharmacokinetic parameters using Phoenix WinNonLin V.8.2 (Certara USA, Princeton, New Jersey, USA).

Biomarker analysis

Tumor biopsy samples were collected during screening to detect NSCLC gene mutation characteristics and expression levels of BCL-2 family proteins (BCL-2, BCL-xL, MCL-1, BCL-2-interacting mediator of cell death (BIM), and phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate-induced protein 1 (Noxa)). Expression of BCL-2 family proteins was assessed via immunohistochemistry with the following primary antibodies: BCL-2 (Dako/Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA; Catalog # IR614, ready to use), BCL-xL (Cell Signaling Technology (CST), Danvers, Massachusetts, USA; Catalog #2764, dilution 1:800), MCL-1 (CST; Catalog #94296, dilution 1:800), BIM (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri, USA; Catalog #B7929, dilution 1:50), and Noxa (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Catalog #ab13654, dilution 1:200; Abcam). Immunohistochemistry staining was visualized using the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (Ventana, Oro Valley, Arizona, USA; Catalog #760-700) and scored as H-score, which is defined as the percentage of stained tumor area multiplied by the mean intensity (0, none; 1+, weak; 2+, moderate; 3+, strong).

Optimal H-score thresholds were determined via a minimum p value approach.16 17 Potential cutoffs were systematically evaluated in 10-unit increments across the observed value range. At each interval, a log-rank test was used to assess differences in PFS between high-expression and low-expression groups. The optimal cut-off was defined as the threshold yielding the maximal statistical separation (lowest p value) for clinical outcomes. This data-driven methodology was applied independently to the TKI-naïve and TKI-resistant cohorts to account for the inherent biological heterogeneity and varying apoptotic “priming” states between these distinct patient populations.

Blood samples were collected during the screening stage and after treatment or study withdrawal to detect circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). The status of EGFR and other osimertinib resistance-related genes in ctDNA was assessed using next-generation sequencing.

Statistical analyses

Safety analyses included all patients who received ≥1 dose of pelcitoclax and osimertinib and had ≥1 safety evaluation. Efficacy analyses included all patients who received treatment and had ≥1 postbaseline tumor evaluation. Biomarker analyses included all patients who received treatment and had data for ≥1 biomarker. The data cut-off date for this report was April 11, 2023.

The estimated ORR and DCR and their 95% CIs were calculated by exact binomial tests. Rates of PFS and 95% CI were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method (The KM plots were created using R-Project V.4.2.2). All tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Results

Seven patients received pelcitoclax 240 mg once weekly, with osimertinib 80 mg once daily, and 57 patients received pelcitoclax 160 mg once weekly, with the same daily dosage of osimertinib. Most patients (54.7%) were women, and most tumors (96.9%) were adenocarcinomas (table 1).

Safety

Treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) of any grade occurred in 59 patients (92.2%), with grade ≥3 TRAEs occurring in 13 (20.3%) (table 2 and online supplemental table S1). The five most common TRAEs were elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST; 68.8%) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT; 64.1%), reduced platelet count (43.8%), as well as elevated amylase (29.7%) and blood creatinine levels (28.1%) (table 2). Three patients (4.7%) experienced serious TRAEs, all of whom discontinued treatment because of them: one (12.5%) in the first-generation EGFR TKI-resistant cohort and two (7.4%) in the EGFR TKI-naive cohort. Among all patients, four (6.3%) experienced dose modifications during treatment. Moreover, eight (12.5%) individuals experienced dose interruption because of TRAEs: three (10.3%) in the third-generation EGFR TKI-resistant cohort, one (12.5%) in the first-generation EGFR TKI-resistant cohort, and four (14.8%) in the EGFR TKI-naive cohort. Overall, six patients (9.4%) experienced grade 1 prolonged QT interval (online supplemental table S1). Four patients (6.4%) experienced TRAEs (with discontinuation), including grade 2 interstitial lung disease (n=1), grade 4 AST increased (n=1), grade 3 ALT increased (n=1), and grade 3 lipase increased (n=1).

Thrombocytopenia is an on-target effect of BCL-xL inhibition. In this study, 28 patients (43.8%) experienced thrombocytopenia, most of which were grade 1 or 2. Only six patients (9.4%) had grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia. Among these, two patients receiving the 240 mg dose had grade 4 thrombocytopenia and four patients had grade 3 events (two each in the 240 mg and 160 mg dose groups). In the four patients treated at 240 mg, the platelet nadir typically occurred on the second day after the first dose, and platelets recovered within 1–2 weeks without supportive care. In contrast, for the two patients in the 160 mg group, platelet nadirs were observed on days 134 and 441 after the first dose, respectively (online supplemental figure S1).

Efficacy

A total of 61 patients were included in the efficacy analysis, and the median (range) follow-up was 6.21 (4.40–7.43) months. Rates of PR, SD, and PD were 47.5% (n=29), 39.3% (n=24), and 13.1% (n=8), respectively (table 3). In the third-generation EGFR TKI-resistant cohort, 3 patients (10.7%) experienced PR, for an ORR of 10.7%; DCR of 75.0% (n=21); and mPFS of 2.7 months. Best overall responses of the target lesions for this cohort are shown in figure 2A. In the first-generation EGFR TKI-resistant cohort, five patients (71.4%) experienced PR, for an ORR of 71.4%; the mPFS was 9.5 months. In the EGFR TKI-naive cohort, 21 patients (80.8%) experienced PR, for an ORR of 80.8%; the mPFS was 16.4 months. Best overall responses of the target lesions for this cohort are shown in figure 3A. Best overall responses of the target lesions from baseline and duration of treatment for the three cohorts are presented in online supplemental figures S2 and S3.

Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetic parameters of pelcitoclax in the dose-finding cohort after single and multiple doses of pelcitoclax combined with osimertinib are summarized in online supplemental figure S4 and online supplemental table S2. After once-weekly intravenous infusions of pelcitoclax for 30 min, pelcitoclax plasma concentrations declined in a biphasic manner, with an initial rapid distribution followed by a slower elimination phase. The median time to maximum plasma concentration (Tmax) was 0.25–0.50 hour. The relationship between maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) and area under the concentration: time curve from time 0 to last measurable concentration (AUC0-t) with escalating doses of pelcitoclax was linear. The mean volume of distribution at steady-state (VSS) was 2.8–18.4 L; mean clearance, 2.16–4.06 L/hour; and mean terminal half-life (T½), 4.20–5.84 hours. No significant accumulation was observed with a once-weekly dosing schedule after multiple doses. Exposures in the dose-expansion and dose-finding cohorts were comparable.

RP2D determination

To determine the RP2D of pelcitoclax combined with osimertinib, we enrolled 13 patients from the dose-finding stage in the safety analysis. Six patients in the pelcitoclax 240 mg group (86%) and six in the 160 mg group were available for analysis. In the 240 mg group, one patient (16.7%) experienced grade 4 thrombocytopenia, which was defined as a DLT; all experienced AEs possibly related to pelcitoclax; one (16.7%) experienced an SAE related to pelcitoclax; and grade ≥3 AEs occurred in five patients (83.3%). Two of these patients had their pelcitoclax doses decreased because of AEs. In the pelcitoclax 160 mg group, no DLTs, treatment-related SAEs, or grade ≥3 AEs were observed, and no AEs led to dose adjustments. The most common drug-related AEs observed were grade 2. The incidence and severity of thrombocytopenia and elevated transaminase levels were significantly lower in the 160 mg group than in the 240 mg group (online supplemental table S3).

The PR rate was 16.7% in patients receiving pelcitoclax 160 mg combined with osimertinib 80 mg, but no PR was observed in the 240 mg pelcitoclax group. The Tmax of pelcitoclax was consistently 0.5 hour in both dose groups, with the T½ of pelcitoclax at 160 mg similar to that at 240 mg, whether in single dose (5.84 hours vs 4.5 hours) or multiple doses (5.39 hours vs 4.99 hours) (online supplemental table S2). These data suggest that the two dose levels have comparable pharmacokinetics.

Based on the safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetic analysis, pelcitoclax 160 mg once weekly, combined with osimertinib 80 mg once daily, was safe, and pelcitoclax 160 mg was defined as the RP2D.

Biomarker analysis

In the third-generation EGFR TKI-resistant cohort, a broad spectrum of previously reported osimertinib resistance mechanisms was detected (figure 2A), including EGFR T790M/C797S cis mutation, amplification of EGFR/MET/ERBB2, and PIK3CA mutations (online supplemental table S5). The presence of these diverse resistance alterations may partly account for the modest response observed with combination therapy compared with the TKI-naive cohort. To assess whether this molecular heterogeneity influenced clinical outcomes, we performed an exploratory subgroup analysis classifying patients into three categories based on their dominant resistance mechanism: (1) on-target alterations (EGFR C797S mutation or EGFR amplification); (2) bypass pathway activation (MET, ERBB2, or MYC amplification; PIK3CA or BRAF mutations); and (3) other or unknown mechanisms. No statistically significant differences in ORR or PFS were observed among these subgroups, suggesting that the diversity of upstream resistance mechanisms alone does not fully explain the variability in response to pelcitoclax plus osimertinib.

Tissue samples for BCL-xL expression analysis were available from 21 patients in this cohort. Despite the modest overall ORR of 10.7%, patients with high BCL-xL expression (H-score >100; n=14) showed an ORR of 14.3% and a DCR of 85.7%, and experienced marginally longer mPFS compared with those with low expression (4.2 vs 2.7 months; p=0.058; figure 2B).

In the EGFR TKI-naive cohort, tumor samples for BCL-xL expression analysis were available from 17 patients. Consistent with the findings in the resistant cohort, patients with high BCL-xL expression (H-score >180) exhibited a trend toward longer mPFS compared with those with low expression (27.5 vs 8.1 months; p=0.143; figure 3B). In contrast, no significant differences in efficacy were observed based on the expression levels of other BCL-2 family proteins, including BCL-2, MCL-1, Noxa, or BIM.

It has been reported that the presence of baseline TP53 mutations was associated with shorter PFS compared with patients without TP53 treated with first-line osimertinib.18 In the current study, blood samples for TP53 sequencing were available from 22 patients (84.6%) in the EGFR TKI-naive cohort. For the 16 patients with disease harboring the TP53 mutation, PR, SD, and PD rates were 87.5% (n=14), 6.3% (n=1), and 6.3% (n=1), respectively; for the six patients with disease harboring wild-type TP53, these rates were 83.3% (n=5), 16.7% (n=1), and 0%, respectively (online supplemental table S3). The mPFS was 16.4 months in EGFR TKI-naive patients with TP53-mutant disease and not reached in patients with wild-type TP53 disease, but this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.372; figure 3C). Similar ORRs were observed in both the TP53-mutant and wild-type subgroups (87.5% vs 83.3%, p=0.38; online supplemental table S4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this trial is the largest to date that has explored the combination of the BCL-2/BCL-xL inhibitor pelcitoclax and osimertinib to treat EGFR-mutated NSCLC in both patients previously treated with third-generation EGFR TKIs and those naive to such treatment. Tolerable safety profiles were observed in patients who received pelcitoclax at RP2D combined with osimertinib, and efficacy was also found in the third-generation EGFR TKI-resistant cohort (ORR, 10.7%), first-generation EGFR TKI-resistant cohort (ORR, 71.4%), and EGFR TKI-naive cohort (ORR, 80.8%). Although somewhat limited by sample sizes, our biomarker analysis also suggested that high expression levels of BCL-xL might be associated with improved efficacy in third-generation EGFR TKI-resistant patients, and satisfactory antitumor activity was observed in TP53-mutant EGFR TKI-naive patients.

Dosing at the RP2D of osimertinib 80 mg daily and pelcitoclax 160 mg weekly was well tolerated. Although 92.2% of participants experienced at least one TRAE, 20.3% of them were grade ≥3, and 4.7% were serious. Similar to a previous small phase 1b trial of the BCL-2 inhibitor navitoclax combined with osimertinib,7 almost half of patients in our trial experienced thrombocytopenia, and 10.3% of these events were grade 3. However, these transient platelet decreases resolved, mostly within 2 weeks, and no related bleeding events occurred. Leukopenia and diarrhea were seldom observed in our trial, and the rate of treatment-related SAEs was notably lower (vs 18% in navitoclax trials).19 Although intertrial comparisons are inconclusive, for context a previous report showed that patients with NSCLC who received osimertinib and bevacizumab as second-line treatment and had acquired T790M mutations exhibited a 47% rate of grade ≥3 TRAEs and a 25% treatment discontinuation rate.20 Higher rates of toxicities have also been observed in a trial of osimertinib combined with chemotherapy.21 Overall, the safety profile of pelcitoclax plus osimertinib was consistent with the known safety profiles of previous studies and does not preclude further development of this combination.

Currently, chemotherapy-free treatment options for NSCLC resistant to third-generation EGFR TKIs are mainly targeted according to specific acquired resistance mechanisms. For patients with secondary C797S/T790M mutation, several fourth-generation TKIs, such as EAI045, JBJ-04-125-02, BLU-945, and BBT-176, are currently under development. For those with off-target resistance, such as bypass pathway-activation MET amplification, dual inhibition of MET and EGFR has exhibited satisfactory antitumor activity, whether through combination TKI treatment or bispecific antibody, with ORRs ranging from 19% to 32%.22 23

The BCL-2 family of proteins represents a critical off-target mechanism in the landscape of acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs.18 24 Previous studies have established that these proteins mediate apoptotic evasion in EGFR-mutant NSCLC during treatment with earlier-generation TKIs, such as gefitinib and erlotinib.25 Notably, high BCL-2 expression has been identified as a biomarker for clinical sensitivity to the BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax in specific lung cancer models.26 In the present trial, we observed a 10.7% (3/28) objective response rate (ORR) in the osimertinib-refractory cohort, which increased to 14.2% among patients with high BCL-xL expression. This observation is highly consistent with the established role of BCL-xL as a key regulator of TKI resistance.27,30 Given that these patients currently lack standardized therapeutic options, our results provide clinical evidence for the viability of targeting the apoptotic machinery in the third-generation TKI-resistant setting.

The profound heterogeneity of resistance mechanisms—ranging from on-target EGFR mutations to bypass pathway activations such as MET or PIK3CA—remains a formidable challenge in the management of EGFR-mutant NSCLC.31 32 Interestingly, our subgroup analysis (which is inherently hypothesis generating) suggested that clinical outcomes were not significantly associated with the specific upstream genomic driver of resistance. Instead, BCL-xL expression emerged as a potential determinant of response, with high-expressing tumors exhibiting numerically superior ORR and PFS. These findings lend support to the hypothesis that primary genetic alterations are diverse but may ultimately converge on a shared downstream dependency—an “apoptotic bottleneck.”33 34 By targeting this convergent node with pelcitoclax, it may be possible to overcome the inherent limitations of therapies directed toward individual, highly heterogeneous mutations.

Among the three responders in the resistant cohort, two exhibited a “dual-positive” phenotype characterized by both high BCL-xL expression and detectable BIM. Conversely, patient #01013 failed to respond despite a maximal BCL-xL H-score of 300, a finding potentially explained by the complete absence of BIM (H-score=0). This contrasting pattern suggests that BIM expression may function as a biological prerequisite for sensitivity to BCL-xL inhibition. This observation aligns with the “apoptotic priming” model,35,38 where the balance between proapoptotic (BIM) and antiapoptotic (BCL-xL) proteins determines sensitivity to BH3 mimetics. Conversely, loss of BIM expression—whether through epigenetic silencing,25 proteasomal degradation,39 or genetic polymorphisms40 41—confers resistance to EGFR inhibitors.28 42 Collectively, these findings imply that a composite biomarker of BIM and BCL-xL expression may offer superior predictive value for pelcitoclax-based combinations, warranting prospective validation in larger, biomarker-stratified cohorts.

We found that EGFR TKI-naive patients with TP53-mutant or TP53–wild-type disease experienced similar ORRs (87.5% and 83.3%, respectively). As mentioned, although results from intertrial comparisons are inconclusive, the mPFS in TKI-naive patients with TP53 mutations who received combined treatment with pelcitoclax and osimertinib was 16.4 (8.11–NR) months, exceeding findings of a previous study of osimertinib alone (12.9 (11.1–14.7) months) in the phase 3 MARIPOSA study, which compared the EGFR TKI to a combination of bispecific antibody amivantamab and TKI lazertinib in first-line treatment.43 The TP53 mutation may induce resistance to EGFR TKIs because TP53 controls apoptosis upstream of BCL-2 and its prosurvival relatives, such as MCL-1.44 45 Therefore, targeting these prosurvival proteins could trigger apoptosis across a range of cancers, irrespective of TP53-mutation status, helping to explain the observations in this study.46 Although preliminary, the findings suggest that the combination of pelcitoclax and osimertinib may benefit patients with NSCLC who have concurrent EGFR and TP53 mutations.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations to this study. First, this was a phase 1b, single-arm trial that included a relatively small sample size. Treatment outcomes should be validated in larger, prospective trials. Second, potentially because of the limited tissue sample size used in the biomarker analysis, no statistical difference in efficacy was observed between patients with different expression levels of BCL-2 family proteins. In general, sample sizes in subgroup analyses (including the biomarker analyses) were small, resulting in insufficient statistical power to draw definitive conclusions. Further trials with larger sample sizes are needed to evaluate associations between expression of BCL-2 family proteins and treatment efficacy. Finally, weekly intravenous administration of pelcitoclax poses a significant challenge in real-world clinical practice, highlighting the need to explore more effective drug delivery methods.

In conclusion, this phase 1b study demonstrated that the combination of pelcitoclax and osimertinib is a potentially viable treatment option in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC, with measurable antitumor activity. The combination may also provide a suitable treatment option for patients who have EGFR-mutated NSCLC with high BCL-xL expression, warranting development of phase 2 and 3 trials.
