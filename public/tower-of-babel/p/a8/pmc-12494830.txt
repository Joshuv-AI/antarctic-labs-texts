Acute Interstitial Nephritis Induced by Monoclonal Antibodies Targeting Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP)—Unveiling a New Association: A Case Report
Authors: Maria J Vargas-Brochero, Poemlarp Mekraksakit, Arvind K Garg, Fernando C Fervenza, Ladan Zand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-induced acute interstitial nephritis represents an idiosyncratic systemic T-cell-mediated hypersensitivity reaction associated with over 200 different agents, and predicting its occurrence is challenging. Monoclonal antibodies (mAb) that target the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) (anti-CGRP-mAbs) are among the most promising therapies for migraine treatment, with an increase in prolonged use and a lack of long-term studies. The immunogenicity of biological therapies potentially leads to the production of antidrug antibodies, potentially resulting in hypersensitivity reactions. Here, we report 2 cases of acute interstitial nephritis after more than 15 months of exposure to anti-CGRP-mAb. Both patients responded to corticosteroid therapy and discontinuation of the offending agent, with recovery of kidney function. To our knowledge, this is the first description of acute interstitial nephritis secondary to anti-CGRP-mAbs. Being aware of the possible association between anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies and interstitial nephritis is critical in the scenario of new therapies for nephrologists.Index Words: Acute interstitial nephritis, drug-induced acute interstitial nephritis, calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibodies, migraine

Migraine is one of the most common and disabling neurological disorders.1 In recent years, therapeutic options for preventative treatment have increased significantly. One class of drugs that changed the landscape was the introduction of the monoclonal antibody (mAb) that targets the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) (anti-CGRP-mAbs).1 The FDA has approved 3 distinct CGRP-mAbs (eptinezumab, fremanezumab, and galcanezumab) for use as migraine prophylactic treatment. Multiple randomized controlled trials have shown the effectiveness of these drugs and their relative short-term safety. However, the duration of follow-up in most trials has been limited to 12-24 weeks, with limited data extending to 15 months of follow-up.2, 3, 4 As such, the long-term safety profile of these drugs is largely unknown.5 Here, we present 2 patients diagnosed with acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) after exposure to anti-CGRP-mAb.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 24-year-old woman with a past history significant for migraine was on fremanezumab 225 mg subcutaneously every 30 days for 18 months with adequate control of her migraine headaches. She presented to the emergency department for evaluation of gastrointestinal symptoms, such as nausea and emesis. Her serum creatinine at the time of admission was 1.5 mg/dL (from a baseline of 0.80 from 7 months prior). In addition, her liver enzymes were mildly elevated. The autoimmune hepatitis workup was negative (Table 1). She denied using any new medications or having any other systemic symptoms. The patient received intravenous normal saline given concern for volume depletion and a computed tomography scan of the abdomen/pelvis without contrast failed to show any abnormal findings. Her serum creatinine remained unchanged after fluid administration. The patient was prescribed esomeprazole, which she only took for 7 days. She then underwent an upper endoscopy that was normal. She presented 2 weeks after her initial presentation and was noted to have a serum creatinine of 3.3 mg /dL, hemoglobin 9.7g/dL, and normal serum albumin. Urinalysis showed 1+ blood and 1+ protein, and 3-5 white blood cells/ high power field. Kidney biopsy showed normal appearing glomeruli with diffuse cortical and focal medullary interstitial inflammation composed of numerous eosinophils with some lymphocytes and monocytes, accompanied by multifocal moderate to severe lymphocytic tubulitis with negative immunofluorescence stain. Immunohistochemical stains for SV40, adenovirus, and cytomegalovirus were negative. The Grocott’s methenamine silver stain, used to detect fungal organisms, and the acid-fast bacilli stain, used to identify mycobacteria, were also negative (Fig 1A).

Fremanezumab was stopped. The patient received methylprednisolone 1 g IV followed by prednisone oral 60 mg daily for 2 weeks, followed by a rapid taper. Case 2

A 47-year-old man with past history significant for hypertension, asthma, focal epilepsy status post right anterior temporal lobectomy and amygdalohippocampectomy, and migraine headaches was noted to have an elevated serum creatinine of 1.37 mg/dL from a baseline of 0.96 mg/dL from a year prior on routine blood work. His medications included chlorthalidone, fluticasone propionate-salmeterol inhaler, and galcanezumab 120 mg monthly injections. He was receiving galcanezumab for 3 years before the increase in serum creatinine was noted. No changes were made in his medications, and a year later, serum creatinine increased further to 1.53 mg/dL, and blood pressure control was noted to be sub-optimal. As a result, chlorthalidone was changed to triamterene/hydrochlorothiazide. Serum creatinine, however, continued to increase and peaked at 1.9 mg/dL (Table 1). Urinalysis was normal, with no evidence of proteinuria or pyuria. A kidney biopsy was performed, which showed normal appearing glomeruli with inflammatory infiltrates predominantly of mononuclear cells in the interstitium with tubulitis and mild to moderate tubulointerstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. There were no viral cytopathic changes identified. Immunofluorescence staining was negative, and electron microscopy showed no evidence of immune deposits (Fig 1B).

The patient was started on prednisone 40 mg daily for 4 weeks with a taper of 10 mg every week until he was off. Following initiation of prednisone, serum creatinine improved to 1.39 mg/dL, but 2 months after being off of prednisone, serum creatinine increased to 2.05 mg/dL. At this point, galcanezumab was stopped. No other course of prednisone or other immunosuppressive therapy was needed. After discontinuation of galcanezumanb, kidney function improved with a serum creatinine of 1.17 mg/dL at the last follow-up.

Discussion

Anti-CGRP-mAbs are effective prophylactic migraine therapies. CGRP is a neuropeptide with a complex and not fully understood role in migraine pathophysiology and acts as a potent vasodilator of intracranial and extracranial vessels and centrally modulates vascular nociception.6 Anti-CGRP-mAbs are designed to block this molecule; they can act on the CGRP receptor or the ligand itself; both fremanezumab (a fully humanized IgG2A) and galcanezumab (a fully humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody) target the ligand.5,6 The long-term safety profile of these drugs is largely unknown as most trial durations were short-term.4,7 In this report, we describe 2 cases of AIN secondary to anti-CGRP-mAbs for the first time. Even though AIN has not been reported as a known side effect, other adverse effects, such as injection site reaction (erythema and pruritus), have been more commonly reported with fremanezumab and galcanezumab.8 In addition, hypersensitivity events, including at least 2 cases of delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction to fremanezumab and galcanezumab, have been reported.9,10 Other adverse events of note have included gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, constipation, and nausea) and elevation in liver enzymes (specifically with fremanezumab), as was seen in our patient.4,8,11, 12, 13

Drug-induced AIN represents an idiosyncratic systemic T-cell-mediated (type IV) hypersensitivity reaction. This condition stands as a prevalent cause of acute kidney injury and a difficult challenge to clinicians due to its diverse etiology. Many drugs are reported to be associated with the presence of AIN, and clinical manifestations exhibit considerable variability. Complicating matters further, patients often find themselves exposed to multiple medications (as seen in the 2 cases presented here), rendering the identification of the culprit agent more challenging. The effect of the drug on inducing AIN is not dose-related, tends to recur on re-exposure, and is characterized by the recognition of the antigen by dendritic cells in the kidney.14 Various mechanisms underlie the hypersensitivity response in the host. First, the drug produces direct toxicity, and the interstitial kidney becomes immunogenic. Second, the host might develop antibodies to the drug. Third, the medication may be immunogenic in individuals with specific T cell receptors or major histocompatibility complexes. Fourth, a metabolite from the drug could be immunogenic. Finally, the haptenization of the drug represents a fifth potential mechanism.15,16

The immunogenicity of biological therapies such as anti-CGRP-mAbs varies widely and is influenced by several factors. One key aspect is the structure of the drug, which may be humanized to different degrees, potentially leading to the production of antidrug antibodies (ADA). These ADA can be generated by B cells through both T cell-dependent and T cell-independent processes.17 Factors such as the route of administration (subcutaneous versus intravenous), dosage, and treatment duration play a role in ADA production. Patient-related factors, including genetic predisposition, comorbid conditions, and concomitant medications, also contribute to ADA production.17 The formation of non-neutralizing antibodies may increase exposure to the drug, thereby reducing its elimination half-life. Additionally, the presence of ADA and how it manifests can lead to hypersensitivity reactions, potentially resulting in adverse effects.17,18

Fremanezumab demonstrated ADA presence in 2.3% of patients, with this percentage increasing in the long term (0.2%-2.3%).13,17 Galcanezumab reported ADA presence in 12% of patients in a long-term study with no clinical manifestations.19 Despite this, the immunogenicity raises questions about the necessity for long-term observation for potential immunologic activation that could contribute to interstitial nephritis, as observed in our 2 patients. The reported type IV reactions in these drugs typically arise 12 hours to several weeks following mAb treatment.17 The possibility that this AIN was also a type IV reaction is supported by the negative immunofluorescence staining in the kidney biopsies and the kidney function recovery after removing the agent. In addition, both cases were exposed to drugs, including proton pump inhibitors that could generate drug-specific T cell-mediated immune reactions, which may have increased the likelihood of a more significant reaction to anti-CGRP-mAbs as previously described with other drugs.20 One of the potential mechanisms of AIN is the formation of ADA, which is dependent on the duration of exposure and commonly reported during long-term follow-up as was the case in our patients who presented after 18 months of treatment.

These 2 patients serve as illustrations of biopsy-proven drug-induced AIN. Comprehensive assessments ruled out infectious and autoimmune causes, and both patients exhibited a positive response to corticosteroid therapy and discontinuation of the culprit drug. Over time, kidney function recovered with a reduction in proteinuria. The imperative now lies in accumulating post-marketing real-world experiences to evaluate the safety profile of these drugs thoroughly. This is particularly crucial in the long-term scenario, where predicting the risk of sustained therapy becomes challenging.
