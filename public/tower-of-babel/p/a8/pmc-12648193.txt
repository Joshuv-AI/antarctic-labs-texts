Microbial Inoculation Strategies for Optimal Cherry Tomato Production
Authors: Thiago Dias Silva, Rhuanito Soranz Ferrarezi, Priscila Jane Romano Gonçalves Selari, Jhon Lennon Bezerra da Silva, Marcos Vinícius da Silva, Marcio Mesquita, Henrique Fonseca Elias de Oliveira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe use of microorganisms is a promising technique in agriculture to provide greater water and nutrient efficiency for crops. The objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of microbial inoculation on plant growth, fruit yield and fruit quality of cherry tomatoes ( Solanum lycopersicum var. cerasiforme ) in a protected environment. The experiment was arranged in a randomized block design with three treatments: (i) inoculation with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858; (ii) inoculation with Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3; and (iii) non‐inoculation, with eight replications. The data were subjected to ANOVA using the F‐test followed by the Tukey test (p < 0.05) and multivariate statistical analysis for principal component analysis. B. seminalis led to a higher germination rate, increased fruit yield (FY) by 4.3% and soluble solids content (SSC) of 12.33% compared to the non‐inoculation treatment. B. subtilis increased plant height (PH) and root mass, FY by 9.56% and SSC by 9.25%. Inoculation increased the mechanical resistance of fruits in terms of compression and puncture. The results of this work indicated that the initial growth of cherry tomatoes was increased by inoculation with B. subtilis and B. seminalis , bringing new possibilities for the sustainable production of this crop since inoculation promoted plant growth, increased FY and improved fruit quality. The study suggests that inoculation with specific strains of B. subtilis and B. seminalis can be beneficial for cherry tomato cultivation in protected environments, highlighting the use of microorganisms in agriculture and their potential for sustainable and efficient crop management.Keywords: Bacillus subtilis , Burkholderia seminalis , microorganisms, solanum lycopersicon var. cerasiforme , sustainable agriculture

Introduction

Growers have been pursuing sustainability by employing biological inputs to control pests and diseases while promoting plant growth in a low‐cost way. Therefore, the use of bioinputs has gained prominence, as they act as bioinsecticides or biofertilizers, promoting plant health and development and ensuring higher crop productivity (Khatoon et al. 2020; Basu et al. 2021; Rocha et al. 2024; Valente et al. 2025) and fruit quality (Rouphael and Colla 2018; Chiomento et al. 2024; Vasques et al. 2024). The use of plant growth‐promoting bacteria (PGPB) stands out, as they can mitigate impacts caused by biotic and abiotic stressors (Alcantara and Porto 2019; Rojas‐Pirela et al. 2024; Timofeeva et al. 2024; Ansari et al. 2025).

To promote plant growth, PGPB primarily utilize three mechanisms: (i) phytostimulation through the synthesis of phytohormones (e.g., auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins) and 1‐aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylate (ACC) deaminase, an enzyme capable of modulating ethylene levels (Rigobelo 2024; Ehinmitan et al. 2024; Rafanomezantsoa et al. 2025); (ii) biofertilization by providing macro‐ and micronutrients such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and iron (Fe; Jalal et al. 2024; Soni et al. 2024); and (iii) biological control through competition with phytopathogens for nutrients, induction of systemic plant resistance, and production of antimicrobials and secondary metabolites (Rabbee and Baek 2020; Chandra et al. 2021; Alamoudi 2024; Kumar et al. 2024; Vasques et al. 2024).

Tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum var. cerasiforme ) belongs to the Solanaceae family and is one of the most consumed and popular vegetables worldwide. Due to its widespread cultivation, especially in protected environments (Silva et al. 2023), crop management strategies focus on minimizing the impacts of pathogens, pests, and/or abiotic stressors such as water deficit and salinity (Cruz‐López et al. 2024; Varadharajan et al. 2025). The use of inoculants in tomato production aims to increase crop productivity, fruit resistance, antioxidant activity, and the concentration of secondary metabolites (Zulfiqar et al. 2020; Kashyap et al. 2023; Anjum et al. 2024).

Among the bacteria used to produce commercial inoculants based on PGPB, the genus Bacillus stands out, composed of rod‐shaped, spore‐forming bacteria, members of the Firmicutes phylum, especially the non‐pathogenic species Bacillus subtilis , commonly found in soils (Ortiz et al. 2022; Shafi et al. 2024). The efficacy among strains can vary significantly; therefore, although there are commercial inoculants of Bacillus subtilis , the ATCC 23858 strain has not yet been tested for this purpose. Some bacteria live inside plants and colonize the interior of the host plant without causing diseases, referred to as endophytes, such as the species Burkholderia seminalis . This species associates with different plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana , Chinese cabbage, Chinese amaranth, lettuce, rice, sugarcane, maize, and onion, promoting plant growth and production (Aizawa et al. 2011; Panhwar et al. 2014; Araújo et al. 2016; Hwang et al. 2021; Oliveira‐Paiva et al. 2024). The Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 strain, isolated endophytically from sugarcane roots and associated with promoting plant growth, is recognized for its ability to produce bioactive molecules to control the development of the phytopathogenic fungus Fusarium oxysporum and the bacterium Burkholderia gladioli (Araújo et al. 2016, 2017; Gonçalves et al. 2019; Elshafie and Camele 2021).

A key contribution of this study is its evaluation of a novel bacterial pairing ( Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3) not previously explored in combination for protected cherry tomato cultivation. Although Bacilus subtilis is well known for its biocontrol and plant growth promotion properties (Sagar et al. 2022; Vasques et al. 2024), the ATCC 23858 strain has not been previously tested as an agricultural inoculant and, given its potential, can significantly benefit the sector. Similarly, the strain Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3, despite being recognized for its ability to produce bioactive compounds and promote growth in other crops, has not been evaluated in solanaceous species, and it may offer important benefits to agriculture. In this regard, this study provides new insights into the synergistic or individual effects of these beneficial inoculants on the growth, productivity, and fruit quality of cherry tomatoes, contributing to the development of sustainable and efficient crop management strategies aligned with global sustainability goals.

Sustainability is the necessary convergence point where global population growth, land use, and food production needs are balanced. Plant growth‐promoting microorganisms have been increasingly used for sustainable food production since they enhance crop productivity while fully or partially reducing the use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides. The number of registered bioinputs available on the market is still limited for certain crops and specific purposes, which justifies research involving more crops and new strains. This approach is aligned with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), mainly, but not limited to, SDG 2—Zero Hunger and SDG 12—Responsible Consumption and Production (Ivezić et al. 2025).

The objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of microbial inoculation with Bacillus subtilis strain ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis strain TC3.4.2R3 on cherry tomato in a protected environment and to examine the influence on plant growth, fruit yield, and fruit quality parameters compared to the non‐inoculation treatment. Additionally, we discuss the potential of microbial inoculation as a sustainable and efficient technique for crop management in protected environments.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Area

The experiment was conducted in a greenhouse located in the experimental area of the Goiano Federal Institute of Education, Science and Technology (IF Goiano—Campus Ceres), located in Ceres, Goiás, Brazil (15°21′02″ S latitude, 49°35′55″ W longitude, and 564 m of altitude; Figure S1). The region's climate was classified as Aw (tropical savannah climate with dry winter characteristics) according to the Köppen‐Geiger classification (Peel et al. 2007).

The temperature and relative humidity inside the greenhouse were recorded using a weather station (Vantage Pro 2; Davis; Hayward). The recorded minimum and maximum air temperatures were 8.8°C and 38.6°C, respectively, and the relative humidity ranged from 41.4% to 85.6%. The average temperature and relative humidity values throughout the study were 23.4°C and 62.4%, respectively (Figure S2).

Laboratory Tests From Inoculation Treatments

Compatibility Test of B. subtilis ATCC 23858 and B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3

The inoculation treatments for both germination assays and greenhouse experiments were determined after confirming the compatibility between the bacteria Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3. The double‐layer agar (DLA) method for assessing antibiosis was used, as described by Romero (2005). The antibiosis test revealed discreet antagonism between the bacterial strains studied (Figure S3). Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 slightly inhibited the growth of Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858, with an inhibition zone of 2.5 mm around the colony. The total halo (colony and inhibition) was 1.2 cm (Figure S3B). Colonies of Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 were inoculated separately at the center of the Petri dishes containing nutrient agar (Kasvi), each in triplicates. The plates were then incubated in biochemical oxygen demand (B.O.D.) at 28°C for 48 h. The colonies were killed by exposure to chloroform vapor for 1 h, with 1 mL of chloroform added to the lid of the plates. Subsequently, 15 mL of nutrient broth (Kasvi), containing 0.5% agar and supplemented with 0.2 mL of Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 suspension (109 CFU ml−1), were added to the plates previously cultured with Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3. The plates were kept in a B.O.D. at 28°C for 48 h, and after this period, the inhibition zones around the colonies were measured.

The in vitro antagonistic activity revealed antagonism between Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3. Consequently, we determined to inoculate the bacteria individually rather than in association.

In Vitro Tomato Seed Germination

For the germination test, a randomized block design was adopted, with three treatments, consisting of inoculation with (i) Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858, (ii) Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3, and (iii) a control non‐inoculation (NI), across eight blocks.

The bacteria were cultivated in nutrient broth (Kasvi) and incubated at 28°C in a Shaker Incubator (model SL 222; Solab, Piracicaba, SP) at 150 rpm for 24 h. The inoculum was standardized to an optical density of 1.0 at 600 nm in a spectrophotometer (Double Beam UV–Visible model GTA‐101; Global Analyzer) to achieve a final concentration of 109 Colony Forming Units (CFU) ml−1.

The cherry tomato seeds (TopSeed Garden blueline series; Monte Alto, São Paulo, Brazil) were superficially disinfected by immersion in a solution of sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl 1%) for 1 min and in 70% alcohol for another minute (Araújo et al. 2001). Subsequently, the seeds were washed in sterile distilled water and laid on sterile filter paper for drying.

After this process, the seeds were microbiolized with the bacterial suspensions for 20 min, at a ratio of 1 mL of bioinoculant per g of seed, in a laminar fume hood. For the non‐inoculation treatment, the seeds were soaked in sterile nutrient broth under the same conditions.

Fifty seeds per block were evaluated, totaling 400 seeds per treatment. The seeds were moistened on sterile blotting paper at a 1:2.5 ratio (w/v)–1 g of blotting paper to 2.5 mL of sterile distilled water. The moistened blotting paper was placed in germination boxes (Gerbox; JPROLAB, São José dos Pinhais), which were incubated at 25°C for 14 days in a B.O.D. incubator without a photoperiod (Brazil 2009). The germination rate was determined at the end of the incubation period [(number of normal seedlings × 100)/50].

Greenhouse Cultivation

A randomized block design was adopted, with three treatments, consisting of inoculation with (i) Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858, (ii) Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3, and (iii) a control non‐inoculation (NI), with eight blocks. Each experimental plot consisted of eight plants.

The spacing used was 0.7 m between plants, 0.7 m between rows, and 1 m between double rows, equivalent to a population of 20,408 plants per hectare. The plants were staked using a vertical twine system, and preventive disease management was carried out through the application of a 1% Bordeaux mixture, a mixture of copper sulphate (CuSO4) and quicklime (CaO). At 10 days after transplanting (DAT), treatments consisted of applying the systemic fungicide Difenoconazole and the systemic, contact, and ingestion insecticide Thiamethoxam (141 g l−1) + Lambda‐cyhalothrin (106 g l−1), as recommended on the product label. The removal of lateral shoots (thinning) was done weekly starting from 30 DAT. Weed management was carried out manually in the pots and the ground throughout the experiment.

Preparation and Planting of Seedlings and Experimental Management

Cherry tomato seeds were disinfected and microbiolized. After that, sowing was carried out in expanded polystyrene trays (Isoeste, Castanhal) with 128 cells filled with a substrate mix (Topstrato HA; Genfértil (Provaso), Mogi Mirim, São Paulo, Brazil) composed of pine bark, peat and vermiculite, 14 N‐7P‐15 K fertilizer, potassium nitrate, and simple superphosphate (fertilizer obtained by adding acids to rocks, containing 18% phosphorus, 16% calcium and 10% sulfur).

At 15 days after sowing (DAS), the emergence speed index (Popinigis 1985) and morphometric analyses of the seedlings were determined. Other seedlings were replanted into 200 cm3 containers filled with the same substrate. Here, reinoculation was performed by drenching the base of the plant with 5 mL of a bacterial suspension (109 CFU ml−1). The treatment non‐inoculation received 5 mL of sterile nutrient broth.

At 30 DAS, seedlings were transplanted in 23.5 cm tall × 27 cm top wide × 23.5 cm base flexible commercial pots with 16 L containing a substrate composed of soil and sand in a 2:1 ratio. The soil used for substrate preparation was composed of 34.9% sand, 19.5% silt, and 45.5% clay; pH (in H2O) 4.4; organic matter: 17.8 g dm−3; calcium (Ca) 3.5 cmolc dm−3; magnesium (Mg): 1.4 cmolc dm−3; potassium (K): 102.7 mg dm−3 and P: 3.5 mg dm−3; base saturation: 60.25%.

After transplanting, the plants were reinoculated by drenching the base with 10 mL of bacterial suspension (109 CFU ml−1). New inoculations were carried out every 2 weeks until 120 DAT. The non‐inoculation treatment received sterile nutrient broth under the same conditions.

Before transplanting, the moisture of the substrates contained in the pots was raised to the pot capacity (0.37 m3 m−3; Casaroli and van De Jong Lier 2008; Agbna et al. 2017; Oliveira et al. 2021) and daily irrigations replenished the water volume necessary to return to this parameter.

From transplanting to 135 DAT, a drip irrigation system was used, using self‐compensating button‐type drippers featuring a flow rate of 2 L h−1. Irrigation was controlled by an automated system described by Campos et al. (2021), which collected and processed data from soil moisture sensors (10HS; METER Group). The sensors underwent specific calibration for the substrate, according to the methodology described by Cobos and Chambers (2010).

An automated system took sensor readings every 30 min, and when the volumetric water content of the substrate dropped below the threshold (0.37 m3 m−3), the irrigation system automatically activated the solenoid valves (HVF‐100; Rain Bird), which remained open for 60 s, which applied a water volume of 33 mL, equivalent to an irrigation depth of 0.60 mm.

Experimental Analyses

Morphometric Analyses of Plants

The morphometric analyses were conducted at 15 and 30 DAS, assessing plant height (PH), stem diameter (SD) and root length (RL) with the help of a digital caliper (King Tools). At 30 DAS, the number of leaves (NL) and root volume (RV) were also evaluated using the graduated cylinder method (Basso 1999).

The biomass production analysis was carried out at 15 and 30 DAS, determining the shoot fresh weight (SFW) and root fresh weight (RFW), shoot dry weight (SDW), and root dry weight (RDW). At 135 DAT, measurements of RV, RL, RFW, RDW, SFW and SDW were conducted, following the methodology described by Borges et al. (2011). The fresh weight was kept in a forced air circulation oven for 72 h at 65°C.

Morphometric Analyses, Fruit Yield and Fruit Quality

Harvests were carried out fortnightly, starting at 60 DAT, and occurred every 2 weeks, totaling four harvests over the experimental period. After each harvest, the equatorial diameter (LatD) and longitudinal diameter (LongD) of the fruits were measured using a digital caliper (King Tools), as well as the average fruit weight (FW), number of fruits per cluster (NFC), number of clusters per plant (NCP), number of seeds per fruit (NSF) and total fruit yield (FY) were measured.

The quality parameters of the tomatoes were assessed based on physical–chemical analyses of pH, soluble solids content (SSC, °BRIX), titratable acidity (TA, mg 100 g−1 citric acid) and water activity (WA), using 10 fruits from each treatment per harvest. The evaluations were carried out in triplicates.

The pH was measured directly in the samples by an electrometric method (AOAC 1998). The total SSC was quantified using a digital refractometer (Brix/RI‐Check; Reichert Technologies), with results expressed in percentage (AOAC 1998).

To measure TA, 5 g of homogenized tomato juice was used in a 100 mL volumetric flask, which was then filled with distilled water. A 10 mL aliquot of this solution was transferred to a 50 mL Erlenmeyer flask, and 2–3 drops of phenolphthalein indicator were added. The sample was titrated with 0.01 N sodium hydroxide solution until it reached a pink coloration.

The WA was measured by directly reading the sample in a portable digital meter (model CX‐2).

The texture of the whole tomatoes was evaluated using a texture analyzer (TA‐XT Plus; Stable Micro Systems), assessing the compression (TC) and puncture (TP) of the fruits. The pre‐ and post‐test speeds were set at 2, 1 and 10 mm s−1, respectively. The maximum height of the samples for calibration was 20 mm, and the probes for TC and TP were P100 and P2, respectively. No lubrication was used, and the deformation of the fruit was set at 50%.

Plant Physiological Analysis

Six leaf discs, each with a diameter of 6 mm, were used for the pigment analysis—chlorophyll a (Ca), chlorophyll b (Cb) and carotenoids (Ct). Leaf discs were placed in a dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution saturated with calcium carbonate and incubated in a water bath at 65°C for 24 h before spectrophotometer readings were taken. The readings were done at wavelengths of 649, 665, and 480 nm (Wellburn 1994).

Six leaf discs of 6 mm diameter were each used to determine the relative water content. After their initial weighing, the leaf discs were soaked in distilled water and stored in a refrigerator for 6 h. They were then weighed again to determine the turgid weight and placed in a forced‐air circulation oven at 80°C for 24 h to determine the dry weight. Statistical Analysis

The data were tabulated and analyzed with the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality of data (5%) to verify homoscedasticity followed by the analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the F‐test. Significant variables were compared using Tukey's test for mean comparison at the 5% and 1% significance levels. All analyses were performed using R Statistical software (version R 3.3.0+ for Windows; R Core Team 2023).

To evaluate the relationship between the inoculations ( Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858, Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and non‐inoculation) and a set of variables—SFW, RFW, SDW, RDW, RL, RV, SSC, FY, NCP, NFC, FW, LWC, SD, LatD, LongD and NSF—a multivariate statistical analysis was conducted using principal component analysis.

We applied the Kaiser criterion to identify correlated variables, based on eigenvalues above 1. This threshold generates principal components with relevant information in the original data (Kaiser 1958). Thus, the possible relationships and/or differences among the set of variables are observed from the correlation. All analyses were performed using R Statistical software (version R 3.3.0+ for Windows; R Core Team 2023).

Results and Discussion

Compatibility Test of Strains

The DLA method for assessing antibiosis revealed a discrete antagonism between the bacterial strains studied. Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 inhibited, albeit discreetly, the growth of Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858, with an inhibition halo of 2.5 mm around the colony.

This informed the definition of the treatments studied in the present work, inoculating Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 separately and not in association.

Inoculation was significant for the germination test (p < 0.05; Table 1). Seed germination depends on embryo viability and dormancy breakage from environmental conditions (Luna‐Martínez et al. 2013). Plant growth‐promoting bacteria can influence dormancy breakage, as the reduction in ethylene levels due to the activity of ACC deaminase enzyme from bacteria present in the seed increases germination, along with the production of indole‐3‐acetic acid, which stimulates cell division to promote embryo growth (Glick et al. 2007; Jalili et al. 2009). Seed germination is also directly related to the production of abscisic acid, ethylene, gibberellins, auxins, and cytokinins (Miransari and Smith 2014). These compounds are produced by bacteria of the genus Bacillus (Wagi and Ahmed 2019).

Cendales et al. (2017), studying the effect of inoculating the Bacillus subtilis strain GIBI 200 on cherry tomato seed germination, demonstrated that there was no statistically significant difference between the germination percentage of inoculated seeds (86.7%) and non‐inoculation seeds (80%). However, a high mortality rate of seedlings was observed in the non‐inoculation treatment (51.6%), while the mortality rate of seedlings in the inoculated treatment was low (6.6%), indicating that inoculation with Bacillus subtilis strain GIBI 200 considerably reduced seedling mortality.

More recently, Kumar et al. (2020) have studied the potential of rhizobacteria Burkholderia gladioli (MTCC 10216), Pseudomonas sp. (MTCC 9002), and Bacillus subtilis (MTCC 8528) in promoting the growth of tomato ( Lycopersicon esculentum L.) and fenugreek ( Trigonella foenum‐graecum L.), individually and in a consortium, and demonstrated that inoculating tomato seeds with Burkholderia gladioli and Bacillus subtilis increased the germination rate compared to the non‐inoculation treatment. However, the association between the bacteria increased the germination rate, which the authors attributed to the production of indole‐3‐acetic acid, an auxin and growth hormone that promotes differential cell elongation and acts as a growth regulator. Tripti et al. (2017) investigated the potential use of agricultural and industrial residues as carrier materials, that is, biochar and ashes, respectively, for the preparation of bio inputs using two plant growth‐promoting rhizobacteria, Bacillus sp. strain A30 and Burkholderia sp. strain L2 (1010 CFU ml−1), in tomato growth. The authors demonstrated that the percentage and rate of germination significantly increased when the seeds were treated with bioformulations compared to the control without them. Compared to the control, the greatest increase in seed germination was observed under both bioformulations prepared using (i) ash+Burkholderia sp. strain L2 and (ii) biochar+Burkholderia sp. strain L2. However, the germination rate was higher when the seeds were treated with the bioformulation biochar+Burkholderia sp. strain L2.

The bacterial species Burkholderia seminalis has been shown to produce indole‐3‐acetic acid, which was tested on tomato seeds, enhancing the lateral roots development and tomato plant growth (Tallapragada et al. 2015).

Effect of the Bacterial Inoculation on the Emergence Speed of Tomato Seeds

Inoculation was significant for the emergence speed index (p < 0.01; Table 2). Seeds inoculated with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 showed higher emergence speed indices, 6.77 and 6.54, respectively (p < 0.01), not statistically different from each other, but differing from the non‐inoculation treatment (3.61), demonstrating that inoculation promotes tomato growth by accelerating seedling emergence.

Inoculation of plant growth‐promoting microorganisms, especially from the genus Bacillus and Burkholderia, aids in seed germination, promoting radicle growth, inducing the formation of root hairs, increasing the number of lateral and adventitious roots (Romagna et al. 2019), enlarging RV and surface area (Tallapragada et al. 2015), enhancing PH, and increasing fresh and dry weight production of both roots and shoots (Tripti et al. 2017; Kumar et al. 2020).

In ‘Cherry 261’ cherry tomatoes, inoculation with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens , another strain with potential as a PGPB, improved variables related to the root system, such as increased volume, length, fresh weight, and dry weight of the roots (Szilagyi‐Zechin et al. 2022).

The increased growth of tomato plants may be associated with the ability of Bacillus subtilis to produce indole‐3‐acetic acid and solubilize phosphate, in addition to the production of other substances (Asari et al. 2016, 2017; Borriss 2016; Rabbee and Baek 2020). Additionally, these bacteria can promote the indirect growth of tomato plants by demonstrating antagonistic activity against the fungus Alternaria solani due to the production of hydrogen cyanide and volatile organic compounds, as well as the enzymes chitinase and glucanase and antioxidants such as peroxidase and polyphenol oxidase (Babu et al. 2015). Purified auxins produced by Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus licheniformis were able to influence the growth of tomato plants, resulting in a 20% increase in root, stem, and leaf growth (Lim and Kim 2009).

The genus Burkholderia harbors bacteria that can benefit plants through their symbiosis with the roots. These microorganisms aid in the solubilization of phosphate, antagonism against phytopathogens, biological fixation of atmospheric nitrogen, degradation of chemical compounds present in the soil, and production of indole‐3‐acetic acid (Compant et al. 2010; Luvizotto et al. 2010; Dourado et al. 2013; Tallapragada et al. 2015). The isolates Burkholderia vietnamiensis ZoB74 and Burkholderia sp. ZoB82 increased the growth parameters of Capsicum frutescens plants (Sabu et al. 2019), another Solanaceae species. Inoculation with Burkholderia gladioli and Bacillus subtilis resulted in taller tomato plants with higher dry weight indices of roots and aboveground parts (Kumar et al. 2020). Additionally, Burkholderia has shown efficiency in nitrogen fixation capacity in tomato plants (Mellado et al. 2007).

Inoculation with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 increased FY compared to the non‐inoculation treatment by 9.56% (p < 0.05), and inoculation with Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 increased it by 4.30% compared to non‐inoculation plants (Table 6). The FY of plants inoculated with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 (17.07 t ha−1) was statistically superior to the non‐inoculation group (15.58 t ha−1). Meanwhile, the FY of plants inoculated with Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 (16.25 t ha−1) did not differ from those inoculated with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 or non‐inoculation plants.

The NCP, NFC, NSF, FW, LatD and LongD were not influenced by inoculation. However, fruits from inoculated plants showed soluble solid content of 9.25% and 12.34% higher compared to fruits from non‐inoculated plants, respectively (Table 6; p < 0.05).

Fruits from the non‐inoculation treatment showed lower WA compared to fruits from inoculated plants (p < 0.05: Table 6). There was no difference in pH among the fruits. Fruits from the non‐inoculation treatment exhibited higher TA compared to fruits from inoculated plants. Regarding texture, fruits from the non‐inoculation treatment showed lower compression and puncture resistance than the other treatments. In these aspects, fruits from plants inoculated with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 stood out for their puncture resistance. This increase in firmness is an important characteristic since it is closely related to fruit quality and shelf life as texture is one of the most important quality attributes for table tomatoes (Liu et al. 2009).

The plants treated with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 showed a higher yield compared to the non‐inoculation plants, a result that is consistent with other studies. The tomato cultivar ‘Río Fuego’ achieved fruit yield around 23% higher per plant when inoculated with Bacillus subtilis BEB‐lSbs (Mena‐Violante and Olalde‐Portugal 2007). Tomato plants inoculated with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens experienced FY increments of about 12%, attributing to an increase in the number and size of fruits per plant (Szilagyi‐Zechin 2016). Additionally, inoculation with strains of Bacillus velezensis (108 CFU ml−1) increased tomato FY by 38% compared to the control (Balderas‐Ruíz et al. 2021). Despite the growth variables increment, plants inoculated with Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 did not yield more fruits than the non‐inoculation plants. In contrast, Burkholderia seminalis strain 869 T2 increased flower and fruit production in peppers (Hwang et al. 2021). Furthermore, this same strain improved the quality of lettuce and cabbage by increasing the harvested head weight, size, nutrient composition, and greater accumulation of soluble sugar (Hung et al. 2023).

The increase in tomato yield is associated with the chlorophyll content of the plants, which allows for a higher photosynthetic rate and, consequently, greater biomass accumulation in leaves and roots, resulting in more productive plants (Pishchik et al. 2018; He et al. 2019). Although yield parameter increases were evident, no difference in chlorophyll levels was observed in the present study.

The NFC was unaffected by inoculation with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3. In contrast, plants inoculated with a mix of PGPB ( Azotobacter chroococcum , Azospirillum brasilense , Pseudomonas fluorescens and Bacillus subtilis ) produced a higher number of fruits per plant compared to non‐inoculation tomato plants under a hydroponic system (Aini et al. 2019). The number and fresh weight of fruits depend on the interaction between the tomato genotype and the root‐associated microbiota (Njeru et al. 2017).

The SSC was higher in plants inoculated with Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3. An increase in the SSC was reported by Aini et al. (2019) in the fruit of plants inoculated with a combination of PGPBs ( Azotobacter chroococcum , Azospirillum brasilense , Pseudomonas fluorescens and Bacillus subtilis ). Unlike our data, another study reported that the inoculation of cherry tomatoes with the genus Bacillus did not influence the soluble solid content of the fruits (Osa et al. 2021). Consumer acceptance and preference for tomatoes are directly related to the sugar and acidity content of the fruits (Yang et al. 2023).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

The principal component analysis in Figure 3 shows the main responses from the set of variables, considering the main samples of inoculation types ( Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858— Bacillus subtilis , Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3— Burkholderia seminalis , and non‐inoculation—control treatment). The sum of the variances of principal component 1 (PC1) and principal component 2 (PC2) amounts to a total variance of approximately 64.83%, with PC1 carrying the most information and explaining the most variability in the data, accounting for 38.39%.

Principal Component Analysis (PC1 and PC2) of the variable set based on the inoculations. Legend: Shoot fresh weight (SFW), root fresh weight (RFW), shoot dry weight (SDW), root dry weight (RDW), root length (RL), root volume (RV), soluble solids content (SSC), fruit yield (FY), number of clusters per plant (NCP), number of fruits per cluster (NFC), average fruit weight (FW), leaf water content (LWC), stem diameter (SD), fruit equatorial diameter (LatD), fruit longitudinal diameter (LongD) and number of seeds per fruit (NSF).

According to the biplot graph, the variables responded to the inoculation treatments ( Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3). The variables LongD, LatD, SD and number of seeds per plant appear with the smallest vectors, indicating that they are the least important variables. In other words, the absence of these variables does not result in a significant loss of information in the analysis. Therefore, the variables with the largest vectors dominate the analysis (Figure 3).

The variables NFC and FW are strongly negatively correlated with FY and NCP. The variable FY showed a strong positive correlation with the NCP. On the other hand, the variables SFW, RFW, SDW, RDW, RV, RL, SSC and LWC stood out for having the largest vectors, significantly correlated, where the inoculated samples have the highest values for these variables (Figure 3).

It is possible to establish that the principal component analysis follows the criterion established by Kaiser (1958), where the eigenvalues are greater than 1, so that the components have a significant load of information, meeting the requirements in principal components 1 and 2.

The total variance of PCA (PC1 + PC2) was approximately 64.83%. Jardim et al. (2020) and Jardim et al. (2021) emphasize that accumulated values total variance of PCA above 60% are quite significant, and principal component analysis is of great importance for understanding the morphometric and yield characteristics of the crop, such as SFW, RFW, SDW, RDW, RL and RV. For this type of variables in cherry tomato cultivation, Rapa et al. (2021) and Silva et al. (2023) found accumulated values close to 70 and 63%, respectively.

There is a strong negative correlation between the variables NFC and FW with the variables FY and NCP (Figure 3). This can be explained by the lower FW from clusters with more fruits. A higher number of clusters with fewer fruits resulted in heavier fruits and, consequently, higher FY (Figure 3).

The strong positive correlation between the contents of SFW, RFW, SDW, RDW, RL and RV shown in the principal component analysis (Figure 3) are closely related, as a higher fresh weight of a material will result in a higher dry weight. These results are positively reflected in root size (volume and length).

Conclusions

The strains Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 and Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 significantly improved the germination rate and promoted radicle growth, inducing the formation of root hairs and a significant increase in the number of roots, with plants from microbiolized seeds demonstrating the highest growth rates and, consequently, a greater accumulation of biomass in the initial growth phase.

The Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 strain promoted greater root development from 15 DAS onwards, while inoculation with Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 was slightly later, promoting greater development from 30 DAS onwards. Thus, these effects were prolonged and extended up to 135 DAT. The Bacillus subtilis ATCC 23858 strain promoted the best results in this study, influencing greater vigor and growth of cherry tomato plants, as well as significantly increasing productivity and mechanical resistance of the fruits, favoring a longer shelf life of cherry tomatoes, according to the conditions adopted in this research.

The findings underscore the potential benefits and the promising impact of employing specific Bacillus subtilis and Burkholderia seminalis strains in cultivating cherry tomatoes in protected environments. This approach promotes plant growth, fruit yield, and fruit quality and emphasizes sustainable and efficient crop management through using microorganisms in agriculture. Also, these results suggest that the host plant species is fully compatible with the tested bacteria.

The study encourages further exploration and application of strains ATCC 23858 and TC3.4.2R3 as bioinoculants to enhance agricultural practices and achieve more resource‐efficient and sustainable crop production of cherry tomatoes.
