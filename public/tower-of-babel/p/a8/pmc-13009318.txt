The Role of Family Care on the Quality of Life of Patients With Permanent Enterostomies: A Chain‐Mediated Effect of Inner Strength and Patient Activation
Authors: Juecen Liu, Yating Xu, Meixuan Song, Xianrong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPermanent enterostomy significantly affects patients' lives, making their postoperative quality of life (QoL) a critical concern in clinical practice. Understanding the psychosocial mechanisms that improve QoL is essential for developing effective interventions.ObjectiveGrounded in the conservation of resources (COR) theory, this study aimed to investigate the mechanism through which family care influences the QoL of patients with permanent enterostomies, specifically by testing the chain‐mediating roles of inner strength (as a psychological resource) and patient activation (as a behavioural resource).MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was conducted among 315 patients with permanent enterostomies from a tertiary hospital. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Family APGAR Index, the Inner Strength Scale (ISS), the Patient Activation Measure (PAM), and the Stoma Quality of Life Scale (Stoma‐QoL). The hypothesized chain mediation model was examined using SPSS PROCESS Macro (Model 6), controlling for key demographic variables.ResultsFamily care was significantly positively correlated with inner strength, patient activation, and QoL (all p < 0.01). Inner strength and patient activation were also significantly correlated with each other and with QoL (all p < 0.01). The chain mediation analysis revealed that family care not only directly predicted QoL but also exerted its influence through three significant indirect pathways: the independent mediation of inner strength, the independent mediation of patient activation, and the chain mediation from inner strength to patient activation. The total indirect effect was significant.ConclusionFamily care, as an external resource, may enhance QoL by fostering patients' inner strength, which in turn promotes proactive health behaviours. These findings support a “resource‐psychology‐behaviour” transformation pathway. They highlight the potential importance of moving beyond purely physiological care. To optimize long‐term outcomes, healthcare professionals could consider developing interventions that integrate family support, psychological empowerment, and behavioural skills training.Keywords: chain mediation effect model, family APGAR, permanent enterostomy, quality of life

Introduction

Permanent enterostomy surgery is a critical treatment for colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, and severe intestinal trauma. While lifesaving, it alters normal physiological elimination, forcing patients to cope with long‐term changes in body image, complex self‐care routines, and social adaptation challenges. These factors significantly impact their quality of life (QoL) [1, 2]. Recent multinational studies indicate that ostomy patients face widespread psychosocial adaptation difficulties across diverse regions—whether in Nordic countries with robust social security systems, culturally family‐oriented Asia, or South America. This suggests that diminished quality of life is not unique to specific health care systems or cultural contexts but rather represents a universal challenge faced by the ostomy community worldwide [3, 4, 5, 6].

Family care serves as a core external resource for postoperative social support, effectively alleviating physical and mental stress while promoting adaptive behaviours [7, 8] Research has indicated that emotional support and daily care involvement from family members can directly enhance patients' mental health [9, 10]. However, the specific mechanisms—how external family support translates into internal psychological and behavioural outcomes—have not been systematically tested in the ostomy population.

Grounded in Hobfoll's Conservation of Resources theory, this study investigates whether family care (an environmental resource) improves QoL through the chain mediation of inner strength (a psychological resource) and patient activation (a behavioural resource), rather than through a direct effect alone [11].

Inner strength represents a psychological resource encompassing stress resistance, self‐acceptance, and hope [12] Having inner strength means being creative and stretchable, believing in one's own ability to act, make choices, and influence one's life trajectory in a perceived meaningful direction [13]. Patient activation refers to the behavioural resource reflecting a patient's knowledge, skills, and confidence in managing their own health [14, 15] The former serves as a stable psychological foundation, while the latter functions as a dynamic behavioural driver. Both play distinct yet complementary roles in clinical interventions. By constructing this multidimensional framework, this study aimed to explore the underlying pathways through which external support activates intrinsic motivation.

Subjects and Methods

Subjects

Convenience sampling was used to select 315 patients with permanent enterostomy who visited the gastroenterology and wound ostomy outpatient departments at a Grade‐A tertiary hospital in Luzhou City from January 2024 to May 2025. Inclusion criteria were: colorectal cancer patients who had undergone permanent enterostomy surgery for at least 3 months prior; were aged ≥ 18 years; had clear consciousness and ability to communicate normally in order to be informed; and agreed to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included patients with tumours that had metastasized to other organs or with comorbidities of other major diseases. This study was approved by the Medical Science Ethics Committee. All participants signed informed consent.

Methods

This study employs a cross‐sectional design to efficiently assess the quality of life among patients with permanent enterostomies and its associated factors. The cross‐sectional approach enables data collection at a single point in time, offering cost‐effectiveness and rapid implementation. Exploratory research is well suited for identifying preliminary associations between family care, inner strength, patient activation, and quality of life.

Survey Tools

① General Information Questionnaire: This questionnaire includes details such as sex, age, marital status, educational level, medical payment method, and time since ostomy surgery.

② Family Care Index Questionnaire (APGAR): The APGAR was developed by Smilkstein [16] and introduced into China [17] in 1999. It is applicable for assessing family care levels in individuals aged 10 years and above. The Cronbach's α coefficient of the scale is 0.830. The questionnaire consists of 5 items—family adaptability, cooperation, growth, emotional support, and intimacy—and is scored on a 3‐point scale: “often this way” = 2 points, “sometimes this way” = 1 point, and “almost never this way” = 0 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 10. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient of the scale is 0.891.

③ Inner Strength Scale (ISS): Developed by Lundman et al. [18] in 2011 and translated and revised by Jia Lin [19] in 2015, the Chinese version of the scale includes 20 items across 5 dimensions: connectivity (social interaction), creativity (problem‐solving ability), determination (self‐esteem and self‐discipline), and flexibility (self‐resistance and self‐expansion). The total score ranges from 20 to 120 points, with higher scores indicating greater inner strength. The explained total variance is 59.345%, and the Cronbach's α coefficient of the scale is 0.88. The five factors of the scale yielded Cronbach's α coefficients of 0.813, 0.618, 0.631, 0.751, and 0.724, respectively. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient of the scale was 0.835. The Cronbach's α coefficients for each dimension were 0.876, 0.723, 0.757, 0.869, and 0.855.

④ Patient Activation Measure (PAM): This scale was developed by Hibbard et al. [20] in 2005 and adapted into Chinese by Hong Yang et al. [21] in 2018. It is primarily used to assess the self‐management engagement of patients with chronic diseases. The scale includes 4 dimensions—beliefs (3 items), cognition (2 items), action (4 items), and skills (4 items)—for a total of 13 items. The scale uses a 5‐point Likert rating system, with scores ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” assigned as 1 to 4 points and 0 points for nonapplicable items. The raw total score ranges from 13 to 52, which is then converted into an activation score using the conversion table from the original authors, with the final score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate greater patient self‐management engagement. The Cronbach's α coefficient of the scale in this study was 0.801.

⑤ Stoma Quality of Life Scale (Stoma‐QoL): Developed by Prieto et al. [22], the scale uses a 4‐point Likert rating system, with 1 point for “never” and 4 points for “always,” resulting in a total score range of 20 to 80 points, where higher scores indicate better quality of life. The Cronbach's α coefficient of the scale is 0.920, and its test–retest reliability is 0.880. The scale was translated into Chinese by Zhang JE et al. [23] in 2011, and principal component analysis revealed four dimensions: social interaction (6 items), the impact of the ostomy bag on the patient (6 items), relationships with family and friends (5 items), and physical and mental condition (3 items). The scoring method is the same as that of the original scale. The Cronbach's α coefficient of the scale in this study is 0.847.

Data Collection Method

A convenience sampling method was used. A presurvey was conducted with 20 patients to correct and adjust any ambiguous or unclear questionnaire items. Before the formal survey, the researchers explained the purpose, content, completion method, and precautions to the patients and their families, and obtained informed consent. The survey was conducted anonymously and one‐on‐one. The questionnaires were distributed and collected onsite. For patients who were unable to complete the questionnaire on their own, the researchers explained each item and filled it out on their behalf. A total of 315 questionnaires were distributed, with 304 valid responses returned, resulting in a valid response rate of 96.5%.

Data Analysis Method

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0 software. For normally distributed quantitative data, the x¯±S were used for description. For data not following a normal distribution, the median and interquartile range were used for description. For categorical data, the frequency and percentage were used. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between family care, inner strength, patient activation, and quality of life in patients with permanent enterostomy, with a significance level of α = 0.05. Chain mediation effects were tested using SPSS macro program PROCESS (Model 6), to examine the mediating effects of inner strength and patient activation between family care and quality of life. Mediation effects were tested using the Bootstrap method, with a significance level of α = 0.05. The bootstrap method involved 5000 resampling iterations. A statistically significant mediation effect was determined if the bootstrap 95% confidence interval did not contain 0.

The results show that the total effect of family care on quality of life is significant (β = 0.257, p < 0.05). The effect of family care → inner strength → quality of life is significant (β = 0.078, p < 0.05), as is the effect of family care → patient activation → quality of life (β = 0.044, p < 0.05). These findings indicate that the chain mediation model with inner strength and patient activation as mediators is valid. After including the mediating variables inner strength and patient activation, the total mediation effect was 0.151 (p < 0.05), suggesting a partial chain mediation effect.

Discussion

Family Care and Its Impact on Quality of Life

As an important external resource, family care has a direct positive effect on the quality of life of permanent stoma patients. Our findings are consistent with recent international studies; for example, research in Turkey and Poland indicates that the family's adaptive coping style is a stronger predictor of patient QoL than the clinical severity of the disease itself [24, 25]. This suggests that emotional support, practical help, and other forms of care provided by family members may directly influence a patient's quality of life, helping the patient to feel cared for and supported, thus enhancing their confidence and courage in coping with the disease [26]. This direct effect aligns with COR theory's perspective on the protective role of external resources [11]; the provision of family care may help patients maintain a good quality of life.

Chain Mediation Effect of Inner Strength and Patient Activation

Inner strength, through promoting patient activation (behavioural resources), indirectly improves quality of life. This supports the “resource transformation” hypothesis in COR theory [11]: external resources may need to be internalized as psychological resources to effectively guide proactive health behaviours. Inner strength may serve not only as the recipient of family caregiving but also as the driving force behind patient activation, highlighting the bridging role of psychological resources in the transition from “external support” to “internalized actions.” The role of family caregiving is not to directly “cure” physiological issues but to enhance quality of life through psychological empowerment and behavioural activation, demonstrating a dynamic process of change. This suggests a potential role for WOC nurses in facilitating psychological and behavioural adaptation. Their responsibilities might extend beyond managing peristomal skin health to include assessing the “dual readiness” of patients and caregivers [27]. It may be beneficial to train family members not only in nursing skills but also in providing positive reinforcement. Clinical interventions could consider moving beyond basic stoma care education toward exploring more systematic, family‐centered coaching models. Research has indicated that family‐centered education can increase the quality of life for patients with permanent enterostomies [28]. Specifically, family education can be divided into three phases: (1) emotional synchronization, which involves alleviating caregiver anxiety; (2) guided participation, which involves WOC nurses overseeing patient self‐care while family members provide positive reinforcement; and (3) achieving autonomous transition, which involves gradually reducing caregiver dependency through behavioural contracts. For instance, WOC nurses might incorporate family support training modules that include emotional communication skills [29]; such modules increase family cohesion and promote supportive care to address various challenges in survivor care [30]. For patients with limited family support, community volunteers may play a key role in facilitating access to community resources. The construction of community support networks [31] and the establishment of a “family‐medical‐social worker” linkage system centered on community hospitals can help fill the gap in family support [32]. Furthermore, the significant role of inner strength highlights a potential avenue for intervention. For example, integrating “strength‐based nursing” approaches into routine follow‐ups could help patients internalize family support as personal resilience [33]. By reinforcing past coping successes, nurses can help patients view family support as a personal resource, supporting their transition from passive recipient to active health manager.

Limitations and Future Prospects

Although this study yielded meaningful results, it has several limitations. For example, the study used a cross‐sectional design, which cannot clarify the causal relationships between variables. Future longitudinal or interventional studies should be conducted to examine the dynamic temporal relationships among variables. The convenience sampling method employed in this study may limit the generalizability of the findings to a broader patient population, and it is recommended that future research adopt more representative sampling strategies. Moreover, the data for this study were collected within a specific Chinese cultural context, where family roles and support patterns may be unique. This suggests that the applicability of the model developed in this study to other cultures requires further validation, which represents an important direction for future research. Future studies should expand sample sizes and conduct investigations across culturally diverse populations to enhance the generalizability of findings. Additionally, future research could explore other factors that might influence the relationship between family care and quality of life, such as social support and economic status, to better understand the complex mechanisms at play and provide more robust theoretical foundations for developing more effective intervention measures.

Additionally, another limitation of this study is that sample size estimation during the design phase relied solely on rules of thumb and did not involve a prior power analysis to determine the minimum sample size. We performed a post hoc power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software. The calculations were based on the observed effect size, the set significance level (α = 0.05), and the total sample size (N = 304). The results indicate that the power for testing this key association was 0.99, exceeding the conventional standard of 0.80. This finding demonstrates that the sample size in this study was sufficient and robust for detecting significant effects within the model, enhancing the robustness of the research conclusions. Future studies may conduct a priori power analysis during the design phase to more accurately estimate the required sample size.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that family care may improve quality of life of permanent enterostomy patients through a chain mediating path: “family care → inner strength → patient activation→ quality of life”. The findings indicate that external support may need to be internalized as psychological resilience to effectively promote proactive self‐management. Therefore, this study supports the potential value of a structured “resource‐empowerment‐activation” framework in clinical care. Future interventions may benefit from prioritizing psychological support and behavioural guidance to facilitate the translation of family resources into long‐term health outcomes.
