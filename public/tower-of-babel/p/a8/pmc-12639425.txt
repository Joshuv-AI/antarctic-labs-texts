Effect of Maternal Moringa oleifera Leaf Supplementation on Maternal and Infant Nutritional Status and Human Milk Output: A Pilot Single-Blinded Cluster-Randomized Trial
Authors: Suzanna L Attia, Patrick M Owuor, Silvia A Odhiambo, Jerusha N Mogaka, Raphael Ondondo, Irma Castro Navarro, Aric Schadler, Kristen McQuerry, George J Fuchs, Janet E Williams, Katlyn N Scarrow, Mark A McGuire, Michelle K McGuire, Carrie Waterman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFeeding of human milk enhances infant health and survival over alternative feeding methods, but early cessation often occurs due to perceived or real insufficient milk supply. Moringa oleifera leaves are nutrient dense and may increase milk output.ObjectivesWe investigated the impact of maternal dried moringa leaf powder supplementation (moringa) on milk composition and output and infant and maternal health.MethodsBreastfeeding mother–infant pairs (n = 50) living in Kisumu County, Kenya, were cluster-randomized by location to consume moringa powder (20 g/d) in corn porridge (the moringa group) or corn porridge alone (the control group) for 3 mo. Milk and maternal/infant blood were collected and 24-h expressed milk volume evaluated at baseline and 3 mo. Maternal and infant anthropometrics were measured monthly. Milk lipid and protein concentrations and maternal and/or infant serum retinol-binding protein, ferritin/soluble transferrin receptor, and insulin-like growth factor (IGF)-1 concentrations were measured.ResultsNearly, all dyads (n = 45, 90%) completed the study. Baseline characteristics were similar between groups. At 3 mo, mothers in the moringa group (mean: 947 mL; 95% CI: 794, 1113 mL) expressed twice as much milk as controls (mean: 618 mL; 95% CI: 486, 763 mL; P = 0.003). Infant serum IGF-1 concentration was greater in the moringa group (mean: 25.2 ng/mL; 95% CI: 21.0, 29.5 ng/mL) than that in the control group (mean: 17.8 ng/mL; 95% CI: 13.6, 22.0 ng/mL; P = 0.017). There was no effect of moringa on total milk lipid and protein concentrations, fatty acids, or infant growth.ConclusionsIn this population, 3 mo of maternal moringa consumption was associated with increased expressed milk output and higher circulating infant IGF-1 without impacting infant growth. Additional studies are needed to understand the mechanisms driving these effects and their influence on long-term health.This trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT04587271 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04587271).Keywords: human milk, maternal and child health, insulin-like growth factor 1, iron, malnutrition, Moringa oleifera, dietary intervention, vitamin A

Introduction

Undernutrition contributes to ≤45% of deaths globally in children under 5 y and is associated with short- and long-term effects at the individual and societal levels [1,2]. This is due to a variety of factors including impaired neurocognitive development, overall health, cognitive capacity, and productivity [1,2]. Providing human milk as the sole source of nutrition for the first 6 mo of life [exclusive breastfeeding (EBF)] is estimated to reduce ≤10% of disease burden and to prevent over 800,000 child deaths in children under 5 y [3,4]. Thus, promotion of EBF is a WHO priority as an essential practice for infant health [5]. Despite this, only 40% of infants receive EBF globally [5,6]. In limited-resource populations with high rates of undernutrition, the need for EBF is even greater [3]. Barriers to EBF often include perceived or actual inadequate milk supply, which can lead to cessation of EBF at all income levels [7,8]. Although 6-mo EBF rates in Kenya are estimated at 61% nationally, they are as low as 10% in more vulnerable populations, with mothers ceasing EBF mainly due to a perception of infant hunger surpassing milk supply [9,10].

An effective plant galactagogue (substance stimulating milk production) that can be grown at home, harvested, and consumed year-round prior to and during lactation might serve as an important tool to increase milk output and potentially improve rates and duration of EBF. Moringa oleifera (moringa) is a rapidly growing tree amenable to cultivation in diverse settings [11]. Moringa has been grown indigenously and consumed as food and traditional medicine for centuries [12]. The most studied species, M oleifera, is native to the Himalayas and cultivatable in a variety of climates including drought-prone regions [13]. Moringa leaves have relatively high concentrations (per dry weight) of protein (0.3 g/g), iron (98 μg/g), provitamin A carotenoids (0.17–0.40 mg/g), B vitamins (24 μg/g vitamin B6, 82 μg/g niacin, and 5.40 μg/g folate), calcium (16–20 mg/g), dietary fiber, and bioactive phytochemicals including isothiocyanates, among other nutrients [11,14,15]. The leaves are increasingly a focus of research due to their high nutrient content (see further), abundant antiinflammatory compounds (e.g., isothiocyanates), and potential to increase milk supply, as detailed further [[16], [17], [18]]. Moringa leaves grow quickly, can be harvested throughout the year, and are easily dried and powdered for shelf-stable use suitable to rapid transportation due to climate- or conflict-induced disaster [13].

A recently published systematic review of controlled intervention trials evaluating moringa’s effect on iron status, vitamin A status, growth, and lactation in humans and animals concluded that doses of ≥7–30 g/d were required to sustain changes in iron or vitamin A status while smaller doses were able to influence milk output [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. More specifically, Estrella et al. [23] found that 24-h expressed milk output increased when mothers consumed 0.5 g/d moringa for 3 d (postpartum days 3–5) compared with controls (319.7 ± 154.1 and 120.2 ± 154.7 mL/d respectively; P ≤ 0.001; n = 68) in a high-quality study [19,23]. The mechanism of this effect is unknown. Current available literature on moringa’s impact on human milk and nutrition thus remains limited with few moderate-quality studies and 1 high-quality study. We sought to further explore the role of moringa as a potential tool in the armamentarium against maternal and childhood malnutrition.

Aim/hypothesis

Our objective was to perform a pilot study to begin initial investigation on the impact of M oleifera leaf powder supplementation (moringa) on maternal and childhood nutritional status by direct exposure (lactating mothers) and indirect exposure (breastfeeding infants). Specifically, we aimed to 1) determine whether moringa supplementation improves infant and maternal anthropometrics, vitamin A, or iron status and 2) evaluate the effect of moringa on human milk output and composition. The primary outcomes for this study were infant growth (weight-for-length z-score, weight-for-age z-score, length-for-age z-score, and insulin-like growth factor) and 24-h pumped milk output. Secondary outcomes were selected indicators of infant and maternal micronutrient status (vitamin A: serum retinol-binding protein; iron: hemoglobin and serum ferritin and soluble transferrin receptor), change in milk composition (lipids, protein, and fatty acid profile), maternal anthropometrics [BMI and mid–upper-arm circumference (MUAC)], and infant and maternal systemic inflammation (C-reactive protein and α1-acid glycoprotein). We hypothesized that moringa would increase milk output and enhance selected milk nutrients, which would lead to increases in infant growth and infant vitamin A status and reductions in infant systemic inflammation. We also hypothesized that moringa would lead to direct improvements in maternal vitamin A and iron status, anthropometrics, and systemic inflammation compared with controls.

Methods

Study overview

Details of the methods have been published elsewhere [24]. We first performed a 1-wk acceptability trial of moringa supplementation in the diet of adult females in Kisumu County, Kenya, of the target intervention of 20 g moringa leaf powder mixed into corn porridge. The acceptability trial established that dividing 20 g of moringa leaf powder into 10 g in 1 cup of corn flour porridge consumed twice daily was the upper limit considered acceptable in terms of taste, texture, and sustained daily consumption in our population. Previous reports indicate that ≤30 g of moringa leaf powder daily has been deemed acceptable in other populations [19]. We then performed a pilot single-blinded cluster-randomized controlled trial of 20 g/d moringa supplementation in corn porridge consumed by lactating mothers (moringa) compared with corn porridge (control) alone for 3 mo added to the participant’s regular diet. Enrollment occurred from July to August 2021 and stopped when enrollment targets were achieved; follow-up activities occurred through November 2021. Blinding was maintained at the level of the primary investigator until the primary outcomes were analyzed and at the level of the performing laboratories and biostatistician until biosample analysis was complete and main secondary outcomes were analyzed. Two subcounty hospitals treating regionally distinct populations were chosen as the 2 clusters. Location of moringa compared with control was determined (randomized) by coin toss. Demographic data, anthropometrics, and additional metadata were collected at baseline and monthly through 3 mo. Capillary and milk samples were collected and 24-h milk output assessed at baseline and 3 mo.

Trial design

Due to our inability to find an affordable and safe placebo, the single-blinded nature of the study meant that participants might share moringa with their communities and thus contaminate any randomization at the level of the individual. To avoid this, we selected 2 subcounty hospitals with similar but regionally distinct populations and randomized at the level of the location (subcounty hospital) and not the individual. We assessed baseline characteristics of each location to evaluate differences in likely confounders between groups. As we only had 2 locations, 1 location received moringa and the other location received the control intervention.

Location

Study activities were conducted in western Kenya at the Chulaimbo Subcounty Hospital and Kombewa Subcounty Hospital, which offer maternal and child health services to Seme and Kisumu West Subcounties in Kenya. Kisumu County is the catchment region of study partner Pamoja Community Based Organization (CBO). Pamoja CBO serves a mission to identify and address the most salient needs affecting the wellbeing of families, especially women and children, in Kisumu County through programmatic action and research. Over 70% of the population of Kisumu County live below the poverty level and childhood malnutrition is common (stunting, 9.1%; wasting, 3%; and underweight, 3.5%, in children <5 y old according to the Kenya Demographic and Health Survey 2022) [25].

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Exclusively breastfeeding women (≥18 y) and their infants born within 30 d of enrollment as singleton full-term (≥36 wk gestation) births were eligible to participate. EBF was strictly defined as receipt of only mother’s own milk and/or medication (no water, food, or other liquid). Mothers were required to be fluent in Luo, Swahili, or English, planning to exclusively breastfeed for the duration of the study, and not regularly consuming moringa (with regularly defined as >2 times/wk in the month prior to enrollment). Infants were required to be able to breastfeed with no significant congenital disease affecting growth or ability to orally feed (eg, trisomy 13 or significant heart disease). HIV positivity alone was not a contraindication. Women with contraindication for lactation as determined by their health care provider (eg, untreated HIV and active tuberculosis), receiving regular fortified food supplementation, refusing to consume corn porridge with or without moringa ≥3 times within the first study month, who chose to withdraw, or were lost to follow-up within the first 2 study weeks were excluded.

Intervention and control

Corn flour prepared as porridge was selected as the medium for the treatment (moringa) and served as the control. Corn flour is a culturally acceptable and common food which does not impact milk production or have a large influence on iron or vitamin A status. Corn flour (KMET) and moringa leaf powder (Botanic) were purchased from the same local sources for all participants and distributed monthly to participants. All participants received a standard volume of corn flour (4 kg monthly), and the intervention group also received 1 kg moringa leaf powder at study start with an additional 500 g at monthly follow-ups to ensure ongoing available supply. At enrollment, participants received a standard measuring spoon with standardized instructions on preparing and consuming porridge daily. Mothers recorded their daily consumption of porridge using a standard pictograph designed and tested prior to use by our Kenyan study team in a study-supplied diary. Study staff reviewed these diaries and weighed remaining moringa leaf powder and/or corn flour at monthly follow-up visits to assess adherence.

Metadata collection

A structured questionnaire created in REDCap was used to collect selected sociodemographic characteristics and obstetric and infant birth history. Demographics, daily moringa and corn porridge consumption, diagnosis of any illnesses, and use of iron and vitamin A supplementation during and after pregnancy or during the study period to either mothers or infants were recorded. At baseline and follow-up, the nationally provided Mother and Child Handbook (a portable paper medical chart kept by the mothers and updated by medical providers) was reviewed for diagnoses of HIV, pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, postpartum hemorrhage, intrauterine growth restriction, polyhydramnios, oligohydramnios, or other prenatal complications.

Anthropometrics

We documented anthropometric measurements including weight and length for infants and MUAC, weight, and initial height for mothers. Two study staff took weight measurements using a digital scale (Seca 803 for infants and Tefal Premiss Bathroom Digital Scale for mothers), infant length using an infantometer (Seca 210), maternal height using a stadiometer (Sumbow wall-mounted stadiometer), and MUAC using a standard MUAC tape measure (BuyImpex). All measurements were made in duplicate and averaged. If a deviation between measurements of >50 g occurred for infant weight, >0.5 cm for length/height, or >0.5 cm for MUAC, then a third measurement was taken, and all measurements averaged. Infant weights were recorded to ±1 g, lengths/heights to ±0.1 cm, and MUAC to ±0.25 cm. Once monthly, measurements were compared for the same individual by each of the 2 study staff to ensure consistency of measurements. Digital scales were calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions; z-scores were calculated using WHO Anthro v3.2.2 (WHO Department of Nutrition) [26].

Milk collection

We collected milk samples at baseline and 3 mo for analysis of milk volume, total lipids, protein, and fatty acids. Participants underwent supervised complete breast expressions with provided manual pumps (Medela Harmony Breastpump) between 7:00 am and 11:00 am from a healthy breast not emptied for ≥2 h. Milk was collected into sterile light-protected (foil-wrapped) 50-mL specimen containers until the breast was fully empty. Milk was gently swirled, ∼15 mL aliquoted into sterile foil-wrapped tubes and then transported on dry ice to the laboratory. It was then stored at −20°C until shipped on dry ice to the United States, where it was stored at −80°C until analysis [27]. Mothers were assessed for mastitis as a self-report in conjunction with a diagnosis made by a health care provider. Women who self-reported breast engorgement and pain were referred to their provider for assessment and asked to report back to the study after clinical review. We were unable to assess for subclinical mastitis.

Milk output

The 24-h milk output was estimated using standardized instructions regarding how to maintain hygienic standards throughout the 24-h milk collection periods. This method was chosen as the most feasible method for our COVID-era study rather than dose-to-mother deuterium dilution or 24-h or 72-h infant test-weighs. Mothers received standardized sterilized manual hand pumps (Medela) at baseline, which were used and then collected, resterilized, and provided back to them for 3-mo measurements. Mothers were instructed to empty both breasts every 2–3 h from sunrise to sunrise (a culturally appropriate time-keeping method due to the lack of frequent use of time-keeping devices), record the volume of milk in a pictographic manner in the parent diary, and then feed the milk to their infant with a study-provided clean cup and clean spoon. Mothers were offered additional instructions on pump sterilization throughout the 24-h collection period and were offered the pumps to keep at the end of the study.

Blood collection and analysis

At baseline and 3 mo, we performed capillary blood collection and fingerstick for markers of iron status, vitamin A status, and inflammation. Study staff performed heel stick (infants) or finger stick (mothers) point-of-care hemoglobin (Hgb) by HemoCue Hb201 (HemoCue America). Trained phlebotomists performed capillary blood collection obtaining ≤2 mL blood, which was subsequently centrifuged locally to obtain infant and maternal serum. Serum was then stored at −20°C until shipped. Samples experienced one potential thaw–freeze cycle due to an unexpected change of laboratory location in Kisumu; however, they were transported on dry ice between laboratories to minimize this chance. Samples were then shipped on dry ice to the performing laboratory and stored at −80°C until analysis. Markers of vitamin A status [retinol-binding protein (RBP)], iron status [ferritin and soluble transferrin receptor (sTFR)], and inflammation (C-reactive protein and α1-acid glycoprotein) were performed using sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays by the VitMin Lab (ELISA; VitMin Lab) [28]. Infant serum insulin-like growth factor (IGF)-1 concentrations were determined using commercially available ELISA assays (Quantikine Human IGF-I/IGF-1 Immunoassay; BioTechne) at the University of Kentucky Biomarker Analysis Laboratory.

RBP, ferritin, and sTFR were corrected for inflammation according to the Biomarkers Reflecting Inflammation and Nutritional Determinants of Anemia (BRINDA) study, which supports the use of serum RBP for vitamin A status and serum ferritin and sTFR for population measures of iron status alongside the need for correction of these for inflammation, except for RBP in women of reproductive age [29,30]. Codes for use in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS; IBM Corp, 2021) provided on request by the BRINDA study team by direct correspondence were used to correct the measures of vitamin A and iron stores for inflammation. Corrected measures were compared with uncorrected measures.

Milk analysis

Milk analysis was performed in the United States (University of Idaho). Milk lipids were extracted from 250 μL milk in duplicate using a modified Folch method, and lipid percentage was determined gravimetrically [31]. Extracted lipids were methylated using base-catalyzed transesterification and analyzed on a gas chromatograph (Hewlett-Packard 6890 Series with auto injector) fitted with a flame ionization detector and a 100- × 0.25-mm, with 0.2-μm film, silica capillary column coated with CP-Sil 88 (Agilent Technologies) [32]. The initial oven temperature started at 70°C then increased 4°C/min to 175°C and held for 3 min. The temperature was then increased to 185°C at a rate of 1°C/min and held for 20 min. Temperature then increased at 3°C/min to 220°C then held for 3 min. A final increase at 10°C/min occurred to reach a final temperature of 230°C for 10 min. Hydrogen was used as the carrier gas. The identities of fatty acid peaks were established by comparing retention times to the Supelco 37 standard (Sigma-Aldrich Co), the Nu-chek standards 68C, 68D, 91, GLC643 and 455 (Nu-check prep, Inc) and individual standards. Fatty acid composition was expressed as weight percent of total fatty acid methyl esters identified [[32], [33], [34]]. Absolute concentrations of each fatty acid in milk were calculated as total lipid multiplied by the relative amount of each fatty acid. Milk protein concentrations were characterized using the Pierce bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Scientific) [35].

Sample size

As described in our published protocol [24], sample size calculations for this pilot study were limited because of the lack of available preliminary data on the potential impact of this intervention on our primary outcome of infant growth. We hypothesize that the effect on growth is secondary to increases in maternal milk output. We therefore based our pilot sample size calculation to ensure adequate assessment of any changes in milk output while accounting for our logistical constraints. Based on data from the highest quality study available prior to ours of moringa’s effect on milk output, which observed a 166% increase in milk output of human milk at day 3 of supplementation with 0.5 g/d [23], we calculated a minimum sample size of 16 mother–infant pairs for 80% power at the 95% CI with an effect size of 250 ± 50 mL/d human milk. Accounting for the possibility of attrition and the possibility for a much smaller effect on growth than on milk output directly, we then increased the sample size to the maximum estimated to fit within our financial and logistical constraints. This was maximized to 50 mother–infant pairs (100 participants).

Ethical considerations

This study is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (clinical trials registration: https://classic.clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT05333939). Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Kentucky Medical institutional review board (#58219) and Amref Health Africa Ethics and Scientific Review Committee in Kenya (#P923-2020). Written informed consent was obtained from each participating mother in her preferred language (Luo, Swahili, or English). Infants with severe or moderate malnutrition or any study participants with significant illness or severe anemia (Hgb <7 g/dL) were referred to the medical center for additional care. Adverse events were reported to the University of Kentucky Medical institutional review board and Amref Health Africa Ethics and Scientific Review Committee.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS v28 (IBM Corp) and R (v 4.3.3) with graphing via SPSS and GraphPad Prism v9.5.1 (Dogmatics). Modeling was performed with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) and R version 3.4 (R Foundation). Because randomization assigned 1 treatment to each location, treatment is completely confounded with location; consequently, all inferential models used treatment as the exposure, and location was not entered simultaneously. Results comparing treatments should therefore be interpreted with this design limitation in mind. Outcomes were analyzed separately at each scheduled time point. Using an intention-to-treat approach, generalized linear mixed models were performed to ensure that the error term of mother–infant pair within treatment was used. Treatment itself was not modeled as a random effect. For binary outcomes, generalized linear mixed models with a logit link were used, also with mother–infant pair within treatment as the random effect. Parametric assumptions were assessed prior to testing and natural log transformations (adding 10 to the original value so that no zero values existed prior to the natural log transformation) were made to continuous variables if necessary. ANOVA in a mixed-effect model was performed on milk fatty acids. An α level of 0.05 was used to determine significance. Continuous variables are presented as mean (95% CI). For variables requiring the natural log transformation, back-transformed mean (95% CI) are presented. Categorical variables are presented with counts and percentages. All enrolled participants who continued study activities through at least the first month were analyzed regardless of loss to follow-up. Differences in milk fatty acid composition between groups were tested using ANOVA with mother–infant pair within treatment as the random effect. Pairwise analysis and estimated marginal means (back transformed when needed) were obtained using emmeans package (v 1.10.0) [36]. False discovery rate was used to correct P values for multiple tests. Pearson correlations reported in this study assume independence among observations, as there were not sufficient degrees of freedom to provide an estimate when accounting for cluster effect (with 2 clusters, degrees of freedom = 1). Missing data were assessed and noted to be missing at random; missing data were therefore excluded from analysis at the variable level. In other words, a participant’s data were only removed in an analysis if their data were missing for that specific variable. A subanalysis of pairs with documented EBF throughout the 3-mo study period was performed for our main outcomes. Due to the limitations of the numbers in this cohort, it was not possible to compare the groups (those with EBF compared with those without) head-to-head for our outcomes.

Results

Demographics

A summary of baseline demographic, social, and medical characteristics of maternal–infant pairs is presented in Table 1. Of 50 mother–infant pairs (infants 44% female) enrolled, 0 were lost to follow-up at month 1, 1 was lost to follow-up at month 2, and 4 were lost to follow-up at month 3 (3 in the control group and 1 in the intervention group) (Supplemental Figure 1). There were no differences in these characteristics in a subcohort of only those who strictly provided EBF throughout the study (Table 2). Infants not receiving EBF at 3 mo (n = 11) received cow milk (n = 2), porridge (n = 4, one of whom for 3 d), water (n = 2), or undescribed food/drink other than medication (n = 3); frequency of the complementary feeding is not otherwise available. Data and/or biosample collections were available for infants for some visits when the same information for mothers was not available, which caused variation in the number of data available for each result. Most infants (n = 27, 54%) were appropriate-for-gestational age, and 19 (38%) were large-for-gestational age. Most mothers were without wasting (MUAC <23 cm) at baseline for end of pregnancy, whereas 5 (10%) experienced wasting (Table 3). Prevalence of maternal wasting did not differ between groups or with HIV status. Maternal MUAC at baseline correlated weakly with infant birth weight (r = 0.25; P = 0.08). The prevalence of maternal HIV was equal between groups (n = 8, 32% in controls; n = 8, 32% in intervention group; P ≥ 0.99). One infant was placed on HIV prophylaxis during the study.

Adherence to treatment and adverse effects

Weighing of corn flour and moringa leaf powder occurred at months 2 and 3 of the study with data available for 45 (90%) and 40 (80%) of mothers at 2 and 3 mo, respectively. In the intervention group, moringa was consumed only by the mother. With a goal of 20 g daily, moringa leaf powder consumption was estimated to be 12 ± 4 g/d between months 1 and 2 of follow-up and 15 ± 3 g/d between months 2 and 3. Across both groups, daily corn flour usage did not differ between groups nor did average consumption of corn flour over time: month 2—mean: 113 g/d (95% CI: 105, 121 g/d) in the moringa group; mean: 117 g/d (95% CI: 109, 125 g/d) in the control group; P = 0.418; month 3—mean: 117 g/d (95% CI: 110, 125 g/d) in the moringa group; mean: 123 g/d (95% CI: 116, 131 g/d) in the control group; P = 0.278. Per our supplier KMET, the corn flour used provides 3.7 kcal/g. The amount of metabolizable energy (in kilocalories) in moringa leaf powder is ∼3 kcal/g [15,37]. Alterations from the instructed use of corn flour were rare. These were children consuming it when the mother was away (n = 1 at 2 mo), infant offered corn flour (n = 1 at 2 months), and mothers consuming it when no other food was available or when other foods were not tolerated (n = 2 at 2 mo). Mothers were asked about adverse effects of corn and/or moringa use at each monthly visit. No adverse effects were reported.

Pumped milk output

Data regarding 24-h expressed milk are provided in Table 3 and Figure 1. Pumped milk output did not differ at baseline between groups (moringa—mean: 551 mL/d; 95% CI: 413, 703 mL/d; control—mean: 591 mL/d; 95% CI: 449, 746 mL/d; n = 25/group; P = 0.260); however, at 3 mo, those receiving moringa had greater pumped milk output than those in the control group (moringa—mean: 947 mL/d; 95% CI: 794, 1113 mL/d; control—mean: 618 mL/d; 95% CI: 486, 763 mL/d; n = 25/group; P = 0.003). In the subcohort of those who exclusively breastfed throughout the study (Table 4), baseline milk output did not differ. At 3 mo, milk output increased in the moringa group, but the difference lessened (moringa—mean: 925 mL/d; 95% CI: 747, 1121 mL/d;, n = 19; control—mean: 668 mL/d; 95% CI: 505, 848 mL/d; n = 20; P = 0.046). In the whole cohort, 2 mothers experienced clinical mastitis during the study (both in the control group); due to the limitation in numbers, it was not possible to analyze the relationship of mastitis to any other outcomes. Of the mothers of infants no longer receiving EBF in the control group, 1 experienced a decrease in pumped milk output over the study period (−285 mL/d), 3 experienced an increase (75–160 mL/d), and 1 intentionally stopped lactation altogether. In the moringa group, all subjects reported increased pumped milk output over the period of the study (range, 45–885 mL/d). Change in milk output over the study period differed between the groups (moringa—mean: 387 mL; 95% CI: −142, 1252 mL; control—mean: −349; 95% CI: −601, 74 mL; P = 0.033). In those exclusively breastfeeding throughout the study, there was an increase of 479 mL (95% CI: 83, 1024 mL) in the moringa group compared with 6 mL (95% CI: −262, 373 mL) in the control group over the study period (P = 0.076).

Milk output at baseline for the entire cohort was negatively correlated with 24-h milk output change over the 3 mo of the study. In other words, the higher milk output was initially, the less it increased over the 3 mo of the study and, in some in the control group, the more it decreased (rs = −0.62; P < 0.001; n = 45) (Figure 2). Infant anthropometrics and IGF-1

Infant size by any measure did not differ between groups at baseline or 3 mo; anthropometrics are summarized in Table 3. Infant growth as measured by average weight gain over time did not correlate with either milk output at 3 mo or change in milk output over time (rs = −0.23; P = 0.13, and rs = −0.10; P = 0.53, respectively; n = 44). Infants of mothers receiving moringa had higher serum IGF-1 concentrations at 3 than did those of mothers in the control group (25.2 ng/mL; 95% CI: 21.0, 29.5 ng/mL, and 17.8 ng/mL; 95% CI: 13.6, 22.0 ng/mL, respectively; P = 0.017) (Figure 1), with no difference at baseline (Table 3). Similar patterns were observed in those exclusively breastfeeding throughout the study except for with IGF-1 (Table 4). In this subgroup, there were similar values at baseline but a narrower difference at 3 mo between groups (moringa—median: 26.3; IQR: 21.3, 31.2; n = 19; control—median: 19.4; IQR: 14.4, 24.4, n = 20 ng/mL; P = 0.056).

Maternal anthropometrics

Maternal MUAC and BMI did not differ between groups at baseline or 3 mo (Table 3). By study end, all mothers with low MUAC (<23 cm) had normalization of their MUAC.

Maternal/infant vitamin A status

Serum RBP concentration with or without adjustment for inflammation did not differ between groups for either mothers or infants (Table 3). No mothers took vitamin A supplementation alone or in a multivitamin prior to giving birth, postpartum, or during the study period. Infant vitamin A deficiency (serum RBP, <0.7 μmol/L) adjusted for inflammation was equally distributed between groups and improved by 3 mo with 14 (56%) in the control group compared with 11 (48%) in the moringa group at baseline (P value not estimable due to limited degrees of freedom). At 3 mo, only 4 mothers in each group (17%) were considered vitamin A deficient (serum RBP, <0.7 μmol/L). Prevalence of vitamin A deficiency adjusted for inflammation did not differ between groups and was very low with only 2 (8%) in each group at baseline (P > 0.99); at 3 mo, there were 3 (13%) in the control group and 1 (4%) in the moringa group (P = 0.35).

Iron status

Infant Hgb trended higher in the moringa group (mean: 11.27 g/dL; 95% CI: 10.90, 11.64 g/dL) than that in the control group (mean: 10.66 g/dL; 95% CI: 10.29, 11.03 g/dL; P = 0.024) at 3 mo (Figure 1). Infant Hgb at 3 mo did not differ by HIV exposure nor did it correlate with milk output at 3 mo and when adjusted for maternal consumption of supplemental iron after birth, the effect persisted; in other words, maternal iron supplementation did not explain this difference (data not shown).

Circulating maternal Hgb was not different between groups at baseline or 3 mo, but prevalence of maternal anemia (Hgb < 11 g/dL) trended higher in the control group at baseline than that in the moringa group (40% compared with 12.5%; P = 0.05); by 3 mo, this difference had resolved (4.5% compared with 8.7%; P ≥ 0.99). Additional measures of iron status (serum ferritin and sTFR) did not differ between control and moringa groups at either time point (Table 3). The vast majority (96%) of women in the control group and 100% of women in the moringa group took supplemental iron during pregnancy; at baseline, when Hgb was measured, only 20% compared with 32% of those in control and moringa groups, respectively, had continued with iron supplementation. By 3 months of the study, only 1 (4%) mother in each group consumed iron supplements. Due to the limited number of clusters, we were unable to control for the effect of postnatal iron supplementation on iron status between groups.

Discussion

We hypothesized that moringa supplementation would enhance infant growth, increase milk output, and improve maternal and infant vitamin A, if not iron status. We found that moringa supplementation showed an association with increased maternal milk output and circulating infant IGF-1 without changes in infant anthropometrics or infant or maternal vitamin A or iron status. Moringa in lactation continues to appear safe, as there have been no adverse effects noted with consumption of moringa leaf powder in any human or animal study under 30 g/d, and we did not find any adverse outcomes in the present study [38].

Milk output

The difference in pumped milk output we observed between groups was more pronounced than expected and more than previously reported; however, the difference in a subanalysis of only exclusively breastfeeding infants showed a more modest difference. It is possible that moringa has a dose-dependent effect, as we used the highest reported dose in a study of lactation to date; preceding human studies have used an upper limit of 0.5 g/day [19,39,40]. It is also possible that moringa does not cause much change in those already producing large amounts of milk. A larger study with more clusters would be prudent to further evaluate this observation. It is also important to further explore these findings with additional measures that include infant milk consumption, such as dose-to-mother deuterium dilution or 24-h infant test weighing, and infant feeding practices. We do not know why some women experienced a decline in their milk output over the study period. The mechanism of increased milk output with moringa supplementation is unclear. Sulistiawati et al. [39] have demonstrated increased serum prolactin associated with moringa supplementation in lactating mothers. Additional proposed mechanisms could be through alterations in gene expression in the milk ducts or additional changes to the hormonal regulation of lactation such as through reductions in serum estrogen [41,42]. It is probable that moringa isothiocyanates, which appear to be the most bioactive component of moringa, may be the driving mediator of any physiologic changes. Moringa isothiocyanates are highly antiinflammatory, and there is evidence that systemic inflammation can disrupt lactation and possibly relate to mammary gland inflammation; however, most of this evidence is in the context of obesity [18,[43], [44], [45]].

Milk composition

Understanding human milk fatty acid changes with moringa consumption is limited to 1 study with incomplete reported data; Sarih et al. [46] report no differences in docosahexaenoic and arachidonic acid in human milk with 0.5 g/d leaf powder (compared with other interventions) given to 23 women in the sixth month of pregnancy for 1 mo. Major fatty acids in moringa leaf include α-linolenic (44%), palmitic (11.8%), and heneicosanoic (14.4%) acids [47]. We did not see any changes in the relative concentrations of these fatty acids in milk produced by mothers receiving moringa. Behenic acid (C22:0), although a fatty acid found in moringa in low concentrations (1% of fatty acids), is also found in various other dietary sources common to our population including peanuts and legumes and is not very well absorbed in the diet [47,48]. Future studies should include detailed dietary intake data and larger cohorts if focusing on milk fatty acid consumption in order to assess for the influence of other dietary fat consumption. In the only other available study evaluating changes in human milk fatty acid composition with moringa supplementation by Sarih et al. [46], they found no differences in docosahexaenoic or arachidonic acid with moringa supplementation; however, it is unclear when the single measured milk sample was taken, and there was no baseline comparison. We did not find any changes in these fatty acids either [[49], [50], [51], [52]]. Presumably, dietary fatty acids derived from moringa should influence milk fatty acids, as this has been observed scientifically since the 1950s [53].

Iron and vitamin A status

We did not observe the improvements in maternal vitamin A and iron status that we expected based on other studies [19]. Kushwaha et al. [20] found that compared to controls, postmenopausal women aged 45–60 years consuming 14 g/d moringa for 3 mo (n = 30 in each group) experienced an increase in Hgb (Δmean + 2 compared with −0.4 %; P < 0.01) and an increase in serum retinol concentration (change in mean serum retinol from baseline to study end in the intervention compared with that in the control group: +3.0 vs +0.5 μg/dL; P < 0.05). Shija et al. [22] found that children aged <2 y with iron deficiency anemia consuming 24 g/d moringa for 6 mo also experienced improved Hgb (Δmean Hgb +2.5 g/dL compared with +1.5g/dL; P = 0.002; n = 109) [22]. In our study, women consumed ∼11.8–14.9 g/d moringa in the intervention group, which should have been sufficient for improvement in these measures; however, they also had a high consumption of supplemental iron prenatally.

Assessing vitamin A and iron status is challenging with controversy over the precision of different outcome measures, but large-scale studies support the use of our selected outcome measures [29,54]. Additionally, the assessment of iron status by Hgb alone is inadequate, as Hgb can be abnormally affected by concurrent sickle cell disease or pre-existing thalassemia and therefore does not always reflect iron stores. Adding measures such as serum ferritin and sTFR increase understanding of iron status [29]. The WHO suggests evaluating vitamin A deficiency in populations via the prevalence of low serum retinol, defined as <0.7 μmol/L. This can provide information about suboptimal vitamin A status of the population, whereas concentrations of <0.35 μmol/L are associated with low liver stores and increased rate of complications, for example, xeropthalmia [55]. These concentrations can be extended to the retinol carrier, RBP [29]. Serum RBP does not necessarily need to be corrected for inflammation in women of reproductive age; however, we did not see differences in assessment of maternal RBP with and without correction. For simplicity, we consistently corrected values including maternal RBP in our bivariate analyses.

Infant growth

Although we saw changes in milk output and infant IGF-1, this study was ultimately underpowered to detect a significant change in infant growth by anthropometric measurements [56]. In addition, in the subcohort analysis of infants exclusively breastfeeding for the 3 mo of the study, the difference was apparent visually but did not reach statistical significance. This merits further study in a larger cohort. IGF-1 is a growth-related hormone and biomarker of active and potential growth, which is suppressed with malnutrition and associated with linear growth and muscle mass accrual [57,58]. Because increases in anthropometry can lag behind the response of IGF-1, it is conceivable that a longer duration of follow-up would have detected a response [56]. Other high-quality studies have not observed changes in infant growth with maternal moringa supplementation, but data are limited [19]. Studies in adults are also limited; however, moringa consumed by adults living with HIV at 30 g/d for 6 mo increased BMI compared with that of controls (Δmean BMI +2.6 kg/m2 compared with +1.0; P = 0.001; n = 60) without body composition testing to suggest if lean or fat mass were specifically affected [21].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the study are the controlled study design randomized at the level of the location to reduce crosscontamination of the intervention in the control group, the use of standardized protocols for biosample and data collection, and the recruitment across a wide region of western Kenya. This study was limited by the small number of clusters; a lack of dietary data; a small sample size for nutritional outcomes, especially for infant growth; and the potential for differences between clusters. As a mitigating factor, the clusters chosen were known to be similar in cultural and dietary practices and were similar in their baseline demographics.

One cluster per arm was limiting for the statistical analyses that could be performed. This led to an inability to fully correct for baseline milk output or perform multivariate modeling. With only 1 location per treatment group, intervention effects cannot be disentangled from location effects. Reported P values are anti-conservative under this design and, therefore, should be viewed as exploratory rather than confirmatory. We performed Pearson correlations and a scatterplot (Figure 2) to help bridge this gap; however, the correlations must be interpreted with caution as they assume independence among observations. Owing to the limited number of clusters, we were unable to correct for baseline characteristics such as baseline milk output.

Because of the small numbers of infants not receiving exclusive breastfeeding for the duration of the study, we were unable to directly compare outcomes among those receiving with outcomes among those not receiving exclusive breastfeeding. We provided a subanalysis of those receiving exclusive breastfeeding to reduce this limitation. Importantly, even provision of corn porridge once during an entire month was considered no longer exclusive breastfeeding. As such, our strict definitions do not allow for further interpretation of those who were nearly always providing EBF. Additionally, we used 24-h expressed milk output, which does not measure overall milk production or infant milk intake. An understanding of milk output would also be enhanced by characterization of feeding and nursing behaviors. Differences in these behaviors between groups could possibly confound the results of milk output. As a mitigating factor, the differences in milk output we observed were large, which reduces the likelihood that they occurred due to chance. This difference narrowed but persisted when limiting observations to only those exclusively breastfeeding for the 3 mo of the study. Future studies should allow for more clusters, use more rigorous methods of measuring milk production and infant milk intake as well as maternal diet, and ideally use a placebo to permit double blinding.

This study would be enhanced by the measurement of serum carotenoids and/or retinols as an additional marker of alterations to vitamin A status including vitamin A precursors from moringa ingestion and more detail on the frequency of complementary feeding. The study results may be generalizable to other populations of mothers and term infants but should be tested in diverse settings and in settings of preterm infants and using varying doses and duration of moringa.

Conclusions

Consumption of a high-dose (20 g/d provided with average consumption of 11–14 g/d) moringa leaf powder by lactating mothers for 3 mo shows an association of increased pumped milk output without diluting milk lipids or protein. It is associated with increased concentrations of serum IGF-1 and Hgb in the infant in general, but not in those infants exclusively breastfed for the duration of the study. Moringa is a safe food supplement, which is easily grown in a variety of climates; as such, it is a very promising candidate for future research. More research is warranted to understand the role of moringa on milk composition, understanding of its effect on milk intake and directly measured nutrient consumption by infants, and its holistic impact on nutritionally compromised populations with high rates of premature discontinuation of EBF.

Future directions

Future studies using moringa as a tool in maternal and child undernutrition should consider enrolling growth-restricted infants or mothers with low milk supply and measure infant milk consumption and feeding patterns.
