Grx2 deficiency accelerates BMSC senescence and senile osteoporosis via CD36 S-glutathionylation
Authors: Muzhe Li, Taxi Wumiti, Jie Sun, Tianchi Zhang, Lining Wang, Qinfeng Zhou, Tianwei Xia, Jirong Shen, Yafeng Zhang, Yong Ma, Yang Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleSenile osteoporosis (SOP) is driven largely by bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction. S-glutathionylation is an important redox modification regulating mitochondrial homeostasis, yet its role in skeletal aging remains unclear. The objective of this study is to systematically investigate the role of Grx2 deficiency-mediated CD36 S-glutathionylation in BMSC senescence and SOP development.MethodsGrx2, p53, and p21 expression in human and mouse BMSCs were analyzed by immunohistochemistry (IHC), western blotting (WB), and polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Aging-related and ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis models were established in Grx2−/− mice for bone metabolism and senescence assessments. BMSC senescence, osteogenesis, and adipogenesis were evaluated by SA-β-Gal and WB, ALP/ARS staining, and ORO staining. Transcriptomic and S-glutathionylated proteomic analyses were performed to identify underlying mechanisms. Fatty acid uptake was quantified using two-color flow cytometry. DAG and MDA levels were measured to assess lipid overload and oxidative injury. Mitochondrial structure and function were evaluated by TEM, Mitotracker, qPCR, NAD+/NADH, ATP, ROS, JC-1, and Seahorse assays. The PI3K/AKT pathway was assessed by WB. Co-IP confirmed CD36 S-glutathionylation, and molecular docking predicted C272 as the key modification site. CD36-C272S mutation and Grx2 overexpression were applied to validate functional mechanisms in vitro and in vivo.ResultsGrx2 deficiency, in both male and ovariectomized female mice, accelerates bone loss, inhibits osteoblast formation without altering osteoclast function, and exacerbates BMSC senescence. Through integrated transcriptomic and S-glutathionylated proteomic analysis, we identified the fatty acid transporter CD36 as a critical downstream target of Grx2. Notably, Grx2 deficiency markedly increases the S-glutathionylation of CD36, which not only enhances its fatty acid uptake capacity, leading to the accumulation of toxic lipid metabolites and oxidative damage, but also impairs mitochondrial energy metabolism by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Overexpression of Grx2 or the C272S mutation in CD36, which disrupts its S-glutathionylation, can break this harmful cycle and inhibit BMSC senescence.ConclusionsGrx2 deficiency-mediated CD36 S-glutathionylation drives BMSC senescence and SOP, providing new insight into the redox regulatory mechanisms underlying skeletal aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00999-5.Keywords: BMSC, Cellular senescence, Senile osteoporosis, Glutaredoxin 2, CD36 S-glutathionylation

Introduction

Senile osteoporosis (SOP) significantly compromises the quality of life in the elderly and is one of the leading causes of fragility fractures. Recent studies have identified the functional decline of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) as a critical factor in the pathogenesis of SOP [1]. Senescent BMSCs exhibit impaired osteogenic potential and a shift toward adipogenic differentiation, thereby disrupting bone remodeling processes [2]. However, the underlying mechanisms driving BMSC senescence remain unclear, hindering the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. Mitochondria, as central regulators of cellular energy metabolism and redox homeostasis, play a pivotal role in determining BMSC fate. Mitochondrial dysfunction compromises respiratory chain activity, reduces ATP production, and suppresses cellular metabolic functions [3]. Concurrently, excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) induces oxidative stress, leading to dysregulation of gene expression and an imbalance between osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation in BMSCs [4]. This process is closely linked to redox status, although the precise regulatory proteins and upstream modulators involved remain poorly defined.

S-glutathionylation, a reversible oxidative post-translational modification of cysteine thiol groups, is known to modulate redox homeostasis, signal transduction, and protein function. Under physiological conditions, S-glutathionylation maintains protein structural integrity and functional stability, thereby preventing excessive oxidative damage. However, under pathological conditions such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular diseases, dysregulated S-glutathionylation can lead to protein malfunction and altered cell fate [5]. Recent findings have demonstrated that S-glutathionylation dynamically regulates the activity and structural conformation of respiratory chain complexes, metabolic enzymes, and membrane proteins, thereby influencing mitochondrial energy output, membrane potential maintenance, and ROS scavenging capacity [6]. Notably, in aging models, imbalances in S-glutathionylation have been shown to impair mitochondrial function and accelerate cellular senescence [7]. Furthermore, emerging evidence links the dynamic changes in this modification to bone metabolism and stem cell fate determination, suggesting its potential involvement in the maintenance of stem cell activity and skeletal homeostasis [8, 9].

Grx2, a mitochondria-specific glutaredoxin, plays a key role in the precise regulation of protein S-glutathionylation, as well as in maintaining mitochondrial function and sustaining energy production. Studies have demonstrated that Grx2 expression progressively declines with age in mice, accompanied by a significant reduction in its enzymatic activity in aged individuals [10]. Liu et al. reported that Grx2 deficiency markedly shortened the lifespan of yeast cells, further confirming the critical role of Grx2 in aging regulation [11]. Chalker et al. found that Grx2 deficiency leads to reduced Complex I activity, decreased ATP synthesis, and impaired cellular energy metabolism [12]. Furthermore, Wu et al. showed that Grx2 directly modulates mitochondrial bioenergetics by regulating the activity of Complex I subunits through S-glutathionylation [13]. Additionally, Grx2 has been reported to enhance the function of Complex IV by regulating Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3 Beta (GSK-3β) and promoting the expression of Nrf2 [14]. Despite increasing interest in its biological functions, the regulatory role of Grx2-mediated S-glutathionylation in BMSC senescence and the pathogenesis of SOP remains unclear.

CD36 is a multifunctional transmembrane scavenger receptor that mediates the transmembrane uptake of long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) and participates in energy metabolism, oxidative stress responses, and inflammation regulation [15]. Studies have demonstrated that excessive activation of CD36 in cardiomyocytes leads to increased fatty acid uptake and the accumulation of lipid metabolic intermediates, resulting in mitochondrial energy imbalance. Conversely, inhibition of CD36 activity effectively reduces lipotoxic stress and restores mitochondrial function [16]. Beyond its metabolic roles, CD36 is also closely linked to stem cell fate determination. For instance, downregulation of SUN1 reduces CD36 expression and suppresses adipogenic differentiation of MSCs, indicating that CD36 is a critical mediator of adipogenic lineage commitment. Similarly, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) can interfere with the canonical Wnt signaling pathway in a CD36-dependent manner, thereby inhibiting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [17]. These findings highlight CD36 as a central hub connecting lipid uptake and signal regulation, suggesting that its dysregulation may contribute to bone aging and stem cell dysfunction. In this study, we aimed to investigate the role and underlying mechanisms of Grx2 deficiency-mediated CD36 S-glutathionylation in BMSC senescence and SOP development by using Grx2−/− mice combined with transcriptomic and S-glutathionylation proteomic analyses. This research is expected to provide novel insights into redox-based regulatory networks in skeletal aging and to identify potential therapeutic strategies for SOP intervention.

Experimental animals

For conventional knockout, global Grx2 knockout mice (Grx2±) with C57BL6/J background were generated by Cyagen Biosciences (License numbers: SYXK (Jiangsu) 2018–0049) using CRISPR-Cas9 systems (sgRNA-A1: TAATCCTGAATGCGGGCGTGAGG; sgRNA-A2: CTGGCCAATCGGGAGATGAAGGG). Male and female Grx2± mice were mated to obtain Grx2−/− and Grx2+/+ mice. The genotype of knockout mice was identified by PCR analyses of genomic DNA extracted from mouse tails. Primers used for genotyping are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Male SAMP6 mice and SAMR1 mice (4 months old, SPF-grade, body weight 20 ± 5 g) were purchased from the Department of Laboratory Animal Science, Peking University Health Science Center (License numbers: SYXK (Beijing) 2019-0032). Male C57BL/6 J mice (3 and 18 months old) were obtained from Aniphe Biolaboratory Inc. (License No. SCXK (Zhejiang) 2024-0004). All animals were housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions. Studies were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval Nos. 202211A035, 202303A021).

Bone marrow collection

Bone marrow samples were obtained from patients undergoing hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture at the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. Based on preoperative dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans, patients were divided into two groups: a control group (including patients with normal BMD or osteopenia) and a model group (patients with osteoporosis) and a model group (osteoporosis). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2020NL-162-02). Bone marrow was flushed from the femoral shaft during surgery and used immediately for BMSC isolation.

Cell culture

Human BMSCs: human BMSCs were isolated from bone marrow samples, as previously described [18]. Briefly, marrow was mixed with PBS, layered over Ficoll, and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min. The mononuclear cell layer was collected, washed, and cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

Mouse primary BMSCs: mouse primary BMSCs were isolated from femurs and tibias of 2-day-old C57BL/6 J pups as described [19]. Briefly, long bones were mechanically minced, and bone marrow was gently dispersed by pipetting. The cell suspension was filtered to remove bone fragments, thereby enriching for bone marrow-derived cells. Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. For senescence studies, BMSCs at passage 2 (young, Y-BMSCs) and passage 7 (senescent, O-BMSCs) were used.

Experimental grouping and intervention

Grx2 expression in aged bone and senescent BMSCs

The senescence model was established by culturing mouse BMSCs to passage 2 (young, Y-BMSCs) and passage 7 (senescent, O-BMSCs). Surface markers (CD90, CD45, CD34) and trilineage differentiation potential were confirmed. Senescence was evaluated by SA-β-Gal staining and p53 expression. Grx family members (Grx1, Grx2, Grx3, Grx5) was measured by qPCR. Intracellular GSH/GSSG ratio was determined. During osteogenic differentiation, the expression of Grx2, COL1A1, and Runx2 was analyzed by qPCR and WB. Human BMSCs isolated from osteoporosis patients (model) and from patients with normal BMD or osteopenia (control) were used for Grx2 mRNA analysis. For the in vivo aging study, 18-month-old (SOP model) and 3-month-old (young) C57BL/6 J mice were used, and femoral Grx2, p21, Runx2, p53 expression were detected by immunohistochemistry and western blotting (WB).

Effects of Grx2 deficiency on bone metabolism

Femurs from male Grx2+/+ and Grx2−/− male mice at 3 and 18 months of age (n = 6 per group) were collected for micro-CT analysis, H&E staining, osteocalcin (OCN) immunohistochemistry, and TRAP staining. Serum was also collected from these mice after fasting for 6 h for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis of bone turnover markers, including PINP and CTX-1. In addition, 6-week-old female Grx2+/+ and Grx2−/− mice underwent sham or ovariectomy (OVX) surgery (n = 6 per subgroup). After 6 weeks, tibiae and femurs were harvested for similar bone metabolic evaluations.

Effects of Grx2 deficiency-mediated CD36 S-glutathionylation on BMSC senescence, fatty acid uptake, and mitochondrial function

Immunofluorescence costaining of Lepr and p21 was performed on femur sections from Grx2+/+ and Grx2−/− male mice (3 and 18 months old, n = 6).

In vitro, Grx2+/+ and Grx2−/− BMSCs at P2 and P7 were used to assess senescence (SA-β-Gal, p16), osteogenesis (ALP/activity, ARS), adipogenesis (ORO), fatty acid uptake (two-color flow cytometry), lipid overload (DAG, MDA), mitochondrial morphology and function (TEM, Mitotracker, qPCR for mitochondrial genes, ATP, ROS, JC-1, and Seahorse (OCR)), and PI3K/AKT pathway (WB). Transcriptomic and S-glutathionylated proteomic analyses were performed, and CD36 glutathionylation was confirmed by Co-IP, with molecular docking predicting its modification site and functional consequences. For H2O2-induced senescence, Grx2+/+ and Grx2−/− BMSCs were treated with 200 μM H2O2 for 2 h, cultured for 3 days, then SA-β-Gal staining was performed.

O-BMSCs were divided into three groups: O-BMSCs, O-BMSCs + Lv-NC, O-BMSCs + Lv-Grx2, and Y-BMSCs as controls. Lentiviral infection was performed with iHiTransG P reagent. Infection efficiency was evaluated via GFP fluorescence and WB, and the aforementioned markers were analyzed 48–72 h postinfection.

Grx2−/− O-BMSCs were divided into two groups: OE-CD36 (WT) and OE-CD36 (C272S). Lentiviral transduction was performed using iHiTransG P reagent, and infection efficiency was assessed by flow cytometry and WB, followed by evaluation of the above markers 48–72 h postinfection.

Following C272S mutation in CD36, Grx2−/− O-BMSCs were treated with or without LY294002 (10 μM), with untreated Grx2−/− O-BMSCs serving as controls. Osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation were assessed by ALP and ORO staining, while mitochondrial function was evaluated by measuring ATP content, ROS levels, and JC-1 assays.

Grx2 overexpression alleviates bone loss by suppressing CD36 S-glutathionylation in an SOP mouse model

SAMP6 mice were used as a SOP model, with SAMR1 mice as controls. Femoral Grx2, Runx2, and p53 were detected by WB. The 4-month-old SAMP6 mice were randomly divided into model, AAV-NC, and AAV-Grx2 groups (n = 5, AAV9-Grx2, 5 × 1011 vg/mouse, tail vein); SAMR1 served as normal control. Transduction efficiency was confirmed by in vivo GFP imaging and WB for FLAG. After 2 months, bone metabolism (micro-CT, H&E, OCN, TRAP), BMSC senescence (Lepr/p21), lipid overload (MDA, DAG), mitochondrial function (ATP, mitochondrial genes (qPCR)), redox status (GSH/GSSG), PI3K/AKT pathway (WB), and CD36 glutathionylation (Co-IP) were evaluated.

Indicators of detection

ALP, ARS, toluidine blue, and ORO staining

For osteogenic differentiation, BMSCs were cultured in osteogenic medium (5 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid, and 0.1 μM dexamethasone) for 14–21 days. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was visualized by BCIP/NBT staining, and quantified using an ALP activity kit. Mineralized matrix was assessed by Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining. For adipogenic differentiation, BMSCs were induced with A-medium (1 μM dexamethasone, 0.5 mM IBMX, 10 μg/mL insulin) for 2 days, then B-medium (10 μg/mL insulin only) for 1 day; this cycle was repeated four times (14 days). Lipid droplets were stained with Oil Red O, eluted with isopropanol, and quantified at 500 nm. For chondrogenic differentiation, BMSCs were seeded as micromass pellets (5 × 105cells/pellet) and cultured in chondrogenic medium (Fenghui Biotechnology) for 21 days. Pellets were fixed, embedded, sectioned, and stained with toluidine blue.

SA-β-Gal staining

The SA-β-Gal staining procedure followed the kit’s instructions. The BMSCs were washed with PBS and incubated in the staining solution for 14 h at 37 °C. Three fields were randomly chosen to count the number of SA-β-Gal positive cells using a light microscope.

Flow cytometry

BMSC phenotyping used anti-CD90-FITC, anti-CD45-PE, anti-CD34-APC. For LCFA uptake, cells were incubated with the fluorescently labeled LCFA analog FL-C16 at 37 °C for 1 h, then with anti-CD36-APC on ice for 30 min. Bivariate analysis was performed as described [20].

Lipid metabolites and oxidative stress markers

MDA levels in mouse serum or BMSC lysates were measured using a commercial assay kit: samples were incubated with MDA working solution, boiled, and absorbance read at 532 nm against a standard curve. DAG levels in tibial homogenates or BMSC lysates were determined by ELISA: samples were added to precoated plates, incubated with enzyme conjugate and chromogenic substrates, and absorbance read at 450 nm. GSH/GSSG ratio was assessed with a commercial kit: mouse femurs or BMSCs were homogenized in lysis buffer, centrifuged, and total glutathione (T‑GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) were measured; GSH concentration was calculated as T‑GSH minus twice GSSG, and the GSH/GSSG ratio was then determined.

Mitochondrial function assays

ATP levels were measured using a bioluminescent assay kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and values were normalized to total protein concentration. Intracellular ROS levels were assessed by incubating cells with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C; after washing, fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was evaluated using JC-1 staining: cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution for 20 min at 37 °C, then washed and observed under a fluorescence microscope; the ratio of red (aggregates, high MMP) to green (monomers, low MMP) fluorescence was calculated. For mitochondrial morphology, live cells were stained with 100 nM MitoTracker Green for 30 min and immediately imaged. For ultrastructural analysis, BMSCs were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, embedded in epoxy resin, and ultrathin sections were examined using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7800). Seahorse analysis was performed using an XFe96 Analyzer with the XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit. Mouse BMSCs were seeded at 3000 cells/well. After sequential injection of oligomycin (1 μM), FCCP (0.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (each 0.5 μM), the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured. Basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production were calculated.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of CD36 (PDB ID: 5LGD_A) was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Both the protein and ligands were preprocessed using Maestro. Noncovalent docking was first performed with the Glide Dock module. Subsequently, covalent docking was carried out using the CovDock module, with disulfide bond formation defined as the reaction type, to dock glutathione (GSH, CAS: 70-18-8) at the Cys272 site and obtain the CD36–GSH complex. Palmitic acid (PA, CAS: 57-10-3) was then docked noncovalently to the GSH-bound CD36 using the Glide module. Interaction analysis and visualization of docking conformations were performed using PyMOL (v3.0.0) and LigPlot + (v2.2.9). Energy minimization of the final docking conformations was conducted using the Prime module.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol, assessed by NanoDrop 2000 and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (150 bp paired-end) using VAHTS Universal V6 kit. Raw reads were filtered by fastp, aligned to the reference genome with HISAT2, and gene expression (FPKM) and read counts obtained with HTSeq-count. PCA was performed in R. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (Q < 0.05, fold change > 1.5). Gene Ontology (GO)/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment was based on hypergeometric distribution. Sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Quantitative proteomic analysis of glutathionylated proteins

BMSCs were lysed in 200 μL lysis buffer and sonicated in an ice-cold water bath. After centrifugation, protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. A total of 3 mg protein was precipitated with cold acetone, and free cysteines were blocked with N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) at 4 °C. Proteins were then reduced with DTT, oxidized with diamide, and labeled with BioGSH (AlMSMASS Co.Ltd. (Shanghai, China)), with chloroform/methanol precipitation steps between each treatment. After resuspension in 6 M urea and dilution with ammonium bicarbonate, proteins were digested with trypsin (1:50, w/w) at 37 °C for 20 h. Peptides were desalted using C18 columns, enriched with streptavidin beads, eluted, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and prepared for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)/MS analysis. Mass spectrometry analysis was carried out at AlMSMASS Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive mass spectrometer coupled with an Easy-nLC 1000 system. Data were analyzed using MaxQuant (v2.1.0.0), and differentially modified proteins were identified based on a p-value < 0.05. Functional annotation and pathway analysis were conducted using DAVID and KEGG databases.

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tibial bone or BMSCs using an RNA extraction kit. cDNA was synthesized with All-in-One RT MasterMix. qPCR was run on an Archimed-X6 system using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH as the internal reference. Primers are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Western blot and Co-IP

Proteins were extracted with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors. After BCA quantification, equal amounts were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, blocked with 5% nonfat milk, and incubated with primary antibodies (Table 1) overnight at 4 °C, then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were detected using eECL. For Co-IP, cell lysates (500 μg) were incubated with anti-glutathione antibody conjugated to rProtein A/G magnetic beads for 2 h at 4 °C. Immunocomplexes were eluted and analyzed by WB for CD36.

In vivo imaging

Following infection with AAV carrying the Grx2 plasmid, in vivo imaging was performed under isoflurane anesthesia to assess green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescence expression. Fluorescence signals were captured using the IVIS Imaging System 200, and quantitative analysis of fluorescence emission radiance per incident excitation intensity (p/s/cm2/sr)/(µW/cm2) was conducted using the ROI function in Living Image software (version 4.3.1).

Serum PINP and CTX-1 measurement

Serum PINP and CTX-1 levels were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, serum was collected by centrifuging blood samples, and then added to precoated ELISA plates. After incubation with enzyme-linked reagents and washing, the substrate was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Micro-CT and bone marrow fat analysis

The distal femur’s microarchitectural characteristics were examined utilizing a micro-CT system (SkyScan 1276, Bruker, Germany). Bones were scanned at high resolution (9 μm) with settings of 50 kV and 456 μA. The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the secondary spongiosa of the distal femoral metaphysis, starting from the 50th slice after the highest point of the growth plate and extending 100 slices toward the proximal femur. Reconstruction used NRecon; analysis used CTAn. Parameters: bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and trabecular pattern factor (Tb.Pf).

For bone marrow fat analysis, the same CT images were processed with Sliceomatic software (v6.0). Within the bone marrow cavity, pixels were classified by thresholds: bone (blue) > 100, bone marrow fat (yellow) 0–40, hematopoietic tissue (red) 40–60 [21].

Histology, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence (IF)

Femoral samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5–7 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for general histological evaluation. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was used to identify osteoclasts. Osteoclast activity was quantified as the number of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells per bone surface (Oc.N/BS), which reflects bone resorption capacity. For IHC, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwave heating in citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by blocking with 10% goat serum. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against OCN, Grx2, or p21. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature, and signals were developed using a DAB substrate solution. OCN-positive cells were quantified as osteoblast number per bone surface (Ob.N/BS), serving as an indicator of bone formation activity. For IF, antigen retrieval was performed similarly, followed by blocking with goat serum. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Lepr and anti-p21 primary antibodies, then with 488- and 594-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 28.0. For comparisons between independent sample groups, independent-samples t-tests were conducted. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. Normality and homogeneity of variance were confirmed before conducting these analyses. Grx2 is closely associated with BMSC senescence and senile osteoporosis

To explore the relationship between Grx2-mediated S-glutathionylation and BMSC senescence as well as SOP progression, we first isolated BMSCs from mice. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed a typical BMSC phenotype with multipotent differentiation potential (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). During continuous passaging, BMSCs exhibited typical senescence features, including increased p53 expression and SA-β-Gal positivity (Supplementary Fig. S1D–G), accompanied by a reduced GSH/GSSG ratio (Figs. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1H) and elevated protein S-glutathionylation levels (Figs. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1I). Given that Grx family proteins specifically catalyze protein S-glutathionylation, we assessed the expression levels of Grx family members. Among them, Grx2 showed the most significant downregulation in O-BMSCs compared with young BMSCs (Y-BMSCs), whereas other members (Grx1, Grx3, and Grx5) exhibited no such prominent change (Fig. 1C, D). Notably, Grx2 expression levels were positively correlated with the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 1E). Further investigation showed that Grx2 expression was progressively upregulated during osteogenic differentiation at both mRNA and protein levels (Figs. 1F–H and Supplementary Fig. S1J–K). Public single-cell transcriptomic data (GEO: GSE313972) further confirmed its moderate expression in Cxcl12/Lepr MSCs (Supplementary Fig. S1L–N). In vivo, immunohistochemistry revealed decreased Grx2 and increased p21 expression in the femurs of 18-month-old SOP model mice compared to 3-month-old controls (Fig. 1I–K). Western blot confirmed reduced Grx2 and Runx2 levels, along with elevated p53 expression in aged femoral tissues (Fig. 1L–O). Importantly, clinical sample analysis revealed that BMSCs derived from patients with osteoporosis exhibited significantly lower Grx2 expression compared with patients with normal BMD or osteopenia (Fig. 1P). Moreover, correlation analysis showed a strong positive association between Grx2 expression and femoral BMD in these clinical samples (Fig. 1Q). Collectively, these in vivo and clinical data strongly support the critical role of Grx2 in BMSC senescence and SOP pathogenesis.

Grx2 deficiency accelerates age-related bone loss in male mice

To investigate whether Grx2 is involved in the regulation of bone metabolism, we generated Grx2 knockout (Grx2−/−) mice and confirmed the genotype via PCR and sequencing (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). Western blot analysis further validated the complete absence of Grx2 protein in the femurs of Grx2−/− mice, confirming successful knockout construction (Fig. 2B). Western blot analysis and quantification of other Grx family members (Grx1, Grx3, and Grx5) showed no significant changes in protein expression after Grx2 knockout (Supplementary Fig. S2D–G), indicating that the observed phenotypes were specifically attributable to Grx2 deficiency. Grx2−/− mice showed no obvious differences in general physiological functions compared with wild-type (Grx2+/+) mice. Micro-CT analysis revealed no significant differences in bone parameters at 3 months of age. However, at 18 months, Grx2−/− mice exhibited significantly decreased BV/TV, BMD, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, along with increased Tb.Sp, indicating accelerated trabecular bone loss (Fig. 2C–I). SliceOmatic analysis further showed increased bone marrow adiposity in aged Grx2−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. S2H). H&E staining confirmed reduced trabecular number and disorganized bone architecture (Fig. 2J). To evaluate bone formation and resorption activities, we performed immunohistochemistry for OCN (an osteoblast marker) and TRAP staining (an osteoclast marker). In 18-month-old Grx2−/− mice, OCN-positive osteoblasts per bone surface (Ob.N/BS) were significantly reduced, in dictating impaired bone formation (Fig. 2K, L). In contrast, TRAP-positive osteoclasts per bone surface (Oc.N/BS) showed only a mild, nonsignificant increase, suggesting no notable enhancement of bone resorption (Fig. 2K–M). Consistent with these findings, serum levels of PINP (a bone formation marker) were significantly decreased in aged Grx2−/− mice, whereas CTX-1 (a bone resorption marker) showed no significant difference between the two genotypes (Fig. 2N–O).

Grx2 deficiency exacerbates OVX-induced osteoporosis

With increasing age, women, especially after menopause, experience significant bone loss, which occurs at a rate substantially faster than that associated with normal physiological aging. To investigate the role of Grx2 in estrogen deficiency-related osteoporosis, we established OVX models in both Grx2+/+ and Grx2−/− mice (Fig. 3A). Micro-CT analysis showed no significant differences between genotypes in Sham groups. However, following OVX, Grx2−/− mice exhibited more severe bone loss than Grx2+/+ mice, as evidenced by further reductions in BV/TV, BMD, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, along with increased Tb.Sp and Tb.Pf (Fig. 3B, C). H&E staining further confirmed these observations, showing that Grx2−/−-OVX mice had notably fewer and more disorganized trabeculae compared with Grx2+/+-OVX controls (Fig. 3D). Consistently, OCN staining showed decreased osteogenesis in Grx2−/−-OVX mice (Fig. 3E, F), whereas TRAP staining revealed no significant differences in osteoclast numbers between groups (Fig. 3E, G).

Grx2 deficiency accelerates BMSC senescence

Femoral p16 staining showed no genotype difference at 3 months, but was markedly increased in Grx2−/− mice at 18 months (Supplementary Fig. S3A). WB analysis of 18-month femoral tissues further revealed elevated p21 levels in Grx2−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. S3B). BMSC senescence compromises osteogenic capacity and contributes to age-related bone loss [4]. To determine whether Grx2 modulates this process, we assessed p21 and Lepr coexpression in femurs. While 3-month-old mice showed no differences, 18-month-old Grx2−/− mice exhibited increased p21-positive and p21/Lepr double-positive cells (Figs. 4A, C and Supplementary Fig. S3F). To further validate these findings at the cellular level, we isolated BMSCs from Grx2+/+ and Grx2−/− mice and subjected them to continuous passaging. qPCR confirmed successful knockout of Grx2 in BMSCs (Supplementary Fig. S3C). SA-β-Gal staining showed a higher proportion of senescent cells in Grx2−/− O-BMSCs (Fig. 4B, D). Similarly, in an H2O2-induced oxidative stress model, Grx2−/− BMSCs exhibited a more severe senescent phenotype, as evidenced by increased SA-β-Gal positivity compared with Grx2+/+ controls (Supplementary Fig. S3G, I). Consistently, western blot analysis revealed that p16 was markedly elevated in Grx2−/− O-BMSCs compared with Grx2+/+ O-BMSCs (Fig. 4E, F). Grx2 deficiency further impaired the osteogenic differentiation of O-BMSCs, as evidenced by reduced ALP staining area and activity (Figs. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S3K–L), fewer mineralized nodules in ARS staining (Figs. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S3M), and downregulated OCN mRNA expression (Supplementary Fig. S3P), while ORO staining revealed increased lipid accumulation, indicating enhanced adipogenic differentiation (Figs. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S3N–O). Conversely, Grx2 overexpression in O-BMSCs (confirmed in Supplementary Fig. S3D–E) reduced p16 expression (Fig. 4I, J), alleviated SA-β-Gal positivity (Fig. 4G, H), and also reduced SA-β-Gal positivity in H2O2-induced senescent BMSCs (Supplementary Fig. S3H, J). Furthermore, Grx2 overexpression partially rescued osteogenesis and suppressed adipogenesis in senescent BMSCs (Figs. 4G, K-M and Supplementary Fig. S3Q–S). Taken together, Grx2 deficiency aggravates BMSC senescence, while Grx2 overexpression reverses these effects.

Multi-omics analysis identifies CD36 as a key downstream target of Grx2

PCA showed clear separation between groups, indicating distinct transcriptional profiles (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Differential analysis identified 2459 upregulated and 2261 downregulated genes (Figs. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S4B). GO enrichment analysis showed that upregulated genes were mainly involved in response to oxidative stress, lipid transport, and regulation of signaling receptor activity, whereas downregulated genes were enriched in fatty acid metabolic process, regulation of aerobic respiration, and regulation of generation of precursor metabolites and energy (Fig. 5B). KEGG analysis confirmed enrichment in PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and TNF signaling pathways (Fig. 5C), indicating a broad transcriptional reprogramming of stress and metabolic networks.

S-glutathionylation proteomics using biotin-GSH labeling identified 1656 and 1648 S-glutathionylated proteins in Grx2+/+ and Grx2−/− O-BMSCs, respectively (Fig. 5D, E, Supplementary Table S3). Hierarchical clustering revealed distinct separation between groups (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Interestingly, CD36, a key regulator of fatty acid uptake and mitochondrial energy balance, was identified among these 178 overlapping proteins (Fig. 5M). S-glutathionylation proteomics revealed a significant increase in CD36 S-glutathionylation under Grx2 deficiency, suggesting its involvement in BMSC senescence (Fig. 5N). Taken together, these results suggest that CD36 is a downstream target of Grx2, and its S-glutathionylation may play a pivotal role in regulating BMSC senescence and bone loss.

CD36 S-glutathionylation at C272 promotes fatty acid uptake activity

CD36 is a key transmembrane scavenger receptor responsible for mediating the uptake of LCFAs and regulating lipid metabolism across various cell types. To determine whether Grx2 affects CD36-dependent lipid metabolism, we assessed LCFA uptake using a fluorescently labeled fatty acid analog (FL-C16) combined with bivariate flow cytometry. The results showed that Grx2 deficiency significantly increased LCFA uptake (Fig. 6A, B), and further analysis revealed that CD36-mediated LCFA uptake activity was markedly enhanced in Grx2-deficient cells (Fig. 6C). In contrast, Grx2 overexpression significantly suppressed this process (Fig. 6D–F). These findings suggest that Grx2 deficiency enhances CD36-mediated fatty acid uptake through a redox-dependent mechanism. Consistently, Grx2 deficiency led to increased intracellular DAG and MDA levels in O-BMSCs, indicating lipid overload and oxidative stress (Figs. 6G, H and Supplementary Fig. S5C, E), while Grx2 overexpression reduced these effects (Supplementary Fig. S5D, F). These findings demonstrate that Grx2 plays a protective role in maintaining lipid metabolic homeostasis.

CD36 contains ten cysteine residues, six of which are located within its extracellular domain (Fig. 6I). Sequence alignment showed that these cysteine residues are highly conserved across species, suggesting their functional importance in CD36 regulation (Supplementary Fig. S5I). Our S-glutathionylated proteomic analysis identified C272 as an S-glutathionylation site on CD36 (Fig. 6J). In silico molecular docking analysis predicted that the C272 residue of CD36 forms a spatially compatible binding pocket for glutathione (GSH) (Fig. 6K). Moreover, the computational model further revealed that the C272–GSH complex resides within the fatty acid-binding pocket, implying a potential regulatory role in fatty acid uptake (Fig. 6L, M). To assess its functional significance, we generated a C272S mutant (Figs. 6N and Supplementary Fig. S5G–H). Compared with wild-type CD36 (CD36 WT), C272S mutation (CD36 C272S) significantly reduced LCFA uptake (Fig. 6O–Q) and decreased DAG and MDA levels (Fig. 6R, S), indicating attenuation of lipid accumulation and oxidative damage. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CD36 S-glutathionylation at C272 promotes fatty acid uptake in BMSCs, leading to the accumulation of toxic lipid metabolites and oxidative injury.

CD36 S-glutathionylation mediates Grx2 deficiency-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in senescent BMSCs

Given that mitochondrial dysfunction is a key event in the development of SOP and BMSC senescence, and that our multi-omics analyses strongly suggested a close association between Grx2 deficiency and mitochondrial metabolic dysregulation, we systematically evaluated the impact of Grx2 deficiency on mitochondrial energy metabolism in BMSCs. Seahorse analysis revealed that, in O-BMSCs, Grx2 deficiency significantly impaired mitochondrial respiration, as evidenced by reduced basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production, whereas no significant differences were observed in Y-BMSCs (Fig. 7A–D). ATP levels were also significantly decreased in Grx2−/− O-BMSCs (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Additionally, the expression of respiratory chain genes (ND1, ND2, Cytb, Cox1, ATP6, and ATP8) was significantly downregulated (Fig. 7E–J).

To explore the consequences of these metabolic changes, we next assessed intracellular ROS levels and MMP. Grx2 deficiency increased ROS accumulation (Fig. 7K, L). MMP, a critical component of the proton motive force in energy metabolism and an indicator of mitochondrial efficiency and functional status, was assessed using JC-1 staining. Grx2−/− O-BMSCs exhibited a markedly reduced JC-1 red/green fluorescence ratio, indicating mitochondrial depolarization and disrupted respiratory chain integrity (Fig. 7K, M). TEM and MitoTracker staining further revealed mitochondrial structural damage in Grx2−/− O-BMSCs, including fragmented mitochondria, cristae loss, and abnormal distribution (Fig. 7K). Given our previous finding that CD36 S-glutathionylation at C272 was markedly increased following Grx2 loss, we next investigated whether this modification contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction. Remarkably, C272S mutation significantly restored ATP levels (Fig. 7Q). Furthermore, C272S mutation markedly reduced ROS accumulation (Fig. 7S, T) and increased JC-1 red fluorescence while decreasing green fluorescence, indicating reestablished mitochondrial membrane potential and improved respiratory efficiency (Fig. 7T, U). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CD36 S-glutathionylation at C272 mediates mitochondrial dysfunction in BMSCs, and blocking this modification partially restores mitochondrial homeostasis.

CD36 S-glutathionylation worsens mitochondrial energy metabolism dysfunction in senescent BMSCs via the PI3K/AKT pathway

As a major regulator of mitochondrial energy metabolism and oxidative stress resistance, the PI3K/AKT pathway plays a pivotal role in maintaining skeletal homeostasis and has been closely linked to BMSC senescence and the pathogenesis of osteoporosis [22]. Our multi-omics analyses also highlighted significant involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway, suggesting it may act downstream of CD36 S-glutathionylation. We therefore assessed its activation status. In Grx2−/− O-BMSCs, C272S mutation significantly restored PI3K and AKT phosphorylation levels (Fig. 8A–C). Similarly, Grx2 overexpression attenuated CD36 S-glutathionylation and was associated with a concomitant restoration of PI3K/AKT signaling (Figs. 8D–E and Supplementary Fig. S7A–D). These findings indicate that Grx2 acts as an essential regulator of PI3K/AKT activity in BMSCs, and CD36 S-glutathionylation at C272 directly modulates this pathway.

To validate the regulatory role of Grx2-mediated deglutathionylation of CD36 in mitochondrial function, BMSC senescence, and bone metabolism in vivo, we used a SAMP6 mouse model. Discussion

Grx2-mediated S-glutathionylation is a reversible redox modification of cysteine residues that plays a critical role in maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis, regulating signal transduction, and preserving mitochondrial function. In recent years, accumulating evidence has highlighted the importance of S-glutathionylation in the aging process. Dysregulation of this modification disrupts cellular antioxidant systems, leading to excessive accumulation of ROS, impaired mitochondrial metabolism, and diminished energy production, ultimately resulting in functional decline and the acceleration of senescent phenotypes [23]. Furthermore, aberrant S-glutathionylation has been closely associated with several age-related pathological conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases and cancer [24, 25]. In this study, we first examined the in vivo relevance of Grx2 in bone homeostasis. Grx2 deficiency was associated with accelerated age-related and ovariectomy-induced bone loss, as evidenced by micro-CT and histological analyses, while Grx2 overexpression alleviated bone abnormalities in senile osteoporotic mice. These in vivo phenotypes prompted us to investigate the underlying redox mechanism in BMSCs. Through integrated multi-omics analyses and in vitro experiments, we identified CD36 as a key redox-regulated target of Grx2. Grx2 deficiency markedly increased the S-glutathionylation level of CD36. Mechanistically, CD36 S-glutathionylation at C272 not only enhanced its fatty acid uptake capacity, leading to the accumulation of toxic lipid metabolites and oxidative damage, but also impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism by suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway. Together, these findings support a redox-associated mechanism by which Grx2 deficiency induces BMSC senescence and bone loss.

In this study, we report for the first time the pivotal role of Grx2 in regulating age-related bone loss. Our in vivo findings demonstrate that Grx2 deficiency aggravates trabecular deterioration in aged and OVX mice, accompanied by downregulation of osteogenic markers, while osteoclast activity was not markedly changed in our current analyses. These histological and CT-based observations directly link Grx2 loss to impaired bone formation rather than enhanced resorption. Notably, Grx2 deficiency enhances BMSC senescence in the aged femur, leading to impaired osteogenic differentiation. Conversely, Grx2 overexpression mitigates BMSC senescence and promotes osteogenic commitment, effectively restoring abnormal bone metabolism in the SOP mouse model. These results collectively suggest an important role for Grx2 in governing BMSC fate and maintaining skeletal integrity, providing preclinical evidence supporting the potential of Grx2-targeted gene therapy. Interestingly, Grx2 deficiency did not affect osteogenic capacity in young mice or in early-passage BMSCs, suggesting that Grx2 function is closely linked to oxidative stress levels induced by aging. This observation aligns with the “oxidative stress threshold theory” proposed by Khosla et al., which postulates that bone metabolic disorders are triggered only when antioxidant defense systems become overwhelmed under specific pathological conditions [26]. Our findings are also in line with previous reports by Wu et al., who showed that Grx2 knockdown sensitized cells to oxidative stress, leading to functional decline only under oxidative conditions [13]. Interestingly, the deficiency of Grx2 did not alter the expression of other major redox-regulatory enzymes [27], suggesting that the phenotypes observed in our model are primarily attributable to the specific loss of Grx2. Consistent with prior studies, we did not observe marked compensatory upregulation of other major glutaredoxin family members at the protein level following Grx2 deficiency.

S-glutathionylation is closely associated with fatty acid metabolism. Previous studies have shown that FABP5 is highly susceptible to S-glutathionylation under oxidative conditions. S-glutathionylation of Cys127 in FABP5 enhances its fatty acid-binding capacity and promotes its nuclear translocation [28]. In addition, GSTA1 can directly interact with FABP1 through S-glutathionylation, facilitating FABP1 degradation, thereby reducing free fatty acid uptake and transport in hepatocytes, decreasing triglyceride accumulation, and improving hepatic lipid metabolic disorders [29]. In the regulation of lipid metabolism, CD36 serves as another crucial transmembrane receptor that not only functions as the primary entry point for LCFA uptake but also participates in lipid signaling and storage regulation, with its activity directly determining lipid deposition and subsequent oxidative metabolism. In this study, we demonstrated for the first time in a senescent BMSC model that Grx2 deficiency increased CD36 S-glutathionylation at C272, thereby enhancing fatty acid uptake and elevating the levels of DAG and MDA. In contrast, Grx2 overexpression or the C272S mutation in CD36 reversed these effects. Supporting our findings, Wang et al. reported that in the extracellular domain of CD36, Cys272 and Cys333 form a disulfide bond, and disruption of this bond by H2S or oxidative stress enhances fatty acid uptake activity [30]. Notably, although these results support a functional role for S-glutathionylation of CD36 at C272 in regulating LCFA uptake activity, we cannot exclude the possibility that S-glutathionylation of CD36 at C272 may also affect LCFA uptake through additional mechanisms, such as alterations in receptor localization, membrane trafficking, or protein stability, all of which require further investigation.

CD36 functions not only as a transporter mediating transmembrane fatty acid uptake but also as a signaling protein that senses lipid overload and regulates mitochondrial quality control. Studies have shown that CD36 mediates the uptake of oxidized oxLDL, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [31]. Overexpression of CD36 suppresses mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, promotes ROS production and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and consequently induces mitochondrial damage and cellular dysfunction [32]. Moreover, following myocardial infarction, elevated CD36 palmitoylation drives its accumulation on the plasma membrane, exacerbating fatty acid overload; conversely, inhibition of palmitoylation redirects CD36 to the mitochondrial outer membrane, where it forms a signaling axis with PGAM5 to initiate mitophagy and optimize mitochondrial network remodeling [16]. These findings further support the close association between CD36 activity and mitochondrial dysfunction. In our study, Grx2 deficiency in O-BMSCs resulted in downregulation of mitochondrial-related genes, reduced ATP production, a decreased NAD+/NADH ratio, diminished MMP, and increased accumulation of ROS. In contrast, the C272S mutation of CD36 restored ATP levels, lowered ROS accumulation, and improved MMP, highlighting the crucial role of CD36 S-glutathionylation in maintaining mitochondrial energy homeostasis.

The PI3K/AKT pathway is a well-established regulator of mitochondrial function and cellular energy balance. Upon activation by upstream receptors, PI3K is phosphorylated and catalyzes the conversion of PIP2 to PIP3, leading to AKT activation. Activated AKT promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, enhances ATP production, and reduces ROS levels, thereby supporting OXPHOS efficiency and antioxidant defense [33, 34]. Grx2 has been shown to interact with the AKT/GSK-3β axis under oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes [35], and Grx1 has similarly been reported to regulate AKT activation in endothelial cells [36]. Recent studies have shown that noise exposure markedly increases CD36 expression in the mouse hippocampus, elevating oxidative stress, reducing PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, and ultimately enhancing neuroinflammation [37]. In our study, we found that either the C272S mutation in CD36 or Grx2 overexpression restored the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, whereas inhibition of this pathway with LY294002 reversed the protective effects conferred by the C272S mutation. Of note, the C272S mutation simultaneously restored PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, reduced lipid metabolite accumulation, and attenuated ROS levels, indicating a complex interplay among these events. Whether PI3K/AKT inhibition is a direct consequence of CD36 S‑glutathionylation or secondary to lipotoxic stress and/or ROS remains to be determined. CD36 is known to directly activate the Src/PI3K/AKT axis in a lipid‑independent manner [38], but chronic fatty acid overload and oxidative stress can also impair PI3K/AKT signaling [39]. We therefore speculate that both direct and indirect mechanisms may contribute. These findings support the role of the PI3K/AKT axis as a critical regulatory node within the Grx2–CD36–mitochondrial signaling network.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be noted. The absence of conditional or tissue-specific Grx2 knockout models limits precise evaluation of cell-specific effects in bone. In addition, the relatively small clinical sample size and potential variability, including sex differences, may affect the generalizability of the results. Moreover, the detailed biochemical mechanisms linking Grx2 activity to CD36 S-glutathionylation, as well as the roles of different Grx2 isoforms, remain to be further clarified. Future studies addressing these aspects will help to strengthen and extend our conclusions.

Conclusions

Grx2 deficiency markedly increases CD36 S-glutathionylation in BMSCs, which not only enhances fatty acid uptake, leading to the accumulation of toxic lipid metabolites and oxidative injury, but also disrupts mitochondrial energy metabolism by suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway. Conversely, the C272S mutation in CD36 or Grx2 overexpression breaks this detrimental cycle, alleviates BMSC senescence, and exerts bone-protective effects. Collectively, these findings reveal that Grx2 deficiency-mediated CD36 S-glutathionylation drives BMSC senescence and SOP, providing new insight into the redox regulatory mechanisms underlying skeletal aging (Fig. 10).
