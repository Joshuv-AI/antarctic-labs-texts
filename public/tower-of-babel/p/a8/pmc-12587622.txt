Prognostic evaluation and experimental validation of cuproptosis-related hub genes identified through weighted gene co-expression network analysis in uveal melanoma
Authors: Siying Chen, Nan Li, Boning Zeng, Yitong Guo, Guangqin Zhang, Chao Sun, Boqing Zhang, Qiuling Chen, Nan Li, Chaowei Zhu, Mingwei Li, Xiao Wang, Shaoxiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUveal melanoma (UVM) is the most common primary intraocular malignancy, poses a formidable challenge due to its high metastatic potential and grim prognosis, but limited treatment options for UVM have prompted studies on immunotherapies. Cuproptosis is a recently discovered form of regulated cell death that has emerged as a potential regulator of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and a modulator of cancer development. However, the association between cuproptosis and UVM remains unclear.MethodsWeighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to identify cuproptosis genes. Transcriptome correlation analysis and differential analysis were conducted following consensus clustering on 221 UVM samples. The objective was to identify cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs). Employing the LASSO Cox regression method, the identified CRGs were utilized to formulate a prognostic model termed CRG_score. Functional enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the biological processes linked to the CRG_score. Additionally, the research delved into the association between CRG_score and the responsiveness to chemotherapy drugs like docetaxel and etoposide. We also explored the effect of cuproptosis on UVM at the cellular level.ResultsUVM patients were classified into two distinct cuproptosis clusters, each exhibiting unique clinicopathological characteristics, prognostic outcomes, immune checkpoint expression levels, and immune infiltration patterns. CRGs were used to develop a prognostic model, CRG_score, with eight pivotal CRGs that accurately predicted the prognosis of UVM (AUC = 0.895). The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis further confirmed the high tumor cell specificity of these eight model genes, validating the rationale for constructing the CRG_score. Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that the CRG_score is associated with pathways involving cell signaling, immune responses, and metabolic regulation. A low CRG_score was associated with a favorable response to chemotherapy drugs such as docetaxel and etoposide. In cellular experiments, it was observed that the cuproptosis inducer elesclomol can impede the growth and proliferation of UVM cells, causing cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase, as well as prompting apoptosis and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS).ConclusionCompared to several previous studies, we have extended the search for CRGs very rigorously in order to increase the value of cuproptosis in the prognosis and treatment of UVM. This whole transcriptome analysis elucidates the crucial role of cuproptosis-related molecular subtypes in the prognosis of UVM, highlights its association with the TME, and provides a scientific basis for clinical drug decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03978-6.Keywords: Cuproptosis, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Uveal melanoma prognosis, Molecular subtypes, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Uveal melanoma (UVM) stands out as the predominant primary malignancy within the eye. It primarily originates from the choroid in adults, with an overall mortality rate of approximately 50% [1, 2]. Metastatic UVM remains challenging to manage (with limited efficacy and poor overall survival (OS)) despite advances in treatment, such as chemotherapy and surgical metastasectomy [3–5]. Drug resistance and tumor recurrence further contribute to the grim prognosis; this emphasizes the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches [6]. Immunotherapies have shown promise, although the response rates to immune checkpoint inhibitors remain modest. This underscores the need for a comprehensive understanding of UVM’s novel molecular characteristics and tumor-immune interactions [7].

Recently, a new form of cell death called cuproptosis has been identified that is triggered by alterations in copper levels and is implicated in tumorigenesis; it differs from apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis [8]. Copper is a vital trace element that plays an important role in several biological processes, including energy metabolism and the detoxification of ROS [9, 10]. Maintaining copper balance is critical to preserve cellular function and survival [11]. The role of copper and cuproptosis in cancer development (including cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis) has attracted increasing attention [12]. Notably, the preliminary analysis of the prognostic value of 10 genes associated with cuproptosis in the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database sheds light on the potential significance of cuproptosis in UVM [13]. However, further investigations are warranted to comprehensively elucidate the clinical implications of all CRGs and their correlation with the TME in UVM.

This study comprehensively investigated the expression patterns and clinical significance of CRGs in UVM (Fig. 1). A comprehensive and reliable set of CRGs, including 33 cuproptosis Genes and 1306 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), was identified through correlation and differential analysis across multiple UVM datasets at the whole transcriptome level. Subsequently, UVM patients were categorized into two specific molecular subtypes associated with cuproptosis based on these gene expressions, each exhibiting unique clinicopathological characteristics, prognostic outcomes, immune checkpoint expression levels, immune infiltration patterns, and drug sensitivity. Furthermore, a highly predictive and valid prognostic model was established. Cellular experiments confirmed the occurrence of cuproptosis in UVM cells and demonstrated its effects on viability, proliferative capacity, apoptosis, and cell cycle regulation in these cells.This study enhances our understanding of the clinical application of cuproptosis-related molecular subtypes and prognostic models, providing valuable information for the development of personalized therapeutic strategies for UVM.

Materials and methods

UVM datasets and preprocessing

The RNA sequencing, mutation expression, and clinical information of UVM were downloaded from the public databases Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) databases. Only datasets containing survival information were included in the analysis. In total, four available datasets were downloaded, including three datasets from the GEO database, GSE22138 (n = 63), GSE44295 (n = 57), and GSE84976 (n = 28), and one TCGA-UVM dataset (n = 80). Elimination of samples with correlations below 0.8 resulted in the inclusion of 221 of the 228 patients in this study (Figure S2 & Table S1). Using the R packages Affy and simpleaffy, the raw “CELL” files from these datasets were subjected to background adjustment and quantile normalization. Fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM) were converted into transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) using the "limma" package for the TCGA-UVM dataset. Gene expression of the four datasets were merged, and batch effects were eliminated by the R package "SVA". The copy number variation matrix was acquired from UCSC-Xena (http://xena.ucsc.edu/). Furthermore, we employed a TCGA-SKCM dataset retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases. For normal tissue samples, we sourced data from the GTEx data portal (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/datasets).

Obtaining and screening cuproptosis genes through weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Thirteen experimentally validated cuproptosis core genes (PDHB, ATP7B, LIAS, ATP7A, LIPT1, FDX1, DLAT, DLD, DBT, DLST, PDHA1, GCSH, and SLC31A1) were extracted from a previous study [8]. Additionally, a set of 169 MSigDB cuproptosis genes was sourced from Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) website (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) (Table S2). WGCNA was employed with a threshold of 0.3 to identify Hub Genes, exploring the relationship between the 169 MSigDB cuproptosis Genes and the 11 experimentally validated cuproptosis core genes (Since GCSH and SLC31A1 were not expressed in GSE22138, these two genes were not included in the subsequent analysis). Based on statistical conventions and empirical research consensus, the absolute value of the correlation coefficient can be classified into three categories: 0.0-0.3 represents weak correlation, 0.3-0.7 represents moderate correlation, and 0.7-1.0 represents strong correlation [14]. Therefore, we use 0.3 as the threshold to determine whether a correlation exists.

Separate WGCNA analyses were performed for each database and the intersection of the results was taken to minimize potential biases across the four databases used in this study. In total, the approach identified 22 MSigDB cuproptosis genes that exhibited consistent correlations with at least one cuproptosis core gene, and these genes were subsequently included in the subsequent analyses. Correlation heat maps were generated using the R packages of psych to visualize correlations among the cuproptosis genes.

Mutation atlas and copy number variation of cuproptosis genes

Any mutations occurring in cuproptosis genes were annotated and visualized by the perl script to provide insights into the mutation landscape. The frequency of copy number variations across the genome was plotted to identify any amplifications or deletions specifically affecting the cuproptosis genes. A genome cycle plot was generated to visualize the copy number variation (CNV) patterns in relation to the cuproptosis genes by the R package of "RCircos".

Unsupervised clustering for cuproptosis clusters and gene clusters

Using the "ConsensusClusterPlus" R package, we classified 221 patients with UVM into cuproptosis clusters A and B, determined by expression patterns of 33 CRGs. The clustering procedure encompassed a gradual and smooth increment in the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) curve, ensuring the avoidance of groups with limited sample size. Unsupervised clustering analysis was performed to classify patients into gene clusters A and B. This classification was established upon the expression patterns of DEGs identified by differential analysis of the cuproptosis clusters.

The clinical characteristics and prognosis of UVM

To investigate disparities in survival outcomes within the two cuproptosis clusters, survival analysis was conducted. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to identify variations among the two clusters. Kaplan-Meier analysis was applied to assess overall survival across different clusters. Associations between molecular subtypes and clinicopathological characteristics (age, gender, and TNM stage) were also examined to assess the clinical significance of the two clusters. Analysis was conducted using the R packages "limma" and "pheatmap", respectively.

Identification and functional annotation of DEGs linked to cuproptosis clusters

Using the "limma" package in R revealed a total of 1306 DEGs between cuproptosis clusters A and B, with a log fold change (log FC) of 0.585 and a significance level of p < 0.05. The volcano plot was generated using the "ggplot2" package to visually depict the upregulated and downregulated DEGs in the two cuproptosis clusters. To improve our understanding of potential functional categories linked to the cuproptosis cluster-associated DEGs. The R package "ClusterProfiler" was used to explore the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways associated with the DEGs. The "org.Hs.eg.db" R package was employed for ID transformation.

Construction and assessment of the prognostic cuproptosis-related score (CRG_score) model

A prognostic model was established and evaluated related to cuproptosis in UVM. We utilized univariate Cox analysis (p < 0.05) to identify prognostic DEGs associated with cuproptosis. An unsupervised cluster analysis was then performed to categorize patients into Gene cluster A and Gene cluster B based on the expression patterns of prognostic CRGs. Finally, LASSO-Cox regression was used to identify the most influential CRGs. The correlation between 33 cuproptosis Genes and the strongest CRGs was investigated using the WGCNA package. This was visualized by heatmap and a co-expression network. We identified 8 hub genes and their correlative coefficients to formulate the CRG_score:

Using the R package "survival", we classified 221 patients into low-risk and high-risk groups based on the median CRG score. Sankey plots visualizing the relationship between cuproptosis clusters, gene clusters, and CRG_score groups were created using R packages ggalluvial, ggplot2, and dplyr. Then, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and PCA were conducted for the two groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the area under the curve (AUC) and assess the predictive accuracy of the CRG_score model.

Functional enrichment analysis of cuproptosis clusters, gene clusters, and the CRG_score model

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was executed to pinpoint unique pathways and functions between the two patient clusters derived from the cuproptosis clusters and gene clusters. The c2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt collection in the MSigDB database was used for the gene sets used in this analysis.

In addition, GSEA (GO, KEGG and hallmark gene sets) was employed to explore enriched pathways and elucidate the underlying factors contributing to significant differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups in the CRG_score model.

Immune cell infiltration analysis of the two groups of patients

For the assessment of immune cell infiltration, we conducted single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) using the R package CIBERSORT. This allowed the estimation of the infiltration of 22 human immune cell types. The ESTIMATE algorithm was utilized to assess immune scores, stromal scores, and total ESTIMATE scores for each patient.

Furthermore, we investigated differences in the expression levels of key immune checkpoints, PD-1 and PD-L1, among patients belonging to the two Gene clusters. We also scrutinized differences in the expression of 37 immune checkpoint genes within the gene clusters. Box plots were generated to visualise these expression differences.

Validation of the prognostic cuproptosis-related model

The prognostic efficacy of the cuproptosis-related model was validated in independent testing cohorts (GSE22138, GSE44295, GSE84976, and TCGA datasets). CRG_scores were calculated for patients with UVM in each cohort, and samples were categorized into high- and low-risk groups according to the median CRG_score. To assess the prognostic significance of the model in each cohort, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed using the R packages "survival" and "survminer". A time-dependent ROC curve analysis was also conducted using the CRG scores. This analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the CRGs model across the test cohorts. This validation process ensures the reliability and general applicability of the prognosis model in predicting patient outcomes for UVM.

Single-cell sequencing data collection and processing

Single-cell transcriptomic data of uveal melanoma were obtained from the GEO database (GSE139829) [15]. Quality control filtering was performed using the R package Seurat, retaining cells with UMI counts > 400, Gene counts between 200 and 8000, and mitochondrial content < 10%. Doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder [16], resulting in a total of 53,362 high-quality cells. Following data normalization, the ScaleData() function was applied with the vars.to.regress option (S.Score and G2M.Score) to eliminate unwanted sources of variation. Batch effects were corrected using the harmony algorithm, and clustering was performed at a resolution of 0.5. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were conducted using UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection). Cell annotation was based on specific marker genes, identifying tumor cells (MLANA, MITF), T cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD8A), plasma cells (IGHG1, IGLV3-1, IGKC, CD79A), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), NK cells (FGFBP2), monocytes/macrophages (CD68), retinal pigment epithelium (RPE65), photoreceptor cells (RCVRN), fibroblasts (COL1A1, COL3A1), and endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF). All visualizations were generated using the R package scplotter.

Correlation clinical characteristics, mutation frequency, drug sensitivity, and CRG_score

Initially, correlation between CRG_score and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, and TNM stage, was assessed using chi-squared tests. Subsequently, the mutation annotation format (MAF) from the TCGA-UVM cohort was generated using the "maftools" R package. This was done to establish the mutation atlas. The semi-inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of commonly used antitumor drugs were obtained by package "pRRophetic" to further explore the correlation between drug sensitivity and CRG_score.

Differential analysis of the expression patterns of model genes between melanoma and normal samples

The differential analysis of RNA expression patterns encompassed 471 melanoma samples and 1306 normal skin samples. We used the R package "limma" to investigate the expression differences of eight model genes between melanoma and normal samples. The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) is a repository of immunohistochemistry (IHC) data representing protein expression patterns (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). The database was utilized to acquire protein expression immunohistochemistry (IHC) images of model genes in clinical samples from melanoma patients for the present study. We employed ImagePro Plus software (version 6.0) to quantify protein expression levels in immunohistochemistry images, and used Prism (version 9.5.1) software to create statistical histograms (t test, p < 0.001).

Cell culture

M619 cell lines were procured from BeiNa Culture Collection (Henan, China), and 92-1 cells were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). UVM cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. The Short Tandem Repeat (STR) cell authentication report was acquired from the supplier. All drug working solutions (DMSO% < 0.001) were prepared by diluting the DMSO solution of the drug with serum-free medium, and serum-free medium without the drug was used as a control for all cellular experiments.

Cell counting kit-8 assay

The viability of UVM cells was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Selleck Chemicals, Texas, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Elesclomol (MedChemexpress, Shanghai, China) in combination with Copper chloride dihydrate (CuCl2, Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) was employed as the inducer of cuproptosis [17]. To determine the IC50 of UVM cells, M619 and 92-1 cells were seeded onto a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/ml. Pre-mix CuCl2 and elesclomol in a 1:1 ratio (elesclomol-CuCl2). Following overnight culture, the cells adhered, and subsequent treatment involved exposure to various concentrations of elesclomol-CuCl2 for 24 h. Tetrathiomolybdate (TTM) (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) served as the inhibitor of cuproptosis [8]. In the experiment involving the combined use of elesclomol-CuCl2 and TTM, cells were initially treated overnight with or without 5 µM TTM, and elesclomol-CuCl2 was added to each well for 24 h. Pictures were taken under a fluorescence microcell imager (ZOE, BIO-RAD, USA), and cell viability was assessed utilizing the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. The optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm.

EdU assay

The proliferation of UVM cells was assessed by a 5-ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine (EdU, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) labeling assay. After treatment with elesclomol-CuCl2, the culture medium was removed, and EdU (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was introduced into each well and incubated for 2 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells were stained sequentially with Azide 594 for 30 min, Hoechst 33,342 for 10 min, and finally observed and photographed by a fluorescence microscope (Cytation 5, Bio-Tek, USA). The proliferation rate of cells was determined by calculating the proportion of EdU-positive nuclei (red) to the total number of blue fluorescent nuclei. Image J Pro plus 6.0 was used for image processing and cell counting.

Cell apoptosis assay

Cells were seeded on a 6-well plate at a density of 3.5 × 10^5 and subjected to treatment with elesclomol-CuCl2 for 24 h. Following trypsinization without EDTA, the cells were harvested, stained with Annexin V/FITC and propidium iodide (PI) using an Apoptosis assay kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, Jiangsu, China) for 10 min. After filtration, flow cytometry was executed using a FACSCalibur equipped with CellQuest Pro software.

Cell cycle assay

In the cell cycle assay, cells were treated with elesclomol-CuCl2 for 12 h before collection and fixation with 75% cold ethanol overnight. Subsequently, the cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI) utilizing a Cell Cycle assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Following filtration, flow cytometry was carried out using a FACSCalibur equipped with CellQuest Pro software.

Cellular ROS assay

Cellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) content was evaluated using the ROS assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, M619 cells were seeded on a 96-well plate and subjected to treatment with elesclomol-CuCl2 at concentrations of 0, 100, and 200 nM for 24 h. To observe ROS by fluorescence microscopy, cells in 96-well plates were labeled with a 10 µmol/L DCFH-DA probe for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence was then observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Cytation 5, Bio-Tek, USA) equipped with Gen5 3.09 software.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA extraction from cultured cells was carried out using a cell total RNA isolation kit (Foregene, Chengdu, China). The total RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) utilizing the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). QRT-PCR was subsequently conducted using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Selleck Chemicals, Texas, USA). Relative gene expression was calculated using the Eq. 2–ΔΔCT and the qRT-PCR results were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.1 (GraphPad Software, California, USA). GAPDH served as an internal loading control. All primers were procured from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and the primer sequences used are detailed in Supplementary Table S5.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data processing were performed using R software (version 4.1.2). For data preprocessing, the Affy and simpleaffy packages were used to process raw "CELL" files, while the limma package was employed to convert FPKM values to TPM and perform differential expression analysis. Batch effects were eliminated using the SVA package. Visualization of copy number variation (CNV) patterns was achieved with the RCircos package, and heatmaps were generated using the psych and pheatmap packages. Unsupervised clustering analysis was conducted using the ConsensusClusterPlus package. Functional annotation of DEGs was performed using the ClusterProfiler and org.Hs.eg.db packages. Survival analysis, including Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox regression, was carried out with the survival and survminer packages. Immune cell infiltration analysis was conducted using the CIBERSORT package, and mutation annotation was performed with the maftools package. Drug sensitivity analysis was conducted using the pRRophetic package. A significance level of p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant for all conducted tests and analyses.

Results

Co-expression networks and correlation analysis of cuproptosis genes in UVM

A total of 169 MSigDB cuproptosis genes were extracted from the GSEA website (Table S2 & 3). These Genes were subjected to correlation analysis with the 13 well-known cuproptosis genes previously identified in a study by using each independent UVM dataset [8]. Taking the intersection of the results, 40 related gene pairs were obtained (correlation coefficient greater than 0.3 and correlation consistently positive or negative), and the related gene pairs and their correlation in each database are shown in Table 1. From the table, we obtained 22 MSigDB cuproptosis Genes, and after combining 11 cuproptosis core genes (GCSH and SLC31A1 had no expression in GSE22138 and were not included in the subsequent analyses), there were a total of 33 genes, called cuproptosis genes (Fig. 2a). Heat maps were generated to visualize the correlation of cuproptosis genes in each dataset (Fig. 2b). GO and KEGG gene category analysis revealed their functional classifications related to copper-related processes (Fig. S1a). The researchers also investigated the somatic mutation rates, copy number variations (CNVs) and their respective chromosomal locations of 33 cuproptosis genes in the TCGA-UVM cohort (Fig. S1b-d). The analysis results suggested that the mutation rates of cuproptosis genes were very low, with only NFE2L2 showing a mutation rate of 1%. Copy number increases of cuproptosis genes appear to be more common than copy number decreases. Chromosomal location analysis indicated that these cuproptosis genes are not concentrated in specific chromosomal regions. These findings provided an overview of the genetic landscape of cuproptosis genes and implied that somatic mutations and copy number variations of cuproptosis genes in UVM may not be the primary pathogenic mechanisms, offering important clues for further study.

Characterization of cuproptosis clusters and the TME in UVM

In our initial approach, we employed a consensus clustering algorithm based on the expression profiles of 33 cuproptosis genes to classify UVM patients. This was undertaken to elucidate the expression characteristics and clinical significance of cuproptosis genes in UVM. The optimal partitioning of patients into cuproptosis cluster A (n = 87) and cuproptosis cluster B (n = 134) was identified (Fig. 3a & Fig. S3). PCA further affirmed the distinct separation of the two cuproptosis clusters (Fig. 3b). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that patients in cuproptosis cluster A exhibited significantly longer overall survival (OS) compared to those in cuproptosis cluster B (Fig. 3c). In addition, the integrated heatmap showed the expression of cuproptosis genes and various clinical features for the two cuproptosis clusters (Fig. 3d).

We further explored the distinctions in biological functions and the tumor immune microenvironment between these two cuproptosis clusters. The GSVA of pathway enrichment demonstrated that cuproptosis cluster A exhibited significant enrichment in metabolic pathways, such as the riboflavin metabolism pathway, propanoate metabolism pathway, histidine metabolism pathway and tyrosine metabolism pathway. In contrast, cuproptosis cluster B was enriched in stromal and immune activation pathways, such as the VEGF signaling pathway, ECM receptor interaction the RIG-I-like receptor pathway, the B cell receptor signaling pathway and so on (Fig. 3e). Conducting CIBERSORT analysis for immune cell infiltration, we observed that cluster B exhibited higher levels of infiltration for various immune cell subsets, including activated B cells, CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, gamma delta T cells, immature B cells, MDSC, mast cells, neutrophils, regulatory T cells, and type 1 T helper cells. Conversely, monocytes and type 2 T helper cells demonstrated higher infiltration levels in cluster A (Fig. 3f). To further evaluate the immune microenvironment, immune scores were calculated using the ESTIMATE package. Cluster B exhibited higher immune and stromal scores, suggesting the activation of the immune and stromal systems in these patients (Fig. 3g).

Identification of gene clusters based on DEGs

Expanding our characterization, we conducted a differential analysis of the two cuproptosis clusters across the transcriptome to acquire a comprehensive understanding of the role of cuproptosis in UVM. The differential gene expression analysis, conducted with the R package Limma, revealed 1306 DEGs associated with cuproptosis. These DEGs were visually depicted in the volcano plot (Fig. S3a). The R package of "WGCNA" was used to analyze the relationship between DEGs and cuproptosis Genes, and 54 DEGs with low cuproptosis correlation were eliminated. Subsequently, 1181 prognostic cuproptosis-related DEGs were identified by univariate Cox analysis using a significance threshold of p < 0.05 (Table S4). Gene category analysis of these DEGs highlighted significant classification of immune-related pathways. This indicated a strong association between cuproptosis, immunity, and the TME (Fig. S3b & c).

The DEGs underwent unsupervised clustering analysis, successfully categorizing patients into two distinct clusters: gene clusters A and B (Fig. S4). Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated that patients in gene cluster B had a worse OS, whereas patients in gene cluster A exhibited a more favorable OS (log-rank test, p < 0.001; Fig. S5a). PCA further affirmed substantial differences in the transcriptional profiles of CRGs between the two clusters (Fig. S5b). Moreover, gene cluster B predominantly consisted of patients from cuproptosis cluster B, while gene cluster A showed a greater proportion of patients with early TNM stage and a relatively younger age group compared to gene cluster B (Fig. S5c).

Characteristics of biological function, immune cell infiltration, and immune checkpoints in gene clusters

To explore biological differences between different gene clusters in UVM patients, we performed GSVA enrichment analysis (Fig. S5d). Gene cluster A showed significant enrichment in metabolic pathways, while gene cluster B demonstrated enrichment in immune-related pathways and ECM receptor interaction, suggesting activation of tumor stroma. Comprehensive analysis using ESTIMATE scores demonstrated that gene cluster B had higher TME scores, suggesting a more immunologically active and stromal-rich TME in comparison to gene cluster A (Fig. S5e). CIBERSORT analysis revealed notable differences in the infiltration levels of various immune cell subsets between the two clusters, with the majority of immune cells showing higher levels of infiltration in gene cluster B (Fig. S5f). Expression of immune checkpoints (PD-1 and PD-L1) was also higher in cluster B, providing insights into the observed better prognosis in patients belonging to gene cluster A (Fig. S5g-h).

Correlation of CRG_score with clinical characteristics, mutation frequency, and drug sensitivity

Initially, in the TCGA cohort, a multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted, which included CRG_score, gender, T stage, and overall stage. The forest plot showed that the CRG score emerged as the only independent prognostic factor (Fig. S9a). Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between low CRG_score and younger age, lower stages, and better survival outcomes (Fig. S9b-d). The "maftools" R package was utilized to assess the distribution differences of somatic mutations between the two CRG_score groups in the TCGA-UVM cohort. The top 10 mutated genes with significant differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups are shown in the waterfall plot (Fig. 8a-b). Notably, the low-risk patients exhibited a higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) for GNAQ, SF3B1, and EIF1AX, while the high-risk patients showed GNA11 and BAP1 mutations (Fig. 8c). These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, indicating the prevalence of activating mutations in GNAQ or GNA11 in UVM cases, and the association of inactivating mutations in the tumor suppressor gene BAP1 with poor prognosis [18–20]. Additionally, mutations in SF3B1 (involved in pre-messenger RNA splicing) are associated with better clinical outcomes, while EIF1AX mutations (encoding eukaryotic translation initiation factor 1 A) are mutually exclusive from BAP1 mutations and are linked to longer OS [21, 22]. These observations were consistent with the prognostic distinctions between the high-risk and low-risk groups.

Discussion

Uveal melanoma is the most common primary ocular malignancy. Existing therapies such as surgery and radiotherapy offer limited efficacy against advanced UVM, resulting in high mortality rates. Hence, there is a critical need to identify valuable prognostic biomarkers and molecular subtypes in UVM to predict patient outcomes and guide personalized treatment strategies. Cuproptosis is a novel copper-dependent regulated cell death that was first proposed by Tsvetkov et al. in 2022. Copper induces cell death by directly binding to lipoylated tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle components. Recent studies showing elevated copper levels in the tumor tissues of cancer patients compared to healthy individuals further support the potential link between copper and cancer biology [11, 26, 27]. Given the emerging connection between cuproptosis and cancer, researchers have been extensively exploring the prognostic value of CRGs in various tumors, including melanoma, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, rheumatoid arthritis, bladder cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, and breast cancer, among others [28–33]. However, the relationship between cuproptosis and UVM is still poorly understood.

This study utilized GSEA Gene sets to identify 169 MSigDB cuproptosis Genes. These Genes were integrated with 11 experimentally validated core cuproptosis genes [8] to enhance the robustness of our analysis; this resulted in a set of 33 cuproptosis Genes specifically relevant to UVM. Correlation analysis across independent datasets consistently showed high correlations between these 33 genes and the cuproptosis core genes. This indicated their strong association with cuproptosis in UVM. Functional enrichment analysis further confirmed the significance of these genes in copper-related processes. This solidifies their involvement in the regulation of cuproptosis in UVM. Despite some inconsistencies between cuproptosis-related gene selection with other studies [13, 31, 34], our comprehensive analysis involved multiple UVM-specific databases, ensuring a more robust gene selection process. This in-depth analysis enhances the reliability and applicability of these genes in UVM prognosis and other clinical applications.

We initially used 33 cuproptosis genes obtained from the literature and GSEA for consensus clustering during development of the prognostic model to identify two distinct "cuproptosis clusters" among the patients. Further analysis of the whole transcriptome data revealed 1306 DEGs between these clusters, leading to the identification of two "gene clusters" based on their expression profiles. COX analysis and LASSO regression on these Genes resulted in the development of a prognostic scoring model containing 8 CRGs (CRG_score model). The Sankey diagram demonstrated similar patient grouping and strong prognostic capabilities for the three classification methods (cuproptosis clusters, gene clusters, and CRG_score). The CRG_score exhibited excellent individual prognostic abilities and a close association with well-known cuproptosis genes. Importantly, the prognostic performance of this model was validated in four independent UVM databases. This further confirmed the reliability and applicability of the CRG_score. Our predictive model exhibits higher predictive power compared with the previous UVM cuproptosis study [13]. Integration of data from multiple independent databases enhanced the robustness and generalizability of the predictive model and provided a deeper understanding of cuproptosis in UVM.

We explored the relationship between TME, two cuproptosis clusters and two risk groups. We found that patients with the cuproptosis cluster B and the high-risk group, who both have a poor prognosis, share similar immune microenvironment characteristics. Cluster B showed higher infiltration levels of immune cells, including activated B cells, activated CD4 + T cells, activated CD8 + T cells, and gamma delta T cells compared to Cluster A. Additionally, immune- and stroma-related pathways were significantly enriched in cluster B, together with higher immune scores and stromal scores. Surprisingly, cluster B did not exhibit the expected survival advantage. Consistent findings were observed in the analysis of patients in gene cluster B and the high CRG_score group. Previous reports show that the TME can be classified into three immune patterns: immune-inflamed phenotype, immune-excluded phenotype, and immune-desert phenotype [35]. A high density of immune cells in the tumor parenchyma is seen in the immuno-inflammatory phenotype, leading to a higher probability of response to immunotherapy. Meanwhile, the immune-excluded phenotype is characterized by abundant immune cells surrounding the tumor parenchyma (but not penetrating the stroma). This resulted in a lower probability of response. The immune-desert phenotype is characterised by lack of T cells in the parenchyma and stroma; this makes it susceptible to immune tolerance or ignorance [36]. Through GSVA enrichment analysis, we first discovered significant activation of the VEGF signaling pathway in patients with cuproptosis cluster B and in high CRG_score group. Angiogenesis plays a critical role in sustaining continuous tumor expansion, and it is associated with increased invasiveness, higher vascular density, tumor metastasis, and tumor recurrence, resulting in poor prognosis [37, 38]. In addition, the immune checkpoints cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA4) and programmed cell death protein 1, both of which are inhibits T cell function and reduces their cytotoxicity against tumor cells [39], leading to immune escape, are highly expressed in high-risk group patients [40, 41]. Based on these points, it can be hypothesised that patients with cluster B and high-risk group have an immune-excluded phenotype. Effective penetration of immune cells into the tumor parenchyma is vital for successful eradication of cancer cells [42]. Activated immune cells are blocked by the surrounding stroma in the immune-excluded phenotype. This suppresses the anti-tumor function of immunotherapy. Therefore, the worse prognosis observed in Cluster B and high-risk group are unsurprising. For this phenotype, therapeutic strategies often require a combination of approaches to improve the TME, enhance immune responses, and breach the stromal barrier.

Additionally, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the UVM cohort (GSE139829). The results demonstrated that 8 model genes were predominantly expressed in tumor cells. This tumor-specific expression supports the CRG_score’s robustness by highlighting its specificity for tumor-intrinsic cuproptosis over TME signals, validating its prognostic utility in UVM.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as a prominent focus in oncology therapeutics and have been successfully used in various cancers. As a VEGF inhibitor, the addition of bevacizumab to conventional chemotherapy has produced significant clinical benefit in a variety of advanced cancers, including metastatic colorectal cancer, cervical cancer non-small-cell lung cancer and glioblastoma [43–46]. Therefore, a combination of anti-angiogenic drugs may be an effective treatment for patients with activation of the VEGF signalling pathway. In addition, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been developed to block the PD-1 and CTLA4 checkpoints with success in melanoma, lung and renal cell carcinoma [47–49]. Anti–CTLA-4 and anti–PDCD-1 antibodies have resulted in long-term disease control in a subgroup of patients with melanoma and may be able to provide an important reference for immunotherapy of UVM [50, 51]. The above results indicate that through the cupropotsis cluster and CRG_score, the characteristics of the immune microenvironment in different patients can be understood, thereby guiding the application of immunosuppressants and anti-angiogenic drugs. Finally, the drug sensitivity analysis revealed that patients with low CRG_scores may exhibit higher sensitivity to four broad-spectrum antineoplastic drugs: Doxorubicin, Docetaxel, Cytarabine and Etoposide. This implies that these drugs could be potentially more effective in treating UVM patients with lower CRG_scores. Hence, the CRG_score is a feasible predictor for chemotherapy response. Overall, the CRG_socre could help predict these effects and select a strategy for patients of UVM before treatment.

To further verify the role of cuproptosis in UVM, we conducted a series of experiments using UVM cells. First, we found the viability and proliferation of UVM cell lines were suppressed after the elesclomol-CuCl2 treatment, while apoptosis was significantly increased. Cell cycle assay and ROS detection assay were performed to verify the apoptosis inducing of elesclomol and stated the probable mechanism. These results were also consistent with former GSEA results. Finally, changes in the relative expression of the cuproptosis core genes FDX1, ATP7B and GCSH were detected by qRT-PCR after the elesclomol-CuCl2 treatment. FDX1 is a reductase known to reduce Cu2+ to its more toxic form Cu1+. ATP7B is a copper exporter. GCSH is related to the synthesis of GSH, and GSH can chelate with copper ions to inhibit cuprotosis (Fig. 11H) [8]. The results showed the relative expression of ATP7A and GCSH was decreased and the relative expression of FDX1 was increased after the elesclomol-CuCl2 treatment, which were consistent with the mechanism of cuproptosis. Furthermore, we found that the cuproptosis inhibitor TTM could reverse the viability inhibition induced by elesclomol-CuCl2. The confirmed inhibitory effect on UVM cells induced by cuproptosis highlights the central role of cuproptosis in the onset and development of UVM, also implies potential therapeutic value for UVM patients. Future experimental research avenues could further explore the correlation between metabolites and the expression of CRGs in key pathways. Additionally, integrating multi-omics analyses offers promise for enhancing our understanding of the intricate molecular mechanisms involved in the pathogenesis of UVM.

There are several key innovations in the study: First, our gene List was derived from published literature and includes Genes related to copper ions identified by GSEA. Integrated analysis of cuproptosis genes at the whole transcriptome level makes the gene List more comprehensive. Second, correlation analysis was utilized to identify Genes closely associated with the 11 well-known cuproptosis genes [8]. This strengthens the relationship between cuproptosis and the prognostic prediction model. Third, we collected the most comprehensive databases of UVM (including four datasets from TCGA and GEO) that enhances the reliability and accuracy of our prediction model. Our predictive model outperforms previous literature on UVM and cuproptosis [13], exhibits higher predictive power and provides greater utility for clinical prediction.

Conclusion

Our study explored the role of CRGs in UVM and revealed their close association with the TME. Two molecular subtypes were identified based on these genes, demonstrating distinct prognosis outcomes, clinical characteristics, TME cell infiltration levels, as well as responses to immunotherapy and chemotherapy among UVM patients. The developed prognostic model exhibited predictive performance and can be validated across various UVM databases. We also investigated the role of cuproptosis in UVM at the cellular level and confirmed that cuproptosis occurs in UVM cells. These findings highlight the clinical significance of cuproptosis genes and provide new insights to guide personalised strategies for UVM patients.
