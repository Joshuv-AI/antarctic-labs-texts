Refining Osteoarthritis Risk Prediction: Average Sagittal Abdominal Diameter Complements and Enhances Body Mass Index With Sex‐Specific Insights From National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey
Authors: Yuwei Zhang, Xiaoshuai Wang, Hu Zhu, Haowei Chen, Zhaohua Zhu, Liangbin Zhou, Michael Tim Yun Ong, Dongquan Shi, Xin Zhang, David J Hunter, Changhai Ding, Rocky S Tuan, Zhong Alan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA), the most prevalent joint disease and a leading cause of disability globally, has its disease burden inadequately captured by body mass index (BMI). As the sole quantified risk factor in current Global Burden of Disease estimates, BMI accounted for only 20% of OA burden. A critical limitation of BMI is its inability to distinguish fat distribution patterns, particularly abdominal adiposity, which is increasingly recognized as a key driver of metabolic and musculoskeletal pathologies. Herein, we hypothesize that anthropometric indicators reflecting central adiposity, such as average sagittal abdominal diameter (ASAD), may outperform BMI in predicting OA risk, especially when considering sex and age differences.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study analyzed 27,791 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey participants (1999–2023) with complete OA diagnosis, anthropometric, and metabolic data. Participants were stratified by sex and age (40‐year cutoff). Multivariable logistic regression, adjusted for confounders, estimated predictor‐OA associations via standardized odds ratios (sORs), and these associations were evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Data were split into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets, with DeLong's test comparing different predictors against BMI.ResultsIn the overall population, ASAD showed a stronger association with OA (sOR = 1.483) than BMI (sOR = 1.436), with comparable validation AUROC (ASAD: 0.857; BMI: 0.854). Sex‐stratified analysis revealed that BMI was the optimal predictor for males (sOR = 1.466; validation AUROC = 0.844), while ASAD outperformed BMI in females (sOR = 1.486 vs. 1.450; validation AUROC = 0.865 vs. 0.863). Further age stratification revealed that in males under 40, both BMI (sOR = 1.261; validation AUROC = 0.750) and ASAD (sOR = 1.194; validation AUROC = 0.889) were the strongest predictors, and that ASAD (sOR = 1.490; validation AUROC = 0.769) and BMI (sOR = 1.482; validation AUROC = 0.736) remained strong for males aged 40 and above. In age‐stratified analyses of females, ASAD showed the strongest consistent association with OA risk, both in participants under 40 (sOR = 1.472; validation AUROC = 0.801) and those aged 40 and above (sOR = 1.421; validation AUROC = 0.764).ConclusionsASAD emerges as a superior predictor for females and a competitive population‐level complement to BMI. BMI remains an optimal OA predictor for males. Within the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey framework, these findings underscore the necessity of integrating abdominal adiposity metrics, particularly ASAD, into OA risk assessment to improve sex‐specific prevention strategies.Keywords: anthropometric predictor, average sagittal abdominal diameter, body mass index, NHANES, osteoarthritis, sex differences

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent joint disorder, predominantly affecting the knees, hands, and hips. OA is characterized by chronic joint pain, stiffness, functional disability in mobility, and gradual erosion of joint cartilage. OA most frequently occurs in patients aged above 40 years, and its prevalence increases steeply with aging and obesity [1, 2]. As a major source of healthcare spending, OA was associated with more than US$ 80.0 billion (95% confidence interval [CI], US$ 72.2–86.1 billion) of medical investment and healthcare burden in the United States in 2016 [3]. Current management strategies for OA include exercise, physical therapy, the use of auxiliary devices (e.g., canes or splints), self‐management interventions (especially for pain and disability), and surgical treatments such as total joint arthroplasty [4, 5, 6].

Notably, modifiable risk factors for OA include high body mass index (BMI), ageing, high‐intensity exercise, and joint injury. Obesity, as another prevalent condition and serious public health issue worldwide, significantly increases the risk of developing OA [7]. Based on the latest report of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD), high BMI is currently the only risk factor for OA for which attributable burden is quantified. High BMI accounted for just 20% of the osteoarthritis burden during 1990–2020 [2], and this finding was confirmed in the latest update in 2021. It is worth mentioning that high BMI, defined as a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 for those aged above 20 years, is a risk factor incorporated in GBD, while the theoretical minimum risk exposure level is 20–25 kg/m2 for comparison. Due to its simplicity and developed relationship with obesity‐related comorbidities [8, 9, 10, 11] or mortality [12, 13], BMI has long been accepted as the proxy for obesity, quantifying obesity with one numerical value in a height‐dependent way [14]. Notwithstanding the ease of application, BMI has two nontrivial shortcomings. First, BMI cannot distinguish the distribution of fat (central or peripheral) within the individual's body; second, it cannot differentiate between the amounts of fat‐free mass and fat mass. Consequently, patients risky for obesity‐related comorbidities are occasionally misclassified [15].

In 1956, Vague identified a relatively large quantity of abdominal adipose tissue deposition as a predictor of obesity‐related comorbidities [16]. Following that, the risk of abnormal deposition of visceral adipose tissues is confirmed by other studies over time [17, 18]. A recent study found that centripetal obesity measures were the most robust independent predictors of mortality, irrespective of BMI classification, underscoring the importance of including fat distribution indicators in addition to BMI to achieve more accurate evaluation of obesity‐related mortality risk [19]. The development of imaging technologies has enabled us to quantify tissues in vivo. For example, the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database has involved total fat mass (TF, g) and percent body fat (TF [%]) given by dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry. NHANES also uses average sagittal abdominal diameter (ASAD) to establish population‐based reference ranges and to improve the assessment of health risks associated with adiposity. Moreover, several new predictors that take into consideration some aspects of body shape, waist size, and metabolic level have been introduced to address one or both of the aforementioned limitations of BMI. The selection of these anthropometric indicators was based on expert consensus recommendations for BMI alternatives, particularly those that better capture central adiposity and metabolic risks [20]. For example, waist circumference (WC) and hip circumference (HC) [21] as well as ratios involving these two parameters, including waist–height ratio (WHtR) [22, 23], weight‐adjusted‐waist index (WWI) [24], a body shape index (ABSI), waist–hip ratio (WHR), body roundness index (BRI) [15], lipid accumulation products (LAP), visceral adiposity index (VAI), and cardiometabolic index (CMI) [23, 25] have been suggested as potentially better assessment tools of body shape and adiposity/metabolic‐related risks. These assessment tools have been used as complementary indicators for each category of lean, overweight, or obese individuals, under the threshold of BMI [26]. Besides, indicators such as WHtR [27] and BRI [15] have emerged as visual tools for health status evaluations in obesity‐related diseases to address the limitations of BMI. However, WHtR, ABSI, WHR, VAI, or CMI have not been analyzed for their association with OA, nor has their predictive efficiency compared with BMI.

This study analyzed the aforementioned anthropometric/metabolic measures in OA risk assessments following the expert‐recommended framework for obesity assessment, and screened out the most useful anthropometric/metabolic risk factors in an attempt to fix the issue that has not been adequately addressed by BMI. We utilized data from the NHANES (1999–2023) for quantitative analysis of each predictor.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The data for the present cross‐sectional study were obtained from the NHANES (1999–2023) databases. As a comprehensive and nationally representative survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics, NHANES collects data about multiple health‐related topics through interviews, physical examinations, and lab tests. Since 1999, NHANES has annually examined approximately 5000 individuals from a nationally representative sample of the United States resident civilian noninstitutionalized population. Data are released in 2‐year cycle, with the exception of the 2017–2020 pre‐pandemic data, which covered a 3.2‐year cycle. To minimize selection bias, the survey uses a multistage, stratified sampling design. Each participant is randomly selected through a statistical process and, after weighting, represents about 65,000 similar individuals in the country. Importantly, all participants had signed written informed consent prior to participating in the project. The methods presented here include case definitions, data collection, and data analysis methods.

Case Definitions

Data related to OA is self‐reported during interviews, including the diagnosis and types of arthritis, as well as the specific age when the individual was informed to have arthritis. OA patients are included if the participants answered “yes” to the question of “ever been told that they had arthritis by a doctor/medical expert, and the related type was OA or degenerative arthritis.” The veracity of self‐reported OA was confirmed by several studies [28, 29].

Participants, Study Size, and Variables

Data of all adult participants (aged beyond 20 years) were included in the research, without missing any details of the focused covariates. Pregnant women were excluded. Ultimately, a total of 27,791 participants with complete information were included in this study. The specific individual inclusion and exclusion processes of the study are shown in Figure 1.

Baseline Variables

Demographic Information

Basic demographic information included age, gender (male and female), ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, Non‐Hispanic White, Non‐Hispanic Black, and other), ratio of family income to poverty, and education level (less than high school; high school or equivalent; college or above,) and pregnancy status at exam.

Basic Physical Condition

Participants were further categorized as lean/normal weight (BMI < 25 kg/m2), overweight (25 ≤ BMI < 30 kg/m2), and obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) according to WHO guideline [8]. Abdominal obesity was confirmed when WHtR ≥ 0.5 [30]. Diagnosis of diabetes was recorded when the fasting blood glucose level ≥ 7 mmol/L or glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% [31]. Other physical conditions were also recorded, including the diagnosis of high blood pressure, chronic kidney disease, coronary heart disease, living conditions, daily drinking level, smoking status, and taking vigorous/moderate‐intensity activity over past 30 days or not.

Biochemical Indicators in the Circulating Level

Biochemical indicators included fasting blood glucose level, glycohemoglobin (%), total cholesterol (TCHO), triglycerides, and high‐density lipoprotein (HDL)‐cholesterol.

Anthropometric/Metabolic Predictors

Anthropometric and metabolic predictors, including TF, TF (%), WC, HC, ASAD, BRI, WHtR, CMI, WWI, ABSI, LAP, VAI, and WHR [32], were analyzed, and their calculation methods requiring secondary computation are provided in Supporting Information S1: Table S1.

Management of Missing Values

Considering that the continuous raw variables in biochemistry analysis were accompanied by missing values, these missing values were converted into categorical variables, then set as dummy variable groups labeled “unclear.” Distribution of the missing data was further analyzed and the kept variables should be missing at random in two groups without causing selection bias.

Data Analysis

All demographic categorical data were reported as survey‐weighted percentage. R × C table weighted Chi‐squared test was used to compare the proportion between OA and non‐OA groups. The continuous data were presented as survey‐weighted mean ± standard deviation (SD). Subsequently, survey‐weighted linear regression was used to compare continuous data regarding normality, linearity, and collinearity. In this study, we performed Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) to evaluate the strength and direction of association between various anthropometric/metabolic predictors and OA risk. Since many anthropometric and metabolic variables (e.g., BMI) in population‐based datasets like NHANES were non‐normally distributed, we used Spearman's ρ as the non‐parametric measure to robustly assess monotonic relationships. Next, to further analyze the detailed relationship, the whole population was stratified by gender and age (taking 40 years as the threshold). This was because the prevalence of OA is much higher in women than in men in numerous epidemiological surveys, and OA most frequently occurs in patients aged above 40 years [1, 2]. Besides, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure that 40 years was a valid cut‐off age. In particular, to dig out the specific markers for OA onset in the young age group (below 40 years), we took the patients with both OA onset age and current age below 40 years as the reference. We also kept those aged and with an OA onset age of 40+ years for comparison in this cross‐sectional study.

Weighted multivariate logistic regression models were built to determine the association between anthropometric/metabolic predictors and the presence of OA. Since the odds ratio (OR) value of WHtR in relationship with OA by binary logistic regression (excluded the effects of multicollinearity, small sample size, and low incidence) was large, we also applied Firth's Logistic Regression Model in this context to address the potential separation issues through bias correction, providing more stable estimates. Model 1 was the crude one without adjustment; Model 2 was set after controlling age, gender, ethnicity, education level, ratio of family income to poverty, alcohol drinking level, smoking status, physical activity (moderate or vigorous), and the main comorbidities that may correlate with OA, including diabetes, high blood pressure, chronic kidney disease, coronary heart disease, and liver conditions. We further analyzed standardized odds ratios (sOR) to enable direct comparison of effect sizes across predictors. Continuous variables were standardized by Z‐score transformation prior to logistic regression modeling (Model 2), with sOR representing the odds change per one‐standard‐deviation increase in each predictor. Likelihood ratio test was used to compare coefficients (β)/OR between different predictors and BMI. Second, we utilized receiver operating characteristic curve (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, [AUROC]) with binary logistic regression to adjust for confounders (based on the above‐mentioned confounding factors in Model 2), in order to accurately measure the capacity of each anthropometric/metabolic predictor in identifying OA. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) for model development and a validation set (30%) for performance evaluation. Delong's test was used to compare the AUROC in each set, especially to determine if there were any statistically significant differences between AUROC value for BMI and those of other indices.

Statistical analyses were conducted by SPSS 22.0, R 4.2.1, and Python v3.11.3. p < 0.05 (two‐sided) was recognized as statistically significant.

Results

As shown in the flowchart and characteristics of the included population in Figure 1 and Supporting Information S1: Table S2, 27791 participants were included, with 3407 participants diagnosed with OA and 24,384 participants classified as non‐OA. The weighted average age of the OA population was (61.53 ± 13.46) years, while the average age of the control group was younger (42.91 ± 15.95) years. There were significant differences in the distribution of gender, ethnicity, ratio of family income to poverty, education level, BMI category, diagnosis of abdominal obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease and liver condition or not; as well as self‐reported alcohol drinking level, smoking status and taking vigorous activity over past 30 days or not. Overall, a higher distribution of female, obese status categorized by BMI threshold, and abdominal obesity categorized by WHtR threshold, frequent alcohol consumer, (previous and current) smoker, patients with high blood pressure, coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease, and liver condition occurred in OA group.

Additionally, we assessed the distribution of selected variables, and only variables that showed no significant differences in the distribution of determined values or were unclear/missing in OA and non‐OA groups were kept (Supporting Information S1: Table S3). Spearman correlation results for every two anthropometric/metabolic predictors with OA risk were displayed in Supporting Information S1: Table S4. Furthermore, we provided the weighted baseline biochemistry and body parameter levels in Supporting Information S1: Table S5, and found that higher levels mostly appeared in the OA group. However, further adjusted models were also essential given the confounding factors involved (as listed in Supporting Information S1: Table S2).

Subsequently, a series of logistic regression analyses were conducted to ascertain the most suitable anthropometric/metabolic predictors for different groups of people. As depicted in Supporting Information S1: Table S6, unstandardized measures revealed particularly strong associations, with WHtR‐defined abdominal obesity demonstrating the robust correlation with OA (OR = 1.851, 95% CI: 1.549–2.212). Notably, the majority of predictors showed statistically significant positive relationships with OA prevalence, with the exception of CMI, ABSI, and WHR which failed to reach significance in Model 2.

After standardization using Z‐scores, taking BMI (sOR = 1.436, 95% CI: 1.435–1.436) as a reference, other indexes, including ASAD (sOR = 1.483, 95% CI: 1.482–1.484); TF% (sOR = 1.451, 95% CI: 1.450–1.451); WC (sOR = 1.438, 95% CI: 1.437–1.438) in the rigorous adjusted model indicated significant stronger positive relationships with the presence of OA. Each standard deviation increase in ASAD corresponds to 48.3% greater adjusted odds of OA presence, representing the most robust effect size among all standardized predictors in our fully adjusted model.

Afterwards, a gender‐stratified regression model was applied (Table 1). The presence of abdominal obesity, overweight, and obesity contributed to the occurrence of OA significantly. BMI remained the significantly highest sOR (sOR = 1.466, 95% CI: 1.465–1.466) among all included anthropometric predictors for OA in males. Meanwhile, ASAD (sOR = 1.486, 95% CI: 1.485–1.486); and TF (sOR = 1.456, 95% CI: 1.456–1.457) were the most recommended anthropometric predictors for OA for females, showing significantly higher ORs compared to BMI according to the likelihood ratio test.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study provides the first large‐scale evidence integrating population‐based abdominal adiposity metrics, specifically ASAD, to address a critical limitation in osteoarthritis risk assessment identified by the GBD initiative [7, 33]. Critically, BMI fails to capture fat distribution patterns and ignores anthropometric heterogeneity, a demonstrated determinant of OA progression. Our NHANES analysis addressed this gap through standardized evaluation of novel predictors, establishing ASAD as a complementary abdominal adiposity marker that augments BMI‐based assessment.

The key advance lies in sex‐stratified prediction efficacy. While BMI remains optimal for males, ASAD emerges as the superior predictor for females. This divergence likely reflects fundamental sex‐specific pathophysiology: women exhibit higher visceral adiposity at equivalent BMI levels, measurable only through direct abdominal dimension quantification [34]. Consequently, our findings provide the physiological basis for precision risk stratification.

Our population‐wide analysis revealed a significant association between abdominal obesity and OA. Multiple anthropometric indices, including BRI, WHtR, TF, TF (%), ASAD, WWI, and WC, demonstrated positive correlations with OA prevalence. Following adjustment for potential confounders and Z‐score standardization, WC, TF, and ASAD showed significantly stronger associations with OA than BMI (in ascending order of sORs). In predictive performance analyses, ASAD and BMI yielded the highest adjusted AUROCs, indicating their superior diagnostic potential for OA risk assessment. These findings collectively suggest that ASAD and BMI may serve as complementary, clinically useful tools for population‐level OA risk evaluation.

As gender significantly affects the occurrence and prognosis of OA, we stratified the population based on gender to find the best predictors for each gender. BMI had a highest sOR value, indicating the strong relationship with OA in male subjects. AUROC results revealed that, BMI was a suitable predictor for OA in male subjects, without statistical difference from others. Taken together, BMI was still the most recommendable OA predictors in male subjects.

On the other hand, ASAD and TF had a significantly stronger relationship with OA than BMI in female subjects. Furthermore, ASAD, WHR, and BMI had the highest predictive value for OA based on AUROC. Thus, ASAD was the top recommendable predictors in female subjects.

To identify specific predictors of early‐onset OA in younger populations (age < 40 years), we conducted gender‐ and age‐stratified analyses. Our results revealed no significant associations between general obesity (including overweight/obesity) or abdominal obesity with OA among male subjects under 40 years, nor between abdominal obesity and OA in female subjects under 40 years. Notably, ASAD emerged as the sole indicator significantly associated with OA presence in males < 40 years based on non‐standardized OR values. Following Z‐score standardization, BMI demonstrated the highest sOR for this subgroup, though without statistically significant differences compared to other parameters. In predictive performance analyses, both ASAD and BMI showed the highest AUROC values. These findings suggest that for male subjects aged under 40 years, BMI and ASAD may represent the most clinically useful indicators for OA risk assessment.

In the male subjects aged ≥ 40 years, our analysis revealed significant associations between (abdominal) obesity and OA. These evaluated anthropometric indices, including BRI, WWI, WHtR, TF, TF (%), ASAD, and WC, which demonstrated significant positive correlations with OA prevalence. Standardized OR analysis further identified ASAD as exhibiting the most robust association with OA presence. While AUROC analysis indicated both BMI and ASAD showed good predictive performance, their predictive capacity did not differ significantly from other indices. Taken together, these findings suggest that ASAD and BMI may serve as optimal predictive markers for OA risk assessment in males aged ≥ 40 years.

For female subjects aged under 40 years, overweight and obesity were both risk factors for OA. ASAD, TF, WC, and WHtR had a stronger positive association with OA than BMI, while BRI and TF (%) were also correlated with OA. Moreover, by AUROC, ASAD had the highest predictive accuracy for OA, significantly distinguishing it from BMI. Therefore, when selecting a predictive tool for female subjects aged under 40 years, we recommend ASAD when assessing risk factors.

For female subjects aged ≥ 40 years, having abdominal obesity, overweight, and obesity were all risk factors for OA. ASAD had stronger positive association with OA than BMI. BRI, WHtR, TF, TF (%), WWI, WC, and LAP were also correlated with OA. Meanwhile, ASAD had the highest predictive accuracy for OA, significantly distinguishing it from BMI by AUROC. The aforementioned ASAD showed ideal predictive accuracy in assessing the risks for OA in female patients aged 40 years and older.

Collectively, our sex‐stratified analyses demonstrate that ASAD and BMI serve as complementary predictors for OA risk assessment across populations. For clinical implementation, we propose a precision framework: BMI remains the primary indicator for males of all ages, while ASAD should be prioritized for females, allowing the identification of hidden high‐risk females with moderate BMI and central obesity. On the one hand, elevated BMI indicates higher mechanical loading on weight‐bearing joints through increased gravitational force, possibly accelerating structural wear [35, 36]. On the other hand, elevated ASAD, as a validated proxy for visceral adipose tissue accumulation [37], may exacerbate metabolically driven joint degeneration. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT), with ASAD being a practical surrogate, can secrete pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‐6, TNF‐α) and adipokines (e.g., leptin, resistin, chemerin, lipocalin 2) that independently drive cartilage catabolism and synovial inflammation [38]. This metabolic pathway is particularly critical in females, who have greater VAT accumulation and greater inflammatory susceptibility at lower BMI thresholds [34, 39]. This sex‐specific divergence is possibly associated with gender‐specific predominant OA pathogenesis pathways [40]. Furthermore, stratified preventive interventions targeting VAT for females with elevated ASAD and reducing joint load for males can be implemented. Our findings support personalized OA therapy, highlighting the potential efficacy of anti‐inflammatory treatments for high‐ASAD females and mechanical load reduction for males. Notably, ASAD can be derived from routine anthropometric measurements with high cost‐effectiveness and does not require advanced imaging or laboratory equipment. This endows ASAD with excellent generalizability across diverse healthcare settings—from tertiary hospitals to community clinics and primary care facilities.

Neither metric distinguishes lean muscle mass from adiposity, potentially misclassifying muscular individuals. However, ASAD is capable of assessing the burden of central obesity by identifying individuals with metabolically active VAT, and hence higher risk of accelerated OA development and joint degeneration, even when their BMI is moderate [15]. This aligns with reported evidence that VAT, as a key target of ASAD assessment, drives pro‐inflammatory adipokine release and metabolic dysfunction, both of which are linked to OA pathogenesis [41]. The standardized measurement of ASAD in NHANES facilitates population‐level VAT stratification, offering insights beyond mechanical loading on joints [32]. Thus, ASAD complements BMI by partially unmasking metabolic contributors to OA pathology, particularly in females, where metabolic dysregulation often precedes overt mechanical stress in OA development [40].

While our study reveals meaningful findings on reliable predictors of OA from the latest update of NHANES, it has several limitations. First, as a cross‐sectional (not longitudinal) study, NHANES cannot establish causality or elucidate potential underlying mechanisms. For example, obesity‐driven fibrosis, a critical mediator of OA progression, cannot be fully characterized in cross‐sectional designs, as fibrotic processes evolve over time [41]. Additionally, the metabolic‐mechanical interaction mechanism and sex‐specific OA pathophysiological pathways (reflected by ASAD's superior performance in females) require further molecular exploration. Second, while NHANES provide useful parameters, including BMI, hyperlipidemia, and other ones not captured in the GBD database, to measure obesity‐ and metabolism‐associated OA risks, its data are limited to the United States population. This may restrict generalizability, as obesity‐related OA pathophysiology, such as fibrotic patterns or adipokine profiles, could vary across ethnicities or regions [42]. Notably, NHANES is a representative multi‐ethnic US population, which mitigates this concern to some extent. Similar databases from other nations would be very helpful in future studies. Moreover, the present indicators were selected for inclusion in this study because they were available in the NHANES dataset. However, it should be noted that there are other anthropometric and metabolic measures that could also provide valuable insights into OA risk and progression. These additional indicators, such as adipokine level [43], thigh muscle cross‐sectional area [44], and visceral adipose tissue cross‐sectional area [45], have been shown to be significant in other studies and may offer useful complementary information. Our current indicators are routine physical examination parameters, ensuring high generalizability of the OA risk prediction model. Future research should consider incorporating these additional measures based on preclinical assessment and clinical longitudinal cohort studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of potential risk factors of OA.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this large‐scale NHANES analysis establishes ASAD as a clinically valuable anthropometric complement to BMI for OA risk prediction, with distinct sex‐ and age‐specific advantages. While BMI remains the optimal overall predictor for males (sOR = 1.466, AUROC = 0.844), ASAD outperforms BMI in females, exhibiting both a stronger association (sOR = 1.486 vs. 1.450) and higher discriminative accuracy (AUROC = 0.865 vs. 0.863). Age‐stratified analyses further reveal that in males under 40, BMI and ASAD exhibit the highest predictive accuracy for OA. In males aged 40 and above, ASAD and BMI maintain the best predictive performance. In females under 40, ASAD emerges as the optimal predictor, with significantly higher discriminative accuracy than BMI. In females aged 40 and above, ASAD again demonstrates the strongest association (by sOR) and highest predictive accuracy, with AUROC being significantly superior to BMI. These findings advocate for integrating ASAD into OA risk assessment frameworks, particularly for female populations, to address limitations of BMI‐centric models. Implementing this approach could enhance precision in sex‐ and age‐targeted preventive interventions.
