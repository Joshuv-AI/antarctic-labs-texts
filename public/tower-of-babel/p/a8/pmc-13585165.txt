The Role of mTOR Inhibitors in CMV Prevention: An Overlooked Aspect of the Fourth International CMV Consensus Guidelines
Authors: Björn Nashan
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the Fourth International Consensus Guidelines on the Management of Cytomegalovirus in Solid Organ Transplantation by Kotton et al.1 The document provides a comprehensive and timely update on CMV diagnostics, prevention, treatment, and immune monitoring in solid organ transplantation and will undoubtedly serve as an important reference for transplant clinicians worldwide. However, we were surprised by the limited discussion of immunosuppressive regimen selection as a strategy to reduce CMV infection and disease.

In particular, the ATHENA trial,2 a prospective randomized multicenter study comparing everolimus-based and mycophenolate-based immunosuppression in de novo kidney transplant recipients, is not discussed. Importantly, the incidence and severity of viral infections, including CMV disease and BK virus infection, were predefined secondary endpoints of the study. The ATHENA investigators demonstrated significantly lower rates of CMV infection in patients receiving everolimus-based regimens compared with mycophenolate/tacrolimus therapy.

These clinical findings were subsequently supported by mechanistic data from the ATHENA immunology3 substudy, which showed that everolimus-treated recipients maintained superior CMV-specific T-cell functionality, with lower expression of exhaustion markers such as PD-1 and CTLA-4 and increased frequencies of multifunctional CMV-specific CD4 T cells. Notably, while 25.6% of patients receiving mycophenolate/tacrolimus developed CMV infection, no CMV infection occurred in the everolimus-treated cohorts included in the substudy.

The omission is particularly relevant because the antiviral properties of mTOR inhibitors are supported by a substantial body of evidence extending well beyond ATHENA. More than a decade ago, Nashan et al. reviewed randomized clinical trial data demonstrating reduced CMV incidence and severity in recipients receiving mTOR inhibitor-based immunosuppression.4 The authors proposed that mTOR inhibition may represent 1 of the few immunosuppressive strategies that simultaneously preserves allograft outcomes while reducing CMV-related complications.

Mechanistic studies further support a direct antiviral effect. Human cytomegalovirus relies on activation of the mTOR signaling pathway to maintain viral protein synthesis and replication, and inhibition of mTOR interferes with critical stages of the viral life cycle. Experimental studies have demonstrated that CMV actively manipulates mTOR signaling and that pharmacologic inhibition of mTOR can impair viral replication.4

More recently, Krüger et al.5 demonstrated distinct effects of commonly used immunosuppressants on CMV-specific immunity. In contrast to mycophenolic acid and tacrolimus-containing combinations, mTOR inhibitors showed a more favorable profile with respect to preservation of antiviral T-cell responses, providing additional biological plausibility for the reduced CMV rates observed in clinical studies.

Taken together, evidence from randomized clinical trials, translational immunology studies, and experimental virology consistently suggests that mTOR inhibitor-based regimens exert clinically relevant antiviral effects against CMV. While antiviral prophylaxis and preemptive monitoring remain the cornerstones of CMV prevention, immunosuppressive regimen selection may represent an underappreciated complementary strategy for reducing CMV burden after transplantation.

Future updates of the international CMV guidelines may therefore benefit from a more detailed evaluation of the available evidence regarding mTOR inhibitor-based immunosuppression and its role in CMV prevention and immune control.

Sincerely,

Björn Nashan
