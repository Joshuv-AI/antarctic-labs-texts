Role of starvation survival response mechanisms on ribosome integrity, antibiotic tolerances, and virulence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms
Authors: Kerry S. Williamson, Michael J. Franklin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial biofilms contain physiologically diverse subpopulations of cells, including cells that are nutrient stressed or dormant. We determined how two dormancy pathways, ribosome hibernation and the stringent response, contribute to the survival and antibiotic tolerance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms. Analyses of whole biofilms and single cells showed that these pathways have differing effects on biofilm cell physiology. Ribosome hibernation, mediated by hibernation promoting factor (HPF), is essential for optimal survival and resuscitation of starved biofilm cells. In the absence of HPF, starved cells progressively lose ribosome integrity. However, loss of HPF does not increase the sensitivity of P. aeruginosa biofilm cells to ciprofloxacin or tobramycin. In contrast, the stringent response, mediated by RelA and SpoT, is not required for viability or ribosome integrity in starved biofilm cells, but does affect biofilm antibiotic tolerance. In a plant model of biofilm infection, disruption of either ribosome hibernation or the stringent response reduced bacterial virulence. The results show that ribosome hibernation preserves ribosomal integrity necessary for recovery from starvation and for pathogenesis, while the stringent response is required for growth arrest, antibiotic tolerance, and pathogenesis. These two ribosome-mediated pathways play distinct yet complementary roles in regulating dormancy and persistence of P. aeruginosa biofilms.

Introduction

Biofilms (surface-associated microbial communities) pose significant threats to human health, contributing to the antibiotic tolerance of bacterial infections1–3. When biofilms form on indwelling medical devices, such as artificial heart valves, catheters, and ventilators4, or directly on host tissues, including lung tissues5 and diabetic ulcers6,7, they are often extremely difficult or impossible to eradicate with antibiotics1,8. Antibiotic tolerance in biofilms is due, in part, to the presence of physiologically heterogeneous subpopulations of cells with differing antibiotic sensitivities9,10. Cells located in nutrient or oxygen limited zones enter a dormant state in which they maintain viability during starvation and tolerate antibiotic treatment, as most antibiotics target active metabolic functions9,11.

Molecular mechanisms have evolved that allow bacteria to sense starvation, enter dormancy, and remain viable while dormant. The classic example of a protective survival strategy is spore formation, which occurs in certain types of bacteria10,12,13. However, non-spore-forming bacteria also change their physiology in response to stress and starvation14–18. Starvation induced physiological changes include reduction in cell size, increase of the membrane surface to cell volume ratio, and maintenance of membrane electrochemical potential. Other physiological changes that occur during starvation include reduced initiation of DNA synthesis, completion of rounds of DNA synthesis, and compaction of DNA into a stable form19. Certain bacteria also produce storage compounds as they sense starvation, such as polyphosphate granules, which may function as phosphate reserves or as signaling molecules for DNA compaction19. Starved cells conserve energy through reduction of energy expensive biosynthetic activities, including translation17,20,21, and by degradation of macromolecules, including ribosomes, for use as energy and nutrient sources22. Therefore, ribosomes play an important role in the ability of bacteria to sense and respond to starvation. Two conserved ribosome associated pathways that enable bacteria to sense starvation and enter dormancy are ribosome hibernation23–25 and the stringent response26.

Ribosome hibernation involves the inactivation and preservation of an essential number of ribosomes that are required for translation when resuscitation ensues. This survival strategy is widespread among Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria27–30 and also occurs in certain eukaryotes31,32. During bacterial ribosome hibernation, ribosome modulation factor (RMF), hibernation promoting factor (HPF), or the HPF paralog YfiA (also termed RaiA) bind ribosomes, resulting in conformational changes that cause the formation of inactive 70S ribosome monomers or 100S ribosome dimers33–35. These inactive complexes protect the ribosomes from the initial steps of degradation by blocking access to the endonuclease cleavage sites on the 23S and 16S rRNA36–38. Ribosome hibernation was first described for the Ɣ - proteobacterium, Escherichia coli29,35,39, but has since been discovered to be widely conserved among bacteria. In E. coli, RMF and HPF bind the mRNA channel of ribosomes, forming hibernating 100S ribosome dimers27,33. YfiA competes with HPF for binding sites and results in formation of 70S ribosome monomers33. In E. coli, RMF stops translation by blocking the mRNA channel near the small subunit ribosomal protein, S123,40. Loss of RMF in E. coli increases sensitivity to gentamicin41 and decreases survival of the cells during stationary phase41, while overexpression of rmf reduces growth42. In contrast, in P. aeruginosa, loss of or overexpression of RMF does not result in any of these phenotypes. P. aeruginosa requires HPF to prevent degradation of rRNA43 and rProteins44 during starvation. Without HPF, ribosomes drop below a critical number required for resuscitation from starvation43,45. HPF is the key component for ribosome hibernation in Gram-positive bacteria and mycobacteria46–52, which do not encode RMF. Instead, these bacteria contain an HPF with a long C-terminal tail, HPFlong, that binds the mRNA channel and inactivates ribosomes by forming dimers at the C-terminal domain, resulting in inactive 100S ribosome dimers in a 50S:30S:30S:50S arrangement27,28. HPFlong and ribosome hibernation contribute to drug tolerance and bacterial persistence in these organisms41,53. How HPF affects antibiotic tolerance of P. aeruginosa, in planktonic cultures or in biofilms, has not been characterized previously.

The stringent response is another bacterial starvation signaling pathway that involves ribosomes26,54. During the stringent response, nutrient limitation is detected as insufficient levels of amino acid-charged tRNAs, which results in ribosome stalling. Ribosome stalling induces the activities of the nucleotide kinase/hydrolases RelA and SpoT to produce and degrade the alarmone signaling compounds guanosine 5′-diphosphate 3′-diphosphate (ppGpp) and triphosphate ((p)ppGpp). These alarmones signal starvation responses at both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels. At the transcriptional level, (p)ppGpp binds the RNA polymerase-binding protein DksA54. Along with the alternative sigma factor RpoS, ppGpp/DksA results in changes in gene expression away from general housekeeping functions toward expression of genes required for stress and starvation responses. In P. aeruginosa, the stringent response affects expression of greater than 30 percent of genes, including genes involved in pathogenesis, such as proteases and global regulators of stress responses55. Due to its role in the regulation of virulence factors, the stringent response contributes to bacterial pathogenesis56 and has been shown to affect virulence in a Drosophila melanogaster feeding model of infection and a rat lung model of infection57. At the post-transcriptional level, (p)ppGpp binds directly to key proteins, including some required for cell growth, affecting their activity26. The stringent response is activated to different extents depending on the level of stress, inducing expression of biofilm-related genes, leading to increased antibiotic tolerance during biofilm formation58,59.

P. aeruginosa forms antibiotic-tolerant biofilms60 and in planktonic culture, can survive for extended periods of time during starvation43,61,62. We previously identified mRNA for the ribosome hibernation factor, HPF, in oxygen-limited regions of P. aeruginosa biofilms6,11 and demonstrated that both HPF and the stringent response are required for survival of P. aeruginosa planktonic cells during starvation43,44. In this study, we investigated the roles of these two starvation-signaling pathways (ribosome hibernation and the stringent response) on the physiology of P. aeruginosa cultured in biofilms and subjected to starvation stress and to antibiotic exposure. Because biofilms contain heterogeneous subpopulations of cells, we determined the effect of these starvation signaling pathways on whole biofilm populations and on single biofilm cells isolated from the populations. We also determined the effects of these dormancy signaling pathways on P. aeruginosa virulence using a plant model of biofilm infection63.

Overall, the results show that ribosome hibernation, mediated by HPF, is required for optimal ribosome integrity during P. aeruginosa starvation and for regrowth of starved P. aeruginosa biofilms cells. Loss of HPF does not increase antibiotic sensitivity of starved P. aeruginosa biofilms to ciprofloxacin or tobramycin. In contrast, the stringent response mutant cells have reduced biofilm formation, but unlike when cultured planktonically, are able to survive extended starvation when in biofilms. The stringent response is not required for ribosome preservation in starved P. aeruginosa biofilm cells. In fact, the stringent response mutant cells have increased ribosome content per cell compared to wild-type cells, even when biofilms are starved. The results indicate that HPF is required for optimal survival of P. aeruginosa in nutrient deprived biofilms, while biofilms of cells lacking the stringent response are unable to enter growth arrest during starvation and are hyper-sensitive to antibiotics. Both ribosome hibernation and the stringent response contribute to P. aeruginosa virulence when growing in biofilms, as demonstrated using a plant model of infection.

Results

Ribosome hibernation factors hpf and rmf are differentially expressed within P. aeruginosa biofilms

Previous work showed that mRNA for the hpf gene is abundant throughout P. aeruginosa biofilms, including in the antibiotic-tolerant cells deep within colony biofilms, while rmf mRNA is primarily found in cells near the biofilm surface, where the bacteria are active but in transition to stationary phase11. To further assay hpf and rmf expression during biofilm growth and starvation, we imaged P. aeruginosa biofilms containing hpf or rmf fused to the yellow fluorescent protein (yfp) (Phpf-hpf-yfp and Prmf-rmf-yfp). For these studies, P. aeruginosa (Phpf-hpf-yfp) and (Prmf-rmf-yfp) strains were cultured as colony biofilms on rich medium for two days and then starved by daily transfers to phosphate buffered saline agar (PBSA) for up to eight additional days. Colony biofilms were selected as the model system because they contain heterogeneous cell populations and hypoxic zones in which cells have entered dormancy6,11,64–67. P. aeruginosa biofilms were cryo-embedded, thin-sectioned to obtain vertical transects, and imaged for YFP fluorescence by epifluorescence microscopy.

After two days of growth, the P. aeruginosa::Phpf-hpf-yfp strain exhibited YFP fluorescence throughout the biofilm, with the highest intensity at the top of the biofilm, near the air/biofilm interface (Fig. 1A). P. aeruginosa::Prmf-rmf-yfp also showed the greatest fluorescence at the top of the biofilm, with reduced signal in the middle and bottom regions (Fig. 1B). Over the course of starvation, overall fluorescence decreased in both reporter strains throughout the biofilms (Fig. 1C–F). To quantify changes in HPF-YFP and RMF-YFP fluorescence during starvation, biofilms were disrupted and dispersed cells were assayed for fluorescence. The Phpf-hpf-yfp strain had a gradual but significant reduction in overall fluorescence over time (p < 0.0001; Fig. S1A). In contrast, the Prmf-rmf-yfp strain showed a rapid loss of fluorescence by 2 days of starvation, followed by a more gradual decline over the next 6 days (p < 0.0001; Fig. S1A), indicating that RMF-YFP is less stably maintained than HPF-YFP during starvation. All strains had similar amounts of cellular biomass, as measured as OD600, throughout the starvation period (p > 0.38), indicating that the reporter constructs did not alter biofilm architecture. The biomass of all strains decreased significantly over time (p < 0.0001, Fig. S1B), reflecting the transition to a slower growth state. These findings are consistent with previous laser microdissection transcriptomic results that showed high levels of hpf mRNA both at the top and at the bottom of biofilms, and high levels of rmf mRNA primarily near the air/biofilm interface11. For these experiments, biofilms were disrupted, and 10 μg of total cellular protein was loaded onto each lane of the gel. The immunoblots show that ribosomal protein S13 (Figs. 2A, S2A) and HPF (Figs. 2B, S2B) are both maintained in biofilms over the course of starvation. Ribosome hibernation factor, HPF, is required for resuscitation of nutrient-limited P. aeruginosa biofilm cells

When cultured planktonically, P. aeruginosa can survive for extended periods under nutrient-limiting conditions43,61,62. Part of the P. aeruginosa starvation survival strategy is ribosome hibernation, in which HPF interacts with ribosomes and protects them from degradation27,40,43,53,68,69. In planktonic cultures, HPF, but not RMF, is required for optimal resuscitation of P. aeruginosa cells from dormancy43. To determine whether HPF and RMF are required for optimal resuscitation of P. aeruginosa biofilm cells from starvation, we cultured wild-type P. aeruginosa PAO1 and the ribosome hibernation mutant strains (∆rmf and ∆hpf) as colony biofilms for two days, then exposed the biofilms to carbon and nitrogen limitation by daily transfers to PBSA for up to eight days. Using this biofilm model, wild-type PAO1 and the ∆rmf and ∆hpf mutant strains formed thick biofilms following two days of growth, averaging 7.4 × 109 CFU per biofilm, with no significant differences in recoverable CFUs among the strains (p > 0.99; Fig. 3). Over eight days of starvation, P. aeruginosa PAO1 maintained viability in biofilms with no significant loss of CFUs (p > 0.93; Fig. 3). Similarly, the ∆rmf strain showed no reduction in viability during starvation (p > 0.68). In contrast, the ∆hpf strain was impaired in its ability to resuscitate following starvation, losing recoverable cells by 6 days of starvation (p = 0.04). This rate of CFU loss occurred more slowly than that of ∆hpf planktonic cells, which lose a significant number of recoverable cells by 3 days of starvation43. Complementation of the ∆hpf strain with a plasmid copy of hpf (∆hpf + hpf) restored biofilm cell survival over 8 days of nutrient limitation (p > 0.81). Starvation resulted in reduced total biomass for all strains, as measured by resuspending biofilms in PBS, homogenizing them, and assaying optical densities (OD600). Significant decreases in OD600 were observed on days 4 and 8 of starvation for all strains (p < 0.0001), regardless of their ability to form CFUs (Fig. S3), consistent with a transition to a slower growth state.Fig. 3Recovery of P. aeruginosa biofilm cells following extended incubation without nutrients at 37 °C.No loss of viability occurred in the PAO1, PAO1∆rmf, or PAO1∆relA∆spoT strains over 8 days of starvation (p > 0.69). Because the P. aeruginosa ∆rmf strain did not exhibit a survival defect during starvation, we tested other putative phenotypes associated with deletion or overexpression of rmf. In E. coli, deletion of rmf has been reported to delay entry into exponential phase, increase growth rate70, and reduce cell viability during stationary phase39. However, none of these phenotypes were observed in P. aeruginosa (Fig. S4A, B). In E. coli, overexpression of rmf has been reported to inhibit growth39,42. In contrast, overexpression of rmf in P. aeruginosa did not impair growth (Fig. S4C, D). Additionally, an E. coli ∆rmf mutant exhibited increased sensitivity to gentamicin41, but the P. aeruginosa ∆rmf strain displayed no difference in gentamicin MIC (Fig. S4E) and did not have increased susceptibility at two gentamicin concentrations over two days of exposure (Fig. S4F, G). Consistent with these results, disk diffusion assays revealed no difference in gentamicin sensitivity between the P. aeruginosa ∆rmf and wild-type strains, nor were there differences observed in responses to eight additional antibiotics (Fig. S4E, H).

Overall, the results indicate that P. aeruginosa biofilm cells require the ribosome hibernation promoting factor HPF for optimal resuscitation from starvation, but do not require RMF. Total cell biomass is similar for the ∆hpf and the wild-type strains, even though the ∆hpf strain is impaired in its ability to form CFUs following starvation, indicating that non-recoverable cells, either non-viable or in a viable but non-culturable state, are maintained in these starved biofilms.

The stringent response affects P. aeruginosa biofilm formation but is not required for resuscitation of biofilm cells from starvation

Previously, Nguyen et al.58 demonstrated that the stringent response is an active starvation mechanism that is important in P. aeruginosa biofilm formation. Here, we further determined the role of the stringent response on P. aeruginosa survival during starvation under biofilm conditions. Consistent with previous findings58, the P. aeruginosa ∆relA∆spoT strain had an impaired ability to form biofilms compared to the wild-type strain (Fig. 3). Although both strains were inoculated with similar CFUs, ∆relA∆spoT biofilms contained approximately six-fold fewer viable cells than wild-type biofilms after two days of growth (p = 0.0084). Despite this impairment in biofilm formation, the ∆relA∆spoT strain had no loss of viability over 8 d of starvation in biofilms (Fig. 3; p > 0.99). This is in contrast to planktonically cultured ∆relA∆spoT cells, which had a 1.3 log reduction in viability after 4 days of starvation43.

Ribosome hibernation and the stringent response affect physiological heterogeneity in the regrowth kinetics of single starved biofilm cells

The results above show the average response of whole biofilm populations during recovery from starvation. However, biofilm cells are physiologically heterogeneous, with individual cells responding differently depending on their local microenvironment71,72. To quantify the heterogeneity in physiological responses of biofilm cells, we characterized the regrowth kinetics of starved biofilms, starting from single cells. Biofilms were cultured for two days and then starved for up to eight days, as above. The biofilms were dispersed by homogenization, then serially diluted so that single cells could be inoculated into the wells of microtiter plates containing the regrowth medium TSB. To ensure that the microtiter plate wells received no more than one cell, cell suspensions were diluted so that there was approximately one viable cell per ten wells. To confirm that biofilm cells were not clumped, dispersed samples were examined microscopically, revealing no cell aggregates for any strains following biofilm growth or starvation (examples shown in Fig. S5).

The regrowth kinetics of single biofilm cells were determined using an OmniLog plate reader, which can generate growth curves from each well of a 96-well plate and can accommodate up to 48 plates simultaneously. Using six independent biological replicates, we analyzed growth starting from 421 individual P. aeruginosa PAO1 cells over the course of starvation (e.g., Day 0–129 cells, Day 4–137 cells, Day 8–155 cells), 536 ∆hpf cells, 419 ∆hpf cells complemented with hpf, and 596 ∆relA∆spoT cells. Lag time was defined as the time required to reach an OmniLog signal value of 50 units, which is indicative of early exponential phase growth and clearly distinguishable from background levels. The standard deviations of the lag times were used as a quantitative measure of physiological heterogeneity among single cells.

For wild-type P. aeruginosa PAO1 biofilm cells prior to starvation, the average regrowth time was 16.3 h with relatively uniform regrowth times (SD = 1.2 h), although there were outliers (Fig. 4). After 4 d of starvation, neither the mean lag time (16.0 h; p = 0.43) nor the standard deviation (SD = 1.1 h; p = 0.22) changed significantly from the initial pre-starvation timepoint. Following 8 days of starvation, a small (0.6 h; p = 0.01) increase in the time to reach early exponential phase was observed, along with increased heterogeneity (SD = 1.7 h, p < 0.0001). The resuscitation of individual P. aeruginosa ∆hpf biofilm cells differed significantly from wild-type cells at every timepoint (p < 0.0001; Fig. 4). Prior to starvation, single ∆hpf biofilm cells required an average of 18.4 h to reach early exponential phase. Following 4 and 8 days of starvation, the times increased significantly, averaging 18.9 h (p = 0.01) and 19.7 h (p < 0.0001), respectively. The P. aeruginosa ∆hpf cells also had greater heterogeneity in the time required for resuscitation, particularly following 4 d of starvation (SD = 2.1 h, p < 0.0001). Complementation of P. aeruginosa ∆hpf with hpf on a plasmid partially restored the wild-type phenotype, with no significant increase in regrowth times following starvation relative to day 0 (p = 0.66; Fig. 4). All strains had similar growth kinetics when cultured planktonically (Fig. S6A), indicating that the differences between strains were not due to variations in growth rates. The results indicate that following starvation in biofilms, fewer ∆hpf cells are capable of resuscitation (Fig. 2), either due to a loss of viability or entry into a viable but nonculturable state. The ∆hpf cells that do resuscitate following starvation have increased lag times and greater heterogeneity in resuscitation kinetics (Fig. 4).

Regrowth kinetics of individual ∆relA∆spoT biofilm cells were similar to those of the wild-type strain. Prior to starvation, the average lag time for single ∆relA∆spoT cells was not different from that of wild-type cells (16.0 h; p = 0.43), and starvation for 4 or 8 days did not significantly increase lag times (p > 0.66; Fig. 4). The heterogeneity of regrowth times also remained unchanged, with comparable standard deviations at all time points (p = 0.21; SD = 1.1 h on day 0, 1.0 h on day 4, and 1.1 h on day 8; Fig. 4). These results contrast with the planktonic response of ∆relA∆spoT cells, which required significantly longer times to resuscitate following 2–5 days of starvation (p < 0.0001) and had increased heterogeneity in recovery times (p = 0.01; Fig. S6B). The results indicate that the stringent response mutant is more recalcitrant to starvation when cultured in biofilms than when grown planktonically.

To validate the OmniLog-based single-cell measurements, we performed an independent, lower-throughput analysis using a standard absorbance plate reader, defining the time to reach exponential phase as OD600 = 0.2. This approach showed trends and statistical significance in resuscitation time and heterogeneity similar to those of the OmniLog system for all strains (Fig. S7), indicating that differences in incubation conditions (static versus shaking) and detection methods did not significantly affect measurements of single cell resuscitation time from starvation.

HPF, but not RMF, affects cellular RNA abundance during starvation of P. aeruginosa biofilms

When cultured and starved planktonically, P. aeruginosa ∆hpf cells have reduced ribosome content and compromised ribosome integrity compared to the wild-type strain43,44. To assess whether similar effects occur in biofilms, we examined ribosome content and integrity of P. aeruginosa cultured and starved in biofilms. Ribosomes can be assessed using: (i) RNA abundance11,43, (ii) the ratio of 23S to 16S rRNA43,45, and (iii) ribosomal protein abundance44. Because rRNA and tRNA comprise over 95% of bacterial RNA, total RNA can serve as an approximate measure of cellular rRNA content73,74. To quantify ribosome abundance in starved P. aeruginosa biofilms, RNA was extracted from wild-type and mutant strain biofilms. After 2 days of biofilm growth, P. aeruginosa PAO1, ∆rmf, ∆hpf, and ∆hpf + hpf biofilms contained similar quantities of total RNA (p > 0.33; Fig. 5A). After 2 days of starvation, total RNA levels decreased in all strains, which is consistent with degradation of stable RNAs in starved cells15,36,75. In wild-type biofilms, RNA levels stabilized after this initial decline and remained relatively constant throughout the remainder of the starvation period (p > 0.24). P. aeruginosa PAO1 ∆rmf biofilms were not different from the wild type at any time point (p > 0.52) and had no significant reduction following 2 days of starvation (p > 0.18). In contrast, ∆hpf biofilms had the largest decrease in total RNA, with a 5.5-fold reduction relative to the wild-type strain after 2 days of starvation (p = 0.04), and RNA levels continued to decline from days 2 through 8 of starvation (p > 0.0001). After 8 d of starvation, ∆hpf biofilms contained 3.4-fold less total RNA than wild-type biofilms (Fig. 5A). Complementation of ∆hpf with hpf on a plasmid restored RNA to wild-type levels at all time points (p > 0.18). Lack of the stringent response results in P. aeruginosa biofilm cells having increased RNA content per cell

We quantified RNA abundance in ∆relA∆spoT biofilms following growth and up to 8 days of starvation. After 2 days of biofilm growth, ∆relA∆spoT biofilms contained less RNA per biofilm than the wild-type strain (p = 0.0067; Fig. 5A), consistent with the six-fold reduction in viable cells observed in ∆relA∆spoT biofilms compared to the wild type (Fig. 3). However, when RNA levels were normalized to viable cells, ∆relA∆spoT cells had 4-fold higher RNA content per cell than wild-type cells prior to starvation (p < 0.0001; Fig. 5B), and this RNA content remained elevated throughout the starvation period. RNA levels in ∆relA∆spoT biofilms remained stable from days 2 through 8 of starvation (p > 0.95). The results indicate that the ∆relA∆spoT strain may be impaired in its ability to repress rRNA synthesis and/or degrade stable RNAs during starvation.

Ribosome hibernation and the stringent response affect ribosome integrity in starved P. aeruginosa biofilms differently

To determine the effect of starvation on ribosome integrity in biofilm cells, RNA from P. aeruginosa biofilms cells was characterized further using an Agilent Bioanalyzer to measure the relative abundance of 23S and 16S rRNA. In exponential phase planktonic cultures grown in rich media, P. aeruginosa PAO1 has a molar ratio of large to small ribosomal subunits of approximately 1:1, corresponding to a mass ratio of approximately 1.9:1 (23S:16S rRNA). The effect of ribosome hibernation and the stringent response on antibiotic tolerance of P. aeruginosa biofilms

Since most antibiotics preferentially kill actively metabolizing cells, we investigated how ribosome hibernation and the stringent response affect antibiotic tolerance of P. aeruginosa biofilms under nutrient replete and starvation conditions, and we compared these responses to those of planktonically cultured cells. Biofilms were grown under nutrient replete conditions for 2 days and then transferred daily to fresh medium containing ciprofloxacin (1 µg × ml−1), tobramycin (20 µg × ml−1), or no antibiotic60, in either nutrient replete (TSA) or starvation (PBSA) conditions. Untreated control biofilms showed no significant changes in viability after three days for any of the three strains, as measured by CFUs (p > 0.42; Fig. S10A). Similarly, untreated planktonic cultures from each strain exhibited no significant changes in CFUs following the three-day time course (p > 0.57; Fig. S10B).

Under nutrient-replete conditions, wild-type P. aeruginosa PAO1 biofilms exposed to ciprofloxacin lost approximately one log of viability after 24 h (p < 0.0001), with no further decrease over the subsequent two days of treatment (p = 0.90; Fig. 8A). The ∆hpf strain displayed a similar response, with no significant difference relative to the wild-type strain (p > 0.81; Fig. 8A). Under starvation conditions (PBSA), the wild-type strain again showed an ~1 log reduction after one day (p < 0.0001), with no additional killing thereafter (p = 0.96; Fig. 8B). The ∆hpf strain did not differ from the wild type(p > 0.47), indicating that HPF does not contribute to ciprofloxacin tolerance in biofilms under either condition. In planktonic cultures, stationary phase wild-type and ∆hpf cells were more susceptible, exhibiting greater than 4 log reductions in viability after three days of treatment in both nutrient replete and starvation conditions (p < 0.0001; Fig. 8C, D). Under these conditions, the ∆hpf strain did not differ significantly from the wild type following antibiotic treatment (p > 0.99). Under nutrient replete conditions, stationary phase cells were highly susceptible to ciprofloxacin, with nearly complete killing (p < 0.0001, Fig. 8C). However, under starvation conditions, ∆relA∆spoT planktonic cells showed limited killing, with a 1.3-log reduction over three days (p = 0.0002; Fig. 8D), comparable to starved ∆relA∆spoT biofilm cells (p = 0.91). Tobramycin treatment produced similar overall responses in the wild-type and ∆hpf strains. The ∆relA∆spoT mutant followed similar, but more pronounced, susceptibility trends in response to tobramycin treatment. Adaptation to antibiotic stress by starved P. aeruginosa biofilm cells

To test whether starvation influences the ability of biofilm cells to recover in the presence of antibiotic stress, we performed regrowth assays over the course of starvation. The MIC of P. aeruginosa PAO1 to ciprofloxacin in TSB was 0.14 + /−0.01 µg × ml−1(Fig. S4E). Therefore, we used a subinhibitory concentration of 0.1 µg × ml−1 (~0.7X MIC) to probe recovery under antibiotic stress without inducing extensive killing. At days 0, 4, and 8 of starvation, biofilms were dispersed and assayed for CFUs and single cell regrowth kinetics, as described above, but in the presence and absence of 0.1 µg × ml−1 ciprofloxacin.

Regrowth times of single dispersed biofilm cells were also highly heterogeneous under ciprofloxacin stress (SD = 9.2 h; p < 0.0001). Following 4 days of biofilm starvation, survival in the presence of ciprofloxacin did not differ significantly from that of non-starved biofilm cells (p = 0.76; Fig. 9A). Discussion

Biofilms contain heterogeneous subpopulations of bacteria, including cells exposed to nutrient limitation and other stresses. Therefore, starvation survival response mechanisms are expressed by bacteria growing in biofilms17,59,78. Among the strategies bacteria use to adapt to starvation are ribosome hibernation and the stringent response, both of which involve ribosome-associated signaling and regulation. During bacterial starvation, ribosomes are recycled as a source of nutrients, resulting in a reduction in the total cellular ribosome content15,20,75. However, bacteria maintain a critical number of ribosomes during starvation to allow translation to resume during resuscitation45,79. Ribosome hibernation factors preserve a subset of ribosomes by protecting them from the first steps of ribosome recycling – cleavage of rRNA by endonucleases37. The stringent response is also mediated by ribosomes26. Starvation, detected by stalled ribosomes with insufficient concentrations of charged tRNAs, signals the synthetase activity of RelA to produce the alarmone (p)ppGpp, which in turn signals changes in gene expression, primarily through its interaction with the RNA polymerase interacting protein DksA and the stationary phase sigma factor RpoS54,80. In the present study, we investigated how ribosome hibernation and the stringent response influence the physiology of P. aeruginosa cultured and starved in biofilms. Overall, the results demonstrate that both mechanisms contribute to survival of biofilms populations during stress, but that disruption of each pathway results in distinct physiological outcomes.

In some cases, the phenotypes of biofilm cultured cells (including starvation survival and antibiotic tolerances) differed from those of planktonic cells43, further highlighting the physiological differences between these growth states. These differences may be due to additional regulatory and protective features specific to biofilms, including quorum sensing81, cyclic-di-GMP signaling82, and the production of extracellular matrix materials83. Those factors were not directly assayed here, making direct comparisons between planktonic and biofilm cells difficult. Instead, we focused on comparing wild-type P. aeruginosa biofilms with mutants defective in the two starvation survival pathways to determine their roles in biofilm physiology.

For these studies, we employed a highly reproducible static colony biofilm model, where biofilms are formed from clonal growth on a surface64,84. This system allows cell to cell interactions and the diffusion and limitation of nutrients and oxygen85, which results in structured microenvironments with steep oxygen gradients. In P. aeruginosa colony biofilms, oxygen concentrations are depleted to less than 5% of atmospheric levels within an approximately 80-micrometer descension into the biofilm6. This oxygen depletion contributes to the formation of dormant, antibiotic-tolerant subpopulations of P. aeruginosa cells and has been demonstrated experimentally11 and through modeling86,87. This system also allows environmental conditions, such as nutrient availability or antibiotic exposure, to be manipulated by transferring intact biofilms on their membrane supports to defined conditions of interest64,84. It is particularly useful for studying the effects of antimicrobial treatment, because changes in cell number can be attributed to cell death rather than detachment, as bacteria are not able to migrate from these spatially restricted biofilms88. In addition, we used a plant model based on biofilm formation on mung bean seedlings63,77 to determine the effect of the two bacterial starvation survival mechanisms on plant development, as an indicator of bacterial virulence.

The imaging results (Fig. 1) show that genes encoding the ribosome hibernation factors hpf and rmf are expressed from their native promoters in P. aeruginosa biofilms. Expression from Prmf and Phpf was greatest at the periphery of the actively growing biofilms near the air/biofilm interface, with Prmf showing the greatest expression in this zone. The results are consistent with our previous laser microdissection and transcriptomics studies11, which demonstrated that cells near the air-biofilm interface are physiologically similar to early stationary phase cells, whereas bacteria deeper within the biofilm experience hypoxia and have likely transitioned to dormancy11,79. Phpf expression was detected throughout the biofilms, even in the deeper hypoxic regions, confirming prior laser microdissection results11. Fluorescence from both reporters decreased over the course of starvation (Fig. 1, S1), consistent with biomass reduction and protein recycling characteristic of starving cells. The ribosomal protein S13 and the hibernation factor HPF were detectable over 8 days of biofilm starvation (Fig. 2). P. aeruginosa biofilm viability was maintained throughout starvation (Fig. 3), indicating that the cellular levels of ribosomes and HPF produced during active growth were sufficient to support survival during extended starvation.

As indicators of the physiological state of starved bacteria, we assayed ribosome abundance and integrity in wild-type P. aeruginosa PAO1 and its mutant derivatives defective in ribosome hibernation or the stringent response. In planktonic cultures, HPF, but not RMF, is required for maintenance of ribosome integrity and viability during starvation43. In wild-type biofilms, ribosome abundance and cellular biomass decreased after two days of starvation (Figs. 5A, S1, S3), consistent with starvation induced cellular remodeling, including macromolecule degradation, reduction of cytoplasmic volume, and decreased cell wall synthesis. These changes likely contribute to increased stress tolerance by lowering metabolic activity. After this initial decline, ribosome abundance (Fig. 5) and relative rRNA levels (Fig. 6) stabilized for the remainder of the starvation period. The ∆rmf strain did not differ from the wild type in any parameter measured. These results indicate that RMF is not required for starvation survival (Fig. 3) or ribosome integrity (Figs. 5, 6) in P. aeruginosa biofilms. Although RMF interacts with resting ribosomes near small subunit protein S1 in E. coli23, deletion or overexpression of rmf did not affect ribosome activity, growth (Fig. S4A–D), or antibiotic resistance of P. aeruginosa (Fig. S4E–H). These findings differ from phenotypic analysis of E. coli RMF41,42 and suggest that RMF, which is highly expressed in these biofilms, may play a role in P. aeruginosa that differs from ribosome hibernation.

For the P. aeruginosa ∆hpf strain, impaired resuscitation correlates with decreased cellular rRNA levels (Fig. 5) and reduced 23S:16S rRNA ratios (Fig. 6 and ref. 43). At the single-cell level, only ~14% of ∆hpf cells remain capable of resuscitation after 8 days of starvation, and these cells exhibit slower and more variable resuscitation kinetics than wild-type cells (Figs. 3, 4). These results indicate that a minority of starved ∆hpf biofilm cells retain a physiological state that allows recovery, while the majority are either dead or in a viable but non-culturable (VBNC) state. Impaired resuscitation at the single-cell level may result from reduced ribosome content, limiting the capacity for de novo protein synthesis during recovery. It is also possible that compromised ribosome integrity could impair translation of proteins required to maintain membrane potential or other essential functions. P. aeruginosa ∆hpf biofilm cells survive starvation longer than ∆hpf planktonic cells (Fig. 3 and ref. 43), potentially due to biofilm-specific signaling or increased access to nutrients released from dying or dead cells within the biofilms.

Since HPF protects ribosomes during starvation, we predicted that loss of HPF would increase antibiotic susceptibility in starved biofilms. Surprisingly, ∆hpf mutant biofilms were not more sensitive than wild-type biofilms to either tobramycin or ciprofloxacin (Fig. 8). Both strains had a ~ 1.4-log reduction in viability following three days of antibiotic treatment, regardless of nutrient availability. Both strains were substantially more susceptible in starved planktonic cultures, with greater than 4 log reductions in viability following tobramycin and ciprofloxacin treatment. The results indicate that loss of hpf does not increase susceptibility to these two antibiotics in either planktonic or biofilm conditions and further highlights the distinct antibiotic tolerances of biofilms relative to planktonic populations.

Since biofilms contain heterogeneous populations of cells, we characterized the physiology of single biofilm cells by measuring regrowth kinetics following starvation and exposure to antibiotic stress. When P. aeruginosa PAO1 starved biofilms were exposed to a sub-inhibitory concentration of ciprofloxacin during regrowth, individual cells required significantly greater time to reach early exponential phase compared to cells without antibiotic present (Figs. 4, 9). The variability in recovery time also increased, and following 8 days of starvation, a subset of outliers exhibited rapid recovery compared to the rest of the population (Fig. 9). The results suggest that a subpopulation of biofilm cells may adapt during starvation to better tolerate subsequent antibiotic stress, even in the absence of prior exposure to the antibiotic. A similar response has been reported for biofilm starvation in E. coli89. Whether this phenotype reflects genetic adaptation or physiological remodeling remains unclear, but future studies will investigate whether these cells acquire heritable resistance to ciprofloxacin.

In the mung bean seedling infection model, loss of HPF attenuated P. aeruginosa virulence, highlighting the importance of ribosome hibernation in biofilm-associated infections. Seedlings inoculated with the ∆hpf strain exhibited improved plant health, with a higher proportion developing leaves compared to those infected with wild-type PAO1, despite equivalent colonization levels (Figs. 10, S11). Complementation of ∆hpf with hpf in trans fully restored virulence, confirming that the attenuation was specifically due to loss of HPF. While the mechanism for this reduced virulence is not clear, it may be due to impaired ribosome integrity or decreased production or secretion of virulence factors, since these cells may be unable to protect ribosomes from degradation under the nutrient-limited conditions provided by the plant host. The results demonstrate that HPF-mediated ribosome protection contributes to the ability of P. aeruginosa biofilms to cause disease, while RMF did not affect virulence in this model.

The stringent response has been shown previously to play a role in the development and antibiotic tolerance of P. aeruginosa biofilms58,59,90,91. Here, we assayed the physiology of stringent response mutant cells in starved P. aeruginosa biofilms. Unlike starved planktonic cultures, ∆relA∆spoT biofilms did not have a decrease in the number of recoverable cells over the course of starvation (Fig. 3 and ref. 43) and had approximately 2.5-fold more RNA per cell than the wild-type strain throughout 8 days of starvation (Fig. 5B). Rather than having a decreased ribosomal 23S:16S rRNA ratio, as expected during slowed growth conditions, the ∆relA∆spoT biofilm cells had higher ratios than wild-type biofilms (Fig. 6). The results show that ∆relA∆spoT biofilm cells that are impaired in growth arrest are dysregulated in ribosome recycling during starvation.

The lack of growth arrest in the ∆relA∆spoT mutant was also associated with increased antibiotic sensitivity. Consistent with previous studies58,59, ∆relA∆spoT biofilms were more sensitive than wild-type biofilms to both the aminoglycoside tobramycin and the fluoroquinolone ciprofloxacin (Fig. 8) under nutrient-replete conditions. However, under nutrient limited conditions, the ∆relA∆spoT mutant strain did not show increased sensitivity to ciprofloxacin, a trend also observed in planktonic cultures, where ∆relA∆spoT cell death was increased in the presence, but not the absence, of nutrients. These responses are likely due to starvation-induced physiological changes and the distinct mechanisms of action of the two antibiotics. Starvation results in compaction of DNA19, which may reduce the efficacy of ciprofloxacin, a DNA gyrase inhibitor. Impaired ribosome recycling the ∆relA∆spoT mutant during growth arrest may leave more active ribosomes available, providing additional targets for tobramycin, which inhibits protein synthesis.

When dispersed from biofilms, ∆relA∆spoT cells recovered more rapidly than wild-type cells in the presence of a subinhibitory concentration of ciprofloxacin (Fig. 9B). This enhanced recovery could be due to the elevated levels of ribosomes per cell maintained by the mutant during starvation, which could increase translational capacity upon resuscitation. Other biofilm-specific factors could also influence the rapid recovery of the ∆relA∆spoT cells in the presence of low levels of ciprofloxacin, such as increased quorum sensing or elevated levels of cyclic-di-GMP.

The stringent response of P. aeruginosa plays a role in bacterial pathogenesis56,92 and has been shown to affect virulence in both a Drosophila feeding model and a rat lung infection model57. Here, we further demonstrate that the stringent response is crucial for P. aeruginosa virulence in a plant biofilm model of infection. Compared to the wild-type strain, P. aeruginosa ∆relA∆spoT biofilms were essentially avirulent in the mung bean seedling model, with plant survival and root and leaf development similar to PBS treated controls (Fig. 10). The ∆relA∆spoT mutant formed biofilms on seedlings in numbers similar to the wild-type strain (Fig. S11), indicating that the loss of virulence was not due to impaired biofilm formation. The avirulent phenotype is likely due to dysregulated expression of virulence factors, as the stringent response regulates up to 30% of the P. aeruginosa genome during stationary phase, including genes encoding pyocyanin, elastases, and siderophores55,57. These results confirm that an intact stringent response is essential for disease development in a live biofilm infection model.

Overall, the results here demonstrate that both ribosome hibernation and the stringent response play distinct roles in P. aeruginosa biofilm physiology. HPF is essential for protecting ribosomes during starvation and enables recovery from starvation, yet it does not affect tolerance to ciprofloxacin or tobramycin. In contrast, the stringent response is not required for survival during biofilm starvation, but its absence reduces antibiotic tolerance and also decreases virulence in a plant model of biofilm infection. Together, the findings highlight how these two pathways uniquely contribute to biofilm resilience and pathogenic potential.

Methods

Bacterial strains

Experiments were performed using Pseudomonas aeruginosa strain PAO1 and its previously described ∆rmf and ∆hpf deletion derivatives11. Complementation experiments were performed using plasmids containing the hpf or the rmf genes under control of the IPTG-inducible promoter on plasmid vector pMF54, as described previously43. The stringent response mutant, P. aeruginosa ∆relA∆spoT, was graciously provided by Dr. Pradeep Singh. Since the P. aeruginosa PAO1 ∆relA∆spoT strain was obtained from a different lab, genomic DNA was extracted from the ∆relA∆spoT strain and sequenced using Oxford Nanopore Technology with custom analysis and annotation (Plasmidsaurus, San Francisco, CA). The strain was found to be isogenic to our laboratory strain of P. aeruginosa PAO1, with the exception of the relA and spoT deletions, and also to be isogenic to the P. aeruginosa PAO1 strain described at pseudomonas.com93. Single-copy promoter transcriptional/translational fusion strains, P. aeruginosa PAO1 Phpf-hpf-yfp and Prmf-rmf-yfp, were constructed by in frame fusions of hpf and rmf to the yellow fluorescent protein gene (yfp) in the transposon Tn7 vector94,95. The Tn7 transposons inserted into the TN7 att site by electroporation with the Tn7 transposase helper plasmid94,95.

Biofilm growth and starvation

Inocula were prepared by culturing strains in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ) at 37 °C. Strains containing plasmids were supplemented with carbenicillin (150 μg × ml−1; Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and 1.0 mM isopropyl ß-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Teknova, Hollister, CA). Cultures were adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.5 using an UltrospecPro 2100 spectrophotometer (Amersham Biosciences/GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL). Aliquots, 30 μl, of the inocula were placed on sterile 13-mm diameter, 0.2-μm pore polycarbonate IsoporeTM filters (Millipore, Burlington, MA) positioned on TSA plates as described previously11. For experiments involving fluorescent reporter strains, sterile black filters (13-mm diameter, 0.2-μm pore polycarbonate; Poretics, GVS Life Sciences, Bologna, Italy) were used to eliminate interference from filter fluorescence. Plates containing filters were incubated at 37 °C, and membranes containing colony biofilms were aseptically transferred to fresh TSA plates after 24 h. Following 48 h growth on TSA, biofilms were transferred to 1.5% agar plates containing phosphate buffered saline, pH 7.1 (PBSA; Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) every 24 h for 8 days at 37 °C96. Biofilms were destructively sampled daily for analysis of cell viability, optical density, rRNA characterization, ribosomal protein abundance, antibiotic tolerance, single cell regrowth, and yellow fluorescent protein fluorescence quantification, as described below.

Analysis of fluorescent reporter strain biofilms

For visualization of P. aeruginosa Phpf-hpf-yfp and Prmf-rmf-yfp reporter fusion strains, colony biofilms were cryoembedded in Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetek USA, Torrance, CA) after 0, 4, and 8 days of starvation and thin-sectioned using a Leica CM1950 Cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) as described previously11. Thin sections (10 µm) were mounted on Superfrost Plus glass slides (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and examined using a Nikon E-800 upright epifluorescence microscope with a 10x objective and a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) filter set (480/30 excitation, 535/40 emission, 515 nm dichroic filter) for visualizing YFP fluorescence. Images were acquired with a Photometrics CoolSnap fx digital camera with a 500-ms exposure time (Roper Technologies, Trenton, NJ) and processed using Zen v3.12 software (Carl Zeiss, Thornwood, New York). Images shown are representative of three independent biological replicates.

For quantification of YFP fluorescence, P. aeruginosa PAO1, PAO1 Phpf-hpf-yfp, and PAO1 Prmf-rmf-yfp biofilms were transferred to 10 ml PBS, pH 7.1 and disrupted by two 30-second vortex cycles at maximum speed (Vortex Genie II; Scientific Industries, Bohemia, NY). Following 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 days of starvation, three 200 µl aliquots of homogenized biofilm cells from each strain were placed in the wells of sterile black, clear bottom 96-well microtiter plates (Corning, Corning, NY). YFP fluorescence (excitation 500 nm; emission 541 nm) and the optical density (OD600) of the biofilm cell suspensions were measured using a Synergy H1 plate reader (Biotek/Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Three biological replicates were performed for each strain. Technical replicates within each biological replicate were averaged, and background fluorescence, determined from P. aeruginosa PAO1 biofilm cells grown under the same conditions, was subtracted from all measurements. Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA with Holm-Sidak correction for multiple comparisons at alpha = 0.05.

Determination of cell viability during nutrient limitation assays

Colony forming units (CFUs) were quantified daily over 8 days of starvation using the drop plate method97. Membranes carrying colony biofilms were transferred with sterile forceps to vials containing 10 ml PBS, pH 7.1 and vortexed for two thirty-second cycles at maximum speed to disrupt the biofilms (Vortex Genie II; Scientific Industries, Bohemia, NY). Cell suspensions were serially diluted in 0.85% NaCl solution, and 60 μl volumes were plated as 10 μl drops on TSA97. Colonies were counted after 18 h of incubation at 37 °C and again at 42 h. For starvation assays, six independent biological replicates were performed for each strain at each time point. The mean CFUs and standard errors of the mean were calculated using Excel v2511 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington).

Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA with Holm-Sidak correction for multiple comparison at alpha = 0.05. To determine significant differences over the course of starvation, for each strain, the CFUs prior to starvation were compared to the CFUs following each subsequent day of starvation. To determine significant differences relative to the wild-type strain, CFUs from each mutant strain were compared to CFUs of the wild-type strain at each timepoint of starvation. All statistical analyses and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism v 10.5.0 (Boston, Massachusetts).

Optical density measurements

Total cellular biomass was estimated by measuring the optical density of resuspended biofilms. Biofilms were resuspended in 10 mL PBS, homogenized, and assayed for optical densities at 600 nm on days 0, 4, and 8 of starvation using an UltrospecPro 2100 spectrophotometer (Fig. S4; Amersham Biosciences/GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL) or a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Fig. S1). Mean OD600 values, standard errors of the mean, and percent reductions in OD600 for each strain were calculated. Experiments were performed with a minimum of three independent biological replicates. Significant differences were assessed using two-way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak correction for multiple comparisons at alpha = 0.05.

For growth analysis, optical densities at 600 nm (OD600) were monitored every 10 min using an Epoch 2 or Synergy H1 plate reader (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Overnight cultures of P. aeruginosa PAO1, P. aeruginosa containing the empty vector control (pMF54), or containing rmf (pMF462) were diluted to an OD600 of 0.1 in TSB. Aliquots (10 μl) were added to the wells of sterile, clear, flat bottom 96-well microtiter plates containing 190 μl TSB or TSB supplemented with 1 mM IPTG. Plates were lidded, sealed, and incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 24 h. Three independent biological replicates were performed, and technical replicates were averaged within each experiment. Mean OD600 values and standard errors from the three independent trials were plotted. The area under each curve was calculated by integration and means and standard errors were plotted. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Holm-Sidak multiple-comparison correction at alpha = 0.05. For comparison of PAO1 and PAO1∆rmf, overnight cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.1, and 100 μl aliquots were added to wells containing 100 μl TSB. Mean OD600 values and standard errors of the mean from three biological replicates were plotted. The area under each growth curve was calculated by integration, and the means and standard errors of the mean areas were plotted. Statistical significance between the two strains was assessed using an unpaired two-tailed t-test at α = 0.05.

Antibiotic susceptibility of biofilm and planktonic cells

Biofilms of P. aeruginosa PAO1, as well as the ∆hpf and ∆relA∆spoT mutant strains, were cultivated as described above. Following two days of growth, colony biofilms were transferred to TSA or PBSA containing either 1 µg × mL−1 ciprofloxacin hydrochloride (Fluka, Buchs, Switzerland) or 20 µg × mL−1 tobramycin (Acros Organics, Antwerp, Belgium) or no antibiotic. Biofilms were incubated at 37 °C for up to three additional days, with aseptic transfers to fresh agar medium every 24 h. Biofilms were disaggregated by vortexing, and viable cells were enumerated as CFUs as described above. Four independent biological replicates were performed for biofilm susceptibility assays.

Antibiotic susceptibility of planktonic cells was evaluated as described previously43. For planktonic cultures, 120 μl aliquots of P. aeruginosa PAO1, ∆hpf, and ∆relA∆spoT overnight cultures were inoculated into 4 ml of TSB and grown to stationary phase (OD600 > 7.0) at 37 °C with rotation. For testing under starvation conditions, approximately 7 × 109 CFUs of each strain were pelleted for 1 min at 8580 × g, washed twice with PBS, resuspended in 1 mL PBS, and added to 25 ml PBS in 125 ml baffled flasks to obtain a density of ~3 × 108 CFU x ml-1 for antibiotic exposure experiments. For testing under nutrient-replete conditions, approximately 7 × 109 CFUs of each strain were added to 25 ml TSB to obtain a density of ~3 × 108 CFU ×ml-1 and then divided into 5 ml aliquots for antibiotic exposure experiments. Antibiotic stock solutions were added to final concentrations of 1 µg × mL−1 ciprofloxacin hydrochloride (Fluka, Buchs, Switzerland) or 20 µg × mL−1 tobramycin (Acros Organics, Antwerp, Belgium). All cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm and sampled for viability as CFUs immediately prior to antibiotic addition and after 1, 2, and 3 days of treatment. Gentamicin susceptibility assays of planktonic cells in PBS were performed as described above using P. aeruginosa PAO1 and P. aeruginosa ∆rmf. CFUs were quantified prior to antibiotic addition and after 1 and 2 days of treatment. Gentamicin (Acros Organics, Antwerp, Belgium) was added to final concentrations of 10 or 15 µg × mL−1. All planktonic experiments were conducted using a minimum of three independent biological replicates; ciprofloxacin treatment of cultures in PBS was conducted with nine biological replicates.

To determine whether the antibiotic susceptibility of mutant strains differed significantly from that of the wild-type strain under both biofilm and planktonic conditions, data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak correction at alpha = 0.05 to generate adjusted p-values. All data are presented as means ± standard errors of the means.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and disk diffusion assays

P. aeruginosa strains were cultured to exponential phase in TSB and mixed with two-fold serial dilutions of antibiotics in 96 well microtiter plates. Outer wells contained sterile medium and served as negative controls. MIC assays were set up according to the CLSI microdilution standard method M0798, except that TSB was used instead of Mueller-Hinton II broth to maintain consistency with the medium used for biofilm assays. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with shaking in a Cytation 5 or Epoch 2 plate reader, and the OD600 was recorded every 10 min for 16 h. MIC values were determined by calculating the area under each growth curve at 16 h and plotting the proportion of bacterial growth relative to the no antibiotic controls against the logarithm of antibiotic concentration, as described previously99. A modified Gompertz function was fit to the resulting dose-response curves to determine MIC values. Three independent biological replicates were performed for tobramycin and gentamicin, and four independent biological replicates were performed for ciprofloxacin. Mean MIC values were plotted with their standard errors. For each antibiotic, unpaired two-tailed t-tests were conducted to assess whether the MIC of PAO1∆rmf was significantly different from that of PAO1 at alpha = 0.05.

Disk diffusion assays were performed as in ref. 96. Disks containing 10 µg gentamicin, 30 µg kanamycin, 30 µg tetracycline, 5 µg levofloxacin, or 30 µg cefotaxime were obtained from Hardy Diagnostics (Santa Maria, California). Disks containing 300 units polymyxin B or 25 µg trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole were obtained from Oxoid (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA). P. aeruginosa PAO1 and PAO1∆rmf strains were cultured from frozen stocks in Mueller Hinton II Broth at 37 °C with rotation and then diluted to an optical density equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland Standard. The cultures were spread plated with a cotton swab onto 4 mm thick Mueller Hinton II agar plates. Antibiotic discs were placed onto the inoculated plates and plates were incubated 37 °C for 16 h. The zones of inhibition were then measured and recorded. Three biological replicates were performed. The mean and standard deviation of the zones of clearing were plotted and unpaired two-tailed t-tests were performed to determine if the susceptibility in the PAO1∆rmf strain was significantly different from that of PAO1 for each antibiotic at alpha = 0.05.

Single cell regrowth assays

On days 0, 4, and 8 of starvation, biofilms were disrupted by vortexing in PBS as described above. The resulting biofilm cell suspensions were diluted to approximately 1 CFU per 100 µl PBS (10 CFU × ml−1). Aliquots (10 µl) were dispensed into the wells of sterile, clear, flat bottom microtiter plates (USA Scientific, Ocala, FL) containing 190 µl TSB per well. Plates were then lidded and sealed with parafilm. Based on the Poisson distribution, this low loading rate (approximately 1 cell per 10 wells) ensured that inoculated wells received no more than a single cell. To verify that wells were inoculated with single cells rather than multicellular clumps, cell suspensions were examined microscopically. Biofilm cell suspensions were diluted 1:10 and 1:100 in PBS, and 2-µL aliquots were spotted onto Tekdon microscope slides (Tekdon Inc, Myakka City, Florida), with four technical replicates per strain per timepoint. Spots were air-dried, heat-fixed, stained with crystal violet, rinsed with reverse osmosis (RO) water, and examined for cell dispersion using a Revolve Microscope (Echo, San Diego, CA) with a 60x oil immersion objective. Microscopic validation was performed for three biological replicates for each strain prior to starvation and after 8 days of starvation.

Microtiter plates containing single cell inocula were placed in a Cytation 5 or Epoch 2 microplate reader (Biotek Instruments/Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and incubated at 37 °C with linear shaking (5 mm, 410 cpm). The optical density at 600 nm was measured every 15 min for 24 h. After 24 h, plates were removed from readers and incubated statically at 37 °C for an additional 24 h to confirm that the 24 h timeframe was sufficient to identify all regrowth events. Data were exported to Excel. The time (h) required for a culture originating from a single cell to reach early exponential phase (OD600 > 0.2) was recorded. A minimum of three independent biological replicates were performed, and at least 45 single-cell growth curves were analyzed for each strain at each timepoint; a total of 769 growth curves originating from single cells were evaluated.

For assays performed using the OmniLog system, Redox Dye Mix A (at 0.75x, Biolog, Hayward, CA) was included in the TSB regrowth medium. Suspensions of biofilm cells harvested after 0, 4, and 8 days of starvation were prepared as described above. Planktonic ∆relA∆spoT suspensions were prepared as described previously43 and diluted to 10 CFU × ml−1 in PBS. Each microtiter well contained 10 µl of cell suspension and 190 µl of medium. Microtiter plates were lidded, sealed, and incubated at 37 °C, and OmniLog signals were measured every 15 minutes for 24 h. After 24 h, plates were removed from readers and incubated statically at 37 °C for an additional 24 h to confirm that the 24 h timeframe was sufficient to identify all regrowth events. Resulting D5E files were converted to CSV-formatted OKA files using the DPM File Manager Data File Converter. Growth curves were visualized and analyzed using Excel. The time (h) required for a culture originating from a single cell to reach early exponential phase (OL signal >50) was determined. For biofilm cells, a minimum of 117 growth curves starting from single cells for each strain at 0, 4, and 8 days of starvation were collected from a minimum of six independent biological replicates; a total of 1972 growth curves originating from single cells were evaluated. For ∆relA∆spoT planktonic samples, a minimum of 60 growth curves were collected at each timepoint from a minimum of four independent biological replicates; a total of 513 growth curves originating from single cells were evaluated.

To determine regrowth kinetics in the presence of antibiotic stress, OmniLog assays were performed as described above using TSB supplemented with 0.1 μg × ml−1 ciprofloxacin (Fluka, Buchs, Switzerland). Plates were incubated for 96 h in the OmniLog system followed by a 24 h static incubation at 37 °C as described above. For each strain at each timepoint, a minimum of 133 single-cell growth curves were evaluated from a minimum of 6 biological replicates; a total of 2375 growth curves originating from single cells were evaluated.

All regrowth assay data were plotted as box plots, with boxes representing the interquartile range (50% of the data), median values indicated by a central line, and whiskers representing the 10th and 90th percentiles. Statistical significance of regrowth times was assessed using one-way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak correction (α = 0.05) and differences in variance were evaluated using an F-test for comparisons between two groups and the Brown-Forsythe test for comparisons among three or more groups.

Antibiotic susceptibility of resuscitating biofilm and planktonic cells

Following 0, 4, and 8 days of starvation, biofilms were disrupted, and viable cells were quantified on TSA and TSA supplemented with 0.1 µg × mL−1 ciprofloxacin. A minimum of four independent biological replicates were performed. Planktonic cultures were starved as described previously43,45,96. On days 0, 1, 3, and 5 of starvation, CFUs were enumerated on TSA and TSA containing 0.1 µg × mL−1 ciprofloxacin. Four independent biological replicates were performed.

Log reductions in viability due to starvation were calculated relative to CFU recovery on TSA at the onset of starvation. Log reductions in viability due to antibiotic exposure were calculated at each timepoint relative to recovery on TSA. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak correction for multiple comparisons at alpha = 0.05.

Ribosomal RNA analysis

Over the course of starvation, 100 µl aliquots of biofilm cell suspensions, homogenized in PBS as described above, were collected daily, frozen at −80 °C, and stored until RNA extraction was performed. RNA was extracted and quantified as described previously43. Briefly, 20 µl of 10x lysis buffer/SDS solution and 100 µl of phenol were added to each suspension, followed by incubation at 65 °C for eight min with intermittent vortexing. Phase separation was performed by centrifugation in heavy phase lock gel tubes (5 Prime Therapeutics, South San Francisco, CA). A second extraction was carried out by the addition of 100 µl phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol solution (Acros Organics, Antwerp, Belgium), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA in the aqueous phase was purified using the RNA Clean and Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA yield was quantified using a NanoDrop1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) as the average of triplicate measurements for each biological sample, and RNA per biofilm and per CFU were calculated. Relative abundances of 23S and 16S rRNA were determined using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 with the Prokaryotic Total RNA 6000 Nano assay (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). All RNA yield and quality analyses were performed using at least three independent biological replicates per strain per time point, and mean values with standard errors were plotted. Significant differences in RNA abundance and rRNA ratios were assessed using two-way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak correction at alpha = 0.05. For each strain, RNA abundance and rRNA ratios were compared across the starvation time course, and mutant strains were compared with those of P. aeruginosa PAO1 at each time point.

Immunoblot analysis of S13 and HPF

To determine relative abundances of the small ribosomal protein S13 and of HPF during starvation, biofilms were disrupted and resuspended in PBS daily as described above. Aliquots (1 mL) were collected by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 3 min at 4 °C, and pellets were stored at −80 °C. For protein sample preparation, pellets were resuspended in 100 μL H20, and total cellular protein concentrations were quantified using the Bradford assay100. For each sample, 10 μg of total protein was incubated with 1 μL of 1:10 diluted Pierce Universal Nuclease (Pierce Biotechnology, Waltham, MA) for 5 min at ambient temperature to reduce viscosity.

Immunoblotting was performed using rabbit antibodies against HPF and the small subunit protein S13, as described previously44,45. Briefly, 10 μg of total cellular protein per sample, in denaturing buffer, was separated on 16% SDS-PAGE gels and then wet transferred to 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham Biosciences, Woburn, Massachusetts). HPF and S13 antibodies44 were generated by purifying 6X-histidine tagged HPF and S13 and raising antisera in rabbits (Lampire Biological Laboratories, Pipersville, PA). IgG fractions were purified using NAbTM Protein A Plus Spin Columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL). Nitrocellulose membranes were blocked with 1.5% skim milk, then probed with anti-HPF or anti-S13 primary antibodies at 1:2000 dilution. Horseradish peroxidase conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) was used as the secondary antibody at 1:10,000 dilution. Protein bands were detected using chemiluminescent substrate101 and visualized on X-ray autoradiography film (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX).

Mung bean biofilm virulence assay

Virulence was assessed using a modified mung bean plant infection model as described in ref. 63. Commercially obtained mung bean seeds (Now Foods, Bloomingdale, Illinois) were surface sterilized by performing two successive 20 min treatments in 20% sodium hypochlorite (Chlorox, Oakland, CA), followed by five rinses with sterile water and two additional 10 min washes with sterile water. Seeds were dried at 37 °C and stored in the dark at room temperature until use.

Seeds were germinated by incubation for 24 h at 33 °C under humidified conditions, after which seed coats were removed using sterile forceps. P. aeruginosa PAO1, ∆rmf, ∆hpf, ∆hpf + hpf, and ∆relA∆spoT strains were cultured overnight in TSB, washed twice with sterile PBS, and resuspended in PBS to a final concentration of ~109 CFU x ml-1. Biofilms were established on the germinated, dehulled seedings by incubating them for 2 h at 37 °C in the bacterial PBS suspensions or in PBS alone, which served as negative controls for infection and positive controls for plant development. At the conclusion of the 2 h inoculation period, each inoculation solution and a representative seedling from each treatment were assayed for CFU using the drop plate method, as described above. Inoculated and control seedlings were placed on the surface of 0.8% agar plates and incubated for 5 days at 37 °C in the dark in sealed containers. Plant health was assessed by recording seedling survival, cotyledon leaf development, and the number of root branches per seedling at the conclusion of the 5-day incubation period.

Four independent biological replicates were performed, each consisting of 20 seedlings per treatment, for a total of 80 seedlings analyzed per bacterial strain. Mean values from each biological replicate were plotted and used for statistical analysis to ensure a conservative and robust inference by defining independence, reducing sensitivity to within-trial variability, and minimizing false positives. Treatment effects were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak correction for multiple comparisons at α = 0.05.
