Molecular genetic analysis of colorectal carcinoma with an aggressive extraintestinal immunohistochemical phenotype
Authors: Jan Hrudka, Markéta Kalinová, Hana Fišerová, Karolína Jelínková, Andrej Nikov, Petr Waldauf, Radoslav Matěj
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading global cause of illness and death. There is a need for identification of better prognostic markers beyond traditional clinical variables like grade and stage. Previous research revealed that abnormal expression of cytokeratin 7 (CK7) and loss of the intestinal-specific Special AT-rich sequence-binding protein 2 (SATB2) are linked to poor CRC prognosis. This study aimed to explore these markers’ prognostic significance alongside two extraintestinal mucins (MUC5AC, MUC6), claudin 18, and MUC4 in 285 CRC cases using immunohistochemistry on tissue microarrays (TMAs). CK7 expression and SATB2-loss were associated with MUC5AC, MUC6, and claudin 18 positivity. These findings suggest a distinct "non-intestinal" immunohistochemical profile in CRC, often right-sided, SATB2-low, with atypical expression of CK7 and non-colorectal mucins (MUC5AC, MUC6). Strong MUC4 expression negatively impacted cancer-specific survival (hazard ratio = 2.7, p = 0.044). Genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing (NGS) in CK7 + CRCs and those with high MUC4 expression revealed prevalent mutations in TP53, APC, BRAF, KRAS, PIK3CA, FBXW7, and SMAD4, consistent with known CRC mutation patterns. NGS also identified druggable variants in BRAF, PIK3CA, and KRAS. CK7 + tumors showed intriguingly common (31.6%) BRAF V600E mutations corelating with poor prognosis, compared to the frequency described in the literature and databases. Further research on larger cohorts with a non-colorectal immunophenotype and high MUC4 expression is needed.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide. Over 1.9 million new CRC cases and 930,000 deaths were estimated in 20201. The incidence and mortality of CRC are projected to nearly double by 20401. In 2020, CRC was the second most common cause of cancer-related death2. In addition to traditional clinical-pathological prognostic factors like TNM staging and histopathological grading, many immunohistochemical studies have sought to identify prognostic markers to facilitate better patient risk stratification. In our previous studies, we identified that aberrant expression of low molecular weight cytokeratin 7 (CK7) and loss of “colonic specific” transcription factor special AT-rich sequence binding protein 2 (SATB2) were independent negative prognostic CRC markers3,4. The objective of this study was to elucidate the prognostic implications and potential associations between CK7 positivity and SATB2-low status, alongside examining (1) the presence of “extraintestinal” mucins MUC5AC and MUC6, (2) MUC4 expression, typically absent in colorectal cancer (CRC) despite being present in physiological intestinal mucosa, (3) claudin 18 expression, a cell surface molecule implicated in various cancers; and their eventual prognostic significance, and (4) to investigate the profiles of tumorigenic driver DNA mutations through next-generation sequencing (NGS) in CRC cases exhibiting immunohistochemical features indicating a poorer prognosis. This study expands upon previous research conducted within the same CRC cohort as analyzed in our aforementioned publications3,4.

Materials and methods

Immunohistochemical analysis of the study cohort

A cohort comprising 285 patients with surgically resected histologically verified colorectal cancer (CRC) and documented follow-up, along with formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue samples, was examined. The study was approved by ethical committee University Hospital Královské Vinohrady (approval number EK-R/04/0/2022) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Tissue microarray (TMA) techniques were employed to create paraffin blocks for immunohistochemical slides, utilizing a TMA Master 3D Histech manual tissue arrayer. From each FFPE invasive adenocarcinoma tissue sample, two cylindrical cores measuring 2 mm were randomly extracted. These cores were then transferred to a recipient TMA paraffin block, with each recipient block containing 20 core samples from 10 cases. Only cases with adequate TMA tissue were considered for inclusion in the study. Immunohistochemistry was performed on four µm-thick tissue sections using a Ventana BenchMark ULTRA auto-stainer (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, Arizona). Monoclonal antibodies targeting SATB2 (CellMarque, clone EP281, dilution 1:200), CK7 (BioSB, clone OV-TL, dilution 1:500), CK20 (BioSB, clone KS20.8, dilution 1:200), MUC4 (BioSB, clone 8Q7, dilution 1:100), MUC5AC (BioSB, clone CLH2, dilution 1:200), MUC6 (Roche, clone MRQ20, ready to use), Claudin 18 (Abcam, clone 34H14L15, dilution 1:400), MutS homolog 2 (MSH2, Roche, clone G219-1129, ready to use), postmeiotic segregation 2 (PMS2, Roche, clone A16-4, ready to use), MutS homolog 6 (MSH6, Roche, clone 44, ready to use), and MutL homolog 1 (MLH1, Roche, clone M1, ready to use) were employed. Positive reactions were detected utilizing an Ultraview Detection System (Ventana Medical Systems), and the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. Subsequently, stained slides underwent dehydration and were covered with a xylene-based mounting medium. Two experienced surgical pathologists (JH and RM) evaluated immunohistochemistry-stained slides using a multihead microscope recording consensus values for paired core samples. In CK7, CK20, and SATB2, any staining intensity was considered, and the average percentage of positive tumor cells in both cores were recorded. For CK7, CK20, and SATB2, thresholds counting ≥ 10%, ≥ 25%, and ≤ 40%, respectively, were used, sorting the cohort into low/high in each marker, as described in our previous publications3,4. In MUC4-moderate-to-strong expression, i.e., in ≥ 75% of tumor cells, was recorded as positive, according to Shamnugam et al.5. In the case of MUC5AC and MUC6, any percentage and staining intensity was considered positive. For claudin 18, the cases were evaluated according to the staining intensity due to a predominantly diffuse staining pattern, as negative, weakly, moderately, and strongly positive similarly to Kubota et al.6; for further analysis, negative and weakly positive samples and moderately and strongly positive samples were clustered together. For the determination of MMR status, tumors exhibiting any evident nuclear staining with MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and MLH1 antibodies were classified as MMR-proficient. Conversely, tumors displaying conspicuous loss of nuclear staining with anti-MMR antibodies, accompanied by positive stromal and lymphocytic staining, were categorized as MMR-deficient. All microscopic evaluations were conducted without access to clinical data or patient follow-up information. Next generation sequencing

Based on immunohistochemistry results and survival analysis, NGS analysis was performed in selected cases with immunohistochemistry profiles that correlated with shortened survival. DNA was extracted from diagnostic specimens by processing eight to ten 5 µm thick sections of FFPE tissue using QIAamp FFPE Tissue Kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Subsequently, DNA concentrations were measured with a Qubit 3 Fluorometer and Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kits (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). The integrity and amplificability of DNA from FFPE tissue samples were assessed following the Biomed-2 recommendations. A custom NGS panel (Agilent Technologies, CA, United States) was designed to identify Single Nucleotide Variants (SNVs) and Indels in 73 genes associated with solid tumors, as described in our recent publication7. The NGS libraries were prepared according to the protocol for Illumina paired-end multiplexed library preparation, involving enzymatic fragmentation and utilizing the SureSelectXT Library Preparation and Capture System (SureSelect XT Target Enrichment, Agilent Technologies). Sequencing of the NGS library was conducted on a MiSeq instrument using 2 × 75 v3 kits (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The resulting FASTQ data underwent rigorous quality-control checks and trimming. Indexing, mapping, alignment, and classification were performed using Varsome Clinical software, with reference to the hg38 reference genome. The minimum frequency of variants was set to 5% with a minimum coverage of 80 reads. For allele frequencies ≥ 50%, we also report variants with a coverage of < 75. The therapeutic relevance of mutations identified in our dataset was investigated using the OncoKB database.

Statistics

Overall survival (OS) was determined by calculating the duration from the date of surgery to either the recorded date of death or the date of the last known follow-up (censoring). Survival analysis involved univariate Kaplan–Meier analysis alongside the log-rank test, with confidence intervals computed using the log–log method. Cancer-specific survival (CSS) was also assessed, wherein patients with causes of death unrelated to colorectal cancer (CRC) were censored at the time of death. Both OS and CSS were subjected to univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, adjusting for patient age. Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox regression were truncated at 5 years of follow-up time. To explore potential correlations between binarized variables, a univariate logistic regression (Pearson’s chi-square test) was conducted. The variables included: (1) localized tumors (UICC stage I + II) versus metastasizing tumors (UICC stage III + IV), (2) low grade (grade 1 + 2) versus high grade (grade 3), (3) right-sided tumors (cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, transverse colon) versus left-sided tumors (splenic flexure, descending colon, sigmoid colon, rectum), (4) MMR-deficient versus MMR-proficient, (5) adenocarcinoma not otherwise specified (NOS) versus mucinous and signet ring carcinoma based on morphology, (6) 5-year survival (yes versus no). Additionally, immunohistochemistry markers (CK7, CK20, SATB2, MUC4, MUC5AC, MUC6, Claudin 18) were dichotomized into positive and negative categories, as described earlier3,4. The p value < 0.05 was regarded statistically significant, the p value < 0.1 borderline significant.

Overall survival analysis

Detailed OS results including 95% confidence intervals are listed in Table 2 and Fig. 2. As referenced in our previous articles3,4, there was a significant negative prognostic impact of CK7 positivity (≥ 10% tumor cells) and SATB2-loss (≤ 40% tumor cells). In the 5-year follow-up, CK7 expression was associated with significantly shorter OS [restricted mean survival time (rmean) = 3.12 vs. 3.40 years, hazard ratio (HR) = 2.13, p = 0.014] comparing CK7 + cases (n = 19) with CK7 − cases (n = 266). Hazard ratio in the multivariate (age-adjusted) Cox regression was HR = 2.27 (p = 0.008). The patients with SATB2-low tumors (n = 54) had significantly shorter 5-year overall OS (rmean = 3.16 vs. 4.01 years, HR = 2.17, p = 0.00019) compared to those with SATB2-high tumors (n = 231); HR = 2.12 (p < 0.001) in the multivariate (age-adjusted) Cox regression. MUC4 expression showed an insignificant trend towards shortened 5-year OS: MUC4-high (≥ 75% tumor cells, n = 7) vs. MUC4-low (n = 277) had a rmean = 2.89 vs. 3.89 years, p = 0.15; HR = 2.06 (p = 0.2) and 2.52 (p = 0.072) in the univariate and multivariate (age-adjusted) Cox regression, respectively. Expression of “non-colorectal” mucins (MUC5AC, MUC6), claudin 18, and CK20 displayed no significant impact on overall survival: MUC5AC + (n = 7) vs. MUC5AC − (n = 287) had a rmean = 3.19 vs. 3.89 years, p = 0.49. MUC6 + (n = 11) vs. MUC6 − (n = 283) had a rmean = 3.67 vs. 3.88 years, p = 0.95. Claudin 18 moderate + strong (n = 73) vs. Claudin 18 negative + weak (n = 211) had a rmean = 3.64 vs. 3.95 years, p = 0.21. Logistic regression analysis/analysis of associations

Detailed results are listed in Supplementary Table 1, with all data being listed in Supplementary Table 2. MUC4-high tumors were borderline significantly associated with high grade (Odds ratio = 4.41, p = 0.059) and mucinous/signet ring cell morphology (OR = 6.68, p = 0.059). Non-colorectal mucin expression showed significant associations in the following: MUC5AC expression was associated with high grade (OR = 8.43, p = 0.01), mucinous or signet ring cell morphology (OR = 11.8, p = 0.008), right-sided localization (OR = 10, p = 0.015), CK7 positivity (OR = 11.8, p = 0.008), and SATB2-low status (OR = 5.88, p = 0.029). MUC6 expression was associated with right-sided localization (OR = 4.35, p = 0.026), MMR-deficiency (OR = 16.679, p < 0.001), CK7 positivity (OR = 5.88, p = 0.031), low (< 25% tumor cells) CK20 expression (OR = 10.0, p < 0.001), SATB2-low status (OR = 5.26, p = 0.009), and borderline significant with high grade (OR = 3.34, p = 0.063). Claudin 18 moderate + strong expression showed significant associations with right-sided localization (OR = 2.44, p = 0.002), mucinous or signet ring cell morphology (OR = 4.59, p = 0.003), CK7 positivity (OR = 3.49, p = 0.012), SATB2-low status (OR = 2.72, p = 0.01), and a borderline significant association with high grade (OR = 1.78, p = 0.071).

Additionally, CK7 expression in ≥ 10% tumor cells was associated with CK20-low expression (OR = 4.35, p = 0.003) and SATB2-low status (OR = 14.3, p < 0.001), and borderline significance with high grade (OR = 2.59, p = 0.073), right-sided localization (OR = 2.22, p = 0.094), and mucinous/signet ring morphology (OR = 3.3, p = 0.1), as shown in our previous publications3,4. SATB2-low expression was associated with advanced (III + IV) UICC stage (OR = 2.33, p = 0.007), high grade (OR = 2.13, p = 0.027), right-sided tumor site (OR = 1.85, p = 0.045), mucinous/signet ring cell histology (OR = 4.17, p = 0.007), and CK20-low expression (OR = 2.4, p = 0.011).

From the aforementioned immunohistochemistry markers, only CK7 (OR = 3.1, p = 0.025) and SATB2-low (OR = 2.94, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with survival < 5 years.

Among traditional prognosticators, MMR deficiency has been significantly associated with survival > 5 years (OR = 3.03, p = 0.044), low UICC stage (OR = 3.12, p = 0.017), high grade (OR = 2.63, p = 0.038), right-sided tumor (OR = 8.65, p < 0.001), and CK20-low expression (OR = 5.36, p < 0.001). Right-sided tumors were prone to be high grade (OR = 2.5, p = 0.002), mucinous/signet ring (OR = 5.56, p = 0.002), CK20-low (OR = 2.8, p < 0.001), and there was a borderline significant association with survival < 5 years (OR = 1.61, p = 0.059). Advanced stage (UICC III + IV) was significantly associated with high grade (OR = 3.08, p < 0.001) and survival < 5 years (OR = 3.45, p < 0.001).

One tumor from the CK7 + cases was excluded from NGS analysis due to poor-quality DNA. Among the CK7 + cases, 12 were identified as SATB2-low status. Using the NGS custom panel, pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations were detected in various numbers of CK7 + cases: fourteen cases showed TP53 mutations, eight BRAF mutations, six APC mutations, five PIK3CA mutations, four FBXW7 and KRAS mutations, two ALK, ARID1A, PTEN, and SMAD4 mutations, and one ATM, ATR, BRCA2, CDK12, CIC, CTNNB1, ERBB2, FAT1, GNA11, GNAS, KIT, POLE, RB1, SMARCA4, and SMARCB1 mutation each. Among the 18 NGS CK7 + cases, 16 were MMR-proficient and 2 were MMR-deficient tumors, as determined by immunohistochemistry.

Among the 7 CRC cases strongly positive for MUC4, we identified pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations in various numbers of cases: four cases showed APC and KRAS mutations, three TP53 mutations, and two ERBB2, MTOR, and NTRK3 mutations each, and one ATM, BRCA2, CDK12, CHEK2, ESR1, FAT1, FBXW7, GNA11, GNAS, MAP2K1, NRAS, PIK3CA, PIK3R1, PTCH1, SMAD4, and SMARCA4 mutation each. Of these, 6 were MMR-proficient and 1 was MMR-deficient, with the latter exhibiting a notable number of mutations (ID20).

The OncoKB database was consulted to identify druggable genomic targets in CRC. Six cases (ID 5, 7, 8, 12, 17, 18) showed BRAF p.Val600Glu (V600E) mutations. One case (ID15) had a KRAS p.Gly12Cys mutation. Additionally, druggable oncogenic PIK3CA mutations were found in six patients (ID3 p.Glu545Lys; ID8 p.Glu542Lys; ID9 p.His1047Arg; ID12 p.Arg93Leu and p.Arg818Cys; ID16 p.Glu545Lys; ID23 p.His1047Leu and p.Arg93Gln).

All the cases with BRAF V600E mutations were female with MMR-proficient tumors, five out of them were right sided, five out of them died on CRC (see Table 4) with mean OS = 1.72 years and CSS = 1.52 years. Among 13 patients with CK7 + BRAF V600E wild type tumors, there were 11 MMR-proficient and 2 MMR-deficient CRCs, and 7 patients died with mean OS = 3.7 years and CSS = 2.28 years.

Discussion

CRC is an important cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality characterized by prognosis variability. Approximately 62% and 56–59% of patients in all stages achieve 5- and 10-year survival, respectively8. Additionally, to histomorphological grade and TNM stage, many proteomic and molecular prognostic markers have been extensively studied.

Typical colon mucosa and the majority of colorectal cancers (CRCs) exhibit SATB2 expression, CK20 expression, and CK7 negativity. CK7 expression is found in only 0–22% of CRCs3. A CRC prognosis worsens in cases that gain aberrant “non-colorectal” expression of CK73,4. Loss of “colorectal-specific” SATB2 has also been identified as an adverse prognostic factor in CRC4,9. Given that SATB2-high status has been associated with a favorable response to chemotherapy9, the loss of SATB2 expression carries significant negative prognostic implications. SATB2 expression is more frequently observed in colorectal cancer (CRC) compared to small bowel adenocarcinoma10.

In addition to the previously described association between CK7 expression and SATB2-low status, we found a significant association of these with extraintestinal mucins (MUC5AC, MUC6) and claudin 18 expression. Mucins are a family of high-molecular-weight glycoproteins primarily synthesized by epithelial cells. MUC5AC and MUC6 are members of the family of secreted mucins. According to the literature, the prognostic impact of MUC5AC is unclear: there are studies describing a beneficial impact and an association with lower regional lymph node metastasis rates11–13. On the other hand, the expression has also been linked to poor differentiation, advanced stage, and a poor prognosis with variable levels of significance14,15. Our study found no impact on survival due to the small number of MUC5AC-positive cases (7/285 = 2.4%). Despite the small number of CRC cases that were MUC5AC+, there was a significant association with high grade, proximal tumor location, and “non-colorectal” markers, i.e., CK7+ and SATB2-low status, suggesting that MUC5AC expression may be a negative prognostic indicator.

MUC6 was originally called gastric mucin due to its abundant expression in the gastric mucosa. However, in the colon mucosa, MUC6 expression is absent or minimal16, although it has been documented in up to 100% of sessile serrated adenomas15,17. Few studies are available that have examined the prognostic role of MUC6 in CRC. Betge et al. describe its rare expression as having a beneficial prognostic impact on CRC11. In an in vitro experiment, MUC6 was described as an inhibitor of epithelial tumor cell invasion18. Our study was limited by the small number of MUC6 + cases (11/285 = 3.9%); as such, we failed to show any prognostic significance. Nonetheless, MUC6 expression was significantly associated with right-sided tumors, MMR-deficiency, and a “non-colorectal” immunoprofile (i.e., CK7+, CK20-low, SATB2-low).

Claudins are integral proteins found on cell surfaces, playing a vital role in forming tight junctions that regulate the passage of molecules between cells. Various claudins exhibit distinct expression patterns across different tissues, including tumors. Downregulation of claudin 18 has been documented in gastric intestinal metaplasia, gastric adenoma, and intestinal-type gastric carcinoma, while its expression has been associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer19. Claudin 18 isotype 2 presents a promising therapeutic target in gastric cancer, with ongoing clinical trials involving the anti-claudin-18.2 drug zolbetuximab20. Additionally, claudin 18 expression has been linked to diffusely growing gastric carcinoma (Borrmann Type 4) and gastrointestinal signet ring cell carcinoma21, findings that are supported by our results. Larger studies and meta-analysis found no significant impact of claudin 18.2 on gastric cancer survival11,22. In non-ampullary small bowel adenocarcinoma, claudin 18 expression was associated with MUC5AC positivity and CK7 and CDX-2 negativity; there was a non-significant trend towards worse cancer-specific survival in claudin-18-positive cases23. Few studies have been performed to explore the prognostic role of claudin 18 in CRC. Matsuda et al. described an association between claudin 18 expression and the gastric/non-colorectal immunophenotype, i.e., MUC5AC positivity and CDX-2 negativity; claudin 18 expression was linked to poor prognosis24. Similar to Arpa et al.'s findings in small bowel adenocarcinoma23, we observed an association between claudin 18 expression and CK7 positivity, as well as SATB2-low status in CRC. However, we also found that claudin 18 expression had no significant impact on survival in our study.

MUC4 is a transmembrane mucin protein that is normally expressed in the epithelial cells of the respiratory and colon mucosa, and its overexpression has been documented in several malignancies15. MUC4 proteins are present on the goblet cells and epithelial cells found in the mucosa of normal human small and large intestines. MUC4 expression has been implicated in enhancing malignant tumor properties through ultrastructural changes in epithelial cells25 and modulation of the HER2/ErbB2 receptor tyrosine kinase26. These mechanisms have been described in pancreatic27 and gallbladder28 cancer. Furthermore, MUC4-mediated pathways have been associated with apoptotic resistance29,30, potentially facilitating tumor development and progression5.

There is evidence of frequent loss of MUC4 expression in CRC31, which is in line with our results. Pai et al. suggest that MUC4 can be downregulated by the WNT/β-catenin pathway, which is present in the majority of CRCs32. Although it is negative in most CRCs, MUC4+ may signify a worse prognosis when present5,33. Since we found only a single study analyzing the prognostic role of MUC4 using immunohistochemistry5, we decided to use the same cut-off value for considering a sample as positive. Using this (75%) relatively high cut-off value, we found an insignificant trend for shortened OS (p = 0.12) and a significantly shorter CSS (p = 0.044) despite the low proportion of high positive cases (n = 7, 2.5%). MUC4 expression was associated with further histological adverse prognostic signs (i.e., high grade and a mucinous/signet ring cell morphology). It is worth noting that Shanmugam et al. reported MUC4 positivity in ≥ 75% of tumor cells in 25% (33/132 cases), which is a notably higher proportion compared to our findings (2.5%)5, despite both studies employing the same anti-MUC4 antibody clone (8Q7). This discrepancy remains unclear from a biological perspective. However, it’s important to recognize that the number of studies analyzing MUC4 in CRC is still limited, and significant variability in MUC4 expression across different populations with CRC cannot be ruled out. However, despite the limited number of positive cases in our study, our results still attained prognostic significance, akin to the observations made by Shanmugam et al. However, MUC4 expression has been described as an epiphenomenon of WNT/β-catenin pathway activation (driven by an APC gene mutation)31. Using NGS, we found APC mutations in 3 out of 7 cases with strong MUC4 positivity.

The genetic basis of colorectal carcinoma was originally described by Vogelstein and Fearon as a multistep process of activation and inactivation of several oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, respectively. The original descriptions include the well-known adenoma-carcinoma sequence, frequent chromosomal aberrations, and involvement of the RAS, TRK, TP53, and APC genes34. Later, sporadic CRC development was linked to chromosomal (CIN) instability, microsatellite instability (MSI), and CpG methylator pathway35. Many gene mutations have been described across subtypes, i.e., APC, TP53, BRAF, KRAS, TTN, PIK3CA, FBXW7, SMAD4, NRAS, TCF7L2, GPC6, CTNNB1, ACVR2A, TGFBR2, MSH3, MSH6, MYOB1, TCF7L2, CASP8, CDC27, FZD3, MIER3, TCERG1, MAP7, and PTPN1236.

In our study, we performed an NGS analysis of the most important genes associated with solid tumors in a subgroup of CRCs having a worse prognosis and for the “non-colorectal” immunophenotype, i.e., CK7 expression, SATB2-loss and eventual gain of extraintestinal proteomic features (i.e., MUC5AC, MUC6, and claudin 18), as well as in cases with high MUC4 expression. We looked for genetic signatures that could be associated with these particular proteomic phenotypes. In line with Vogelstein’s genetic CRC paradigm, the results of our NGS DNA analysis revealed multiple oncogenic mutations from the usual spectrum of CRC-associated drivers; TP53, APC, BRAF, KRAS, PIK3CA, FBXW7, and SMAD4 were the most prevalent genetic alterations. TP53, KRAS, APC, PIK3CA, and FBXW7 were also described as usual somatic mutations in a pioneering study on CRC37. Amongst studies from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and other genetic studies from various populations, these mutations have been described in the following percentages of CRC cases: 24–82% APC, 47–74% TP53, 34–54% KRAS, 10–35% PIK3CA, 4–15% BRAF, 11–15% FBXW7, and 5–23% SMAD438–42.

In six cases, the BRAF p.Val600Glu (V600E) mutation was detected. In BRAF V600E-mutated colorectal cancer, treatment with encorafenib + cetuximab was classified at level 1, while encorafenib + panitumumab was categorized at level 2 in the OncoKB database43. Interestingly, six out of nineteen (31.6%) CK7 + tumors exhibited the BRAF V600E mutation, all of which were in female patients with MMR-proficient SATB2-low tumors and poor survival rates (OS = 1.72 years, CSS = 1.52 years). This mutation has been reported to occur in a smaller proportion of colorectal carcinomas in large datasets and is associated with poor prognostic outcomes. There are 11% of BRAF V600E-mutated CRCs in meta-analyses44,45, and 1454/11,536 (12.6%) of CRCs in the Czech Republic (unpublished data from Czech Reference Laboratories). BRAF V600E mutations are rarer in MMR-proficient compared to MMR-deficient CRCs. Toon et al. described significantly worse prognoses in BRAF V600E mutated and MMR-proficient CRCs compared to mismatch repair-proficient BRAF wild type, mismatch repair-deficient BRAF V600E mutant, and mismatch repair-deficient BRAF wild type46. Research focusing on the association between “non-intestinal” CK7-expression, BRAF V600E mutations, and MMR status would be highly valuable in larger datasets, though it is challenging due to the overall rarity of CK7-expressing CRCs. Loupakis et al. identified CK7 expression in 12.9% of 155 BRAF V600E mutated CRCs, and the CK7 was associated with worse survival (HR = 2.17, 95% CI 1.10–4.29, p = 0.026)47.

Focusing on further druggable mutations, one case with KRAS p.Gly12Cys was found, which has been described to predict response to dagrasib + Cetuximab, Adagrasib + Panitumumab, Sotorasib + Cetuximab, Sotorasib + Panitumumab at level 243. Of note, all cases with druggable BRAF and KRAS mutations were CK7 + and MUC4low. However, the cohort size analyzed by NGS is too small to facilitate statistical analysis. RLY-2608, a newly described inhibitor of PI3Kα48, a lipid kinase encoded by PIK3CA, has been categorized at level 4 for all solid tumors harboring any oncogenic PIK3CA mutation43.

In conclusion, findings from our NGS vary from the spectrum of established CRC associations with somatic mutations only in terms of enriched BRAF V600E mutation in tumors expressing CK7 comparing our frequency with the literature and databases. However, significant prognostic features were noted using immunohistochemistry, especially CK7 positivity, and loss of SATB2 expression; additionally, these hallmarks were associated with the expression of non-colorectal mucin MUC5AC, MUC6, and claudin 18, possible target of newly established biological therapy. CRC with these “aggressive immunophenotypes” had oncogenic driver mutations typical of CRC. Further investigations using a larger cohort of CRC patients with a “non-colorectal immunophenotype” and high MUC4 expression would be of great interest.
