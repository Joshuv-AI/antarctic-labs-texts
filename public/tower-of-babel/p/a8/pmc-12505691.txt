Investigation of the effect of low torque and speed values on postoperative pain after root canal treatment: a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Betül Karaağaç, Ahmet Demirhan Uygun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to investigate the effect of using low torque and speed values in root canal shaping on post-operative pain in asymptomatic, nonvital, mandibular first molars.MethodsThis randomised clinical trial included 80 patients. Patients were divided into four groups according to different torque and speed values of the Fanta V Taper Gold rotary file system used during root canal shaping: 1 N torque and 250 rpm speed, 1 N torque and 350 rpm speed, 2 N torque and 250 rpm speed, and 2 N torque and 350 rpm speed. Root canal preparation time (in seconds) was recorded during shaping. Patients recorded their post operative pain intensity on a 10 cm visual analogue scale at 6th, 24th, 48th, 72nd hours and 7th day. Chi-square test was used to compare gender, tooth localisation and presence/absence of periapical lesions, Kruskal-Wallis test was used for age comparison, Friedman and Wilcoxon tests were used for comparisons of groups over time, and Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons between different torque and speed groups. (P < 0.05)ResultsAccording to the results of statistical analysis, low torque and speed values did not cause a significant difference in post operative pain level at different time intervals after the procedure. Root canal preparation time at the time of the procedure was significantly longer in the group shaped with 1 N, 250 RPM. There was no significant difference in post procedure analgesic drug intake according to different torque and speed values.ConclusionWithin the limitations of this study, it was determined that the application of torque and speed lower than the values specified by the manufacturer did not provide superiority in post operative pain after the procedure, and low torque and speed values prolonged the preparation time during the procedure.Trial registrationThe study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University on 2023-02-03, with the decision number 2023/72. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06309095) on 2024-02-21. All study protocols were predefined and strictly followed, with no deviations from the original methodology.Keywords: Speed, Root canal treatment, Post-operative pain, Torque

Background

Root canal treatment is a widely performed procedure aimed at eliminating organic and inorganic debris, microorganisms, and their by-products from the root canal system in teeth diagnosed with irreversible pulpitis or pulp necrosis. The key elements for successful RCT include effective cleaning of the root canal system, shaping within biological limits, hermetic filling, and proper restoration. Mechanical preparation combined with irrigation solutions is used to achieve effective canal cleaning [1].

To facilitate shaping procedures and reduce treatment time, motorized systems have been developed. Endodontic motors designed for NiTi rotary instruments maintain a constant rotation speed regardless of canal conditions by controlling the torque value. Torque is defined as the force required to rotate the instrument during canal shaping [2]. Torque is a critical parameter that must be adjusted according to the values required by different instruments. The torque applied to the file should be sufficient to cut dentin effectively, yet low enough to prevent permanent deformation of the instrument. The optimal torque setting for each file is specified by the manufacturer. At high torque values, the instrument actively cuts but may bind within the canal, leading to deformation or fracture [2]. In contrast, low torque values enhance tactile feedback, enable longer instrument usage, and provide safer shaping [3]. However, low torque may prolong treatment time, reduce cutting efficiency, and increase the risk of operator-induced stress and instrument fracture. Increased frictional forces from operator stress elevate the temperature within the canal, potentially causing post operative (post-op) discomfort [4]. Speed, if set too high, can improve cutting efficiency but may result in loss of control, reduced tactile sensation, or alterations in canal curvature during shaping [4]. Each NiTi file system is designed with specific torque and speed recommendations determined by the manufacturer to optimize performance and minimize the risk of instrument failure.

Mechanical performance of NiTi instruments can vary according to design, alloy composition, manufacturing processes, and kinematics, which may influence their clinical safety and efficiency. Previous in vitro research compared different reciprocating instruments and reported significant differences in cyclic fatigue resistance, torsional resistance, and flexibility [5]. One such system, the Fanta V Taper Gold (Fanta Dental, Shenzhen, China) rotary file system, comprises six files, including three shaping files (SX, S1, S2) and three finishing files (F1, F2, F3). The SX, S1, and S2 files feature convex triangular cross-sectional designs that enhance cutting efficiency through modified alloy technology, while the F1, F2, and F3 files employ a triangular cross-sectional design to improve fracture resistance. The manufacturer recommends using these files at 2 N torque and 350 rpm [6].

Despite advancements in endodontics, post-op pain remains one of the most common complications of root canal treatment. The prevalence of post-op pain after RCT ranges from 3% to 58% [7]. Studies have examined various rotary file systems, preparation techniques, file kinematics, manufacturing processes, designs, and single versus sequential file systems regarding their impact on post-op pain.

During the literature review, no study was found, to the best of our knowledge, that directly compared the torque and speed values of rotary instruments used in root canal treatment in relation to their effect on post-operative pain. Higher torque values have been associated with an increased risk of dentinal cracks, and many clinicians prefer lower torque settings to minimize the risk of instrument fracture and dentinal defects. However, low torque may require greater operator pressure and increase frictional forces, which could potentially lead to patient discomfort. Despite these contrasting considerations, the specific impact of torque and speed settings on post-operative pain remains unverified. Therefore, the research question was formulated using the PICO framework (Patient/Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome): Does the use of rotary instruments at low torque and speed values in nonvital mandibular first molars requiring root canal treatment lead to a significant reduction in post-operative pain compared to the torque and speed values recommended by the manufacturer? Accordingly, this study aims to investigate whether low torque and speed settings affect post-operative pain levels in asymptomatic, nonvital mandibular first molars. The null hypothesis is that variations in torque and speed values will not result in significant differences in post-operative pain following endodontic treatment.

Methods

This randomised clinical trial was planned as a single-centre and single-blinded study in Afyonkarahisar University of Health Sciences, Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Endodontics. For the study, approval was obtained from Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Clinical Research Ethics Committee with the decision dated 2023-02-03 and numbered 2023/72 from the category of ‘Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee’. After ethics committee approval was completed, clinical trials were registered on the website (http://www.clinicaltrials.gov, NCT06309095) on 2024-02-21 and CONSORT guidelines were followed.

All patients were informed about the study before starting the treatment process. It was stated that the treatment to be performed was not different from routine root canal treatment, and that the different torque and speed values to be applied in the shaping protocol were lower than the value recommended by the manufacturer. Patients to be included in the study were informed both verbally and in writing with the ‘Informed Voluntary Consent’ form and their signatures were obtained.

G*Power programme (G*Power 3.1 for Macintosh; Heinrich Heine, Universitat Dusseldorf, Dusseldorf, Germany) was used to determine the number of patients to be included in the study. According to the G-Power analysis results, a total of 80 patients were included with an α value of 0.05 and a power scale of 80%, with 20 patients per group. Randomised sequence generation and allocation concealment were performed by a person not included in the study. A web program, Research Randomizer (https://www.randomizer.org/), was used for simple randomisation of the 80 patients included in the study as an equal number between the groups without any restriction. In the application, numbers ranging from 1 to 80 were randomly divided into four groups. For the allocation concealment procedure, the study group assigned to each patient was written on envelopes, which remained confidential until the start of treatment. However, the treating physician was not blinded.

Treatment protocol

The treatment of the patients included in the study was planned to be performed by a single physician (B.K.) who was in the last year of his speciality training in the department of endodontics. Before starting the treatment, each tooth was determined to be nonvital by negative response to electric pulp test (Sybron Dental Specialties, CA, ABD) and cold test (Pulp Spray, Cerkamed, Stalowa Wola, Poland).

All patients underwent mandibular local anaesthesia with articaine hydrochloride (Maxicaine, VEM İlaç San. ve Tic. A.Ş., Istanbul) containing 1:100,000 adrenaline. After caries removal of all selected mandibular first molars, endodontic access cavities were prepared under rubberdam (Dura Dam, Top Glove, Malaysia) isolation. After the endodontic access cavity was opened, the working lengths of the canals were determined with the help of a Woodpex III (Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co, Guilin, China) apex locator using a number 10 Dentsply C + file (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). The determined working length was confirmed with the help of periapical radiography taken by parallel method.

Root canal preparation was performed according to the randomization group assigned to each patient, as indicated in concealed envelopes. Accordingly, the torque and speed settings specified in the envelope were adjusted on the VDW.Silver Reciproc endomotor (VDW, Munich, Germany) prior to treatment. In this way, root canal procedures were standardized in full compliance with the randomly assigned parameters for each patient.

The file systems used in root canal preparation were disposable for each patient and were not reused during any other procedure. During shaping, the canals were irrigated with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl, Microvem AF, Istanbul, Turkey) using a number 30 irrigation needle (Maxi Probe; Dentsply Sirona) at each file change. Canal shaping was performed until no visible debris remained, without standardization of canal enlargement. All instruments required to achieve a clean canal were used at full working length. After root canal preparation, 5.25% NaOCl, 17% ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA, Microvem AF, Istanbul, Turkey) and distilled water were applied at 5 ml each. All solutions used in the irrigation procedure were injected 2 mm shorter than the working length. The root canals were dried with sterile paper cones and filled with gutta-percha and AH Plus canal filling (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) using the lateral condensation technique. The access cavity was restored and periapical radiographs were taken to check for missing canal filling or overfilling. No patient was excluded from the study because no incomplete canal filling or overflow was detected in the treated patients.

Post-op pain assessment

Post procedure pain levels of the patients were evaluated using VAS. Patients were given a 10 cm long chart to record their pain levels at 6th, 24th, 48th and 72nd hours and at the end of 1 st week. Patients were told that the starting line at the left end of the straight line on the chart represented the absence of pain and the ending line at the right end represented the most unbearable pain. Patients were told how to mark their pain levels and were asked to mark their pain levels on the chart at specified times. Pain was categorised as no pain (0), mild pain (1–3), moderate pain (4–6) and severe pain (7–10) [8]. After one week, the patients were recalled for follow-up and their visual analog scale (VAS) forms were evaluated.

It was explained that the patients might feel pain after the procedure, and 400 mg ibuprofen was prescribed to the patients to be used only in case of severe pain. It was stated that the patients should note whether they used ibuprofen or not and the amount of use on the lower part of the VAS scale.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 (IBM, NY, USA), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. The results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). The Chi-square test was applied to compare gender, tooth localization, and the presence or absence of periapical lesions, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for age comparisons. The normality of the pain data was assessed using the Shapiro Wilk test, which revealed that the data were not normally distributed; hence, non-parametric tests were applied. Accordingly, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare groups at each time point, and the Friedman and Wilcoxon tests were employed to compare time points within each group.

Twenty patients who did not fulfil the inclusion criteria were excluded from the study. The 80 patients who met the inclusion criteria were equally distributed to the four study groups. Discussion

In this randomized clinical trial, the effect of low torque and speed on post-op pain in asymptomatic mandibular first molars was investigated. According to the findings of this study, varying torque and speed settings during root canal treatment did not significantly influence post-op pain. Consequently, the null hypothesis of the study was accepted. Since no prior studies have explored the specific impact of torque and speed values on post-op pain, the results of the current study cannot be directly compared to previous literature.

Post-op pain following endodontic treatment has a multifactorial etiology. It is commonly caused by acute inflammation in the periapical region, triggered by chemical, mechanical, microbial damage, or the host’s immune response [9]. While post-op pain is frequently encountered after root canal treatment, certain factors prior to the procedure may influence the intensity of pain. These include variables such as age, gender, tooth type and location, pre-procedural pulp status, presence of a periapical lesion, swelling, and fistula formation [10]. Although numerous studies have attempted to interpret the role of these factors, their influence on post-op pain remains unclear due to inconsistencies in study groups and methodologies [11]. In this study, no significant differences were observed between the groups regarding gender, age, tooth number, or the presence/absence of periapical lesions. Previous research on post-op pain has highlighted the challenges in accurately assessing pain due to its subjective and multifactorial nature [8].

Rotary file systems, which have become routine in endodontic practice, play a critical role in facilitating treatment and reducing treatment time when used with endodontic motors. All files (S1, S2, F1, F2 and F3) in the Fanta V Taper Gold rotary file system mentioned in the materials and methods of the study were preferred because the manufacturer recommends the same torque and speed. In a study in the literature examining the cyclic fatigue resistance of Fanta V Taper Gold files, which were developed similar to the ProTaper Gold system, it was reported that Fanta V Taper Gold files showed a significantly longer fracture time compared to ProTaper Gold files and caused a threefold difference in the number of cycles to fracture [12]. In the related study, it was stated that Fanta V Taper Gold files are more cost-effective than ProTaper Gold files and provide safe use [12].

Various rotary file systems and kinematics have been extensively examined in relation to post-op pain following endodontic treatment. Studies have explored the effects of using rotary instruments versus hand files, rotational versus reciprocal file systems, and single-file versus multi-file systems on post-op pain [8, 13]. However, the effect of different speed and torque settings on the endodontic motor, which controls the rotary file’s movement within the canal, has not been previously investigated.

Dane et al. [14] examined dentin cracking at different torque levels using the ProTaper Universal (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) rotary instrument system, reporting that higher torque values resulted in more dentin cracks. This was attributed to the increased stress on the dentin surface in the high torque group. Altunbaş and Gürsu [15] investigated the fracture resistance of teeth at the torque levels recommended by the manufacturer of the ProTaper Universal rotary file system, finding that higher torque values significantly reduced the fracture resistance of teeth compared to lower torque values. Using lower torque values during root canal preparation is considered safer by practitioners to prevent instrument fracture and dentin crack formation [14, 16]. Despite the advantages of low torque, it has been reported that it may increase patient discomfort by forcing the operator to apply more pressure and increasing frictional forces [4].

Post-op pain after endodontic treatment results from inflammation of the periapical region, caused by mechanical, chemical, or microbial factors during treatment. While clinicians can control certain aspects of canal shaping with different file systems, kinematics, and preparation techniques, controlling the virulence of microorganisms spreading to periapical tissues is almost impossible. Studies have shown that no shaping method can entirely prevent apical debris extrusion [17]. Altunbaş and Toyoğlu [18] investigated apical extrusion using the ProTaper Universal rotary file system at torque levels specified by the manufacturer. In their study on extracted premolar teeth, they found no significant difference in apical extrusion between high and low torque values within the manufacturer-recommended torque range. It was suggested that the difference between torque values was minor, and the lower torque caused the instrument to rotate counterclockwise continuously, reducing the tendency to screw in and the risk of over-instrumentation [18]. In the present study, the absence of significant differences in post-op pain between groups with the same speed but different torque values may be attributed to minimal differences in apical extrusion. The literature indicates that debris extrusion is the most critical determinant of post-op pain. However, in vitro studies on debris extrusion cannot fully replicate clinical conditions due to the absence of factors such as periodontal tissue pressure and host immune response, and thus cannot provide conclusive evidence regarding post-op pain [19].

Low torque and speed values have disadvantages, such as reducing cutting efficiency and slowing down instrument progression [20]. One parameter analyzed in the present study was preparation time. It was observed that as torque and speed values increased, the average preparation time decreased, and the group with 1 N torque and 250 RPM had a significantly longer preparation time compared to the other three groups. Pedulla et al. [21] examined the preparation time of standardized plastic teeth at three different rotational speeds (350 rpm, 250 rpm, and 150 rpm) using Mtwo (VDW, Munich, Germany) rotary files. They found that the preparation time was significantly longer at 150 rpm compared to 350 rpm and 250 rpm, with no significant difference between 350 rpm and 250 rpm. However, in contrast to Pedulla et al. [19], the present study found a significant difference in preparation time between the 250 rpm and 350 rpm groups at 1 N torque. The variation in findings is likely due to the differences between in vitro environments and clinical conditions, such as the use of plastic teeth.

Pak and White [22] reported in their review that the average prevalence of pain 24 h after endodontic treatment was approximately 40%, while pain prevalence after one week was around 11%. Pain decreased markedly within the first two days, dropping to 10% or less by the 7th day [21]. Similarly, Figueiredo et al. [23] evaluated post-op pain in root canal treatments completed using the single cone and lateral condensation techniques in teeth with apical periodontitis. They found that 40% of participants experienced pain within the first 24 h, regardless of the filling method used, with a gradual decrease in pain until the 7th day, when the incidence dropped to 2%. In the present study, the results were comparable, with 41.3% of patients reporting pain at 24 h, which decreased to 11.4% by the end of the first week.

Various methods are suggested in the literature for managing post-op pain during and after root canal treatment [24, 25]. In this study, NSAIDs were prescribed to ensure standardization, as they are simple to administer and widely accepted by patients. Specifically, 400 mg of ibuprofen was prescribed, consistent with numerous studies on post-op pain in the literature [8, 13, 26]. The analysis revealed no significant differences in pain relief between the groups who received analgesics.

The VAS, the most commonly used pain scale in clinical studies within the endodontic literature [27], was employed in this study. The VAS scale is favored for its simplicity, ease of use, and clear understanding by patients. Its advantages include applicability across all age groups. However, it remains a subjective measure, entirely dependent on the patient’s self-assessment, and its accuracy cannot be definitively proven.

One strength of this study is its randomized, blinded design, with patients being randomly assigned to groups. This methodology provides a high level of evidence, as the rotary file system used during treatment, canal irrigation, and canal filling procedures were performed in a standardized manner by a single practitioner, reducing factors that could compromise standardization. A limitation of the study is that the practitioner performing the root canal treatment was not blinded to the torque and speed values being used.

Newly developed NiTi rotary file systems allow higher torque and speed settings compared to older systems, thanks to advances in alloy technology, increased flexibility, and different kinematics. However, one limitation of this study is the potential risk of complications, such as instrument fracture, perforation, or apical transportation, when shaping is performed at higher torque and speed values than those recommended by the manufacturer. In this study, only the effects of low torque and speed values on post-op pain were analyzed. Additionally, the study tested only one file system (Fanta V Taper Gold), which limits the generalizability of the findings to other rotary systems, as mechanical performance and metallurgical characteristics can vary considerably between systems [5]. Further clinical studies using different rotary systems are needed to verify whether the present findings are consistent across instruments with varying designs, kinematics, and metallurgical properties.

Postoperative pain in endodontics is a complex phenomenon involving multiple mechanisms, including inflammatory responses triggered by mechanical, chemical, and microbial factors. Mechanical parameters such as torque and speed during instrumentation can theoretically influence pain by affecting dentinal integrity and periapical tissue stress. Lower torque settings might be expected to reduce stress on dentinal walls, potentially minimizing microcrack formation and subsequent nociceptive stimulation. However, the present study found no significant reduction in postoperative pain with lower torque (1 Ncm) compared to higher torque (2 Ncm). In our opinion, one possible explanation could be that the torque difference between the groups was insufficient to induce clinically meaningful differences in dentinal microcrack development or hydrodynamic effects within the root canal system. Moreover, the increased instrumentation time observed in the low-torque group may have offset potential benefits, as longer operative durations could lead to increased patient discomfort through prolonged tissue manipulation or irritation. These findings highlight that while mechanical factors are important, postoperative pain is multifactorial and influenced by a balance of variables, including treatment duration, microbial control, host immune response, and individual pain perception. Future studies employing larger torque differentials and incorporating objective measures of dentinal damage could further elucidate these relationships.

In assessing the clinical relevance of the statistically significance observed in this study, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Due to the limited sample size, the results cannot be assumed to be fully generalizable to the broader population or directly applicable in clinical practice. Since the torque and speed values influence the instrument’s cutting efficiency and ability to shape the canal, their effect on in-canal cleaning and disinfection should also be considered.

In this context, there is a need for further research examining the effects of different torque and speed settings across various rotary systems in clinical studies with diverse methodologies and measurement approaches. The collective outcomes of such studies will help establish clearer clinical guidelines and optimize rotary instrumentation techniques in endodontics.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this clinical study, the low torque and speed value did not reduce post-op pain in asymptomatic, mandibular first molars. However, the low torque and speed value prolonged the preparation time, resulting in increased treatment time. Despite the advantages of low torque and speed in in-vitro studies, such as not decreasing the fracture resistance of teeth and causing less cyclic fatigue in files, clinical studies on low torque and speed are very insufficient. In future studies, the effect of low torque and speed values and even high torque and speed values can be examined.
