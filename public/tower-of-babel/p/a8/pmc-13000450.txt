Structure of an initiation complex of mycobacterial helicase-nuclease AdnAB at a blunt double-strand break reveals local melting that engages the 3′ tracking strand at the ratchet pawl of the helicase motor
Authors: Garrett M Warren, M Jason de la Cruz, Yehuda Goldgur, Stewart Shuman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycobacterial AdnAB is a heterodimeric helicase-nuclease that initiates homologous recombination by resecting double-strand breaks. The AdnB subunit hydrolyzes ATP to drive single-nucleotide steps of 3′-to-5′ translocation of AdnAB on the tracking DNA strand via a ratchet-like mechanism. AdnB Trp325, which makes a π stack on a nucleobase 5′ of a flipped-out nucleoside, is the ratchet pawl without which ATP hydrolysis is mechanically futile. Here we report a cryo-EM (cryogenic electron microscopy) structure of AdnAB in complex with a blunt-ended DNA. AdnAB forms an initiation complex by melting five terminal base pairs and threading the splayed out 5′ and 3′ single strands into the AdnA nuclease domain and the AdnB motor domain, respectively. The melted 5-nucleotide 3′ tracking strand is in-gear with respect to the ratchet and pawl of the AdnB motor. An analogous π-stacking aromatic pawl is present in the motor subunit of the bacterial end-resection enzymes Bacillus AddAB and Escherichia coli RecBCD. Our results highlight a common theme whereby binding of the motor-nuclease to a blunt DSB end, in the absence of ATP hydrolysis, is coupled to local melting of the terminal base pairs that suffices to engage the “clutch” of the motor on the tracking strand.

Introduction

Mycobacterial AdnAB is an ATP-dependent helicase-nuclease that initiates homologous recombination by unwinding and resecting DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) [1, 2]. The AdnA and AdnB subunits are encoded in a two-gene operon that is transcriptionally upregulated in response to DNA damage [3–5]. AdnA (1045-aa) and AdnB (1095-aa) are paralogs composed of an N-terminal ATP-binding motor or motor-like domain and a C-terminal nuclease domain (Fig. 1A). AdnAB is present in 18% of bacterial genomes surveyed, with nearly all of the AdnAB expressing taxa belonging to the phylum Actinobacteria [6].

A model for DSB resection by AdnAB has been developed, based on ensemble and single-molecule biochemistry, mutagenesis, atomic force microscopy, and cryo-electron microscopy [1, 7–11], that entails sequential end-recognition, motor engagement, and translocation phases. Once the motor is engaged (i.e. by threading of a 3′ single-strand end into the AdnB helicase domain), AdnAB resects duplex DNA rapidly (∼450 bp/s on average in single-molecule assays) and processively (∼37 kbp average resection tract length) [10]. The average rate of DSB resection corresponds to the apparent kcat of 415 s−1 for ssDNA-dependent ATP hydrolysis in ensemble assays [7], implying that 1 bp is unwound by AdnAB per ATP hydrolyzed. There is a division of labor between the subunits whereby: (i) the AdnB motor domain drives translocation in the 3′-to-5′ direction on the 3′ tracking strand, effectively pumping the 3′ strand through the trailing AdnA motor-like domain and into the AdnB nuclease domain, which cleaves the displaced 3′ strand; and (ii) the displaced 5′ strand is threaded through the AdnA nuclease domain, leading to 5′ strand cleavage (Fig. 1A). An AdnA iron-sulfur cluster subdomain at the DNA Y-junction maintains the split trajectories of the unwound 5′ and 3′ strands [10].

Insights into the physical basis for the coupling of ATP hydrolysis to DNA unwinding emerged from a set of cryo-EM structures of DNA-free AdnAB and of AdnAB bound to a pre-melted Y-fork duplex DNA with 5′ and 3′ tails, which revealed the path of the 3′ tracking strand through the AdnB motor and a network of amino acid side chain contacts to the phosphates and nucleobases of a 7- to 8-nucleotide 3′ ssDNA tail. AdnB residues Trp325, Arg326, Phe254, and Leu142 stack on and between the nucleobases, acting as baffles to partition the 3′ ssDNA into discrete base-stacked segments. It is proposed that protein subdomain movements within the AdnB motor, synchronized to the ATP hydrolytic cycle, drive passage of the nucleobases over the amino acid baffles in the 3′ to 5′ direction, and the baffles prevent backward sliding in a pawl-and-ratchet-like fashion [10].

Mutational analysis of the AdnB•DNA interface identified Trp325 in helicase motif III as the singular essential constituent of the ratchet pawl, insofar as a W325A mutant AdnAB was inert in ATP-dependent DSB resection and defective for ATP-dependent translocation on ssDNA, notwithstanding that W325A AdnAB and wild-type AdnAB had the same specific activity in ssDNA-dependent ATP hydrolysis and similar Km values for the ssDNA activator of ATP hydrolysis [11]. The essential Trp325 makes a π stack on a nucleobase 5′ of a nucleoside that is flipped out between base-stacked segments of the tracking strand. Via conservative substitutions in which Trp325 was replaced by other aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, histidine) or by a gamma-branched aliphatic amino acid (leucine), we found that the phenylalanine and tyrosine variants retained DNA resection activity, while the His mutant was severely defective, and the leucine mutant was inert à la alanine [12]. A cryo-EM structure of the W325A AdnAB mutant bound to tailed duplex DNA revealed that the AdnB peptide segment comprising mutated motif III was disordered and the normally flipped out nucleobase reverted to a continuous base-stacked arrangement with its neighbors [12]. We concluded that π stacking of Trp325 over a tracking strand nucleobase elicits and stabilizes a flipped-out conformation that is necessary to create a ratchet pawl in the AdnB motor. There is no functionally analogous pawl within the AdnA motor-like domain [11]. Moreover, there is no direct evidence as yet that AdnA has an auxiliary motor activity or that AdnA hydrolyzes ATP during DSB resection, notwithstanding that the AdnA subunit can bind ATP and has a seemingly intact set of phosphohydrolase motifs in its ATP-binding site [10]. It is possible that AdnA provides a passive conduit for the tracking strand en route to the AdnB nuclease.

A key issue to be addressed is how AdnAB interacts with a DSB end to engage the motor by positioning the 3′ strand over the ratchet pawl. Two simple models can be invoked whereby: (i) engagement is a two-step process entailing ATP-independent binding to the DSB end, followed by ATP-dependent melting of the terminal base pairs; or (ii) engagement occurs in a single-step, independent of ATP binding or hydrolysis, in which binding of AdnAB to the DSB end is coupled to melting of the terminal base pairs. There is precedent for the latter mechanism in the case of Escherichia coli RecBCD and RecBC enzymes, where their binding to a blunt DSB end in the absence of ATP elicits unpairing of the terminal 4–6 bp, as gauged by KMnO4 sensitivity [13, 14]. A cryo-EM study of RecBCD bound to a blunt duplex substrate in the presence of magnesium, but no ATP, highlighted heterogeneity in the extent of end melting [15]. One class of RecBCD•DSB structures revealed 11-nt of 5′ terminal single-strand DNA and 4-nt of 3′ terminal single-strand DNA (with a segment of the melted 3′ strand being flexible and thereby unresolved). A second class of RecBCD•DSB complexes displayed melting of only four terminal base pairs [15].

In the case of AdnAB, atomic force microscopy imaging of the products of the reaction of AdnAB with BamHI-cut pUC19 DNA (comprising DSB ends with 4-nucleotide 5′ single-strand tails) in the presence of magnesium, but without ATP, revealed a predominance of linear double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) molecules with an AdnAB protein positioned at one of the DSB ends, and a minority of DNAs with proteins at both DSB ends [9]. These AdnAB•DSB complexes initiated unwinding of the linear plasmid when provided with ATP [9]. In an instructive experiment, it was shown that ATP-independent binding of AdnAB to a blunt DSB end of 5′ 32P-labeled SmaI-cut pUC19 allowed access of the 5′ strand to the AdnA nuclease active site, which quantitatively cleaved the 5′ strand to liberate 32P-labeled dinucleotide (pGG) and trinucleotide (pGGG) products [9, 11]. Because the cryo-EM structures of AdnAB bound to a pre-formed Y-junction showed that the AdnA nuclease cleaves the displaced 5′ strand two nucleotides from the ssDNA-dsDNA junction, we surmised that ATP-independent binding to the blunt DSB end was coupled to melting of four or five terminal nucleotides, which might suffice for the 3′ strand to be engaged by the ratchet pawl [11].

In the present study, we report a single-particle cryo-EM structure of AdnAB bound to a blunt DNA end that shows melting of five terminal base pairs and threading of the 5′ and 3′ strands into the AdnA nuclease and AdnB motor domains, respectively. In this “initiation complex”, the 5-nucleotide 3′ single strand fulfills its stacking interaction with Trp325 and makes contacts with other key amino acids needed to get the motor “in gear” to initiate duplex unwinding and resection. We also visualize and interrogate by mutagenesis the nucleobase contacts to AMPPNP in the AdnB motor.

Materials and methods

Recombinant AdnAB proteins

Helicase-active/nuclease-dead AdnAB heterodimer [1] was produced in E. coli BL21(DE3) by co-expression of His10Smt3-tagged AdnA(D934A) and untagged AdnB(D1014A) subunits and purified from soluble extracts by sequential nickel-agarose affinity chromatography, cleavage of the His10Smt3 tag by treatment with the Smt3-specific protease Ulp1, separation of tag-free AdnAB from His10Smt3 by passage over a second nickel-agarose column, and a final Superdex-200 gel filtration step [1, 10]. Nuclease-active AdnAB heterodimers with alanine substitutions in the adenosine-binding site of AdnB were produced by co-expression of His10-Smt3-tagged AdnA and untagged AdnB-Ala subunits. The protein concentrations were determined by using the BioRad dye reagent with BSA as the standard.

Preparation of the AdnAB•DNA complex

The DNA ligand was a 59-mer synthetic oligonucleotide designed to form a blunt-ended hairpin duplex structure (Fig. 1B). A mixture of 2 µM AdnA(D934A)-AdnB(D1014A), 10 µM DNA, 1 mM AMPPNP, and 2 mM MgCl2 in 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl was incubated for 30 min on ice. The sample was filtered through a 0.2 µm spin-filter and then used to prepare EM grids.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

Aliquots (3.5 µl) of a 0.3 mg/ml solution of AdnA(D934A)-AdnB(D1014A)•DNA complex were applied to UltrAuFoil 300 mesh R1.2/R1.3 grids (Quantifoil), which were plasma-cleaned for 120 s at 0.37 mbar and 15 mA in a ‘Pelco easiGlow’ glow discharge cleaning system (Ted Pella). Grids were blotted for 1.5 s with 100% humidity at 4°C and flash frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were collected on a 300 keV Titan Krios electron micoroscope (Thermo Fisher). Data were recorded on a Gatan K3 direct electron detector in an automated fashion using SerialEM [16], with exposures collected every 0.1 µm within a defocus range of −1.0 to −2.5 μm. Micrographs (n = 5126) were collected at an electron dose rate of 20 e− pixel−1 s−1 with a total exposure time of 3 s; intermediate frames were recorded every 0.075 s for a total of 40 frames, giving a total exposure dose of 52.9 e− Å−2 s−1.

Cryo-EM image processing

Movie frames were preprocessed in CryoSPARC Live, version 4.4.0 [17]. A total of 3404 of the 5126 exposures were accepted after threshold filtering in CryoSPARC Live. Drift correction was performed via Patch Motion Correction [17] using a maximum alignment resolution to 4 Å with 2 × Fourier-cropping from a super-resolution pixel size of 0.532 Å. Contrast transfer function parameters were estimated by CryoSPARC’s Patch CTF module [17]. The blob picker in CryoSPARC Live was employed, using a particle diameter range of 50–200 Å and a low-pass filter of 20 Å, to pick 2546 841 particles. Particles were binned by 3 and extracted using a 100-pixel box size, then subjected to a 50-class 2D classification. A total of 311 176 particles were manually selected from coherent particle classes. From these particles, several iterative rounds of 2D classification and subsequent 3D classification via ab initio 3D map generation were done to refine particle selection leading to a medium-resolution 3D coulomb potential map at 6.72 Å with 85 992 particles. After unbinning these particles, 85 176 particles were extracted at a box size of 300 pixels, and refined to 3.72 Å. These particles were used to train the Topaz autopicking program [18], from which 231 487 particles were subsequently found in 3195 micrographs. These were re-extracted at full resolution and subjected to several rounds of ab initio, 2D, and 3D classifications, as well as 10 heterogeneous refinement steps, until a nonuniform refinement of 68 963 particles produced a 3.43 Å map (Supplementary Fig. S1A). An earlier subset of 104 148 particles before the Topaz step above were processed independently in RELION-3.0.8 for one round of Bayesian polishing [19], then reimported into CryoSPARC. Five successive rounds of heterogeneous refinement were done to clean up the particle stack via 1 good class and 4 bad/decoy classes. Two iterative 3D classification steps, coupled with nonuniform refinement jobs, produced a 3.43 Å map. Particles were then grouped into separate exposure groups for optimizing CTF parameters and for aberration correction of image-beam shift data collection. These particles were used in a final nonuniform refinement to generate a 3.36 Å resolution map of AdnAB•DNA•AMPPNP (Supplementary Fig. S1B). The resolution of this map was estimated in CryoSPARC using the Fourier shell correlation criterion of 0.143 [20]. Local resolution (Supplementary Fig. S2A) was calculated by the ‘blocres’ program [21] implemented in CryoSPARC.

Model building and refinement

The model of the AdnAB•(blunt DNA)•AMPPNP complex was built by docking the structure of the AdnAB•(Y-fork DNA)•AMPPNP complex (PDB 6PPU) into the cryo-EM maps using Phenix [22]. The docked model was rebuilt and confirmed in O [23] based on the bulky amino acid side chains in the protein primary structure. The model was refined by real-space refinement in Phenix [22] by applying geometric and secondary structure restraints.

Results

Cryo-EM structure of AdnAB bound to a blunt-end duplex DNA

A helicase-active/nuclease-dead version of AdnAB with single alanine mutations in the AdnA and AdnB nuclease domain active sites [1] was preincubated with a blunt-ended hairpin duplex DNA substrate (Fig. 1B) in the presence of 1 mM AMPPNP and 2 mM magnesium, then applied to grids for single-particle cryo-EM analysis. A set of 104 148 2D particles (Supplementary Fig. S1) was used to generate a model of the DNA-bound AdnAB at an overall resolution of 3.36 Å (Supplementary Table S1). The structure is shown in stereo in Fig. 1B with the protein subunits depicted as semi-transparent cartoon models and the DNA as a stick model with a cartoon trace through the phosphodiester backbone. AMPPNP is situated in the AdnA motor domain, just as it was in the DNA-free AdnAB heterodimer and in the complex of AdnAB bound to a Y-fork DNA [10]. In the present structure, we see for the first time that AMPPNP also occupies the AdnB motor. As noted previously [10], the AdnB nuclease domain was not visible in DNA-bound complexes. The portion of the input blunt DNA that was modeled (highlighted in cyan in Fig. 1B) comprised an 11-bp duplex segment from which a 3-nucleotide 5′ single-strand tail and a 5-nucleotide 3′ single-strand tail emanate. The 5′ tail is threaded into the AdnA nuclease domain while the 3′ tail penetrates the AdnB motor domain. The nucleotides of the 3′ tail correspond to the terminal nucleotides of the blunt duplex, signifying that the binding of AdnAB to a blunt DSB end in the absence of ATP hydrolysis elicits melting of the terminal five base pairs. The 5′ ssDNA tail within the catalytically inactive AdnA nuclease domain is two nucleotides shorter than the 3′ tail; we presume that the 5′-terminal dinucleotide that exits the nuclease domain is disordered.

The salient insights from this structure are that the melted out 3′ tail (5′-pT−1pA−2pT−3pG−4pA−5–3′OH, with nucleotides numbered according to their positions relative to the duplex junction) is “in-gear” with respect to the ratchet pawl of the AdnB motor (Fig. 2). A stereo view of the 3′-DNA-AdnB interface highlights that Trp325 and Arg326 in motif III intercalate into the 3′ tail, with Trp325 making a π-stack on the A−2 nucleobase and Arg326 making a cation–π stack on the G−4 nucleobase, thereby flipping out the T−3 nucleobase. The 3′-terminal GpAOH dinucleotide adopts a base-stacked conformation in which the terminal A−5 nucleobase is book-ended by a π-stack on Phe254 and by orthogonal packing against His210 (Fig. 2). Additional protein contacts are made by Asn438 and Thr663 to the 1pA−2pT−3 phosphate and by Arg81 to the G−4pA−5 phosphate. Arg436 and His665 pack against the A−2 deoxynucleoside.

In sum, the AdnB motor interface with the melted 3′ strand of a blunt DSB end mimics that of the AdnB motor with the equivalent segment of a longer 3′ tail observed in the complexes formed by AdnAB on a preformed Y-fork DNA [10]. It is most noteworthy that the AdnB motif III peptide segment 323YGWRGS328 that includes intercalating residues Trp325 and Arg326 is disordered in the cryo-EM structure of the DNA-free AdnAB apoenzyme but became ordered and engaged to the 3′ tracking strand via intercalation and base flipping when AdnAB was bound to the pre-formed Y-fork DNA [10]. The previous findings that AdnB motif III was disordered when AdnA-AdnB(W325A) bound to the Y-fork DNA and that there was no flipped-out base in the 3′ tracking strand was taken to signify that the DNA-triggered conformational change in motif III depends on Trp325 and this conformational change sets the ratchet pawl. We can now infer from the present structure that the binding of AdnAB to a blunt DSB suffices to establish such an initiation-competent state.

ATP site in the AdnB motor

Mutational analyses of the signature ATP•Mg binding motifs in the AdnB motor domain implicated the AdnB subunit as solely responsible for the ssDNA-dependent ATPase, ATP-dependent nuclease, and DNA helicase activities of the AdnAB heterodimer in vitro [1, 7]. The in vivo DNA repair functions of AdnAB are also strictly dependent on AdnB’s ATPase activity [9]. AdnAB specifically utilizes ATP, dATP, GTP, or dGTP as substrates for ssDNA-dependent NTP phosphohydrolase, NTP-dependent nuclease, DNA helicase, and DSB resection activities [1, 7]. AdnAB is inactive with CTP and weakly active with UTP [1, 7]. The present cryo-EM structure of AdnAB engaged at a melted blunt DSB end is the first in which we visualize a nucleotide in the AdnB motor domain. As expected, the triphosphate moiety of AMPPNP is docked in the motif I P-loop 49GAGKT53 via main-chain amide contacts. The potentially instructive findings with respect to NTP specificity are that the adenine nucleobase is held in a π–π–π sandwich between Phe25 and Trp357 (Fig. 3). This stacking will presumably favor purines over pyrimidines based on the larger ring size of the former. The Thr28 hydroxyl group and the Gln31 side chain amide are poised to hydrogen bond with adenine-N6 (3.5 Å) and adenine-N7 (3.5 Å), respectively (Fig. 3). The same interactions would be feasible with guanine-O6 and guanine-N7. There are no contacts seen to the ribose hydroxyls of AMPPNP.

ATP site in the AdnB motor. Stereo view of AMPPNP (stick model with gray carbons and yellow phosphorus atoms) in the AdnB motor domain, highlighting AdnB contacts with the purine nucleobase. Selected amino acids are rendered as stick models with beige carbons.

To interrogate the contribution of the AdnB adenine contacts to AdnAB function, we introduced single-alanine substitutions in lieu of Phe25 and Trp357 and a double-alanine replacement of Thr28 and Gln31. Nuclease-active AdnAB heterodimers bearing AdnB subunit mutants F25A, W357A, and T28A•Q31A were purified in parallel with wild-type AdnAB (Fig. 4A) and assayed for DNA-dependent ATPase activity by enzyme titration in reactions containing 1 mM ATP and a 44-mer single-strand DNA cofactor (Fig. 4B). The specific activity of wild-type AdnAB (gauged by linear regression fit of the data) was 12.0 ± 0.9 nmol ATP converted to ADP per pmol of enzyme. The ATPase activity of the F25A mutant (11.8 ± 0.7 nmol/pmol) was unchanged vis-à-vis wild-type. The T28A•Q31A mutant (7.8 ± 0.6 nmol/pmol) was two-thirds as active as wild-type AdnAB. The W357A mutant (3.8 ± 0.1 nmol/pmol) was three-fold less active than wild-type AdnAB (Fig. 4B). Assay of the wild-type and W357A AdnAB enzymes for their ability to catalyze resection of blunt-end linear duplex pUC19 plasmid DNA revealed that the rate of resection at 20°C in the presence of 1 mM ATP was ∼9-fold slower in the case of the W357A mutant, as surmised from the similar levels of residual pUC19 after 10 s in the wild-type reaction and 90 s in the W357A reaction (Fig. 4C).

The screening assays of ATP hydrolysis and DSB resection by alanine mutants were performed at 1 mM ATP, which is in excess of the apparent Km for ATP of 28 µM determined for AdnAB resection velocity in a single-molecule assay format [10]. Given that the nucleobase contacts are remote from the site of phosphohydrolase chemistry, we suspected that any observed decrement in ATP hydrolysis or DSB resection activity by ATP-binding site mutants would stem from reduced affinity for the ATP substrate. To address this issue for the W357A mutant, we titrated ATP in the DSB resection assay, performed at 37°C, and entailing a 1 min reaction for wild-type AdnAB and a 2 min reaction for the W357A mutant (which afforded similar extents of resection in the presence of 1 mM ATP). As shown in Fig. 4D, omission of ATP resulted in no detectable resection. From the ATP titration profiles, we see that the extents of resection by wild-type AdnAB at 32 µM and 64 µM ATP were similar to those achieved by the W357A mutant at 125 µM and 250 µM ATP, indicative of approximately 4-fold reduced affinity for ATP.

To query whether the base-edge hydrogen bonds from Thr28 and Gln31 are determinants of purine specificity, we compared the DSB resection activity of wild-type AdnAB and the T28A•Q31A mutant in the presence of 1 mM ATP, GTP, UTP, and CTP. The two enzymes displayed similar properties, whereby purine NTPase were the best substrates, with GTP supporting more extensive resection than ATP, as gauged by the sizes of the DNA fragments generated (Fig. 4E). UTP was less active than the purines and CTP supported no detectable end resection (Fig. 4E). We surmise that nucleobase hydrogen bond contacts via Thr28 and Gln31 are not decisive with regard to AdnAB’s NTP preferences.

Discussion

Multi-subunit motor-nucleases such as Mycobacterium AdnAB, Bacillus AddAB, and E. coli RecBCD recognize, unwind, and resect DSB ends during homologous recombination in bacteria. The N-terminal motor domains of the respective AdnB, AddA, and RecB subunits hydrolyze ATP to drive rapid and processive 3′ to 5′ translocation of the AdnAB, AddAB, and RecBCD machines along the tracking DNA strand [7, 10, 24, 25]. The ATP hydrolysis is mechanically productive when oscillating protein domain motions synchronized with the ATPase cycle propel the DNA tracking strand forward by a single-nucleotide step, in a pawl-and-ratchet-like fashion. Our prior studies of AdnAB identified AdnB Trp325, which intercalates into the tracking strand and stacks on a nucleobase, as the singular essential constituent of the ratchet pawl, without which ATP hydrolysis on ssDNA is mechanically futile [11, 12].

Here we explored the question of how a bacterial motor-nuclease initiates productive unwinding when it encounters a blunt DSB end, by determining a cryo-EM structure of AdnAB that was exposed to a blunt-end hairpin duplex in the presence of magnesium and a nonhydrolyzable ATP analog AMPPNP. Our prior biochemical assays had suggested that four or five base pairs are unwound when AdnAB binds stably at a blunt DSB end in the absence of ATP [9]. We now show that AdnAB forms a bona fide initiation complex at a DSB end by melting out five terminal base pairs and threading the splayed out 5′ and 3′ single strands into the AdnA nuclease domain and the AdnB motor domain, respectively. The key point is that the melted 5-nucleotide 3′ tracking strand is “in-gear” with respect to the ratchet and pawl of the AdnB motor. Specifically, Trp325 intercalates into the 3′ strand and stacks on a nucleobase, resulting in flipping out of the adjacent nucleobase, essentially as occurs when AdnAB is engaged on a pre-formed Y-fork DNA substrate.

An analogous aromatic ratchet pawl is present in structures of Bacillus AddAB bound to pre-formed Y-fork DNAs [26] (see PBD 4CEJ), whereby AddA Phe446 stacks on a nucleobase on the 3′ tracking strand and flips out the adjacent nucleoside, In addition, the vicinal AddA residue Arg447 makes a cation–π stack on the nucleobase on the other side of the flipped out nucleoside, akin to what we see for Arg326 in AdnB. A structure solved of AddAB bound to a DSB with a 4-nucleotide 3′ single-strand overhang in the absence of magnesium or ATP showed threading of the 3′-tail into the AddA motor and stacking of Arg447 on a nucleobase of the tail but did not reveal any melting of the recessed double-strand junction [27] (see PDB 3U44). Thus, it is not yet clear the extent to which AddAB would melt out the end of a blunt DSB end.

In RecBCD•DNA complexes formed on Y-fork DNAs, the RecB Phe422 side chain stacks on a nucleobase on the 3′ tracking strand situated at the −2 position relative to the duplex junction and unstacks the adjacent nucleoside [28] (see PDB 6SJB), just as we see in the present AdnAB structure. As noted in the Introduction, there is biochemical and structural evidence that RecBCD binds and melts the end of a blunt DSB without a need for ATP hydrolysis [13–15]. In the RecBCD-blunt DSB initiation complex in which a 4-nucleotide 3′ single-strand is modeled in the RecB motor, Phe422 again stacks on the −2 nucleobase and flips out the −3 nucleoside [15] (see PDB 7MR4). We speculate that the ability of RecBCD to melt out as many as eleven 5′ nucleotides upon binding a blunt DSB, versus melting of only five base pairs in the AdnAB initiation complex, reflect the threading of the 11-nucleotide 5′ strand through the C-terminal domain of RecC (analogous to the AdnA nuclease domain) so that the 5′-terminal six nucleotide segment extends into the RecD subunit (which has no equivalent in AdnAB). These differences notwithstanding, the common theme for AdnAB and RecBCD is that the binding of the motor-nuclease to a blunt DSB end is coupled to limited melting of the base pairs that suffices to engage the “clutch” of the motor on the tracking strand.

In the present structure of the AdnAB initiation complex, the presence of an adenine nucleotide substrate in the AdnB motor provided clues to the purine nucleotide specificity of the AdnAB NTP phosphohydrolase and DSB resection activities [1, 7], whereby the adenine nucleobase is sandwiched in a π-stack between Phe25 and Trp357 and is poised to receive hydrogen bonds to adenine-N6 and adenine-N7 from Thr28 and Gln31. It is instructive to compare the adenosine interactions seen here for AdnAB with those observed in a cryo-EM structure of RecBCD with AMPPNP engaged in the phosphohydrolase active site of the RecB subunit (29; see PDB 5LD2), wherein the adenine base is sandwiched in a π-stack between Phe31 and Trp447 (Fig. 5A). RecB Trp447 is situated in a Trp447-Arg448 motif that is the conserved counterpart of the AdnB Trp357–Arg358 dipeptide (Fig. 3). However, RecB Phe31 occupies a different position in the tertiary structure than does AdnB Phe25. To wit: (i) RecB Phe31 is located in the P-loop α-helix immediately following the 26GTGKT30 A-box motif that coordinates the ATP phosphates (Fig. 5A); whereas (ii) AdnB Phe25 emanates from an α-helix upstream of the P-loop that has no counterpart in RecB. Moreover, the RecB•AMPPNP interface entails no enzymic contacts to the adenine base edge atoms, which may explain the observation that RecB catalyzes DNA-dependent hydrolysis of GTP, CTP, and UTP at rates 35%–50% that of ATP [30]. By contrast, AdnB does not hydrolyze CTP and is weakly active with UTP [1, 7].

In the crystal structure of the Bacillus AddAB•(3′-tailed DNA)•AMPPNP complex, the adenine base makes only a single π-stack on AddA Phe478 [26] (see PDB 4CEJ) (Fig. 5B), which is the equivalent of Trp357 in AdnB (Fig. 3). The AddA adenine is adjacent to Thr12, and Gln15 (the equivalents of AdnB Thr28 and Gln31), such that Gln15 makes hydrogen bonds from Oε and Oδ to adenine N6 and N7, respectively [26] (Fig. 5B). Note that AddA Trp11, adjacent to Thr12 and Gln15, does not participate in a π-stack on the adenine nucleobase (Fig. 5B). The adenine-specific hydrogen bonds to Gln15 beget a prediction that Bacillus AddAB would display selectivity for ATP as the nucleotide substrate for hydrolysis and DSB resection. All published studies of AddAB have been conducted with ATP and (to our inspection) there have been no reports of the ability of AddAB to utilize other NTPs.

Although we were attracted to the idea that subtracting the AdnB nucleobase edge contacts via T28A-Q31A mutation might eliminate the preference of AdnAB for purine nucleotides, and potentially reverse the discrimination against pyrimidine nucleotides, our results show that the T28A-Q31A enzyme remains purine NTP-specific in DSB resection. Thus, we suspect that the nucleobase pocket of AdnB favors purines on the basis of shape rather than hydrogen bonding to the base edge atoms.
