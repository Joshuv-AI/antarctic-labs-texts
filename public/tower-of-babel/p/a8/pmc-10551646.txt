Traumatic bilateral testicular amputation in a 31 year old male: A case report
Authors: Oscar Atwine, Denis Mucunguzi, Racheal Ainomugisha, Joshua Muhumuza, Marvin Mutakooha Mwesigye
Published: 2023

Introduction

Genitourinary injuries are responsible for about 10 % of all admitted trauma cases [1]. Injury to the testes is very rare, accounting for less than 1 % of urological injuries [2]. Testicular loss is commonly associated with animal bites, military trauma, self-mutilation especially in psychiatry patients but very rare in cases of assault [3]. Anatomically, testes are suspended within the scrotal sac by the spermatic cord and covered by a thin layer of serous fluid. These two factors offer a protective mechanism from trauma. In cases of penetrating injuries, these protective mechanisms are not enough to stop injury to the testis [4]. Injury due to blunt trauma is encountered more often than penetrating injuries that involve use of sharp objects, velocity missiles and others [5]. Bilateral testicular re-implantation which is a micro-vascular surgery procedure, has been recommend by the British Association of Urological Surgeons for patients with testicular amputation who present within 12 h of injury in centers that are well equipped with micro-vascular surgeons [6]. We present a case of a 31 year old male managed for bilateral traumatic testicular amputation following assault, at a Regional Referral Hospital in Uganda and has been reported in line with the SCARE 2020 criteria [7].

Case presentation

A 31 year old male referred to the emergency department of the Regional Referral Hospital with a history of trauma to his scrotum had been well till 16 h prior to presentation when he was attacked by unknown assailants who broke into his house in the night. Patient was reportedly beaten before his scrotum was pulled and amputated with eventual profuse bleeding. After rescue by the neighbors, a pressure dressing was placed at a low level health facility before referral to the Regional Hospital.

At arrival, the patient was in hypovolemic shock evidenced by altered mental state (GCS = 13/15), cold extremities, severe conjunctiva pallor, blood pressure of 70/50 mm Hg and a pulse rate of 130 beats/min. Patient was resuscitated according to the advanced trauma life support (ATLS) protocols and transfused with 2 units of whole blood. Local examination revealed loss of scrotal skin, with bilateral visible testis detached and outside the scrotum (Fig. 1). Patient also had a localized hematoma around the inguinal region. Following resuscitation, emergency scrotal exploration was done. Intra-operatively, the testes were dark in color, completely detached from the spermatic cord with extensive loss of scrotal skin and part of penile skin ventrally. Evacuation of approximately 150 cc hematoma was done plus debridement of necrotic testes and achieving hemostasis (Fig. 2). The vessels were identified and ligated to stop the bleeding, while all tissues that appeared ischemic were debrided away making sure that no ischemic tissues were left in place. Deranged results were hemoglobin (8.0 g/dl), and prothrombin time (16 s). International Normalized Ratio (INR), the rest of complete blood count, HIV serology, hepatitis B surface antigens and testosterone were normal.

Post-operatively, the patient received 2 more units of whole blood, intravenous broad spectrum antibiotics and daily dressing. The lead surgeon was a urologist. Retrograde cystourethrogram was done, that revealed no evidence of urethral injury. On 2nd post-operative day, post transfusion hemoglobin was 10 mg/dl, and serum testosterone level was below the normal range (2.3 ng/ml). The Patient was discharged on the 12th postoperative day with oral levofloxacin, oral tramadol and oral testosterone supplementation. Wound dressing was to be continued at the nearby health facility. At 3 weeks post injury, the hemoglobin and testosterone levels were normal and the patient was referred to the plastic surgeon for reconstruction.

Discussion

Genitourinary trauma that leads to testicular loss is very rare due to the anatomical position of the testis, role of cremasteric reflex and the mobility in the scrotum [1]. Its blood supply is rarely affected by trauma to the scrotum since the testicular artery comes directly from the aorta. In this case, the mechanism of injury was a rare one in which the testis were put under traction and cut with a sharp instrument.

Testicular loss is a very rare condition, but once it happens, is usually associated with self-mutilation especially in psychiatry and substance abuse patients [3]. In this case, it was a rare case in which the cause of injury was due to assault where the assailants used a sharp instrument to cut off both testes. Our patient denied any history of any mental illness or substance abuse that could be associated with testicular injury since most cases reported in literature are due to self-genital mutilation. A thorough physical examination was all that was necessary in making a diagnosis, but the ATLS protocol was important in early identification of shock and resuscitation.

Management of testicular amputation should involve a multidisciplinary team that starts with resuscitation according to ATLS protocols, followed by preservation of function through micro-vascular re-implantation of the testis, aggressive debridement, achieving hemostasis and re-construction of the scrotum with acceptable cosmesis. In this case, resuscitation was done according to ATLS protocols, aggressive debridement was done under good anesthesia, and ligation of bilateral testicular arteries and its branches was done to achieve hemostasis. A microsurgical approach with re-anastomosis is possible if the patient presents early within 12 h and expertise is available. This type of surgery was not done in this case since the patient presented late (greater than 12 h) due to delays in referral and emergency transportation especially during night times. There was also lack of expertize especially a vascular surgeon to help in restoration of testicular blood supply [6].

About 95 % of testosterone is primarily produced from the male gonads. In this case we anticipated that the patient would develop signs and symptoms of hypogonadism, therefore testosterone was measured and found to be low for which reason testosterone replacement therapy was initiated.

Few standardized protocols exist especially in the low income countries due to lack of adequate funding of the health system and low incidence of male external genital injuries. In our facility, mechanisms are being put in place for early identification and management of these urologic emergencies in order to have good patient outcomes. This case was reported to the police, investigations were carried out and security measures are being put in place to reduce such occurrences.

Conclusion

Bilateral Testicular amputation is a very rare type of genitourinary injury. Its management requires a multidisciplinary approach but requires a lot of expertize especially in microsurgical approach. General practitioners should be able to identify these injuries by thorough clinical evaluation to ensure timely resuscitation and referral for definitive management in order to improve outcomes.
