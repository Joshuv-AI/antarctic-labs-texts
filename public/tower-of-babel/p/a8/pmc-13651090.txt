Burnout among international versus domestic medical graduates in the United States and United Kingdom: a systematic review and meta-analysis of trainees and early-career physicians
Authors: Fahd Niaz Shaikh, Muhammad Hasnain Mankani, Amal Mahmood, Hanzla Asim, Mariam Shahabi, Hasan Fareed Siddiqui, Muhammad Muntazir Mehdi Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternational medical graduates constitute a substantial fraction of the physician workforce in high-income destination countries. Whether their burnout rates differ from those of domestic medical graduates has not been quantitatively synthesized in any prior meta-analysis we identified through searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, Scopus, and CINAHL conducted on 27 March 2026 and re-run with expanded population terms on 27 April 2026 (Supplement S1).ObjectiveTo synthesize available comparative burnout estimates between international and domestic medical graduate trainees and early-career physicians.MethodsStudies were eligible if they reported burnout as a dichotomous outcome with extractable odds ratios. Effect sizes were pooled on the log-OR scale using random-effects meta-analysis with Paule-Mandel τ² estimation and Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment to confidence intervals, paired with a 95% prediction interval. DerSimonian-Laird is reported as a comparability analysis only. Risk of bias was assessed using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale; certainty of evidence was rated using GRADE.ResultsWe identified five effect sizes from four studies comprising 92,235 physician-responses. Because two UK survey waves may include overlapping respondents, the primary meta-analysis used four independent effect sizes from four studies comprising 73,049 physician-responses (Al-Haddad 2021); the five-effect all-wave analysis is reported as sensitivity. Under the primary HKSJ-Paule-Mandel random-effects model, international medical graduate trainees and early-career physicians had lower odds of reporting burnout than their domestic counterparts (pooled OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.49–0.87; 95% prediction interval 0.37–1.15; I²=90.3%). The prediction interval crosses 1.0; certainty of evidence was rated VERY LOW (GRADE).ConclusionsAcross this small evidence base, all effect sizes favoured international graduate trainees, but the magnitude is uncertain (HKSJ 95% CI 0.49–0.87; 95% prediction interval 0.37–1.15) and the certainty of evidence is VERY LOW. Given the small evidence base, conceptually non-equivalent burnout instruments across studies, mixed adjustment status across studies, English-language eligibility restriction, and confinement to predominantly trainee populations in two destination countries (US, UK), this finding should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating signal rather than a settled conclusion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09787-3.

Research in context

What is already known about the topic?

Physician burnout affects a substantial fraction of practitioners in high-income countries [1–3] and drives workforce attrition, medical errors, and reduced quality of care [4–7]. International medical graduates constitute approximately one quarter of the US physician workforce [8, 9] and face a constellation of stressors not encountered by domestic graduates [10–12]. No prior meta-analysis identified by our search has quantitatively compared burnout between international and domestic medical graduates.

What does this study add to the literature?

Across four studies and five effect sizes from the United States and United Kingdom, international medical graduate trainees and early-career physicians had lower odds of reporting burnout under the primary random-effects model with Hartung-Knapp adjustment (pooled OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.49–0.87; 95% prediction interval 0.37–1.15). Findings from Doe et al. derive specifically from graduating family medicine residents completing the ABFM Initial Certification Questionnaire 2019–2021 [13]. Results are concordant in direction across the two included countries and three burnout instruments, but are confined to residents and early-career physicians; whether the pattern extends to mid- or late-career physicians remains unknown. However, the 95% prediction interval crosses 1.0 (0.37–1.15) and the certainty of evidence is VERY LOW, meaning a future study cannot be confidently predicted to replicate this direction of association.

What are the policy implications?

Programs may benefit from considering whether their wellness offerings address the qualitatively different stressor profiles of international medical graduate trainees, including visa-related anxiety and discrimination, which may not manifest as burnout on standard instruments but still require targeted institutional support [14, 15]. Given the small evidence base and prediction interval crossing 1.0, primary research with longitudinal designs, culturally validated instruments, and multidimensional wellbeing assessment is required before policy changes are made.

Background

Physician migration across national borders represents one of the defining features of modern healthcare delivery. In the United States, international medical graduates fill approximately one quarter of all residency positions and the practising physician workforce [8, 9]. The United Kingdom, Australia, and Canada depend on overseas-trained doctors to comparable or greater degrees. Without these physicians, healthcare systems in high-income destination countries would face immediate workforce shortages [10].

Burnout among physicians has attracted substantial research and policy attention over the past two decades. Large epidemiological surveys have documented elevated burnout prevalence in multiple specialties [1–3], with downstream consequences including medical errors, reduced quality of care, workforce attrition, and physician suicide [4–7]. Institutional investment in wellness programs, schedule reforms, and mental health support has increased substantially in response to these findings.

Yet a conspicuous gap persists in this literature. The overwhelming majority of burnout research treats physicians as a homogeneous professional group, stratifying results by specialty, practice setting, or career stage but rarely by country of medical training. International medical graduates face a distinctive constellation of stressors that domestic graduates do not encounter: credential verification that may span years, language and cultural barriers in clinical settings, visa-dependent employment with restricted job mobility, separation from family and professional networks, and exposure to overt or subtle discrimination [10–12]. Given these stressors, elevated burnout in this population is a plausible — though untested — prior expectation.

A small number of studies have directly compared burnout between international and domestic medical graduates, with results that appear to challenge that prior expectation. However, these studies span different countries, use different instruments, survey different physician populations, and apply different adjustment strategies. No systematic attempt has been made to synthesise their findings quantitatively. The Bhugra et al. World Psychiatric Association Commission on the mental health of international medical graduates, published in 2024, provided an authoritative narrative review but did not undertake formal meta-analytic pooling of comparative burnout data [10].

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses that gap. We sought to answer a single question: do international medical graduate trainees and early-career physicians experience burnout at different rates than their domestic counterparts? The answer carries implications for how healthcare systems allocate wellness resources, structure orientation programs, and conceptualise the occupational needs of an increasingly mobile physician workforce.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [16]. This review was not prospectively registered on PROSPERO. We acknowledge this as a limitation because it prevents readers from independently verifying that the eligibility criteria, outcome definitions, and pooling plan were specified before study selection and extraction. We did not retrospectively register the review because prospective registration cannot be backdated, and registration after study completion would be misleading rather than transparent. Instead, before conducting the revised search and re-analysis described in this revision, we deposited on OSF (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/D5Q2N) on 27 April 2026: the protocol, the complete database strategies for all five databases, the screening log, the full data-extraction sheet, the analytic R code (metafor v4.8) used for all pooled estimates and sensitivity analyses, and a dated record of every deviation from the originally submitted manuscript. This deposition improves transparency but does not substitute for prospective registration; we list non-registration as a Limitation (§ 4).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) observational design (cross-sectional, cohort, or survey); (2) participants included physicians who were international medical graduates and physicians who were domestic medical graduates; (3) burnout was measured using a validated instrument; (4) burnout was reported as a dichotomous outcome (above/below an instrument-defined threshold) such that ORs with 95% CIs could be extracted directly from the published article or computed from a 2 × 2 frequency table — effect sizes derived from continuous-scale regression were not eligible for the primary pool; and (5) published in English in a peer-reviewed journal. Eligibility was restricted to English-language reports; the database searches themselves applied no language restrictions.

Studies were excluded if they: (1) reported burnout only among international graduates without a domestic graduate comparator; (2) used non-validated or ad hoc measures of burnout; (3) were conference abstracts, editorials, or commentaries without original data; or (4) were qualitative studies without quantitative outcomes.

International medical graduates were defined as physicians who obtained their primary medical degree outside the country in which they were practising or training at the time of the study. The specific terminology varied across studies and included international medical graduates, foreign medical graduates, overseas-trained doctors, overseas-qualified doctors, internationally trained doctors, foreign-born physicians, non-US medical graduates, and physicians with primary medical qualification obtained outside the destination country.

Information sources and search strategy

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, Scopus, and CINAHL from database inception through 27 March 2026, with a revised search incorporating expanded population terms conducted on 27 April 2026. Full Boolean strings for all five databases are reported in Supplement S1. The search strategy combined terms for the population of interest (international medical graduates, foreign medical graduates, overseas-trained doctors, overseas-qualified doctors, non-US medical graduates, foreign-born physicians, primary medical qualification, internationally trained doctors) with terms for the outcome (burnout, emotional exhaustion, depersonalization/depersonalisation, professional fulfilment, Maslach Burnout Inventory, Copenhagen Burnout Inventory). The originally submitted strategy included a restrictive outcome filter “(prevalence OR survey OR cross-sectional OR comparison)” that we removed in the revised search at the recommendation of a reviewer. Reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were hand-searched for additional eligible publications. No date restrictions were applied during the search phase. Eligibility was restricted to English-language reports (§ 2.2).

The revised search yielded 142 unique records after deduplication (vs. 88 in the original 27 March 2026 search). Eighteen advanced to full-text screening (vs. 12 originally). No additional eligible study was identified at full-text screening; reasons for full-text exclusions are listed in Supplement S2. The final evidence base remains four studies and five effect sizes.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts in Covidence against eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible articles were retrieved and assessed independently by both reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer adjudicating where consensus could not be reached. Inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s κ) was 0.81 for inclusion decisions and 0.74 for risk-of-bias scoring.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers into a standardised form. Extracted variables included first author, publication year, country, study design, total sample size, number of international and domestic medical graduates, burnout instrument, burnout definition and threshold, burnout prevalence by group, odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, and the covariate set used in any adjusted analysis.

We distinguish three OR types in Table S1: (i) extracted adjusted ORs from a multivariable logistic regression model (West, Doe); (ii) unadjusted 2 × 2 ORs computed from published frequency tables (Al-Haddad 2019, Al-Haddad 2021); and (iii) the published logistic-regression OR for dichotomous CBI work-related burnout extracted as published, not recomputed from prevalence figures (St Onge — burnout dichotomised as scores above the average burnout reported for chief physicians in the PUMA reference study, applied across the three CBI subscales [17]). When a study reported results from multiple survey waves, each wave was treated as a separate data point in the all-wave (k = 5) sensitivity analysis only; the primary analysis (k = 4) used Al-Haddad 2021 alone to avoid potentially correlated waves.

Risk of bias assessment

Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale adapted for cross-sectional studies, evaluating selection, comparability, and outcome domains. Per-study and per-domain scores are reported in Table S2, together with the inter-rater Cohen’s κ values from § 2.4. Studies scoring seven or higher out of a possible ten points were classified as high quality, four to six as moderate, and below four as low quality.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis was a random-effects meta-analysis on log odds ratios, with between-study variance τ² estimated by the Paule-Mandel method and 95% confidence intervals constructed using the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment, paired with a 95% prediction interval [18–20]. The Hartung-Knapp adjustment is recommended for random-effects meta-analyses of small numbers of studies with substantial heterogeneity ([21]). A secondary analysis used REML τ² with HKSJ adjustment. The DerSimonian-Laird estimator is reported only as a comparability analysis with the originally submitted specification, alongside the variance-estimator and random-effects guidance cited above [20, 21].

Because only one included study (Al-Haddad) contributes potentially correlated survey waves (2019, 2021), a three-level multilevel model is statistically underpowered (one cluster cannot identify within-study dependence) and was not used. Our primary dependence handling uses the larger and more recent Al-Haddad 2021 wave alone (k = 4 effect sizes). Both-wave inclusion (k = 5, “all-wave” analysis) is presented as sensitivity only.

Heterogeneity was quantified using the Cochran Q statistic (significance at p < 0.10) and the I² statistic. I² values were interpreted as low (< 25%), moderate (25–75%), or high (> 75%). Planned sensitivity analyses included: leave-one-out re-pooling at k = 4; the all-wave k = 5 analysis; the matched-N DL k = 4 estimate; and the secondary REML-HKSJ k = 4 estimate. A narrative comparison of the two adjusted ORs (West, Doe) is presented in § 3.6 without pooling, because k = 2 random-effects pooling is unstable. Subgroup analyses by country and by instrument were planned but, given k = 4 in the primary analysis (UK contributes only Al-Haddad 2021), are demoted to descriptive observations in the main interpretation. Numerical subgroup pooling appears only in the all-wave (k = 5) sensitivity table and is labelled exploratory throughout. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test on the all-wave (k = 5) data, with the explicit caveat that visual inspection of funnel plots is unreliable when fewer than ten studies are pooled [22].

Certainty of evidence was rated using GRADE [23] and reported in Table S4.

All analyses were conducted in R 4.5.3 using the metafor package v4.8.0 [24]. The analysis script is deposited on OSF.

Results

Study selection

The original 27 March 2026 database search identified 88 unique records after deduplication. The revised search of 27 April 2026 with expanded population terms identified 142 unique records. After title and abstract screening, 18 articles advanced to full-text review. Fourteen were excluded at full-text screening: 7 did not report a domestic graduate comparator, 4 lacked a validated burnout instrument, 2 were qualitative-only studies, and 1 was a narrative review without original data (Supplement S2). Four studies providing five study-wave effect estimates met all eligibility criteria and were included in the quantitative synthesis (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The four included studies were published between 2011 and 2024 and collectively enrolled 92,235 physician-responses across all five effect sizes (k = 5; Table S1). Because the two UK survey waves (Al-Haddad 2019 and 2021) may include overlapping respondents, the primary meta-analysis used four independent effect sizes from four studies comprising 73,049 physician-responses (k = 4; Al-Haddad 2021 only); the five-effect all-wave analysis is reported as sensitivity. Three studies originated from the United States and one from the United Kingdom, contributing two survey-wave estimates from 2019 to 2021.

West et al. (2011) surveyed 16,394 internal medicine residents in the United States (7,743 US medical graduates and 8,571 international medical graduates), reporting burnout in 45.1% of international graduates versus 58.7% of US graduates (adjusted OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.65–0.76; recalculated from the published 99% CI to a 95% CI for pooling) [25]. Al-Haddad et al. analysed General Medical Council National Training Survey data in the United Kingdom using the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (personal + work-related), reporting burnout in 42.9% of international graduates versus 51.2% of domestic graduates in 2019 (unadjusted 2 × 2 OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.68–0.76) and 50.2% versus 57.1% in 2021 (unadjusted 2 × 2 OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.71–0.80) [26]; the original article notes overlap between the 2019 and 2021 cohorts due to the multi-year duration of UK specialty training. St Onge et al. (2022) surveyed 222 first-year US internal medicine residents using the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory and dichotomised burnout as scores higher than the average reported for chief physicians in the PUMA reference study (i.e., the comparator threshold used in the original article rather than a pre-specified numerical CBI cut-point); under that definition, work-related burnout was reported in 26% of international graduates versus 49% of domestic graduates, with a published logistic-regression OR of 0.50 (95% CI 0.25–0.99) [17]. Doe et al. (2024) analysed American Board of Family Medicine Initial Certification Questionnaire data from 11,570 graduating family medicine residents using a single-item validated burnout measure, reporting burnout in 26.7% of international graduates versus 40.3% of US graduates (adjusted OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.48–0.60) [13].

The four studies used three distinct burnout instruments with non-equivalent thresholds (single-item callousness/EE ≥ 1/week; CBI three-subscale dichotomisation against the PUMA chief-physician reference; CBI personal+work-related per GMC scoring guide). These instruments measure overlapping but conceptually non-equivalent constructs — the single-item measure captures a narrower dimension of burnout (emotional exhaustion/depersonalisation frequency ≥ 1/week) than the multi-dimensional CBI, and neither instrument has been cross-culturally validated for international medical graduate populations. Inter-instrument equivalence across these three instruments has not been established. Adjustment status differed across studies: West and Doe report adjusted multivariable ORs; Al-Haddad ORs are unadjusted 2 × 2 estimates; the St Onge OR is a published logistic-regression OR on a dichotomous outcome.

Pooled estimate

Under the primary HKSJ-Paule-Mandel random-effects model on four independent effect sizes (Al-Haddad 2021 only), the pooled odds ratio was 0.65 (95% CI 0.49–0.87), with a 95% prediction interval of 0.37–1.15 (Fig. 2). The 95% prediction interval, computed at k = 4, is informative but imprecise. A pooled odds ratio of 0.65 corresponds to 35% lower odds of reporting burnout among international graduates. Because burnout prevalence in these populations is high, the OR should not be interpreted as an equivalent risk reduction. Across plausible baseline burnout risks of 40–60% in domestic graduate populations, the corresponding risk ratio range is approximately 0.76–0.82, equivalent to roughly 18–24% lower probability of reporting burnout. Readers should not equate “lower odds” with “lower risk” of equivalent magnitude. We have not designated this difference clinically meaningful because no consensus threshold for clinical or policy significance in burnout-prevalence comparisons has been published.

The DerSimonian-Laird estimator (k = 5, original specification) gave a pooled OR of 0.68 (95% CI 0.61–0.75), narrower than the primary HKSJ-PM CI as expected because DL Wald-type inference under-covers at k < 10 with high heterogeneity. The all-wave HKSJ-PM sensitivity analysis (k = 5, including Al-Haddad 2019) gave a pooled OR of 0.67 (95% CI 0.56–0.81), with a 95% prediction interval of 0.44–1.02 (Table S3).

Heterogeneity

Substantial heterogeneity was observed (Q = 28.98, df = 3, p < 0.001; I²=90.3%; τ²=0.0232 in the primary analysis). This heterogeneity reflects substantive between-study differences across multiple dimensions: country of practice (US vs. UK), burnout instrument (single-item vs. CBI), training level (R1 internal medicine vs. all UK trainees vs. graduating family medicine residents), study era (2011 vs. 2019–2024), and adjustment-set differences (multivariable vs. unadjusted vs. single-covariate logistic). While all five effect sizes favoured international graduates, the magnitude varied substantially across studies; this heterogeneity reflects substantive between-study differences and is not a benign feature of the evidence base.

Subgroup analyses (Descriptive Only)

Because the primary analysis contains only four independent effect sizes, with the United Kingdom contributing only Al-Haddad 2021, pooled subgroup estimates by country are not feasible under the primary specification. Subgroup analyses are therefore reported only as descriptive observations in this section and as exploratory pooled estimates in the all-wave (k = 5) sensitivity analysis (Table S3); they were not used for inference. We note that country, instrument, population, and study era are confounded with one another in this evidence base — the only UK studies use CBI; US studies use a mix of single-item and CBI; instrument and population are not balanced across countries — so any apparent subgroup difference cannot be attributed to a single factor.

Sensitivity analyses

Leave-one-out re-pooling under the primary HKSJ-PM specification yielded pooled ORs ranging from 0.61 to 0.73 (Table S3). Exclusion of either West or St Onge produced 95% confidence intervals that crossed 1.0 (West-excluded OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.36–1.08; St Onge-excluded OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.43–1.03), indicating the pooled signal is sensitive to individual studies. Exclusion of Al-Haddad 2021 attenuated heterogeneity substantially (I² 78.7%) and tightened the estimate to 0.61 (0.40–0.92). The all-wave sensitivity analysis (k = 5, HKSJ-PM) gave a pooled OR of 0.67 (95% CI 0.56–0.81; 95% PI 0.44–1.02) and the matched-N DL k = 4 estimator gave 0.65 (95% CI 0.55–0.77). The secondary REML-HKSJ k = 4 estimate (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.49–0.87) is essentially identical to the primary Paule-Mandel-HKSJ result.

A narrative comparison of the two adjusted multivariable ORs (West 2011: OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.65–0.76; Doe 2024: OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.48–0.60) is presented without pooling. Two-study random-effects pooling is unstable; we therefore do not report a pooled adjusted-only estimate. The point estimates differ by 0.16 OR units, which is an additional source of heterogeneity in the primary pool.

Publication bias

Formal statistical assessment of publication bias is uninformative at k = 5; visual inspection of funnel plots is itself unreliable, particularly when fewer than ten studies are pooled [22]. The Egger regression test on k = 5 was not statistically significant (t = − 1.15, p = 0.33), but this test is known to be uninformative at small k. We therefore do not draw any inference about publication bias from the funnel plot, which is presented (Fig. 3) for completeness only. We note that the small evidence base, the absence of grey-literature search, and the English-only eligibility restriction collectively raise the possibility of selective reporting that this synthesis cannot detect.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that, across a small evidence base of four studies and five effect sizes from the United States and United Kingdom, international medical graduate trainees and early-career physicians had lower odds of reporting burnout than their domestic counterparts under the primary HKSJ-Paule-Mandel random-effects model (pooled OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.49–0.87; 95% prediction interval 0.37–1.15). The pooled estimate should be interpreted in the context of three core constraints: the instruments are not interchangeable, the adjustment sets are not uniform, and the evidence base is too small to support subgroup analyses that could disentangle these sources of variation. All five individual effect estimates favoured international graduates, but the magnitude varied substantially (I²=90.3%). Furthermore, the prediction interval crosses 1.0 — meaning the direction of association cannot be confidently predicted for a future study in a comparable setting. This, together with the VERY LOW GRADE rating, positions the finding as an exploratory signal requiring confirmation rather than a practice-informing conclusion.

The lower burnout observed among international graduates is counterintuitive. These physicians navigate a gauntlet of stressors that their domestic counterparts do not face: multi-year credentialing processes, financial expenditure on licensing examinations, visa dependency that limits employment mobility, geographical separation from family, and cultural adjustment in clinical and social settings. Several explanatory frameworks merit consideration as candidate hypotheses requiring primary-data testing, not as inferences this synthesis can support.

One candidate explanation, not directly tested in this synthesis, is selection. The pathway from foreign medical school to active practice in the United States or United Kingdom may select for individuals with persistence, adaptability, and tolerance for uncertainty; physicians who lack these psychological resources may self-select out of the migration pathway at various stages, leaving a survivorship-biased cohort in the practising workforce. Shakir et al. reported higher resilience scores among international neurosurgery residents [27]; we cite this as illustrative only, not confirmatory.

One candidate explanation, not directly tested in this synthesis, is differential baseline expectations. International graduates who have trained or worked in resource-limited settings may calibrate their expectations differently, perceiving working conditions in high-income healthcare systems as comparatively manageable even when domestic graduates experience those same conditions as onerous. This reframing is not denial of difficulty but rather a different reference point for evaluating occupational strain.

One candidate explanation, not directly tested in this synthesis, is cultural variation in symptom expression. Research instruments developed and validated predominantly in Western populations may not capture the idioms through which burnout manifests in physicians from South Asian, Middle Eastern, or sub-Saharan African cultural contexts. Lower reported burnout scores might partially reflect measurement limitations rather than genuinely lower distress.

One candidate explanation, not directly tested in this synthesis, is professional identity investment. International graduates who have invested extraordinary effort to obtain medical licensure in a second country may derive heightened meaning from their professional roles, consistent with theoretical models linking vocational calling to reduced burnout susceptibility. Khan et al. documented elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and stress among USMLE aspirants [28]; we cite this as illustrative only, not confirmatory.

These candidate explanations carry an important caveat. Lower burnout does not imply the absence of occupational distress. International graduates may experience qualitatively different stressors, including discrimination, imposter syndrome, loneliness, and fear of deportation, that conventional burnout instruments fail to capture. Healey et al. found that, among international graduates reporting workplace discrimination, more than three-quarters described subtle interpersonal forms of inequitable treatment [14]; Koenig et al. documented elevated imposter syndrome among international internal medicine residents in the United States [15]. The signal identified in this synthesis should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that international graduates require less institutional support, but as evidence that their support needs may differ in character from those of their domestic peers.

Adjudication among these candidate explanations requires longitudinal, instrumented, or qualitative primary research, not further meta-analytic work on the existing small evidence base.

Interpretation of the pooled estimate

The pooled odds ratio of 0.65 (95% CI 0.49–0.87) should be interpreted as a summary association rather than an estimate of a common causal effect. The primary pool combines effect estimates from different analytic approaches: two adjusted multivariable odds ratios (West, Doe), one unadjusted 2 × 2 odds ratio (Al-Haddad 2021), and one published logistic-regression odds ratio for a dichotomous burnout outcome (St Onge). Because adjustment sets differed substantially across studies — the covariates available and included in each model reflect different assumptions about confounding — the pooled estimate describes the direction and central tendency of the association across the available evidence rather than quantifying a single parameter that would replicate under a uniform analytic specification. Readers should interpret the pooled odds ratio as a pragmatic synthesis of the available comparative data, not as a causal estimate.

Implications for future research

What this evidence base actually permits is a question, not an answer. Three primary-research lines would help close the gap.

First, future surveys should not measure burnout in isolation. Pair the CBI or single-item burnout score with depression and anxiety screens, a discrimination measure, and a financial-strain item; otherwise, “lower burnout” in international graduates remains ambiguous between genuine resilience, suppressed reporting, and a measurement instrument missing the relevant distress.

Second, the cross-sectional design across all four included studies cannot tell us whether the burnout signal we observed is a stable trait or a survivor effect that wears off. A longitudinal cohort followed from the credentialing phase through year five of practice would clarify whether the observed burnout signal reflects a stable difference, selective survival through the migration pathway, or changes that emerge after practice conditions accumulate.

Third, no published evidence we found establishes that the CBI or single-item burnout measures behave equivalently across culturally diverse physician populations. Until instrument validation studies demonstrate that they do, comparative claims about IMG-versus-DMG burnout — including ours — should be read with that caveat.

Conclusions

In this small evidence base of four studies and five effect sizes from the United States and United Kingdom, all five effect sizes favoured international medical graduate trainees and early-career physicians, but the magnitude is uncertain (HKSJ 95% CI 0.49–0.87; 95% prediction interval 0.37–1.15, crossing 1.0), and the certainty of evidence is VERY LOW (GRADE). Rather than concluding that international graduates are thriving, policymakers and institutional leaders should recognise that burnout measures alone do not capture the full spectrum of occupational distress in this population. Future research should employ longitudinal designs, culturally validated instruments, and multidimensional wellbeing assessments to move beyond the burnout paradox identified here and toward genuinely equitable workforce support. Readers should note that the pooled estimate represents a summary association across heterogeneous study designs and instruments, not a single causal parameter.
