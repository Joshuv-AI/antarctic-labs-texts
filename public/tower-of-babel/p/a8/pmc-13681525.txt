ACE2-using coronaviruses: A global concern
Authors: Zepeng Xu, André Costa Lobato, Kefang Liu, George Fu Gao
Published: 2025

Recently, several studies have reported Merbecoviruses from bat and mink using angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as the host cell entry receptor [1,2]. These reports raise global concern and highlight the significance of constant surveillance for animal coronaviruses (CoVs). Since the outbreak of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) in 2003, scientists have characterized various animal CoVs that use ACE2 as a receptor. During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, more animal CoVs from all over the world have been reinvestigated and found to utilize ACE2 as a receptor. Here, we briefly summarize the characteristics of ACE2-binding CoVs and discuss the mechanisms underlying the acquisition of ACE2-binding capabilities.

RESERVOIR AND INTERMEDIATE HOSTS OF ACE2-USING CORONAVIRUSES

Surveillance of wildlife hosts is an important step in understanding the emergence of zoonotic viruses and the prevention of Disease X. To date, at least 34 CoVs (including SARS-CoV and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 [SARS-CoV-2]) have been experimentally validated using ACE2 as a functional receptor, including 27 Sarbecoviruses, 6 Merbecoviruses, and 1 Setracovirus in the family Coronaviridae (Figure 1A, Table S1). Among the 34 CoVs, 27 utilize human ACE2. The sample collection period spanned from 2003 to 2020 but experimental validation of ACE2 usage by most CoVs has been conducted in recent years, reflecting heightened academic attention since the SARS-CoV-2 outbreak. The spike (S) proteins of the Sarbecoviruses share amino acid (AA) identities ranging from 67.5% to 98.4%, reflecting the sequential flexibility of ACE2 usage. Among the 34 ACE2-using coronaviruses, 28, including Sarbecoviruses and Merbecoviruses, were isolated from bats, highlighting the importance of bats as reservoir hosts.

Bats were recognized as reservoir hosts of Sarbecoviruses in 2005, when a series of SARS-CoV-related bat CoVs were characterized (Table S1). After the SARS-CoV-2 outbreak, researchers have identified multiple SARS-CoV-2-related CoVs in bats from stored or newly collected samples. RaTG13 was the first identified SARS-CoV-2-related CoV and shares 96.2% genomic identity with SARS-CoV-2. BANAL-20-52, identified in Northern Laos, is the CoV most closely related to SARS-CoV-2, with a 96.8% genomic identity, and its receptor-binding interface exhibits only one distinct residue. Another study reported that CX1, which demonstrated a high genomic identity (93%), was the first related CoV to carry N501Y (numbering based on SARS-CoV-2) on its S protein, a substitution that significantly enhances human infectivity. Experimental validation revealed that the S proteins of both BANAL-20-52 and CX1 mediate efficient pseudovirus entry into human ACE2 (hACE2)-expressing cells [3]. Additionally, many other Sarbecoviruses have been identified in bats, further highlighting the important role of bats as reservoir hosts, such as RaTG15, RmYN02, RmYN04, RmYN05, RmYN06, RmYN08, RshSTT182, RshSTT200, RacCS203, Rc-o319, PrC31, BANAL-20-103, BANAL-20-236, ZC45, ZXC21, WIV01, and WIV16 (Table S1).

Other hosts also play critical roles in the interspecies transmission of coronaviruses. Palm civets are speculated to be potential intermediate hosts of SARS-CoV, and mutations in the SARS-CoV receptor-binding domain (RBD) may have been acquired in palm civets, which promote infection efficiency by strengthening hACE2 binding. However, in recent years, another opinion has challenged the methodology of phylogenetic analysis, arguing that palm civet SARS-CoV originates from human-to-palm civet transmission, not the other way around [4,5]. However, it is noteworthy that one of the opponents has retracted his criticism [4]. On the other hand, pangolins are recognized as an important potential intermediate host as CoVs have been identified in pangolins and have demonstrated high genomic identity to SARS-CoV-2 (85.5% for GX/P2V/2017, 92.4% for GD/1/2019). Furthermore, GD/1/2019 effectively infected human cells and tracheal epithelial organoids, and infection of hACE2 transgenic mice caused pulmonary damage, whereas GX/P2V/2017 demonstrated infectivity in mouse models [6].

Merbecoviruses that use ACE2 as a functional receptor have also been characterized in bats and adopt distinct binding interfaces on ACE2. NeoCoV, the closest relative of Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), shares 85% genomic identity but only 63% S protein AA identity and has been reported to efficiently use bat ACE2 orthologs for cell entry. Structural analysis revealed that the ACE2-binding interface is different from that of Sarbecoviruses and hCoV-NL63, with only residues 329 and 330 overlapping, respectively. A single substitution (T510F) of the NeoCoV RBD substantially enhanced binding to hACE2 and allowed pseudovirus entry into HEK293T-hACE2 cells. PDF-2180, a close relative of NeoCoV, also demonstrated ACE2 usage in the same study. Three additional ACE2-binding Merbecoviruses, HKU5, MOW15-22, and PnNL2018B, have also been characterized in bats. Minks have recently been reported to carry an HKU5-related Merbecovirus MRCoV, which can infect the cells of minks, bats, monkeys, and humans. Structural analysis revealed largely overlapping footprints compared to those of SARS-CoV-2, despite the structural differences between the two RBDs [2].

GLOBAL DISTRIBUTION OF ACE2-USING CORONAVIRUSES

The geographical distribution of ACE2-binding CoVs covers Asia, Europe, and Africa, indicating a global spillover threat. Southeast Asia has attracted intense efforts to monitor wildlife and zoonotic pathogens. The majority of ACE2-binding coronaviruses are identified in bats, including Sarbecoviruses and Merbecoviruses.

In other regions, the sampling was somewhat insufficient because of less emphasis and inadequate monitoring. Furthermore, many of these under-sampled countries are undergoing social and environmental changes (political instability, rapid population growth, urbanization, wildlife farming and trade, land-use conversion from forested habitats to agricultural land, etc.), representing neglected hotspots for zoonotic leakage. BtKY72, identified in Africa, uses ACE2 orthologs from bats and camels. Wells et al. reported three CoVs sequenced from Africa with no detected binding to ACE2, representing a distinct clade/lineage that shows higher divergence from the two subclades (1a and 1b) represented by SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 [7]. In Europe, both Sarbecoviruses (RhGB07, Khosta-1, and Khosta-2) and Merbecoviruses (MOW15-22 and PnNL2018B) have been characterized.

Notably, despite no reported ACE2-binding animal CoVs, β-CoVs have been detected in North and South America and Oceania, and bat hosts of Merbecoviruses are widely distributed in the Amazon and Mexico, indicating neglected spillover risk [8].

ACQUISITION OF ACE2 USAGE AMONG DISTINCT LINEAGES OF CORONAVIRUSES

The acquisition of CoV ACE2-binding properties is not yet fully understood, and evidence suggests that both convergent evolution and recombination play important roles. The ACE2-binding CoVs span α-CoV Setracovirus and β-CoV Sarbecovirus and Merbecovirus. HCoV-NL63, as the only ACE2-binding α-CoV, shows markedly different RBD structural characteristics containing two layers of 3-stranded β-sheets, in contrast to the 5-stranded β-sheet of β-CoVs. However, the hCoV-NL63 RBD binds to ACE2 with an interface at the top of ACE2 that largely overlaps with Sarbecoviruses. In contrast, Merbecoviruses, except for HKU5-related CoVs, exhibited varied footprints, suggesting multiple evolutionary acquisition events (Figure 1B–1C).

Within the Sarbecoviruses, phylogenetic analysis suggested that the lineage related to SARS-CoV-2 is more ancestral and that the SARS-CoV-belonging lineage likely acquired ACE2 binding through recombination with the lineage, including SARS-CoV-2. Furthermore, three CoVs sequenced from Africa have been reported to be phylogenetic intermediates between SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2, whose RBD contains one of the two deletions responsible for the inability to bind ACE2 [7]. However, another study suggested that ACE2 binding is an ancestral trait of Sarbecoviruses that was subsequently lost in some lineages and reconstructed the ancient RBD sequence upon ACE2 usage acquisition [9]. Recent studies by multiple teams have proposed a clear serotyping [10]. The correlation between the serotype and ACE2-binding in CoVs should be clarified in future studies.

Within Merbecoviruses, the adoption of a distinct ACE2-binding interface suggests that ACE2 usage was independently acquired during convergent evolution. Notably, the binding interface demonstrated a geographic correlation with sampling locations as Merbecoviruses discovered in Europe (MOW15-22 and PnNL2018B), Africa (NeoCoV and PDF2180), and Asia (HKU5 and MRCoV) exhibited distinct binding interface configurations, thereby providing additional evidence supporting the convergent evolution hypothesis.

Although the mechanism of ACE2 usage is yet to be fully elucidated, the multiple independent acquisition events and broad distribution of ACE2-using CoVs suggest certain evolutionary advantages that may include, but are not limited to, the ubiquitous tissue distribution and evolutionary conservation of ACE2. Despite regional variations in the reported CoVs, disparities in surveillance capacity cannot be excluded as contributing factors. The viruses do not recognize borders, and this global challenge calls for global cooperation in data sharing.
