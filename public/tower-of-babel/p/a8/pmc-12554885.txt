Fertility preservation in the setting of a new uterine smooth muscle tumor of uncertain malignant potential (STUMP) diagnosis
Authors: Madelyn E. Tung-Mabry, Simrit K. Warring, J. Kenneth Schoolmeester, Maryam Shahi, Wendaline M. VanBuren, Jamie N. Bakkum-Gamez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights:•Uterine STUMPs are infrequently diagnosed in women of childbearing age.•While STUMPs often behave like benign tumors they can recur or progress to leiomyosarcoma.•Myomectomy for temporary fertility preservation can be considered in STUMP management.

Introduction

Uterine smooth muscle tumors of uncertain malignant potential (STUMPs) are rare neoplasms characterized by an overall favorable prognosis, with reported five-year recurrence-free survival rates ranging from 92 % to 100 % (Bacanakgil et al., 2017). STUMPs have been described as occurring in women ranging from 20 to 75 years of age but are typically diagnosed in women in their fifth decade of life (Bacanakgil et al., 2017). Common clinical symptoms include pelvic pain or pressure, abnormal uterine bleeding, and palpable pelvic mass, similar to leiomyoma or leiomyosarcoma (Şahin et al., 2019). The risk factors for STUMP development remain poorly defined; however, current evidence suggests that pregnancy does not appear to alter the risk (Gadducci and Zannoni, 2019). Classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2003, STUMPs are defined as neoplasms unable to be definitively diagnosed as either benign leiomyomas or malignant leiomyosarcomas due to failing to meet all generally accepted criteria for either (Gupta et al., 2018). In the absence of definitive diagnostic criteria, STUMPs are often challenging to identify and typically require histopathological evaluation for confirmation; alternatively, a dedicated MRI protocol may be employed for risk stratification (Hindman et al., 2023).

STUMPs vary in behavior and prognosis, making patient-care team decisions about treatment challenging. Although hysterectomy has traditionally been considered the most effective method of treatment, a 2024 systematic review conducted at the National Cancer Institute of Milan yielded results indicating that resection of STUMP via myomectomy may be able to serve as a temporizing treatment for younger patients desiring preserved fertility (Bogani et al., 2025). Successful pregnancy after a STUMP diagnosis and subsequent myomectomy was first reported in 2016, with only a few cases published since that time (Bogani et al., 2025, Campbell et al., 2016). Optimal management remains unclear. We present two cases of STUMP myomectomy followed by successful pregnancies and delayed total hysterectomy, with both patients currently disease-free without further intervention. Based on the available literature, we present recommendations for the management of new and recurrent uterine STUMP cases, with an emphasis on balancing fertility preservation and the prevention of leiomyosarcoma.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 32-year-old woman, Gravida 2 Para 1011 was referred to our gynecologic oncology surgery clinic six months after a spontaneous abortion to discuss treatment options for a suspected epithelial ovarian neoplasm versus uterine mass. On a routine obstetric ultrasound during her first pregnancy three years prior, a 5.8 cm isoechoic mass abutting the right ovary and right lower uterine segment was noted and thought to be most consistent with an exophytic fibroid. Three years later, the patient experienced a spontaneous abortion, prompting a pelvic ultrasound that demonstrated a 19.6 × 15.9 × 11.8 cm mixed solid and cystic mass with multiple thick septations and color Doppler flow, abutting the uterus in the right pelvis (Fig. 1A). During her initial consultation, the patient expressed desire for fertility-preserving treatment. Thus, an exploratory laparotomy with likely right salpingo-oophorectomy to remove the large pelvic mass while preserving reproductive potential was recommended. Intraoperative findings revealed that the mass was not ovarian in origin, but rather arose from the right side of the posterior uterus as a smooth-surfaced subserosal neoplasm superficially involving the posterior myometrium. Given the patient’s desire to preserve her uterus, the mass was resected using via open myomectomy without morcellation. Given the size of the mass, it was found to extend to the right uterine cornua and vascular supply included that from the right utero-ovarian ligament necessitating sacrifice of the right utero-ovarian ligament. A right salpingectomy was performed and the right ovary was preserved and transposed via oophoropexy. The posterior myomectomy site was reapproximated in multiple layers. Frozen section pathology revealed a low-grade spindle cell neoplasm (Fig. 1C). Final pathology of the uterine mass demonstrated a highly mitotic STUMP (15–20 mitoses/10 high power fields (HPFs)) that did not meet criteria for leiomyosarcoma (Fig. 1D-H). A hysterectomy was recommended for definitive management; however, given the uncertain biological behavior of STUMP tumors and following shared decision making, the patient opted for continued conservative, fertility-preserving management with close surveillance.

As clinical studies have not demonstrated an optimal minimum time between myomectomy and attempting pregnancy that would favorably impact uterine rupture risk and expert recommendations suggest waiting anywhere between three and twelve months to attempt pregnancy (Margueritte et al., 2021), the patient was advised to wait for six months after her myomectomy before attempting to conceive. Pregnancy attempts began six months after the myomectomy with continued benign appearing uterus on MR imaging every six months. Additionally, to ensure the myomectomy had not impacted the left fallopian tube’s patency, a hysterosalpingogram (HSG) was performed which showed unilateral patency (Fig. 2A). After nine months of unsuccessful attempted spontaneous conception, a secondary infertility workup was initiated. Sperm analysis was conducted and normal. The patient was prescribed clomiphene citrate to induce ovulation. At 27 months post-myomectomy, at age 35, she conceived and carried a singleton pregnancy to term with delivery by planned caesarean section. While her pregnancy was additionally complicated by placenta previa and gestational hypertension, her cesarean section was uncomplicated and there was no evidence of STUMP at the time of pelvic exploration. Six months after delivery, a follow-up MRI revealed a right-sided three cm predominantly cystic adnexal lesion with areas of nodularity. Five months later, a subsequent MRI revealed significant growth of the right-sided lesion, suggesting STUMP recurrence and hysterectomy was recommended (Fig. 2B). She underwent a robotic-assisted hysterectomy and left salpingectomy, preserving both ovaries in situ. The right adnexal mass was found to be arising from the right posterior lower uterine segment (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). Surgical pathology revealed the recurrent mass to be a localized, highly cellular leiomyoma rather than a STUMP, with no other areas of disease (Fig. 2E). In summary, the patient initially underwent fertility-sparing surgical management via open myomectomy at the time of STUMP diagnosis. She subsequently achieved pregnancy resulting in a live birth via cesearean section three years later and one year after delivery—four years after her initial STUMP diagnosis—she underwent completion hysterectomy secondary to imaging findings concerning for recurrence. Case 2

A 29-year-old nulligravid woman was referred to our gynecologic oncology clinic with a uterine spindle cell neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential diagnosed at an outside institution with biopsy of an intramural uterine mass during attempted robotic myomectomy. She had a history of heavy and irregular menstruation and had experienced increasing dysmenorrhea. MR imaging revealed that over the prior seven years, her uterus had enlarged from a size of 9.9 × 6.4 x 5.4 cm with an anterior fibroid measuring 3.4 × 3.8 cm to measurements of 12.4 × 9.8 x 11.2 cm with an abnormal-appearing complex cystic intramural mass measuring 6.7 × 7.8 x 8.5 cm (Fig. 3A). After surgical consultation, she underwent an open myomectomy (Fig. 3B, C). A vertical elliptical incision was made on the anterior uterus, circumscribing the prior biopsy site with a margin of healthy appearing myometrium around the mass. Dissection was carried down around the intramural uterine mass, maintaining a cuff of surrounding normal appearing myometrium and avoiding rupture of the tumor. The uterine cavity was entered during the dissection. After the mass was excised and freed from the myometrium, the endometrium was reapproximated and the myometrial defect was closed in multiple layers. Pathologic review determined the uterine mass to be a highly mitotic STUMP (7 mitoses/10 HPF) (Fig. 3D-G) and fertility was able to be cautiously preserved. Following normal uterine appearance with no evidence of STUMP recurrence on pelvic MRI at 3 and 7 months out from the myomectomy as well as an HSG demonstrating bilateral fallopian tube patency, she began attempting pregnancy and eventually spontaneously conceived a singleton pregnancy 22 months after her myomectomy. With continued surveillance via pelvic imaging, a 6.6 x 6.8 x 7 cm mass with imaging appearance consistent with an endometrioma was identified involving her left ovary approximately six months after her spontaneous abortion (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, she underwent a robotic-assisted left salpingo oophorectomy with confirmation of the left ovarian mass being an endometrioma. During the procedure, a 4.7 cm mass involving the right anterior abdominal wall peritoneum was discovered and completely excised (Fig. 4B). Subsequent pathology revealed the excised mass to be a STUMP recurrence (Fig. 4C) and upon further review, the mass had been evident on her preoperative MRI (Fig. 4A). She went on to spontaneously conceive two subsequent singleton pregnancies (approximately 4 and 6 years after initial STUMP diagnosis), both of which were uneventful and delivered at term via planned caesarean sections with bilateral salpingectomy performed at the time of the second cesarean section. Having completed childbearing, she underwent a laparoscopic total hysterectomy 18 months after her second cesarean section (7.5 years after her initial STUMP diagnosis) and retainined her right ovary. There were no peritoneal implants at surgery or on preoperative MR, her uterus appeared normal with a small subserosal fibroid and final pathology revealed no evidence of persistent or recurrent STUMP. Discussion

We present two patients in which shared decision-making balanced fertility preservation with neoplastic resection to optimize completion of family building. While both patients were recommended to undergo total hysterectomy at the time of their original STUMP diagnosis as the criterion standard treatment for uterine STUMPs (Gadducci and Zannoni, 2019), both elected initial conservative management via myomectomy and successfully led to a total of four pregnancies and three term live births between them. Both patients we report on were first diagnosed with their STUMP tumors in 2018 and since then, data on fertility-preserving management of STUMP tumors has grown. In 2025, Bogani et al. published a systematic review that included nine retrospective studies involving 159 people with STUMP tumors initially treated with myomectomy. Likely the largest study on this rare tumor diagnosis, they found that while patients managed with myomectomy were more likely to develop a STUMP recurrence compared to patients treated with hysterectomy (n = 168) (11.9 % vs 4.1 %, respectively; p = 0.046), there was no significant difference between the two groups in the rate of uterine sarcoma development (1.3 % vs 2.4 %, respectively; p = 0.68) (Bogani et al., 2025). Additionally, pregnancy did not seem to increase the risk of STUMP recurrence and nearly 40 % of those who desired to conceive were able to achieve pregnancy. While both of our patients experienced recurrences, one may have been secondary to disruption of the tumor during an attempted myomectomy converted to diagnostic excision of a portion of the tumor and the other was confined to the uterus and, while technically a smooth muscle neoplasm, did not reach mitotic index criteria for STUMP. With relatively long-term follow-up for both of our patients, we have not observed further recurrences and both were able to conceive and safely carry pregnancies to term. Our case series adds reassuring outcome data in the setting of a rare tumor. Taken together, these findings suggest that myomectomy can be considered a safe temporizing treatment for STUMP in women desiring fertility preservation, with hysterectomy representing the longer-term recommendation once patients have completed childbearing.

Our series also underscores the importance of accurate diagnosis of a STUMP tumor when considering fertility-preserving management via myomectomy and that higher mitotic index may portend a greater chance of recurrence. In fact, Borella et al. demonstrated that STUMPs with 6 or more mitoses/10 high power fields (HPFs) were at higher risk for recurrence (Borella et al., 2022). Using this cutoff to define low mitotic v. high mitotic counts, highly mitotic STUMPs were almost 5 times more likely to recur. Both cases examined in our case series would have been considered to have high mitotic counts at primary diagnosis, with one STUMP having a mitotic index of 15–20/10 HPFs and the other demonstrating a mitotic index of 7/10 HPFs. As such, mitotic index may help guide clinical decision-making, especially counseling on recurrence risks in the setting of considering fertility-preserving myomectomy and surveillance strategies.

Multidisciplinary collaborative care was also a cornerstone of the successful outcomes demonstrated in these two cases. Highlighted in a study by Bucuri et al., combined efforts of experts across disciplines is one of the most effective methods in optimizing patient outcomes in the setting of a STUMP diagnosis (Bucuri et al., 2024). In these cases, care management involved expertise from pathology, radiology, and gynecologic oncology surgery focused on tumor management, while reproductive endocrinology, infertility specialty care, and maternal-fetal medicine both played important roles in improving fertility and carefully handling the elevated-risk pregnancies in which cesarean section was advised due to the prior myomectomies. Uterine STUMPS continue to represent rare tumors and collaboratively pooling opinions improve clinical ability to most accurately diagnose and carefully manage STUMPs (Basaran et al., 2017). Given the high stakes associated with an incorrect STUMP diagnosis, careful processing of clinicopathologic information, close surveillance for clinical or radiological findings, optimally with MRI, that may suggest transformation to or recurrence as malignancy, and a recommendation for completion hysterectomy once fertility is no longer desired appear to be safe alternatives to upfront hysterectomy.

Conclusion

Uterine STUMPs, although challenging to define optimal conservative treatment due to their unpredictable behavior and rare incidence, appear to allow an opportunity for temporary fertility preserving management. As this case series and recent systematic reviews have suggested, short-term disease management via myomectomy appears to be safe. However, since STUMP recurrence rates also seem to be lower after hysterectomy, hysterectomy after childbearing is complete appears advisable. Furthermore, care teams should consider approaching fertility preservation in the context of a uterine STUMP carefully, with a multidisciplinary approach and close surveillance.
