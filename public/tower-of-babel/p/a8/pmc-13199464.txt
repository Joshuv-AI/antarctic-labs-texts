Collaborative relationships, disciplinary and global culture, social identity and scientific status shape how scholars cite prior work
Authors: Xiaohuan Xia, Mathieu Ouellet, Shubhankar P. Patankar, Isabella C. Stallworthy, Julia K. Brynildsen, Diana I. Tamir, Dani S. Bassett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Modern science is formally structured around scholarly publication, where scientific knowledge is canonized through citation. Precisely how citations are given and accrued can provide information about the value of discovery, the history of scientific ideas, the structure of fields, and the space or scope of inquiry. Yet parsing this information has been challenging because citations are not simply present or absent; rather, they differ in purpose, function, and sentiment. In this paper, we investigated how critical and favorable sentiments were distributed across citations, and tested the hypothesis that more favorable sentiment would be utilized among ingroup members, whereas more critical sentiment would be utilized toward outgroup members. We considered three group types: collaborators, those with a similar h-index, and those with the same gender. We observed that citation sentiment was more favorable to collaborators than non-collaborators, an effect that was modulated by both gender and h-index. Further, we observed that disciplinary and country-level factors impacting a scholar’s perception of the size of their ingroup also explained citation sentiment: Largely experimental fields that engage in regular scholarly synthesis through review articles employed less citation sentiment, as did countries with more collectivist attitudes and greater acceptance of social hierarchies. Collectively, we demonstrated how sociocultural groups displayed ingroup preferences in the use of sentiment in scientific communication. Our study contributes to a broader understanding of how human factors influence the practice of science, and underscores the importance of considering the larger sociocultural contexts in which science progresses.

Introduction

In the modern era, science has often been assumed to be an objective process1. By this descriptor, scholars have variously meant faithfulness to facts, absence of normative commitments, and freedom from personal biases2. Yet with an increasing understanding of human psychology has come an acknowledgment that even the most scrupulous of researchers cannot achieve various types of objectivity; for example, (1) the mechanical objectivity of suppressing the universal human propensity to judge and aestheticize, or (2) the aperspectival objectivity of eliminating individual idiosyncracies3. The growing appreciation of human subjectivity has motivated a turn from the personal to the community, where objectivity is considered a quality that characterizes a collection or population of studies4, and hence is a feature of scientific communities and their practices5. In communities, objectivity can further be thought of as occurring in degrees, such that any method of inquiry is only objective to the degree that it permits transformative criticism through shared standards, equality of intellectual authority, avenues for criticism, and uptake of criticism6.

Yet, the above discourse begs the question: Need objectivity always be fundamentally at odds with subjectivity? In a complementary line of work, it has been noted that strong objectivity can be maintained without the requirement of value-neutrality7 or the suppression of all aspects of the self8, including one’s epistemological standpoint9 or ethical commitments10. The potential coexistence of certain objectivities and certain subjectivities has proven useful in evaluating the scientific self, which exists among values, standpoints, cognitive biases, and human perceptions11. Indeed, scientists specifically, and scholars more broadly, exist in multiply-dependent cultural contexts—from the local collaborative group or research institution to the scientific discipline or the geographically-defined culture—that each evince distinct norms, values, currencies, and artifacts12. For example, separate disciplines recommend distinct forms of scientific rationality, methods to investigate the world, and factors that others in the community would appreciate13; separate countries can have social and funding norms that impact collaborative tendencies14; and even different fields of thought work under distinct notions of theoretical aesthetics, the value of parsimony, and preferred characteristics of explanatory structures15.

Scientific selves are not autonomous and culture-free individuals but are shaped by interactions within scientific networks, disciplinary communities, and social contexts11, each associated with its own standards, mores, practices, and languages12. This multidimensional culture within which scientists exist accumulates its archives through scientific publications12. Although many papers go uncited or quickly become obsolete16, others become canonized knowledge through citation17. Citation networks have played a key role in the sociology of science and its critical evaluation18–21, with citations serving many functions and roles; they can be differently valued22, be positive or negative, supportive or contrastive, attribute creativity or indicate status as a “classic”23,24, misrepresent the cited work or be chosen for social reasons25, and more. Among these functions, citation sentiment has gained much attention26.

Most studies classify citation sentiment as positive or negative26, while others emphasize its critical or contrasting dimension27,28. While these classification themes are common, most methods show limited performance26, with few using deep learning or more sophisticated approaches28,29 to assess sentiment accurately and efficiently. In addition, computational and data accessibility limitations have made large-scale scientific sentiment analysis beyond basic statistics challenging. As such, using a large language model to parse citation sentences in batches could help large-scale citation analysis. Further, to tap into dimensions beyond basic sentiment descriptions, the sociocultural context of researchers could help interpret sentiment patterns. Since citations involve both citing and cited authors, it is important to understand how social context such as ingroup/outgroup relations between author groups can explain the observed sentiment. While studies have identified ingroup/outgroup biases in citation count related to authors’ affiliation to journal editors30 and to journals’ affiliation31, citation sentiment ingroup biases and differences remain understudied.

Here, we used a large language model to classify citation sentiment at scale (Fig. 1), while examining its dependence on sociocultural group dynamics (Fig. 2). Group affiliations can be very powerful, as social identity theory shows that people are motivated to favor their ingroup to boost self-esteem derived from group membership, and even arbitrary grouping induces such a bias32,33. Intergroup conflicts from competing resources can further intensify ingroup favoritism and outgroup hostility34. Similarly, scientific communities may exhibit such ingroup dynamics. As such, our framework (Fig. 2) studied whether the ingroup dynamics could appear in science through varied sentiment towards each other, given researchers’ sociocultural identities or affiliations. We studied two ways in which ingroup dynamics might be observed in sentiment. First, sentiment between members might vary depending on the groups (such as a group of collaborators) to which they belong. Second, sentiment might also be generally different given larger contextual dimensions, such as disciplinary practice, along which perceived ingroup size might vary. To ensure that our assessment was computationally tractable while remaining sensitive to local cultural norms, we focused on neuroscience, analyzing papers from 56,034 last authors across 181 journals (impact factor ≥3 in 2022). Neuroscience suited this analysis due to its youth, interdisciplinarity, methodological diversity, and a marked intersection of opinions35,36, and hence comprising a rich locus of citation sentiment patterns. We focused on a ternary sentiment definition (neutral, favorable, or critical) to examine sociocultural ingroup/outgroup dynamics in scientific citations. We divided our broader investigation into five subareas based on the relevant literature, each accompanied by a specific testable hypothesis. First, to examine how sentiment might reflect a natural academic ingroup/outgroup relation, namely collaboration, we measured collaboration distance: the geodesic distance in the collaboration network (built from coauthorship data) between citer and citee37 at the year of citation. In prior work, ingroup biases have been found in citation count related to author’s affiliation to journal editors30 and to journal’s affiliation31, and in coauthorship related to ethnic groups (researchers of similar ethnicity tend to coauthor more frequently)38. Thus, we hypothesized that such an ingroup bias would extend to citation languages related to collaboration and coauthorship; that is, that people would cite collaborators more favorably and less critically than non-collaborators. (We refer to this hypothesis as “H1”.) Beyond collaboration, we next examined how citation sentiment might track ingroup/outgroup dynamics among scholars of varying h-indices, a marker of social status in science. We hypothesized that scholars would cite others (outgroup) with different h-indices more critically and less favorably than those (ingroup) with similar h-indices (H2). An element that can interact with these scientific factors such as coauthorship and status is personal identity, for example along dimensions of gender, sex, race, ethnicity, class, and (dis)ability. Drawing on a broad literature demonstrating that gender has a known relation to writing style (including language use, rhetorical strategies, and authorial positioning39–43) and citation practice18,44–48, we hypothesized that gender would interact with collaboration in explaining citation sentiment in science (H3).

Thus far, we hypothesized that citation sentiment would track ingroup/outgroup dynamics across interpersonal factors from status (h-index) and relationships (collaboration) to personal identity (gender). We next turned to larger social groups in the form of scholarly disciplines, which have their own social norms, cultures49,50, and identities51. Prior work demonstrates that disciplines differ in communication52, each having unique rhetoric53, writing styles54, and information sharing processes52. Further, literature showed that the perceived ingroup size differs between people55 and that ingroup perception can be changed by shared cultural context56. Building on prior literature, we hypothesized that in disciplines with larger perceived ingroup sizes, scholars would exhibit more ingroup behavior, namely less critical and more favorable sentiment (H4).

With hypotheses H1–H4, then, we considered a scholar’s personal factors and their location within social structures from small collaborations to wider disciplines. But the sociocultural milieu for scholars extends outwards to the national scale, with each country having distinct cultures that can manifest in scientific practice. Cultural differences in academic writing are well-known, and typically studied under the framing of intercultural or contrastive rhetoric and applied linguistics57–59. Importantly, smaller cultures (e.g., personal, disciplinary) can interact with national culture60, often in somewhat tacit and unthinking writing practices61. Prior work examines cultural differences in economic functionality, capitalism, and individualism that manifest in both academic and popular writing61–63. Importantly, cultural differences extend beyond writing style to citation practices64, including voice and stance65,66, density67, dialogic contraction/expansion64, and acknowledgment of limitations68. To examine national culture, we probed how cultural differences at the country scale might impact citation sentiment. We expected that such cultural variation would include variation in the perceived size of the ingroup size. Intuitively, we reasoned that two factors in Hofstede’s 6D model69,70—individualism and power-distance—could affect the perception of ingroup size in a sociocultural sense. Individualism emphasizes individuals over collectives, which could result in smaller perceived social groups. Power-distance emphasizes the breadth of hierarchies from subordinates to superiors, which could result in larger perceived social groups. Accordingly, we hypothesized that low individualism and high power-distance cultures would have a larger perceived ingroup size, and hence would adopt ingroup behaviors more generally, including less critical and more favorable citation sentiment (H5).

In testing these hypotheses, we show how collaborative relationships, disciplinary and global culture, social identity and scientific status shape how we as scientists cite the work of other scholars, underscoring the larger sociocultural context in which science progresses.

Methods

Citation and author data

We used PubMed Central and the Web of Science API to build our datasets for analyses. The PubMed Central (PMC) Open Access Subset (OA) includes millions of research articles and pre-prints alongside relevant metadata, such as article type, venue of publication, and institutional information71. We gathered all English language research and review articles from PMC OA relating to neuroscience that were published between 1998 and 2023 and that cited at least one article. In parallel, using the Journal Citation Reports by Clarivate, we compiled a list of journals classified under the “neurosciences” category with an impact factor of at least 3 in the year 2022. We manually added three more related journals (“Epilepsia”, “NeuroImage: Clinical”, and “Nature Reviews: Neurology”; they also had a journal impact factor of at least 3) creating a list of 181 journals in which the gathered articles were published. Each article in the PMC OA subset was stored in an extensible markup language (XML) file with attached tags delineating its several components. For instance, the <article-title> tag encloses an article’s title, <abstract> encloses the abstract, and <xref> encloses a citation in the body of the text.

Using these and other relevant tags, we extracted each article’s title, list of authors, and sentences containing citations to other works in the same dataset. Sentences in captions, footnotes, and abstract are excluded. The dataset contained 627,108 citation sentences, 383,292 unique authors (56,034 last authors) across 27 departments and 27 countries and regions identified. From the full list of authors, we focused on last authors only throughout the manuscript, except when building collaboration networks, for which all authors were used.

To study the relation between citation sentiment and sociocultural groups, we examined sociocultural factors including collaboration, gender, h-index, discipline, and country. To assess collaboration, we constructed yearly collaboration networks where nodes represent authors and edges represent coauthorship of papers published up to and including that year in our dataset. Then, we define the collaboration distance between scholars to be the shortest path length between two authors in the collaboration network at the year of the citing paper’s publication. To assess gender, we used Gender API72 to infer probabilistic gender from the names of the last authors, including only names where the underlying model is at least 70% confident in its prediction and has a sample size of at least 20. Qualitatively, Gender API demonstrated high gender prediction accuracy on non-Asian names, least accuracy on Asian names, but was still overall one of the best performing gender prediction tools73,74. To assess h-index, we obtained 69.24% of last authors’ h-indices from the Web of Science. To assess discipline and country, we recorded the last author affiliations from the database and from which we extracted department and country/region information whenever available. We operationalized “similar h-index groups” as the groups of citations where the last author h-index difference between the citer and the citee was in the range [ −15, 15), with a min-max difference of 30; remaining h-index groups shared the same difference and were addressed as “different h-index groups”. Because a paper’s last author could be affiliated with more than one department or country, the citation sentences of a given paper would be considered for every department or country involved. In our analyses, we only used data where variables of interest were not missing. To study citation sentiment restricted to our dataset, we chose only the articles in the largest weakly connected component in the citation network, built from filtered papers (as described earlier) where both citing and cited papers were in our dataset (citations determined using reference lists). Among them, we chose only those from which we were able to parse the citation sentences. This resulted in 108,909 articles. For Welch’s t-tests and weighted linear regressions F-tests, data distributions were assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested. For t-tests and non-weighted linear regressions F-tests, data distributions were assumed to be both normal and homoscedastic, but these were not formally tested. This study was not preregistered. Ethics was not needed for this observational study where data are either publicly available, available through Web of Science, or inferred.

Measuring sentiment

Conceptually, we were motivated to assess the sentiment that scholars use when they cite other scholars’ work. We considered three types: neutral, favorable, and critical. Neutral sentiment is observed in factual phrases or statements, devoid of opinion (e.g., “was reported”, “has been shown”, “can alter”, “can play a role”, “is”, “responds to”). Favorable sentiment is observed in phrases or statements that indicate a positive assessment or evaluation (e.g., adjectival phrases like “elegantly shown”, “optimized method”, “powerful tools”) or that indicate consensus or consistency (e.g., “converging results”, “conclusion is consistent”). Critical sentiment is observed in phrases or statements that indicate debate (“the focus of debate”), disagreement (“while other studies do X, we argue Y”), difference (“in contrast to a previous report”), distinction (“is not applicable here”), distance (“despite the fact that many studies have evaluated X, very few have evaluated Y”), or limitation (“however, X method limits desirable outcome Y”). In this formulation, then, sentiment captures both social and epistemic alignment by measuring evaluation and intellectual commonality, respectively.

To move from conception to quantification, we assessed citation sentiment using gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14, a large language model (LLM) by OpenAI. LLMs are trained on a large corpus of text data and can generate language across various domains. Through their exposure during training to diverse contexts, they can recognize subjective elements of human language, such as tone75, irony76,77, sarcasm78, and implied meaning79. We used gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 to classify sentiment as critical, neutral, or favorable, and we defined citation sentiment as the sentiment of sentences containing citations to papers in our dataset. We measured sentiment at the sentence level for two reasons. First, sentences are the smallest unit that isolates individual sentiments without overlap. Second, prior work shows limited improvement when extending context beyond sentences without manual selection28, while substantially increasing computational costs.

We started with an initial prompt and definition of citation sentiment adapted from prior literature27. We then carried out multiple iterations in which the LLM model rated a subset of sentences and three human raters graded the LLM answers indicating whether they agreed or disagreed with the LLM. If the average agreement with the LLM and/or the interrater agreement among human raters were not qualitatively satisfactory, we would improve the prompt and redo the process. The final prompt has human-LLM agreement proportions of 0.78 CI (0.66, 0.88), 0.83 CI (0.71, 0.92), 0.8 CI (0.68, 0.89) (n = 60 using 95% Clopper-Pearson exact interval assuming binomial distribution for each of three raters), and interrater agreement (Gwet’s AC1 score) among three human raters of 0.89, 95% CI (0.80, 0.97), p < 0.001, n = 60. Further details on the prompt engineering process and model performance validation are available in Supplementary Methods. Each entry in the citation data contained a single sentence with possibly multiple citations. Consider, for instance, the following sentence with two citations: “Lorem ipsum odor amet [citation 1], consectetuer adipiscing elit [citation 2].” This text results in two entries for the LLM to judge: (1) “Lorem ipsum odor amet [citation 1], consectetuer adipiscing elit”, and (2) “Lorem ipsum odor amet, consectetuer adipiscing elit [citation 2].” Using the OpenAI API, we passed each entry to the model separately for sentiment analysis. In practice, each citation location in a sentence was replaced by the Unicode character with encoding U+272A (see Supplementary Methods for full model prompt). It is common for an article to cite another article multiple times in different locations in the text. We aggregated overall sentiment for a given citer-citee paper pair through the following order of precedence: Critical sentiment took precedence over favorable sentiment, and both took precedence over neutral sentiment (Fig. 1)80,81. We used this aggregated sentiment measure throughout the analysis. We defined “ingroup/outgroup bias” as being more favorable or less critical when citing ingroup members. We defined (coauthorship) “sentiment bias” as the difference in sentiment between citing non-collaborators and citing collaborators relative to null expectations, which we explain in greater detail below.

Null model construction

We used a null model to benchmark observed sentiment values against those that might emerge by random chance. For instance, in a preliminary qualitative analysis, we found that papers with similar content were likely to involve critical citations, thereby increasing the likelihood that such a citation in the data was an outcome of the similarity of work and not an underlying bias of interest. Also descriptively, we observed similar effects with article type (review vs. research) and the number of times an article references another: A review article tends to be more neutral in sentiment compared to a research article (see Supplementary Table 1), and an article that cites another article more frequently tends to be more critical. Additionally, because we aggregated sentiment, the citation frequency would affect the resulting sentiment (see Supplementary Notes 1). We used these factors to measure the expected probability of observing a critical, neutral, or favorable sentiment for any given citer-citee pair. First, relating to shared content, we created three quantile bins for high, medium, and low similarity, where similarity was measured as the cosine distance (normalized between −1 and + 1) between the vector embeddings of article titles. The article titles were extracted using a PMC parser82 wherever available. The embeddings were generated using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small model. Additionally, 15 bins were associated with citation frequency and 2 with article type. Initially, for each of the 90 (3 × 15 × 2) resulting combinations, we performed maximum likelihood estimation to compute the probability that a citation in the data belonged to a given group. However, if a bin had fewer than 500 samples, we used the marginal distribution over title similarity to create a larger bin. In these instances, we chose to ignore the impact of title similarity since it qualitatively had the weakest effect on citation sentiment compared to article type and citation frequency. Further, when a citing-cited pair had a citation frequency greater than 15, we used the corresponding bin for 15 citations instead. This procedure allowed us to assign a null probability of three sentiments to a citing-cited pair from one of the 57 resulting bins.

Consider a set of citations corresponding to a broader category of interest, such as citations between collaborators or citations between authors with an h-index difference of 30. Given such a set, we can measure the proportions of favorable, neutral, and critical citations within it for both the empirical observations and the null realizations drawn using the assigned null probabilities. However, if the set is small in size, the measured proportions may not accurately represent the true underlying distribution of sentiments for the group. Additionally, there are variations to null realizations due to the probabilistic nature of our null model. To mitigate these two concerns, we used a bootstrapping approach. We created 1000 resampled sets, each of which drew n samples with replacement from the original n observations. In each resampled set, we then measured the empirical sentiment proportions as well as null sentiment proportions using null realizations, and calculated the percentage difference of the empirical proportions relative to the null proportions. This resulting difference measure (which we refer to as “sentiment”) served as our outcome variable (y-axis) across all analyses. As a result, these 1000 resampled sets produced 1000 × 3 sentiment values. Bootstrapping in this manner helps to construct robust distributions and derive more accurate measures of variability. This approach combined with our null model design thus accounts for varying group sizes, null model randomness, and the three identified confounding factors. For a given sentiment, each data point in scatter plots and each box in box plots corresponds to these distribution samples (n = 1000), where the scatter points and their error bars are mean values and standard deviations from these 1000 samples. We tested group differences in sentiment using a Welch’s t-test, which is built for unequal variances and robust to deviations from normality in large samples such as those we have in our dataset.

Departmental and cultural measures

To examine how citation sentiment might reflect ingroup/outgroup dynamics according to disciplinary and global culture, we evaluated measures at the departmental and country scales. To examine disciplinary culture, we selected 28 departments (see Supplementary Table 2 for a full list of 28 departments). To examine global culture, we extracted the country or region of the last author’s affiliation. To ensure sufficient sample sizes for the corresponding regression analyses, we subsampled this broader dataset to only include disciplines and countries that had at least 100 citations towards collaborators. This procedure resulted in 27 departments and 27 countries and regions (see Supplementary Table 3 for a full list of 27 countries and regions). In the regression analyses, we assumed linearity and heteroskedasticity, which are shown in the trends and the varying error bars (standard deviations of the bootstrapped samples) in the scatter plots; as such, the linear regressions were fitted with weighted least squares using inverse-variance weights.

We hypothesized that disciplines with larger perceived ingroup size would display more ingroup behavior (H4). In order to test this hypothesis, we considered two aspects of disciplinary culture as proxies for perceived ingroup size. The first disciplinary culture measure was scholarly synthesis, defined as the proportion of review papers in a discipline. Review papers typically cited more references than research papers (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for statistics of a linear relationship between the synthesis score and average reference list length), exposing authors to a wider variety of scholarly works, sub-communities, theoretical commitments, modeling approaches, and subfields. A high synthesis score thus indicates a greater exposure to diverse scholarly circles, suggesting an enlarged perceived scholarly ingroup size. Scholars perceiving a larger ingroup size may exhibit ingroup behavior more in their general communications, regardless of the cited groups. This translated into a testable hypothesis that disciplines with a high synthesis score would cite others more favorably or less critically in general. We identified the review or research article type by the article-type variable from the XML file for each paper.

The second disciplinary culture measure was scholarly benchwork score, defined as the proportion of more theoretical or computational papers, or drylab papers (in contrast to more experimental or empirical papers, or wetlab papers). We reasoned that, in neuroscience, wetlab disciplines tend to have larger labs, conferences, and funding levels, thereby enlarging the scholars’ perception of the ingroup size. We also noted that disciplines with a higher ratio of wetlab papers also tended to have longer reference lists (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for details, including full statistics), further consistent with differences in both actual and perceived community size. Whether a paper was a wetlab paper was measured using the same model (gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14) as well as largely the same iterative process to refine the prompt as the sentiment case; the only differences in the iterative validation process were that no rationales for answers were involved and the number of samples to parse was smaller but the text parsed was full text in the benchwork case. The final prompt has human-LLM agreement proportions of 0.93 CI (0.8, 0.98), 0.9 CI (0.76, 0.97), 0.97 CI (0.87, 1) (n = 40 using 95% Clopper-Pearson exact interval assuming binomial distribution for each of three raters), and interrater agreement (Gwet’s AC1 score) among three human raters of 0.92, 95% CI (0.84, 1.00), p < 0.001, n = 40. For each of the 28 departments, we first sampled 100 articles, excluding reviews. We then prompted the model using the final prompt to determine whether a given article was a product of benchwork by examining the textual content of the paper. The final prompt text as well as model performance supplied to the LLM are available in Supplementary Methods. The content was extracted using the same PMC parser82 as the one we used for title extraction, and up to 100,000 tokens (roughly 75K words) of text were included for each article. The benchwork score for a department was defined as the fraction of papers identified by the model having originated in a wet lab.

In addition to synthesis and benchwork, we selected paper team size (average number of authors on a paper) and interdisciplinary/international collaborations (average number of departments/countries of all authors’ affiliations of a paper) as potential alternative ingroup perception-related measures, reasoning that larger teams or collaborations could enlarge the perceived ingroup size. Further, it would be of interest to consider how some of these factors might interact with others, given the evidence from social psychology that individuals can be affiliated with multiple groups, and a complex interplay between those group identities can influence observed behavior83–85. As an initial effort in this direction, we considered how personal factors (such as gender) could interact with other factors to explain sentiment86. Specifically, we conducted a moderator analysis to test an additional hypothesis that gender would moderate the relationship between status and sentiment, and between disciplinary synthesis and sentiment.

To examine how citation sentiment might vary by perceived ingroup size in different countries, we evaluated two measures from Hofstede’s 6-D model of global culture: individualism and power-distance69,70. Individualism is the “extent to which people feel independent, as opposed to being interdependent as members of larger wholes”87; in this sense, individualist culture favors the interests of the individual over the collective69,70. Hence, a person who holds individualistic attitudes can be described as having a small perceived ingroup size (a size of 1: the individual), whereas a person who holds collectivistic attitudes can be described as having a larger perceived ingroup size (the size of the collective). Further, power-distance is the “extent to which the less powerful members of organizations and institutions (like the family) accept and expect that power is distributed unequally”87. It reflects the acceptance of social hierarchy (spanning from superiors to subordinates), and foregrounds loyalty and deference with the understanding that superiors care for subordinates, who in turn show respect and often avoid challenging authority69,70. As such, high power-distance can be associated with a larger perceived size of the ingroup (e.g., subordinates and superiors being part of the same group), whereas low power-distance can be associated with a smaller perceived ingroup size (e.g., subordinates seeing themselves as a separate group, standing against superiors).

Citation diversity statement

Recent work in several fields of science has identified a bias in citation practices such that papers from women and other minority scholars are under-cited relative to the number of such papers in the field45–48,88–92. Here, we sought to proactively consider choosing references that reflect the diversity of the field in thought, form of contribution, gender, race, ethnicity, and other factors. First, we obtained the predicted gender of the first and last author of each reference by using databases that store the probability of a first name being carried by a woman46,93. By this measure (and excluding self-citations to the first and last authors of our current paper), our references contain 19.18% woman(first)/woman(last), 12.76% man/woman, 10.31% woman/man, and 57.76% man/man. This method is limited in that (a) names, pronouns, and social media profiles used to construct the databases may not, in every case, be indicative of gender identity, (b) it cannot account for intersex, non-binary, or transgender people, and (c) the ability to assign a predicted gender for European names may be better than for Chinese names, especially when written in the Roman alphabet. Second, we obtained predicted racial/ethnic category of the first and last author of each reference by databases that store the probability of a first and last name being carried by an author of color94,95. By this measure (and excluding self-citations), our references contain 15.22% author of color (first)/author of color(last), 16.36% white author/author of color, 15.83% author of color/white author, and 52.59% white author/white author. This method is limited in that (a) names and Florida Voter Data to make the predictions may not be indicative of racial/ethnic identity, and (b) it cannot account for Indigenous and mixed-race authors, or those who may face differential biases due to the ambiguous racialization or ethnicization of their names. We look forward to future work that could help us to better understand how to support equitable practices in science.

Results

In a large connected citation network of 108,909 papers from the PMC Open Access Subset, we assessed one type of sentiment (neutral, favorable, or critical) for the sentences in the presence of a citation. After measuring the sentiment of 627,108 extracted citation sentences, counted by the number of citation locations in a sentence, we aggregated them by citer-citee pairs (see Methods). Overall, 8.83% were critical, 30.64% were favorable, and 60.53% were neutral (see Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1 for the qualitative effects of aggregation on sentiment distribution).

Citation sentiment is most favorable to collaborators

We hypothesized that people would cite collaborators more favorably and less critically than non-collaborators (H1). Consistent with our hypothesis, scholars tended to express more favorable sentiment to their collaborators than to non-collaborators (Fig. 3A; two-sided Welch’s t-test, 4.77% more favorable in average sentiment proportions, 95% CI (4.69%, 4.84%), t(1216.70) = 127.18, p < 0.001), and less critical sentiment (Fig. 3A; two-sided Welch’s t-test, 12.91% less critical in average sentiment proportions, 95% CI (12.77%, 13.04%), t(1300.99) = 189.14, p < 0.001). Accordingly, we tested whether social proximity alone would affect sentiment after controlling for topic proximity with our null model. Discussion

Scientific writing is a social act96, a site of cultural production97, and a means of communicating with peers for various purposes98. Citations and their sentiment form a key communication channel. Early work examined this channel by cataloging evaluative verbs in citation sentences to assess the sentiments of evaluation and negation99, while later studies used computational methods to assess citation functions and motivations from content100. These functions have been categorized as positive, negative, and neutral sentiment101, or uncertainty versus utility102. We extended prior work by analyzing over 100,000 papers across 181 neuroscience journals using a large language model for efficient sentiment quantification, while partitioning the data into different groups to examine their sociocultural correlates.

We hypothesized that ingroup/outgroup dynamics would influence the sentiment of sentences in the presence of citations, such that less critical sentiment and more favorable sentiment would be used when citing ingroup members. We also expected a larger perceived ingroup size to manifest in less critical or more favorable language, regardless of the cited groups. We systematically tested our expectations across collaboration history, status similarity, and gender groups. Results confirmed significant sentiment variations that tracked ingroup/outgroup membership across various group-defining factors, with overall sentiment trends explained by disciplinary and country cultures thought to modulate perceived ingroup size. Collectively, the data point to a focal role for ingroup-outgroup dynamics, hierarchy, power, and social status in the use of sentiment in modern citation practices.

Citation sentiment, coauthorship, and status

Communication is critical for cooperation103. In science, cooperation can take on many forms, but one key type that has risen in prominence recently is collaboration104,105. How might communication through citation sentiment reflect and support collaborative relationships? One mechanism by which citation might support collaborative relationships is by indicating inclusion in an author’s social ingroup. Here, we evaluate collaboration via coauthorship—a widely accepted proxy even though not all collaborations are rewarded with coauthorship106. We found that scholars cited their collaborators more favorably and less critically than they cited non-collaborators, consistent with a pattern of behavior in which ingroup relationships were marked by positive citation sentiment. We further found that citations made between authors who would later become collaborators were significantly more critical and less favorable than citations made between collaborators. This pattern suggests that collaboration may transform critical sentiment into favorable sentiment. An alternative explanation is topic switching, where scholars work on more aligned topics after collaboration. Qualitatively, our null model partially addresses this potential explanation by controlling for title similarity as a proxy for topic alignment. Utilizing science communication practices such as favorable sentiment could support collaboration among ingroup members by increasing trust, reducing competition, and supporting the social cohesion that allows for knowledge transfer107. In turn, scholarly collaboration can enhance the quality of the scientific work108, which can then provide scholars with greater status in the scientific community.

Communication sustains not only cooperation but also social status systems that can either support109 or undermine cooperation110. In science, a prominent system of social status or hierarchy combines research productivity and quality111. Using h-index as a proxy, we found that scholars cited different h-index groups more critically and less favorably than when scholars cited similar h-index groups if they were not collaborators, but the pattern reversed (mostly for critical sentiment) if they were collaborators. It is interesting to consider to what degree this variation by h-index might track academic seniority, with which the h-index is non-linearly associated112,113, and might be further influenced by gender, institution, and other factors114–116. Future efforts considering academic age as measured from CVs or survey responses could further quantify this relationship117,118. More generally, our observation of an interaction between collaboration and status in explaining citation sentiment is consistent with evidence from social psychology that individuals can be affiliated with multiple groups, and a complex interplay between those group identities can influence observed behaviors83–85. It is further consistent with evidence in science of science that prestige may interact with coauthorship in terms of coauthor’s publication rates119 and citation counts120. Here, we showed that features of scientific language (such as sentiment) comprised another dimension along which status and collaboration jointly operated, revealing rich social dynamics in scientific communication.

Citation sentiment and gender

The non-scholarly systems that scientists work within exist not only at the large scale of countries but also at the small scale of identities. A key sociocultural difference exists between those people socialized (or living) as women and those socialized (or living) as men121. These differences arise along multiple dimensions of experience that could impact science, for example in linguistic behavior122 and in social behavior123. Consistent with these general observations, gender differences have been identified in how scholars engage in the scientific process, from writing styles124 and research areas125 to self-promotion practices126, self-citation127, positive presentation of their own results128, and use of generic statements, framing an idea as broad, timeless, and universally true124. Here, we demonstrated that those gender differences also extended to the sentiment with which scholars cited one another. We found that men cited with greater sentiment—both critical and favorable—and women cited with greater sentiment bias, being more favorable to collaborators than to non-collaborators.

Why might men cite with more affect than women? Prior work has provided evidence that men engage socially in science, more so than women; for example, men are more likely to share their papers socially, and preferentially do so with other men129. This out-sized social engagement in science holds both in laboratory settings and in evaluations of real-world behavior on social media126,130. Broadly, it suggests that men may treat science as a small-scale society, where prior evidence marks a key interdependence between cooperation (here, perhaps supported by favorable sentiment) and status hierarchy (here, perhaps upheld by critical sentiment)131. Why might women cite with greater sentiment bias than men, assigning more favorable (and less critical) sentiment to collaborators than to non-collaborators? Prior work suggests that women, on average, tend to use more affiliative language whereas men use more aggressive language (and are more generally talkative)132. Those affiliative tendencies may manifest in the use of citation sentiment to strengthen ingroup relations, via more favorable and less critical language.

It is notable that the degree of ingroup bias and its interaction with collaboration status both depended on gender identity. This interaction is again consistent with the fact that individuals can be affiliated with multiple groups, and a complex interplay between those group identities can influence observed behaviors83–85. Future work could consider how gender differences might intersect with other dimensions of identity including race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, and socioeconomic status.

Citation sentiment and discipline

Disciplines differ in their values, practices, criteria for progress, models, justification, and evidence, as well as their level of explanation133–135. A key open question is how citation practices might track these disciplinary differences. Prior work has uncovered disciplinary differences in citation density65,136–138, citation functions102,139, citation integration65,136,137, sources of citations140,141, types and tenses of reporting clauses140, frequency of reporting verbs136, and preferences for particular types of reporting verbs65,140,142. Here, we examined disciplinary differences in citation sentiment, as they related to variations in perceived ingroup size. Prior work in social psychology demonstrates that people differ in the perception of ingroup size55, and that ingroup perception can be enlarged or changed by context and the understanding of a shared culture56. In science, exposure to sub-communities and subfields may increase the perception of one’s ingroup size, and we hypothesized that scholars with a larger perceived ingroup size would exhibit more ingroup behavior (such as more favorable language) to others in general.

To examine variations in scholarly exposure across disciplines, we considered their practical methods and practices143 and in particular investigated their markedly growing practice of publishing review articles144. We found that disciplines which published a larger ratio of review articles to empirical articles displayed less sentiment overall (either critical or favorable), instead citing science more neutrally. Reduced critical language in disciplines more regularly engaging in scholarly synthesis aligns with our expectations. However, reduced favorable language is not consistent with our hypothesis. It is possible that critical and favorable citation sentiment are not clean opposites, and that instead distinct social values and functions are assigned to the two sentiment types.

To further examine differences in perceived group size across disciplines, we considered variations across experimental and theoretical research, with the former being associated with larger labs, conferences, and funding levels than the latter. Consistent with this variation, we found that experimental fields (operationalized as those with more wetlab papers) showed longer reference lists (see Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting greater exposure to and thus perception of larger scholarly communities. We hypothesized that disciplines with a higher benchwork score would display behavior consistent with a larger perceived ingroup size, including less critical and more favorable sentiment. Notably, we found that the favorable sentiment effects were much weaker than critical sentiment effects, again suggesting that the two types of sentiment might have asymmetric underlying social mechanisms.

We also considered several alternative proxies for perceived ingroup size in a discipline: team size, interdisciplinary and international collaborations. Our analysis did not yield any evidence for these disciplinary level proxies to explain citation sentiment. Future dedicated research on disciplinary practices may enhance our understanding of how disciplinary settings affect sentiment in scientific writing.

Citation sentiment and country

Although scientists live and work within an explicitly scholarly social milieu—comprised of collaborative relationships and disciplinary identities—they also exist within broader cultural structures. For example, research priorities and funding levels differ significantly across countries145,146. Further, social structures of prestige and marginalization impact what science is done and who does it19,147, often undermining discovery and innovation148,149.

Here, we considered the ways in which differences in culture across countries could impact scientific writing, specifically in the domain of citation sentiment. Aligned with our consistent focus on ingroup/outgroup dynamics, we examined country-level factors that might impact the perceived ingroup size of a scholar. Drawing on Hofstede’s 6D-model, we considered individualism and power-distance. Notably, individualism—as opposed to collectivism—prioritizes individual interests over group interests69, consistent with a smaller perceived ingroup size. By contrast, power-distance, which concerns the acceptance of unequal power along a social hierarchy69, can broaden the size of the perceived ingroup to comprise both superiors and subordinates. Prior work has considered relations between such cultural factors and various aspects of ingroup perception150,151. We extended this prior work by testing the hypothesis that scholars in low individualism and high power-distance countries would employ more favorable and less critical sentiment consistent with a larger perceived ingroup size. Consistent with our hypothesis, we found that high power-distance and low individualism countries69,70 displayed less critical and more favorable sentiment. These results suggest that cultural differences impacting the perception of the ingroup can explain patterns of favorable and critical citations. Our work adds to existing literature showing that non-academic cultural factors can influence language and communication more broadly152,153.

Limitations

Several methodological considerations are pertinent to our study. Our sentiment method captured both evaluative judgments and expressed commonalities and differences in the presence of cited works, not exclusive to sentiment towards cited works. This broadens our hypotheses beyond direct evaluative sentiment. Since sentiment can be defined in multiple ways154, findings must be interpreted within our constrained definition. Our limited data per individual author prevents predicting single-citer behavior. Instead, our results characterize group-level patterns. While we relied on XML metadata in PMCOA papers to identify article types, the uncertainties in XML tags could introduce systematic noise. Another limitation is that our collaboration data relied on coauthorship from PMCOA papers, which is a conservative estimate that can miss collaborative relationships that do not result in coauthorship. Further, a variety of academic relationships existing outside of collaboration (e.g., shared advisor, shared alma mater, shared side of an argument, shared theoretical commitment, etc.) were not quantified in this study, but would be of interest to investigate in future work. In addition, the authors’ h-indices from Web of Science reflect values at the time of retrieval rather than at the time of citation, partially capturing eventual status rather than contemporaneous status. Beyond direct data constraints, limitations also arise from how we examined social groups. Culture studies face conceptual complexity, where multiple dimensions and scales of culture matter60,61. Although we built a multiscale framework to study selected factors, a different framework focusing on within-field variations and their links with larger social structures could produce valuable insights on the intersectionality and mechanisms of disciplinary expression. For example, disciplinary epistemological traditions and research methods155, funding, larger economic and social forces and various scientific capitals156 in a discipline, and social links between disciplines and politics, economy, and bureaucracy157 are all potential factors that could explain language and other disciplinary practices. These warrant consideration in research dedicated to in-depth investigations in scientific fields and their larger social relations. Our results are limited to neuroscience and may not generalize to other fields. It seems plausible that similar patterns could emerge in other fields, sharing characteristics such as relative youth allowing malleable subdisciplines, marked interdisciplinarity drawing on older fields, and a diversity of opinions, methods, and experimental approaches35,36. Topic specializations86 and other within-discipline factors (e.g., disciplinary age and diversification) may also expand the dimensions through which disciplinary sentiment expression can be studied.

Conclusions

Growing evidence across the fields of psychology, sociology, and science of science underscores the humanity that scholars bring to science. Individually, we contribute epistemological standpoints, cognitive biases, perceptions, and values; collectively, we bring disciplinary culture of preferred practices, methods, standards, and explanations. We uncovered ingroup biases and differences in scientific citation sentiment in neuroscience papers, across multiscale sociocultural groups of status, collaboration, gender, discipline and country. Uncovering the social construction of science serves both scholarly and ethical purposes, as obscuring that construction is an epistemic harm158. Looking to the future, important open questions remain, including how citation sentiment might have changed over time, how the relation between sentiment and culture might be impacted by serendipitous discoveries or paradigm shifts, and how other aspects of affect might manifest in science communication more broadly.
