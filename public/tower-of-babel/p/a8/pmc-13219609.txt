Plasma aldosterone concentration and valvular heart disease in hypertensive patients: a large cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhiqiang Zhang, Jing Hong, Junli Hu, Qing Zhu, Ting Wu, Di Shen, Pan Zhou, Yingying Zhang, Rui Ma, Shuaiwei Song, Nanfang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prior research has demonstrated that plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) is critically involved in cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, investigations specifically focusing on its association with valvular heart disease (VHD) remain limited. To address this gap in knowledge, this study aims to examine the relationship between PAC and VHD in hypertensive patients. The analysis included data from 35,622 hospitalized patients between January 2010 and February 2025. A multivariable logistic regression model was employed to evaluate the main relationship. To refine this relationship, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to investigate dose–response relationships and pinpoint possible inflection points. Additionally, subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the data’ overall robustness. Logistic regression analysis showed a strong association of VHD with PAC levels. This association was further characterized using RCS, which illustrated a nonlinear dose-response relationship. Notably, the RCS model identified specific saturation points with threshold effects at PAC levels of 14.5 ng/dL, 14.2 ng/dL, and 15.0 ng/dL. The robustness of these findings was confirmed through consistent results in extensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses. This study offers the first proof of a substantial correlation between high PAC levels and VHD in hypertension patients. Large-scale, prospective investigations are necessary to validate these results and elucidate the causal link.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46943-7.

Introduction

Globally, cardiovascular disease (CVD) continues to be one of the main causes of death and disability, putting a constant burden on healthcare systems1,2. Valvular heart disease (VHD), as a substantial contributor to this burden, has emerged as a significant global health concern3. Defined by impaired function of one or more cardiac valves, VHD shows a marked rise in prevalence with advancing age. In China, its weighted prevalence has been estimated at 3.8%, affecting about 25 million individuals and illustrating a growing challenge to healthcare resources4–10.

Concurrently, hypertension has become a highly prevalent chronic condition globally, driven by socioeconomic development and lifestyle changes11. As a strong CVD risk factor, hypertension is strongly associated with multi-organ damage, particularly affecting the heart, kidneys, and vascular systems12–15. Notably, VHD is a prevalent complication among hypertensive patients that significantly impairs quality of life and long-term prognosis16. This link is underscored by large-scale cohort studies demonstrating a significant correlation between hypertension and VHD, highlighting the critical importance of its early detection, prevention, and treatment to improve clinical outcomes16,17.

Historically, research on VHD has primarily focused on the morphology, histology, and congenital or genetic factors of heart valves5,18–22. Within this context, the potential role of plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) in VHD pathogenesis has received comparatively little attention. PAC is a key mineralocorticoid hormone responsible for regulating sodium-water balance and blood pressure, playing a crucial role in maintaining electrolyte and fluid homeostasis23,24. Beyond its physiological functions, it exhibits diverse pathophysiological effects. Aldosterone has a significant part in the onset and evolution of hypertension and the cardiovascular problems that accompany it, according to increasing evidence25,26. Critically, excessive PAC can induce detrimental processes such as enhanced inflammation, oxidative stress, and cardiac fibrosis23,24. Furthermore, its chronic excess has been demonstrated to substantially elevate the risk of various cardiovascular and metabolic diseases24,27–30. Despite these established connections to broader cardiovascular pathology, the particular connection between PAC and VHD in hypertensive patients remains unclear, and the underlying mechanisms await elucidation.

To address this knowledge gap, this investigation aims to clarify the connection between VHD and PAC in hypertensive patients. By conducting a comprehensive analysis of clinical characteristics and PAC levels, we seek to evaluate this relationship. Our objectives are to deepen the understanding of VHD pathophysiology in the context of hypertension and to explore potential novel therapeutic targets. Ultimately, these findings may contribute to strategies for improving cardiovascular outcomes and reducing adverse events in this patient population.

Materials and methods

Study population

These large-scale cross-sectional studies included hypertensive patients who visited the Xinjiang Hypertension Center from January 2010 to February 2025. Initially, a total of 43,326 participants met our inclusion criteria. To look into how PAC modifies hypertension individuals’ VHD, we first excluded participants who lacked echocardiography, those who lacked PAC examination. Secondly, we excluded patients with severe renal and hepatic dysfunction, endocrine disorders (such as hypothyroidism, and hyperthyroidism), infective endocarditis, rheumatic valvular disease or other valvular heart diseases, coronary heart disease, and heart failure. We also excluded patients with a history of congenital heart disease, myocardial infarction, Cushing’s syndrome, hyperadrenalism or adrenal insufficiency, and those on long-term diuretic therapy. Following these screenings, 35,622 participants were retained for the analysis (Fig. 1). This study was conducted in full compliance with the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies (https://www.strobe-statement.org/).

Data collection and definitions

Demographic, lifestyle, and medical history data were extracted from participants’ electronic medical records. Following an 8 to 12-hour overnight fast, venous blood samples were collected the following morning. A comprehensive panel of laboratory indicators was measured using an automated analyzer, in accordance with clinical guidelines and hospital protocols. This panel included: liver enzymes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), uric acid (UA), serum electrolytes (potassium, calcium, phosphate), lipid profile, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and thyroid hormones. All hormonal assays were performed strictly adhering to current guidelines and methodologies established in previous studies by our team31,32.

Additionally, cardiac structure and function were assessed via echocardiography. The documented medical history encompassed a range of conditions, including hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, infective endocarditis (IE), rheumatic valvular disease (RVHD), congenital heart disease (CHD), myocardial infarction (MI), coronary heart disease (CHD), and heart failure (HF). Detailed protocols for anthropometric measurements, blood pressure (BP), body mass index (BMI), and the specific diagnostic criteria for all listed medical conditions are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Outcome

A groundbreaking study shows that VHD is primarily characterized by either mild or severe stenosis, as well as moderate or severe regurgitation33. The primary endpoints in this study comprised mitral and tricuspid regurgitation as two subtypes, along with combined overall valvular insufficiency. Assessment of valve stenosis and regurgitation was conducted in accordance with the Society of Echocardiography guidelines using Doppler echocardiography34. The severity of mitral stenosis was categorized according to valve area: mild (> 1.5 cm²), moderate (1.0–1.5 cm²), and severe (< 1.0 cm²) 35. Patients with a mitral valve area ≤ 2.0 cm² were included in the analysis, while tricuspid stenosis was defined as a tricuspid valve area ≤ 1.0 cm² 36. Assessment of valvular regurgitation was based on both quantitative and semi-quantitative approaches. Additional echocardiographic details are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analysis

The study cohort was categorized into quartiles according to PAC. For missing data in some variables, we employed multiple imputation methods. The missing rates for each key variable are detailed in (Supplementary Figure S1). Assessment of multicollinearity via the variance inflation factor (VIF) revealed that all variables had VIFs under 5, suggesting absence of substantial multicollinearity (Supplementary Table S1). We employed multivariate logistic regression to quantify the relationship between PAC levels and VHD, expressed as odds ratios (ORs). A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was further utilized to characterize the dose-response association. This approach was supplemented by a two-stage comparison analysis targeting inflection points identified in the RCS curve. Subgroup evaluations were conducted to examine the influence of PAC levels across different strata related to VHD. E-value analyses were conducted to determine the strength of unmeasured confounding that would be needed to explain away the observed associations. Comprehensive details regarding statistical methodologies are available in the supplementary appendix.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3) and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistical.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Table 1 presents baseline parameters categorized by PAC quartiles. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of PAC concentrations. Among the 35,622 patients included in the study, 19,718 (55.4%) were male. Compared to the low PAC group, patients in the high PAC quartile were younger, had higher blood pressure, and lower rates of current smoking and drinking. Laboratory tests revealed that Cr and FPG levels were significantly higher in the high PAC group, while serum calcium and potassium levels were significantly lower. High PAC patients were more likely to be prescribed calcium channel blockers (CCB) or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEIs/ARBs) (Table 1). The most important finding was that the risks of overall valvular insufficiency, mitral and tricuspid regurgitation increased with higher PAC levels (Fig. 3). Additionally, when we further grouped participants according to whether they had valvular regurgitation or not, the result obtained was still that participants with valve insufficiency had a higher PAC. Relationship between PAC and overall valvular insufficiency and Its Subtypes

The relationship between PAC and the risk of overall valvular insufficiency and its subtypes, such as mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression (Table 2). In Model 1, each 1 ng/dL and 5 ng/dL increment in PAC was associated with a 1.03-fold increase (odds ratio [OR], 1.03; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02–1.03) and a 1.15-fold increase (OR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.12–1.17) in the likelihood of overall valvular insufficiency, respectively. These associations persisted in the fully adjusted Model 5, with corresponding risk elevations of 1.03-fold (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.02–1.04) and 1.16-fold (OR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.13–1.18) for every 1 ng/dL and 5 ng/dL rise in PAC, respectively.

In summary, higher PAC was significantly associated with an increased likelihood of overall valvular insufficiency, both per 1 ng/dL and per 5 ng/dL increment. When participants were stratified into lower (Q1–Q2) and higher (Q3–Q4) PAC categories, the risk of overall valvular insufficiency was 1.37-fold greater in Model 1 (odds ratio [OR], 1.37; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.25–1.50). These associations persisted after full adjustment in Model 5. Similar patterns were observed across quartiles; in Model 5, the ORs for Q2, Q3, and Q4 relative to Q1 were 1.16 (95% CI, 1.01–1.34), 1.49 (95% CI, 1.30–1.70), and 1.58 (95% CI, 1.38–1.81), respectively. Consistent dose–response trends were also evident for both mitral and tricuspid regurgitation.

Furthermore, we used RCS to explore the dose-response relationship between PAC and overall valvular insufficiency and their subtypes (Fig. 4). The analyses demonstrated that the risks of o overall valvular insufficiency, as well as the subtypes of mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, increased significantly once PAC surpassed 14.5 ng/dL, 14.2 ng/dL, and 15.0 ng/dL, respectively. Two-stage analysis indicated a 1.56-fold higher risk of overall valvular insufficiency in participants with PAC > 14.5 ng/dL compared with those ≤ 14.5 ng/dL (Table 3). For the subtypes (mitral and tricuspid regurgitation), individuals with PAC above the identified thresholds had a significantly higher risk compared with those at or below the corresponding cut-off values (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

The robustness of the conclusions was examined through detailed subgroup analyses and E-value assessments. Subgroup analyses were performed according to stratification factors such as sex, age, BMI, smoking status, and the presence of diabetes (Fig. 6). The link between elevated PAC and the risk of overall valvular insufficiency, encompassing mitral and tricuspid subtypes, was consistently evident and remained unaffected by the choice of stratification strategy. To assess the potential impact of antihypertensive therapy on valvular outcomes, subgroup analyses were stratified by the use of CCB, β-blockers, and renin–angiotensin system inhibitors (ACEIs/ARBs) (Supplementary Table S3). This analysis examined whether the association between PAC and valvular regurgitation remained consistent under different medication regimens. The findings indicated that none of the stratification variables materially altered the association strength. Across all predefined subgroups, PAC maintained a positive correlation with valvular lesions, aligning with the primary results and underscoring its potential involvement in valvular disease pathogenesis. Moreover, E-value estimation suggested that only strong, unmeasured confounders could fully account for the observed relationships, thereby affirming the robustness of the conclusions.

Discussion

The present research is the first to reveal a significant correlation between PAC and VHD in the hypertensive population. Furthermore, threshold analysis indicated that when PAC exceeded specific cutoffs (> 14.5 ng/dL, > 14.2 ng/dL, and > 15.0 ng/dL, respectively), the risk of developing total valvular insufficiency, mitral regurgitation, and tricuspid regurgitation increased by 1.56-fold, 1.50-fold, and 2.04-fold, respectively. These findings suggest that elevated PAC is an independent factor associated with the development of VHD in hypertensive patients, which could serve as an effective strategy for the early intervention of VHD in this population.

PAC, a mineralocorticoid tightly regulated by adrenocortical glomerular cells, is primarily synthesized in response to extracellular potassium concentration, adrenocorticotropic hormone, and the renin-angiotensin system (RAAS)25,37,38. Its primary physiological role is to promote potassium excretion and sodium retention39. Previous studies have shown that most hypertensive patients have elevated PAC40,41. In a meta-analysis, Xueyi Wu and colleagues reported that sustained PAC elevation contributes to arterial wall fibrosis and thickening, endothelial dysfunction, and hypertrophy or proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells, ultimately heightening the risk of cardiovascular disease42. Similarly, Mariona Guitart-Mampel and colleagues found that high aldosterone levels may promote oxidative stress and inflammatory responses by increasing enzymatic and inflammatory factor expression43,44. Furthermore, an increased probability of cardiovascular events is linked to excessive PAC levels in patients with primary hyperaldosteronism (PA)45,46. The fact that PAC antagonist therapy has been shown to significantly reduce cardiovascular mortality indirectly supports the idea that elevated PAC levels can induce cardiovascular disease, potentially leading to the development and progression of VHD47. In addition, relevant studies have found that the pathogenic effect of PAC on the valve can also exist independently of blood pressure. The mechanism involves the existence of an independent local RAAS in the heart valve tissue, which can directly act on adjacent valvular interstitial cells (VICs) through paracrine or autocrine signaling48,49. Furthermore, in vitro cell experiments confirmed that even in the absence of mechanical stretch, PAC can still induce an increase in collagen synthesis, which ultimately affects the normal function of the valve50. Moreover, a recent investigation by Rui Shen et al. employed two-sample Mendelian randomization to demonstrate a causal link between elevated PAC and detrimental alterations in cardiac structure and function among individuals with PA 51. While much prior research has relied heavily on animal models, this study benefits from data derived from extensive, large-scale clinical cross-sectional studies. It leverages a substantial sample size and stringent multivariate adjustments, providing the first robust evidence specifically linking PAC levels with VHD in hypertensive patients.

Currently, there is no unified theory on how plasma PAC mediates valvular injury in hypertensive patients. It is speculated that the mechanism may involve multifactorial interactions. First, hypertension activates the RAAS, leading to increased aldosterone secretion. Elevated aldosterone not only exacerbates hypertension through various pathways but also directly promotes myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis, creating a vicious cycle that is difficult to break. This cycle alters the structure and function of the heart, ultimately increasing the incidence of VHD 52–54. Second, prolonged high levels of PAC can cause left ventricular thickening and myocardial stiffening, resulting in impaired diastolic function. This impairment may subsequently affect systolic function, and changes in heart structure can affect the normal positioning and functioning of the heart valves28,55,56. Third, the role of PAC in VHD is cell-specific57. On the one hand, PAC directly acts on VICs by upregulating myocardial growth ton-1 (CT-1), inducing its conversion to myofibroblasts. This leads to excessive extracellular matrix remodeling and abnormal accumulation of protein polysaccharides, which is an important pathological basis for mucinous degeneration58. On the other hand, PAC also induces endothelial-interstial transformation (EndMT) by upregulation of CD14 on valve endothelial cells (VECs), so that endothelial cells can obtain interstial phenotype and directly participate in matrix remodeling57. At the same time, there is a parasecretory interaction between VICs and VECs, forming a positive feedback loop and promoting the destruction of the valve structure57,59. Fourth, prior research has indicated that hypertension frequently occurs alongside other cardiovascular risk determinants, including dyslipidemia and impaired glucose metabolism. These comorbidities interact with elevated PAC levels through diverse pathways, thereby intensifying cardiotoxic outcomes60,61. For example, high PAC levels can worsen insulin resistance, affecting blood glucose metabolism, while elevated blood lipids can promote the formation of atherosclerotic plaques. Together with hypertension, these factors increase the burden on the heart and elevate the risk of VHD62,63. This also explains the significant interactions observed in the analysis of the diabetes subgroup, indicating that diabetes status significantly modifies the strength of the association between PAC and VHD. In addition, we observed a significant interaction between elevated PAC levels and both smoking and BMI. This association may be explained by smoking-induced endothelial dysfunction, which compromises the vascular defense against calcium salt deposition and the adhesion of inflammatory cells64. Furthermore, in patients with obesity combined with VHD, the increased volume and pressure load exert a dual burden. Over the long term, chronic hemodynamic stress induces compensatory changes in the left ventricle, eventually resulting in cardiac remodeling65. Furthermore, visceral adiposity serves as an important source of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α 66. These inflammatory and metabolic disturbances directly activate valvular interstitial cells, leading to extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, and calcification.67. Overall, the interaction of these mechanisms and higher PAC levels may contribute to the initiation and advancement of VHD.

This large-scale cross-sectional investigation employed stringent exclusion criteria to enhance the validity of the results and represents the first attempt to clarify the link between PAC and valvular abnormalities among individuals with hypertension. We employed various statistical analysis methods to enhance the study’s accuracy and reliability. Nonetheless, consideration must be given to the inherent limitations of this investigation. In the beginning, this study’s cross-sectional methodology has intrinsic limitations that preclude drawing conclusions about a causal association between PAC and VHD in hypertensive individuals. Second, echocardiography was used as the primary diagnostic method for VHD; however, the accuracy of the diagnosis may be limited if supplementary imaging modalities such as transesophageal echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, and computed tomography are not used. Third, although PAC was measured in this study, corresponding data on plasma renin activity (PRA) were unavailable, preventing the calculation of the aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR). The absence of renin data makes it difficult to determine whether the elevated PAC reflects PA, secondary activation of the RAAS, or merely serves as an indicator of overall disease severity. Although patients with known endocrine disorders were excluded at baseline, the potential influence of subclinical PA cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, future prospective studies should include renin measurements to further elucidate the specific pathophysiological mechanisms involved. Fourth, as this study is retrospective and spans a long period, we cannot guarantee that standardized measurement conditions were maintained throughout the entire study period. Over time, differences in measurement protocols, equipment calibration, or operators may have introduced information bias, which should be taken into account when interpreting the results of this study. Fifth, our study population was limited to northern China, which may necessitate caution when generalizing the results to other populations. Finally, although the multivariate model was carefully adjusted for established confounders, residual bias may remain owing to unmeasured factors, including genetic variability and previous exposure to anticancer therapies.

Conclusion

To conclude, our findings indicate that increased PAC levels in patients with hypertension are closely linked to overall valvular insufficiency, including its subtypes (mitral and tricuspid regurgitation). This relationship is especially noteworthy in individuals with diabetes or higher body mass index. Furthermore, PAC levels below approximately 14.2–15 ng/dL were significantly associated with a reduced risk of VHD. While the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes a definitive causal inference, these findings provide valuable insights into the clinical management of this population. Consequently, these results warrant further validation through large-scale, multicenter prospective studies.
