Improving the definition of glioblastoma resection borders with intraoperative cone-beam computed tomography
Authors: Ryosuke Mori, Yasuharu Akasaki, Katharina Otani, Mohamed Ashraf, Kostadin Karagiozov, Yuichi Murayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: To assess the effectiveness of intraoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) in defining tumor resection margins during glioblastoma surgery.Methods: This study included glioblastoma patients who underwent surgery between 2017 and 2023. All patients underwent pre- and postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and intraoperative CBCT before and after craniotomy. Tumors and cavities were segmented by two neurosurgeons, and fusion images were generated. Interrater and intermethod agreements were evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), and volume shapes were compared using Dice and Hausdorff similarity metrics.Results: A total of 21 glioblastoma tumors and 20 cavities were analyzed. The median segmented volume was 32.8 cm3 (CBCT) versus 34.4 cm3 (MRI), with excellent agreement between modalities (ICC ≥0.90). The dice index was 0.79, and the Hausdorff 95% index was 14% relative to tumor diameter. Clinical outcomes were analyzed for 18 cases, where the total resection of contrast-enhancing tumor was considered feasible preoperatively. Intraoperative CBCT suggested no residual tumor in 13 (72%) cases, all of which were confirmed as total removal on postoperative MRI. CBCT detected suspected residual contrast-enhancing lesions in 3 (17%) cases (mean volume: 1.05 cm3, range: 0.42–1.59 cm3); additional resection achieved total removal in all cases. Conversely, postoperative MRI identified small residual tumors in 2 (11%) cases (0.73–1.57 cm3) that were not detected by intraoperative CBCT.Conclusion: CBCT provides intraoperative visualization of glioblastomas, postcraniotomy bleeding, and cavities, enhancing resection margin definition. It is a promising tool for improving glioblastoma resection in a hybrid operating room.

INTRODUCTION

Progress in the surgical management of patients with glioblastoma is strongly influenced by recent advances in imaging technologies. Surgeons have the difficult task of removing the tumor with sufficiently wide margins to minimize recurrence, when preserving the maximum amount of normal brain tissue; however, the infiltrative growth of glioblastomas makes dissection planes visually undetectable during surgery. Navigation systems based on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) allow the detection of margins to facilitate adequate planning when maximizing the precision of surgical resection of glioblastomas.[20] However, the mismatch between registered preoperative magnetic resonance (MR) images and “real-time” intraoperative position of brain structures often makes navigation inaccurate.[3,17] Intraoperative MRI has been shown to improve the extent of tumor resection by visualizing residual tumors and updating the navigation system;[2,5,6,10-12,15] however, MR-compatible operating suites have high installation costs and require specialized equipment, making treatment expensive.[4] As a possible alternative, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) image acquisition can be performed with digital subtraction angiography systems installed in the operating room; such hybrid operating rooms are currently becoming more widely available. Therefore, CBCT imaging represents a more cost-effective alternative to intraoperative MRI for tumor visualization.[16] CBCT imaging is useful for detecting residual tumors during pituitary surgery;[13] however, its utility in craniotomy remains unclear. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the potential of CBCT as an intraoperative imaging tool for the precise determination of tumor resection margins during glioblastoma surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study involved a retrospective review of all patients diagnosed with glioblastoma who underwent surgical resection at our institution between February 2017 and March 2023. All patients who underwent CBCT imaging during craniotomy, in addition to routine preoperative and postoperative MRI scans, were enrolled in the study. Patient data were retrieved from the patient records. This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of our institution (29–353 [8969]). Patient consent specific to participation in this study was waived by the IRB owing to the retrospective study design; however, the patients were informed of the choice to opt out.

Surgical procedure

Surgical planning was performed using neuronavigation (Vector Vision Sky; BrainLAB AG, Munich, Germany). Sapphire radiolucent disposable skull pins (Integra LifeSciences, Plainsboro, New Jersey, US) were used for head fixation to reduce metal artifacts on CBCT imaging. Before draping, the necessary space between the patient’s head in the clamp and the rotating C-arm of the robotic C-arm angiography system (ARTIS pheno; Siemens Healthcare AG, Forchheim, Germany) was ensured to guarantee nonconflicting movement during CBCT acquisition. The first intraoperative CBCT image was acquired prior to craniotomy. Tumors were microsurgically resected under neuronavigation with 5-aminolevulinic acid fluorescence guidance. When surgeons considered tumor resection complete, the dura mater was sutured with several stitches, the resection cavity was filled with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and the bone and skin flap were repositioned. All radiopaque surgical devices, instruments, and materials were removed from the surgical field, which was covered with a protective surgical drape. A second intraoperative CBCT image was subsequently acquired to confirm complete resection of the tumor, including its margins. When this was not confirmed and a residual tumor was suspected on the postresection CBCT, the obtained CBCT image data were transferred to the navigation system, and the surgery was resumed with the updated information to perform re-resection. After the completion of re-resection, closure of the surgical approach was performed if required. All head fixation devices were removed, and final CBCT images were acquired.

Preoperative and postoperative MRI

Preoperative MR was performed at the time of initial diagnosis, and surgery was generally initiated within 2 weeks. Postoperative MR was routinely performed several days after the surgery. T1-weighted 3D images and volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination images were acquired following contrast injection using a 1.5 Tesla MRI system (Siemens Healthcare AG, Erlangen, Germany).

Intraoperative CBCT imaging

All intraoperative examinations were performed using a 2nd generation robotic C-arm angiography system (ARTIS pheno; Siemens Healthcare AG, Forchheim, Germany) equipped with a 30 cm × 40 cm flat panel detector and an X-ray tube that rotated around the patient for image acquisition [Figure 1]. CBCT was performed without changing the patient’s position on the operating table, following a previously reported intraoperative imaging method for transsphenoidal surgery.[13] The acquisition parameters were as follows: acquisition time, 6 s; X-ray tube voltage, 109 kV; 512 × 512 matrix; total rotation angle, 200°; 496 projections; and dose, 1.20 microGy/frame. Contrast material (Omnipaque300, Daiichi Sankyo Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was administered on two occasions during surgery: once prior to craniotomy and once after tumor resection. On each occasion, 100 mL of contrast material was manually injected as a bolus over approximately 30 s, and CBCT acquisition was performed 3 min after injection. Thus, the total intraoperative iodinated contrast volume was 200 mL per patient. To ensure patient safety, renal function was evaluated preoperatively in all patients using serum creatinine levels and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Patients with renal impairment (eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2) or a known history of allergy to iodinated contrast agents were excluded from contrast-enhanced intraoperative CBCT. Imaging data were reconstructed on a system workstation (syngo X Workplace, Siemens Healthcare AG) that included a metal artifact reduction application (SMART, Siemens Healthcare AG). Information on radiation dose was retrieved from the dose reports automatically generated by the robotic C-arm angiography system.

The robotic C-arm angiography system capable of acquiring cone-beam computed tomography images in the hybrid operating room.

Segmentation and comparison of tumor and cavity shapes on MR and CBCT images

Each preoperative image set consisted of both MR and CBCT images, on which each surgeon independently segmented the contrast-enhancing tumor regions. Next, the surgeons fused the MR and CBCT images to generate fusion images. After surgery, all of the postoperative image sets comprised CBCT and MR images, in which each surgeon independently segmented the resection cavity and fused the CBCT and MR cavity images. Another separate set of fusion images was subsequently generated by fusing the independently evaluated CBCT images obtained by each surgeon before and after resection [Figure 2].

Flow diagram of images used for method validation in the present study: Segmentations of tumors and cavities on magnetic resonance (MR) and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images by each of the surgeons. Fusion images between MR and CBCT images to assess intermethod agreement, and fusion images between segmentations by each surgeon on magnetic resonance or on CBCT to assess interrater agreement.

Two board-certified neurosurgeons (RM and YA, with 14 and 26 years of experience, respectively) who perform the surgeries independently postprocessed all images on the robotic C-arm angiography system workstation (Syngo X-Workplace, Siemens Healthcare AG) using an automatic segmentation application (3D/4D imaging, Siemens Healthcare AG) while blinded to each other’s work. Because both raters participated in the surgical procedures, blinding to intraoperative CBCT findings during postoperative MRI segmentation was not feasible. On the workstation screen, the surgeons marked one point inside the tumor or cavity with a mouse click using the cursor and a second point outside of the tumor or cavity with a second mouse click. The software then automatically segmented the selected volume based on the parameters for the point set inside the tumor or cavity, and the surgeon could add minor manual corrections to the outlines of the segmented volume of the tumor or cavity. The values of all the segmented volumes of the tumors and cavities calculated using the application were recorded on a worksheet.

Method evaluation

Further, for validation of the method, anonymized images with segmented tumors and cavities for image fusion were transferred to a stand-alone computer and analyzed using an open-source software (3D Slicer, https://www.slicer.org/). The agreement between the segmented volumes was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for absolute agreement. A two-way mixed-effects model was applied for intramethod (CBCT and MR) agreement for each neurosurgeon, while a two-way random-effects model was applied to assess intrarater agreement (two neurosurgeons) for each modality. ICC values were interpreted in accordance with published guidelines:[9,18] <0.5, poor agreement; 0.5–0.75, moderate agreement; 0.75– 0.90, good agreement; and ≥0.90, excellent agreement.

Agreement between CBCT and MRI volumetric measurements was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis. For each lesion, the paired difference (CBCT volume minus MRI volume) was plotted against the paired mean volume. Systematic bias was estimated as the mean of the paired differences, and 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated as bias ± 1.96 × standard deviation of the differences. Sensitivity analyses were performed by stratifying comparisons according to the time interval between CBCT and MRI (tumors: ≤7 vs. >7 days; cavities: ≤2 vs. >2 days) and overall across the full interval range. We further assessed the similarity in the shape of the volumes, and the Dice and Hausdorff similarity metrics (maximum, 95%, and average) were used as geometrical indices to compare the segmented tumor, cavity, and both (cavity and tumor) volumes, that is, the similarity between volumes segmented on CBCT and MRI and the similarity between volumes segmented by the two neurosurgeons on CBCT and MRI, respectively. Both the Dice and Hausdorff similarity metrics were calculated using an open-source image processing software (3D Slicer, https://www.slicer.org/). The Dice metrics were used to quantify the overlap between two volumes on a continuous scale of 0–1, with 1 indicating a complete overlap. The Hausdorff metrics compute the distances between two areas for nonoverlapping parts, with 0 mm indicating complete overlap and providing three options. The average Hausdorff index reports the average of tangential distances, the maximum Hausdorff index reports the maximum among distances, and the 95% Hausdorff index reports the 95-percentile distance.[19] Because the Hausdorff index is sensitive to outliers, we selected the 95% Hausdorff index as the metric for this study. In addition, because the Dice and average Hausdorff indices were based on averages, we selected the Dice index for this study.

The results of the Bland–Altman analysis for the volumetric agreement between CBCT and MRI segmentation are shown in Figure 4 and Table 4 for neurosurgeon 1. For tumors (n = 21; imaging interval: 1–27 days), mean volumes were 39.44 cm3 on CBCT and 37.42 cm3 on MRI, with an overall bias of +2.02 cm3 (95% confidence interval [CI]: −3.48–7.51) and LoA of −21.64–25.68 cm3. When stratified by imaging interval, tumors scanned ≤7 days apart showed a bias of −2.46 cm3 (95% CI −7.23–2.32) with LoA of −16.38–11.47 cm3, whereas tumors scanned >7 days apart showed a bias of +6.94 cm3 (95% CI −3.58–17.47) with LoA of −21.89–35.78 cm3. This larger bias for longer timing suggests that tumor growth occurred between preoperative MRI and intraoperative CBCT imaging. For cavities (n = 20; imaging interval: 1–15 days), mean volumes were 39.59 cm3 on CBCT and 43.94 cm3 on MRI, with an overall bias of −4.35 cm3 (95% CI −9.72–1.02) and LoA of −26.83–18.13 cm3. Stratified by interval, cavities ≤2 days apart had a bias of −5.41 cm3 (95% CI −12.95–2.12) with LoA of −29.87–19.04 cm3, while cavities >2 days apart had a bias of −2.37 cm3 (95% CI −11.54–6.80) with LoA of −21.81–17.07 cm3. The results for the segmentations by neurosurgeon 2 are shown in Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S1. Across all tumor pairs, the mean differences were close to zero-to-small positive values (+2.02 cm3 for neurosurgeon 1; +2.89 cm3 for neurosurgeon 2), indicating little systematic over- or underestimation on average. However, the LoA span roughly ~35–50 cm3 (e.g., −21.64–25.68 cm3; −15.55–21.34 cm3), implying that for an individual lesion, CBCT and MRI volumes can differ substantially even when the mean bias is small. Timing appears to influence tumor agreement, especially when imaging is >7 days apart. In both readers, tumors with >7 days between scans show positive bias (CBCT larger than MRI): +6.94 cm3 (neurosurgeon 1) and +8.65 cm3 (neurosurgeon 2).

Importantly, for neurosurgeon 2, the 95% CI for bias excludes 0 (2.18–15.12), consistent with a statistically detectable systematic difference in that stratum. For cavities, bias is consistently negative (CBCT smaller than MRI), again with broad LoA. The overall cavity bias is −4.35 cm3 (neurosurgeon 1) and −2.41 cm3 (neurosurgeon 2), suggesting a tendency for lower volumes on CBCT compared with MRI. Yet, the 95% CIs for cavity bias include 0 in all strata shown, so the evidence for a consistent systematic offset is weaker than for tumors >7 days (neurosurgeon 2). The Bland–Altman plots [Figure 4 and Supplementary Figure S1] also suggested magnitude-dependent disagreement for tumor volumes, with larger tumors showing greater absolute differences between modalities. This pattern is consistent with heteroscedasticity and indicates that agreement expressed in absolute units (cm3) may vary across the measurement range. Accordingly, relative agreement (percentage difference or ratio-based LoA after log transformation) may provide a more clinically meaningful assessment of interchangeability across small and large tumor volumes.

Clinical results

Intraoperative CBCT was performed in all cases without technical difficulties, and no adverse effects or complications were observed, including postoperative infection or contrast-related adverse events such as allergic reactions or contrast-induced nephropathy, during or after surgery. The time required from preparation for CBCT scanning to the resumption of surgery, including the time needed for reconstruction and data transfer to the navigation system, was approximately 15 min on average. The mean dose area product (DAP) per CBCT acquisition was 65.2 Gy•cm2 (range: 40.7–88.5).

Certain cases of glioblastoma, especially those with diffuse progression or those located in eloquent areas, are inherently challenging to achieve total resection. Among the 28 cases, clinical outcomes were analyzed for 18 cases where gross total resection (GTR) of contrast-enhancing tumor was considered feasible preoperatively [Figure 3b, with results shown in Table 7. Of these 18 cases, no residual tumor was suspected based on intraoperative CBCT, and GTR was confirmed with postoperative MRI in 13 (72%, [Figure 6]) cases. In 3 (17%) cases, contrast-enhanced lesions were observed on CBCT (mean volume: 1.05 cm3, range: 0.42–1.59 cm3) and residual tumor was suspected. Consequently, re-resection was performed, and GTR was confirmed on postoperative MRI [Figure 7]. On the other hand, there were 2 (11%) cases in which residual tumor was suspected at the resection cavity margin on postoperative MRI (0.73–1.57 cm3), but these lesions could not be identified on intraoperative CBCT [Figure 8]. DISCUSSION

Overall, the results of the present study showed that intraoperative CBCT can contribute to the precision of glioma resection. Comparison of the images of preoperative CBCT and MRI showed that contrast-enhanced lesions were well visualized with both modalities, and we found high agreement in their volumes and shapes, with few exceptions. In cases without a match, the volumes tended to be larger on the CBCT images. This difference might have resulted from the period between MRI and surgery, during which the malignant nature of glioblastoma leads to rapid enlargement within a short period. However, these differences did not represent a significant clinical issue.

In the postoperative CBCT images, the removal cavity was well-visualized and matched with MRI. Re-accumulation of CSF or recovery from brain shifts may have contributed to any difference. Morphological changes due to the decrease in compression of the surrounding brain tissue caused by tumor resection and residual edema may also have influenced the reduction of the removal cavity in large tumor cases. These pre- and postoperative results support the potential of CBCT as an intraoperative imaging modality.

In fact, even minimal tumor remnants could be identified using CBCT, contributing to improved resection rates, particularly in well-visualized lesions where contrast enhancement in deep areas of the cavity was easier to detect. However, postoperative residual tumors may be overlooked in the presence of blood or air within the resection cavity, as observed in our series. In such situations, intraoperative MRI may offer an advantage. To mitigate these failure modes, operative strategies such as careful intraoperative head positioning, meticulous hemostasis, and secure dural closure can help reduce air or hematoma accumulation. In addition, the residual cavity was filled with artificial CSF prior to CBCT acquisition to displace residual air and provide a more uniform medium for imaging. In our practice, the dura is roughly closed with some approximating stitches, the bone flap is replaced, and the skin edges are loosely adapted before imaging to stabilize and isolate the cavity and minimize displacement, when avoiding significant prolongation of surgical time. These steps help standardize imaging conditions and improve CBCT visualization, but further refinements are clearly possible. Further technical improvements remain necessary, particularly to address image resolution and artifacts caused by cranial fixation pins. Recently developed carbon fiber-reinforced polymer skull pins may reduce such artifacts and improve visualization, representing a potential avenue for optimization.[14]

In the present study, enhanced lesions were well visualized on CBCT images; however, areas that showed no enhancement but had high intensity on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MRI were not clearly recognized. Although intraoperative CT has been reported to improve the extent of resection, even in low-grade gliomas,[7] the utility of CBCT for nonenhancing lesions still requires improvement.

Compared with other intraoperative adjuncts, CBCT offers several workflow-related advantages. Acquisition requires approximately 15 min, making it feasible without significant interruption of surgery, whereas intraoperative MRI may take around 1 h and can disrupt the surgical workflow.[8] CBCT also allows vascular imaging and is more accessible and cost-effective in many institutions. In contrast, intraoperative MRI provides superior detection of residual tumors, including nonenhancing FLAIR lesions, but is resource-demanding. Intraoperative ultrasound is real-time, inexpensive, and repeatable, yet its image quality is operator-dependent and can be limited by bone or air artifacts.[21] CBCT, therefore, occupies a niche where moderate resolution, rapid acquisition, and integration into the hybrid operating room workflow are prioritized.

To provide clinically interpretable information regarding radiation exposure, we evaluated the radiation dose of intraoperative CBCT using the DAP per scan. In our series, the mean DAP per acquisition was 65.2 Gy•cm2. Although direct comparison between CBCT and conventional CT is challenging because of differences in acquisition geometry and dose metrics, a phantom study comparing C-arm flat-detector CT (DynaCT) and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated that DynaCT can achieve diagnostically acceptable intracranial imaging at lower radiation doses than standard MSCT protocols.[1] These findings suggest that intraoperative CBCT provides a relatively dose-efficient imaging modality when maintaining adequate visualization of contrast-enhanced lesions and resection cavities. From a clinical perspective, the radiation dose of a single intraoperative CBCT acquisition is considered comparable to or lower than that of a routine diagnostic head CT, suggesting its feasibility as an intraoperative imaging adjunct.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of patients was small, and all were recruited from a single institution. Second, CBCT and MRI were not performed simultaneously, which may have introduced some errors owing to tumor growth, shape change, or both, potentially increasing the difference in the volume and shape of the segmented images. A comparison with the “golden standard” of an intraoperative MRI was not performed. Third, 7 (25%) of the 28 preoperative tumor images and 8 (29%) of the 28 postoperative cavity images were excluded, which may have biased the analysis results. Tumors with diffuse infiltrative growth patterns and postoperative cavities containing air or blood clots, conditions commonly encountered in routine glioblastoma surgery, were excluded due to unclear margins or insufficient image quality. Consequently, the findings of this study may be more applicable to relatively well-demarcated tumors and clean postoperative cavities, limiting the generalizability of our results to real-world glioblastoma cases.

Our results showed that intraoperative CBCT imaging is feasible, but further studies are required to determine its clinical efficacy, its impact on the extent of excision, and whether it translates into overall survival benefits.

CONCLUSION

Overall, the present study showed that intraoperative CBCT allows the visualization of the glioblastoma and eventual postoperative bleeding. Visualization using this technique can contribute to a better definition of the resection margins in these tumors.
