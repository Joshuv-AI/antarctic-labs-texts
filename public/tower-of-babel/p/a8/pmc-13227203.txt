Navigating the tension between missed diagnosis and overdiagnosis: A comparison of the recent Chinese Urological Association and European Association of Urology guidelines on prostate biopsy
Authors: Jin Ji, Xianqi Shen, Zenghui Zhou, Xu Gao
Published: 2026

Epidemiological differences and clinical realities

Prostate biopsy remains the gold standard for the definitive diagnosis of prostate cancer (PCa). This narrative comparative analysis (intended as a conceptual discussion rather than a systematic review) revealed substantial discrepancies (summarized in Table 1) in biopsy strategies between the European Association of Urology (EAU) 2025 [1] and the Chinese Urological Association (CUA) 2024 [2] guidelines, even though both frameworks provide comprehensive guidance tailored to the epidemiological characteristics and socioeconomic contexts of their respective populations. In Europe and North America, PCa is the most prevalent malignancy among men [3]. Owing to the widespread adoption of early prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening, a significant number of low-risk PCa cases have been identified. Consequently, the primary challenge for European public health systems is not missed diagnosis but overdiagnosis and overtreatment. This imposes substantial socioeconomic and psychological burdens on patients and has prompted the EAU to adopt a deliberately conservative stance in its guidelines to minimize the detection of clinically insignificant PCa. However, the clinical landscape in China presents a markedly different paradigm. Unlike Western countries with prevalent organized screening, China relies predominantly on opportunistic case-finding rather than nationwide screening [4]. Patients are often identified because of symptoms or incidentally detected PSA elevation, which leads to a higher pre-test probability of clinically significant disease. Consequently, China has witnessed a rapid increase in the incidence of PCa, a trend characterized by a high proportion of locally advanced and metastatic cases at initial diagnosis and elevated mortality rates [5]. Accordingly, to align with the domestic clinical environment, the CUA prioritizes the optimization of screening efficacy for high-risk and lethal PCa in its guidelines. Criteria for initial biopsy and the clinical application of biomarkers

In response to divergent epidemiological landscapes, the CUA and EAU exhibit significant discrepancies in their interpretations of PSA thresholds and the strategic weighting of derivative indices such as PSA density (PSAD) and free-to-total PSA ratio (f/t PSA).

High-risk population (PSA concentration >10 ng/mL)

Although both guidelines categorize patients with a PSA concentration >10 ng/mL as a high-risk cohort for PCa and consistently identify them as priority candidates for pathological confirmation, their clinical mandates differ. While the CUA guidelines define this threshold as an “absolute indication” for biopsy [6], the EAU 2025 guidelines adopt a more nuanced approach. For patients exceeding this threshold, the EAU still encourages the integration of multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) and emphasizes informed consent through shared decision-making prior to intervention. These subtle distinctions in recommendation strength and procedural approach reflect the disparate clinical profiles of biopsy candidates encountered in their respective healthcare environments.

The diagnostic “gray zone” (PSA concentration of 4–10 ng/mL)

Both frameworks acknowledge the suboptimal specificity of total PSA (tPSA) within this range and the necessity for auxiliary tools for secondary risk stratification. Unlike the EAU's more restrictive thresholds, which are supported by long-term randomized trials (e.g., European Randomised Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer [ERSPC]), the CUA's retention of broader biopsy indications is driven by pragmatic consensus aimed at addressing the higher pre-test probability of cancer in a non-screened population, rather than Level 1 evidence. The CUA guidelines note that the biopsy positivity rate for Chinese patients in the gray zone is approximately 25%, which is significantly lower than the 40% observed in Western cohorts [7]. This discrepancy stems not only from genetic and environmental variations but also from fundamental differences in disease composition. Compared with Western countries, China has a lower incidence of PCa and a similar prevalence of benign prostatic hyperplasia. Consequently, PSA elevations in the Chinese gray zone are more likely to be driven by benign conditions, leading to markedly reduced specificity for malignancy [8]. In this context, precise screening becomes paramount. Given the current lack of large-scale, domestically validated risk calculators in China, the direct application of Western-derived models may lead to a miscalculation of risk. While the CUA advocates for the development of nomograms based on Chinese big data, its guidelines recommend more accessible secondary indices to enhance the positive predictive value. Specifically, a biopsy is actively recommended when the f/t PSA is <0.16 or the PSAD is >0.15 ng/mL2. This “point-data” approach facilitates rapid stratification, particularly in primary care settings. In scenarios where pre-biopsy mpMRI is not performed for men with PSA levels below 10 ng/mL, the f/t PSA remains a vital “gatekeeper”. This accessible biomarker effectively stratifies risk and reduces the incidence of unnecessary biopsies for clinically insignificant PCa [9], serving as a crucial safety net in the absence of advanced imaging.

Conversely, the EAU guidelines suggest that although individual markers such as f/t PSA are useful, their weighting should be integrated into comprehensive assessment frameworks. Specifically, the EAU advocates abandoning single threshold-based judgments in favor of multivariable risk calculators (e.g., ERSPC or Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial) [10], which function as sophisticated secondary tools by quantifying factors such as age, family history, and digital rectal examination findings. In the final decision-making phase, the EAU demonstrates a more aggressive “reductive” strategy. The EAU emphasizes a combined analysis of mpMRI results and PSAD and explicitly states that biopsy may be safely omitted in MRI-negative cases (Prostate Imaging-Reporting and Data System [PI-RADS] ≤2) with a low-risk PSAD (<0.15 ng/mL2). This quantifiable standard epitomizes the EAU’s core philosophy of minimizing overdiagnosis within the paradigm of precision medicine.

Beyond these established metrics, emerging measurable biomarkers are showing significant promise. Recent studies have highlighted that novel biomarkers can further optimize screening strategies and disease management, potentially offering superior specificity compared with PSA alone [11].

The EAU assigns a high recommendation grade to blood-based assays, such as the Prostate Health Index and the 4Kscore [12], which indicates that their synergy with MRI significantly reduces unnecessary biopsies. The CUA also recognizes the utility of the Prostate Health Index and 4Kscore in enhancing the detection of clinically significant PCa (csPCa) but maintains a conservative stance because of limited domestic accessibility. With respect to non-invasive urinary diagnostics, the EAU details the evidence supporting exosome and gene methylation assays such as SelectMDx [13] and ExoDx [14]. The CUA remains cautious and emphasizes the need for validation against the specific genomic landscape of the Chinese population. Notably, recent research in China has begun to bridge this gap. For instance, a single-center study has developed a urinary extracellular vesicle-based prostate score model that innovatively incorporates population-specific markers to account for the genomic landscape of Chinese patients (e.g., lower prevalence of ERG gene fusions) [15]. Preliminary data from this work suggest that the model's efficacy in reducing unnecessary biopsies is comparable to or superior to that of imported kits [15]. However, it is important to note that these emerging tools are currently considered hypothesis-generating. The CUA guidelines view them as supplementary research options requiring robust multicenter validation before they can be recommended as clinically validated alternatives to established metrics.

The role of multiparametric MRI in biopsy decision-making

The integration of mpMRI has fundamentally reshaped the decision-making pathways for prostate biopsy. However, regarding the specific clinical weighting and timing of its application, the EAU and CUA guidelines reflect two distinct philosophies as follows: “proactive-avant-garde” versus “conservative-pragmatic”. The EAU guidelines have established an “MRI-first, if not the only” diagnostic pathway [16] and strongly recommend that mpMRI should precede biopsy in all patients with suspected localized PCa. An additional groundbreaking recommendation is that biopsy may be omitted in patients with negative MRI findings (PI-RADS ≤2) and low clinical risk (e.g., a PSAD <0.15 ng/mL2). This is supported by high-level evidence (Level 1a), such as the PRECISION trial [17]. The EAU's “MRI-first” pathway is underpinned by this Level 1 evidence. In contrast, the CUA's recommendation to perform systematic biopsy (SB) even in MRI-negative cases reflects a consensus-based strategy, acknowledging the heterogeneity of MRI quality across clinical centers in China. Notably, the EAU designates its MRI-first approach as a “weak recommendation”, emphasizing that the decision to omit biopsy relies on shared decision-making to weigh the marginal risk of missing csPCa against the benefits of avoiding invasive procedures. Clinicians must inform patients that although omitting a biopsy is generally safe, a marginal risk of missing csPCa remains, and the final decision should rest with the fully informed patient. Furthermore, the implementation of this strategy requires a rigorous threshold: it is contingent upon high-quality MRI—comprising both high-quality scanning protocols and expert interpretation—otherwise, omitting the biopsy could compromise patient safety. For patients with positive MRI results (PI-RADS ≥3), a combined approach of a targeted biopsy (TB) and SB is recommended.

While the CUA guidelines also highly value the utility of MRI and suggest a routine MRI prior to biopsy for patients with a PSA concentration >10 ng/mL, they adopt a more conservative “MRI-first, but not exclusive” strategy for those in the “gray zone”. Even with a negative MRI, SB is still recommended if the PSAD is elevated or if other clinical indicators are abnormal. This reflects a high degree of vigilance against missing high-risk cases, particularly as the biological characteristics of MRI-negative PCa in the Chinese population have not yet been fully elucidated. Additionally, given the economic value of biparametric MRI, which is advocated by the CUA for primary care promotion, we believe that further scrutiny is warranted [18]. While biparametric MRI offers a cost-effective alternative, its widespread implementation in China's primary care settings currently faces challenges related to standardization and quality control. Given the heterogeneity in scanner quality and interpretation expertise, relying solely on biparametric MRI may be premature without further real-world validation data specific to the Chinese healthcare infrastructure.

Nevertheless, the gap between the two guidelines is expected to narrow over time. As high-quality mpMRI becomes more accessible and interpretation standards are harmonized across Chinese medical centers, we anticipate that the CUA will progressively align with the EAU's MRI-first paradigm. In the near future, mpMRI is likely to be routinely integrated into clinical practice even for patients with PSA levels below 10 ng/mL, serving as a critical filter to further reduce unnecessary biopsies while maintaining diagnostic sensitivity.

Implementation standards for combined TB and SB

In transitioning to the procedural phase of biopsy, both the EAU and CUA guidelines cite major international studies, such as MRI-FIRST [16], 4M [19], and PRECISION [17], and acknowledge that isolated TB may overlook a subset (9%–18%) of csPCa that is detectable only through systematic sampling [16,19]. Consequently, the combined strategy of “TB+SB” remains the consensus standard of care recommended by both frameworks. However, a divergence emerges regarding the tolerance for missed diagnosis and future procedural trends. The EAU guidelines involve cautious “reductive” exploration, which initiates discussions on whether whole-gland SB could be simplified or even omitted in specific high-risk scenarios (e.g., prominent PI-RADS 5 lesions). Alternatively, they propose “perilesional sampling” as a compromise that can be used to mitigate procedural trauma and overdiagnosis within the paradigm of precision medicine. In contrast, the CUA guidelines reflect a more conservative “safety-net” philosophy. These guidelines explicitly state that given the current disparities in medical homogeneity and MRI diagnostic efficacy across China, retaining SB is an indispensable safeguard against missing high-risk cases. Accordingly, for biopsy-naive patients, the CUA strongly mandates a rigorous combined protocol (typically 12-core SB plus 3-core TB) and advises against the premature adoption of “targeted-only” approaches at this stage to ensure that the diagnostic baseline is maintained.

Evolution of biopsy approaches and perioperative infection control

With respect to the selection of a biopsy approach and in light of the potential risk of urosepsis (approximately 1%–3%) associated with transrectal (TR) biopsy and the escalating global crisis of antimicrobial resistance [20], the latest EAU guidelines strongly recommend the transperineal (TP) approach with a “zero-infection” objective. While the CUA guidelines equally acknowledge the definitive advantages of the TP approach in mitigating infectious complications and optimizing sampling of the prostatic apex, they do not advocate for complete abandonment of the TR route. Specifically, the TR approach retains indispensable value in terms of health economics and clinical versatility. First, regarding technical dissemination, the TR approach features a flatter learning curve and lower dependence on specialized hardware and remains the main technique for screening in primary health care settings. Second, regarding clinical throughput, the TR approach allows for rapid turnover, making it better suited for the high-volume outpatient demands encountered in major Chinese medical centers. Consequently, the CUA does not reflexively dismiss TR biopsy; instead, it advocates for flexible selection based on institutional resources. By mandating detailed prophylactic antibiotic protocols (such as escalated regimens for populations at high risk of resistance) to minimize TR-related infection risks, the CUA demonstrates a pragmatic equilibrium between pursuing technical advancement and ensuring broad medical accessibility.

Identification of high-risk pathological subtypes and reporting standards

The pathological diagnosis of biopsy specimens serves as the gold standard for guiding subsequent therapeutic interventions. While the EAU and CUA guidelines are highly convergent regarding the standardization of pathological reports, they distinctly emphasize certain high-risk pathological features. For example, the EAU guidelines specifically mandate that for biopsy specimens with a Gleason score of 7 (International Society of Urological Pathology [ISUP] Grade Group 2 or 3), the percentage of Gleason pattern 4 must be explicitly reported. This detail is pivotal for identifying “favorable intermediate-risk” patients, who are often considered potential candidates for active surveillance (AS). Conversely, patients with a high percentage of Gleason pattern 4 should be classified into the “unfavorable intermediate-risk” group, which requires immediate active treatment. Similarly, the CUA guidelines require rigorous quantification of the percentage of tumor involvement to evaluate tumor burden. In terms of adverse prognostic factors, both guidelines mandate the separate documentation of intraductal carcinoma of the prostate [21] and invasive cribriform patterns [22]. According to the ISUP consensus, all cribriform structures are definitively classified as Gleason pattern 4 and are associated with more aggressive biological behavior; the detection of this feature is generally regarded as a contraindication for AS. Furthermore, the CUA guidelines stipulate that the discovery of isolated intraductal carcinoma of the prostate on initial biopsy often suggests the presence of unsampled high-grade invasive carcinoma in the vicinity; thus, a repeat biopsy is strongly recommended to prevent underdiagnosis.

It is crucial to acknowledge that a more sensitive diagnostic strategy, as favored by the CUA, inevitably increases the detection of low-risk disease. To mitigate the downstream risk of overtreatment, the rigorous implementation of robust AS protocols becomes paramount. While the EAU utilizes upfront risk stratification (e.g., MRI) to avoid diagnosing insignificant cancer, the CUA strategy relies on post-diagnostic selection—using AS as the primary management for low-risk patients—to ensure that “safety-net” diagnosis does not lead to unnecessary intervention.

Conclusion: divergent strategic philosophies in prostate biopsy

The EAU guidelines epitomize a quintessential Western perspective that is rooted in public health economics. Their primary objective is to maximize patient quality of life while meticulously controlling societal healthcare costs. By constructing a rigorous “filter” comprising multivariable risk calculators, mandatory pre-biopsy MRI, and the omission of biopsy in MRI-negative cases, the EAU seeks to selectively identify only csPCa. Furthermore, the transition toward TP biopsy to eliminate infection risks underscores a profound restraint regarding overdiagnosis and procedure-related complications.

In contrast, the CUA guidelines prioritize the detection efficiency of high-risk cases, which reflects an Eastern “safety-net” approach. Confronted with the stark reality of late-stage presentation and heavy tumor burdens among newly diagnosed patients in China, the CUA remains cautious about restricting diagnostic access. By maintaining SB as a complement to MRI-targeted sampling and preserving the clinical status of the TR approach, owing to the uneven distribution of medical resources, the CUA employs a more sensitive strategy. As we look beyond current standard practices, the diagnostic landscape is rapidly evolving. Emerging imaging modalities, such as gallium-68 prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/CT, are showing promise in refining risk stratification, even for men under AS [23]. Furthermore, the integration of genetic alterations, particularly BRCA mutations, is becoming increasingly critical for the early detection of csPCa [24]. Collectively, future iterations of both guidelines will likely incorporate advanced molecular and genetic profiling to further optimize the balance between missed diagnosis and overdiagnosis. Ultimately, this proactive stance is designed to provide an advantage in the race against high-risk PCa and to ensure that no curable patients are overlooked because of insufficient diagnostic intensity.
