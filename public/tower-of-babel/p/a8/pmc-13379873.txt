Syncope and Scar in a Former Elite Basketball Player
Authors: Deen L. Garba, Konstantinos N. Aronis, Lili A. Barouch, Jasmin Sahbaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentricular tachycardia (VT) is a recognized late sequela of prior myocarditis. Accurate diagnosis and risk stratification are essential in athletes presenting with unexplained arrhythmia and scar-related cardiomyopathy.Case SummaryA 47-year-old former competitive basketball player presented with exertional syncope. Evaluation revealed mildy elevated troponin levels, apical wall motion abnormalities, and a fixed perfusion defect. Coronary angiography excluded obstructive disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed an inferolateral scar consistent with prior myocarditis. He received an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. VT was inducible on electrophysiology study, so epicardial VT ablation was attempted but was incomplete given phrenic nerve proximity. Fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on PET/CT initially raised concern for sarcoidosis, but a follow-up PET/CT demonstrated no active inflammation, and initial findings were attributed to postablation inflammation.DiscussionThis case highlights the utility of multimodality imaging in evaluating scar-related VT, the challenges of epicardial VT ablation, and shared decision-making in return to play for athletes with postmyocarditis scar-related arrhythmias.Take-Home MessagesMultimodality imaging is essential when evaluating scar-related VT in athletes. Epicardial ablation near the phrenic nerve may limit complete substrate modification and affects long-term management decisions.

Management

During the index admission, the patient underwent an electrophysiology study given unexplained exertional syncope, documented nonsustained VT, and evidence of nonischemic myocardial scar on imaging. Programmed ventricular stimulation induced sustained monomorphic VT, consistent with scar-mediated VT. In light of this finding, an ICD was placed for secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death. He was started on beta-blocker therapy, and after shared decision-making he was scheduled for catheter ablation as an outpatient.

Approximately 2 weeks later, while awaiting ablation, he experienced sustained VT resulting in an appropriate ICD shock. This episode occurred after missing 3 consecutive days of metoprolol, highlighting the importance of strict medication adherence in suppressing adrenergically mediated ventricular arrhythmias. He was subsequently transitioned to nadolol because of its nonselective beta-adrenergic blockade, longer half-life, and more consistent 24-hour coverage, which may be advantageous in patients with exercise-triggered arrhythmias.

He then underwent catheter ablation. Substrate mapping demonstrated extensive low-voltage areas in the inferolateral left ventricular wall corresponding to the region of LGE seen on CMR, consistent with prior myocarditis-related scar. Activation and pace mapping localized the critical isthmus of the VT circuit to an epicardial substrate. The phrenic nerve was identified in this area. Epicardial radiofrequency ablation was therefore performed. Percutaneous phrenic nerve displacement techniques were performed (phrenic displacement using intrapericardial vascular balloon), but it was not possible to completely displace the phrenic nerve. Thus, his ablation remained only partially successful given the proximity of the phrenic nerve to the ablation target. This scenario illustrates a recognized limitation of epicardial VT ablation in nonischemic cardiomyopathy with lateral or posterolateral scar, where arrhythmogenic tissue often lies near the phrenic nerve, restricting the ability to deliver effective lesions.

Two months after ablation, FDG-PET demonstrated focal metabolic uptake in the lateral wall and apex. Given the distribution and clinical context, this finding raised concern for inflammatory cardiomyopathy such as cardiac sarcoidosis. However, the patient had no extracardiac manifestations of sarcoidosis, and the uptake was localized to the ablation region.

Eight months later, repeat PET/CT demonstrated complete resolution of FDG uptake with persistent scar in the mid to apical lateral wall and associated hypokinesis. Left ventricular EF had improved to 64%. Transthoracic echocardiography at that time showed preserved systolic function (EF: 55%-60%), mild left ventricular hypertrophy, no regional wall motion abnormalities, and an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 27 mm Hg. The normalization of metabolic activity supported postablation inflammatory changes rather than active sarcoidosis or ongoing myocarditis, thereby avoiding unnecessary immunosuppressive therapy.

Follow-Up

The patient was counseled extensively regarding ICD implications, arrhythmic risk, and recommendations regarding physical activity. Shared decision-making involved a detailed discussion of the risks and benefits of continued athletic participation and potential activity modifications. He has remained clinically well on nadolol, exercising regularly and living without functional limitations. He has not experienced recurrent VT or ICD shocks after ablation and remains off membrane-active antiarrhythmic therapy.

Discussion

Evaluation of syncope in the athlete

Syncope during exertion in an athlete warrants evaluation for arrhythmic, structural, or ischemic disease.1 The differential includes benign conditions such as vasovagal syncope or dehydration, as well as high-risk etiologies including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, coronary anomalies, and primary electrical disorders. Accordingly, evaluation typically includes electrocardiogram, echocardiography, ambulatory monitoring, and often advanced imaging. Invasive coronary angiography and electrophysiology studies may be warranted when suspicion for scar-mediated arrhythmia persists or when noninvasive testing is inconclusive.2

In this case, the patient's mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin levels at presentation contributed to clinical concern for myocardial injury. Troponin testing is not routinely obtained in all syncope evaluations but can provide important diagnostic information in athletes presenting with exertional symptoms. Elevation may reflect acute or subacute myocardial inflammation, ischemia, or demand injury and therefore broadens the differential diagnosis beyond primary electrical disorders.3 Although CMR did not demonstrate myocardial edema on T2 sequences sufficient to meet Lake Louise criteria for definite active myocarditis, the presence of subepicardial LGE in a nonischemic distribution suggested prior inflammatory injury.4 The absence of edema favors healed or resolving myocarditis rather than active disease. FDG-PET is a useful adjunct for detecting myocardial inflammation when CMR findings are equivocal or when distinguishing active from chronic scar is clinically important.5 However, the results should always be interpreted in the right clinical context. In our patient, FDG avidity was most likely due to ablation, which caused local inflammation, rather than myocarditis-caused inflammation.

The detection of myocardial scar is clinically significant, as LGE on CMR confers a significant risk for ventricular arrhythmias and adverse outcomes in nonischemic cardiomyopathy.6 In this case, the combination of nonischemic scar distribution and inducible VT confirmed an arrhythmogenic substrate.

An electrophysiology study was pursued given unexplained exertional syncope, documented nonsustained VT, and structural abnormalities on imaging. Contemporary expert consensus on arrhythmias in athletes supports electrophysiology studies for risk stratification when ventricular arrhythmias occur in the setting of myocardial scar or when malignant arrhythmia is suspected despite nondiagnostic surface testing.2 Inducible sustained VT in this context establishes a substrate capable of causing syncope or sudden cardiac arrest and supports consideration of secondary prevention therapy.

ICD placement was therefore indicated for secondary prevention after inducible sustained VT. ICD therapy has been shown in randomized trials to reduce mortality in patients with prior sustained ventricular arrhythmias or cardiac arrest, whereas catheter ablation primarily reduces arrhythmia burden and ICD shocks without demonstrating mortality benefit.7,8 Accordingly, ablation does not replace ICD therapy in patients with structural heart disease.

VT ablation was performed to reduce VT burden, although epicardial substrates are often challenging given anatomic constraints. In our case, the arrhythmogenic substrate was located near the phrenic nerve, limiting the ability to deliver complete radiofrequency lesions without risk of diaphragmatic paralysis.9 Despite the incomplete ablation, our patient has remained free of VT for 2.5 years and is now off antiarrhythmic medications.

Return-to-play decisions in athletes with scar-related VT remain complex. Historically, the presence of sustained VT or an ICD was considered a contraindication to competitive sports participation. Contemporary data and guidelines, however, emphasize individualized assessment and shared decision-making rather than blanket restriction.10,11 Large registries of athletes with ICDs have demonstrated that sports participation may be undertaken without excess mortality, though appropriate shocks can occur.10 In this case, participation at a competitive level was considered high risk given incomplete substrate modification and prior sustained arrhythmia, whereas recreational activity was permitted in the setting of clinical stability, absence of recurrent VT, and normalization of inflammatory imaging.

Shared decision-making was therefore central to management. Rather than imposing absolute restrictions, discussions focused on balancing residual arrhythmic risk, the protective role of the ICD, the patient's goals, and quality of life. The patient, a former competitive athlete now engaged primarily in recreational activity, elected to continue moderate exercise with ongoing medical therapy and surveillance. He has remained clinically stable without recurrent VT or ICD therapies since optimization of treatment.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the value of multimodality imaging, invasive coronary and electrophysiologic evaluation, and genetic exclusion to collectively inform the diagnosis of scar-mediated ventricular tachycardia in athletes. It also emphasizes the importance of ICD therapy, ablation strategies, and strict medication adherence. With careful management, patients with postmyocarditis arrhythmic scar can achieve long-term stability and safely maintain active lifestyles.
