Quantification of macrophage activity in knee synovial tissue using [18F]FEPPA positron emission tomography
Authors: Zachary J. Koudys, Brent A. Lanting, Garth Blackler, Joseph Daniel Klapak, Matthew Fox, C. Thomas Appleton, Jonathan D. Thiessen, Matthew G. Teeter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveInflammation in knee osteoarthritis (OA) is mediated primarily by synovial tissue macrophages, but non-invasive measurement of macrophage activity in vivo is a challenge. Activated macrophages markedly increase expression of the translocator protein (TSPO). In the context of other diseases TSPO-PET using the [18F]FEPPA tracer (binding to TSPO) has been reported to be an effective method for imaging macrophages in vivo. The goal of this study was to validate the use of [18F]FEPPA PET radiotracer to accurately measure macrophage activation in knee synovial tissue.DesignTen participants with late-stage OA scheduled for knee replacement surgery were recruited. Prior to surgery, 5 of the participants underwent a clinical [18F]FEPPA PET/MRI scan and the standard uptake value (SUV) in the suprapatellar synovial region was calculated. Suprapatellar synovial tissue samples were collected from all participants at the time of surgery and were imaged ex vivo with [18F]FEPPA autoradiography. Tracer uptake was compared to TSPO antibody immunostaining in serial tissue sections. The correlation between the [18F]FEPPA uptake and gold standard TSPO fluorescent intensity was measured.RESULTSThe autoradiography [18F]FEPPA signal in the synovial lining was correlated with the TSPO signal in the. lining macrophages measured by immunohistochemistry (r = 0.81, p = 0.0040, CI [0.37, 0.95]). Similarly, PET/MRI scans demonstrated similar correlation between SUV in synovial tissues in vivo and in situ TSPO signal in lining macrophages {r = 0.90, p = 0.083, CI [0.086, 0.99])Conclusion[18F]FEPPA uptake in knee synovial tissue is strongly correlated to the expression of TSPO in synovial lining macrophages, suggesting that clinical [18F]FEPPA PET/MRI may be a valid measure of true macrophage activity in synovial tissues in knee OA. [18F]FEPPA may be an effective tool to assess macrophage activation both ex vivo and in vivo, and should be investigated further to assess its performance and measurement properties in different clinical contexts of knee OA including disease states and responses to treatment.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a complex disease that is characterized by pathological changes across all knee-joint tissue types including, cartilage, bone, ligaments, menisci, and the synovial membrane [1]. Inflammation of the synovial lining of the knee (synovitis) plays major roles in mediating pain and disease progression in patients with symptomatic OA [[2], [3], [4]]. Macrophages are the dominant immune cell in the knee synovium and regulate the inflammatory process through secretion of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines [5]. Activated macrophages secrete cytokines including IL-1β and TNF-α, among many others, and stimulate nociception and catabolic processes that lead to structural joint damage. Therefore, measuring synovial macrophage activity is an important goal for OA research and in the future, for assessment of contributors to clinical OA symptoms [6]. Synovitis is present in the vast majority of patients with symptomatic OA when measured using morphological tools such as ultrasound or MRI, but assessing the biological activity of joint inflammation remains very challenging. The development of tools to measure the activity of synovitis (in addition to the morphology) in patients with OA is needed to improve our understanding of mechanisms of knee synovial macrophage dysregulation in the progression of OA [7,8].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been used to characterize synovitis as well as cartilage degradation and bone remodelling [9]. Although non-contrast MRI is a gold standard for assessing morphological changes in joint soft tissue structures, it cannot assess the biological activity of inflammation. Excitingly, positron emission tomography (PET) has been used to image metabolic and immune changes in joint disease [[10], [11], [12]]. Several commercially available PET tracers have been used to study metabolic processes in OA. Sodium fluoride ( [18F]NaF) has been shown to probe bone remodelling [13]. Fluorodeoxyglucose ( [18F]FDG) is a widely used marker for glucose metabolism and is useful for imaging acute cellular changes in OA, including active inflammatory cells [14]. Imaging synovial proliferation as an indicator for synovitis has been proposed with choline ( [11C]Choline) and high uptake was seen in sites of arthritic change [15].

The translocator protein (TSPO) is an 18 kDa protein on the surface of mitochondria. The function of this protein is unclear but a role in steroidogenesis, apoptosis, and cholesterol transport has been proposed [16]. Since macrophages are the most metabolically active cell in joint tissues, TSPO is over-expressed on the surface of activated macrophages and has been increasingly popular as a target for imaging active inflammation [17]. TSPO-targeting PET tracers have been used to examine the etiology of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [18]. [18F]FEPPA is a 2nd generation TSPO radioligand with a high affinity to TSPO capable of imaging both activated microglia and macrophages [19]. Current applications of [18F]FEPPA in PET imaging have been limited mainly to imaging activated brain glial cells associated with inflammation [20].

Non-invasive imaging with hybrid [18F]FEPPA PET/MRI could be used to measure the biological activity of macrophages in synovial tissues in OA, which would improve our understanding of inflammatory and structural changes at early and late stages of disease [21,22]. However, the application of this tool in patients with knee OA requires validation. Therefore, this study aims to validate the use of [18F]FEPPA in the quantification of macrophage activation in synovial tissue of patients with knee OA, and to investigate the feasibility of using [18F]FEPPA PET/MRI in the assessment of synovitis in vivo.

Methods

Study design

We quantified macrophage activation using autoradiography ( [18F]FEPPA for TSPO) in histopathological sections of lateral suprapatellar synovial tissues collected at the time of routine arthroplasty from 10 participants with late-stage radiographic knee OA. These autoradiography measures were validated in serial tissue sections using immunofluorescence (IF) for TSPO in CD68+ and CD68- synovial macrophages (Fig. 1). A subset of 5 participants were recruited prospectively from the Rorabeck-Bourne Joint Replacement Clinic (University Hospital, London, Ontario, Canada) to undergo an additional clinical PET/MRI scan with [18F]FEPPA for in vivo quantification of synovial macrophage activation (Fig. 2) prior to arthroplasty and synovial tissue collection. The sample size of the full cohort was chosen based on power calculations from a previous cadaveric [18F]FEPPA validation [23]. Sex of participants who did not receive a clinical scan was selected to match the participants who did receive [18F]FEPPA PET/MRI. Study participants

This study was approved by Western University Health Science Research Ethics Board (HSREB #118,545 or 109,255) and Lawson Health Research Institute (Lawson R-21–506). Authorization to manufacture radiopharmaceuticals was given by Health Canada and approval to administer [18F]FEPPA as described in this study was given by the Human Radionuclide Safety and Scientific Review Committee. All participants provided informed written consent at the time of enrollment in this study.

The primary inclusion criterion was a diagnosis of late-stage knee OA requiring unilateral total knee arthroplasty. Exclusion criteria were inflammatory arthritis, previous infection in the index knee, and prior knee surgery, as were contraindications for PET and MRI including pregnancy and pacemakers. Participants were also excluded if tissue samples collected during surgery were inadequate for analysis.

[18F]FEPPA pet radioligand

[18F]FEPPA offers highly specific signal and robust quantification but like many other TSPO targeting radioligands, it is affected by a single nucleotide polymorphism in the TSPO coding gene (rs6971) [24]. This polymorphism is a single nucleotide substitution from A to T at position 147 in the fifth transmembrane domain. The resulting steric hinderance of [18F]FEPPA binding produces 3 distinct phenotypes: C—C (high), C-T (mixed), and T-T (low). Genotyping for this polymorphism was performed as previously described using peripheral whole blood [24]. Low affinity binder (LAB) polymorphisms were grounds for exclusion; high affinity binders (HAB) and mixed affinity binders (MAB) were included.\

Synovial tissue preparation

Synovial tissue was collected from participants at the time of TKA. Immediately after opening the knee joint capsule, a single synovial tissue sample for biopsy was isolated from a standardized location in the lateral suprapatellar recess. The tissue was placed in a formalin tube and transported for further preparation. Two adjacent synovial tissue explants were isolated from the original biopsy and fixed at 4 °C in 4 % paraformaldehyde overnight. The following morning, 1 was cryopreserved in optimal cutting temperature medium and the other processed and embedded in paraffin.

Paraffin embedding

Tissue samples were transferred to a 70 % ethanol solution until being run through a tissue processor to dehydrate and imbue the tissue with paraffin wax. The tissue was embedded in paraffin wax and 5 µm sections were collected on slides using a microtome. Slides were baked at 60C for 25 min and then cooled to room temperature. Sections were then deparaffinized using xylene and rehydrated with decreasing concentrations of ethanol (100 %, 95 %, 70 %, 0 %)

Cryopreservation

Synovial tissue was fixed overnight at 4C in 4 % paraformaldehyde, then bathed in a 20 % sucrose solution for 48 h, followed by a 40 % sucrose solution for another 48 h at 4C. Tissue was cryopreserved in optimal cutting temperature compound (Fisher Scientific, 23–730–571) by rapidly freezing on dry ice and stored at −80C. Tissue temperature was equilibrated to the cryostat (Leica CM1950) for 30 min until −25C and 2 serial sections per slide were cut to 8 µm thickness. Samples were mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific, 12–550–15) and stored at −80C until needed.

Autoradiography

Cryosectioned tissue was allowed to thaw to room temperature and then washed for 15 min in a submersion of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS – 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mMKH2PO4). Sections were incubated in PBS solution containing 5 MBq/L of [18F]FEPPA for 1 hour for adequate tissue uptake. The slides were washed with ice cold PBS for 60 s followed by a final 60 second wash with ice cold distilled water. Finally, the slides were allowed to air dry and placed in a digital autoradiograph (BeaQuant Beaver JTV-18–0009). Images were gathered over 6 h with an image size of 23.04 cm2. Each pixel was 100 µm2 giving an image resolution of 2304 × 2304 pixels.

Autoradiography images were segmented with the ROI defined as the whole tissue (BeaQuant, Beamage 3.0). The mean particle count per square millimeter was calculated as the measure of [18F]FEPPA uptake concentration in the tissue.

Immunofluorescence

Cryosectioned tissue was removed from the freezer and allowed to thaw to room temperature. Slides were hydrated in 1X PBS for 20 min. The glass slides were blotted dry, and a hydrophobic ring drawn around each tissue section to create a barrier for antigen retrieval. Heat mediated antigen retrieval was performed by microwaving sodium citrate buffer (pH=6.0) to 95C. Slides were submerged and allowed to cool to room temperature. Tissue was permeabilized with 0.2 % Triton X-100 in 1X PBS for 10 min. Then the tissue sections were washed 3 times with 1X PBS for 2 min each. Tissues were blocked with 1X PBS containing 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 1 hour at room temperature.

Primary antibodies were applied to one section and isotype control antibodies to the other section and incubated overnight at 4C. Primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-TSPO (Abcam, ab109497) 1.9 µg/ml and mouse anti-CD68 (Abcam, ab201340) 0.4 µg/ml. Isotype controls for any nonspecific immunoglobulin binding that may occur were rabbit IgG isotype control (Abcam, ab171870) 1.9 µg/ml and mouse IgG isotype control (Abcam, ab170190) 0.4 µg/ml. The next morning slides were washed 3 times in 1X PBS for 2 min each.

Secondary antibodies were applied to each section and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature and protected from light. Goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa 488 (Jackson Immunoresearch, 111–545–144) and goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa 647 (Jackson Immunoresearch, 115–605–003) were used. Sections were washed 3 times in 1X PBS for 2 min each. Sections were mounted with ProLong Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Fisher Scientific, P36931) and cured for 24 h, protected from light.

Images were acquired on a confocal microscope (Leica SP8 confocal). DAPI images were gathered using a HyD detector with a gain of 10 %. The 488 nm wavelength was imaged using a PMT detector with a gain of 450 V and the 647 nm wavelength was imaged using a PMT detector with a gain of 600 V. The images were gathered with a resolution of 2048 × 2048 pixels, with a frame average of 2 and a z slice thickness of 0.5 µm. Max projection images were produced with identical settings on LAS-X built-in software to ensure equivalence for comparison.

Images were analyzed in ImageJ 1.53t (National Institutes of Health, USA). Multi-channel images were segmented using Cellpose (www.cellpose.org); an open source, deep learning-based cell segmentation algorithm. Confocal images were segmented with a flow_threshold of 0.4, cellprob_threshold of 0 and a stitch_threshold of 0. The algorithm ran the suggested “cyto” model [25]. Segmented mask outlines were exported to ImageJ and the generated cell ROIs were examined (Z. K.) and adjusted if the learning model missed any regions of cell signal. TSPO mean intensity and average segmentation area statistics were drawn from the subset of all cell ROIs that were CD68+ and CD68- in both the intima and subintima.

Histopathology

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections from 5 slides representing every 100 µm distance along the tissue sample were stained with Harris Hematoxylin (Leica, 3,801,562) and Eosin Y (Fisher Scientific, E511). The tissue was incubated in Harris hematoxylin for 5 min followed by a 5-minute rinse in tap water. Tissue was then dipped in 1X acid alcohol to remove hematoxylin from the cytoplasm followed by another 5-minute rinse in tap water. Another dip in 1X alkaline water was done to turn the hematoxylin blue followed by a 5-minute tap water rinse. Eosin Y was incubated with the tissue for 5 min and then dipped in alcohol twice in 95 % ethanol solution and twice in 100 % ethanol solution to remove any weakly bound areas of Eosin. Lastly, tissue samples were dipped in xylene 30 times and mounted in xylene based mounting media (Cytoseal) and a coverslip was placed on top.

The most damaged region of each slide was scored at 200X magnification on a scale of 0 (none) to 3 (severe) for each of six components including synovial lining thickness (hyperplasia), synovial infiltration, fibrin deposition, vascularization, fibrosis, and perivascular edema, and the median score of the 5 slides was used as described in previous studies [26,27]. The rater had been calibrated against an expert rater with inter-rater reliability kappa values of 0.6 or above.

PET/MRI protocol

Bilateral knee scans with [18F]FEPPA were performed on 5 of the recruited participants at the Lawson Health Research Institute (St. Joseph's Hospital, London, Ontario, Canada) using a 3T Siemens Biograph mMR PET/MRI (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA). [18F]FEPPA prepared for intravenous injection was manufactured in the Nordal Cyclotron & Radiochemistry Facility (St. Joseph's Hospital, London, Ontario, Canada). Up to 185 MBq of [18F]FEPPA was injected as an intravenous bolus followed by a 20 ml saline flush. After a 45-minute tracer uptake period, participants were loaded into the bore of the scanner. A flexible body RF array (mMR Body Matrix) and posterior spine RF array (mMR Spine Matrix) were positioned over the participants’ knees to allow simultaneous bilateral imaging (Fig. 2). A 30-minute list mode PET acquisition was started at the beginning of the MRI protocol. PET images were reconstructed using the Ordinary Poisson Ordered Subsets Expectation Maximization method with an object space of 172 × 172 × 127 and a voxel size of 4.17 × 4.17 × 2.03 mm3. A two-point DIXON MRI acquisition was acquired to segment water, tissue, fat, and air for MR-based attenuation correction (MRAC) of the PET images [28]. The MRI protocol included coronal T1-weighted images for MRAC, sagittal-T2 weighted images for high quality images used to segment the supra-patellar synovium [29], and sagittal dual echo steady state (DESS) to distinguish cartilage from synovial fluid [30]. Participants with a previous knee replacement in the contralateral knee had the MRI protocol modified to metal artifact reduction sequences available in Siemens Advanced WARP package. These included 2D axial, sagittal, and coronal fast spin echo (FSE) images with view angle tilting and 3D FSE images with slice encoding for metal artifact correction and the short inversion time inversion-recovery (STIR) technique for fat suppression [31]. In addition to the metal correction protocol, MRAC µ maps were manually corrected using a method to in-paint the missing MR data created by metal artifacts using linear attenuation coefficient values for tissue, bone, and available 3D implant models, that was validated in a cadaveric knee study by Bourdon et al. [23]. PET data sets were retrospectively reconstructed with manually corrected µ maps to correct for photon attenuation. MRI sequence parameters are included in Table 2.

Using 3D DESS or T2 FSE MRI, suprapatellar synovial volume was manually segmented with 3D slicer (www.slicer.org). The tibial diaphysis was segmented as a background measure of tracer uptake in a region where little uptake was expected. All surrounding muscles within the MRI field of view (FOV) were segmented. MRI synovial segmentations were applied to the fused PET data to extract the signal intensity of [18F]FEPPA in the synovium (Fig. 2). Standardized uptake values (SUV) for [18F]FEPPA was calculated using the mean signal intensity, body weight, and decay-corrected injected dose. SUVmax was calculated in the same way using the maximum signal intensity in the synovial ROI.

Statistical analysis

All data was assessed for normality using a Q-Q plot. Depending on the normality of the data, Pearson or Spearman correlations were calculated. Correlations that would produce monotonic relationships were analyzed with the non-parametric Spearman correlation (ρ) when at least one variable was measured on an ordinal scale. Level of significance was set at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation and significance were calculated between the [18F]FEPPA SUV drawn from PET data and the macrophage activation determined from IF. All statistical tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism v9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

Autoradiography

We next used autoradiography on fresh-frozen synovial tissue biopsy sections to correlate the expression of TSPO in the synovial lining with the [18F]FEPPA signal. The mean tissue size was 6.0 ± 4.9 mm2 (range, 0.36 – 18.6 mm2) (Fig. 4A). The mean [18F]FEPPA signal was 46.7 ± 26.5 counts of radioactive decay/min/mm2 of intimal tissue (range, 12.5 – 116.6) (Fig 4B). PET/MRI

The utility and validity of [18F]FEPPA PET-MRI for assessing knee synovial macrophage activity in vivo in patients with knee OA needs validation We correlated the [18F]FEPPA signal from PET-MRI images collected before arthroplasty, to TSPO expression (immunofluorescence, above) in synovial biopsies from the same patients/knees collected during arthroplasty. Intense [18F]FEPPA uptake was observed in the suprapatellar synovium of each participant (Fig 2A). The [18F]FEPPA SUVmean in the index knees was 0.50 ± 0.15 (range, 0.22 – 0.68). There was a strong, but not statistically significant correlation between SUVmean calculated from [18F]FEPPA PET/MRI in the synovium and the intensity of immunofluorescent TSPO antibody staining in the intima of the same tissue (Spearman r = 0.90, p = 0.083, CI [0.086, 0.99]) (Fig. 2C). There was no correlation between SUVmax and immunofluorescent TSPO staining. Interestingly, previous TKA implant components in the contralateral knee were present in participants 1 and 3. These two participants had the highest PET SUV in the contralateral knee relative to the index knee among all 5 participants. This data was only used anecdotally and was to be expected, since arthroplasty does not cure OA and only replaces damaged bone and cartilage tissues, leaving the synovial lining intact. [18F]FEPPA SUVmean in the tibial diaphysis was 0.11 ± 0.06 (range, 0.03 – 0.20). The ratio between synovial SUVmean and SUVbone in the tibia was 4.54. The [18F]FEPPA SUVmean in the muscles surrounding the index knee was 0.43 ± 0.13 (range, 0.15 – 0.55).

Discussion

This study aimed to validate a non-invasive method of assessing synovial tissue macrophage activation with PET-MRI, by detecting TSPO with [18F]FEPPA. We detected a range of TSPO signals from synovial tissues using autoradiography for [18F]FEPPA (TSPO) that was primarily localized to CD68+ macrophages in the synovial intimal lining, and to a lesser extent in the sub-intima. We validated the same signal from PET-MRI measures in vivo by comparing these non-invasive measures to synovial tissue samples collected afterward during total knee arthroplasty.

Recent evidence has suggested that the inflammatory process and mediators may play an important role in the initiation and progression of OA. Inflammation mediates pain and disease progression in OA, as opposed to being a secondary feature of OA as once thought. It is therefore important to recognize and measure inflammatory processes in OA, which are critical to tissue damage and wound repair pathways [32]. An understanding of the role of inflammation in the progression of OA is therefore critical to developing disease modifying treatments. The synovium is a critical tissue in maintaining joint homeostasis and regulating inflammation [33]. As the dominant resident and recruited immune cell of the synovium, macrophages become activated in OA to mediate the inflammatory process and are therefore important to understanding the pathology of OA [34].

There are other well validated MRI and PET techniques for quantifying synovitis in the knee joint. Contrast enhanced MRI with gadolinium has been widely used to assess synovitis but lacks the ability to directly assess inflammatory cell activity. FDG PET is far more widely available than [18F]FEPPA. FDG targets glucose metabolism and can be used to measure increased synovial tissue metabolism in synovitis, but it does not directly target inflammatory cells. It is likely that the synovial inflammatory response is heterogenous and depends on many factors including age, sex, genetics, environmental factors, and the type of insult that provokes the immune response. Measuring synovitis alone may mask important relationships of immune cells in the progression of OA. To date, it has been challenging to measure synovial macrophage activation without direct measures on tissue biopsies, although some methods have been developed. Kraus et al. used SPECT-CT with 99mTc-EC20 (etarfolatide) to target the overexpression of folate receptor-β on activated macrophages and showed an association between macrophage activation and radiographic OA features [35]. The work presented in this study adds a novel component to nuclear imaging in the assessment of macrophage activation in vivo by validating tracer uptake signal directly to synovial tissue biopsies.

We demonstrated in this study that activated macrophages in synovial tissue can be detected using [18F]FEPPA, which targets TSPO overexpression on activated macrophages. We observed a strong correlation between ex vivo autoradiographic [18F]FEPPA uptake and macrophage activation measured by immunofluorescence. A strong correlation existed between [18F]FEPPA uptake and TSPO expression for both the synovial intima alone and whole tissue analyses. This study focused on the synovial intima where the highest density of macrophages is found. Previous studies have used TSPO-targeting PET tracers in assessment of the inflammatory burden in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Van der Laken et al. showed that there was higher expression of TSPO+ cells measured with [11C]PK11195 in RA patients with severe synovial edema compared to patients with mild edema [36]. Narayan et al. used autoradiography and [11C]PBR28 to show an elevated level of fibroblast-like synoviocytes and activated macrophages in the synovium of RA patients over healthy controls [37]. Our study, however, is the first to examine the use of TSPO PET in the assessment of OA. [18F]FEPPA has advantages over earlier generation TSPO-targeting radioligands in binding affinity and lipophilicity [38]. For example, [11C]PK11195 is limited by high nonspecific binding. [18F]FEPPA binding characteristics in knee synovial tissue were examined through the ratio of SUVmean between ROIs in the suprapatellar synovium and the tibial diaphysis. The ratio between synovial and bone SUV was 4.54. [11C]PK11195 has highly nonspecific binding and was found to have a contrast ratio of 1.6 in previous work using the metacarpal bone as a reference ROI in patients with RA [39]. In this study we showed high specific binding of [18F]FEPPA in the suprapatellar synovium relative to uptake in the tibial diaphysis. We noted high [18F]FEPPA uptake in skeletal muscle surrounding the knee joint. [18F]FEPPA uptake in femoral and tibial muscles was comparable to synovial uptake, 0.43 and 0.5 respectively. TSPO is a mitochondrial membrane protein involved in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis and energy generation. Increased [18F]FEPPA uptake in surrounding muscle may be related to the density of TSPO-containing mitochondria that support the energy generation of muscle tissue. This could pose a challenge for all TSPO-targeting PET tracers where background muscle uptake could obscure the target uptake. The knee joint capsule is separated from surrounding muscle and nonspecific muscle uptake did not affect ROIs generated in the joint space for this study.

Bone marrow lesions (BML) are a common feature of OA and can be visualized with T2 MRI. The inflammatory nature of BMLs is still debated. We observed high focal [18F]FEPPA uptake in the patellar subchondral bone coincident with a BML visualized on MRI (Fig. 2). Several studies have demonstrated relationships between damage of overlying cartilage and BMLs. There is also co-occurrence of meniscal damage and BML severity. Although the pathology of these lesions in OA is unclear, [18F]FEPPA PET/MRI may be useful for quantifying and characterizing the activation of macrophages in BMLs [40].

The synovial intima consists mainly of macrophages and fibroblast-like synoviocytes [41]. Infiltration of immune cells occurs during chronic synovitis. This study cohort had synovial tissue with 46.1 – 100 % CD68+ cells. This suggests that a large proportion of synovial intima tissue consisted of CD68+cells, which is a widely accepted marker for macrophages. All participants had highly activated macrophages with a large proportion of cells in the synovial intima also expressing TSPO.

To validate the utility of PET/MRI for accurate quantification of activated synovial macrophage content, we found a strong correlation between [18F]FEPPA PET SUVmean and TSPO immunofluorescent signal, although it was not statistically significant. However, a similar trend in Spearman r values was observed as in autoradiographic validation. Based on our results and other studies, we expect that this is likely due to limited power with only 5 patient/tissue samples, but overall, these results are highly encouraging. Due to the high cost and resource requirements of PET/MRI, it was challenging to conduct a validation study in a larger number of patients. Nonetheless, the consistency of our in vivo results is supported by the strong ex vivo validation we conducted simultaneously. There was no correlation between SUVmax and TSPO immunofluorescent signal. This could be because tissue sections from synovial samples used for immunofluorescent staining were likely not from the small voxels of focal [18F]FEPPA uptake but would correlate more strongly to the mean tissue signal.

We conducted this study in a convenience sample of patients who were undergoing primary knee arthroplasty. In the future, in vivo imaging will be needed to understand the relationship between inflammation and OA symptoms, phenotypes, responses to treatment, and disease progression. Previous studies have demonstrated the ability to track macrophages in vivo through other nuclear medicine approaches [42,43] or MRI [44]. In addition, similar studies have looked at assessing macrophage activation in RA using older generation TSPO targeting tracers or assessing features of OA using sodium fluoride or fluorodeoxyglucose PET tracers [10,13,37]. These results are promising and represent a novel progression in assessment of synovitis by using a next generation PET tracer ( [18F]FEPPA) to target synovial macrophages in combination with excellent soft tissue contrast of 3T MRI sequences. This imaging protocol may allow assessment of the relationship between macrophages and disease progression, validate future anti-inflammatory disease interventions, or further investigation of the role of macrophages in mediating the activation and proliferation of fibroblasts in the development of fibrosis in OA.

The relationship between chronic synovitis and fibrosis in OA is not clear, but fibrosis is a consequence of chronic inflammation [45]. Synovial fibrosis can occur during the progression of OA. Fibrosis is characterized by excessive fibroblast proliferation and an imbalance between collagen synthesis and break-down. Hepatic macrophages regulate liver fibrosis by secretion of pro-inflammatory factors in the early stages of liver damage and then promote the resolution of hepatic fibrosis through the secretion of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) [[46], [47], [48]]. Similar fibrosis mediation has been observed in the heart, kidney, and bowels where macrophage polarization phenotype regulates either the promotion or resolution of fibrosis. Studies have observed that inflammation occurs more in the early stages of OA and fibrosis is reported in the later stages [49,50]. It is possible that with chronic activation the regulatory function of macrophages to control fibroblast differentiation and fibrin deposition may be lost as OA progresses. This may lead to a gradual loss of fibroblast regulation over time and a slow imbalance towards fibrosis in the synovial tissue. It might be useful to study macrophage activation longitudinally to assess the relationship between chronic macrophage activation, fibroblast activation, and development of knee stiffness in OA.

Conclusion

[18F]FEPPA uptake in knee synovial tissue is strongly correlated to the expression of TSPO in synovial lining macrophages, suggesting that clinical [18F]FEPPA PET/MRI may be a valid measure of true macrophage activity in synovial tissues in knee OA. [18F]FEPPA may be an effective tool to assess macrophage activation both ex vivo and in vivo, and should be investigated further to assess its performance and measurement properties in different clinical contexts of knee OA including disease states and responses to treatment.
