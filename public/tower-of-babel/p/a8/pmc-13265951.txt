An affinity reagent-conjugated biotin ligase for amplified cell surface labelling in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Beck Holden, Mohammed Mutaher, Raktima Raychowdhury, Matt Bakalar, Abraham Lopez, Marc A Schwartz, Esther Olajide, Marina Santos, Nir Hacohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeting cells for ablation, gene therapy or drug delivery is an ongoing challenge in development of therapeutics. With the goal of efficiently labeling cell surfaces based on target recognition, we linked the well-established engineered ultraID biotin ligase to cell-surface affinity reagents to enable catalytic installation of tags on the surface of cells. Biotin ligase conjugated to nanobodies against cell surface proteins exhibited an order of magnitude increase in cell labeling compared to nanobody alone. Biotin labeling of cells remained efficient at low nanomolar concentrations of the conjugate, and was stable for at least 4 h. By testing a swath of antibody-biotin ligase conjugates against various targets and cell types, we confirmed the feasibility and generalizability of this approach. Administration of biotin ligase-conjugated antibodies into animals efficiently biotinylated the expected target cells. Biotinylated target cells were subsequently detectable with fluorescent streptavidin injected into the same mice. Enzyme mediated cell targeting thus provides an efficient and modular platform for biotinylation and detection of cells expressing specific surface targets with potential applications for research and therapeutics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46452-7.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Drug discovery

Introduction

The ability to target precise subsets of cells is crucial for a wide variety of research questions and many emerging clinical applications, including antibody therapeutics, antibody–drug conjugates, chimeric antigen receptor T cells, and gene therapy1–5. We broadly define cell targeting as identifying cells based on a unique and recognizable feature, typically surface markers, to enable detection or manipulation of specific cell types.

Standard approaches consist of a targeting domain complexed with or covalently attached to a cargo, drug or detection reagent. The targeting domain can consist of an antibody (or antibody fragment), peptide-MHC, synthetic peptide, aptamer, or other selective binding moiety3,6–9. Under this definition, ‘targeting’ encompasses everything from familiar immunostaining reagents, like fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies or pMHC tetramers, to barcoded antibodies, aptamer-based diagnostic reagents and CAR-T therapeutics9–13. However, these approaches sometimes fall short in both sensitivity and precision; for example, failing to identify rare cell subsets or those denoted by low expression of surface markers; struggling from toxicity associated with off-target activity; and being generally limited to a single identifying surface marker14–17. This final weakness is particularly relevant for immune cells, where physiologically distinct subsets often share overlapping patterns of surface marker expression, and cancer cells, which are often identified using markers that are present (albeit at lower levels) on healthy cells18–23.

Innovative new cell targeting approaches have been developed that address some but not all of these concerns. Some, like bi- and multi-specific antibodies, seek to incorporate greater selectivity by combining multiple targeting domains; while tuning binding affinities can offer some control, these approaches often fall short of distinguishing a population from subsets expressing only one of the markers2,24–26. An innovative technology from the Baker lab combines cage & lock/key domains to engineer an ‘AND’ gate. Other groups utilize environment-specific features to trigger activity, for example antibodies activated by elevated extracellular ATP levels within tumors27. These approaches, while promising, are not well positioned to address sensitivity concerns and may require engineering and tailoring for new applications. Conversely, multimeric approaches that effectively increase sensitivity by boosting avidity offer the same precision as conventional methods9,28,29. There is a need for new, easy-to-implement cell targeting techniques that offer greater precision and sensitivity than existing methods.

Enzyme-based detection methods have been implemented extensively to increase signal sensitivity; for example, employing horseradish peroxidase and substrates or products that are readily detectable (or attached to detectable moiety, ex. biotin tyramide)30,31. Enzymatic detection methods are useful for enzyme-linked immunosorbance assays, immunohistochemistry, fluorescence in situ hybridization, proximity labeling to detect protein–protein interactions (ex. APEX), and a variety of assays31–33. However, they often require harsh reagents like hydrogen peroxide that destroy biological samples and are restricted to in vitro use. A fully biocompatible, non-destructive system could enable new and more complex experiments and allow for safe implementation in living systems.

Others have similarly recognized the need for biocompatible cell labeling tools. Two recent methods, TyroID and LaccID, provide elegant approaches for proximity labeling at the cell surface under gentle conditions—TyroID through a tyrosinase variant that installs ortho-quinone handles on nearby proteins, and LaccID through an engineered laccase that uses O2 rather than H2O2 to oxidize labeling substrates34,35. Here, we aim to contribute an additional, complementary tool that relies on convenient, off-the-shelf affinity reagents and an engineered biotin ligase for a variety of cell labeling applications.

Here, we aimed to develop a biocompatible, enzyme-based cell targeting system. In such an approach, a targeting domain directs an enzyme to the surface of target cells, where the enzyme iteratively attaches a cargo or recognition handle to the cell surface. We believe that this approach offers several distinct advantages over existing methods. First, it could take advantage of the catalytic nature of enzymes to improve sensitivity via built-in signal amplification. Second, it introduces new parameters to tune, including substrate concentrations and enzyme kinetics. Third, it creates new opportunities for greater precision through conditional enzymatic activity, such as compartment-specific targeting based on local environmental features like pH or ATP concentration, or the use of activatable enzyme systems that require multiple markers to effectively target cells.

There were several key design criteria to consider in selecting an enzyme to serve as the basis of our targeting system. It requires an enzyme that is convenient to use; biocompatible and non-toxic, along with its substrates, in vivo; and which can be broadly applied to a variety of cell types without engineering or pre-treatment. In other words, an enzyme that reacts with a functional group that is near-universally present at the cell surface. In designing our system, we were able to draw upon a wealth of existing enzyme-based labeling tools, for example proximity labeling systems like BioID in the protein–protein interaction space36–38. The requirements for these systems largely, though not entirely, overlap our requirements for a cell labeling tool. We selected engineered biotin ligases as the basis for our targeting system.

Several biotin ligases have been engineered extensively for proximity labeling: for example, a variant of the E. coli protein BirA termed BioID and similar variant of the analogous Aquifex aeolicus protein (BioID2)39. These enzymes are normally responsible for the ATP-dependent biotinylation of biotin carboxyl carrier protein; however, introducing an R → G point mutation in the active site has been shown to permit the biotinoyl-5’-adenylate intermediate to exit the active site and react with an available lysine residue (or other substrate) it encounters within approximately 10–20 nm (by which point it is hydrolyzed)38–41. Given the near ubiquity of solvent-exposed lysine residues in cell surface proteins, the vast majority of cell types should be readily amenable to labeling with these modified biotin ligases without requiring any engineering or pre-treatment. Biotin then serves as a convenient recognition handle that enables a wide range of applications, such as detection, ablation, activation, and delivery, by taking advantage of the wealth of existing streptavidin and anti-biotin reagents.

Results

Designing a biotin ligase-based system for cell targeting

Our goal was to create an enzyme based cell targeting system comprised of a targeting domain covalently linked to a biotin ligase that biotinylates nearby lysine residues at the surface of target cells, providing convenient recognition handles to enable a range of applications using various biotin-binding reagents such as streptavidin or anti-biotin antibodies (Fig. 1A). In addition to the targeted biotin ligase, this system requires biotin and ATP, both inexpensive, convenient and biocompatible at the working concentrations for biotin ligases42–44.

We took a modular approach to designing our system, expressing biotin ligase as a fusion protein with the SpyCatcher protein (v003) so that it could be readily combined with a variety of targeting domains, including nanobodies fused with the corresponding 16-mer SpyTag sequence and produced in bacteria, or purified antibodies chemically conjugated to a synthetic SpyTag peptide (Figure S1A)45. Alternatively, a given candidate nAb/Ab could be expressed as a single protein fused to biotin ligase for scale-up and further study.

We selected a previously reported truncation variant of A. aeolicus biotin ligase called ‘ultraID’ for both its ease of expression and its rapid biotinylation activity. We arrived at this after testing several biotin ligase variants (BioID, BioID2, TurboID, microID) with varying expression or activity limitations46,47. See Figure S1 for further detail on biotin ligase variants expression and testing. We confirmed that our recombinantly expressed ultraID was active by assessing biotinylation activity in vitro, with activity detected after only 10 min (Figure S1B-C).

The high biotinylation activity exhibited by ultraID translated to efficient cell labeling. We combined SpyCatcher-ultraID with an anti-human CD4 nanobody (nAb) containing an N-terminal SpyTag sequence, generating aCD4-ultraID conjugate. We treated U937 cells (a CD4+ human monocyte-derived cell line) with either aCD4 targeted or non-targeted ultraID (10 nM), biotin (50 µM), and ATP (1 mM) for 1 h at 37 °C, then washed and stained with APC streptavidin to compare relative surface biotinylation efficiency (Fig. 1B). Whereas aCD4-ultraID efficiently labeled U937 cells over the treatment period, the non-targeted ultraID (ie., SpyCatcher-ultraID alone with no targeting domain) exhibited negligible surface biotinylation, demonstrating that the observed biotin labeling required aCD4 nanobody binding (Fig. 1B). We selected ultraID to develop our system moving forward, hereafter referred to as biotin ligase.

We then conjugated the ultraID biotin ligase to an anti-mouse CD47 nanobody and assessed targeted biotinylation of B16 cells, a murine melanoma line expressing CD47 (Fig. 1C). As before, the targeted enzyme efficiently biotinylated cells, while the non-targeted enzyme exhibited negligible activity. We also included an ‘off-target’ condition, treating B16 cells with biotin ligase linked to an irrelevant nanobody (anti-human CD4). This off-target biotin ligase exhibited negligible activity, equivalent to the non-targeted enzyme, confirming that enzymatic cell surface biotinylation under these conditions required an appropriate targeting ligand.

We assessed targeted vs. non-targeted biotin labeling across a range of biotin ligase treatment concentrations in order to test this selectivity and determine the optimal treatment window. Using the two model systems above, we treated U937 and B16 cells with a range of concentrations of our targeted and non-targeted biotin ligase, using anti-human CD4 nAb and anti-mouse CD47 nAb, respectively, as targeting domains (Fig. 1D–E). Biotin labeling was detected with enzyme concentrations as low as 500 pM for the U937s and 250 pM for the B16s, with optimal activity around 10–30 nM. Note that some background biotinylation was observed for the non-targeted biotin ligase at about 50–100 nM, although it remained significantly lower than targeted labeling. Thus, we created an enzyme-based targeting system that selectively biotinylates the surface of target cells based on the targeting ligand.

Surface biotinylation increases with treatment time and persists for several hours

We next characterized the change in biotin labeling over time in order to determine optimal treatment duration. Using our two model cell lines and targeting nanobodies from previous experiments, we treated U937 cells with aCD4 biotin ligase and B16 cells with aCD47 biotin ligase (with biotin and ATP) continuously for varying times before evaluating relative biotinylation efficiency (Fig. 2A). Biotin labeling was detectable as early as 10 min for U937s and 30 min for B16s, then increased with continuous treatment (Fig. 2B–C). Biotinylation activity at shorter timescales was confirmed in an independent experiment that also demonstrated minimal to partial loss in activity when performing this treatment at room temperature rather than 37 °C (Figure S2A-B). Practical implementation of this system will likely require finding a balance between optimal biotin labeling and ease of use. The majority of our experiments used a 1–2 h incubation at 37 °C; however, this could be shortened and/or performed at room temperature for convenience while preserving sufficient activity.

Another important consideration for practical implementation is the duration over which enzymatically-attached biotin is retained at the cell surface. Loss of biotin signal is expected due to natural turnover of cell surface proteins, and understanding the rate of this signal loss could be important for some applications (ex. in vivo labeling). To assess this, we treated cells with non-targeted and targeted biotin ligase (aCD4 nanobody with U937 cells or aCD47 nanobody with B16s), biotin and ATP for 1 h at 37 °C, then exchanged the treatment media for fresh media without enzyme, biotin or ATP and incubated for an additional 0–23 h prior to analysis (Fig. 2D). Note that the timecourse was set up in a staggered format, so that the washing, staining and analysis steps were performed simultaneously for all conditions.

Cell surface biotin levels remained constant for an hour after the treatment ended before beginning to drop, with some cell surface biotin still detectable at the 24 h timepoint. Loss of surface biotinylation was more rapid for U937s than B16s, with half-lives of 4.1 h and 7.7 h, respectively (Fig. 2E–F). The difference in decay rates could be due to differences in membrane protein turnover rates between the two cell lines, indicating that there will likely be some variability in surface biotin retention for different cell types and applications. Despite this variability, these findings indicate that our targeting system installs a recognition handle that can be readily detected for at least several hours after labeling.

Targeted biotin ligase is selective for cells expressing the appropriate surface marker

In order to determine the selectivity of our approach within a mixture of target and non-target cells, we conjugated aCD4 nanobody to biotin ligase in order to label CD4+ T cells within a sample of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Briefly, an aliquot of previously-isolated and frozen PBMCs was thawed, washed and plated at a density of 200 k cells per well in a 96-well plate. PBMCs were treated with 25 nM of our aCD4 biotin ligase (or non-targeted control), 50 µM biotin and 1 mM ATP for 1 h at 37 °C, then washed, stained and analyzed via flow cytometry. Only cells expressing CD4 were biotinylated, with CD4+ T cells exhibiting strong PE streptavidin signal and negligible signal from CD8+ T cells, B cells, and other CD4- lymphocytes (Fig. 3A, S3). A cell population expressing lower but detectable levels of CD4 were biotinylated at correspondingly lower levels. Thus, our enzyme-based system exclusively targets cells expressing the appropriate marker in the presence of a variety of different cell subsets.

To further probe this selectivity, we examined the fidelity of surface biotinylation in a population with varying ratios of target and non-target cells. CD4 expressing cells (U937s) were mixed with CD4- cells (modified Jurkat line) such that CD4+ cells represented 0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 50%, 90%, or 100% of the total population. CD4+ cells were efficiently biotinylated by aCD4 biotin ligase while CD4- cells remained unlabeled across all target: non-target cell ratios, even when target cells constituted only 5% of the population (Fig. 3B, Figure S4). Targeting efficiency decreased slightly as the percentage of target cells decreased, but was relatively stable overall, further confirming the precision of enzyme-based targeting.

Enzyme-based targeting is more sensitive than nanobody labeling alone

To assess the sensitivity of our enzymatic approach as compared to ‘conventional’ targeting (i.e., direct binding by a nanobody or antibody), we made a biotinylated nanobody similarly detectable after staining with PE streptavidin (Figure S5A). We treated SpyCatcher biotin ligase with 1–10 molar equivalents of NHS-biotin, then quenched the reaction prior to aCD47 nanobody conjugation (in order to avoid potentially disrupting the nanobody’s binding site). We then treated B16 cells with 25 nM of these biotinylated constructs (no biotin or ATP) or 25 nM aCD47 biotin ligase with 50 µM biotin and 1 mM ATP then washed, stained and analyzed cells via flow cytometry (Fig. 3C).

Enzymatic labeling yielded significantly higher signal than conventional labeling by the biotinylated nanobody constructs. After one hour, targeted biotin ligase exhibited 15 × higher biotin labeling than the ‘conventional’ constructs with a 1:1 biotin: nanobody ratio, 10 × higher than the 2:1 biotin: nanobody construct (i.e., two biotin molecules per nanobody), and 3.2 × higher than the 10:1 biotin: nanobody construct (Fig. 3C). Note that there is potential inefficiency in the NHS-biotin labeling reaction, which likely accounts for the inconsistencies in the observed fold-change for ‘conventional labeling’ constructs with different biotin: nanobody ratios. Even considering this possibility, our system exhibited consistently higher activity than all of the ‘conventional labeling’ conditions with aCD47 nanobody, indicating that enzyme-mediated targeting could be a promising approach to increase sensitivity over some existing methods.

We recognize that this format (a biotinylated nanobody stained with PE streptavidin) does not perfectly reproduce conventional labeling, which would more accurately consist of a nanobody linked to PE. However, given the extremely high affinity of the biotin-streptavidin interaction and the substantial excess of streptavidin-PE used in staining, we do not anticipate a notable loss in staining efficiency caused by this extra step; rather, we selected this experimental design in an effort to maintain staining consistency across ‘conventional’ nanobody binding and our biotin ligase system and minimize any potential discrepancies (e.g., potential PE quenching during handling for a nanobody-PE conjugation). Future efforts to systematically compare sensitivity between this method and conventional labeling should likely use multiple approaches, including this technique and a ‘conventional’ nanobody-fluorophore conjugate, to obtain a comprehensive comparison.

Biotin labeling occurs at ATP concentrations of 50–200 µM

One mechanism by which enzyme-based targeting could enable greater precision than conventional strategies is by introducing local environment-specific requirements; for example by requiring a substrate that is not present in many physiologic compartments. While abundant inside the cell, extracellular ATP is typically 10–100 nM; the key exception being the tumor microenvironment. Due to a variety of mechanisms, including release from uncontrolled cell death and an increase in ATP transporter activity, extracellular ATP in the tumor environment is several orders of magnitude higher than in healthy tissue48–50. While extracellular ATP levels likely vary substantially from one tumor type to another, prior work estimates that tumor extracellular ATP is generally in the 50–200 µM range26. Since many enzymes, including our biotin ligases, are ATP dependent, we might take advantage of this localized 100- to 1000-fold change in extracellular ATP abundance to introduce additional selectivity to our targeting system.

To begin evaluating this possibility, we assessed targeted enzymatic biotinylation across a range of ATP concentrations. Briefly, we treated U937 or B16 cells with 25 nM biotin ligase (using aCD4 and aCD47 nanobodies as targeting domains, respectively), 50 µM biotin, and a range of ATP concentrations from 1 µM to 1 mM (Fig. 4A, B). Biotin labeling increased with ATP concentration, as expected, with the highest observed activity at 1 mM ATP (the concentration used in previous experiments). Significant targeted biotinylation was observed across the tumor-relevant extracellular ATP concentration range of 50–200 µM, highlighted in gray. Furthermore, there was no biotinylation at concentrations of extracellular ATP found in healthy tissues (10–100 nM). This suggests that our enzyme-based targeting system may be able to detect and manipulate cells with a given surface marker in a tumor while ignoring cells with the same marker in other compartments. Such an application will require further validation in multiple in vivo tumor models.

Modular design facilitates targeting a variety of markers across different cell types

We next wanted to determine whether our approach is generalizable across cell types and target surface markers. To probe this, we obtained a synthetic SpyTag peptide with an azide conjugation handle and commercial antibodies (in a low azide format) against a range of human and murine targets and determined a simple bioconjugation strategy to readily generate antibody-biotin ligase combinations (Figure S6). We then treated a diverse set of cell types with Ab-biotin ligase conjugates targeting a range of markers, including: murine CD3, CD8a, CD44, PD-L1, LAG3, TIM3, and TCRb; as well as human CD3, CD4, CD28, CD44, and Her2 (Fig. 5). We observed consistent, target-dependent biotinylation across murine cells (Fig. 5A) and human cells (Fig. 5B), including different immortalized and primary cell types. We demonstrated that this targeted enzymatic labeling approach is generally applicable across a variety of cell types with different origins, characteristics, and surface markers.

It is worth noting that comparing our approach to conventional antibody staining with manufacturer recommended and/or standard in-house staining protocols did not yield a gain in sensitivity and in some cases was less sensitive (Figure S7).

Targeted biotin ligase retains its activity and precision in vivo

We next evaluated whether our targeting system is functional in an in vivo setting. To test this, we injected mice intraperitoneally with targeted biotin ligase, biotin and ATP in saline, followed by a supplemental injection two hours later containing only biotin and ATP. One hour later, mice were sacrificed, spleens were harvested, and biotinylation of splenocytes was assessed via flow cytometry (Fig. 5C). We tested four conditions: an aCD44 antibody-biotin ligase conjugate, an aCD8a antibody-biotin ligase conjugate, a non-targeted biotin ligase, and a negative control (biotin and ATP only, no biotin ligase). As expected, the non-targeted ligase did not label splenocytes in vivo, while the aCD44-targeted ligase labeled a broad population of splenocytes and the aCD8-targeted ligase labeled a smaller subset (Fig. 5D). Using an independent CD8a antibody stain to distinguish CD8+ and CD8- splenocytes, we confirmed that labeling with the aCD8 ligase was restricted to CD8+ cells (Fig. 5E). These results were reproduced in an independent experiment (Figure S8), demonstrating that our biotin ligase can efficiently and selectively biotinylate cells in vivo, enabling potential downstream applications.

To determine whether biotinylated cells could be addressed directly in vivo, we assessed in situ binding of a fluorescent streptavidin conjugate. Mice were injected with targeted biotin ligase, biotin, and ATP, followed three hours later by an injection of PE streptavidin or saline as a control (Fig. 5F). After one hour, mice were sacrificed, spleens were harvested, and splenocytes were analyzed via flow cytometry. Splenocytes from each mouse were split into two samples, one of which was stained in vitro with PE streptavidin to serve as a control. Notably, the biotin concentration used in this experiment was reduced 100-fold without affecting biotinylation efficiency. Lowering free biotin levels likely minimized competition for streptavidin binding and improved in vivo signal. We observed robust labeling of biotinylated CD8+ splenocytes by PE streptavidin in vivo, confirming that our biotin ligase system can be utilized along with a streptavidin conjugate to target cells entirely in vivo (Fig. 5G–H). We confirmed these findings in an independent follow-up experiment (Figure S9A-C).

Discussion

We designed an enzyme-based targeting system as a new approach towards identifying precise cell subsets. This system consists of an engineered biotin ligase covalently attached to a targeting domain (such as a nanobody or antibody) that binds a target surface marker, allowing the ligase to biotinylate surrounding cell surface proteins. Using low to mid-nM concentrations of targeted biotin ligase, we observed target-dependent labeling without requiring pre-treatment or manipulation of the target cells. We also characterized the temporal dynamics; detectable biotin labeling occurring within 30 min and maximum labeling achieved by 6–8 h. Biotinylation signal remained constant for an hour post-treatment then began to decline, presumably due to normal turnover of membrane proteins, with variability between cell types and observed half-lives of about 4–8 h. These dynamics should enable convenient use of the system as a detection reagent or cell manipulation tool in vitro or in vivo. Notably, labeling could likely be accelerated or slowed by substituting a different biotin ligase.

We then evaluated several key factors for our system’s utility: its precision and sensitivity, ATP dependence, and generalizability. We confirmed that it selectively labels target cells in the presence of decoy cells, including a complex PBMC mixture with a variety of decoy cell types. In designing this system, we hoped the biotin ligase would provide signal amplification; and we observed an order of magnitude increase in labeling sensitivity compared to nanobody binding alone. Notably, this significant signal boost was not observed for most antibody-biotin ligase conjugates, suggesting a direction for future development. We also evaluated targeting efficiency as a function of ATP concentration and saw that, while maximal activity occurs around 1 mM ATP, significant targeted biotin labeling occurred in the mid- to high-µM range. This is of particular interest because it could allow for localized intratumoral labeling, i.e., taking advantage of the elevated extracellular ATP levels within tumors to avoid targeting cells outside of the tumor microenvironment. We confirmed that this approach is generalizable across cell types and surface markers by attaching a range of commercial antibodies to our biotin ligase and labeling a variety of human and mouse cell types. Our biotin ligase-based system can interchangeably target different populations from a variety of sources without manipulation or pre-treatment.

Finally, we demonstrated that our system can be applied in vivo, expanding the potential use-cases for this targeting system. We demonstrate its activity and selectivity in vivo by labeling CD44+ and CD8+ splenocytes in mice. We further confirm that cells tagged with biotin are addressable in vivo, opening a wide range of potential applications. Future efforts will expand upon these in vivo pilots to evaluate selectivity for the tumor microenvironment, as suggested by our in vitro ATP titration study.

We suspect that the lack of increased sensitivity compated to conventional antibodies (Figure S7) may be due to the bulkier targeting domain interfering with the biotin ligase’s ability to access cell surface proteins, since the early experiments were designed with a ~ 15 kDa nanobody as the targeting moiety rather than a ~ 150 kDa antibody. This could potentially be addressed with careful linker design (ex., increased length, tuning flexibility). Another possible factor is the NHS conjugation to install the heterobifunctional linker, if it installed the linker on lysine residues proximal to the binding site which might interfere with target binding. This could be addressed by expressing the antibody-biotin ligase construct as a fusion protein in an antibody expression system; although this could limit the system’s utility as an “off the shelf” technology compatible with a range of commercially available antibodies and other targeting moieties. We anticipate linker design to be the most appropriate direction to optimize moving forward.

Future work will explore applications for this platform beyond detection, such as: selective ablation of tumor cells using a streptavidin-toxin conjugate or anti-biotin antibody (via ADCC), activation of a defined immune subpopulation by delivering a cytokine or transcription factor, and genetic modification with biotin-targeted viral vectors or CRISPR/Cas vehicles. This system could also be utilized to direct anti-biotin CAR-T cells, a previously reported strategy to simplify CAR-T engineering51. Additionally, this system could provide another biocompatible tool for cell surface proximity labeling of protein–protein interactions (with added spatial validation needed via a membrane co-localization study). By taking advantage of this system’s modularity, ‘off the shelf’ reagents could be utilized for many applications.

A final advantage of an enzyme-based targeting approach is the opportunity it presents to introduce further control, such as designing a system that recognizes subsets defined by two or more markers (i.e., an “AND” gate) by taking advantage of existing strategies to control enzyme activity: for example, an inactive variant of our biotin ligase that requires a corresponding protease to sever a blocking domain, a kinase to add a necessary phosphate group; or a split enzyme format. We believe that enzyme-based methods represent a powerful new tool for precision cell targeting.

Materials and methods

Cloning and recombinant protein expression

Cloning and recombinant protein expression

Custom gene blocks for biotin ligase and nanobody constructs were designed using Genscript’s codon optimization tool and ordered from IDT, then inserted into a pFloat.T7 plasmid via Gibson cloning using a HiFi DNA Assembly kit from New England Biolabs according to manufacturer’s protocol. Plasmids containing biotin ligase or nanobody genes were then transformed into 5a E. coli competent cells (New England Biolabs) and spread on LB plates with 30 µg/mL kanamycin. The following day, colonies were selected to inoculate cultures for plasmid analysis and/or amplification, then grown overnight with 25 µg/mL kanamycin and isolated with QIAprep Spin miniprep kit (Qiagen) or ZymoPURE II midiprep kit (Zymogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

For recombinant protein production, relevant plasmids were transformed into BL21 Star (DE3)pLysS One Shot Chemically Competent E. coli (Invitrogen), spread on plates with 25 µg/mL kanamycin and grown overnight at 37 °C. Colonies were picked to inoculate 3 mL cultures (LB broth with 25 µg/mL kanamycin), which were grown for 6 h at 37 °C (with 270 rpm) and then used to inoculate 100 mL cultures (TB broth with 25 µg/mL kanamycin). These cultures were incubated at 37 °C (with 270 rpm shaking) until they reached an OD600 of ~ 0.6–0.8, at which point 50 µM IPTG was added to induce recombinant protein expression (or 0–500 µM IPTG during troubleshooting experiments) and flasks were returned to an incubator to grow at 18 °C overnight (270 rpm shaking). Cultures were then centrifuged at 5000×g to pellet bacteria, and bacterial pellets were either frozen at – 20 °C for storage or lysed immediately. Lysis was performed using B-PER Complete Bacterial Protein Extraction Reagent (Qiagen) with the following additives: MgCl2 to a final concentration of 2 mM, Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo), and Benzonase nuclease (Millipore Sigma, ~ 0.05 units/µL). Lysis reactions were incubated at room temperature for 15 min with gentle mixing, then centrifuged at 14,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatant (containing soluble protein) was then applied to Co/Ni–NTA resin for affinity purification. Resin was incubated with lysis supernatant for 1 h at 4 °C, then washed 3 × with ice cold wash buffer (PBS with 20 mM imidazole) and eluted using PBS with 300 mM imidazole. Eluate was concentrated and exchanged to 20 mM Tris buffer with 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0. Purified protein concentration was determined using a Nanodrop 8000 Spectrophotometer and adjusted to 10 µM prior to aliquoting and flash-freezing for long-term storage at – 80 °C.

For in vivo studies, protein production was performed in a mammalian cell line, Expi293F cells. Cells were cultured in Expi293 medium (Gibco) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and grown at 37C with shaking at 300 rpm in a humidified tissue culture incubator supplemented with 5% CO2. A 200 mL culture that contained 200 million cells with a viability greater than 90% was transiently transfected with 200 mg of endotoxin free plasmid DNA using lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The plasmid contained SpyCatcher-biotin ligase with a C-terminal His-6 tag for easy purification. Six hours after transfection the media was removed by centrifugation, cells were resuspended in a fresh 200 mL media and allowed to grow for 3 days. Cell supernatant was then harvested and concentrated to 5–7 ml using a 10 K amicon filtration unit. It was then purified using NTA-Cobalt-based ligand containing Dynabeads (Invitrogen) made for purification of His-tagged proteins. Specifically, we added 1:1 ratio the concentrated supernatant and a buffer containing 10 mM imidazole, 0.02% Tween 20 and 4 mM MgCl2 in 2xPBS and incubated it with Dynabeads for 30 min at 4 °C on a rotating platform. The mixture was then placed on a magnet and beads were washed three times with 20 mM Imidazole in PBS + 0.01% Tween-20. Finally, the pure protein was eluted with 1.5–2 ml of 500 mM Imidazole in PBS + 0.01% Tween-20. The purified, endotoxin free SpyCatcher biotin ligase was verified by Immunoblotting, quantitated, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C for further use.

2.Bioconjugation

Bioconjugation

A custom SpyTag peptide with an N-terminal azido-lysine was ordered from Genscript (sequence: LysN3-GGSGGSGGSRGVPHIVMVDAYKRYK, 90% purity) in a lyophilized form. Upon receipt, N3-SpyTag peptide was suspended in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma) to prepare 1 mM and 10 mM stock solutions then stored immediately at − 20 °C. Commercial antibodies were purchased from BioLegend in an UltraLEAF format (ultra-low endotoxin, azide free) to ensure ease of compatibility with ‘click’ chemistry. Dibenzocyclooctyne-PEG4-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl ester (DBCO-NHS) was purchased from Sigma.

Bioconjugation was performed in two reaction steps (see Figure S6): using DBCO-NHS to install a strained alkyne functional group on the commercial antibody, then conjugating N3-SpyTag via copper-free click chemistry. A typical reaction combined 6 µM antibody with 40 molar equivalents DBCO-NHS in PBS incubated at room temperature for 2 h with gentle mixing, although 3–12 µM antibody and 20–80 equivalents DBCO-NHS were tested during reaction optimization. The reaction was quenched with Tris buffer (final concentration 100 µM Tris, 300 mM NaCl) for another 20 min at room temperature, then de-salted and cleaned up to remove unreacted DBCO-NHS using Zeba spin columns (Thermo, 0.5 mL size and 40 k MWCO) following manufacturer protocol. The resulting DBCO-antibody conjugate (~ 5 µM) was then combined with 4 molar equivalents of azide-SpyTag (2.4 µL of 1 mM stock) and reacted overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. The reaction mixture was then subjected to another cleanup step to remove unreacted peptide as before (Thermo, 0.5 mL size and 40 k MWCO). Prior to storage, sodium azide was added to a final concentration of 0.06% (from a 0.6% w/v sodium azide stock solution, Sigma) as a preservative. SpyTag-antibody conjugate concentrations were measured via Nanodrop.

The sensitivity comparison experiments (Fig. 3C) required a version of the nanobody-biotin ligase conjugate that was itself biotinylated, such that PE streptavidin could be used as a readout and its relative binding efficiency could be directly compared to enzymatic cell targeting (Figure S5B). To produce this variant, we mixed purified SpyCatcher biotin ligase at a final concentration of 1 µM in PBS with 1–10 molar equivalents of Biotin-PEG4-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl (NHS-biotin, Sigma) and incubated at room temperature for 2 h with gentle mixing. Excess NHS-biotin was then removed using a spin desalting column as above, and the biotinylated SpyCatcher-biotin ligase was then conjugated to the appropriate aCD47 nanobody. Note that while the purpose of this biotinylated construct was to measure direct nanobody binding to the cell surface, we conjugated biotin to the enzyme before addition of the nanobody in order to avoid any potential disruption of the nanobody’s binding site from the NHS-biotin reaction.

For all targeted enzyme constructs, biotin ligase was conjugated to the appropriate targeting domain using the SpyCatcher/SpyTag system (version 3). This was the case for both nanobodies, which were expressed in-house with the SpyTag sequence, as well as commercial antibodies, which were tagged with a SpyTag peptide as described above. SpyCatcher/SpyTag conjugations were set up according to the following protocol­­: an aliquot of SpyCatcher-biotin ligase was thawed and immediately diluted to a final concentration of 1 µM in PBS, then purified SpyTag-nanobody/antibody was added to a final concentration of 1 µM (except for non-targeted controls, which had no nanobody or antibody) and the reaction was incubated for 10 min at room temperature. In some troubleshooting experiments, 2–5 equivalents of SpyTag-nanobody/antibody were used. Reactions were set up on a 10–100 µL scale as needed to generate material for cell targeting experiments and used immediately.

3.Mammalian cell culture

Mammalian cell culture

Cells were handled under sterile conditions and maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in either RPMI 1640 or DMEM supplemented with 100 U/mL pen/strep, 2 mM GlutaMax and 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum. All cell culture reagents were purchased from Gibco unless otherwise specified. B16, U937, B3Z, Jurkat and ExpiF293 cells were obtained from ATCC and MCF-7 cells were a kind gift from Leif Ellisen at MGH. Cells were used up to passage 20 and were regularly tested for mycoplasma.

5.Enzyme based targeting experiments

Enzyme based targeting experiments

Cells were plated in a 96-well plate (1–2 × 104 cells/well) and incubated overnight prior to treatment. Immediately prior to experiment, treatment media was prepared by adding 100 mM ATP stock (Thermo) to a final concentration of 1 mM and 50 mM biotin stock (Sigma, DMSO stock solution prepared in-house) to a final concentration of 50 µM (unless indicated otherwise, i.e. for ATP concentration range experiment in Fig. 4).

Targeted biotin ligase constructs (or non-targeted controls) were produced as described above and the appropriate construct was added for each experimental condition. Cells were centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min (for suspension cells only) and media was aspirated, then replaced with treatment media containing biotin ligase, biotin, and ATP. Cells were returned to incubator and maintained at 37 °C for one hour (unless otherwise indicated, i.e. time course experiments), after which cells were washed and stained for flow cytometry analysis. For adherent cells only: treatment media was aspirated and 40 µL trypsin (0.25%, Gibco) was added to each well, then cells were incubated for 5 min at 37 °C after which 100 µL media was added to quench trypsinization. For all cells: cells were centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min and media was removed. Ice cold wash buffer (BioLegend, 100 µL) was added to each well with gentle mixing. This step was repeated for two total washes, then 50 µL of staining buffer was added to each well and cells were incubated, covered, at 4 °C for 20 min. Staining buffer consisted of wash buffer with 1:100 PE streptavidin (BioLegend). For some experiments, staining antibodies were also used (indicated in figures, all from BioLegend). After staining, cells were washed twice as before, then suspended in 100 µL buffer with 1:1000 SyTox Blue Live/Dead stain. Stained cells were analyzed using a Beckman CytoFLEX S or CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer. Raw data was processed using FlowJo v10 and GraphPad Prism was used for plotting and statistical analysis where relevant. Reported MFI values represent the median fluorescent intensity of the relevant cell population (after gating for live singlets), reported as the average of triplicate measurements unless indicated otherwise. “Relative MFI” values were determined by normalizing to a negative control (stained but untreated), calculated as: (MFISample—MFIControl)/MFIControl, where MFISample is the average MFI of the triplicate sample and MFIControl is the average MFI of the triplicate negative control wells.

In vivo experiments

Animal safety and handling

C57BL/6 mice were bred in-house at the Broad Institute Vivarium from strains originally obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Both male and female mice were used in all the in vivo experiments with an average body weight of 25 g at time of experiments. General health and appearance were monitored throughout all experiments. All animal procedures were approved by the Broad Institute Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard and conducted in accordance with institutional and federal guidelines. All methods and experiments are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Euthanasia

Mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation. CO₂ was delivered at a displacement rate of 30–70% chamber volume per minute and animals were kept in the chamber for at least one minute past the cessation of breathing. Cervical dislocation was performed after the animals were unconscious and had stopped breathing to ensure death prior to dissection.

Reagents and injections

All reagents made for injections were prepared using sterile technique in a biosafety cabinet. For all experiments, reagents including antibody-biotin ligase (1.75 µM), biotin (100 mM or 100 uM), and ATP (10 mM) were injected intraperitoneally (IP). For any experiments testing in situ binding of PE streptavidin to selectively biotinylated cells, PE streptavidin was injected intravenously through the retro orbital (RO) sinus.

IP injections

Mice were injected using a 27G × ½” needle in the lower right quadrant of the body under sterile conditions in a biosafety cabinet. Gentle aspiration was used to ensure that the needle had not entered any visceral organs or blood vessels. If fluid was not aspirated, the injection was performed. If fluid was aspirated, the needle was removed, and veterinary staff was consulted before continuing. Afterward, mice were visually observed to ensure hemostasis.

RO injections

Prior to injection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and anesthesia depth was validated. For each mouse, 100 uL of PE Streptavidin (BioLegend) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL was injected intravenously through the retro orbital sinus using a 29G × 1/2” needle. To prevent leakage and stop any bleeding, the eyes were manually closed and slight pressure was applied immediately after the needle was withdrawn. Afterward, mice were visually observed to ensure hemostasis.

Tissue processing

Spleens were harvested from mice after experimental procedures and processed immediately for flow cytometry analysis. Spleens were dissociated using glass frosted slides and treated with ACK lysis buffer for one minute. Following a series of washes and filter steps, splenocytes were counted and stained using antibodies for cell surface receptors. Prepared samples were run on a flow cytometer and data analysis was performed in FlowJo.
