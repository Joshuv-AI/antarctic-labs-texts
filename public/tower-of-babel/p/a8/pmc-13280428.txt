Introduction

In many countries, including Australia, the health of people residing in rural areas is poorer than that of people residing in metropolitan areas [1]. There are many factors that contribute to this divide, including structural barriers such as poorer access to health services. This can influence health literacy levels and the embedding of positive health behaviours, both of which can impact the prevalence of chronic disease [1].

One way of improving the health outcomes for rural communities is to ensure access to quality health services, underpinned by evidence‐based practice and care appropriate to community needs. These elements require rural health services with research capacity. The literature about research capacity building (RCB) acknowledges that such activities strengthen program implementation, evaluation and aid empirical evidence [2, 3, 4]. According to Cooke's framework [5], RCB relies on skills and confidence, support and partnerships, as well as research that is ‘close to practice’ and appropriately disseminated. In rural areas, contextual factors, such as varying degrees of prioritising and operationalising research, are particularly influential in building research capacity [6], as is the availability of rurally‐based researchers with local knowledge and connections [3, 4].

The Rural Health Academic Network (RHAN) was established in 2006 to build research capacity among rural health services in Victoria by embedding researchers into rural health services. Designed as a hub and spoke model, RHAN is a joint venture between a university department of rural health and rural health services. Each of the health service partners, both individually and collectively, has unique strengths, challenges, health service priorities and community health issues. For this reason, RHAN researchers at each site adapt and respond to local context and needs.

In 2021, RHAN began the development of an illustrative conceptual model (the model) to promote how it functions to build the research capacity of rural health services. Based on program logic, logic frameworks and Theory of Change (ToC) concepts, such graphics can illustrate how a complex initiative achieves outcomes [7, 8]. RHAN selected ToC guidance to inform the model due to its flexibility, capacity to capture complexity, and the need to incorporate the assumptions of Cooke's RCB framework. ToC concepts provide an advantage over other approaches (logic model or logic framework methods), which tend to be rigid and linear and most useful for singular programs, projects or interventions [8, 9]. The rationale for developing the RHAN model was to provide a shared understanding of the Network as an initiative which flexes to incorporate multiple and unique contexts; to add to knowledge about RCB; and to inform new RHAN coordinators and broader partners about its capabilities. This quality improvement paper presents the development of the RHAN model and discusses the challenges and benefits of this approach.

Methods

Setting

Current RHAN coordinators are embedded in four North‐Northeastern Victorian rural health services representing Modified Monash Model (MM) 3–5 categories. RHAN coordinators (embedded researchers) lead RCB activities by teaching into RCB programmes, identifying gaps in skill sets, and connecting people through collaborative activities. The coordinator roles are jointly funded and supervised via agreements between the university and each health service. Most often, they are recruited from within the health service or through health partners, prioritising local rural knowledge and experience. They have ‘ears to the ground’, live and work in the same communities, and have broad personal and professional networks.

Intervention

RHAN undertook the development of the model through collaborative group discussions. These were undertaken during scheduled monthly RHAN meetings, involving four RHAN coordinators and their three university supervisors. Progress was further reviewed during the one‐hour monthly progress meetings at the health service site with each individual coordinator's health service supervisor (a manager or executive). One RHAN coordinator was tasked with drafting the diagram (see Figure 1), which was circulated and recirculated via email between meetings to incorporate suggested edits and updates.

The Rural Health Academic Network illustrative conceptual model 2025.

To explain how these discussions resulted in the Network's model, we utilise a ‘checklist for reporting of ToCs in public health intervention development’ [7]. The checklist was an outcome of a systematic review by Breuer et al. [7], which aimed to examine the global application of ToCs in the development and evaluation of public health interventions. Subsequently, it has been recommended by Lam et al. [10], as an important guidance tool.

Utilising Breuer's Checklist

Breuer's checklist covers five domains (including subdomains) posed as questions: (1) Is the ToC approach defined? (2) Is the ToC development process described? (3) Is the resultant ToC depicted diagrammatically? (4) Is the process of intervention development from the ToC described? and (5) Is the way in which the ToC was used to develop and implement the evaluation described? Results

The model highlights the diversity of work the RHAN coordinators may be involved in, without specifying each unique action. This can range from leading Research Governance‐Research and Innovation Committees and Human Research Ethics Committees, organising a local research symposium, or assisting allied health students with placement project plans. Discussion

This paper has described the RHAN initiative, which aims to build research capacity within rural health services. It has presented a quality improvement process of developing an illustrative conceptual model to depict the work of the Network and the embedded rural research coordinators. It highlights support of RCB through research expertise, collaboration and partnerships, and adaptive responses to health services and community needs; all critical elements for RCB in the rural context [3, 4]. There have been challenges and benefits in model development, application and operationalisation, and review.

Challenges and Benefits of Model Development

The challenges of developing a model to describe an initiative that builds research capacity varied. Previous authors note that building capacity to engage in and use research contends with negative attitudes, the absence of contextual relevancy, and relationship and communication barriers [11, 12]. It was challenging to capture the overarching RHAN model through a one‐page conceptual illustration that could be interpreted and understood by a variety of audiences.

RHAN was mindful that the model required plain (non‐academic) language, the presentation of a framework which was broad but also meaningful to local partners and captured the varied work of coordinators as a Network. In addition, the development process had to be transparent and replicable; this was strengthened by using Breuer's checklist. As noted in the literature, details about the development of concepts such as a ToC are scarce, giving the impression that the process is superficial [13, 14].

The benefits of developing the model through discussion ensured RHAN coordinators were invested in the process by emphasising the value‐add to their work. Importantly, the development process led to mutual understanding about the need to illustrate the Network as an overarching concept. Whilst also acknowledging each health service site's unique and common characteristics. This was important to assist with translatable insights from individual sites and to then report outcomes as a collective [10, 15]. The model also fulfilled a communication function to showcase the initiative and inform new RHAN coordinators, collaborators and stakeholders about the way it works. Development was also beneficial in considering promotion to wider audiences such as academics, government decision‐makers and community‐based representatives.

Challenges and Benefits in Model Application and Operationalisation

The application of the model presented challenges as it was not designed to be a step‐by‐step instruction manual for all research endeavours undertaken at each rural health service site. The unique differences in scope and approaches of the coordinators and the health service contexts required a model which could be applied with flexibility whilst maintaining its vision and core strategies.

The operationalisation of the model is similarly challenging if it exclusively relies upon comparisons between sites to determine the initiative's impact [16]. It is intended to encourage a nuanced presentation of outputs and outcomes across the divergent health service sites. This supports the Network's belief that comparisons between sites would be inappropriate. With further development, it has the potential to provide meaningful guidance about reporting of outcomes leading to impact.

The operationalisation of the model has benefits for macro uses, such as examining RCB across the footprint of a university department of rural health, to micro uses, such as presenting individual coordinators' actions within their health service. The Network is utilising the model to endorse these different levels (macro–micro) of outputs and outcomes to diverse audiences, such as funders, reporting bodies, in grant applications, other researchers, healthcare networks, and communities served by the health services.

Challenges and Benefits in Model Review

It is acknowledged that the constant iteration required for the RHAN model may pose a barrier to its further development. In adopting a continuous learning cycle and viewing it as a living document, this fosters commitment to its regular review. RHAN will use the review process as an opportunity for learning and fostering innovation. This encourages appreciation of how outputs and outcomes of an endeavour may alter over time and across sites. This process intends to assist with tracking what happens as a site evolves and matures. Along with investigating the benefits of continuity over time, when engagement and investment continue, and partnerships grow and consolidate. Ultimately, it may provide a framework to examine RHAN's contribution to the impact on community health.

Regular review ensures the Network remains informed about current changes in healthcare, acknowledges issues of researcher isolation, lack of critical mass, and the need for generalist, interdisciplinary research. This assists with making strategic choices about what works in which context [17, 18]. RHAN is committed to promoting a model that is suitable for the complex present and also adaptable to the uncertain future [19].

Overall, the RHAN model highlights some of the barriers and facilitators of rural health service‐led research and capacity building. Particularly, the barriers to reporting long‐term impact. It emphasises facilitating factors such as partnering with academic institutions, availability of research coordinators, aligning research projects to health service priorities, and increasing the confidence and skills of the rural health workforce to undertake projects [6, 11, 20].

Limitations

There are limitations with this model development. Although informed by ToC concepts, the Network's mechanisms for change are not yet fully articulated. A further gap is a formalised feedback loop from the health service sites, which is not yet fully developed and inserted. Another is community input to understand opinions on value, trust, and acceptance of place‐based rural research. As highlighted, the Network can track outcomes, but measures for overall impact, the relationships formed, and causal pathways to RCB are not always measurable. However, the health services are confident that RHAN is increasing RCB and have willingly funded and progressed partnerships long‐term. The broad approach described in development, application, and review is likely to be useful for others aiming to build research capacity in rural service settings.

Conclusion

The development of the RHAN model supports the fundamentals of what is known about building research capacity rurally. This paper has highlighted the complexity of a Network designed to increase rural research capacity and discussed the challenges and benefits in documenting such a model. Consequently, we would invite other rurally located sectors, concerned with strengthening research capacity, to engage in such a process to represent their work.

An ethics approval was not required for this paper. As the development of the RHAN model is the outcome of a process of ongoing reflective practice by a group of interdisciplinary researchers, rather than a research project, an ethics application was not required to undertake or report it.
