CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miR-223-3p serves as a potential biomarker for systemic lupus erythematosus and attenuates lupus nephritis in NZBWF1/J mice
Authors: Shu-Zhen Xu, Peng Wang, Sha-Sha Tao, Hai-Fen Wei, Yu-Tong Tan, Xiao-Xiao Li, Xiao-Ke Yang, Hui-Hui Shen, Guo-Cui Wu, Hai-Feng Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough emerging evidence links exosomal miRNAs to autoimmunity, the specific role of CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to identify key exosomal miRNAs derived from CD4 + T cells in SLE and to assess their diagnostic and therapeutic potential. We found that CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miR‑223‑3p was downregulated in SLE patients and demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.65–0.77). When combined with conventional clinical markers, it provided incremental diagnostic value. Its correlation with clinical markers (C3/C4) was specific to the CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal fraction, distinguishing it from the plasma‑derived counterpart. Functionally, miR-223-3p overexpression inhibited the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and T cell apoptosis while promoting mitophagy via FBXW7. Additionally, it disrupted pathological T‑B cell interactions by suppressing BAFF and CD19 expression. In a murine lupus model (NZBWF1/J), administration of exosomal miR‑223‑3p alleviated lupus nephritis, as evidenced by reduced proteinuria, lower Anti‑dsDNA titers, and diminished renal immune complex deposition. Collectively, our study identified CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miR‑223‑3p as a cell-origin-associated biomarker in SLE, while also acting as a pathogenic regulator through the FBXW7/mitophagy axis and modulation of T-B cell crosstalk. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disorder with a global prevalence of approximately 30 to 70 per 100,000 individuals, predominantly affecting women of reproductive age [1, 2]. The exact etiology and pathogenesis of SLE are still incompletely understood. The absence of curative therapies necessitates lifelong pharmacological treatment, imposing substantial psychological and economic burdens. Moreover, disease-related complications contribute to a markedly increased mortality risk compared with the general population [3]. Current diagnostic approaches, which rely primarily on serological markers, often lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity. Therefore, it is vital to identify novel disease-specific biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Exosomes are nanosized extracellular vesicles, typically 30 to 200 nm in diameter, secreted by many cell types [4]. These lipid‑bilayer vesicles transport diverse bioactive cargoes, including proteins and microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby enabling intercellular communication [5, 6]. Upon release from immune cells, exosomes modulate immune synapses and are internalized by recipient cells, regulating processes such as antigen presentation and inflammatory responses [7, 8]. Notably, exosomal miRNAs can suppress inflammatory gene expression in target cells and participate in immune coordination [9]. Increasing evidence suggests that exosomal miRNAs are promising biomarkers for SLE. For instance, plasma exosomal miR-21, miR-150, and miR-29c correlate with lupus nephritis (LN) chronicity [9] and show promise for early detection of renal fibrosis and predicting disease progression [10, 11]. Longitudinal studies further demonstrate that urinary exosomal let-7a and miR-21 levels are reduced during active LN compared with remission, indicating their potential utility in disease staging [12]. In addition, renal tubular cell-derived exosomes enriched with miR-107, miR-31, and miR-135b-5p are correlated with clinical outcomes in LN patients [13]. Nevertheless, current research has predominantly focused on exosomal miRNAs derived from mixed biofluids, which represent heterogeneous vesicle populations originating from multiple cellular sources [14]. Given that exosomal cargo composition and biological function are closely tied to their cellular source [15], immune cell-derived exosomal miRNAs may offer superior diagnostic precision and mechanistic insight.

CD4 + T cells are central regulators of immune homeostasis. Dysfunctional CD4 + T cells can induce abnormal B cell activation and the production of pathogenic autoantibodies, thus contributing to the onset and progression of SLE [16]. T cell-derived exosomes serve as essential mediators of intercellular communication, participating in a wide range of immune regulatory processes. Accordingly, systematic investigation of CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs holds promise for identifying disease‑specific biomarkers and elucidating novel pathogenic mechanisms in SLE. In the current study, we profiled CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs in SLE and assessed their clinical relevance in an independent cohort. We further elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which a signature miRNA modulates T-B cell crosstalk and suggested the therapeutic efficacy of exosome-mediated miRNA delivery in the NZBWF1/J murine lupus model. This study offers a comprehensive framework for unravelling the role of CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs in SLE pathogenesis, revealing their dual promise as both diagnostic biomarkers and innovative therapeutic targets.

Subjects and methods

Study subjects and ethics statement

The Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University approved the study (No. YX2022103) in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent and permission to use biological specimens for research were obtained from all subjects before participation. Patients with SLE were recruited from the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, as well as the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China from March 2023 to August 2025. All patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) satisfied a minimum of four out of the eleven revised criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology for SLE [17]. Patients were classified as having LN based on the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for SLE and the 2024 ACR Guideline for the Screening, Treatment and Management of Lupus Nephritis. Disease activity was evaluated using the Safety of Estrogens in Lupus National Assessment and the SLE Disease Activity Index (SELENA-SLEDAI) score [18]. The exclusion criteria encompassed patients diagnosed with other autoimmune diseases, individuals suffering from systemic infections and malignant tumors, as well as those with severe heart, liver, or kidney failure, and persons who declined to participate in the study. Healthy control subjects, matched for age and sex, were recruited from the community, all of whom had no history of autoimmune disease and did not meet any classification criteria for SLE.

Clinical features and laboratory indicators

Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, and residential area, were obtained through face-to-face interviews. Data on height, weight, blood pressure, medication use, and laboratory indicators were retrieved from medical records. Medications were classified as immunosuppressants, biologics, immunomodulators, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Laboratory indicators, including IgG, IgM, and IgA, antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-double-stranded DNA (Anti-dsDNA) antibody, anti-Smith (Anti-Sm) antibody, anti-Sjögren’s Syndrome-related antigen A (Anti-SSA) antibody, anti-Sjögren’s Syndrome-related antigen B (Anti-SSB) antibody, anti-nuclear ribonucleoprotein (Anti-RNP) antibody, anti-ribosomal P-protein autoantibody (Anti-rRNP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), complement C3, complement C4, clinical manifestations, and SLEDAI score were evaluated by two experienced physicians, including rash and photosensitivity, oral ulcers, arthritis, serositis, myositis, LN, neuropsychiatric (NP) SLE, hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytopenia. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by height in meters squared.

Blood sample preparation and extraction

For each participant, a 5 mL fasting blood sample was collected into an EDTA vacutainer. Samples were stored in a portable, insulated cooler with ice packs at 4 °C for no more than 2 h before transport to the laboratory for immediate analysis. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density‑gradient centrifugation at 500 g for 25 min using Human Peripheral Blood Lymphocyte Separation Medium (LTS10771, TBDsciences). Plasma was then carefully aspirated and stored at −80 °C.

Cell isolation and cell sorting

CD4 + T cells were isolated from PBMCs by negative selection using the Naive CD4 + T Cell Isolation Kit II for human use (130-094-131, Miltenyi Biotec). The purity of the isolated CD4 + T cells was evaluated by flow cytometric analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

The isolated primary CD4 + T cells were seeded in 12-well plates with basic RPMI 1640 medium (C11875500BT, Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) depleted of exosomes (CY104, CellorLab) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (C0222, Beyotime). All exosome-related experiments were performed under conditions that eliminate exogenous exosome contamination. The culture supernatants were collected at two-day intervals. Jurkat Clone E6-1 cells (CL-0129) were obtained from Procell and cultured with basic RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. Raji cells (TCH-C304) were procured from HyCyte and cultured under the same conditions. For activation assays, Jurkat cells were stimulated with recombinant human IL-2 protein (abs04045, Absin) (10 ng/mL) in the presence of anti-human CD3 mAb (S0B0009, STARTER) (5 µg/mL) and anti-human CD28 mAb (S0B0010, STARTER) (1 µg/mL) for 24 h. Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T cells (CL-0005) were acquired from Procell and cultured using DMEM high glucose supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Exosome isolation

Exosomes secreted by CD4 + T cells were isolated from culture supernatants using the VEX Exosome Isolation Reagent (R601, Vazyme). Exosomes sourced from plasma were isolated using the VEX Exosome Isolation Reagent (from plasma) (R603, Vazyme), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The quantity and size distribution of exosomes were analyzed using a ZetaView system (Particle Metrix). Before conducting the sample measurements, the instrument underwent calibration using standard 100 nm polystyrene beads. Particle concentration (particles/mL) and modal hydrodynamic diameter (nm) were determined using the integrated NTA software. All samples were processed in batch mode to ensure consistent analytical conditions.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM analysis, 10 µL of isolated exosomes were deposited onto a copper grid and incubated for 10 min at ambient temperature. Excess liquid was subsequently removed using filter paper. After air-drying, the samples were negatively stained with 10 µL of a 2% uranyl acetate solution and were subsequently analyzed using an HT7700 transmission electron microscope.

Western blot

Protein concentrations were measured using the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime). Exosomes and cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime). Both lysis buffers were supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (#8553, Cell Signaling Technology) to inhibit protease activity. Lysates prepared with equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). After rinsing with TBS three times and blocking with Protein Free Rapid Blocking Buffer (PS108, Epizyme) for 1 h at room temperature, the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting the target proteins: anti-CD9 (DL210002, DLM Biotech), anti-CD63 (DL210003, DLM Biotech), anti-CD81 (66866-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-Calnexin (10427-2-AP, Proteintech) and anti-TSG101 (DL210005, DLM Biotech), FBXW7 Polyclonal antibody (28424-1-AP, Proteintech), PINK1 Polyclonal antibody (23274-1-AP, Proteintech), PARK2/Parkin Polyclonal antibody (14060-1-AP, Proteintech), LC3 Polyclonal antibody (14600-1-AP, Proteintech), P62/SQSTM1 Polyclonal antibody (18420-1-AP, Proteintech). After thorough washing, secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP were incubated for 1 h, followed by another wash. ECL reagent (SQ201, Epizyme) was added to the membranes to visualize the immunoreactive protein bands. Band intensities were measured using ImageJ software, and signals of the target protein were normalized to β-actin (20536-1-AP, Proteintech) or GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech).

miRNA-sequencing and library construction

Total RNA from CD4 + T cell-derived exosomes was extracted using RNAiso Plus (9108, Takara). RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis and qualified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 and concentration ≥ 200 ng/µL were considered for subsequent cDNA library preparation. Small RNA libraries were constructed using the TruSeq SmallRNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA). Briefly, mature miRNAs were ligated with 3’ and 5’ adapters by T4 RNA Ligase 2 (truncated) and T4 RNA Ligase, respectively. The ligated products were reverse transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase. The resulting cDNA was amplified via PCR, incorporating 6-nucleotide indices for multiplexed sequencing. The amplified libraries were purified by 6% Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE) to enrich for fragments corresponding to 140–160 bp. Following library quality control using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina, USA).

Bioinformatics and differential expression analysis

Raw sequencing data underwent rigorous quality control. First, adapters and low-quality reads (Phred quality score < 20 in more than 40% of bases) were removed using fastp. Clean reads were then filtered by size, retaining only sequences between 18 and 36 nt. For classification and quantification, reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) and the miRBase database using Bowtie. Non-miRNA sequences (tRNA, rRNA, sRNA, and snRNA) were identified and excluded using blastn against the Ensembl and Rfam databases. miRNA quantification was performed using miRDeep2.

For normalization, expression levels were calculated as Reads Per Million (RPM). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 R package, which utilizes empirical Bayes shrinkage to stabilize dispersion estimates. To enhance stability during the discovery phase, miRNAs were filtered to retain those with raw counts ≥ 20 in at least 2 samples. Differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) were defined as |log2(Fold Change) |>1 and an adjusted P value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction). Target gene prediction was performed by intersecting miRanda and RNAhybrid predictions to minimize false positives and provide a more conservative interpretation of functional pathways. Raw sequencing data have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE328518.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA/miRNA from cells or exosomes was isolated using the Total RNA Isolation Reagent (YY101, Epizyme). The quality and integrity of the extracted RNA were evaluated using a microspectrophotometer (K5600C, KAIAO Technology). Reverse transcription was performed with the BioRT Master HiSensi cDNA First Strand Synthesis Kit (BSB40M1, BIOER Technology) or the miRNA First Strand cDNA Synthesis (Tailing Reaction) Kit (B532451, Sangon Biotech). The mRNA levels were quantified using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (RR820Q, TaKaRa) and normalized to GAPDH levels. The levels of miRNAs were quantified with the microRNAs qPCR Kit (SYBR Green Method) (B532461, Sangon Biotech). For cellular miRNA (from Jurkat cells or primary CD4 + T cells), expression levels were normalized to U6 snRNA (RNU6B) as an endogenous control. For exosomal and plasma miRNAs, synthetic 1 pmol cel-miR-39-3p (miRB0000010, Ribobio) was spiked into each sample prior to RNA isolation. cel-miR-39-3p was used as an exogenous spike-in control to correct for variations in RNA extraction efficiency and RT-qPCR between samples. All relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Each sample was tested in triplicate. The primer sequences for miRNA and mRNA are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Transfection

To evaluate the impact of miRNA on the functionality of CD4 + T cells, miRNA mimics or inhibitors for gain- or loss-of-function studies were transfected using the GA-RNA Plus Transfection Kit (GATP1001, GeneAdv). siRNA transfection was conducted using the riboFECTTM CP Transfection Kit (C10511-05, RiboBio). Plasmid transfection was carried out with Effectene Transfection Reagent (301427, QIAGEN).

ELISA

The levels of human TNF-α (EH0302), IL-2 (EH0189), IL-1β (EH0185), IL-10 (EH0173), BAFF (EH0042), and soluble CD19 (sCD19) (EH4869) in plasma samples were quantified using ELISA kits (Finetest). The following kits were used to quantify the respective indices in the serum of mice: Mouse Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) (EM1607, Finetest), mouse anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) antibody (EM1471, Finetest), IgG (EK271, MultiSciences), IL-1β (EK201B, MultiSciences), IL-6 (EK206, MultiSciences), and IFN-γ (EK280, MultiSciences).

Flow cytometry

The isolated CD4 + T cells from human PBMCs were subsequently stained with FITC Mouse anti-Human CD3 Antibody (OKT3) (abs180023, Absin) and PE Mouse anti-Human CD4 Antibody (OKT4) (abs180037, Absin) at ambient temperature for 30 min. Following staining, the cells were washed and analyzed using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer.

The apoptosis assay of Jurkat cells was performed using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1062, Beyotime). Jurkat cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 10 to 20 min, and then immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. Annexin V+/PI- cells were classified as early apoptotic, whereas Annexin V+/PI+ cells were categorized as late apoptotic.

Internalization of exosomes

The exosomes derived from Jurkat cells were stained with PKH67 (HY-D1421, MedChemExpress) and subsequently added to Raji cells. After 24 h, the mixture was stained with 5 µg/mL DAPI dihydrochloride (HY-D0814, MedChemExpress) for 3 min. The distribution of exosomes was examined using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell co-culture

A non-contact co-culture system was established using Transwell inserts (TCS016006, Biofil) to examine the influence of exosomes secreted by CD4 + T cells on B cell function. Jurkat donor cells were pre-treated with GW4869 (20 µM, HY-19363, MedChemExpress) or solvent control for 24 h, then co-cultured with Raji B cells in transwell inserts. For the exosome uptake inhibition assay, Raji cells were pre-treated with Dynasore (80 µM, HY-15304, MedChemExpress) for 1 h to inhibit dynamin-dependent endocytosis, followed by the addition of exosomes from LV-miR-223 Jurkat cells. After 48 h, BAFF and CD19 expression were analyzed.

Target gene prediction and luciferase reporter assay

For experimental validation of direct miRNA targets, potential binding sites were predicted using four independent algorithms: TargetScanHuman 8.0, miRDB v6.0, miRWalk v3.0, and miRDIP v4.1. Genes predicted by all four algorithms were considered candidate targets for subsequent assays. A fragment of the target gene’s 3′UTR containing the predicted miRNA binding site was cloned downstream of the luciferase reporter gene in the pmiRGLO vector to generate the reporter construct (RiboBio). For miRNA target gene validation, human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T cells were transfected with a 3′ UTR-pmiRGLO target gene vector, along with miRNA mimics or a miRNA negative control. After a 48-h incubation, the cells were lysed, and the relative luciferase activity was quantified using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (11402ES60, YEASEN). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized against Renilla luciferase activity.

Lentiviral transduction

Lentiviral vectors supplied by Hanbio were used to establish stable cell lines expressing the designated microRNA. Jurkat cells were transduced with a lentivirus encoding pre-miRNA at a multiplicity of infection of 50 and subsequently cultured in fresh media supplemented with puromycin after 24 h. The stable cell lines were harvested after 72 h and expanded for subsequent experimental procedures.

Mice

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (LLSC20220343). Eight-week-old female NZBWF1/J mice (JAX stock #100008) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory, USA [19]. The mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free animal facility, maintained at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 1 °C, under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with 50 ± 5% humidity. The body weight and proteinuria levels of the mice were monitored weekly and were scored by a researcher blinded to the experimental treatments administered to the animals. To assess the therapeutic efficacy of exosomal miRNA, exosomes with specifically overexpressed miRNA or control vehicles were labeled with PKH67 dye and administered through tail vein injections weekly, commencing once the proteinuria level reached approximately 1 g/L [20].

Whole-body fluorescence imaging

To examine the in vivo distribution of exosomes, they were tagged with PKH67. This fluorescent dye binds to cells and emits green fluorescence. The labeled exosomes were then injected into mice through the tail vein. Mice underwent to whole-body fluorescence imaging at 0 h, 8 h, 24 h, 72 h, and prior to sacrifice.

Urine protein and renal function assessment

The protein level in urine was assessed weekly using Urine Protein Test Strips (URIT, 1 V). The rank level was defined using a color-coded bar: – (0 g/L), ± (0.15 g/L), + (0.3 g/L), ++ (1.0 g/L), +++ (3.0 g/L), ++++ (≥ 5.0 g/L). Serum creatinine levels were quantified using the Amplex Red Creatinine Assay Kit (S0291S, Beyotime), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were measured using the Urea Assay Kit, Colorimetric (S0574, Beyotime).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

H&E staining was performed to assess histopathological changes in renal tissues. Briefly, the samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were subsequently deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min and eosin for 2 min. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series and clearing in xylene, coverslips were mounted, and images were captured using CaseViewer 2.4.

Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining

PAS staining specifically emphasizes carbohydrate-rich structures, including glycogen, glycoproteins, mucopolysaccharides, and basement membranes, by imparting a distinct magenta coloration. In this procedure, periodic acid oxidizes carbohydrate hydroxyl groups to aldehydes, which then react with Schiff’s reagent to form a characteristic magenta precipitate. The stained sections were imaged using CaseViewer 2.4.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was employed to assess the expression and subcellular localization of IgG and C3 within murine renal tissue. Tissue sections, 4 μm thick, were initially fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde to maintain structural integrity. Triton X-100 was employed to enhance antibody penetration, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin to mitigate non-specific binding. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (A22110, Abbkine), and a polyclonal antibody recognizing C3/C3b/C3c (21337-1-AP, Proteintech) were applied. Subsequently, immunofluorescence signals were captured using CaseViewer 2.4.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD), or as medians with interquartile ranges [M (P25, P75)], depending on their distribution, as assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and proportions [n (%)]. The mean differences between the two groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data). Bivariate correlations were assessed via Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient to examine relationships between miRNA expression and clinical or serological parameters. For subgroup analyses involving multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied to control for type I error. FDR-corrected P (Padj) values are reported. Associations with Padj < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Associations with nominal P < 0.05 but Padj ≥ 0.05 were considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. The diagnostic performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the computation of the area under the curve (AUC). Comparison of AUCs between different biomarkers was performed using the DeLong test. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the independent association of CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p with SLE. For longitudinal data with missing values due to animal attrition, a linear mixed model (LMM) was used to evaluate the effect of treatment, time, and their interaction. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the Log-rank test. Data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.3.1. Statistical significance was established at a two-tailed P value below 0.05.

Results

Mapping the expression of CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs

Exosomes isolated from CD4 + T-cell culture supernatants and plasma were characterized in accordance with MISEV2018 guidelines [21]. TEM images clearly showed typical cup-shaped vesicles with intact lipid bilayers (Fig. 1a and b). Membrane proteins CD9, CD63, CD81, and the cytoplasmic protein TSG101 were identified in the exosome lysate; conversely, the negative control calnexin was not detected (Fig. 1c). NTA revealed a median diameter of 171.9 nm for CD4 + T cell-derived exosomes (4.7 × 109 particles/mL) and 117.7 nm for plasma-derived exosomes (2.7 × 1012 particles/mL) (Fig. 1d and e). These characterizations confirm the successful isolation and identification of high-purity exosomes from both cellular and plasma sources.

As an initial exploratory step to identify candidate exosomal miRNAs, we performed miRNA sequencing of CD4 + T cell-derived exosomes from five patients with newly diagnosed SLE and three age-matched healthy control (HC) subjects. (Supplementary Table 2). Of 2967 exosomal miRNAs identified, 418 miRNAs (148 upregulated and 270 downregulated) were differentially expressed, based on the criteria of |log2 (fold change) | > ± 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05 (Fig. 1f). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the top 50 differentially expressed miRNAs revealed a distinct expression pattern between the SLE patients and HC subjects (Fig. 1g).

To elucidate the biological functions of the differentially expressed miRNAs, target genes were predicted using miRanda v3.3a and RNAhybrid v2.2. Only genes predicted by both algorithms were retained for subsequent enrichment analysis. Significantly enriched GO terms included those for vesicle transport and T cell activation, transmembrane transport and transcription factor activity, and cell junctions and receptor complexes (Fig. 1h). KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the endocytosis, cAMP signaling, and axon guidance pathways (Fig. 1i). These findings position CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs at the intersection of immune dysregulation and target organ susceptibility in SLE, rendering them potential candidates for understanding disease mechanisms and therapeutic exploration.

Validation of exosomal miR-223-3p expression and its diagnostic performance in the SLE cohort

We acknowledge that the small sample size in this discovery phase limits statistical power. Therefore, candidate miRNAs require validation in larger, independent cohorts before further functional characterization. Based on the initial sequencing, miR-223-3p emerged as the most downregulated exosomal miRNA and was selected for validation in an independent cohort (Table 1). Peripheral blood was collected from 110 SLE patients and matched HC individuals for the analysis of CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNA (Supplementary Table 3). CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miR-223-3p (Exo‑miR‑223‑3p) levels were significantly lower in SLE patients than in HC subjects, confirming the sequencing data (Z = −5.148, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2a). In contrast to the findings in CD4 + T cell-derived exosomes, plasma Exo-miR-223-3p levels were elevated in the SLE patients (n = 210) relative to HC population (n = 210) (Z = 7.453, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2b). Notably, total plasma miR-223-3p levels did not differ between SLE (n = 200) and HC groups (n = 200) (Z = 1.036, P = 0.3003; Fig. 2c), indicating that bulk circulating miRNA measurements may obscure disease-relevant signals. This divergent pattern suggests that miR-223-3p is actively and selectively loaded into exosomes rather than being uniformly dysregulated. These findings highlight the importance of specifying exosomal origin when interpreting miRNA changes and support a potential role for miR-223-3p in immune cell communication and disease development.

ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic potential of miR-223-3p in SLE. The AUC for both Exo-miR-223-3p derived from CD4 + T cells and plasma exceeded 0.7 (Fig. 2d and e), indicating a moderate capacity to discriminate SLE patients from healthy individuals. In contrast, plasma miR‑223‑3p exhibited limited diagnostic utility (AUC = 0.53; Fig. 2f).

We compared the diagnostic performance of CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p alone with that of its combination with conventional SLE serum markers (Anti-dsDNA, Anti-Sm, and low complement levels). As shown in Supplementary Table 4, CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miR-223-3p alone showed moderate diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.63–0.77). However, the combination of CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p with anti-dsDNA yielded an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.85–0.94, P < 0.0001), while the full panel achieved the highest AUC of 0.93. These findings indicate that CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p provides incremental diagnostic value when combined with existing clinical markers (Supplementary Fig. 1). Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p and SLE risk. As shown in Supplementary Table 5, CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p (ΔCT value) was significantly associated with SLE risk in both unadjusted and adjusted models. In the crude model, the OR was 1.295 (95% CI: 1.169–1.434, P < 0.0001). After full adjustment for sex, age, Anti-dsDNA, Anti-Sm, and low complement levels, CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p remained significantly associated with SLE (OR = 1.293, 95% CI: 1.077–1.553, P = 0.0059), indicating that this exosomal miRNA is independently associated with SLE beyond conventional serological markers.

Correlation of Exo-miR-223-3p with clinical and immunological indicators in SLE

To elucidate its clinical relevance, we evaluated the associations of Exo‑miR‑223‑3p with serological autoantibodies and complement levels. The clinical features of SLE patients are presented in Table 1. In the CD4 + T cell-derived exosome and plasma-derived exosome SLE cohorts, more than 90% were ANA positive, and over 60% were positive for Anti-dsDNA. However, Exo-miR-223-3p levels from either source were not associated with ANA or Anti-dsDNA status (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 3a and c). Notably, in exploratory analyses, CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p levels showed nominal associations with Anti-Sm (P = 0.0367) and Anti-rRNP (P = 0.0456) status. However, these associations did not survive FDR correction for multiple comparisons (Padj = 0.3670 and Padj = 0.2280, respectively). Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger independent cohorts (Fig. 3a). In contrast, plasma‑derived Exo-miR‑223‑3p levels showed no significant correlation with any of these autoantibodies (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 3c).

Exosomal miR‑223‑3p attenuates B cell activation by modulating cytokine secretion and apoptosis in T cells

We assessed Exo-miR-223-3p function by transfecting Jurkat cells with a miR-223-3p mimic or inhibitor, along with their respective negative controls (NC). Transfection efficacy was confirmed (Fig. 5a). Overexpression of miR‑223‑3p decreased the IL‑6 and TNF‑α mRNA levels (Fig. 5b). In contrast, inhibition of miR‑223‑3p led to a reversal of the TNF‑α decrease, without affecting IL‑1β or IL‑6 expression (Fig. 5c). Additionally, inhibition of miR‑223‑3p led to a rise in apoptotic cells, this effect was mitigated by the miR‑223‑3p overexpression (Fig. 5d). These functional experiments demonstrated that miR‑223‑3p modulates both inflammatory cytokine production and apoptosis in Jurkat cells, highlighting its role in regulating key T cell pathways.

To further investigate T‑B cell communication, a stable Jurkat cell line overexpressing miR‑223‑3p was established using lentiviral vectors (Supplementary Fig. 2). Exosomes were isolated from this transgenic line, labeled with the fluorescent dye PKH67, and subsequently applied to Raji B cells. Electron microscopy images demonstrated clear colocalization of the PKH67-labeled exosomes with Raji cells, thereby confirming that exosomes secreted by miR‑223‑3p-overexpressing Jurkat cells can be internalized by Raji B cells (Fig. 5e).

Based on the confirmed exosomal transfer, we next examined whether exosomes derived from LV‑miR‑223 Jurkat cells could modulate the function of Raji B cells. A transwell-based co‑culture system was established to allow exosome‑mediated communication between T and B cells while preventing direct cell‑cell contact (Fig. 5f). To confirm the exosome-dependent nature of observed effects, Jurkat donor cells were pre-treated with the exosome secretion inhibitor GW4869 (20 µM, 24 h) or solvent control, and treated Jurkat cells were co-cultured with Raji B cells in transwell inserts. Compared to the vehicle control, GW4869 treatment of donor cells abolished the suppressive effect on BAFF and CD19 expression in Raji cells (Fig. 5f). Purified exosomes from LV-miR-223 Jurkat cells, when directly added to Raji cells were sufficient to suppress BAFF and CD19 levels. Pre-treatment of Raji cells with Dynasore, an inhibitor of dynamin-dependent endocytosis, blocked the exosome-mediated effect, indicating that exosome internalization is required (Fig. 5g). Collectively, these results demonstrate that T cell-derived exosomes, specifically those enriched with miR-223-3p, directly modulate B cell activation markers.

miR-223-3p regulates mitophagy in T cells by directly targeting FBXW7

To clarify how miR-223-3p influences T cell function, we first aimed to identify its direct downstream targets. Bioinformatics predictions yielded 23 candidate target genes (Supplementary Table 6; Fig. 6a). We prioritized genes implicated in immune regulation and inflammatory responses for experimental validation. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the miR-223-3p mimic suppressed FBXW7 and IL6ST expression compared to the NC group (Fig. 6b). Conversely, the miR-223-3p inhibitor increased FBXW7 mRNA expression; however, it did not influence the expression of the other five genes (Fig. 6c).

As miRNA inhibitors can have indirect effects, confirmation of direct targeting requires assessing protein expression or performing functional validation via a luciferase reporter assay. We constructed luciferase reporter vectors containing the wild-type (WT) or mutated (MUT) 3′-UTR sequences of FBXW7 and IL6ST to validate the miR-223-3p binding sites. Co-transfection of the miR-223-3p mimic with the FBXW7 WT 3′-UTR reporter into HEK 293 T cells resulted in a marked decrease in luciferase activity relative to the mimic NC control. This inhibitory effect was abolished when the binding sites were mutated (Fig. 6d). Conversely, the miR-223-3p mimic failed to suppress luciferase activity of the IL6ST WT 3′-UTR reporter. Consistent with these findings, FBXW7 protein levels were lower in LV-miR-223 Jurkat cells than those in control (LV-NC) cells (Fig. 6e). Taken together, these data demonstrate that FBXW7 is a direct target gene of miR‑223‑3p.

FBXW7 regulates mitochondrial homeostasis by promoting Parkin-dependent mitophagy through substrate ubiquitination and degradation as part of the SCF ubiquitin ligase complex. We observed that miR-223-3p upregulation in Jurkat cells significantly increased PINK1 expression, promoted LC3-II conversion, and decreased p62 levels, indicating enhanced mitophagy and autophagic flux (Fig. 6f). Conversely, FBXW7 overexpression suppressed these effects, while FBXW7 knockdown recapitulated them (Fig. 6g and h), confirming that miR-223-3p regulates mitophagy through FBXW7. To determine whether the effects of miR-223-3p are specifically mediated through FBXW7, we performed rescue experiments by transfecting an FBXW7 overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-FBXW7) or an empty control in LV-223-Jurkat cells. These rescue experiments confirm that FBXW7 mediates miR-223-3p’s effects on inflammation (Fig. 6i), apoptosis (Fig. 6j), and mitophagy (Fig. 6k). Morphological validation by confocal microscopy further supported these findings. Dual-fluorescence imaging showed that miR-223-3p overexpression robustly increased colocalization of mitochondria (DsRed-Mito) and autophagosomes (EGFP-LC3), as evidenced by a striking increase in yellow puncta (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Importantly, this enhanced mitophagy was effectively reversed by ectopic restoration of FBXW7 expression (Supplementary Fig. 3b). These visual data corroborate our molecular findings, confirming that the miR-223-3p/FBXW7 axis is a critical regulatory pathway for mitophagy in T cells.

We further evaluated mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) as an indicator of mitochondrial health using TMRE staining. Overexpression of miR-223-3p markedly increased TMRE fluorescence, indicating a higher mitochondrial membrane potential (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Importantly, this protective effect was significantly attenuated when FBXW7 was restored by plasmid transfection (Supplementary Fig. 4b). These functional results align with our morphological findings, confirming that the miR-223-3p/FBXW7 axis is essential for maintaining mitochondrial integrity during mitophagy in T cells.

To further validate these mechanisms in authentic clinical samples, we evaluated primary CD4 + T cells isolated from the peripheral blood of SLE patients and HCs. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5a, miR-223-3p expression was significantly lower in primary CD4 + T cells from SLE patients than in HCs. Concurrently, its target protein, FBXW7, was aberrantly upregulated, accompanied by significant alterations in key mitophagy-related proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5b). These results directly confirm at the primary cell level that miR-223-3p deficiency and the consequent dysregulation of the FBXW7/mitophagy axis are critical features of SLE pathogenesis.

Therapeutic delivery of miR-223-3p via exosomes alleviates LN in NZBWF1/J mice

Given the crucial role of miR-223-3p in maintaining T cell homeostasis in vitro, this study aimed to investigate whether upregulation of miR-223-3p could confer therapeutic benefits in SLE in vivo. Exosomes were isolated from LV-miR-223 Jurkat cells (Exo-miR-223-3p) or LV-NC (Exo-NC) (Fig. 7a). Approximately 1 × 1010 particles of exosomes were administered via weekly tail vein injection until the urinary protein level reached 1 g/L, which occurred at week 26 in our experimental timeline (Fig. 7b; Supplementary Fig. 6). Initially, each group comprised five mice; however, by week 31, one mouse died in the Exo-miR-223-3p group, whereas two mice in the Exo-NC group died during the 32nd and 34th weeks. To assess the longitudinal therapeutic efficacy of Exo-miR-223-3p while accounting for animal attrition and missing data, we used an LMM. The Type III Tests of Fixed Effects demonstrated highly significant interaction effects between treatment group and observation time for both body weight (F = 7.481, P < 0.0001) and proteinuria (F = 4.208, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 7c and d). These statistical results indicate that the treatment and control groups exhibited significantly divergent trajectories over the course of the study. Specifically, mice treated with Exo-miR-223-3p showed a stabilized weight profile and a significantly attenuated progression of proteinuria compared to the control group. Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed a trend toward improved survival in the treatment group, although the difference was not statistically significant (χ2 = 0.2645, P = 0.6071), likely due to the limited cohort size (Supplementary Fig. 7). The kidneys, spleens, and blood were harvested from all mice one week after the final injection at week 35. Both groups had similar spleen sizes (Supplementary Fig. 8). Serum levels of IgG and ANA were comparable between groups whereas serum Anti-dsDNA titers were reduced in the Exo-miR-223-3p-treated mice. Furthermore, this group showed lower serum IFN-γ and IL-6 levels. Consistent with the improvement in proteinuria, the Exo-miR-223-3p group exhibited lower creatinine (P = 0.0550) and blood urea nitrogen (P = 0.0975) compared to the control group. These results indicate a promising, although not yet statistically significant, trend toward the preservation of renal function in SLE mice following treatment (Fig. 7e).

H&E staining of kidney sections demonstrated pathological alterations in the Exo-NC group, including glomerular enlargement, mesangial expansion accompanied by increased matrix deposition, collapse of capillary loops, and adhesion to the glomerular capsule. Although inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in both groups, it was notably attenuated in mice treated with Exo-miR-223-3p (Fig. 7g). PAS staining further demonstrated mesangial expansion and capillary wall thickening in the Exo-NC group, which obscured the normal glomerular architecture. These pathological features were substantially alleviated by Exo-miR-223-3p treatment (Fig. 7i). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining for C3 and IgG revealed markedly brighter deposition in the glomeruli of the Exo-NC group compared to the Exo-miR-223-3p-treated group, suggesting that miR-223-3p-enriched exosomes reduced immune complex deposition (Fig. 7h and j) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Immunofluorescence staining of the harvested renal sections clearly demonstrated the presence of PKH67-labeled exosomes (green fluorescence) accumulating in the critical glomerular and tubulointerstitial regions (Supplementary Fig. 10). This confirms the effective anatomical targeting and deep tissue penetration of the engineered exosomes into nephritic lesions. These findings indicate that upregulating miR-223-3p via exosome delivery represents a promising therapeutic approach for SLE, though further validation is required.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized the landscape of CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs in SLE and identified miR-223-3p as a CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal biomarker with functional and therapeutic relevance. We demonstrated that CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p is dysregulated in SLE, modulates inflammatory cytokine production and apoptosis in T cells, regulates B cell activation through intercellular communication, and mechanistically promotes mitophagy via direct targeting of FBXW7. Importantly, exosome-mediated delivery of miR-223-3p ameliorated LN and improved disease indices in NZBWF1/J mice. Together, these findings highlight the potential of CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p as both a biomarker and therapeutic candidate for SLE.

Currently, studies on the association between exosomal miRNAs and SLE have primarily focused on plasma or other humoral sources. However, the biological properties and functional relevance of exosomes are intrinsically linked to their cellular origin, yet studies specifically addressing immune cell-derived exosomal miRNAs remain limited. Our study systematically profiled the landscape of CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs and identified decreased Exo-miR‑223‑3p expression among SLE patients. While circulating [22] and fecal [23] miR-223-3p have previously been proposed as promising diagnostic biomarkers for SLE, these measurements were derived from heterogeneous sources and lacked cellular specificity. In contrast, our findings provide a cell-origin-defined biomarker with enhanced immunological relevance, thereby offering improved diagnostic precision and mechanistic insight into T cell-mediated immune dysregulation in SLE. Although CD4 + T cell-derived and plasma-derived Exo-miR‑223‑3p exhibited opposite expression trends, they showed similar diagnostic efficacy. We acknowledge that the diagnostic performance of CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p as a standalone marker is moderate. Therefore, we propose Exo-miR-223-3p as a potential auxiliary biomarker that may complement existing clinical indicators. Notably, adding Exo-miR-223-3p to a panel of conventional markers significantly improved diagnostic accuracy, suggesting its value in a multi-marker panel approach. Contrary to the prior report [22], we found no differences in plasma-derived miR‑223‑3p levels between patients with SLE and HCs. This underscores the importance of explicitly identifying the cellular origin of the exosomal source. Subgroup analysis further revealed that only CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p was differentially expressed between Anti‑Sm antibody‑positive and negative patients, whereas plasma-derived Exo-miR-223-3p showed no association with any clinical manifestations or serological indices. After applying FDR correction, the associations with Anti-Sm and Anti-rRNP no longer reached statistical significance. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in independent cohorts before firm conclusions can be drawn. Consistently, correlation analysis indicated that CD4 + T cell‑derived Exo-miR-223-3p exerted a superior performance in correlation with serum C3 and C4 levels than its plasma counterpart, with lower expression levels of CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR‑223‑3p corresponding to decreased C3 and C4 levels. These findings underscore the need to define the cellular origin of exosomal miRNAs. Analysis of bulk plasma exosomes, which comprise a heterogeneous mixture originating from various cellular sources, may potentially obscure disease-specific pathogenic signals that are detectable only within immune cell-derived exosomes. This concept is supported by emerging evidence from other diseases. A study on chronic kidney disease (CKD) demonstrated that circulating miR-186-5p associated with proteinuria in focal segmental glomerulosclerosis predominantly originates from activated CD8 + T cell-derived exosomes [24]. Pawluczyk et al. reported that Exo-miR‑483‑5p contributes to renal functional decline in IgA nephropathy [25]. In our study, CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p showed exclusive correlations with key cytokines involved in T and B cell activation, whereas plasma-derived Exo-miR-223-3p displayed no such associations. Collectively, these findings reinforce the opinion that CD4 + T cell-derived exosomal miRNAs exhibit a more specific and immunologically relevant association with SLE than those from bulk plasma.

In addition to serving as disease-associated biomarkers, exosomal miRNAs have been increasingly recognized as active regulators of immune cell function [26]. Emerging studies indicate that exosomes may constitute an underrecognized component of the immune system, enabling rapid systemic amplification of immune signals without the requirement for cellular proximity or direct cell-to-cell interactions. Immune cell-derived exosomes promote immunity against compromised cells, highlighting a novel mechanism in the immune response [7]. CD4 + helper T cells coordinate adaptive immune responses by providing co-stimulatory signals and cytokines. In lupus, infiltration by activated CD4 + T cells is observed in affected tissues, including the kidneys and skin, where they directly participate in local inflammation and tissue injury [27, 28]. In this context, our study demonstrated that miR-223-3p overexpression attenuated proinflammatory cytokine secretion and reduced apoptotic capacity, supporting a functional role for this miRNA in regulating T cell inflammatory responses. The immunoregulatory role of miR-223-3p is not limited to SLE but extends to diverse pathological conditions, underscoring its context- and cell-type-dependent functions. Inhibition of miR-223-3p restores Klotho expression and mitigates cellular senescence in CKD patients [29]. Tumor-derived Exo-miR-223-3p promotes M2 macrophage polarization, leading to IL-17 secretion, which enhances colon cancer cell proliferation and migration [30]. miR-223-3p, enriched in apoptotic bodies derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSC), inhibits osteoclast differentiation and reduces alveolar bone resorption [31]. These findings highlight miR-223-3p as a versatile regulatory miRNA whose biological effects are significantly contingent upon cellular origin and disease context.

T cells play a fundamental role in SLE by driving systemic autoimmunity and end-organ damage. After immune tolerance breaks down, reciprocal T and B cell interactions perpetuate inflammation. Specifically, T cell help to B cells is critical for generating high-titer pathogenic autoantibodies [32]. Our findings support the concept that CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR-223-3p regulates T-B cell communication. Jurkat overexpression of miR‑223‑3p altered BAFF and CD19 levels in B cells, and GW4869 inhibition abolished this effect, confirming an exosome‑dependent mechanism. While Jurkat and Raji cell lines provide a stable and controllable system for mechanistic dissection, their malignant nature may not fully recapitulate the immune exhaustion or specific signaling rewiring found in primary SLE T and B cells. These findings provide a mechanistic framework that warrants further validation in primary patient-derived co-culture systems.

In general, miRNAs exert their function by base-pairing with target mRNAs to mediate post-transcriptional regulation [33, 34]. Our integrated methodology has confirmed miR‑223‑3p directly targets FBXW7, leading to its downregulation. Reduced FBXW7 expression relieves its inhibitory effect on mitophagy, thereby enhancing autophagic flux. This mitophagy enhancement reduces inflammatory cytokine production and decreases T cell apoptosis. Rescue experiments confirmed that FBXW7 restoration reverses the effects, establishing FBXW7 as the key mediator of miR-223-3p’s functions in this context. The regulatory significance of the miR‑223‑3p/FBXW7 axis is further supported across diverse pathological contexts. In diabetic retinopathy, an hnRNPA2B1-targeting peptide exerts therapeutic effects by modulating the Notch1/Akt/mTOR and STAT4/miR-223-3p/FBXW7 pathways under hyperglycemic conditions [35]. Similarly, the lncRNA GAS5 alleviates intestinal barrier dysfunction induced by sepsis by modulating the miR-223-3p/FBXW7 pathway and inhibiting nuclear factor kappa-B (NF‑κB) signaling [36]. Also, in dry eye disease, MSC‑derived Exo-miR‑223‑3p mitigates ocular surface damage and inflammation by downregulating FBXW7 [37]. This evidence suggests a conserved regulatory module through which miR-223-3p regulates cellular homeostasis. FBXW7 functions as the substrate recognition module within the Skp1-Cul1-F-box (SCF) ubiquitin ligase E3 complex [38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that FBXW7 is involved in mitophagy. TUG1 inhibits mitochondrial autophagy via the FBXW7/SIRT1 axis, consequently augmenting neuronal apoptosis induced by oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion [39]. FBXW7 is involved in the adipogenesis process of sterol regulatory element-binding protein by facilitating mitochondrial translocation and modulating mitophagy [40, 41].

Our findings further demonstrated that overexpression of miR‑223‑3p in Jurkat cells inhibits FBXW7 expression, thereby promoting mitophagy in Jurkat cells. In the pathophysiology of lupus, mitochondrial dysfunction in T cells is a pivotal factor contributing to abnormal T cell activation and necrosis [42, 43]. The resulting necrotic debris contributes to autoantibody production, inflammation, and organ injury [44]. Increased biogenesis and defective mitophagy are among the key mechanisms driving these mitochondrial abnormalities [45]. Mitophagy tends to be suppressed in T cells from SLE patients and lupus mice, a phenotype associated with the overexpression of the small GTPase RAB4A [46]. This aligns with earlier reports of autophagy resistance in lupus T cells, reflected in diminished autophagic flux upon stimulation [47]. Within this context, our observation that miR-223-3p enhances mitophagy in Jurkat cells supports a model in which the miR-223-3p/FBXW7 axis operates as a positive regulator of mitochondrial clearance, thereby contributing to the restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis and normal T-cell function in SLE.

Exosomal miRNAs have attracted increasing attention as therapeutic agents and delivery vehicles for inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [48–50]. In a mouse model of collagen-induced arthritis, intraperitoneal injection of Exo-miR-192-5p reduced systemic inflammation and alleviated joint damage [51]. Similarly, Exo-miR-181c derived from human umbilical cord blood MSCs can suppress the expression of NF-κB/p65 and TLR4, thereby diminishing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and mitigating inflammatory damage [52]. In alignment with these findings, we demonstrated the therapeutic potential of CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR‑223‑3p in a murine SLE model. Exo‑miR‑223‑3p administration improved physiological outcomes, including the preservation of body weight and a reduction in proteinuria. Additionally, it markedly reduced serum levels of Anti‑dsDNA and IL‑6. Renal histopathology demonstrated notable improvement, characterized by a decrease in glomerular inflammation and a reduction in the deposition of IgG and C3 complement. These findings collectively underscore the significant therapeutic potential of exosomal miRNA‑based interventions, with Exo-miR‑223‑3p emerging as a promising candidate for SLE treatment.

This study presents several limitations. First, the discovery cohort was small and consisted exclusively of females, which may limit generalizability. Future studies in male SLE patients and larger discovery cohorts are warranted. The enrolled SLE cohort primarily comprised patients with moderate disease activity, thereby lacking representation from individuals with minimal or highly active disease. This homogeneity constrains the generalizability of Exo-miR‑223‑3p as a biomarker across the entire disease spectrum, and its diagnostic sensitivity and specificity require validation in a larger SLE population with diverse clinical features. Second, although we identified that miR-223-3p regulates mitophagy by targeting FBXW7, the precise downstream molecular pathways of FBXW7 in SLE CD4 + T cells and their causal links to the observed inflammatory and apoptotic phenotypes remain to be elucidated. Third, our work did not determine whether the observed effects of miR‑223‑3p are specific to its presence in CD4 + T cell-derived exosomes as opposed to other vesicular or non-vesicular forms. Finally, following systemic administration in the murine model, we did not assess the in vivo homing efficiency of the administered exosomes to specific target tissues or their potential off-target effects, which are critical considerations for therapeutic development.

In summary, this study identifies CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR‑223‑3p as a biologically and clinically relevant mediator in the pathogenesis of SLE, highlighting the importance of cellular origin in defining the functional and diagnostic value of exosomal miRNAs. We demonstrate that this exosomal miRNA regulates T cell function and pathogenic T‑B cell communication through the FBXW7/mitophagy axis. Collectively, these findings position CD4 + T cell-derived Exo-miR‑223‑3p as a pivotal regulator of immune dysfunction with potential as a diagnostic biomarker and a promising candidate for targeted therapy in SLE.
