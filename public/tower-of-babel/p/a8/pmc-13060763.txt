Weight loss and metabolic improvements dominate the microbiome response in the MIND diet intervention: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Robin M. Voigt, Adit Chaudhary, Ankur Naqib, Phillip A. Engen, Darbaz Adnan, Klodian Dhana, Stefan J. Green, Michelle Villanueva, Puja Agarwal, Lisa L. Barnes, Frank Sacks, Ali Keshavarzian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONObservational studies link the MIND diet to reduced risk of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and slower cognitive decline. However, a recent randomized controlled trial found no differential cognitive benefit of the MIND diet over a control diet in the context of shared caloric restriction. Given that both groups achieved significant weight loss and metabolic improvements, this study aimed to disentangle the impact of the MIND diet and host metabolic improvements on the intestinal microbiome.METHODSA subset of participants (n = 213) from the MIND trial were analyzed in this study. Clinical data and stool samples were collected at baseline, Year 1, Year 2, and Year 3, and longitudinal changes in microbiome composition were assessed via shotgun metagenomics.RESULTSBoth groups exhibited significant, transient microbiome remodeling at Year 1 (the period of most active weight loss). The control group demonstrated a broad range of altered metabolic pathways, whereas the MIND diet group showed only one, suggesting a functional buffering effect of the MIND diet. Prospective modeling independent of diet group revealed that a poorer cognitive trajectory was significantly associated with increased inositol degradation (PWY‐7237) and purine nucleotide salvage (PWY66‐409); conversely, a better cognitive trajectory was associated with increased degradation of deoxy sugars (FUC‐RHAMCAT‐PWY).DISCUSSIONCaloric restriction, weight loss, and host metabolic improvement are the dominant factors shaping the intestinal microbiome, overshadowing diet‐specific taxonomic shifts. The MIND diet appeared to provide a modest stabilizing effect on the microbial functional profile against perturbations during active weight loss; however, these dietary associations did not persist in covariate‐adjusted models, suggesting that host metabolic improvements remained the primary driver of functional shifts.

BACKGROUND

The relationship between diet and brain health has emerged as an area of significant interest in neurodegenerative research. The Mediterranean‐Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet, a hybrid of the Mediterranean and DASH patterns, has garnered significant attention due to its observational associations with a reduced risk of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and slower rates of cognitive decline. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 By emphasizing consumption of neuroprotective foods such as green leafy vegetables, berries, nuts, olive oil, legumes/beans, and whole grains, while limiting red and processed meat, fatty foods, and sweets, the MIND diet is hypothesized to provide a unique nutritional profile that supports healthy brain aging. To rigorously evaluate this impact, the 3‐year randomized controlled MIND trial was conducted in cognitively unimpaired older adults with AD risk factors.

Unexpectedly, the MIND trial demonstrated that while global cognitive function improved significantly over time in both the MIND and Control groups, there was no impact of the MIND diet on cognition. 6 This clinical trajectory was mirrored by significant weight loss and metabolic improvements in both groups, driven by a shared goal of mild caloric restriction. 6 Because general metabolic health and weight reduction are well‐established to have positive effects on brain health and cognition, 7 , 8 , 9 the parallel trajectories of these cohorts present a unique opportunity to isolate the relative contributions of host metabolic state versus dietary composition on the intestinal microbiome.

The intestinal microbiome is a core regulator of host metabolism and is highly sensitive to both caloric intake and nutrient quality. 10 Given that the microbiota–gut–brain axis is increasingly recognized as a key mediator in neurodegenerative disease, understanding how dietary interventions reshape this community in the context of weight loss is essential. This study hypothesized that the MIND diet would induce unique, beneficial adaptations in the intestinal microbiome. 11 , 12 Additionally, by analyzing shotgun metagenomic data across the 3‐year trial period, this study aimed to disentangle the impact of physiological change (i.e., weight loss, improved metabolic health) and dietary pattern on the intestinal microbiome.

METHODS

Methodological details are found in the Supporting Information Methods and the raw data in the Supporting Information Data.

Study design and participants

This study analyzed a subset of participants from the MIND trial, 6 a 3‐year, randomized controlled trial comparing the MIND diet to a control diet, both with mild caloric restriction. Participants were cognitively unimpaired adults aged 65 to 84 with a family history of AD, a suboptimal diet, and a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25. For this microbiome substudy, 213 participants were recruited from the Chicago site (110 MIND diet, 103 control diet). Therefore, this cohort represents a subset of the total MIND trial population and was powered to detect moderate to large effects rather than subtle microbial shifts. Demographic characteristics did not differ between groups (Table 1).

Data collection and sample processing

Cognitive performance (via a 12‐test battery yielding a global cognition score), 13 dietary adherence, 6 , 14 fasting serum, and stool samples were collected at baseline and annually for 3 years. Microbial DNA was isolated from stool samples and subjected to shotgun metagenomic sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Except for one sample, the samples had a median sequencing depth of 16.7 million reads/sample (range: 5.1 to 38 million reads/sample) (Supporting Information Data). Raw sequence data are available on the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (PRJNA1086951). Serum biomarkers, including amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (pTau181), were quantified using a Quanterix Simoa analyzer (details in the Supporting Information Methods).

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

Demographic comparisons were made using Student's t‐test, chi‐squared test, or Fisher's exact test. Changes in clinical variables were analyzed using a two‐way repeated measure analysis of variance (rmANOVA) in GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0).

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: Observational studies consistently associate higher adherence to the MIND diet with slower cognitive decline and reduced AD risk. However, the recent 3‐year randomized MIND trial demonstrated no differential cognitive benefit of the MIND diet compared with a control diet when both interventions incorporated mild caloric restriction and produced comparable weight loss and metabolic improvements. Prior studies have not disentangled diet‐specific effects from the dominant influence of metabolic improvement on the intestinal microbiome in this context. Interpretation: Leveraging the parallel clinical trajectories of the MIND and control arms, this longitudinal shotgun metagenomic analysis isolates the relative contributions of caloric restriction–associated metabolic improvement versus dietary composition on the intestinal microbiome. The data show that metabolic improvement is the primary driver of global microbiome remodeling, producing transient, reversible shifts in community structure without sustained changes in diversity. Within this framework, the MIND diet did not induce broad taxonomic restructuring but instead buffered microbial functional responses during active weight loss, as evidenced by markedly fewer perturbations in microbial metabolic pathways. These findings indicate that diet quality modulates microbial functional stability rather than overriding metabolically driven community dynamics. Future directions: This study identifies microbial functional resilience (not taxonomic change) as a distinguishing biological signature of the MIND diet, independent of short‐term cognitive outcomes. Future work should test whether preservation of microbial functional capacity during metabolic stress predicts long‐term cognitive trajectories, delayed neurodegeneration, or reduced biomarker progression. Microbiome‐derived functional features may ultimately serve as sensitive biomarkers for evaluating dietary interventions aimed at AD prevention, particularly in populations undergoing metabolic improvement.

After removing human reads and quality filtering, taxonomic and functional profiles were generated using MetaPhlAn4 and HUMAnN3, respectively. Changes in the abundance of taxa over time (i.e., Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes [F/B] ratio, short‐chain fatty acid [SCFA]‐producing taxa) were analyzed using a two‐way repeated measure analysis of variance (rmANOVA) in GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0). All other statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.2).

Alpha and beta diversity were calculated to assess community‐level changes. To identify microbial taxa and functional pathways associated with the MIND diet over time, linear mixed‐effects models using MaAsLin2 were implemented, with participant ID as a random effect. Models were adjusted for key demographic and clinical covariates that impacted the microbiome (detailed in Supporting Information Methods, e.g., age, education, diabetes, medication use, employment status), and p values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Given the high dimensionality of microbiome data, a false discovery rate (FDR) of q < 0.25 was selected to minimize Type II error (i.e., missing true biological signals), while acknowledging the increased risk of false positives. This is the default q value threshold recommended in the MaAsLin2 R package and is commonly applied to human microbiome analysis. 15 , 16 , 17 Exact p and q values are provided to allow readers to assess the confidence of the outcomes. All analysis code is publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/Aditchaudhary/MIND‐Metagenomics‐MaAsLin2.git).

To assess the relationship between clinical characteristics independent of diet group, a prospective cohort analysis was performed using mixed‐effects modeling. MaAsLin2 was used to regress diet (i.e., MIND score, fiber), metabolic changes (i.e., weight, waist circumference), and cognition (i.e., Z‐score) against microbiome features (i.e., taxa, pathways) across all timepoints. Participant ID was used as a random effect, and models were adjusted for key demographic and clinical covariates detailed in the Supporting Information Methods.

RESULTS

Concordant metabolic changes observed in both dietary groups

In the subset of MIND trial participants examined in this study, both the MIND and control groups demonstrated a significant increase in MIND diet scores over the 3‐year study period, but the MIND diet group consistently achieved significantly higher MIND diet scores at Year 1, Year 2, and Year 3 compared to the control group (Figure 1A). Both groups successfully achieved mild caloric restriction, which was reflected as reduced intake of fat, protein, and carbohydrate (Figure S1). While overall caloric intake was similar, the MIND diet group consumed significantly more vegetable protein and total, soluble, and insoluble fiber (Figure S1). The increase in fiber was uniform across fiber types (i.e., soluble and insoluble) rather than a specific enrichment in fermentable soluble fibers.

Both groups exhibited weight loss and reduced adiposity (Figure 1B,C). While both groups achieved comparable results by Year 3, a significant group‐by‐time interaction was observed (Figure 1B). These were accompanied by significant improvements in metabolic health markers in both groups, including blood pressure (Figure 1D), cholesterol profiles (triglycerides, HDL; Figure 1E,F), and inflammation markers (C‐reactive protein [CRP], adiponectin) (Table S1).

Cognitive function improved significantly over time in both groups; however, the MIND diet did not provide a differential benefit over the control diet (Figure 1G, Table S2). Similarly, the MIND diet did not impact the AD biomarkers assessed in this study (i.e., Aβ40, Aβ42, pTau181) (Table S2). This finding, while unexpected, presents a unique opportunity to explore the underlying biological mechanisms, particularly how weight loss and metabolic improvement in the presence or absence of the MIND diet influence the intestinal microbiome.

Distinct, transient remodeling of intestinal microbiome

At baseline, the overall composition of the intestinal microbiome was indistinguishable between control and MIND diet groups (species‐level PERMANOVA: p = 0.95, PERMDISP: p = 0.67, Figure S2). Similarly, baseline assessments of alpha‐diversity indices, which reflect the within‐sample richness of microbial species, showed no significant differences between groups (i.e., Shannon entropy, Simpson Index, Pielou evenness, or observed features) (Table S3).

At Year 1, significant transient shifts in the intestinal microbiome were observed in both the MIND diet and control groups compared to baseline (PERMANOVA: control: q < 0.02, MIND: q < 0.01). However, no other significant differences in the overall community structure (i.e., beta diversity) were noted within or between groups across the subsequent 3 years of the study (Figure 2, Figure S3). Alpha‐diversity indices largely remained stable over time in both groups (Table S3).

The transient nature of microbiome changes was apparent in examinations of specific bacterial groups at both the phylum and species taxonomic levels. The F/B ratio significantly decreased in both groups at Year 1 (Figure S4A), and the relative abundance of putative SCFA‐producing species was perturbed at Year 1. Specifically, the relative abundance of acetate‐producing species increased, while the relative abundance of putative butyrate‐producing species decreased (Figure S4B,C). No significant differences were detected in the relative abundance of propionate‐producing bacteria (Figure S4D). The specific bacterial taxa used for the inferred SCFA‐production analyses are detailed in the Supporting Information Data. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22

Subtle taxonomic and metabolic pathway alterations based on diet

Utilizing stringent significance criteria (MaAsLin 2, q < 0.25) revealed subtle microbial taxonomic shifts within each group that were largely transient. In the control group, the relative abundance of three species was significantly altered at Year 1: Blautia wexlerae and Candidatus Cibiobacter qucibialis were both decreased, while Parabacteroides merdae was increased (Figure 3A–D). The MIND diet group also showed significant changes in the relative abundance of three species: Ruthenibacterium lactatiformans decreased in Year 1, Alistipes putredinis increased in Year 1, and Lachnospira eligens increased in both Years 1 and 2 (Figure 3A,E–G). It is important to note that similar trends were observed in the relative abundance of these species in both the control and MIND diet groups, with the magnitude of the changes dictating significance. Analysis with less stringent criteria (i.e., p < 0.05) revealed additional taxonomic shifts (Table S4).

Metagenomic function pathway analysis using stringent significance criteria (MaAsLin 2, q < 0.25) identified 15 metabolic pathways significantly altered in the control diet and only one in the MIND diet group (Figure 4A). One pathway, amino acid catabolism (PWY‐5130: 2‐oxobutanoate degradation I), was significantly upregulated in both the control and MIND diet groups at Year 1 compared to baseline (Figure 4A,B). Additional metabolic alterations were observed exclusively in the control group. Specifically, pathways involved in microbial biosynthesis were significantly upregulated, including those for biotin (BIOTIN‐BIOSYNTHESIS‐PWY, PWY‐6519), fatty acid elongation (FASYN‐ELONG‐PWY, PWY‐5989, PWY‐6282, PWY‐7664, PWY0‐862), and formaldehyde assimilation (PWY‐1861). In contrast, the relative abundance of several pathways related to carbohydrate degradation and fermentation was significantly decreased in the control group. These included pathways for the catabolism of complex polysaccharides (GLCMANNANAUT‐PWY), simple sugars (LACTOSECAT‐PWY), and carbohydrate‐derived alcohols (PWY‐7013). Furthermore, multiple fermentation‐related pathways were also suppressed (FERMENTATION‐PWY, PWY‐5100, P41‐PWY). Utilization of less stringent significance criteria (i.e., MaAsLin, p < 0.02) identified more impacted pathways in both the control and MIND diet groups (Table S5).

To account for other factors influencing the microbiome, a redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed. 23 , 24 This analysis identified several significant clinical variables, including age, employment status (retired, part‐time, full‐time), diabetes (or suspected diabetes), waist circumference, and regular use of medications including biologics, non‐steroidal ant‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), opioids, PPI/H2 blockers, steroids, and anti‐inflammatory medications as factors that significantly impacted the intestinal microbiome in this study (Figure S5). Considering these factors as covariates largely confirmed the significant species previously identified in both groups; however, one additional species Clostridium phoceensis (decreased at Year 1) was identified in the control group, and Ruthenibacterium lactatiformans was no longer significant in the MIND group in this analysis. No pathways were significantly impacted in either group when considering these covariates.

Group assignment agnostic analysis

To identify the most robust microbial responses independent of diet assignment, a group agnostic analysis was performed on the combined cohort (i.e., Control + MIND, Table S6) exclusively at Year 1, corresponding to the period of most active weight loss and metabolic improvement. Taxonomic changes at Year 1 were characterized by increased abundance of Lachnospira eligens, Parabacteroides merdae, and Alistipes putredinis alongside a concurrent decrease in the abundance of Blautia wexlerae. At the pathway level, Year 1 was marked by the broad upregulation of biosynthetic and degradative pathways, including 2‐oxobutanoate degradation I, L‐citrulline biosynthesis, and several fatty acid biosynthesis processes (e.g., stearate and palmitoleate biosynthesis), while pathways for complex carbohydrate degradation, such as the superpathway of N‐acetylglucosamine degradation, were downregulated (Table S6).

Continuous modeling of the entire cohort (i.e., Control and MIND combined, n = 213 participants) revealed links between specific microbiome features and host health trajectories over 3 years (total observations n = 638, Table 2). Changes in host adiposity were coupled with shifts in fermentation potential. Specifically, waist circumference was positively associated with the abundance of Streptococcus parasanguinis and pathways for heterolactic fermentation (P122‐PWY) and (5Z)‐dodecenoate biosynthesis II (PWY‐7858). Waist circumference is also negatively associated with the abundance of Enterocloster bolteae and pyruvate fermentation to butanoate (CENTFERM‐PWY). Weight loss was negatively associated with pyruvate fermentation to acetone (PWY‐6588). Diet quality and fiber availability also modulated microbial features. A greater increase in MIND diet score was associated with reduced abundance of Ruminococcus torques, while total dietary fiber was associated with reduced abundance of Barnesiella intestinihominis. Soluble fiber was negatively associated with the NAD salvage pathway III (NAD Biosynthesis II) and positively associated with putrescine biosynthesis (PWY‐6305). While no taxon tracked with cognitive changes, Z‐score change was positively associated with fucose and rhamnose degradation (FUC‐RHAMCAT‐PWY) and negatively with purine nucleotide salvage (PWY66‐409) and myo‐, chiro‐, and scyllo‐inositol degradation (PWY‐7237).

DISCUSSION

In this 3‐year longitudinal analysis of a subset of participants enrolled in the MIND diet trial, both the MIND diet and control groups (each incorporating mild caloric restriction) achieved sustained weight loss and improvements in metabolic health. Cognitive performance improved across the cohort, but the MIND diet did not differ from the control group for cognition or AD biomarkers (AB40, AB42, pTau181). The parallel clinical trajectories in control and MIND diet groups provided a valuable experimental context in which host metabolic improvement was effectively held constant, providing an experimental context in which the relative contributions of metabolic improvement and dietary composition to microbiome change could be evaluated.

Host metabolic improvement is dominant driver of microbiome remodeling

Across both groups, host metabolic state emerged as the primary determinant of global microbiome structure. Significant shifts in beta diversity were observed at Year 1, coinciding with the active phase of weight loss, while alpha diversity remained largely unchanged. This pattern is consistent with taxonomic turnover (i.e., replacement of metabolically responsive taxa by functionally similar or niche competing organisms) rather than a change in overall community richness. Similar patterns have been reported in response to weight loss, bariatric surgery, and caloric restriction, where metabolic perturbation induces transient restructuring without changes in diversity. 25 , 26 , 27

By Year 3, microbial community composition largely returned to baseline despite sustained weight loss, suggesting a transient period of microbiome remodeling followed by establishment of a new homeostatic equilibrium. The consistency of this pattern across both groups supports the conclusion that metabolic improvement (rather than dietary composition) was the dominant factor shaping global microbiome structure.

Shared metabolic adaptations further reinforced this conclusion. Both groups exhibited a reduced F/B ratio at Year 1, a feature frequently associated with improved metabolic health and reduced adiposity. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 This shift was accompanied by altered fermentation potential, including increased acetate‐producing and decreased putative butyrate‐producing taxa. 27 , 33 , 34 While reductions in butyrate‐producing taxa are often interpreted as unfavorable, similar patterns have been reported during caloric restriction and may reflect substrate limitation or cohort‐specific baseline microbiome characteristics rather than pathological change. 35 , 36 The shared upregulation of 2‐oxobutanoate degradation (PWY‐5130) in both groups likely reflects increased reliance on amino acid metabolism during caloric restriction, independent of diet composition.

The sustained increase in Lachnospira eligens in the MIND group is notable given its role in fiber fermentation and SCFA production, with emerging links to cardiometabolic and neuroprotective outcomes. 37 , 38 However, the modest effect size and its presence in group‐agnostic models suggest this signal reflects an interaction between improved metabolic state and enhanced substrate availability, rather than a diet‐exclusive restructuring of the microbiome. Future evaluations into the role of Lachnospira eligens are needed to understand the relevance of this observation.

Continuous modeling further supported metabolic health as a key driver of microbiome features. Positive associations between waist circumference and heterolactic fermentation (P122‐PWY) and fatty acid biosynthesis (PWY‐7858) suggest that higher adiposity favors microbes with higher energy‐harvesting and lipid‐producing capabilities. 39 Conversely, negative associations with central fermentation pathways (CENTFERM‐PWY, PWY‐6588) indicate that metabolic improvement is accompanied by a shift toward canonical anaerobic metabolism. Although individual taxonomic coefficients (i.e., Streptococcus parasanguinis, Enterocloster bolteae) were modest, their persistence after stringent correction supports their interpretation as sentinel markers of host metabolic state.

Notably, these conclusions were consistent after adjustment for measured metabolic covariates, reinforcing that host physiology, rather than diet assignment alone, accounted for the majority of structural variation observed.

Diet quality modestly modulates microbial stress responses during weight loss

While metabolic improvement anchored community‐level restructuring, diet quality modulated how the microbiome adapted to this transition. Rather than inducing broad microbial community restructuring, the MIND diet was associated with comparatively fewer functional perturbations during the active phase of caloric restriction in unadjusted analyses.

In the control group, reductions in SCFA‐producing taxa (i.e., Blautia wexlerae, Candidatus Cibiobacter qucibialis) and enrichment of Parabacteroides merdae are consistent with reduced intake of fermentable fibers, healthy fats, and micronutrients emphasized by the MIND diet. These shifts align with prior work linking such taxa to metabolic and cognitive resilience. 40 , 41 , 42 Functionally, the control group exhibited widespread perturbations at Year 1, including upregulation of biotin biosynthesis (PWY‐6519), multiple fatty acid biosynthesis and elongation pathways (PWY‐5989, PWY‐6282, PWY‐7664, PWY0‐862), and concurrent suppression of fermentation pathways (P41‐PWY, PWY‐5100). Collectively, these changes are consistent with a compensatory microbial response to reduced availability of dietary substrates and micronutrients during caloric restriction.

In contrast, the MIND diet group demonstrated relative stability in microbial features profiles during the metabolic transition. Given that the MIND diet emphasizes intake of biotin‐rich foods (e.g., legumes, nuts, seeds, whole grains), healthy fats (e.g., olive oil, nuts, fatty fish), and fiber‐rich plant foods (e.g., green leafy vegetables, berries, legumes), the absence of compensatory upregulation in microbial biosynthetic pathways suggests that sufficient exogenous substrates were available to meet microbial metabolic demands. These findings suggest that diet quality may influence the pattern of microbial functional adaptation during caloric restriction. However, it is important to note that these diet‐specific pathway associations did not persist in covariate‐adjusted models incorporating host metabolic variables. This attenuation indicates that the apparent functional stabilization observed in unadjusted analyses was not independent of metabolic improvement.

Diet‐related modulation was also evident in specific taxa and pathways linked to intestinal barrier function. The negative association between MIND diet score and Ruminococcus torques (a mucin‐degrading taxon linked to barrier dysfunction and inflammation) suggests that increased availability of complex dietary fibers reduced reliance on host‐derived mucins as an energy source. In parallel, soluble fiber intake was positively associated with increased microbial putrescine biosynthesis (PWY‐6305). Polyamines such as putrescine play a critical role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity through stabilization of tight junction proteins and modulation of immune responses, providing a plausible mechanistic link between MIND diet‐associated fiber intake and preservation of the intestinal barrier. 43 , 44

Notably, these effects occurred despite total fiber intake remaining below recommended levels. The negative association between total fiber intake and Barnesiella intestinihominis (a metabolically flexible generalist) likely reflects niche competition, whereby expansion of fiber‐responsive primary degraders (e.g., Lachnospira eligens) limits resources available for generalist taxa. Together, these patterns support a “fiber gap” hypothesis: Although diet quality improved, absolute fiber intake may have remained below the threshold required to fully sustain fermentative capacity in older adults.

Microbial functional stability tracks with cognitive trajectories

Microbial functional capacity (rather than the abundance of specific taxa) emerged as the most cognitively relevant signal with cognition in this study. Cohort‐wide modeling identified microbial pathways associated with longitudinal cognitive trajectories. These associations should be interpreted as mechanistic and associative, not as evidence of cognitive benefit attributable to the MIND diet intervention.

Better cognitive performance over time was associated with increased microbial capacity for degradation of fucose‐ and rhamnose‐containing carbohydrates (FUC‐RHAMCAT‐PWY), substrates derived from both complex plant glycans and the intestinal mucus layer. Utilization of these substrates likely reflects preservation of a specialized mucus–microbiome niche that supports barrier integrity and immune homeostasis. 45 , 46 In older adults (who commonly experience age‐related barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation), maintenance of this mucus–microbiome interface may be particularly important for limiting systemic inflammation and its downstream effects on brain health. 47 , 48

Conversely, poorer cognitive trajectories were associated with increased microbial capacity for myo‐, chiro‐, and scyllo‐inositol degradation (PWY‐7237) and purine nucleotide salvage (PWY66‐409). Inositol is critical for host cellular signaling and is often derived from plant‐based fibers (emphasized in the MIND diet), and increased microbial degradation of these compounds might signify a metabolically strained environment. In alignment with this interpretation, increased purine salvage may reflect microbial stress or altered growth dynamics, consistent with a metabolically strained intestinal environment. 49

Importantly, no individual bacterial taxa were significantly associated with cognitive outcomes after correction for multiple testing. This absence of taxonomic signals underscores a central finding of this study: What the microbiome is doing functionally is a more sensitive and cognitively relevant readout than the specific taxa that are present. This function‐focused paradigm aligns with emerging microbiome literature in aging and neurodegeneration. 50

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Stool samples reflect recent dietary exposure, and collection time was not standardized, introducing potential variability. While sequencing depth was sufficient for taxonomic‐ and pathway‐level analyses, detection of low‐abundance gene families may have been limited. Next, this analysis represents a subset of the MIND trial cohort (potentially limiting generalizability), and unmeasured environmental factors (including disruptions related to the COVID‐19 pandemic during Year 3) may have influenced microbial dynamics. Future studies incorporating higher frequency sampling, direct metabolomic profiling, and longer follow‐up are essential to determine whether sustained metabolic improvement and dietary buffering of microbial function ultimately translate into durable cognitive or biomarker effects.

Implications for dietary interventions in cognitive aging

Collectively, these findings support a model in which host metabolic improvement is the dominant factor shaping the intestinal microbiome, while diet quality may shape patterns of microbial functional adaptation during metabolic transition, although these effects appear secondary to host‐driven physiological change. From a translational perspective, these results suggest that dietary strategies for cognitive aging may not require dramatic taxonomic remodeling of the microbiome. Instead, modest but sustained improvements in diet quality (particularly nutrient density and fermentable substrate availability) may represent a more achievable and clinically relevant target for supporting microbial functional capacity and intestinal health, which may be relevant to long‐term brain health.
