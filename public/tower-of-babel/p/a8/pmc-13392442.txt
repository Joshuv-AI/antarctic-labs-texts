BactProNET: a structural-mechanistic platform for interpreting target-mediated antimicrobial resistance
Authors: Ke Wang, Yiru Liu, Xue Wang, Haitao Zhou, Longfei Chen, Jingyun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotics are the frontline therapy for bacterial infections, yet their efficacy is critically threatened by antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a crisis largely driven by mutations in protein targets. While this mechanism is prevalent, data on its structural impact remains highly dispersed, and existing resources such as CARD, ResFinder, MEGARes, and NDARO prioritize gene identification over mechanistic insight. To address this gap, we developed BactProNET, a bioinformatics platform focused specifically on target-mediated resistance caused by amino acid substitutions in antibiotic target proteins. It provides structural and evolutionary analysis distinct from broader AMR databases. Its core innovation is a multi-level data integration that establishes a wild-type reference system and enables comparative analysis with mutant proteins to facilitate mechanistic inference. The platform integrates curated resistance mutations with AlphaFold2-predicted three-dimensional (3D) structures, both wild-type and mutant molecular docking models defining an “optimal binding” baseline and its potential disruption by resistance mutations, and integrated phylogenetic and sequence alignment views. BactProNET currently houses data on 44 bacterial species, 107 protein targets, 196 mutation sites (representing 243 unique amino acid substitutions), 323 antibiotics, and 640 wild-type plus 1,697 mutant docking models. The platform is accessible via an interface with embedded basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) and multiple sequence alignment (MSA) tools. As a one-stop platform for target-mediated resistance, it facilitates the interpretation of AMR’s molecular mechanisms and provides a data-driven foundation for the rational design of next-generation antimicrobial drugs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53667-1.Keywords: BactProNET, Antibiotic resistance, Database, Target protein, Target-mediated resistance, Amino acid mutation, Molecular dockingSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

Since the mid-20th century, the widespread use of antibiotics has dramatically reduced morbidity and mortality from bacterial infectious diseases1. However, with the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, bacterial resistance has become increasingly severe. This leads to a continuous decline in the effectiveness of conventional treatment regimens, posing a serious challenge to global public health2–4.

Among the diverse mechanisms of bacterial resistance, genetic mutation of the antibiotic’s protein target is one of the most direct and prevalent mechanism5,6. For instance, mutations in Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs) can confer resistance to β-lactam antibiotics7, while mutations in DNA gyrase or topoisomerase IV are a well-established cause of resistance to fluoroquinolones8. Such alterations of key amino acid residues can impair antibiotic efficacy by reducing drug-binding affinity, inducing conformational changes in the target protein, or creating steric hindrance that obstructs drug binding5,8. Consequently, a systematic investigation of these mutations at the sequence, structural, and functional levels of the protein target is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of antibiotic resistance.

While a wealth of research has reported on resistance mutations, this information remains highly dispersed. Existing databases serve distinct but partial roles. CARD9 and ResFinder10 excel at resistance gene identification, while MEGARes11 and NDARO12 provide board, sequence-based AMR detection across diverse mechanisms (e.g., efflux pumps, enzymatic inactivation). Structural repositories such as PDB13 and UniProt14 offer protein structures and annotations but lack systematic integration with resistance mutations. However, none of these resources systematically integrate protein structure, drug interactions, evolutionary context, and direct comparison between wild-type and mutant drug binding. This integrated perspective is essential for the mechanistic elucidation of target-mediated resistance (see Supplementary Table S1 for a detailed comparison). This forces researcher investigating specific mechanisms to undertake the tedious task of manually retrieving and integrating data from disparate resources such as the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI)15, UniProt, PDB, and the primary literature.

To address this bottleneck, we developed BactProNET, a bioinformatics platform for the structural and evolutionary analysis of target-mediated resistance resulting from amino acid substitutions in antibiotic target proteins. The current version of the platform focuses on this specific resistance mechanism and does not cover other AMR mechanisms such as drug efflux, enzymatic degradation, or permeability changes. This focused scope is intended to enable detailed structural analysis of how individual mutations may affect drug binding, an aspect that is largely absent from existing resources. Unlike platforms that focus on gene identification, BactProNET aims to support mechanistic interpretation by allowing users to explore how a specific amino acid substitution might impair drug binding. To achieve this, the platform integrates mutation sites with predicted 3D structures and comparative molecular docking of both wild-type and mutant proteins. It provides a “wild-type-drug optimal binding model” as an analytical baseline, allowing direct comparison with mutant binding modes, a feature not available in other databases. BactProNET is therefore not a substitute for existing gene catalogs but a complementary resource intended to assist structure-based hypothesis generation for target-mediated resistance.

Materials and methods

Data collection and integration

To ensure data accuracy and comprehensiveness, the BactProNET dataset was constructed through a dual strategy of manual literature curation and public database integration.

Literature curation of resistance mutations

A team of three independent curators screened 3,247 articles from PubMed using a search template of ([Protein Name[Title/Abstract]]) AND (resistance[Title/Abstract]) AND (mutation[Title/Abstract]). After deduplication using EndNote X9, two-phase screening (title/abstract and full-text) was performed against predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria (detailed in Supplementary Methods). This process yielded 330 articles for data extraction. Curators manually extracted bacterial species, protein targets, amino acid mutations, and associated antibiotics. A mutation was classified as a “key resistance mutation” only if the original authors explicitly reported that the mutation confers resistance. Conflicting reports were resolved using a priority hierarchy based on isogenic strain backgrounds and recency, with unresolved cases flagged as “ambiguous”.

Integration of public databases

Concurrently, comprehensive antibiotic information—including 2D/3D chemical structures, mechanisms of action, and clinical indications—was aggregated from PDB, PubChem16, ChEMBL17, CARD, and DrugBank18. Protein sequences and basic biological information for bacterial species and protein targets were obtained from NCBI and UniProt.

Through this integration, the curated mutation data were standardized and enriched with structural, functional, and taxonomic annotations. A detailed description of the manual curation protocol is provided in the Supplementary Methods. All curated mutations listed in the database have been experimentally validated in the original publications (e.g., MIC assays, site-directed mutagenesis); a representative summary of validation methods for each mutation is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Bioinformatics analysis pipeline

To provide deeper insights beyond raw data, BactProNET integrates a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis pipeline. The process begins with sequence alignment to assess protein conservation and standardize mutation sites. Using the highly conserved quinolone target protein DNA gyrase subunit A (GyrA) as a representative example, sequences containing resistance mutations from various bacteria were pair-wise aligned against the well-characterized Escherichia coli (E. coli) GyrA template via the CLUSTALW algorithm. Alignment quality was assessed by retaining only gap-free regions containing two or more mutation positions (see Supplementary Table S3). This method enabled the mapping of resistance mutations from diverse sources onto homologous positions in the reference sequence, culminating in the generation of an integrated mutation distribution map. This procedure was applied to all target proteins in the database.

To establish a structural foundation for mechanistic investigation, the AlphaFold2 algorithm was utilized to predict high-accuracy 3D structures from wild-type FASTA sequences provided by NCBI. Model confidence was assessed using predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) scores and Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) metrics (see Supplementary Table S4). These structural models served as the basis for molecular docking studies, performed with the AutoDock4 software suite, to elucidate drug-target interaction modes. This workflow involved preparing the receptor protein and antibiotic ligand, defining a grid box (120 Å × 120 Å × 120 Å) centered at the geometric center of each protein to enable global protein surface docking, and selecting the lowest-energy conformation from the most populated cluster as the optimal binding model. For proteins with known active sites, predicted binding poses overlapping with those regions were prioritized for interpretation. Detailed docking parameters (grid size, GA runs, clustering criteria, ligand and receptor preparation, binding site definition, etc.) are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

To assess the structural impact of resistance mutations, we performed mutant docking analysis. Mutant protein structures were generated by introducing specific amino acid substitutions (e.g., Ser83Arg in GyrA) into the wild-type AlphaFold2 model using PyMOL’s mutagenesis wizard. The mutated residues were energy-minimized while keeping the rest of the protein fixed. Molecular docking was then performed on the mutant models using the same parameters as for the wild-type to enable direct comparison of binding modes and energies.

Finally, to contextualize these findings within an evolutionary framework, phylogenetic analysis was conducted using MEGA11. A phylogenetic tree was constructed from wild-type amino acid sequences using the maximum likelihood (ML) method with the LG + G+I substitution model, with branch support assessed by 1000 bootstrap replicates. Identified resistance mutation sites were subsequently mapped onto this tree, providing an integrated visualization that reveals the evolutionary patterns of resistance mutations.

Evolutionary analysis scope

Shannon entropy and conservation analyses were performed only for GyrA, GyrB, and ParC. These three targets share sufficient sequence homology across diverse bacterial species to enable reliable multiple sequence alignment and conservation scoring. For other antibiotic classes in the database (e.g., beta-lactams, aminoglycosides), the target proteins lack adequate sequence homology across species, making meaningful evolutionary analysis infeasible. Therefore, all evolutionary conclusions in this study are restricted to these three fluoroquinolone targets.

Database architecture and web interface

The BactProNET platform is deployed on a Linux server with a backend integrating an Apache web server, a MySQL database, and a Node.js runtime environment (see Supplementary Table S6 for version information). The front-end user interface was built with HTML5, CSS3, and JavaScript, employing the Bootstrap framework, jQuery, and the NGL Viewer library for interactive 3D molecular visualization. The platform supports keyword search and category-based browsing and incorporates online BLAST and MSA tools to provide a comprehensive platform for data retrieval, visualization, and preliminary analysis.

Data statistics

The current release of BactProNET contains a systematically curated dataset encompassing 107 protein targets from 44 bacterial species and comprehensive information for 323 antibiotics. A central feature is the annotation of 196 key resistance mutation sites (representing 243 unique amino acid substitutions), which were identified and consolidated from a review of 330 peer-reviewed articles (see Supplementary Methods for curation details; see Supplementary Table S1 for a comparison with existing databases). To facilitate a structural understanding of these interactions, the database includes a library of 640 wild-type and 1,697 mutant protein-antibiotic molecular docking models (Fig. 3).

Quantitative prediction of mutation effects on protein stability

To complement docking-derived binding affinities, we separately assessed the impact of resistance mutations on protein folding stability using mCSM, which calculates ΔΔG values from static structures. It should be noticed that ΔΔG differs from docking-derived ΔG, the former reflects stability changes, the latter binding affinity. As summarized in Supplementary Table S7, the majority of resistance mutations (91.8%) exhibited destabilizing effects (negative ΔΔG), with consistent patterns observed across different antibiotic classes and target proteins. These computational predictions suggest that destabilizing effects on target protein stability are correlated with target-mediated antimicrobial resistance, consistent with a possible mechanistic contribution.

Case Study: A One-stop Integrated Analysis of GyrA from Proteus mirabilis using BactProNET

To demonstrate the platform’s utility, this case study presents a complete workflow analyzing GyrA from Proteus mirabilis, using the fluoroquinolone antibiotic Enoxacin as an exemplar drug. The analysis was initiated by querying “Proteus mirabilis” on the BactProNET platform. The target, “DNA gyrase subunit A [Proteus mirabilis] (3D Model),” was then selected from the results to access its dedicated data page and molecular docking interface.

Step 1: Structural Impact of a Resistance Mutation on Drug Binding.

To directly assess how a resistance mutation affects drug binding, we performed molecular docking using both wild-type and mutant GyrA models. The Ser83Arg mutation, a well-characterized fluoroquinolone resistance substitution, was introduced into the wild-type structure. Docking of Enoxacin to the wild-type GyrA yielded a binding energy of -6.79 kcal/mol with a hydrogen bond formed to ARG91 within the active site (Fig. 4A). In contrast, docking to the Ser83Arg mutant resulted in a weaker binding energy of -4.68 kcal/mol, corresponding to an approximately 35-fold increase in the inhibition constant (from 10.53 µM to 369.59 µM). Strikingly, the mutant complex showed no hydrogen bonds, and the bound Enoxacin was located far from the active site region (Fig. 4B), indicating that the drug no longer binds to its functional target site. This dramatic change in binding mode provides computational evidence consistent with the interpretation that the Ser83Arg mutation confers resistance to Enoxacin by disrupting the drug-binding mode.

Molecular docking of Enoxacin to wild-type and mutant GyrA from Proteus mirabilis. Left panels show the overall structure; right panels detail the active site. (A) Wild-type GyrA binds Enoxacin at the active site with a binding energy of -6.79 kcal/mol, forming a hydrogen bond with ARG91 (dashed yellow line). (B) The Ser83Arg mutant shows reduced binding affinity (-4.68 kcal/mol). The drug binds to a location far from the active site, with no hydrogen bonds to active-site residues. This altered binding mode explains the loss of drug efficacy.

Step 2: Correlating Resistance with Evolutionary and Sequence Context.

An integrated view juxtaposes a ML phylogenetic tree and a MSA (Fig. 5), directly linking evolutionary divergence to functional protein regions. All reported resistance mutations are highlighted on the MSA, providing immediate visual and evolutionary context for their distribution.

Evolutionary Distribution of Resistance Mutations in DNA Gyrase (GyrA). The maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree (left), constructed from an alignment of wild-type GyrA protein sequences, is presented alongside a corresponding profile of literature-curated resistance mutations (right). All mutation sites are numbered according to the homologous positions in the Escherichia coli GyrA reference sequence.

To quantitatively assess the evolutionary constraint on these resistance mutations, we calculated Shannon entropy and conservation scores for regions containing known resistance mutations in GyrA, GyrB, and ParC. For GyrA, known fluoroquinolone resistance mutations (e.g., Ser83, Asp87) are located in the 61–90 region, which showed low entropy (mean = 0.41, see Supplementary Data S1) and moderate conservation (mean = 0.59, see Supplementary Data S2). For GyrB, the region spanning residues 451–480 (containing known resistance mutations, e.g., Asp463 and Ser464) showed lower entropy (mean = 0.30, see Supplementary Data S3) and higher conservation (mean = 0.70, see Supplementary Data S4). For ParC, the region spanning residues 61–90 (containing known resistance mutations, e.g., Ser79, Ser80, Asp83, Glu84) showed the lowest entropy (mean = 0.28, see Supplementary Data S5) and the highest conservation (mean = 0.72, see Supplementary Data S6). These results suggest that in the three fluoroquinolone targets examined (GyrA, GyrB, and ParC), resistance mutations preferentially occur at evolutionarily conserved positions critical for protein function, consistent with the known fitness cost associated with fluoroquinolone resistance.

Step 3: Real-time Validation of Novel Sequences.

Built-in BLAST and MSA tools enable the real-time validation of user-submitted sequences. A novel GyrA sequence from a resistant strain can be directly compared against references to rapidly identify resistance-conferring mutations, accelerating the research cycle.

Validation of the BactProNET docking and modeling pipeline

To systematically validate the reliability of our structural modeling and docking approaches, we performed multiple complementary analyses.

AlphaFold2 model validation.We compared the AlphaFold2-predicted GyrA model with experimental ternary complex structures (PDB: 4Z2C and 5CDQ). The predicted protein aligned well with the protein components of both structures, with root mean square deviation (RMSD) values of 0.770 Å (2,655 atoms aligned) for 4Z2C and 0.377 Å (2,812 atoms aligned) for 5CDQ (see Supplementary Figure S1), confirming that the AlphaFold2 model accurately captures the overall structure of DNA gyrase. Both 4Z2C and 5CDQ are ternary complexes containing DNA gyrase, DNA, and moxifloxacin, whereas our model represents DNA gyrase alone (without DNA). Consequently, the binding positions of moxifloxacin differ substantially between the experimental structures and our model, with centroid distances of 78.0 Å (4Z2C) and 88.9 Å (5CDQ). This discrepancy reflects the conformational change in the binding pocket induced by DNA binding, which is a known feature of the fluoroquinolone mechanism of action, rather than an error in our docking methodology.

Discussion

The core innovation of BactProNET is that it provides researchers with a “wild-type reference system” coupled with comparative mutant docking analysis. By performing structural prediction on the wild-type protein and molecular docking with antibiotics, we identify and display a single, energetically optimal binding conformation for each drug-target pair. By also performing docking on mutant proteins (1,697 models across 107 targets), the platform allows users to compare wild-type and mutant binding modes. This comparative framework may serve as a foundation for subsequent analysis. Users can map known resistance mutations onto the 3D model to infer how a specific amino acid substitution might disrupt optimal binding. Such disruption may occur through steric hindrance, charge alteration, or loss of key interactions, potentially contributing to resistance.

The practical value of BactProNET is further enhanced by its built-in tools and external links. The embedded BLAST and MSA tools allow users to analyze their own sequences in real time, while links to resources such as NCBI and PDB may help users access broader data ecosystems.

Benchmarking against existing databases

To contextualize the coverage and utility of BactProNET, we compared its data scope and functionality with existing antimicrobial resistance and structural databases, including CARD, ResFinder, MEGARes, NDARO, PDB, and UniProt (see Supplementary Table S1 for a detailed comparison). In terms of scale, CARD, as a comprehensive database, draws from 3,354 publications, covering 414 pathogens and 18 drug classes. NDARO encompasses 753 bacterial species, while PDB and UniProt provide over 250,000 structures and 570,000 reviewed protein entries, respectively. BactProNET, in contrast, focuses specifically on target-protein amino acid substitutions, currently covering 107 target proteins and 196 mutation sites (243 unique substitutions) curated from 330 publications.

Although other databases offer larger data volumes, they lack this type of mechanistic integration. BactProNET provides pre-computed wild-type and mutant docking models (640 wild-type and 1,697 mutant models), as well as ΔG, ΔΔG, and hydrogen bond interaction data. These resources may help facilitate direct comparison of structural changes in drug binding before and after mutation. A systematic benchmark of query performance and usability across platforms has not yet been conducted and would be a valuable direction for future work.

Validation of the computational pipeline

To assess the reliability of our structural modeling and docking approach, we performed several validation analyses. The AlphaFold2-predicted GyrA model aligned with experimental structures (RMSD: 0.770 Å for 4Z2C and 0.377 Å for 5CDQ). Known resistance mutations mapped onto the optimal binding model were spatially located within the QRDR. These observations support the potential reliability of our computational pipeline, though experimental validation remains the gold standard.

Integrated observations from multi-level data

By integrating protein structure, drug interactions, evolutionary context, and wild-type versus mutant comparisons, BactProNET reveals otherwise hidden patterns. First, comparative docking shows how mutations alter drug binding. For the GyrA Ser83Arg mutant, binding energy increased by 2.11 kcal/mol (~ 35-fold inhibition constant increase), accompanied by binding site displacement and loss of the ARG91 hydrogen bond. Such changes in both affinity and binding mode are easily overlooked when examining sequence or structure alone. Second, in the three fluoroquinolone targets examined (GyrA, GyrB, and ParC), resistance mutations map preferentially to evolutionarily constrained regions. Quantitative analysis showed that GyrA residues 61–90 exhibit low entropy and moderate conservation, while GyrB residues 451–480 and ParC residues 61–90 similarly showed low entropy but higher conservation (see Supplementary Data S1–S6). This indicates a non‑random clustering in functionally important domains within these fluoroquinolone targets. Of note, using Shannon entropy and co-evolutionary analysis, Sanyal et al. (2025) independently observed that resistance mutations in dihydropteroate synthase, an antibiotic target from a different protein family, also preferentially occur at evolutionarily conserved positions19. Third, ΔΔG analysis of 243 mutations using mCSM revealed destabilizing effects in 91.8% of cases, consistent across antibiotic classes (see Supplementary Table S7), implicating target protein destabilization as a common feature of target‑mediated resistance. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that resistance mutations commonly disrupt binding, cluster in conserved regions, and destabilize target proteins.

Limitations

We recognize several limitations of the current platform. First, all computational predictions and structural inferences presented in this study, including AlphaFold2-predicted structures, docking models, and ΔΔG stability estimates, require experimental validation (e.g., site-directed mutagenesis, binding affinity assays, or X-ray crystallography) before being considered conclusive. Computationally predicted models may not be fully equivalent to experimentally resolved structures. Second, the database currently focuses on resistance mediated by target-site amino acid substitutions and does not yet cover other important mechanisms such as drug efflux or enzymatic degradation. Third, the strategy of presenting a single optimal docking conformation, while enhancing clarity, does not capture the full ensemble of possible binding modes or molecular dynamics. Fourth, while we have compared data coverage and functionality with existing databases (see Supplementary Table S1), a systematic benchmark of query performance and user usability across platforms has not yet been conducted and would be an important direction for future evaluation.

Sustainability and long-term maintenance

BactProNET is intended to be maintained as an ongoing resource through the following strategies: (1) annual data updates incorporating newly published resistance mutations and peer-reviewed literature; (2) community engagement through user feedback and contribution channels; (3) versioned releases with documentation of updates; and (4) institutional hosting with server maintenance and backup protocols. The platform is planned to remain accessible as an open resource for the research community.

Future directions

Looking ahead, planned upgrades for BactProNET include: (1) exploring the integration of molecular dynamics simulations to investigate binding dynamics; (2) potentially expanding the scope to include other resistance mechanisms (e.g., efflux pumps, enzymatic degradation) to build a more comprehensive AMR knowledge graph; and (3) considering the development of API access to enable programmatic queries and integration with other bioinformatics pipelines.

As a bioinformatics resource, BactProNET aims to contribute to bridging genetic mutation data with the structural basis of drug action. By providing a comparative wild-type and mutant docking framework, it may offer an analytical pathway for both research and education. We hope that BactProNET can serve as a useful resource in the global effort against antibiotic resistance, potentially supporting the discovery cycle from identifying resistance mutations to exploring novel therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, BactProNET represents a step toward bridging the gap between genotype and phenotype in the study of target-mediated antimicrobial resistance. By providing a comparative framework that integrates curated mutation data, AlphaFold2-predicted structures, wild-type and mutant molecular docking baselines, and evolutionary analysis, the platform may support structure-based hypothesis generation for how amino acid substitutions could impair drug binding. This approach is intended to complement existing gene-centric databases by enabling comparison between wild-type and mutant binding modes.

The potential utility of BactProNET is illustrated by its current dataset and user-centric design. The database encompasses 44 bacterial species, 323 antibiotics, 107 protein targets, 196 mutation sites (243 unique substitutions), 640 wild-type docking models, and 1,697 mutant docking models. Its interface combines visualization with embedded analytical tools for sequence analysis. We acknowledge current limitations, including a reliance on computational models and a focused scope on target-mediated resistance. Nevertheless, BactProNET is intended to provide an extensible foundation that may be maintained through annual updates and community engagement.

By consolidating dispersed mutation and structural data into a unified comparative framework, BactProNET aims to assist researchers in interpreting the molecular basis of resistance and may inform the rational design of next-generation antimicrobial agents. Future enhancements will focus on incorporating molecular dynamics simulations, broadening coverage to other resistance mechanisms, and enabling programmatic API access. We hope this platform will serve as a practical complement to existing tools in the ongoing global effort to address antimicrobial resistance.
