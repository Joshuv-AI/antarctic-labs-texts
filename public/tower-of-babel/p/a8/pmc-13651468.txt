Risk prediction models for acute kidney injury in patients with acute pancreatitis: a systematic review
Authors: Lumei Huang, Jinfeng Zang, Juan Li, Yufeng Li, Zhenqing Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a life-threatening complication of acute pancreatitis (AP), significantly increasing mortality and extending hospital stay. Early identification of high-risk patients is crucial for timely intervention yet remains clinically challenging. Although several prediction models have been developed, their methodological quality, predictive performance, and clinical applicability vary greatly and lack comprehensive evaluation. A systematic synthesis of existing evidence is therefore imperative to inform clinical practice and guide future research.MethodsTwo reviewers examined Chinese databases (including CNKI, VIP, SinoMed, and Wanfang), along with PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus, to identify risk prediction models for AKI in patients with AP published from inception to December 2025. Two reviewers independently reviewed the literature, retrieved relevant information, and evaluated the risk of bias using the PROBAST tool. The main performance measures of the models, including AUC, calibration, validation method, and predictors, were retrieved.ResultsOur review assessed 20 studies with 9481 patients. The prevalence of AKI varied between 13.8% and 72.8%. The reported AUCs of the prediction models ranged from 0.735 to 0.920. All 20 studies were assessed as having high overall risk of bias according to the PROBAST tool, primarily owing to retrospective study designs, lack of external validation, and incomplete model development processes.ConclusionsExisting AKI prediction models for AP patients show moderate to good apparent discrimination but are based on studies with uniformly high risk of bias. No model can be recommended for routine clinical use without prospective external validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05121-9.

Take-home message

This systematic review identifies, evaluates, and synthesises published risk prediction models for acute kidney injury (AKI) in patients with acute pancreatitis (AP), emphasising heterogeneity in their development, performance, and validation.These findings provide a systematic overview of existing prediction models and their methodological limitations, and offer methodological recommendations for future researchers to develop higher-quality models with reduced bias and improved transparency. Due to uniformly high risk of bias, these models require prospective external validation before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) represents a prevalent gastrointestinal emergency, with a rising incidence worldwide, thereby imposing a significant burden on healthcare systems [1]. Although the majority of cases are mild, around 20–40% of patients experience severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), which is marked by persistent organ failure and elevated mortality rates [2]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a significant life-threatening complication linked to severe adverse outcomes in patients with SAP. For example, in patients with AP who also have AKI, the survival rates for those receiving surgical intensive care treatment and during hospitalisation were merely 23% and 21% of the survival rates observed in patients with AP without AKI [3]. The pathophysiology of AKI in AP encompasses various mechanisms, such as systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), hypovolemia, abdominal compartment syndrome, and direct renal injury caused by pancreatic enzymes and cytokines [4, 5]. The initial week post-hospital admission is vital for the development of AKI, with prompt recognition essential for timely intervention and improved patient outcomes [6].

Despite improvements in supportive care, the early identification of AP patients at high risk for AKI continues to pose challenges. The clinical need for robust prediction models is underscored by limitations of current AKI detection approaches. Serum creatinine, the traditional marker of kidney function, rises only after a significant decline in glomerular filtration rate, making it a delayed indicator of renal injury. Although several novel biomarkers and electronic alert systems have been developed for early AKI detection, their implementation has not consistently translated into improved clinical outcomes [7]. Conversely, the application of AKI care bundles—targeted interventions including haemodynamic optimisation, avoidance of nephrotoxins, and glycaemic control—has demonstrated benefit in high-risk populations [8, 9], supporting the premise that effective risk stratification, rather than detection alone, is essential for timely intervention. General critical care scores, including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), are used for risk assessment [9, 10]. Isolated laboratory markers, such as the triglyceride-glucose index, atherogenic index of plasma, and intra-bladder pressure, have been evaluated for their predictive value regarding AKI in patients with AP [11–13]. However, these general tools and isolated markers exhibit a lack of specificity for the AP population and may not accurately represent the distinct pathophysiology of AKI in this context. Several studies have proposed dedicated risk prediction models that incorporate clinical, biochemical, and imaging parameters to enhance AKI risk stratification [14, 15]. These models exhibit significant variability in predictive variables, methodological rigour, and reported performance, complicating clinicians’ ability to assess their real-world applicability.

The existing evidence for these models remains incomplete. A recent systematic review by Zhu et al. [16] examined this topic but lacked a formal quality assessment and rigorous meta-analysis. Our study addresses these limitations by performing PROBAST-based evaluation and quantitative synthesis with sensitivity analysis. Specifically, this review aims to: (1) identify and evaluate all published risk prediction models for AKI in AP patients; (2) assess their methodological quality using standardised tools; (3) compare prediction performance metrics, such as AUC, sensitivity, and specificity, through meta-analysis where feasible; and (4) examine their clinical applicability and potential implementation barriers. The findings will offer clinicians and reviewers clear evidence-based summaries of the most effective tools and guide the future creation of more accurate prediction models.

Methods

The study protocol was registered on the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) with registration number CRD420251079495.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of Chinese databases, including CNKI, VIP, Wanfang, and SinoMed, as well as PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus, for studies related to AKI in AP patients from their inception to December 2025. The search strategy incorporated a combination of MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms and free-text keywords, including: (‘pancreatitis’ OR ‘acute pancreatitis’ OR ‘AP’) AND (‘acute kidney injury’ OR ‘AKI’) AND (‘risk prediction model’ OR ‘prediction model’ OR ‘prediction’ OR ‘scoring system’ OR ‘nomograph’ OR ‘risk score’). To reduce publication bias, we manually examined the reference lists of retrieved studies and pertinent review articles to identify any potentially eligible studies that were not included in the initial database search. The detailed search strategy for each database was provided in Supplementary Materials 1.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included prospective or retrospective cohort studies that created and/or validated prediction models, nomographs, or scoring systems for the incidence of AKI in patients with AP. The PICOTS framework was used as advised in the Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies (CHARMS) [17] checklist for the systematic evaluation of prediction modelling studies.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with AP; (2) studies that developed and/or validated risk prediction models for AKI in AP patients; (3) models reporting sufficient data to extract or calculate performance metrics (such as AUC, sensitivity, specificity); and (4) the outcome was AKI.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) case reports, reviews, editorials, letters, and conference abstracts (unless full data are available); (2) studies not written in English or Chinese; (3) studies with insufficient data to extract performance metrics even after contacting authors; (4) studies that focused solely on identifying prediction or risk factors for AKI in AP patients lacking multivariable modelling.

Study selection and screening

Two reviewers (L.M.H. and J.L.) independently conducted the study screening process to minimise bias in study selection [19]. Duplicate records across databases (including between Chinese and English databases) were identified using Zotero and manually verified before removal. After removing duplicate records using Zotero, we first screened studies based on titles and abstracts according to predefined eligibility criteria. We examined a comprehensive evaluation of the eligible studies. We also manually reviewed the reference lists of all included studies to identify any potentially relevant publications. Disagreements between reviewers occurred most frequently in the predictor domain and were primarily resolved through discussion; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by the third reviewer (J.F.Z.).

Quality assessment

We performed quality assessment using the validated Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) [20] by two reviewers (L.M.H. and Z.Q.R.). This standardized checklist evaluates both risk of bias and clinical applicability in prediction model studies through 20 signaling questions across four critical domains: (1) participant selection, (2) predictor variables, (3) outcome determination, and (4) analytical approach. Each question was rated using a five-tier classification system (‘yes,’ ‘probably yes,’ ‘no,’ ‘probably no,’ or ‘no information’). This field can only be considered “low risk” if all the signal issues respond with “yes” or “probably yes”. We may classify any signal issue as “high risk” if it receives a “no” or “probably no” response. When a certain signal issue is judged as “no information” while all the other signals are classified as “low risk”, then this field is classified as “unclear”. Moreover, when developing model research, the category will be elevated to high-risk if all four domains are assessed as low-risk and external validation is absent. Conversely, research focused on model validation may still be regarded as overall low-risk, provided that the validated model is developed from a substantial dataset and internal validation is performed during the development stage.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (L.M.H. and Y.F.L.) independently extracted data using a standardised form. The following data were extracted from each study: (1) study characteristics: the first author, publication year, country, study type, AP Definition, AP severity, AKI Definition, AKI severity, AKI time, modelling sample size, data source, model type, and AKI cases/sample size; (2) Model information: model development method, model performance (including the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CI, sensitivity, specificity, and calibration statistics), type of validation, final predictors, and model display. We also aimed to extract clinically informative metrics such as positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV); however, these were rarely reported in the included studies. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or third reviewer (J.F.Z) consultation. We attempted to contact authors twice for missing data. All extracted data underwent cross-verification against original publications to ensure accuracy.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with Stata 15.0 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Given the anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and model development methods, and the small number of eligible models (n = 4), a quantitative synthesis was performed as an “exploratory analysis” and is presented in the Supplementary Material 2. The main text presents a narrative synthesis of model characteristics, performance metrics, and PROBAST assessments.

Results

Study identification and selection

A total of 1667 studies were initially searched and identified. Following the removal of 286 duplicates from all databases, we assessed 1381 titles and abstracts for eligibility. Subsequent to this screening, 45 studies were chosen for additional evaluation. In the evaluation process, 25 studies were excluded for failing to construct prediction models or for concentrating exclusively on risk variables. A total of 20 studies were included [14, 15, 21–38]. Study characteristics

Table 1 presents a summary of fundamental data from the included studies. Among the 20 studies analyzed, 18 were retrospective cohort studies, and 2 were prospective cohort studies. The publications spanned from 2018 to 2025, with 17 conducted in China and 3 in the USA. For AP, 9 studies employed the Atlanta Criteria [39], 1 study employed the AGA Guideline [40], whereas 10 studies adopted various editions of the Chinese Guidelines [41–43]. The spectrum of AP severity included in the studies also varied: 5 studies focused exclusively on Severe Acute Pancreatitis (SAP), 2 studies included Moderately Severe and Severe AP (MSAP/SAP), 2 studies covered the full spectrum from Mild to Severe AP (MAP/MSAP/SAP), and the remaining 11 studies included all AP patients without severity stratification. For AKI, 19 studies adopted the KDIGO criteria. One of the early studies used an alternative definition based on urine output and serum creatinine. Among the studies reporting the severity staging of AKI (n = 9), all used the KDIGO staging system (I-III stages). The time window for predicting AKI is typically defined as within 48 h to 7 days after admission (n = 17). Other definitions include within 72 h after admission (n = 1) and within 14 days after AP diagnosis (n = 1). The total sample size across all studies was 9481 patients, among whom 3891 developed AKI. The reported AKI incidence varied widely, ranging from 13.8% to 72.8%.

Table 2 presents an overview of the information related to the included prediction models. In terms of data processing, most studies (18 out of 20) used the direct exclusion method to handle missing data, while 8 studies employed the multiple imputation method. Continuous variables are usually included in the analysis in their original form. In the studies analysed, the event per variable (EPV) for 17 studies exceeds 10. Only two studies [26, 37] employed LASSO regression to mitigate the risk of overfitting. Many studies utilised traditional stepwise regression, potentially inflating the predictive performance. Six studies [15, 21, 24, 30, 32, 36] employed machine learning methods, including the Random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and classification tree (Cat); The reported AUC range is between 0.735 and 0.920. Calibration degree tests were performed in seventeen studies [14, 21, 22, 24–34, 36–38], utilising the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and calibration curve as the main methodologies. C-reactive protein (CRP) and serum creatinine (Scr) were the most frequently utilised predictors, appearing in seven models. Additional commonly utilised predictors comprised albumin (ALB), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, and Cystatin C (Cys C). The most common form of model presentation was a nomogram. Overall, these studies exhibit significant heterogeneity in terms of methodology, particularly in data processing, variable selection, and validation strategies. The complete comparison of all models is moved to the main result description or Supplementary Table 2.

Risk of bias in studies

Table 3 presents the applicability and bias risk of the selected research. All 20 studies were assessed to possess a significant overall risk of bias, considerably undermining the reliability of their findings and current clinical applicability. In the participant domain, eighteen studies [14, 15, 21–28, 30–36, 38] were considered to have high-risk as they were retrospective cohort studies, due to their data collection methodology; only two studies [29, 37] were classified as prospective cohort studies. In the predictor domain, four studies [25, 27, 30, 36] were considered to have an unclear risk of bias. All four studies used the MIMIC-IV database. Model developers usually chose variables from the database that were readily accessible and well-documented, such as vital signs and laboratory data. However, they may have neglected other clinically significant predictors that were difficult to standardise and extract from electronic health records. In the outcome domain, twelve studies [14, 22, 24, 25, 28–31, 34, 35, 37, 38] were classified as high-risk. Twelve studies [14, 22, 24, 25, 28–31, 34, 35, 37, 38] did not differentiate the severity of AKI. One study [14] did not measure the time point for AKI. In the analysis domain, the model development and validation processes were generally poorly described in all studies [14, 15, 21–38]. Three studies [25, 28, 35] converted continuous variables into categorical variables either partially or completely. Ten studies [21, 24–28, 31, 33, 36, 37] only evaluated AUC, lacking evaluation of sensitivity and specificity. Six studies [14, 15, 22, 23, 29, 35] did not report or only used the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test to evaluate the calibration. Additionally, the external validation of these models was often insufficient in seventeen studies [14, 15, 21–32, 35, 37, 38]. The number of events for EPV in ten studies [14, 15, 22–24, 28, 31, 34, 35, 37] was all less than 20. And no study has provided information regarding the complexity of data.

In terms of the assessment of applicability risk, nine studies [14, 23, 26, 30–33, 35, 37] were evaluated and classified as having a high applicability risk. Seven studies [14, 23, 26, 31, 33, 35, 37] exclusively enrolled patients with moderate-to-severe AP, a specific subgroup that may not be representative of the broader scope of AP patients intended to be covered by this review. In the predictor domain, the time intervals for assessing predictive factors and determining results in the two studies remain uncertain [30, 32]. In the analysis domain, all studies exhibit a low risk.

Models validation

Most models in the examined studies underwent either internal or external validation. A total of nine performed internal validation [21, 23–27, 30–32, 38], one study conducted external validation [34], two incorporated both internal and external validation [31, 34], and seven studies [14, 15, 22, 28, 29, 35, 37] developed models without any validation.

Exploratory quantitative synthesis

The exploratory quantitative synthesis, including forest plots and sensitivity analyses, is presented in Supplementary Material 2. Due to substantial heterogeneity, limited model numbers, and high risk of bias across all included studies, this analysis does not provide a reliable summary estimate and does not inform the main conclusions of this review.

Discussion

AKI is associated with the risk of mortality and prolonged hospitalization, which is a preventable complication of AP [44]. The reported incidence of AKI in AP varies widely, likely due to differences in diagnostic criteria and clinical management practices [45]. It is crucial to effectively assess the potential risk of AKI in AP patients to identify high-risk individuals and implement early interventions, which can significantly improve clinical outcomes. This systematic review identified a growing number of risk prediction models for AKI in patients with AP, although most models are derived from single-center studies. However, according to the PROBAST checklist, all included studies were deemed to have a high risk of bias, limiting the clinical applicability of these prediction models.

The existing framework of prediction models for AKI in AP largely depends on conventional statistical methods, primarily utilising logistic regression as the main technique in most studies reviewed. This systematic review identified only six studies [15, 21, 24, 30, 32, 36] that utilised ML techniques. Machine learning models possess the capacity for enhanced predictive accuracy by identifying complex, non-linear relationships and higher-order interactions among predictors—abilities that are fundamentally constrained in traditional linear models [46]. This advantage is especially prominent in clinical settings where risk factors may display non-linear or time-dependent characteristics. Nonetheless, the efficacy of machine learning models is not consistently superior; in cases with restricted sample sizes, appropriately specified logistic regression models may exhibit similar or even enhanced effectiveness and stability [47]. Moreover, machine learning methods present an increased likelihood of overfitting in limited medical datasets, requiring strong internal validation techniques like cross-validation and regularisation. Their complexity frequently results in reduced interpretability compared to traditional regression, resulting in their designation as “black boxes.” This limitation is addressed by emerging explanation frameworks such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values [48]. Future research should focus on establishing larger, multicentric cohorts to effectively utilise the capabilities of machine learning. A promising approach entails hybrid strategies that utilise machine learning for feature selection or pattern discovery, while maintaining interpretable models such as logistic regression for final predictions, thereby achieving a balance between performance and clinical applicability.

Internal validation employs the model development dataset to assess the consistency of the modelling process and reduce overfitting, facilitating a more precise evaluation of model performance. Techniques such as bootstrapping and cross-validation can be employed to refine the model, thereby improving its stability and reliability within the original cohort. In contrast, external validation assesses the model using a completely independent dataset to evaluate its extensibility and generalisability. This essential step evaluates the model’s performance across various populations and real-world clinical contexts, providing a thorough understanding of its applicability. This review indicates that internal validation was conducted for only twelve prediction models [21, 23–27, 30–33, 36, 38], while external validation was performed on just three models [31, 32, 34]. The restricted validation presents significant issues concerning the applicability of these models in diverse clinical settings. Prediction models necessitate thorough external validation to confirm their reliability before broad clinical application. The absence of external validation notably limits their applicability across diverse populations, necessitating caution from clinicians when evaluating their utility in specific local contexts. The identified limitations can be ascribed to the prevalence of single-center designs and inadequate sample sizes in the original studies. Future studies focused on developing prediction models for AKI in AP patients must utilise strong internal validation methods and emphasise external validation via multi-center collaborations to guarantee generalisability and clinical significance.

This study demonstrates that the prediction model consists of 4 to 10 components, most of which can be evaluated objectively and are easily accessible. The analysis of predictor frequency indicated significant consistencies among the developed models. CRP and Scr were the most commonly used predictors. Other frequently used predictors included ALB, APACHE II score, and Cys C. The significance of CRP is anticipated, as it serves as a key biomarker of the systemic inflammatory response in AP. The resulting cytokine storm may lead to renal endothelial dysfunction, microcirculatory failure, and ultimately AKI [49]. The consistent inclusion of Scr, a classical marker of renal function, underscores the significance of pre-existing or early renal impairment in forecasting progressive AKI [50]. The inclusion of Cys C across various models is significant. The inclusion of this indicator serves as a more sensitive and earlier measure of glomerular filtration rate reduction compared to serum creatinine, aiming to improve the biological accuracy and timeliness of AKI prediction [51]. The APACHE II score is a composite measure of disease severity that reflects the systemic derangements in SAP, which predispose patients to organ failure, including AKI [52]. Hypoalbuminemia indicates significant systemic inflammation and capillary leak, potentially resulting in intravascular volume depletion and diminished renal perfusion [53]. Future research must differentiate between models intended for risk stratification at admission and those aimed at prognostication during hospitalisation to inform clinical decision-making at various time intervals. Clinicians must remain vigilant for AKI in AP patients exhibiting elevated CRP, Scr, or Cys C, as well as those with elevated APACHE II scores or reduced albumin levels. In high-risk patients, personalised management strategies, including aggressive fluid resuscitation based on volume status and the avoidance of nephrotoxins, should be implemented to prevent AKI.

A critical methodological distinction merits explicit discussion. Several models included predictors such as serum creatinine, cystatin C, SOFA score, APACHE II score, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, and sepsis—variables that may reflect early manifestations of AKI or established illness severity rather than true baseline risk factors. Depending on the timing of measurement relative to AKI onset, these predictors may introduce temporal leakage. While most included studies defined the prediction window as within 48 h to 7 days of admission, the exact timing of predictor measurement relative to AKI diagnosis was often incompletely reported. Future studies should clearly distinguish between true prediction models (using baseline or pre-event predictors) and early detection models (using concurrent indicators of evolving AKI), as these serve different clinical purposes and require different methodological safeguards against overestimation of performance.

The report on the study findings fails to consistently adhere to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [54], and the details regarding the model development process are insufficient, which undermines the reliability of the quality assessment. Different missing data handling methods (direct exclusion in 18 studies vs. multiple imputation in 8 studies) may introduce heterogeneity; this was observed across included studies but could not be quantitatively assessed due to incomplete reporting of the proportion and pattern of missing data in most studies. While most studies met the minimum criterion of an EPV value ≥ 10, a widely recognised threshold for mitigating overfitting, the consistently elevated EPV values noted may still indicate an optimistic bias in model coefficients. This optimism may subsequently reduce model performance in practical clinical settings. Discrimination addresses only one aspect of model performance. For clinical implementation, calibration, DCA, and external validation are equally essential. Most models exhibited acceptable discrimination (AUC > 0.75); however, three studies [15, 23, 35] did not provide calibration measures. Inadequate calibration may lead to substantial overestimation or underestimation of the probability of AKI, a factor of particular importance in patients with severe AP. This inaccuracy may result in the misallocation of intensive care resources or the inappropriate initiation of renal replacement therapy. Moreover, the majority of AKI prediction models lacked the reporting of clinically informative metrics, specifically positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV). Prevalence-dependent indices are crucial for the interpretation of diagnostic test results [55]. Among the ten studies analysed [14, 15, 22, 23, 29, 30, 32, 34, 35, 38], sensitivity and specificity were frequently reported, whereas PPV and NPV were infrequently mentioned. Therefore, even acceptable discrimination does not imply clinical usefulness. Readers should not interpret the reported AUCs as evidence of clinical readiness.

It is important to recognise several limitations inherent in the review. Firstly, our conclusion is limited by the quality of the original research methods. As pointed out in the PROBAST assessment, all the included models were at high risk of bias, mainly due to their retrospective, single-centre design and the almost universal lack of strict external validation. This implies that the discriminatory ability might be optimistic and not applicable to a broader and more realistic clinical setting. Secondly, the repeated use of the same database (such as MIMIC-IV) limits its applicability in different medical settings. More importantly, even within the same database, we observed significant differences in the incidence of AKI (ranging from 54.0% to 72.8%), which is likely due to differences in patient selection criteria or data processing methods. Across all included studies, the variation was even wider (13.8% to 72.8%), reflecting major differences in case mix (ICU versus non-ICU, SAP versus all AP severities), AKI ascertainment methods, and healthcare settings. Such variation fundamentally limits the transportability of model performance—a model developed in a high-incidence ICU cohort may not perform similarly in a lower-incidence general ward setting—and undermines direct comparisons across studies. This inconsistency highlights the lack of standardization in current research and raises doubts about the reliability of the summary conclusions. The leave-one-out analysis does not resolve the conceptual concern—it merely indicates that no single study arithmetically dominates the estimate. The issue is structural (shared data source) rather than merely statistical, and cannot be fully addressed by sensitivity analyses. Thirdly, due to the inclusion of studies being limited to English and Chinese literature and the potential language bias, these factors remind us not to directly apply the existing models to different racial or regional situations. Finally, from a clinical perspective, the predictive indicators analysed in the included models are mostly static, single-measurement data. They are unable to capture the dynamic development trajectory of the disease and the crucial impact of time-sensitive therapeutic interventions, which are of vital importance for clinical decision-making and patient prognosis. More importantly, the uniformly high risk of bias across all included studies, as assessed by PROBAST, precludes direct clinical implementation of these models at present. Clinicians should interpret their predictions cautiously, and no model can be recommended for routine use until prospective external validation studies demonstrate reproducible performance in real-world settings.

Conclusion

This systematic review of 20 studies found that existing AKI prediction models for AP patients report moderate to good apparent discrimination reported in individual studies, with reported AUCs ranging from 0.735 to 0.920. However, discrimination alone does not imply clinical utility; calibration, DCA, and external validation are essential for assessing whether prediction models are useful in practice. The majority of the included studies were evaluated as having a high risk of bias based on the PROBAST tool, mainly due to deficiencies in the analysis domain, including insufficient management of continuous predictors and absent data. The current prediction models for AKI in AP fail to adhere to the methodological standards set by PROBAST. To improve the quality and credibility of future research, model developers should become acquainted with the PROBAST checklist to reduce bias and comply with the reporting guidelines of the TRIPOD statement. Future initiatives must focus on the development and, crucially, the extensive multinational external validation of new models employing rigorous methodologies. Despite the moderate to good apparent discrimination reported in individual studies, none of the existing models are yet suitable for direct clinical implementation. External validation in prospective, multicentre cohorts is a prerequisite before clinical adoption.
