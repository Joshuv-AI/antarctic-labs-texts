Evolving roles of hospital administrators in India: a qualitative study on health information technology competencies for improved healthcare delivery
Authors: Jabarethina Gnanadhas, John Komattu Chacko, Anitha Ravichandran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital administrators (HAs) aim to strike a balance between providing efficient and compassionate treatment. Health informatics and service management abilities are two areas that are evolving alongside digital health. This study outlines the health information technology (IT) competencies necessary for sustainability in the healthcare industry and looks at the competencies needed for HAs to manage everyday responsibilities.MethodsGrounded theory is used in this qualitative study and reported based on the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines. The participants were HAs who worked in different healthcare fields and held MBAs from the same private university. Respondents were selected through purposeful sampling, and consent was obtained before conducting telephone conversations. Data were gathered until the point of theoretical saturation. Every theme was outlined in a thematic framework.ResultsHealthcare administrators gain knowledge about using applications and technology through the service. A hospital setting is a good place for healthcare administrators to begin their careers to fully understand the intricacies of the field. The key findings of the study indicate that HAs recognised policies and procedures as important areas of competency that required focused attention. HAs are increasingly relying on mid- to advanced-level healthcare technologies in their daily work. Each administrator has gained competencies according to their experience and opportunities. A few years of domain expertise make healthcare administrators competent and educated about every aspect of their profession.ConclusionsAccording to healthcare administrators, practice and experience serve as the cornerstones on which their competencies are built. In the rapidly changing environment of digital healthcare, interdepartmental collaboration is essential for long-term success and adaptability, especially between HAs, IT personnel and medical specialists. Developing capabilities and resilience in this dynamic environment requires a diverse approach.

Introduction

Hospitals have been more interested in health information technology (HIT) in response to the growing need for a rapid digital transformation nationwide.1 The sustainability of healthcare delivery has increased over the past 20 years, largely due to the fast-expanding fields of digital health and health informatics. Health service administrators have to deal with the exponential rise in the knowledge of digital health and planning and managing digital tools and technologies in a dynamic environment. The developing landscape of digital health causes the continued development of health informatics and health service management competencies. One fundamental question is whether health service administrators can oversee and direct the strategic and operational implementation and transformation of the system while continuing to perform routine administrative and operational responsibilities that are typically managed by hospital administrators (HAs). HAs in India could develop the ability to engage with both technical competence and strategic leadership to understand the factors that facilitate or hinder the successful integration of digital health into the healthcare system. These abilities will help maximise the benefits of adopting and growing health informatics and digital health.2 Health professionals are the key players in the digital transformation of the healthcare industry to enhance healthcare digitalisation.3 Most hospital information management (HIM) leaders with power have learnt their leadership qualities through experience gained on the job. HIM professionals now need to complement their management-focused skill set with leadership skills because of the difficulty of navigating a quickly developing healthcare delivery system that involves data gathered, processed and used outside of the conventional HIM boundaries.4

The development of healthcare professionals’ eHealth competencies is a key factor in the adoption of health IT. The range of competencies comprises moral, social, cognitive and motivational skills aimed at fulfilling obligations, settling conflicts and accomplishing goals through the application of behaviours and knowledge.5 Digital health means ‘the field of knowledge and practice associated with the development and use of digital technologies to improve health’.6 Digital health expands the concept of eHealth. eHealth refers to the application of IT and electronic communication to enhance clinical and business procedures that are used by patients, healthcare organisations and healthcare professionals in terms of quality, efficacy and accessibility.7 HIT is the use of computers and other technology in healthcare settings. The term information and communications technology (ICT) may be used when the application of HIT involves a substantial networking or communications component. HIT has the potential to improve healthcare quality and safety while reducing costs, which is why policy-makers and other leaders in the field are interested in it.8

Competency is the capacity to perform professionally in a given circumstance with efficiency and effectiveness, which calls for the fusion of knowledge, abilities, ethics and attitude.911 HIT competencies have so far been referred to by a number of terms in the literature. The Health Information Technology Competencies (HITCOMP) framework offers five domains: administration, direct patient care, engineering/information systems/ICT, informatics and research/biomedicine.3 HIT competencies cover topics including decision support systems, system implementation and data management. Health informatics is in the vanguard of the medical technology revolution, integrating communications, IT and healthcare to improve patient care.12 HIT competencies are necessary to ensure the security and privacy of digital health data, as well as to comprehend the moral and legal ramifications of employing technology in healthcare.13

HAs’ operational management significantly improves healthcare delivery when supported by strong HIT competencies. Integrating HIT into hospital operations leads to improved workflow, better patient outcomes and higher organisational performance.14 Healthcare professionals’ viewpoints continue to be the primary focus of the competencies under investigation. HIT competencies include confidence, self-efficacy and attitudes about digital technologies. These have been found to have an impact on how digital technologies are used in the healthcare industry.3 However, ‘healthcare providers and managers predominantly face infrastructure, technical, training, legal, ethics, time and workload-related barriers to using digital health technologies (DHTs), regardless of the level of care or digital technology’. (Borges do Nascimento et al, p161)15

The healthcare industry is rapidly evolving its use of technology due to advancements in operating systems, software development and the ongoing enhancement of professional competencies. As increasingly sophisticated systems are created, it becomes essential to understand how new technologies can be successfully integrated into existing healthcare procedures. DHTs have demonstrated potential in enhancing healthcare workers’ access, mental health, clinical management and service delivery.16 However, there is still a great need for carefully planned research that evaluates these technologies in relation to healthcare workers and clinically significant outcomes.17

The study aims to explore the evolving roles of HAs by assessing their perceptions of HIT competencies required for improved healthcare delivery, managing day-to-day operations and adapting to digital transformation for long-term sustainability in the era of Digital India.

HAs’ HIT competencies for healthcare delivery

Hospitals are intricate institutions that need careful management to run efficiently. HAs deal with the daily operations and strategic planning of healthcare facilities, ensuring efficient management of quality control, finances and human resources. Their primary responsibility is to use state-of-the-art technology to improve operational efficiency and patient-centred treatment. The goal of HA is to achieve a balance between providing compassionate care and optimising operational efficiency.

In the domain of healthcare administration, there are three main levels of management: front line, middle and senior. The responsibilities and expectations of low-level and high-level managers vary greatly. For instance, workers want higher levels of dominance from high-level managers than low-level managers and greater interpersonal skills from low-level managers than high-level managers.12 The competencies required of individuals in managerial positions are more strongly associated with processes (a sequence of connected actions to accomplish a goal) than with positions (holding managerial or other types of roles).13 Since the competencies of all health professionals are interdependent, applying them all at once can yield the greatest value. Health professionals may deliver patient-centred care as members of an interdisciplinary team if they receive training that prioritises informatics, quality improvement techniques and evidence-based practice.14

As new technologies arise, the competencies related to digital health need to be updated regularly. Methods

Study design, settings and participants

This qualitative study uses the grounded theory technique and is reported based on Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines. The goal of grounded theory is to generate or develop a theory from data that has been methodically collected and examined through comparative analysis.18 19 The introduction of grounded theory presents it as an inductive, comparative approach that offers methodical instructions for obtaining, combining, evaluating and conceptualising qualitative evidence to create a theory.20

The study participants consisted of HAs who were former students and earned their Master’s in Business Administration in Hospital and Health Systems Management from the same Private University, and who are currently employed in different healthcare industry disciplines. By concentrating only on graduates from one private university, the invited participants’ academic background and fundamental understanding were guaranteed to be consistent. The selection strategy minimised variability from varying curricula or institutional norms by guaranteeing a uniform educational background. This approach improves internal validity21 by attributing perspective differences to work experiences rather than differences in education. The participants’ present employment spans multiple healthcare fields and distinct designations, despite attending the same university, which reduces selection bias.22 HAs use HIT at various operational levels, necessitating different levels of expertise, and their diverse real-world experience contributes to a large and varied dataset that supports the goal of the study while upholding methodological rigour.23

Purposive sampling was used to identify the respondents. Purposive sampling is a methodological approach for selecting environments, resources or persons with particular attributes pertinent to the study, who are most likely to yield significant insights into the topic that is being explored.24 Those alumni who were employed were included, and alumni who were pursuing advanced degrees during the time of the study were excluded. The sample size for the grounded theory technique of qualitative research will be determined based on theoretical saturation. ‘25–30 participants is the minimum sample size required to reach saturation and redundancy in grounded theory studies that use in-depth interviews’. (Dworkin, P1320)25 We interviewed 27 HAs. The time frame for this cross-sectional study was April–May 2024.

We made an introductory call to the participants to describe the purpose and objectives of the study and to obtain their verbal consent to participate. We scheduled an appointment based on each participant’s availability using online resources. Telephone interviews were conducted. “For a range of practical and ethical reasons, there are occasions when the telephone may be a preferable or entirely justified methodological choice for a given project or specific interview”. (Irvine, P215)26 The interviews were conducted via telephone to accommodate the demanding schedules of HAs. Their constant engagement with multiple stakeholders and an unpredictable work environment made videoconferencing impractical. Telephone interviews provided the necessary flexibility, allowing participation without disrupting their professional duties.27 The interview guide was developed collaboratively by the first and second authors and subsequently refined by the full research team. While grounded theory emphasises inductive analysis, our initial literature review served to provide sensitising concepts, helping to orient our understanding and identify a relevant research gap. The guide itself began with broad, open-ended questions designed to explore participants’ experiences without imposing a preconceived framework. Consistent with grounded theory principles, this guide was iteratively refined throughout the data collection process as new themes emerged. The final guide, therefore, was a dynamic instrument that enabled us to maintain a balance between structure and the flexibility required for a constant comparative analysis of the data. Relevant references that informed our initial understanding of the research context have been included. Although grounded theory emphasises inductive category development, relevant literature1 3 5 28 29 was consulted to situate the study and identify gaps. Prior findings were treated as sensitising concepts,19 and the semistructured interview guide was iteratively refined (shown in online supplemental material) during data collection to remain responsive to emerging themes. While the HITCOMP paradigm’s Administrative Interaction quadrant served as a sensitising concept in framing the initial interview questions, the guide was iteratively refined in accordance with grounded theory principles to remain responsive to emerging themes. The key areas of interview included project management, standards and protocols, rules and procedures, administration/governance, legal compliance, risk management, finance, expertise, dependency, skill developed and challenges.

Many participants have shifted from various fields within the healthcare industry based on their career prospects. Nobody refused to take part in this study. This outcome was facilitated by purposive sampling, where only employed alumni were included, as well as an introductory call that explained the study objectives, assured confidentiality and offered flexible scheduling. These measures likely contributed to full participation. A comprehensive, one-on-one telephone interview lasted between 30 and 45 min. The participants permitted the researchers to take notes on the interviews. When we initially asked for permission to record the interviews, the HAs appeared more reserved. To encourage open discussion, we ceased recording and began taking detailed notes instead. One team member interviewed while the other took notes during the pair interviews. HAs were incorporated through conference calls in multiple cases. At the conclusion of each interview, we asked the participants if we understood them. Every interview ended with a discussion of initial codes. After the memo was drafted and transcribed, certain HAs were contacted again for clarification. Their feedback contributed to data validation and provided additional insights that strengthened the analysis.

From the transcription, the researchers created codes. A consensus was reached to finalise the themes by discussing any discrepancies in the coding. We constantly compared the results of each interview before moving on to the next round of data collection. Based on the new codes, more interviews were set up. Data collection was pursued until theoretical saturation was achieved, that is, the point at which categories were sufficiently developed and no new insights emerged.30 After the themes were further refined, a thematic framework was developed that provided a concise summary of the core meaning and key elements of each theme.

Data validation

To enhance the trustworthiness and rigour of our findings, we employed multiple qualitative validation techniques. Credibility was strengthened through member checking: participants reviewed their interview transcripts and clarified their statements. For example, participants HA17 and HA22 refined their descriptions of digital monitoring, informing our analysis of ‘HAs’ Perspectives on Digital Technology Applications in Healthcare’ and its subtheme ‘Diagnosing Disease’. Similarly, participant HA16 clarified the rationale for legal competencies, enriching our understanding of challenges in using HIT.

Dependability and confirmability were enhanced through peer debriefing. Each interview was conducted with two researchers present, and all coauthors engaged in iterative team discussions to review and challenge interpretations. This collaborative approach extended to coding and theme development, ensuring that findings emerged through constant comparative analysis.

Finally, we upheld participant confidentiality and anonymity throughout the study, and our reporting adheres to the SRQR, reflecting our commitment to rigour and transparency.

Reflexivity statement

A reflexive approach was maintained throughout the research to acknowledge and understand how our prior experiences and identities may have influenced the data analysis. Our research team comprised individuals from the management discipline with diverse professional backgrounds and career stages. We captured our evolving perspectives and subjective responses through reflexive memos, which served as a critical tool for self-awareness. For instance, our varying experiences with HIT led to iterative discussions, where we consciously challenged our preconceived notions to ensure that our interpretations remained grounded in the participants’ experiences rather than our own. This process of collaborative coding and constant team debriefing was essential for minimising interpretive bias and enriching our theoretical findings.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

The analysis yielded one overarching theme—HAs’ Evolving Roles and Competencies in HIT—which was supported by five interrelated subthemes. Presenting the analytical framework upfront provides a roadmap for readers and enhances clarity in interpreting the results. The subthemes capture administrators’ lived experiences with HIT: (1) dependency on digital technology in daily operations, (2) rationale for adopting HIT, (3) competency development through professional experience, (4) competencies required for administrative roles and (5) challenges encountered in using HIT. HAs’ perspective on digital technology applications in healthcare

Anyone can receive healthcare that is more flexible and accessible with the use of medical digital technologies.31 Digitalisation is a blessing and an essential feature in today’s hospitals. Digital technologies are required from the time of patient registration until their discharge (HA2). The details given by the HAs about the usage of healthcare digital applications are listed here:

Patient tracking to reduce waiting time

As patient trackers help to enhance the process, the printed barcode is given at the registration counter. HA1 described: A Scanner is in every department, like radiology and laboratory. If a patient waits in a department for a long time, it can be easily identified, and it is a foolproof system. Everything has to be brought from registration to discharge via the hospital information system. I will connect the patient to the IP number and track as soon as they have registered (HA6).

Diagnosing disease: in-person and remote

The diagnostic process involves a wide range of technology and instruments.32 HA17 expressed: We are using digitalisation for testing the patient. Healthcare professionals can remotely check vital signs like blood pressure, heart rate, respiration rate, SPO2 levels, temperature and ECG on patients using Artificial Intelligence (AI). HA22 stated: Nowadays, we are monitoring patients through Dazee Bed, a digital screen, and we are using AI to monitor the patient.

Incident reporting system for analysis

IT professionals create software to help the HA use the data. HA will raise the occurrences through the final report and will examine them. HA3 expressed: IT workers use the intranet to create the forms. We use digital sources for incident reporting.

Queue management

Software for hospital queue management makes patients feel less anxious. HA21 described: The process of digitisation starts when tokens are distributed for queue management. The token display system will be used for queue control during service registration.

Statistical database for decision-making

A report that HA will eventually get from the digital software will allow them to get a complete database. To make better decisions, they could compare it daily. HA19 stated: I will receive the entire dashboard facts, which are the specific hospitals' statistics, through technology.

Digital marketing

Using technology is necessary to enhance the brand’s image. HA13 said: I have to pay attention to the remarks and the way the website is being created. I began utilizing technology to learn how to enhance our YouTube promotion.

Financial management

Every hospital develops its in-house billing software, which is managed and controlled by the hospital’s IT department. HA14 expressed: We have specialised software for tracking financial matters.

Telemedicine

Using telecommunications technology makes remote medical consultations and care possible. These capabilities include electronic medical record sharing, messaging, videoconferencing and remote monitoring. HA9 stated: We provide telemedicine to patients and schedule appointments. HA15 stated: My hospital has its application, similar to Practo. They'll conduct online consultations. I work in the back office where patients schedule appointments.

Online feedback

A patient feedback form is a set of questions designed to help healthcare professionals get input from patients about their general experience at the medical centre. HA21 described: Patients have the option to provide feedback online. Outpatients can use the digital display feedback form, and Inpatient provides input through the tab.

Is it possible to develop competencies through experience?

The term ‘competence’ refers to competency systems that define skill, knowledge and attitude clusters necessary for employment.33 Competencies culminate in several knowledge streams, technologies and skill sets that provide an organisation with a competitive edge.34 Regardless of their position, employees in the healthcare industry share a common perspective on issues and believe that a certain set of competencies is required to improve performance.35 During the interviews, health administrators (HAs) were asked to identify relevant administrative interaction competencies using the HITCOMP tool. Discussion

The rapidly evolving digital health ecosystem requires HAs to transition from traditional managerial roles to technologically adept, data-driven decision-making roles. Our study’s findings indicate that whereas MBA Hospital and Health Systems graduates frequently transition between healthcare domains, those with a hospital background are more adaptable and exposed. These professionals emphasise that the ability to translate knowledge into practical, employable skills is crucial for both sustaining and advancing in the field. While knowing provides a necessary foundation, it is the development and application of relevant skills that ultimately determine one’s capacity to succeed and remain competitive. Knowledge can be turned into a talent through intelligent work and application. The integrated synthesis is given in figure 2.

The synthesis presented in figure 2 of the study shows that although the respondents hold the same university degree, they serve across diverse management domains such as quality, operations, general management, patient relations, human resources, healthcare IT, consultancy, insurance and marketing. All healthcare administrators (HAs) reported using HIT in their respective roles, primarily for interaction and coordination purposes. Among them, those in healthcare IT demonstrated the most effective use of HIT, followed by those in general management and patient relationship management.

It is imperative that administrators have a solid grasp of institutional rules and standard operating procedures due to the distinct operational structures of the hospital environment. Except for professionals in marketing, HAs across all other domains affirmed the use of established policies and procedures to guide their administrative interactions. Regardless of their specialisation, the majority of HAs acknowledged that their roles have enabled them to develop strong analytical and people management skills. Furthermore, both controllable and uncontrollable challenges were reported as common across all domains. HAs in middle and high management are involved in risk management and legal matters.

Another key area where theoretical concepts are clarified and internalised through practical application is financial management. Given that tariffs and financial policies often change significantly from year to year, field-based experience becomes essential. Engaging directly with hospital operations allows professionals to gain practical insights into financial management, reinforcing the notion that understanding the financial workings of healthcare organisations requires extensive hands-on exposure.36

The significance of HAs is further elevated by the increasing integration of digital technology in healthcare, which calls for competence in digital tools, data analytics and health informatics. Proficiency in data management software and analytical functions, such as pivot tables and VLOOKUP, has become essential for effectively managing the diverse responsibilities of healthcare administration. One’s expertise will be improved by the supply and demand of their portfolio. A thorough review identified key digital health competencies among healthcare practitioners. These competencies included self-assessed abilities, understanding and using digital technologies, and the psychological and emotional aspects of digital technology. The evaluation emphasised the need for comprehensive training programmes to effectively develop these competencies.3 Supporting this, prior research has demonstrated that DHTs enhance clinical protocol adherence, interprofessional communication and the mental health, abilities and personal competencies of healthcare professionals—skills and capacities that are increasingly important for effective healthcare administration.17

HAs need to collaborate with India’s flagship programmes, including the National Health Stack, eSanjeevani and the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, in order to interact with the broader digital transformation goal.3739 These initiatives require administrators to manage healthcare data governance and digital infrastructure with technical proficiency, agility and digital literacy.3740 In this context, the HITCOMP Framework offers a modular, scalable paradigm for developing digital competencies across all levels of healthcare, from primary to tertiary.28 The adaptability of HITCOMP also makes it appropriate for incorporation into short-term certification courses, capacity-building initiatives and postgraduate healthcare management curricula.

However, multiple frameworks, including the Saudi Health Informatics Competency Framework, TIGER, Management Competency Assessment Programme (MCAP) and HITCOMP, need to be contextualised for the Indian context. Localised frameworks that take worker capacities and structural inequities into account are necessary due to India’s fragmented digital infrastructure, variety in healthcare delivery and differences in digital literacy.1 41 42 India has difficulties implementing the WHO and Canadian Inter-Professional eHealth Competencies because of the country’s poor rural digital infrastructure and disparate regulatory frameworks. Meanwhile, if modified into blended learning models that accommodate working administrators, the International Medical Informatics Association’s suggested Health Informatics Core Competencies could help Indian professionals.5 The Allied Health Professional Digital Health Competency Framework might potentially address the fragmentation and training deficits currently present in this field if it were focused on urban and rehabilitative settings. The MCAP, which was originally designed for other health systems, can be adapted to assess and enhance programmatic delivery and Indian district health administration skills.

HAs encountered diverse challenges, some of which are uncontrollable or unpredictable, particularly those related to acceptance and human attitudes. HAs stated that they acquired core competencies through practical experience, including data handling, critical thinking, communication, problem-solving, people management and data analytics. They underlined that using, generating, analysing and managing data is a daily requirement of their jobs. These findings are consistent with previous research showing that service providers, supervisors, managers and decision-makers at various levels of the health system continually use and analyse data.1 10 43 44 Therefore, developing excellent data management abilities is crucial for system performance and efficient decision-making. Lastly, these competencies can be institutionalised through capacity-building at private universities, NHM, AIIMS, PGIMER and IGNOU, fostering a large-scale, digitally competent health workforce.

Strengths and limitations

In this study, administrators working in various parts of India participated in telephone interviews. As most have cross-domain experiences and frequently shift roles, the synthesis classifies them based on their current positions. The inclusion of participants with diverse professional backgrounds and varying levels of experience adds value by capturing multiple viewpoints relevant to healthcare delivery. However, this study did not examine domain-specific differences or potential variations in HIT competencies by gender and professional experience within each domain, as these aspects were beyond the scope of our objectives. Future research could explore these dimensions to provide deeper insights into how demographic and professional factors influence HIT competency development. This study solely examines the perceptions of HAs on the hospital information system, drawing on their personal experiences and self-assessed competencies. It is important to remember that administrators will likely change their opinions and levels of knowledge with increased exposure and experience. Also, the study did not explore the interdependencies of administrators with other departments, such as IT, and did not explore the perspectives of doctors, nurses and paramedics on their digital competency requirements. These areas can be considered for future prospective research.

The primary data for this study were collected through detailed note-taking instead of audio recordings. This approach was adopted to respect participant preferences and encourage open dialogue, as initial requests for audio-recording were met with notable hesitation. While our method, which included a two-person interview team and immediate collaborative review of notes, helped ensure accuracy and completeness, it did not allow for the capture of subtle non-verbal cues, such as tone, pauses and voice modulation. This absence of verbatim transcripts is a limitation of the study, as grounded theory relies on rich, nuanced data.

Conclusions

HA differs and develops in their level of competency according to their background and experiences. Even if anyone with an MBA can improve their skills, real progress necessitates knowing the motivations and goals behind their actions. While having an MBA may help with performance, sustained growth and sustainability in the profession necessitate ongoing competency development in digital healthcare. Hospital IT staff, administrators and other healthcare professionals need to work together to assess results effectively using HIT.

In conclusion, India’s HAs are leading the charge to operationalise the nation’s digital health agenda. Their changing responsibilities necessitate a blend of data-driven decision-making, policy understanding, digital competence and management savvy. For India to develop a healthcare leadership that is both technologically savvy and prepared for the future, it would be essential to include frameworks like HITCOMP into national training programmes and academic curriculum in addition to contextualising international models.
