Managing indirect disaster-related deaths after the 2025 Myanmar earthquake: A public health proposal
Authors: Motohiro Tsuboi, Hiroyuki Sasaki, Hyejeong Park, Phue Thet Khaing, Masatsugu Orui, Yasuto Kunii, Shinichi Egawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 2025 Myanmar earthquake caused extensive destruction. Beyond 3,800 direct disaster-related deaths (direct DRDs) and 5,100 injuries, indirect disaster-related deaths (indirect DRDs) remain a persistent concern. Indirect DRDs are deaths caused by secondary health effects after a disaster, rather than by the direct physical impact of the hazard itself. They may arise through disrupted healthcare delivery, deteriorating living and evacuation environments, interruptions to chronic-disease management, and psychosocial stress. Evidence from Japan, including the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake, shows that prolonged displacement and repeated disruptions in health and social systems can sustain mortality risk over time. Based on these insights, this Perspective synthesizes publicly available United Nations and humanitarian reports to identify risks of indirect DRDs in Myanmar. Months after the earthquake, many survivors remain in fragile shelters with degraded water, sanitation, and hygiene conditions. Health facilities were damaged or closed, healthcare access remains constrained, and vulnerable groups—including children, pregnant women, older adults, persons with disabilities, and patients with chronic diseases—face sustained risks. These conditions increase the likelihood of infectious disease outbreaks, chronic disease decompensation, heat illness, and psychosocial distress. Priorities include continuity of essential services, access to noncommunicable disease medicines, maternal–child health and psychosocial support, improved shelter and WASH conditions, standardized documentation of disaster relevance in medical records and death certification, and sustained, coordinated recovery. Framing indirect DRDs as preventable and measurable can strengthen protection of vulnerable groups, resilience, accountability, and dignity.

Significance for public health

Indirect disaster-related deaths (indirect DRDs), which are defined as deaths caused by secondary health effects arising after a disaster rather than by the direct physical impact of the hazard itself, are a substantial, preventable share of disaster mortality that can persist for months and years after onset. After the 2025 Myanmar earthquake, prolonged displacement, damaged or closed health facilities, workforce shortages, and large numbers of children, pregnant women, older adults, and persons with disabilities create sustained mortality risks. Treating indirect DRDs as measurable public health outcomes enables action across disaster phases: maintaining primary care and noncommunicable disease treatment, safeguarding maternal–child and mental health, improving shelter and water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) conditions, and prioritizing outreach to vulnerable populations. Standardized documentation of disaster relevance in medical records and death certification can strengthen surveillance, accountability, and cross-country comparability. This Perspective integrates evidence from Japan and other disasters to inform recovery strategies that reduce preventable mortality.

On March 28, 2025, a magnitude 7.7 earthquake (GLIDE#EQ-2025-000043-MMR) struck along the 1,200 km Sagaing Fault in central Myanmar, causing widespread and severe damage. 1 By April 25, 2025, four weeks after the event, the United Nations (UN) Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) reported 3,800 deaths, 5,100 injuries, and 116 people missing. 2 Humanitarian needs reached a record level: a cumulative total of about 20 million people—combining pre-existing needs with those arising after the earthquake—required some form of assistance, including 2 million in urgent need. 2 According to OCHA, the heavy monsoon rains that followed the earthquake further deteriorated living conditions and left many survivors in unstable environments, including tents and temporary shelters. 2 Such conditions raise serious concerns for long-term health impacts and highlight the importance of discussing disaster-related deaths (DRDs).

DRDs are broadly classified into direct DRDs and indirect DRDs. 3 Direct DRDs are deaths caused by the immediate physical effects of hazards, such as earthquakes, tsunamis, or radiation exposure following nuclear accidents. In contrast, this study defines indirect disaster-related deaths (indirect DRDs) as deaths caused by secondary health effects that arise after surviving the direct physical impact of the hazard itself, irrespective of hazard type. These deaths may occur through disrupted health, medical, and welfare services; deteriorating evacuation and living environments, including water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH); exacerbation of pre-existing vulnerabilities; and psychosocial impacts such as stress, isolation, grief, and mental health deterioration. This study conceptualizes these processes as a causal pathway from hazard → system disruption → risk mechanisms → health outcomes. Hazards can damage health and social systems, disrupt essential services, and deteriorate living environments. These disruptions generate interacting risk mechanisms—such as interruption of care, poor shelter and WASH conditions, worsening chronic disease or frailty, and psychosocial stress—which may ultimately lead to adverse health outcomes, including indirect DRDs. These pathways may emerge and persist across the disaster timeline, from the acute response phase (hours to days), through early recovery (days to weeks), to longer recovery (months to years). After the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake (GEJE), prolonged evacuation was associated with sustained indirect DRDs. In Fukushima Prefecture, repeated changes in evacuation environments caused by the combined impacts of the earthquake, tsunami, and nuclear disaster led to a unique mortality pattern, in which indirect DRDs (n=2,329) far outnumbered the direct DRDs (n=1,610) within the prefecture.3,5,6 Similarly, after the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake, an increasing trend in indirect DRDs was observed even six months after the disaster, particularly among older adults, and the number of indirect DRDs (n=431 as of Oct. 9, 2025, in Ishikawa Prefecture), exceeded the direct DRDs (n=228).6,7 These precedents indicate that indirect DRDs represent a persistent public health challenge for the affected people from the acute phase to the mid- and long-term after onset. Based on these insights, this perspective synthesizes publicly available UN and other humanitarian reports to identify risks of indirect DRDs in the current Myanmar context, serving as a secondary review rather than primary data work. However, peer-reviewed evidence on indirect DRDs remains limited and fragmented, particularly in low- and middle-income settings and in disasters where mortality surveillance systems are weak. This evidence gap itself underscores the need to synthesize available disaster, humanitarian, and public health information to clarify plausible pathways of delayed mortality and to inform future surveillance and prevention strategies.

System disruption

Environmental deterioration due to evacuation

As of August 2025, OCHA reported that, since the event, the cumulative number of people reached by humanitarian assistance was about 1.3 million, while shelter support had cumulatively reached about 357,000. 2 Earlier, as of June 2025, United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) reported 18,200 people across 103 displacement sites, and over 265,000 relyinsg on tents, relatives’ homes, or makeshift arrangements. 8 These conditions indicate that hundreds of thousands remain in fragile housing. With the onset of the monsoon season, further deterioration in WASH and an increased risk of infectious disease outbreaks are of concern. The World Health Organization (WHO) had warned of these environmental and sanitation risks. 9

Disrupted healthcare access

According to major international agencies, approximately 700 health facilities were affected, severely undermining health service delivery. 8 OCHA further noted that funding shortages forced the closure of at least 65 health facilities, depriving more than 212,000 people of access to medical care. 2 In addition, the number of healthcare workers was estimated to be fewer than 3 per 10,000 population after the earthquake—far below the WHO-recommended standard of 22.8 per 10,000. 10 Thus, damaged facilities, workforce shortages, and financial constraints have severely disrupted healthcare provision. Before the disaster, however, Myanmar lacked universal health coverage (UHC); the Social Security Board (SSB) scheme primarily covers formal-sector workers, leaving most people to pay out of pocket, which accounts for approximately 80% of current health expenditure. 11 This financial strain likely contributes to medication discontinuation and heightened risk of indirect DRDs among displaced populations. In addition to these structural disruptions, fragmented and uncoordinated response efforts across multiple actors may further reduce the effectiveness of health and humanitarian interventions. In Myanmar, pre-existing conflict, constrained humanitarian access, and reliance on multiple governmental, non-governmental, ethnic, and community-based actors may complicate coordination, information sharing, and continuity of care.

Risk mechanisms

Impacts on vulnerable groups

Table 1 summarizes the estimated affected populations, key health and social risks, and supporting data sources for major vulnerable groups after the 2025 Myanmar earthquake. UNICEF reported that the earthquake affected 58 townships, leaving about 6.3 million people—including nearly 2 million children—in urgent need of life-saving assistance, underscoring the need for health, education, and psychosocial support. 12 United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) further estimated that over 4.6 million women of reproductive age, including more than 220,000 pregnant women, live in the affected areas. 13 Moreover, older adults and persons with disabilities have faced specific vulnerabilities in the aftermath of the 2025 Myanmar earthquake. Needs assessment revealed that 86% of older adults reported at least one health condition, yet 50% lacked access to necessary medicines, and 20% had no access at all. Basic services were severely disrupted: 82% of older adults lacked adequate access to toilets, and many with mobility limitations faced significant barriers to sanitation and daily living. Only 11% possessed essential assistive devices, such as walking aids, leaving many excluded from support systems. 14 Although Myanmar-specific data on patients requiring life-sustaining therapies remain limited, evidence from previous disasters in Japan suggests that patients dependent on dialysis, home mechanical ventilation, home oxygen therapy, and continuous treatment for chronic conditions, such as diabetes, hypertension, and malignancies, may face life-threatening risks when medicines, electricity, transportation, or referral systems are disrupted. Evidence from Fukushima also suggests that people with pre-existing mental disorders may be particularly vulnerable during forced evacuation and repeated relocation. 15 Among psychiatric inpatients who were mandatorily evacuated after the Fukushima nuclear disaster, mortality increased markedly, and this excess mortality persisted during long-term follow-up.16,17 In addition, prolonged insecurity, displacement, and economic hardship may contribute to disaster-related suicide, as documented in Fukushima Prefecture following the nuclear accident. 18 Collectively, these factors illustrate how biological vulnerability and social disadvantage interact to amplify long-term mortality risks. In the Myanmar context, access to health services and humanitarian support may be unevenly distributed, often reflecting pre-existing socioeconomic inequalities that are further exacerbated after disasters. Unclear referral pathways and limited accessible reporting mechanisms may prevent affected populations from seeking or receiving appropriate care, even when services are partially available. Health outcome

Possibility of long-term increase in indirect DRDs

The current situation in Myanmar suggests that indirect DRD risk may persist well beyond the acute phase. Several months after the earthquake, many survivors remain in unstable living conditions, with continuing risks from overcrowded or fragile shelters, deteriorated WASH conditions, disrupted healthcare access, interrupted chronic-disease management, heat-related illness, and psychosocial distress. The World Bank’s Myanmar Economic Monitor (June 2025) estimated approximately USD 11 billion in damages (14% of gross domestic product (GDP)) and projected a 2.5% GDP contraction in FY2025/26, with poverty rising by nearly 3 percentage points. 19 This economic deterioration may further increase financial barriers to care, particularly in settings with high out-of-pocket health expenditures and no universal health coverage. Experience from large-scale disasters in Japan indicates that cumulative disruptions in healthcare, social support, and living conditions can contribute to delayed indirect DRDs. 5 Similar patterns have been observed elsewhere. After the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes, a retrospective observational study of non-traumatic patients found increased pneumonia admissions and pneumonia-related deaths compared with the previous year. Earthquake exposure was also associated with higher 3-week mortality (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.03–2.43) and 3-month mortality (OR 1.55, 95% CI 1.03–2.36), illustrating how health-system disruption and mass displacement can increase post-disaster mortality in a middle-income setting. 20 These findings suggest that indirect DRDs are a broader public health concern when disasters disrupt health systems, living conditions, and continuity of care. Japan’s experience further illustrates this risk. After the GEJE, 3,789 deaths were administratively certified as indirect DRDs in addition to approximately 18,500 direct deaths. 3 These certified cases indicate that health deterioration related to the disaster can lead to death through pathways other than the immediate physical impact of the hazard. After the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake, disaster-related deaths also continued to be certified for several months, particularly among older adults. Although Japan and Myanmar differ substantially in health financing, baseline morbidity, health-system capacity, access to care, population ageing, and data completeness, these examples show that deaths may occur not only from the direct impact of the earthquake but also through deteriorating living conditions, healthcare disruption, interruption of chronic-disease management, and reduced access to essential services. Reducing indirect DRDs therefore requires surveillance and interventions beyond the acute emergency response. Priorities include improving shelter and WASH conditions, maintaining access to primary care and essential medicines, supporting chronic-disease management, strengthening mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), and monitoring health deterioration among vulnerable populations over the medium and longer term. 21 International collaboration is also needed to protect vulnerable populations and mitigate longer-term indirect DRD risk in Myanmar. A remote-access qualitative study protocol investigating coping mindsets among working-age adults has been reported, and mapping cultural and spiritual practices and mutual aid may inform MHPSS design and community resilience in low-resource, low-access settings. 22

Documentation and surveillance of indirect DRDs

As shown in Supplementary Table 1, Japan, the United States, and Myanmar differ substantially in how they identify, document, and use information on indirect DRDs, particularly in legal frameworks, death certification, and statistical systems. In Japan, local governments certify DRDs upon applications from bereaved families, because certification is linked to disaster condolence grants. In the United States, CDC/NCHS guidance recommends documenting disaster-relatedness on death certificates using cause-of-death text and external cause codes. 23

Although no international standard currently exists for epidemiological statistics on indirect DRDs, Supplementary Table 1 underscores the importance of systematically documenting disaster relevance in death certificates and medical records. Publicly accepted statistics based on medical documentation would enable risk analysis, improve accountability, and support efforts to reduce preventable DRDs.

Recommendations

Action package to reduce indirect DRDs

To translate the conceptual framework into operational priorities, we propose a phase-based action package for reducing indirect DRDs after major disasters, aligned with the WHO Health Emergency and Disaster Risk Management Framework 24 and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030. 25 Both frameworks emphasize proactive risk reduction, preparedness for effective response, and “Build Back Better” in recovery, rehabilitation, and reconstruction. Myanmar is hazard-prone and faces high disaster risk. According to the INFORM disaster risk index, Myanmar’s overall disaster risk is 6.9, categorized as very high, with high scores for hazard and exposure, vulnerability, and lack of coping capacity.26,27 These conditions underscore the need to strengthen health-system resilience before, during, and after disasters. The action package organizes priorities across three practical time windows: the first 2 weeks, the first 3 months, and 3–12 months after the disaster. For each phase, Table 2 summarizes the main priorities, lead actors, minimum resources, implementation steps, and key dependencies. These time windows are guideposts rather than fixed thresholds and should be adapted to local epidemiology, security conditions, access constraints, community priorities, and recovery context. Across all phases, coordination among health, WASH, shelter, protection, and social welfare actors is essential. The core objective is to prevent secondary health impacts from progressing into avoidable deaths. Monitoring and evaluation framework

To support implementation and accountability, we propose a concise monitoring and evaluation (M&E) framework to track trends in indirect DRDs and related risk mechanisms. Key indicators should capture continuity of essential services and emerging health risks, including: (1) continuity of care for noncommunicable diseases, such as access to essential medications; (2) maternal and newborn health service coverage, such as antenatal care visits and skilled birth attendance; (3) WASH indicators, including access to safe water, sanitation, and hygiene facilities; (4) early signals of infectious disease outbreaks, such as syndromic surveillance alerts; (5) MHPSS services use; and (6) geographical, financial, and security-related access constraints. Data sources may include routine health facility reporting, disease surveillance systems, cluster reporting mechanisms, rapid needs assessments, and community-based reporting. Reporting frequency should be adapted to the disaster phase, with weekly reporting in acute and early recovery phases when feasible and monthly reporting during longer-term recovery. Monitoring should continue across phases because post-disaster data are often incomplete, delayed, and heterogeneous in quality. Systematic monitoring can identify gaps, guide adaptive responses, and reduce preventable mortality. Because this Perspective is based on publicly available humanitarian reports rather than individual-level mortality data, it cannot estimate the actual number of indirect DRDs in Myanmar. Nevertheless, converging evidence on displacement, health-service disruption, WASH deterioration, and vulnerable populations indicates a credible risk of delayed and preventable mortality.

Conclusion

Indirect DRDs are a preventable public health burden that can persist beyond the acute phase of a disaster. In Myanmar and similar settings, prolonged displacement, deteriorating WASH and shelter conditions, disrupted healthcare access, interrupted chronic-disease management, and psychosocial stress may increase delayed mortality risk, particularly among older adults, persons with disabilities, patients with chronic diseases, pregnant and postpartum women, children, and people with mental disorders. Reducing indirect DRDs requires sustained cross-sectoral action across disaster phases. Priorities include maintaining access to primary care and essential medicines, supporting maternal and child health, strengthening MHPSS, improving shelter and WASH conditions, and ensuring outreach to high-risk households. Donors and international partners should support acute relief and longer-term recovery, including bridge financing for local health staff, restoration of medicine and supply chains, reduction of out-of-pocket barriers, and community-based follow-up. Standardized documentation of disaster relevance in clinical records and death certificates should begin as early as possible and be integrated into routine reporting systems. Embedding indirect DRD monitoring into national disaster statistics could strengthen surveillance, accountability, and evidence-based recovery planning, thereby helping reduce preventable deaths after major disasters.
