Genetic Insight into Gorlin–Goltz Syndrome
Authors: M Madhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gorlin-Goltz syndrome, also called Basal cell nevus syndrome (BCNS), Nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (NBCCS), or Basal nevus cell carcinoma syndrome (BNCCS) is a rare, inherited, autosomal dominant genodermatoses, with variable expression and complete penetrance, characterized by the occurrence of multiple basal cell carcinomas (BCCs) at a young age, palmoplantar pits, keratocystic odontogenic tumors, intracranial ectopic calcifications, facial dysmorphism, and ocular and skeletal anomalies. It occurs due to a defective hedgehog cell signaling pathway, caused by heterozygous germ-line mutations in either Patched 1 (PTCH1), Suppressor of fused (SUFU), Smoothened (SMO), or Patched 2 (PTCH2) genes, leading to tumorigenesis and various developmental anomalies. Because of variable phenotypic expression, the syndrome is difficult to diagnose and is often diagnosed late, increasing the risk of morbidity and rarely mortality in the patients. The gene mutated also determines the phenotypic expression of the syndrome. Detection of the gene mutated plays an important role in, antenatal diagnosis, confirming the diagnosis when in doubt clinically and predictive diagnosis in family members of the affected individuals, which helps us to diagnose the syndrome early, hence screen for various clinical manifestations at the appropriate age according to the gene that is mutated and initiate early treatment to reduce morbidity and mortality.

Introduction

Gorlin-Goltz syndrome, also known as Basal cell nevus syndrome (BCNS), Nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (NBCCS), or Basal nevus cell carcinoma syndrome (BNCCS) is a rare, inherited, autosomal dominant genodermatoses, with variable expression and complete penetrance, characterized by the occurrence of multiple basal cell carcinomas (BCCs) at a young age, palmoplantar pits, keratocystic odontogenic tumors (KOTs), intracranial ectopic calcifications, facial dysmorphism, and ocular and skeletal anomalies. It occurs due to a defective hedgehog cell signaling pathway leading to tumorigenesis and various developmental anomalies.[1] While 70–80% of the cases are inherited, 20-30% of the cases are sporadic, with heterozygous germ-line mutation being in the Patched 1 (PTCH1) gene in around 85% of the cases and in Suppressor of fused (SUFU) gene in around 5% of the cases. Occasionally mutations in Patched 2 (PTCH2) or Smoothened (SMO) gene also lead to Gorlin–Goltz syndrome.[2]

History

The first evidence of Gorlin–Goltz syndrome dates back to Ancient Egypt, as two mummies recovered from that period show skeletal features like bifid ribs, jaw cysts, and short 4th metacarpals.[3]

In 1894, Jarisch and White described the first case exhibiting clinical manifestations resembling Gorlin–Goltz syndrome. Cutaneous manifestations of the syndrome like multiple BCCs and palmoplantar pits were first described by Politzer in 1905. But it came to be recognized as a syndrome in 1960 when Gorlin and Goltz found a consistent association of multiple BCCs, odontoid cysts, and bifid ribs in the patients.[4]

Epidemiology

Gorlin-Goltz syndrome has been reported from all over the world, in all ethnic groups with no sex predilection, with an incidence of 1 in 18,976 births.[5] The prevalence varies from 1 in 30,000 to 1 in 2,56,000 depending on ethnicity and environmental differences, being more in lighter pigmented individuals and those with increased exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light, like Americans and Europeans, while being less in darker pigmented individuals and those with less exposure to UV light, like Asians and Africans.[6] With its variable phenotypic expression, Gorlin-Goltz syndrome is often difficult to diagnose, and the cases are typically identified late, most commonly between 17-35 years of age.[5]

Knudson’s two-hit hypothesis and protective homozygosity:

Knudson’s two-hit hypothesis states that two events (mutations), one in each chromosome should occur for a cell to become malignant. Normally when a cell is homozygous, two mutations should occur, one in the allele of each homologous chromosome, for a cell to become malignant. But when the cell is heterozygous, only one mutation in the allele that is not mutated is enough to make it malignant. This shows that homozygosity is protective, which is lost in Gorlin-Goltz syndrome due to heterozygous germline mutation, making the patients prone to malignancies, as only one more sporadic mutation in the allele that is not mutated (second hit), either by exposure to ionizing radiation, UV exposure, aging, or any other factor is enough for malignant transformation to occur.[7]

Basis of Hedgehog cell signaling pathway:

The Hedgehog (Hh) cell signaling pathway plays a very important role in cell proliferation and differentiation, playing an important role in embryogenesis. The important poly-peptide ligand bringing about this action is called sonic hedgehog (SHH), which is a secretory, intra-cellular cell signaling molecule. SHH also plays a major role in embryogenesis and metamorphosis in Drosophila melanogaster fruit fly larvae. In the absence of a gene that codes for SHH, the larvae turn out to look like hedgehog with short spiny projections, so the name hedgehog is used for that secretary molecule and also the pathway it acts on.[8]

1. PTCH1:

PTCH1 coded by PTCH1 located on chromosome 9q22.3, is a trans-membrane glycoprotein that maintains G-protein coupled receptor SMO in an unphosphorylated/inactive state, which prevents activation and translocation of Glioma associated (GLI) family of transcription factors into the nucleus, preventing transcription of downstream target genes or activation of signal transduction cascade required for cell proliferation and differentiation. So PTCH1 acts as a tumor suppressor gene[9] [Chart 1].

Action of PTCH1 and SUFU

2. SUFU:

SUFU coded by SUFU located on chromosome 10q24.32, is a cytoplasmic complex that directly binds to GLI transcription factors, and prevents their activation and translocation into the nucleus. So SUFU acts as a tumor suppressor gene[10] [Chart 1].

3. SHH:

When SHH ligand binds to its primary receptor on PTCH1, the inhibitory effect of PTCH1 on SMO is lost, leading to phosphorylation/activation of SMO, which in turn activates GLI transcription factors, releasing them from the inhibitory effect of SUFU. The activated GLI transcription factors translocate into the nucleus and bring about transcription of downstream target genes or activation of signal transduction cascade required for cell proliferation and differentiation[10] [Chart 2].

Action of SHH and PTCH2

4. PTCH2:

PTCH2 coded by PTCH2 located on chromosome 1p34.1 is a trans-membrane glycoprotein homologous to PTCH1. There is an up-regulation of the PTCH2 that codes for PTCH2 by the signal transduction cascade initiated by binding SHH to PTCH1. Coded PTCH2 competes with PTCH1 for binding of SHH, resulting in more number of unbound PTCH1, which inhibits SMO. By this negative feedback mechanism, PTCH2 acts as a tumor suppressor gene[11] [Chart 2].

There should be a balance between SHH stimulating, and PTCH1, SUFU, and PTCH2 inhibiting cell proliferation and differentiation, to bring about controlled cell proliferation and differentiation required for normal embryogenesis and to prevent tumorigenesis.

Pathogenesis of Gorlin-Goltz syndrome:

Tumor suppressor genes PTCH1, SUFU, and PTCH2 undergo loss of function mutation in cases of Gorlin–Goltz syndrome, mainly by deletion or by extension, missense or nonsense mutations, resulting in loss of inhibition they had on Hh signaling pathway, leading to its sustained activity, that results in sustained cell proliferation and abnormal differentiation, resulting in tumorigenesis and defective embryogenesis. SMO undergoes activating mutation resulting in sustained Hh pathway activity.[12]

Genetic screening is the gold standard to confirm the diagnosis of Gorlin-Goltz syndrome. It’s done either by serial single-gene testing/multigene panel testing/genomic testing.

Genetics of Gorlin–Goltz syndrome guiding the treatment of Gorlin-Goltz syndrome malignancies and the other sporadic malignancies:

Defective Hh cell signaling pathway due to mutations in PTCH1/SUFU/SMO/PTCH2 in addition to Gorlin-Goltz syndrome, is involved in the pathogenesis of sporadic BCCs, medulloblastomas, and KOTs.[28] So any molecule that inhibits this aberrant Hh cell signaling pathway activity can be used not only in Gorlin-Goltz syndrome malignancies but also in sporadic BCCs, medulloblastomas, and KOTs.[28]

Small molecular substances like Vismodegib, Sonidegib, and Patidegib, which bind to and inhibit SMO, called hedgehog pathway inhibitors (HPIs) can be used in the treatment of Gorlin–Goltz syndrome malignancies and sporadic BCCs, medulloblastomas and KOTs.[29]

Vismodegib was FDA approved in 2012 to be used in patients with locally advanced BCCs, metastatic BCCs, and recurrent BCCs.[30]

The main concern with HPIs is the side effects. These include dysgeusia, diarrhea, amenorrhoea, hair loss, loss of weight, and muscle cramps, because of which the drug is often discontinued.[2930] To overcome these intermittent regimens are now being tried. In addition to that, HPIs only have tumor-suppressing activity and the tumors may recur after stopping the treatment.[2930] And also patients may have preexisting resistance to HPIs or they may develop resistance to HPIs after starting treatment with HPIs, due to point mutations in the inhibitory binding site of SMO, in which scenario response to treatment with HPIs is not seen.[2930]

Conclusion

Understanding the genetics of Gorlin-Goltz syndrome is important as it guides in, understanding the syndrome’s variable phenotypic expression, planning screening protocols to reduce morbidity, and treatment of syndrome-associated malignancies and sporadic BCCs, medulloblastomas, and KOTs.
