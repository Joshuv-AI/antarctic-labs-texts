Induction of Nephron–Ductal Dual Lineages via Early Retinoic Acid Signaling Establishes a Platform for Fully Patterned Kidney Organoids
Authors: Jung Hwa Lim, Seeun Kim, Dong Hyun Lee, Dae Hun Kim, Hye Bin Lee, Ye Seul Son, Mi‐Young Son, Soo Jin Oh, Ji‐Yoon Lee, Mi‐Ok Lee, Hyun Soo Cho, Dae‐Soo Kim, Cho‐Rok Jung, Hyun Mi Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKidney organoids are powerful tools for renal disease modeling and nephrotoxicity screening, yet their limited structural complexity—particularly the underdevelopment of ureteric bud (UB) lineages—remains a major limitation. A novel differentiation protocol is developed that short‐term activation of retinoic acid (RA) signaling during the early intermediate mesoderm (IM) stage, enabling co‐induction of anterior and posterior IM lineages. This eliminates the need for UB co‐culture and supports the formation of kidney organoids containing complete nephron segments. BMP7 treatment during the maturation phase mimics UB‐derived signals, further enhancing tubular maturation. Single‐cell transcriptomic analysis confirms that RA activation promotes dual IM induction at early stages, while BMP7 enhances organoid maturity. These dual IMs enable the generation of tubule and collecting duct organoids, which serve as segment‐specific models for renal reabsorption, efflux, and nephron‐targeted drug response assessment. Furthermore, the organoids are integrated into a networking cell culture system with enterocytes and hepatocytes, establishing a physiologically integrated model for systemic drug evaluation. This platform enables more accurate in vitro analysis of drug absorption, metabolism, and nephrotoxicity, including NSAID‐induced injury. Overall, this approach offers a robust, scalable, and physiologically relevant platform for next‐generation renal modeling and pharmacological research.Keywords: anterior and posterior intermediate mesoderm, bone morphogenetic protein 7, collecting duct, full segmented kidney organoid, retinoic acid

Introduction

The generation of kidney organoids from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) has opened new avenues for modeling renal development, investigating congenital and acquired kidney diseases, and screening for nephrotoxic compounds.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] Despite advances in differentiation protocols, kidney organoids remain limited in their capacity to fully recapitulate in vivo renal architecture and function. Major challenges include their small size, lack of vascularization, limited maturation, and crucially, the poor development of the ureteric bud (UB) lineage.[ 5 , 6 ] This deficit impairs the formation of collecting ducts (CD) and their integration with nephron segments, ultimately hindering the organoid's physiological relevance and utility.

In the developing embryo, the anterior and posterior intermediate mesoderm (IM), derived from the primitive streak (PS), give rise to the UB and metanephric mesenchyme (MM), respectively. These two progenitor populations interact through reciprocal signaling to drive nephron induction and branching morphogenesis, leading to a fully segmented kidney structure.[ 7 , 8 ] UB and MM become more mature through interactions with intrinsic factors and secreted signaling molecules in the developing kidney.[ 9 , 10 ] However, most current kidney organoid protocols exhibit an imbalance in lineage specification, with predominant MM‐like differentiation and limited UB formation, resulting in immature or disconnected tubular networks. This limitation stems in part from suboptimal timing and intensity of extrinsic signaling cues that fail to mimic the dynamic environment of embryonic kidney development.[ 11 ]

To overcome the challenges associated with kidney organoid patterning and lineage specification, several strategies have been explored. Co‐culture of separately differentiated UB‐ and MM‐like cells has enabled formation of more mature collecting duct structures,[ 9 , 12 , 13 ] but this approach requires labor‐intensive isolation and recombination steps, limiting scalability and reproducibility. Other studies have employed organoid assembloids incorporating stromal or endothelial components, or used microfluidic platforms to improve maturation.[ 14 ] Recent efforts have focused on inducing both UB and MM lineages within single‐organoid protocols by temporally staged delivery of morphogens such as WNT agonists, FGF9, and retinoic acid (RA),[ 15 ] although partial success has been achieved, challenges remain, including off‐target differentiation and inconsistent anterior–posterior patterning of the IM, which hinders coordinated lineage specification.

Retinoic acid (RA), derived from vitamin A, plays a crucial role in vertebrate embryonic development. Vitamin A deficiency can result in various developmental abnormalities affecting different systems.[ 16 ] In kidney development, RA is essential for regulating nephron branching and determining the final number of nephrons. RA signaling from the stromal mesenchyme is essential for UB branching and differentiation of anterior and posterior IM during kidney development.[ 17 , 18 , 19 ] However, prolonged or high‐dose RA exposure suppresses the differentiation of posterior IM and inhibits the formation of MM, including SIX2+ nephron progenitor cells. These dual effects necessitate precise spatiotemporal control of RA signaling to enable balanced anterior–posterior patterning.[ 7 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] Bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7) signaling is essential for both in vivo kidney development and in vitro kidney organogenesis. As mice lacking BMP7 exhibit dysplastic kidneys and die immediately after birth, BMP7 has been considered essential for normal kidney development. Moreover, BMP7 promotes the survival and growth of renal mesenchymal cells and plays a role in nephron formation, and supports the survival and renewal of nephron progenitor cells.[ 24 , 25 , 26 ]

In this study, we present a simplified, co‐culture‐free kidney organoid differentiation protocol that enables robust and reproducible generation of segmented nephron structures, including collecting ducts. By applying low‐dose RA in combination with FGF9 during early IM specification, we promote balanced anterior–posterior patterning and dual commitment to both UE and MM lineages. RA is withdrawn after short exposure to prevent inhibition of MM specification, while FGF9 is continued to support mesodermal differentiation. Subsequent BMP7 stimulation enhances the survival, expansion, and maturation of nephron progenitors, resulting in kidney organoids with improved structural integration and functional potential. This one‐pot strategy circumvents the need for complex recombination or organoid assembly steps and offers a scalable and efficient platform for modeling kidney development and disease.

Results

The Effects of RA on the Anterior and Posterior IM Differentiation Leading to Maturation of Kidney Organoids with Collecting Duct

To generate anterior (AIM) and posterior IM (PIM) simultaneously, which led to the generation of ureteric epithelium (UE) and MM, respectively, we examined the effects of RA on the differentiation of AIM and PIM (Figure 1a). PSCs were induced to differentiate into PS using 10 µM CHIR99021, resulting in the expression of PS markers and loss of pluripotency (Figure 1b).[ 23 ] Subsequently, different concentrations of RA were tested to determine the optimal condition for AIM and PIM differentiation. Preliminary experiments revealed that 1 µM RA promoted the differentiation of IM into kidney organoids (Figure S1a,b, Supporting Information). Therefore, an experiment was conducted to determine the effective concentration of RA below 1 µM (specifically 0.1 and 1 µM) for the differentiation of both AIM and PIM, as well as kidney organoids. (Figure 1c,d). The relative mRNA expression levels indicated that AIM and PIM differentiation was promoted in the RA‐treated cells compared with that in the untreated cells, and especially the expression of AIM markers, UB precursor increased significantly in the cells treated with 0.1 µM RA compared with that in cells untreated or treated with 1 µM RA (Figure 1c). FACS analysis showed OSR1 (representative IM marker), HOXD11 (PIM marker), EYA1 (PIM marker), LHX1 (AIM marker), and HOXB7 (AIM marker) showed significantly higher levels in the 0.1 µM RA‐treated cells than in untreated or 1 µM RA‐treated cells (Figure 1d). Kidney organoids generated using these IM cells showed tubule structures throughout the organoids, whereas tubular structures were found only in the peripheral side of organoids that were generated from IM without the RA (Figure 1e). Positive results were also obtained for multi‐lineage markers, such as podocin for podocytes, LTL for proximal tubules, PNA for loop of Henle and distal tubule, and DBA for collecting ducts. In particular, the collecting duct differentiation rate was relatively high in the 0.1 µM RA treatment group (Figure 1f,g). To optimize RA duration, cells were treated with 0.1 µM RA for 1–3 days (day 4–7). mRNA and protein analysis showed that 2‐day treatment was optimal for AIM and PIM induction (Figure S2a,b, Supporting Information). H&E and immunostaining confirmed kidney organoids from RA‐treated IM had more multi‐lineage tubule segments, with slightly greater collecting duct differentiation in the 2‐day 0.1 µM RA group (Figure S2c, Supporting Information).

scRNA‐seq was used to analyze the various cell types present in IM cells to evaluate whether activating the RA signaling pathway early in IM differentiation effectively promotes dual lineage IM differentiation. We generated a dataset representing 9167 cells from day 7 IM without RA (IM CTL) and 5336 cells from day 7 IM with RA signaling (IM with RA), and after filtering out the cells by quality control and correcting for batch effects, cells were visualized with a UMAP plot (Figure S3a–f, Supporting Information; Figure 2a). To identify the cell types in the RA‐treated intermediate mesoderm, single‐cell sequencing data from IM with RA were integrated and clustered together with the IM CTL. Eight cell clusters were identified, representing AIM, anterior patterning cells, PIM, posterior patterning cells, proliferating progenitor, pre‐tubular aggregate (PTA), endothelial cells (ENDO), and stress‐responsive state cells (SSC). We identified a cluster characterized by high HSPs and stress‐associated genes (JUN), likely representing a stress‐induced state rather than a defined lineage cell type. Notably, the RA‐treated condition showed increased cell density in both AIM and PIM clusters, with AIM cells detected only in the RA‐treated group, indicating a crucial role for RA in AIM specification (Figure 2a). Cluster identity was established by comparison of cluster marker genes to markers of known human kidney development cell types.[ 27 ] The violin plot result showed the expression of three different marker genes across these clusters (Figure 2b; Figure S4a, Supporting Information). Notably, cells within the AIM cluster demonstrated high expression levels of SIM1, GCH1, FOXC1, and ITGA6, in addition to PAX2, GATA3, and LHX1, further supporting the identification of this population as anterior intermediate mesoderm (Figure 2c; Figure S4b, Supporting Information). In the anterior patterning cluster, although the expression profiles of individual genes were partially overlapping with other clusters, we observed that the majority of key genes known to regulate anterior patterning—such as MEIS, PBX1, and anterior HOX genes—were highly expressed (Figure S4c. Supporting Information). The MEIS–PBX–HOX transcriptional complex plays a crucial role in kidney development by sustaining HOX gene expression, regulating mesenchyme–UB interactions, and promoting nephron patterning and segmentation.[ 28 ] Compared to RA untreated IMs (CTL IM), violin plots demonstrated that the expression levels of AIM, anterior patterning cells, PIM, and PTA‐specific genes were increased in IM with RA (Figure S4a, Supporting Information). These findings were associated with an increased proportion of AIM, anterior patterning cells, PIM, and PTA cells in the IM with RA (Figure 2d). PIM markers such as OSR1, EYA1, and PAX8 expressing cells were significantly increased in IM with RA compared to the IM CTL, and AIM markers such as LHX1, GATA3, and HOXB7 expressing cells were also considerably elevated in those cells compared to the IM CTL (Figure 2e). We also confirmed that the RA signaling pathway is activated in IM with RA signaling cells through the up‐ and down‐regulation of receptors and downstream targets (Figure S5, Supporting Information). These results suggest that short‐term treatment with 0.1 µM RA during early IM induction promotes both dual lineage IM differentiation, thereby enhancing the formation of segmented and mature kidney organoids containing nephron and collecting duct structures

BMP7 Enhances the Efficacy of Tubule Differentiation, Lumen Formation, and Tubule Polarity in Kidney Organoids

Incomplete polarization is a current limitation in kidney organoid development, leading to underdeveloped lumen and epithelium. To verify the effects of BMP7 on tubule maturity, a BMP7 treatment protocol was designed for a further maturation period (Figure 3a). Bright field, H&E, and immunohistochemical results showed that the BMP7‐treated kidney organoids had relatively high portions of tubule‐like structures and tubule segments compared to those of treated FGF9 alone (Figure 3b; Figure S6a, Supporting Information). Kidney organoids generated using BMP7 showed increased expression levels of genes related to various kidney tubule segments compared with the kidney organoids cultured without BMP7; the expression levels of most genes were similar to those in the human kidney (Figure 3c). In addition, enhanced polarization of the proximal tubule, with apical enrichment of the brush border marker LTL and primary cilia (acetylated tubulin), was observed in the kidney organoids cultured with BMP7 (Figure 3d). In addition, we performed co‐staining with representative markers of the proximal tubule, distal tubule, and collecting duct to verify whether the markers used in our research accurately represent kidney tubule segments. As a result, we confirmed that the markers used in this study were co‐expressed with established tubule markers, indicating their relevance in identifying specific kidney tubule structures. (Figure S6b, Supporting Information). Additionally, not only were all representative markers of the tubule segments detected within the kidney organoid (Figure 3e), but the vertical section also partially illustrated the sequential arrangement of the glomerulus, proximal convoluted tubule, Henle's loop, distal convoluted tubule, and collecting duct within the organoid. (Figure 3f). Transmission electron micrographs of the BMP7‐treated kidney organoids showed both brush border and primary cilia structures in the proximal tubule, podocyte foot process, and principal cells and intercalated cells in the collecting duct (Figure S6c, Supporting Information). Moreover, the advantage of the differentiation protocol developed in this study is that IM cells contain both AIM and PIM, allowing us to differentiate UB and MM at the same time. To further confirm the heterogeneity of IM cells, we conducted experiments to generate collecting duct organoids derived from UB and tubule organoids derived from the MM.[ 29 , 30 ] The differentiated organoids showed morphology similar to tubule organoids and collecting ducts, respectively (Figure 3g). Quantitative RT‐PCR analysis demonstrated dynamic changes in gene expression during differentiation. IM markers progressively decreased over time, whereas kidney tubule markers in tubule organoid and CD‐specific markers in CD organoids significantly increased (Figure 3h; Figure S7a,b, Supporting Information). The tubule organoid expressed tubule segment‐specific markers, LTL, AQP1, UMOD, and AQP2. In addition, the collecting duct organoids expressed DBA, AQP2, ATP6V1B1, and FOXI1 (Figure 3i).

Another limitation of PSC‐derived organoid differentiation is the lack of reproducibility and batch variation. To verify whether the developed protocol in this study could be applied to iPSCs from different cell origins, three different iPSCs reprogrammed from skin fibroblasts, PBMCs, and umbilical cord blood cells were induced to differentiate using the developed protocol. As a result, they showed similar differentiation patterns (Figure S8a, Supporting Information), differentiation efficiencies (Figure S8b, Supporting Information), and organoid maturation (Figure S8c, Supporting Information) to levels shown in ESCs. These findings indicate that the differentiation protocol developed in this study could produce kidney organoids with high reproducibility and maturity, with full segment tubule structures, including the collecting duct.

Dual Lineage of IM Differentiation and Tubule Cell Maturation, Including Collecting Duct Differentiation, is Significantly Dependent on the Activation of RA and BMP7 Signaling

To examine RA signaling in AIM and PIM differentiation, we blocked RA signaling using AGN193109. Treatment with AGN193109 dose‐dependently inhibited PS cell viability, reducing growth by 50% at 2 µM. Based on this, we used 1 µM AGN193109 in combination with 0.1 µM RA during IM differentiation to evaluate the direct effects of RA signaling. To assess RA signaling activation, we analyzed the expression of RA downstream target genes in IM cells from day 4–7. RA treatment transiently increased the expression of RA‐responsive markers on day 6, which declined following RA withdrawal. This upregulation was significantly suppressed by AGN193109, confirming the specificity of RA‐mediated transcriptional responses (Figure 4a; Figure S9a, Supporting Information). We further examined the role of RA in IM differentiation by analyzing HOX genes involved in early axial patterning and IM‐related markers. RA treatment upregulated anterior HOX genes such as HOXA4 and HOXB4 on day 6, while posterior HOX genes, including HOXA9, HOXA10, and HOXA11, were induced on day 7 after RA withdrawal. IM‐related markers also increased after day 6, and this pattern was attenuated by AGN193109 (Figure 4a). Consistently, protein levels of general IM, PIM, and AIM markers at day 7 were also reduced in AGN193109‐treated cells (Figure 4b). Moreover, RA inhibition impaired kidney organoid differentiation, notably reducing the development of podocytes and collecting duct (Figure 4c), supporting a critical role for RA in multi‐lineage kidney differentiation. To investigate the effect of RA timing on lineage specification, we compared continuous, transient, and no RA exposure in the presence of FGF9 after WNT activation. Transient RA treatment led to balanced induction of both UE (GATA3) and MM (SIX2, PAX2) markers, whereas prolonged RA favored UE differentiation while suppressing MM markers, as confirmed by qRT‐PCR and Western blot analyses (Figure S9b–d, Supporting Information). These results indicate that transient RA signaling promotes a more balanced induction of both UE and MM lineages during early kidney differentiation.

Next, we demonstrated the effect of BMP7 in kidney organoids differentiation and maturation by using LDN193189 to inhibit BMP7 signaling. Treatment with LDN193189 resulted in decreased phosphorylation of SMAD1/5/9, a downstream target of BMP7, compared to the untreated group. (Figure 4d). Gene expression analysis revealed a marked reduction in kidney segment‐specific mature markers in kidney organoids (Figure 4e) as well as early distal and collecting duct development markers (Figure S10a,b, Supporting Information) treated with LDN191389, highlighting a broad impairment in nephron differentiation and maturation. Tubular structure differentiation was impaired, with the podocytes and collecting duct most affected (Figure 4f; Figure S10c,d, Supporting Information). Proximal tubule maturation was also disrupted, as evidenced by reduced polarity and cilia formation (Figure 4g). These results support the position that RA signaling is critical for anterior IM differentiation and that BMP7 signaling affects tubule maturation and differentiation.

Integrated Single‐Cell Transcriptomic Analysis of Human Fetal Kidney and Kidney Organoid Reveals Nephron Lineage Convergence and Enhanced Collecting Duct Maturation in Organoids

scRNA‐seq was used to analyze cell types in kidney organoids, providing a detailed characterization beyond immunofluorescence. A dataset of 6476 cells from day 18 kidney organoids was generated. To facilitate direct comparison between organoid and human fetal kidneys, we combined our organoid single‐cell data with published human fetal kidney single‐cell data.[ 31 ] The human fetal kidney cells were obtained at 11 and 16 weeks of development, corresponding to the stages of initial nephron structure formation and active branching morphogenesis with ongoing nephron development, respectively. After quality control and batch effect correction (Figure S11a–d, Supporting Information), UMAP visualization identified fourteen clusters: interstitial cells (IC), podocyte precursor (PD precursor), podocyte (PD), polarizing podocyte (Polarizing PD), ureteric bud and collecting duct (UBCD), collecting duct (CDC), developing distal tubule (DDTC), distal tubule (DTC), developing MM (DMM), proximal tubule cells (PTC), mesangial cells (MSC), nephron progenitor cells/pre‐tubular aggregates (NPC/PTA), endothelial cells (ENDO), and leukocytes (Leuko)(Figure 5a; Figure S11e,f, Supporting Information). Among them, six clusters—IC, PD, DDTC, DT, PTC, and NPC/PTA—were commonly found in both organoid and fetal kidney, indicating shared nephron lineage populations. In contrast, four clusters—polarizing PD, DMM, Mesan, and CDC—were unique to the kidney organoid, while four clusters—PD precursor, UBCD, ENDO, and Leuko—were exclusive to the fetal kidney, reflecting developmental stage‐specific populations (Figure 5b; Figure S12a, Supporting Information). The violin plots were used to assess the identity of the nine clusters identified in the kidney organoid by showing the expression patterns of two distinct marker genes across the clusters (Figure S12b,c, Supporting Information). A polarizing PD cluster uniquely identified in kidney organoids co‐expressed canonical podocyte markers (MAFB, FOXC2, WT1, NPHS1, NPHS2, and PODXL) and polarity‐ and cytoskeleton‐related genes (PARD3B, NEBL, ACTR3, and DLG1) (Figure S12d, Supporting Information). This gene expression profile indicates a more mature podocyte state compared to fetal PD or precursors, suggesting the development of advanced cellular architecture, including foot process formation and functional polarization.

To further investigate the identity of the CDC cluster unique to the kidney organoid, we compared it to the UBCD cluster found only in the fetal kidney. Differential expression analysis of the gene set related to the development of the ureteric‐derived collecting system revealed that the organoid‐derived CDC cluster showed higher expression of markers associated with more mature collecting duct cells, including SCNN1A, KCNJ16, TMPRSS4, TFAP2B, and TFCP2L1, as well as RA signaling related genes (RARB, ETV4, and ETV5)(Figure 5c). These findings suggest that the kidney organoid contains collecting duct cells with a more advanced maturation state than those present in the fetal kidney at 16 weeks of gestation. To validate this observation, we further compared the transcriptomic profile of the organoid to bulk RNA‐seq data from human kidneys across developmental stages ranging from 7 weeks to school‐aged child. Heatmap analysis of tubular segment‐specific markers demonstrated that the organoid most closely resembled the expression pattern of the newborn kidney, with clear enrichment of proximal, distal tubule‐related genes as well as collecting duct‐specific genes (Figure 5d; Figure S13, Supporting Information). In addition, Key nephrotoxicity‐related transporter genes were predominantly expressed in the proximal tubule cluster of our kidney organoids, with several showing higher expression than in the fetal kidney, indicating a more advanced maturation state of this segment (Figure 5e). These data collectively indicate that the nephron segments, particularly the collecting duct lineage, in our kidney organoid model represent a relatively mature developmental state.

Functional Reabsorption and Efflux in a Polarized Renal Epithelial Model Derived From Kidney Organoids

Reliable in vitro kidney models require selective transporter activity and polarized reabsorption and efflux. While our kidney organoids exhibit mature segment identity, their inconsistent polarity limits accurate assessment of directional transport. To establish a polarized renal epithelial model, we cultured dual‐IM‐derived tubule organoids on Transwell inserts under air‐liquid interface (ALI) conditions, generating renal tubule epithelial cells from kidney organoids (KORTECs, Figure 6a). Over time, the cells underwent morphological changes and formed a dense epithelial monolayer by day 18 of ALI culture (Figure 6a). Histological analysis and immunofluorescence staining at ALI day 18 confirmed a well‐organized epithelial structure with tight junctions and expression of both basolateral and apical transporters (Figure 6b). Transcriptomic profiling revealed that KORTECs upregulated key transporter genes compared to the immortalized HK‐2 cell line. Both apical (e.g., P‐gp, PEPT1, OATs) and basolateral (e.g., OCTs, ENT1/2) transporters were enriched, indicating preserved segment identity and proper polarization (Figure 6c). To assess barrier function, we measured transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), which was significantly higher in KORTECs than HK‐2 cells, indicating superior barrier integrity (Figure 6d). FITC‐dextran leakage assays further demonstrated low paracellular permeability, supporting the formation of a tight epithelial barrier (Figure 6e). Functional reabsorption capacity was evaluated using an albumin uptake assay, which demonstrated significantly greater apical albumin uptake in KORTECs compared to HK‐2 cells. This uptake was mediated by megalin and cubilin, and the enhanced expression of CUBN in KORTECs further supported the observed reabsorptive activity. These results indicate that KORTECs possess more physiologically relevant endocytic and apical reabsorption functions than conventional proximal tubule cell lines. (Figure 6f,g). To assess selective excretory function, KORTECs were treated basolaterally with CDFDA. Upon cellular entry, CDFDA is hydrolyzed to CDF, a substrate of MRP2/4 efflux pumps. After 2 h, CDF was detected on the apical side, indicating active luminal efflux. This efflux was inhibited by the MRP2/4 inhibitor MK571, confirming functional MRP2/4‐mediated excretory activity (Figure 6h). To further evaluate drug excretion capacity, KORTECs were exposed basolaterally to alpelisib, a PI3K inhibitor used in breast cancer treatment (Figure 6i). After 48 h, alpelisib was detected on both basolateral and apical sides in a concentration‐dependent manner, suggesting active luminal drug efflux. These results demonstrate that KORTECs form a polarized renal epithelial monolayer capable of segment‐specific reabsorption and efflux, thus providing a robust in vitro model for studying renal transport functions, including nephrotoxicity assessment.

Accurate and Relevant Nephrotoxicity Assessment using Kidney Organoids by Networking Cell Culture System (NCCS)

To evaluate nephrotoxicity, kidney organoids were treated with known nephrotoxic agents, including cisplatin, cyclosporine A, and doxorubicin. Apoptosis analysis showed 75%, 56%, and 65% apoptotic cells following treatment with 25 µM cisplatin, 20 µM cyclosporine A, and 2.5 µM doxorubicin, respectively (Figure S14a, Supporting Information). Kidney injury markers (KIM1, LCN2, clusterin) (Figure S14b, Supporting Information) and apoptosis markers (Figure S14c, Supporting Information) were significantly upregulated in drug‐treated organoids. Gentamicin‐induced nephrotoxicity, which is associated with impaired urine concentration due to collecting duct dysfunction, was also investigated.[ 32 ] As our kidney model had segments of the renal tubule, including the collecting duct, we examined the nephrotoxicity events that occurred in the collecting duct using gentamicin. Immunostaining revealed pronounced cell death in the collecting duct, particularly in organoids derived from RA‐treated IM (Figure S14d, Supporting Information). Additionally, cisplatin treatment resulted in a concentration‐dependent increase in KIM‐1 expression (Figure S15a, Supporting Information), accompanied by a reduction in proximal tubule structures, increased cell death (Figure S15b, Supporting Information), and elevated mRNA expression of kidney injury markers (Figure S15c, Supporting Information).

Next, to evaluate the in vivo mimicking of nephrotoxicity, we applied the generated kidney organoids to a previously described networking cell culture system (NCCS),[ 33 ] that mimics the human oral drug processing axis by interconnecting intestinal (Caco‐2), hepatic (HepaRG), and renal (kidney organoid‐derived epithelium) models through a dynamic circulation platform (Figure 7a). Differentiated Caco‐2 and HepaRG cells were used for the absorption and metabolism models, and differentiated kidney organoids were applied to the NCCS for the toxicity model. We first validated the functionality of each component within the NCCS. Caco‐2 monolayers exhibited tight junction integrity, confirmed by TEER measurements (Figure 7b). Immunofluorescence analysis supported these tight junction integrity staining with tight junction protein (TJP1), occludin, and E‐cadherin (Figure 7c). HepaRG cells expressed hepatic‐specific markers with key phase I and II drug‐metabolizing enzymes (CYP3A4, CYP2E1), including APAP metabolism‐related enzymes, indicating preserved hepatic metabolic activity (Figure 7d,e). Nephrotoxicity was evaluated using non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) on the NCCS, as this environment is suitable for testing the toxicity of a metabolic product. APAP is a widely used NSAID, but its overdose induces liver damage owing to its toxic metabolite N‐acetyl‐p‐benzoquinoneimine (NAPQI) produced by the CYP2E1 enzyme and the GSH pathway, and plays a role in the elimination of NAPQI (Figure 7f). APAP concentration during the NCCS assessment is shown in Figure 7g. The levels of APAP‐GSH and 4‐aminophenol and APAP‐Cys were significantly increased in a time‐dependent manner in the medium collected from the NCCS (Figure 7h–j). Apoptosis and damage maker expression increased in kidney organoids within the full NCCS configuration compared to kidney organoids cultured alone (Figure 7k,l). Furthermore, cleaved‐caspase 3‐expressing cells were observed in the tubule segments and collecting ducts of the kidney organoids (Figure 7m). These results suggest that well‐differentiated and matured kidney organoids provide a more accurate and in vivo‐mimicking functional platform to assess renal toxicity in vitro.

Discussion

Recent improvements in the directed differentiation of PSCs to kidney organoids have created an advanced experimental platform for potential use in model human development, disease assessment, and drug toxicity tests.[ 34 , 35 ] However, challenges such as insufficient cell maturation and limited functional validation still hinder the physiological relevance of kidney organoids. Previous studies have attempted to overcome this by inducing UB progenitors via RA or co‐culturing with MM cells. Some used RA selectively for UB or MM differentiation, or in later maturation stages, while others applied RA during the IM phase but failed to yield fully developed kidney organoids.[ 14 , 15 , 25 , 30 ] In contrast, our study introduces a novel strategy to simultaneously induce efficient differentiation of both UB and MM lineages by optimizing RA treatment conditions. By carefully modulating the concentration and timing of RA exposure, we established a controlled differentiation environment that supports the concurrent development of UB and MM without compromising the efficiency of either lineage. This approach not only advances our understanding of kidney development but also provides a robust platform for generating kidney organoids with physiologically relevant segmental diversity. Our findings suggest that RA may play a more complex role in nephrogenesis than previously thought. Instead of acting as a binary switch between UE and MM fates, RA could function as a modulator that fine‐tunes the balance between these lineages depending on dosage, exposure duration, and the presence of other signaling factors. During kidney development, BMP7 signaling derived from the UB regulates the morphogenesis and structural development of the kidney by interacting with neighboring renal cells in the microenvironment.[ 26 , 36 ] We investigated the effects of BMP7 and its signaling on tubule maturation, with a focus on the polarization and development of the lumen in the kidney organoid. Furthermore, we confirmed that by inhibiting BMP7 signaling, not only did tubular cell maturation decrease, but also the differentiation rate of tubule segment differentiation. Furthermore, single‐cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that early RA treatment during IM differentiation influences not only AIM but also PIM differentiation as well as anterior‐posterior axial patterning. Direct comparison between fetal kidney and kidney organoids demonstrated that organoids generated using our protocol exhibit more mature collecting duct and glomerular cell populations than those found in 16‐week fetal kidneys. These findings support the conclusion that brief RA exposure during the early IM differentiation stage, combined with BMP7 treatment during organoid maturation, provides an effective strategy for generating kidney organoids with full segmental composition and enhanced cellular maturity.

Nephrotoxic drug effects on kidney organoids were evaluated, mimicking in vivo nephrotoxicity, resulting in an increased proportion of apoptotic cells in the kidney organoid. Moreover, gentamicin‐induced nephrotoxicity occurred in both the proximal tubule and collecting duct in our kidney model, which is similar to the events that occur in the body, indicating our kidney models provide a more realistic environment for assessing nephrotoxicity. Furthermore, utilizing the NCCS, we assessed the nephrotoxic effects of drug metabolites by mimicking drug absorption and metabolism in the body through networking cultivation with the intestine and liver. NSAID‐based drugs, such as acetaminophen, do not exhibit medicinal and toxic effects when present in a prodrug state but only when metabolized by liver enzymes. Most of the substances used in the previously known kidney toxicity model were usually directly nephrotoxic, such as cisplatin.[ 23 ] However, for prodrugs, the effects on the kidneys cannot be seen when treated as is, nor can the effects of the activated drug be directly confirmed when administered orally, and consequently, an in vivo mimic of the circulatory culture system is required.[ 33 ] Moreover, our study presents a platform utilizing dual‐lineage IM that enables the directed differentiation into both tubule organoids and collecting duct organoids. By leveraging this system, we not only established lineage‐specific organoids but also demonstrated functional validation of drug transport in the differentiated tubule organoids, including basolateral uptake and apical efflux. This highlights the potential of our model as a more accurate and physiologically relevant platform for nephrotoxicity assessment.

One of the limitations of the representative kidney organoid differentiation methods that have been developed to date is that they have off‐target cells, of which neuronal cells account for the majority. RA is well known as a factor that enables the differentiation of various cell lines, including neurons. Therefore, the use of a lower concentration of RA in kidney organoid differentiation would be advantageous in terms of off‐target effects.[ 37 ] However, in this study, early activation of RA signaling in the IM stage not only promoted the simultaneous differentiation of both AIM and PIM through anterior–posterior axis patterning, but also led to more mature expression patterns of tubule segment‐specific genes compared to fetal kidney when the cells were further differentiated into kidney organoids. These findings suggest that, rather than causing off‐target effects, RA activation provides an effective strategy for generating more mature kidney organoids by enhancing coordinated dual‐lineage IM differentiation.

In conclusion, we established an efficient and simplified method for generating fully differentiated kidney organoids using RA and BMP7 signaling. This protocol holds great potential for disease modeling and offers a valuable platform for accurate nephrotoxicity assessment. Beyond reducing the time required for differentiation, our in vivo–mimicking approach recapitulates diverse kidney segments and reflects the predominant features of various kidney diseases.

Experimental Section

Cell Culture and IM Differentiation

hPSCs, including H9 human embryonic stem cells (WiCell WA09, WiCell Research Institute, Madison, WI, USA) and human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), were maintained as previously described.[ 38 , 39 ] hiPSCs were reprogrammed using episomal vectors from fibroblasts (CRL2097; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA)[ 38 ] and peripheral blood mononuclear cells.[ 40 ] iPSCs derived from umbilical cord blood were provided by Korea National Stem Cell Bank, originally provided by Catholic University. This study was approved by the Korean Public IRB (IRB number: P01‐201409‐ES‐01‐09, P01‐201609‐31‐002). For IM differentiation, hPSCs were seeded onto matrigel‐coated flasks and cultured in mTeSR1 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). At 30–40% confluency (approximately day 2–3), cells were induced with primitive streak (PS) differentiation medium composed of basal medium (advanced RPMI 1640 (Invitrogen), 1× B27 supplement (Invitrogen), 1× NEAA, 1× GlutaMAX, and penicillin‐streptomycin), and 10 µM CHIR99021 (Tocris) for 4 days. From day 4 to day 7, PS cells were treated with 100 ng mL−1 FGF9 (Peprotech), 1 µg mL−1 heparin (Sigma–Aldrich), and various concentrations of retinoic acid (RA; 0, 0.1, and 1 µM for 3 days) or treatment durations (0.1 µM for 1–3 days) in basal medium.

Generation of Mature Kidney Organoids

IM cells were dissociated using Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies), and 1 × 10⁵ cells per well were seeded into ultra‐low attachment 96‐well plates (Corning), followed by centrifugation. Aggregates were cultured in basal differentiation medium supplemented with 100 ng mL−1 FGF9, 1 µg mL−1 heparin, and 5 µM Y‐27632 (Tocris) for 1 day. After incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2, 5–6 aggregates were transferred onto collagen‐coated Transwell inserts (#3491, Corning), treated with 5 µM CHIR99021 for 1 h, and then maintained in basal medium containing 100 ng mL−1 FGF9 and 100 ng mL−1 BMP7 (Peprotech) for 10–14 days. Medium was replaced daily, avoiding overflow across the insert membrane.

Generation of Tubule, Collecting Duct Organoids and Renal Tubule Epithelial Cells Derived From Kidney Organoid (KORTECs)

To generate tubule organoids, differentiated IM cells were embedded in Matrigel (Corning) and cultured in advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1.5% B27, 1 mM N‐acetylcysteine (Sigma–Aldrich), 5 µM A8301 (Tocris), 100 ng mL−1 R‐spondin1 (Peprotech), 50 ng mL−1 FGF10 (Peprotech), 50 ng mL−1 EGF (Peprotech), 50 ng mL−1 BMP7, and 10 µM Y‐27632. Medium was changed every 2 days. Tubule‐like structures appeared within 7–14 days, and organoids were passaged weekly. For the generation of renal tubule epithelial cells derived from kidney organoids (KORTECs), ≈250 000 cells from dissociated tubule organoids were seeded onto PET membrane Transwell inserts (24‐well format, #912612, SPL Life Sciences) and cultured in tubule organoid medium. Upon reaching confluency, the apical medium was removed, and KORTECs were cultured under air‐liquid interface (ALI) conditions to induce epithelial polarization. Care was taken to prevent overflow of the medium above the membrane. To generate collecting duct organoids, IM‐derived cells were embedded in Matrigel and cultured in advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1.5% B27, 1 mM N‐acetylcysteine, 1× ITS (Gibco), 200 nM LDN193189 (Tocris), 3 µM CHIR99021, 5 µM SB202190 (Tocris), 0.2 µM A8301, 100 ng mL−1 R‐spondin1, 50 ng mL−1 FGF7 (Peprotech), 50 ng mL−1 EGF, 50 ng mL−1 GDNF (Peprotech), 0.1 µM RA, and 10 µM Y‐27632. Medium was replaced every 2 days, and organoid morphology developed within 14–21 days.

Statistical Analysis

All data show representative data of more than three independent replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 7.05; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
