Outpatients' Readiness for Engagement in Health Information Technology: Implications for Patient Education in University Hospitals of Southern Iran
Authors: Effat Abbasi Montazeri, Javad Zarei, Bahareh Ghavami Hoseinpour, Mohammad Afkandeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSuccessful implementation of health information technology (HIT) depends largely on patients' readiness to engage with digital systems. Patient readiness encompasses multiple dimensions, including technological skills, communication preferences, information needs, and privacy concerns. Despite the rapid expansion of digital health services, limited evidence exists regarding outpatients' readiness for engagement in HIT in developing healthcare settings, particularly in university hospitals in Iran. This study aimed to assess outpatients' readiness for engagement in health information technology and examine differences across readiness dimensions.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted in 2024 among 400 adult outpatients attending specialty and subspecialty clinics at academic hospitals affiliated with Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences using stratified random sampling. Readiness for engagement in health information technology was assessed using the 28‐item Patient Readiness to Engage in Health Information Technology (PRE‐HIT) questionnaire, which measures eight dimensions: Health Information Need (HIN), Computer Anxiety (CA), Computer/Internet Experience and Expertise (CIEE), Non‐Face‐to‐Face Communication Preference (NFCP), Awareness of Health Information (NNGN), Relationship with Doctor (RD), Cell Phone Expertise (CPE), and Internet Privacy Concern (IPC). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, one‐sample t‐tests, and the Friedman test.ResultsOverall readiness for engagement in HIT was favorable. The highest mean score was observed for Non‐Face‐to‐Face Communication Preference (M = 3.72, SD = 0.72), whereas the lowest score was related to Computer Anxiety (M = 2.68, SD = 0.65). All dimensions except Computer Anxiety were significantly above the neutral midpoint (p < 0.05). Significant differences were observed among the eight dimensions (χ2 = 513.50, p < 0.001).ConclusionThis study highlights the high readiness of outpatients to engage with digital health technologies, particularly mobile health tools and non‐face‐to‐face communication. However, the findings also underscore significant challenges, including computer anxiety and privacy concerns, which may hinder full adoption. To improve patient engagement, digital health interventions should prioritize user‐friendly designs, clear educational resources to reduce anxiety, and robust privacy safeguards. These insights are critical for designing patient‐centered digital health strategies that enhance both accessibility and trust in HIT systems.

Introduction

Digital transformation is redefining healthcare through Health Information Technologies (HITs), which serve as the backbone of eHealth to promote global well‐being and reduce costs [1]. Collectively, these digital advances ranging from electronic prescribing to telemedicine offer transformative benefits such as improved provider‐patient communication, enhanced accessibility, and the promotion of health equity [2, 3, 4, 5]. However, the success of these systems in patient‐centered models depends heavily on patient readiness, a multidimensional construct encompassing the technological skills, confidence, and motivational factors that determine an individual's ability to use digital resources [6]. Ultimately, ensuring this readiness is a critical priority for healthcare governance, as even the most sophisticated HITs fail to achieve their full potential without the active engagement and preparedness of the end‐users [7].

Despite the recognized importance of user involvement, translating these principles of readiness and engagement into operational practice remains a significant challenge. Implementing specific patient and family engagement strategies such as establishing clear expectations and involving users in governance is essential to bridge this gap and reduce technological inequities [8]. Furthermore, understanding patient preferences and integrating them into policy decisions is critical for assessing needs and defining priorities [9]. In the Iranian context, however, a critical analysis of 13 national policy documents from 2004 to 2020 revealed that these strategic ideals have not been fully realized. The analysis identified systemic failures, including a lack of long‐term strategic planning, poor coherence between policies, and a disproportionate focus on Electronic Health Records (EHRs) instead of holistic patient engagement [10]. These findings highlight a documented misalignment between high‐level policy goals and the actual readiness levels of the population, leaving a gap in how national strategies translate to individual patient preparedness.

The successful adoption of these eHealth services is influenced by a complex array of factors, ranging from user digital literacy to technical usability. Challenges such as low digital literacy and poor acceptance of information technology continue to hinder engagement, particularly among vulnerable populations where disparities in digital access may exacerbate existing health inequities [5, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Despite the importance of examining patients' familiarity and acceptance as primary end‐users of HIT, most Iranian studies have disproportionately focused on healthcare providers, such as physicians and nurses [18, 19, 20, 21], leaving a significant research gap regarding the readiness of outpatients. To address these gaps, this study investigates outpatient readiness for digital health engagement in Iran. Specifically, we sought to answer the following research question: “What is the current level of outpatient readiness for HIT across its multiple dimensions, and how do demographic characteristics influence this readiness?” We hypothesized that while general interest in digital services might be high, significant gaps in technical skills and infrastructure access would persist among different outpatient groups.

Methods

Study Design

This descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted in 2024 at the six academic hospitals affiliated with Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, including specialty and subspecialty clinics. The total number of outpatient visits during the third quarter of 2024 was estimated based on monthly outpatient statistics from the previous 2 years (2022–2023), provided by the Statistics and Medical Records Office of the university's Vice‐Chancellor for Treatment.

Setting and Participants

The study population consisted of adult outpatients attending specialty and subspecialty clinics at the university hospitals during the third quarter of 2024. Monthly outpatient statistics from the previous 2 years (2022–2023) were obtained from the Statistics and Medical Records Office of the university's Vice‐Chancellor for Treatment to estimate the total outpatient population. Based on these records, the minimum required sample size was calculated using the standard formula for estimating a proportion in cross‐sectional studies: Z2⋅p⋅(1−p)d2=n where Z = 1.96, corresponds to a 95% confidence level, p = 05 represents the expected proportion of outpatient readiness (assuming maximum variability), and d = 0.05 indicates the margin of error. Based on this calculation, the required sample size was 384 participants. To account for potential non‐response or incomplete questionnaires, 400 patients were recruited. A stratified random sampling method was applied. The sample was proportionally allocated according to the average monthly outpatient volume of each hospital. In addition, gender distribution was considered during sampling to ensure proportional representation of male and female patients according to their actual attendance rates in the outpatient clinics. Regarding marital status, participants were categorized as either “married” or “single”; the latter category was inclusive of individuals who were never married, divorced, or widowed.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants were eligible if they were aged 18–60 years, able to read and write in Persian, and provided written informed consent. Individuals with a professional or academic background in information technology were excluded to avoid potential bias, as their advanced digital literacy and technical expertise could artificially inflate readiness scores and limit the generalizability of findings to the general outpatient population. Patients with severe disease‐related complications that impaired their ability to complete the questionnaire were also excluded. Patients who declined participation or submitted incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis.

Instrument

Data were collected using the Patient Readiness for Health Information Technology (PRE‐HIT) questionnaire, a 28‐item instrument originally developed by Koopman et al. in 2014 to assess patients' readiness to engage with health information technologies [22]. The PRE‐HIT evaluates eight dimensions: Health Information Need (HIN), Computer Anxiety (CA), Computer/Internet Experience and Expertise (CIEE), Non‐Face‐to‐Face Communication Preference, Awareness of Health Information (NNGN), Relationship with Doctor (RD), Cell Phone Expertise (CPE), and Internet Privacy Concern (IPC).

Each item is rated on a four‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), yielding a total possible score ranging from 28 to 112, with higher scores indicating greater readiness for engagement with health information technologies.

The psychometric properties of the instrument have been established in international settings. Koopman et al. reported acceptable test–retest reliability coefficients ranging from r = 0.60 to 0.85 [22].

The PRE‐HIT questionnaire was culturally adapted and psychometrically validated for the Iranian population by Safdari et al. [23]. Their validation study reported adequate content validity (CVI > 0.49), satisfactory face validity (FVI > 0.79), and acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.729). These psychometric properties confirm the suitability of the Persian version for assessing outpatient readiness in the Iranian healthcare context, ensuring the methodological robustness of the current study.

Variables

The primary study variables focus on patient engagement readiness, which is operationally defined through the eight dimensions of the Patient Readiness for Health Information Technology (PRE‐HIT) questionnaire. In this study, patient engagement represents the multidimensional capacity of patients to interact with and utilize HIT tools. Each variable was operationally defined as the mean score derived from the corresponding items within each subscale, where higher scores indicate greater levels of readiness and engagement in that specific domain. The study variables were defined as follows [23].

Health Information Need (HIN)

Operationally defined as the mean score of items assessing the participant's recognition that their current knowledge is inadequate to achieve personal health goals within a specific context or situation. Higher scores indicate greater perceived need for health information.

Computer Anxiety (CA)

Operationally defined as the mean score of items measuring fear, worry, or discomfort associated with the inability to use computer technology effectively or concern that computer use may cause errors or damage. Higher scores indicate greater computer‐related anxiety.

Computer/Internet Experience and Expertise (CIEE)

Operationally defined as the mean score of items evaluating participants' prior experience, familiarity, and perceived competence in using computers and the internet, including ability to solve usage problems. Higher scores reflect higher experience and technical expertise.

Preferred Mode of Interaction (PMI)

Operationally defined as the mean score of items assessing participants' preferred ways of contacting physicians, seeking health information, and selecting trusted sources. Higher scores indicate stronger preference for non‐face‐to‐face or electronic modes of interaction.

No News Is Good News (NNGN)

Operationally defined as the mean score of items evaluating participants' attitude toward seeking health information online, including recognition that excessive information may be overwhelming or distressing. Higher scores reflect greater caution in information‐seeking.

Relationship With Doctor (RD)

Operationally defined as the mean score of items assessing participants’ trust in their physicians as reliable sources of health information and their confidence in the physician's ability to manage personal health. Higher scores indicate stronger trust and better perceived relationship.

Cell Phone Expertise (CPE)

Operationally defined as the mean score of items assessing participants’ ability and confidence in using cell phones for online activities, texting, and accessing digital health services. Higher scores indicate greater mobile phone competence.

Internet Privacy Concern (IPC)

Operationally defined as the mean score of items measuring concern about personal information transmitted over the internet potentially being accessed by unauthorized third parties. Higher scores reflect greater privacy concern.

Data Collection Procedure

Following hospital approvals, the research team visited specialty and subspecialty clinics. Eligible patients were invited to participate, provided with study information, and asked to complete the PRE‐HIT questionnaire on‐site. The researcher remained present to ensure completeness and address any missing responses, offering assistance as needed to clarify questions and ensure accurate data collection. This on‐site supervision helped ensure complete and accurate responses, reducing the likelihood of missing data.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (mean, SD, skewness, and kurtosis) to summarize the central tendency and distribution of the variables. Following standard statistical procedures [24], inferential statistics included prespecified one‐sample t‐tests to compare sample means against the neutral midpoint of 3. Additionally, exploratory Friedman tests were used to assess differences across multiple dimensions. To further strengthen the analysis, effect sizes were calculated: Cohen's d for t‐tests (interpreted as small = 0.2, medium = 0.5, large = 0.8) and Kendall's W for the Friedman test (interpreted as small = 0.1, medium = 0.3, large = 0.5). Following a significant Friedman test, post‐hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted significance level = 0.0003). Statistical significance for all tests was set at p < 0.01, and all analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess outpatient readiness for engagement with digital health technologies in university hospitals of southern Iran. The findings demonstrate that outpatients in Ahvaz exhibit a relatively high overall readiness for health information technologies (HITs); however, this readiness is multifaceted and necessitates targeted facilitation across several key dimensions, including health information needs, computer anxiety, internet experience, preference for non‐face‐to‐face communication, information awareness, trust in physicians, mobile expertise, and privacy concerns.

In light of these dimensions, a primary area of readiness was the information‐seeking behavior; patients expressed a significant interest in searching for disease‐related information, symptoms, and treatment options online. This aligns with existing evidence that considers digital health literacy an empowering factor, particularly in resource‐limited environments [25]. In comparison, Casilang et al. (2020) reported similar mobile and internet health‐seeking patterns in the Dominican Republic, suggesting a global trend in low‐ to middle‐income contexts [26]. A possible explanation for this high motivation in our study is the deeper cognitive shift toward patient autonomy and the increasing ubiquity of digital tools in daily Iranian life. Consequently, the implication is that HIT design should move beyond passive education toward active digital interventions that channel this intrinsic motivation into physician‐monitored pathways to mitigate the risks of misinformation.

Parallel to this proactive searching behavior, the emotional response to technology was equally encouraging. Technology anxiety in our population was notably low, with average scores significantly below the theoretical midpoint, indicating that HITs are perceived as a low‐stress, routine process. This challenges prevalent stereotypes of “digital fear” in older or less‐educated populations and instead links low anxiety to the functional self‐efficacy generated by regular digital exposure. While recent studies have highlighted the role of well‐designed applications in reducing user stress [27], Onyeaka et al. (2020) caution that such results may not be universal if user‐centered interfaces or technical support are absent [28]. The observed low anxiety in our study may be explained by the high penetration of social media and mobile apps in Iran, which has normalized digital interactions for the average user. Therefore, HIT developers must maintain this low‐stress environment by ensuring that intuitive design and technical support are integrated to prevent reactive anxiety during potential system failures.

Such low levels of anxiety are arguably underpinned by the patients' substantial internet experience and skills. In this dimension, patients demonstrated high experiential proficiency, exceeding the theoretical midpoint in tasks such as navigation, email use, and data access. This corresponds with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which views technical skill as a psychological buffer against technology resistance [29]. Similarly, De las Cuevas (2024) linked higher digital literacy to increased participation in treatment decisions, consistent with patient engagement models that view skills as facilitators of self‐management [30]. However, we identified a critical gap: unlike some global models, there remains a lack of formal digital health education in the studied region. This suggests that while informal skills acquired through everyday devices are high, the absence of structured training may disenfranchise vulnerable subgroups. Thus, healthcare providers should integrate formal digital training into routine care to ensure equitable access to HIT resources.

This technical proficiency, combined with low anxiety, appears to have translated into a distinct preference for modernized communication. Patients in this study showed a clear preference for non‐face‐to‐face channels, reflecting a paradigm shift in traditional healthcare delivery. According to Media Richness Theory, the transition toward digital feedback reflects a prioritization of efficiency and autonomy over traditional synchronous methods [31]. For instance, studies in sensitive care areas, such as HIV management, have shown a preference for SMS due to its privacy‐preserving nature [32]. The explanation for this preference in our study likely stems from the desire for better time management and reduced travel burdens in a crowded hospital setting. The implication is that HIT implementation must strike a meticulous balance: providing automated efficiency while maintaining the essential human connection to ensure care remains personalized and empathetic.

Beyond communication preferences, health information awareness emerged as a significant driver of readiness. Our findings show that awareness was significantly above the midpoint, driven by active patient monitoring. This aligns with self‐monitoring models that view such behaviors as adaptive; however, the sensitivity of health information remains a “double‐edged sword.” While active searching can improve outcomes, overload or conflicting sources can generate secondary anxiety [33]. A possible explanation for the high awareness in this study is the increased access to health‐related content on social platforms in Iran. Consequently, HITs should not only provide information but also facilitate choice and filter content to prevent information fatigue, particularly in environments with limited professional guidance.

Directly linked to information management is the dimension of trust and the provider relationship. The findings indicate that the relationship with the doctor remains the primary priority for patients, who view physicians as the trusted reference point amidst digital streams. This is consistent with established trust models in healthcare and the work of Tartaglia et al. (2024), who emphasize that high digital literacy does not replace provider trust but complements it [34]. From a critical perspective, if HITs are implemented in a way that diminishes the physician's role, it could negatively affect the continuity of care [3]. Therefore, digital tools must be designed as collaborative aids that strengthen, rather than replace, the physician‐patient bond.

The practical application of this trust increasingly occurs through mobile platforms, which served as ubiquitous health assistants in our study. This prominent mobile experience corresponds with the Diffusion of Innovations theory, highlighting the relative advantages and compatibility of smartphones [35]. However, in comparison, Guracho et al. (2024) uncovered significant intention‐behavior gaps in other contexts (e.g., Ethiopia), which reflects potential mismatches also possible in Iranian settings due to unsupported interfaces or privacy concerns [35]. The explanation for this gap may lie in the difference between having access and having “supportive infrastructure.” This highlights that structural changes in health system architecture are necessary beyond just device availability to enable effective adoption.

Despite this mobile ubiquity, the strategic bottleneck remains privacy concerns. Even with overall positive readiness, privacy continues to be a significant barrier. Privacy calculus theory explains this, suggesting that patients weigh the clinical benefits of HITs against perceived risks to their data. This finding aligns with Alhammad (2024), who noted that weak privacy frameworks could hinder usage [36]. Furthermore, as emphasized by Zhou and Parmanto (2020), increasing awareness without appropriate privacy frameworks can be counterproductive, escalating anxiety and distrust [37]. In the Iranian context, these concerns suggest that digital trust is dependent not just on data transparency but also on technical and ethical assurances. Consequently, policy‐makers must prioritize the development of robust data protection protocols to secure long‐term patient engagement in digital health ecosystems.

Privacy Calculus Theory explains this, as patients constantly weigh clinical benefits against perceived data risks. This aligns with stakeholder perspectives emphasizing that digital trust is not a static byproduct of technology but a dynamic quality requiring technical and ethical assurances [38].

Implications for Practice and Policy

The findings underscore the importance of integrating HITs in a way that balances automation with human connection. Behavioral readiness suggests a strong intrinsic motivation among patients, which should be guided through active digital interventions. Technological skills indicate that infrastructure and formal training can harness patient capabilities. Addressing psychological and privacy concerns is essential to maintaining trust, engagement, and equitable access. Facility‐based sampling, however, may limit the generalizability of these findings to the broader community, and policymakers should consider the potential underestimation of readiness when designing national or community‐based programs.

Collectively, these insights can inform the design of patient‐centered digital health programs, targeted training initiatives, and policies that enhance adoption while safeguarding privacy and promoting digital equity in Iranian healthcare. However, to ensure these interventions are effective across diverse population segments, future research should aim to include more representative sampling from community‐based settings and explore the disparities in readiness between different demographic and geographic groups.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into outpatient readiness for health information technologies (HITs) in the context of academic hospitals, there are notable limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings.

Firstly, the facility‐based sampling method used in this study likely underestimates community‐level readiness for HITs. Since the sample was restricted to patients attending specialty and subspecialty clinics at academic hospitals, it does not fully capture the broader population of outpatient users, especially those who do not engage with academic healthcare facilities. As a result, the findings may not accurately reflect the readiness of the general public or community‐based patients, who may have different levels of engagement with HITs due to varying levels of access, health literacy, or digital infrastructure. This limitation potentially biases the prevalence of readiness downward, as individuals with less access to healthcare services might demonstrate lower readiness levels. Moreover, this sampling method could also affect the generalizability of the results, limiting their applicability to more diverse healthcare settings, including rural or resource‐constrained environments where HIT adoption might differ significantly.

Secondly, the generalizability of the results may be impacted by the overrepresentation of specific demographic groups (e.g., patients attending academic hospitals), which may not be reflective of the broader population. This sampling bias should be carefully considered when applying the findings to national or community‐wide digital health initiatives.

Finaly, specific technological and digital interaction factors, such as app usability complexity or stable internet connectivity, were not directly assessed, potentially influencing patients' real‐world experiences. These contextual factors are critical for actual adoption but were beyond the scope of this study.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that outpatients demonstrate a substantial level of interest and readiness to engage with digital health technologies, as reflected by their responses relative to the neutral midpoint of the measurement scale. Participants showed motivation to access health information, manage their time efficiently, and apply their digital skills effectively. However, concerns regarding data privacy and the preservation of human connection remain notable barriers that can influence acceptance and overall experience. These results emphasize that technology alone is insufficient; successful adoption of digital health tools requires integration with patient education, physician support, user‐centered design, and robust data protection measures. By aligning technological innovation with human‐centered practices, digital health systems can empower patients while maintaining trust, security, and personalized care. In the context of increasing adoption of digital health systems in hospital settings, understanding patients' perceptions and readiness is essential to achieving sustainable and equitable implementation.

Implications for Practice and Education

The findings of this study have important implications for the design and implementation of digital health interventions in hospital settings. Patients demonstrated a high level of willingness and readiness to use digital health technologies, while also emphasizing the importance of maintaining a human connection with healthcare providers.
