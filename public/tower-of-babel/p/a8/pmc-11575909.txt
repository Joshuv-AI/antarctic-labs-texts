Patient Enrollment to Industry-Sponsored Versus Federally-Sponsored Cancer Clinical Trials
Authors: Joseph M. Unger, Hong Xiao, Riha Vaidya, Michael LeBlanc
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThe conduct of cancer clinical research in the United States is supported by both private and public sponsors. Industry aims to obtain new drug approvals. Federally-sponsored trials examine a broad set of research questions that are not typically addressed by industry; these trials, which are also more commonly conducted in diverse populations, were recently shown to have contributed to gains of 14 million life-years for patients with cancer. Despite the different mandates, the proportion of patients who might participate in industry-sponsored versus federally-sponsored cancer studies is unknown.METHODSWe evaluated trial enrollment patterns from 2008 to 2022 using ClinicalTrials.gov data. The ratio of enrollments attributable to industry versus federal sponsors was estimated. A large set of estimates on the basis of different combinations of study characteristics were generated. Point estimates were determined as the mean of combinations and confidence limits by the IQR. Five-year intervals were examined to smooth annual variation.RESULTSIn total, N = 26,080 studies were examined. The estimated enrollment ratio from 2018 to 2022 for all industry-sponsored versus federally-sponsored trials was 8.1 (IQR, 6.2-9.9). For adult trials, the ratio increased from 4.8 (IQR, 4.4-5.3) during 2008-2012 to 9.6 (IQR, 7.4-11.8) during 2018-2022; for trials in children, the ratio increased from 0.7 (IQR, 0.6-0.7) to 2.3 (IQR, 1.8-2.7). Despite increasing cancer incidence, enrollment counts for federally-sponsored trials were flat over the study period.CONCLUSIONIn the United States, there is a growing reliance on industry to conduct cancer clinical research. Underinvestment in federally-sponsored research comes at a cost for both patients and researchers, with lost opportunities for scientific, clinical, and population advances.

INTRODUCTION

The conduct of cancer clinical research in the United States is supported by both private and public sponsors. Industry-sponsored clinical trials are conducted to support new drug approvals from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). By contrast, trials sponsored by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) within the National Institutes of Health (NIH) are conducted by both individual investigators and trial networks. A defining feature of federally-sponsored trials is that they investigate a broad set of clinical research questions that are generally not addressed by industry, such as combining treatment modalities or examining whether approved drugs work in other cancers.1,2 Moreover, federally-sponsored trial networks conduct a wide variety of other types of studies (eg, quality of life, biorepository) and trials in populations with cancer that are more demographically diverse than industry-sponsored trials.2-5 Furthermore, federally-sponsored trials were recently shown to have contributed to gains in 14 million life-years for patients with cancer in the United States and have also had substantial clinical and scientific impacts over time, all at a modest investment on behalf of taxpayers.6

Despite the different mandates between industry and the NIH, there is no comprehensive, published evidence comparing overall enrollments with trials for each sponsor. This information is vital for policymakers aiming to understand the role of taxpayer funding to support clinical research. Our objective was to characterize the extent to which these very different models of oncology research draw from the limited number of patients with cancer who participate in trials.7,8

METHODS

Data

We used data from ClinicalTrials.gov to evaluate trial enrollment patterns beginning January 1, 2008, shortly after the 2007 FDA Amendment Act mandated that sponsors register all interventional trials.9 Data were analyzed through December 31, 2022 (15 years, inclusive). The trial sampling period began on January 1, 2005, since many trials enrolling after 2008 activated before 2008. Thus, 26,080 interventional trials were examined. We linked the ClinicalTrials.gov data set to data from the NCI's Clinical Trials Reporting Program (CTRP) to aid in the estimation of selected study parameters.10 In total, 20,398 trials (78.2%) of all interventional studies (N = 26,080) were successfully linked to the CTRP database. Observational studies, studies primarily conducted in healthy volunteers, and studies without start or completion dates were excluded.

Primary Outcome

Annual trial-level enrollment, the unit of analysis, was calculated as total enrollment divided by enrollment time. Trial-level enrollment estimates were based on actual enrollment, if available. For incomplete trials without actual enrollment, actual enrollment was imputed using quantile regression (stratified by planned enrollment, trial status, phase, lead sponsor, and trial purpose). This strategy was chosen over excluding incomplete trials to allow examination of enrollment patterns throughout the study period and to limit a potential bias from preferentially including more rapidly enrolling completed studies. Trial enrollment time was calculated as the difference between start date and enrollment completion. Because completion dates in ClinicalTrials.gov represent end of follow-up rather than end of enrollment, the end of enrollment was estimated using data from the CTRP linkage (see Statistical Analysis).11

Only US enrollments were used. For incomplete studies, for which international enrollment was unavailable, we multiplied enrollment totals by the average proportion of enrollments from international sites for completed trials (stratified by phase, study purpose, and intervention type).

Primary Independent Variable

The primary independent variable was sponsor category. ClinicalTrials.gov categorizes sponsors broadly as industry versus NIH (or also, US federal) versus other (ie, sponsored by institutions or other organizations). Designations of federal funding include those with any mechanism of NIH support, including cooperative group and R01-funded trials. Studies might have a single sponsor or multiple sponsors (ie, Industry|Other). Prior studies have commonly attributed enrollments entirely to the trial's lead sponsor.11 This strategy fails to account for shared scientific and financial contributions for trials with multiple sponsors; instead, we attributed enrollments to sponsors using a prespecified algorithm. For single-sponsor trials, enrollments were attributed to the sole sponsor. For studies with two sponsors, enrollments were attributed using proportional assignment, iteratively in 5% increments, from 50% attribution (the most conservative level of lead sponsor attribution) to 100% (the most liberal level). The remaining enrollments were assigned to the secondary sponsor. For trials with three sponsors, we applied a geometric sequence. Thus, if 70% of enrollments were attributed to the lead sponsor, then 21% (70% of the remaining enrollments) were attributed to the secondary sponsor, and the remaining 9% were attributed to the tertiary sponsor.

Funder categories were modified if contributing sponsorship, not otherwise specified in ClinicalTrials.gov, was identified using the Data Table 4 Specified Funding Source field in the CTRP data.12

Conventional strategies for enrollment attribution were also applied. Enrollments from single-sponsor studies were wholly attributed to the sponsor. Second, enrollments were wholly attributed to the lead sponsor. Finally, trials were classified according to whether there was any federal sponsorship. This approach favors the contributions of federal over industry sponsorship but is meaningful for understanding the extent of federal contributions to studies at any level, even if tertiary.13,14

Point estimates were determined as the mean of n = 990 (ie, 3 × 3 × 10 × 11) generated combinations and confidence limits by the IQR. Five-year intervals were examined to smooth annual variation. Estimates were derived overall and separately by age: adult versus child (eg, trials including patients younger than 18 years). Our primary outcome was the annual ratio over all combinations for the most recent 5-year period 2018-2022 (inclusive); secondary end points included 10-year (2013-2022) and 15-year (2008-2022) estimates, as well as separate estimates for each of the three study purpose and intervention type categories. On the basis of the model, enrollment counts for all interventional studies of any phase were also estimated. Enrollment ratios according to the conventional sponsor categorizations (by sole sponsor, lead sponsor, or any federal sponsorship) for the three study categories (interventional v treatment v drug/biological) were separately estimated. Patterns in new trial activations over time (using a 5-year rolling average) were examined. Finally, although this report focuses on industry-sponsored versus federally-sponsored trials, we also compared enrollment ratios for industry versus trials funded by other (nonindustrial) sources.

RESULTS

Overall, among 26,080 interventional trials examined, 82.7% were treatment trials and 70.7% were drug/biological agent treatment trials (Table 1). Most trials were solely sponsored by industry (31.7%), other (24.3%), or federal (4.9%); were phase I or II (73.9%) trials; were drug (65.3%) or biological (11.0%) trials; and were conducted in adults (90.1%).

The estimated ratio of enrollments attributable to industry versus NIH sponsorship was 8.1 (IQR, 6.2-9.9; Fig 1). This ratio differed substantially between trials conducted for adults (ratio = 9.6; IQR, 7.4-11.8) compared with children (ratio = 2.3; IQR, 1.8-2.7).

When examined according to any federal sponsorship, for all trials combined, the industry-to-NIH ratio increased from 1.5 to 2.6 for intervention trials and from 2.0 to 4.7 for drug/biological treatment trials (Fig 4B). Consistent trajectories were observed for trials in both adults and children.

Trials Funded by Other Sources

From 2008 to 2022, for adult trials, enrollments attributable to industry support were more than twice as great (ratio = 2.3; IQR, 2.0-2.8) than enrollments attributable to other sources (Appendix Table A2). In trials for children, enrollments attributable to industry were less overall (2008-2022; ratio = 0.6; IQR, 0.5-0.7), although by 2018-2022, enrollments attributable to industry were greater than enrollments attributable to other sources (ratio = 1.1; IQR, 0.8-1.3).

DISCUSSION

From 2018 to 2022, cancer clinical trial enrollment attributable to industry sponsorship was more than eight times greater compared with federal sponsorship and more than nine times greater in adult trials. In adult trials, this ratio was twice as great in 2018-2022 (ratio = 9.6) compared with the prior decade (ratio = 4.8). Similar increases over time were found for trials in children. In addition, over the study period, the ratio of new trial activations increased dramatically from 3.2 to 9.1 for adults and from 1.1 to 3.9 for children. Thus, industry-sponsored trials may continue to increase their share of patient enrollments in the coming years. For adult trials, enrollments attributable to industry were two times greater than enrollments attributable to other sources.

These results demonstrate an increasing reliance in the United States on industry to conduct cancer clinical research. This highlights, on the one hand, increased investment on the part of industry sponsors in the discovery process. This increased investment on the part of industry has helped accelerate the era of precision medicine and immuno-oncology and has resulted in many new agents discovered with demonstrable public health benefits.19,20 For instance, one study showed how advances in targeted therapies has contributed substantially to reductions in mortality and increases in survival after diagnosis for patients with lung cancer.21 However, it also points to the comparative underinvestment in federally-sponsored cancer research. Even as incident cancer cases in the United States increased 18% from 1.53 million in 2010 to 1.81 million in 2020 and industry-sponsored enrollments more than doubled, enrollments attributable to NIH sponsorship were flat (14,030/y between 2008 and 2012 and 14,626/y between 2018 and 2022).22,23 In fact, adjusted for biomedical price inflation and indexed in constant (2020) US dollars (USD), the NCI's cooperative group clinical trials budget was 6% lower in purchasing power in 2020 ($295.6 million USD) compared with 2010 ($314.3 million USD).24-26

Federal underinvestment in cancer clinical research has implications for the pace of new treatment discovery. A recent study showed that the NCI's network research groups contributed to gains of 14.2 million life-years for patients with cancer over 4 decades at a cost to taxpayers of only $326 USD/life-year.6 In fact, the estimated average cost to develop three new oncology drugs by pharmaceutical companies (approximately $5 billion USD) would, on its own, have supported the total funding required for all four NCI-sponsored adult network groups since 1980.6,27-30 The conduct of these trials also contributes substantially to clinical care guidelines, to scientific research, to mentoring, and to elements of clinical research not typically pursued by industry, such as cancer control and prevention.1 Federally-sponsored trials also enroll much more racially, geographically, and ethnically diverse populations of patients, with representation of Black patients three times greater for federally-sponsored trials than industry trials.2-4 Thus, if support for federally-sponsored clinical cancer research over the past decade had kept pace with industry, then population, clinical, and scientific advances—applicable to patients of all sociodemographic backgrounds—would likely have also increased substantially.

Enrollment ratios increased substantially as the trial definition narrowed from active interventional trials to trials testing experimental drugs/biological agents only, highlighting the different missions of the sponsors. Industry focuses on obtaining approval for new drugs from the FDA, while federally-sponsored trials address a broader set of research questions with potentially high public health impact that may include other, non–drug-related treatment modalities or interventional approaches.1 Indeed, for such studies, our results showed enrollment ratios of 0.8 and 0.4 for trials in adults and children, respectively, indicating more federal sponsorship in these interventional settings. Additionally, the more independently a trial was conducted, the greater the ratio of industry to NIH enrollments, with enrollment ratios highest among trials with single sponsors. This observation suggests a greater capacity for industry to conduct trials independently, while NIH-sponsored trials are more likely to rely on industry collaborations to facilitate trial conduct, adding to the already substantial requirements for data and specimen sharing for ancillary research for NIH trials. Such NIH-industry collaborations may require lengthy contractual negotiations when a federal sponsor is testing a pharmaceutical company agent. This could limit the independence with which federal trial networks may investigate alternative schedules and dosage for experimental agents.

Historically, trials in children were predominantly conducted by federal or institutional sponsors.31 Our findings show increasing contributions to pediatric cancer research from industry, especially for drugs or biological agents. This is likely a by-product of the FDA Reauthorization Act of 2017, which included a provision to require drug sponsors seeking a new drug approval in adults with cancer to also study the drug in the pediatric population.32

This study has limitations. Given the common issues of misclassification or nonreporting of study details in ClinicalTrials.gov,15,16,18 we calculated the mean and IQR from a large sample of estimates representing combinations of different study specifications. This approach may limit interpretation with respect to specific study attributes but also accounts for uncertainty with respect to parameter assumptions and reduces the likelihood of large errors in estimation. Furthermore, depending on the category of trials of interest, our base case findings may be conservative, as results were even larger in the subset of trials conducted for treatment and larger yet among drug/biological trials. To allocate enrollments for multisponsor trials, we applied an algorithm that was agnostic, favoring neither industry nor NIH sponsor contributions. This approach more fairly attributes sponsor contributions to patient enrollment for multisponsor trials. However, the findings may not be directly comparable with prior literature or to expected enrollment totals for specific sponsors.13,33

Our study demonstrates that the landscape of clinical cancer research in the United States is shifting toward one dominated by industry. This has policy implications since industry sponsors focus predominantly on drug and biological agent interventions, whereas federally-sponsored trials are more inclusive of different combinations of agents and modalities and are more oriented toward research questions important for a diverse population of patients. These different models for cancer clinical research both serve vital functions in new treatment discovery and are, ultimately, complementary. Nonetheless, an underinvestment to the main avenues for federally supported clinical research is occurring at a time when such research is greatly needed to perform the trials that industry will not do. Thus, policymakers should aim to address the increasing imbalance between industry and federally funded cancer clinical research to benefit both researchers and patients in terms of scientific, clinical, and population advances.
