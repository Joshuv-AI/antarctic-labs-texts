Linking extent of return to fasting state after oral glucose tolerance test to future risk of prediabetes and type 2 diabetes: Insights from the TLGS
Authors: Soroush Masrouri, Seyed Saeed Tamehri Zadeh, Maryam Tohidi, Fereidoun Azizi, Farzad Hadaegh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo assess the risk of difference between 2 h post‐load plasma glucose (2 h‐PG) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) on incident prediabetes/type 2 diabetes (T2DM) among normoglycemic individuals.MethodsAmong 4,971 individuals aged ≥20 years, the associations of the difference between 2 h‐PG and FPG with outcomes were examined using multivariable‐adjusted Cox regression analysis. Participants were categorized into three groups: a low post‐load group (2 h‐PG ≤ FPG, as the reference group); a high post‐load group (2 h‐PG > FPG and ≥75th percentile of the difference); and a medium post‐load group (2 h‐PG > FPG and <75th percentile of the difference), which was further categorized into three groups by equal ranges.ResultsOver a median of 11.5 years of follow‐up, 2,331 new cases of prediabetes/type 2 diabetes and 360 cases of type 2 diabetes occurred. Greater risks of incident prediabetes/type 2 diabetes in second (9–16 mg/dL) and third (17–24 mg/dL) medium post‐load, as well as high post‐load (≥25 mg/dL) categories, were found, with hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) of 1.26 (1.11–1.44), 1.32 (1.15–1.51), and 1.69 (1.51–1.90), respectively; the issue was more prominent among women (P for interaction = 0.005). The risk of incident type 2 diabetes was also higher for these categories. After further adjustment for the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance, result remained essentially unchanged. Even among individuals with low normal FPG (i.e., <90 mg/dL), ≥9 mg/dL difference between 2 h‐PG and FPG increased the risk of composite prediabetes/ type 2 diabetes.ConclusionsGreater levels of 2 h‐PG as low as 9 mg/dL than FPG among normoglycemic individuals is a harbinger of prediabetes/type 2 diabetes development.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes is a major worldwide health issue estimated to affect approximately 463 million individuals globally in 2019 1 . The Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region has the second‐highest increasing estimated prevalence rate of diabetes in the world (87%) from 2021 to 2045 2 . In Iran, it was shown that about 4.5 million adults suffered from diabetes in 2011 3 , and this number is estimated to rise to about 9.2 million by 2030 4 .

In clinical practice, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2 h post‐load glucose (2 h‐PG) following oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) are three commonly used methods to diagnose prediabetes and diabetes 5 . Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) is a well‐known risk factor for diabetes, among whom 30%–40% will eventually develop type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) over 5–10 years 6 . However, it was also shown that about 40% of subjects who develop type 2 diabetes are within the normal glucose tolerance (NGT) range after an OGTT 7 , implying that there is a large proportion of individuals with a normal glucose tolerance who constitute a large reservoir for future development of type 2 diabetes 8 .

It has been shown that applying an OGTT will significantly enhance the chance of detecting undiagnosed diabetes by identifying those with IGT 9 , 10 . Nevertheless, data concerning the clinical applicability of 2 h‐PG for risk stratification in normoglycemic individuals, especially when coupled with FPG, are scarce. It was shown that NGT individuals with 2 h‐PG near or below the FPG value are more sensitive to insulin than those with a 2 h‐PG higher than the FPG value 11 . Among individuals with NGT from the San Antonio Heart Study, a higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes in those with a 2 h‐PG higher than the FPG relative to those with a FPG higher than the 2 h‐PG was found, however, not independent of indices of insulin sensitivity and secretion 6 . Another cohort study that was conducted among normoglycemic American individuals assessed the association of difference between the 2 h‐PG and the FPG with incident prediabetes and type 2 diabetes and found that a difference between the 2 h‐PG and the FPG concentration of >0.9 mmol/L can be used to stratify individuals at higher risk for developing type 2 diabetes, but not prediabetes 12 . Previously, we showed that lowering the FPG cutoff point to 90 mg/dL is associated with a zero near‐term risk of type 2 diabetes in the presence of well‐known type 2 diabetes risk factors 13 ; however, it is still unclear whether the difference between 2 h‐PG and FPG among these individuals is associated with a higher risk of developing dysglycemia. Therefore, we designed the current study to evaluate the association of a difference between the 2 h‐PG and the FPG with incident prediabetes/type 2 diabetes in those with a normal fasting glucose (NFG) and NGT in the large prospective Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) cohort. We further assessed whether these associations exist among individuals with a low normal FPG (i.e., <90 mg/dL).

METHODS

Sample size

In this study, the sample size for the outcome of type 2 diabetes, which had fewer events than dysglycemia, was calculated at 2,688 participants. This calculation was based on expected effect sizes and event rates derived from prior studies by Vivek et al. 12 and Derakhshan et al. 14 We set a pre‐specified statistical power of 90%, with a significance level of α = 0.05, and included a 10% adjustment for non‐adherence.

Study population

This prospective study is designed within the TLGS framework, a longitudinal population‐based cohort study that commenced in 1999 to determine the prevalence of non‐communicable diseases among urban residents of Tehran. Recruiting participants for the TLGS was conducted in two phases [the first (1999–2001: n = 15,005) and the second (2001–2005: n = 3,550)]. Participants were followed prospectively at four subsequent periods in approximately 3 year intervals (i.e., third phase: 2005–2008, fourth phase: 2009–2011, fifth phase: 2012–2015, and sixth phase: 2015–2018). Details of the study design and methodology are available elsewhere 15 .

As shown in Figure 1, for the current study, of the total of 9,138 participants aged ≥20 years (6,735 from phase 1 and 2,403 from phase 2) who entered the phase at 2001–2005, we first excluded those with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes at baseline (n = 2,741). Of the 6,397 remaining participants, we excluded 583 TLGS participants with incomplete information on baseline FPG, or 2 h‐PG, 567 without at least one follow‐up visit ascertaining prediabetes/type 2 diabetes status, and 276 who were missing baseline confounders. Finally, 4,971 normoglycemic participants were selected for the analysis. Additionally, as a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the analyses in the presence of homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) in a subpopulation with available insulin data (n = 3,075).

Flow chart for the selection of the TLGS participants for the analysis.

Clinical and laboratory measurements

Standardized protocols were used to collect clinical and laboratory measurements for all participants during baseline and follow‐up visits. Trained interviewers, using standardized questionnaires, collected data on demographics, marital status, educational level, medication use, smoking status, family history of type 2 diabetes, and history of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Weight was measured using a digital scale and was recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg, with light clothing and without shoes. Height was measured standing with a tape meter, without shoes. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Using a standardized mercury sphygmomanometer, after the participant had been sitting quietly for 15 min, systolic and diastolic blood pressures (SBP and DBP, respectively) were taken twice, 1 min apart, on the right arm; we used the mean of the two measurements for the analyses. For all participants, after 12–14 h of overnight fasting, blood samples were obtained via venipuncture and biochemically analyzed on the blood collection day using commercial kits (Pars Azmoon Inc., Tehran, Iran) and a Selectra 2 auto‐analyzer (Vital Scientific, Spankeren, The Netherlands) at the TLGS research laboratory. For the 2 h‐PG, a standard OGTT was conducted. Serum glucose concentrations were measured using an enzymatic colorimetric method with glucose oxidase. Total cholesterol (TC) was assayed using the enzymatic colorimetric method with cholesterol esterase and cholesterol oxidase. High‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) was measured after precipitation of the apolipoprotein B containing lipoproteins with phosphotungstic acid and triglycerides (TG) were assayed using glycerol phosphate oxidase.

Definitions

At baseline and follow‐up visits, the type 2 diabetes status was assessed and defined as FPG ≥7.0 mmol/L or 2 h‐PG ≥11.1 mmol/L or using anti‐diabetic medications. Subjects were classified as having prediabetes if they met the following criteria: FPG 5.6–6.9 mmol/L or 2 h‐PG 7.8–11.0 mmol/L. The normoglycemia state was defined as a FPG of <5.6 mmol/L plus a 2 h‐PG of <7.8 mmol/L. The exposure variable in the current study was the difference in glucose concentrations between FPG and 2 h‐PG. Participants were categorized into three groups based on their difference between 2 h‐PG and FPG (2 h‐PG – FPG): low post‐load if 2 h‐PG ≤ FPG (reference group); those with 2 h‐PG > FPG were categorized in medium post‐load and high post‐load groups, respectively, if their difference between FPG and 2 h‐PG were distributed into <75th and ≥75th percentiles (i.e., 25 mg/dL) 12 . We further divided the medium post‐load category into three equal groups, each with a range of 8 mg/dL. This division was done to investigate whether the associations observed in the medium post‐load category existed for any higher values of 2 h‐PG compared with FPG. The HOMA‐IR was calculated as the following: HOMA‐IR = [fasting insulin (μU/mL) × FPG (mmol/L)]/22.5 16 . Current smoking was defined as any smoking record at the time of examination. A family history of type 2 diabetes was recorded as positive once the participant had at least one parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes. Marital status was categorized into married and non‐married groups. Educational levels were categorized into two groups based on self‐reports: <12 and ≥12 years of education. Hypertension was defined as one of the following criteria: SBP ≥140 mmHg or DBP ≥90 mmHg or taking anti‐hypertensive agents 17 . Dyslipidemia was defined as TG ≥200 mg/dL, TC ≥240 mg/dL, HDL <40 mg/dL (<35 mg/dL for men), or taking lipid‐lowering agents 18 .

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics of the study population according to categories of the difference between 2 h‐PG and FPG were expressed as mean ± SD and frequencies (%) for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The primary outcome of our study is incident composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes. To enhance the relevance and comparability of our findings across broader research contexts, we also examined incident type 2 diabetes as a secondary outcome. Modeling was performed using Cox regression analysis to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the post‐load plasma glucose categories for the incidence of composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes or type 2 diabetes. The Cox model outcome was the time to develop composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes or type 2 diabetes, which was calculated as the time to the TLGS follow‐up visit where the diagnosis was made. Once the subjects developed prediabetes/type 2 diabetes after a baseline visit, they were considered to have had an event and were censored. Those who did not have an event had their data censored for analysis at the time of death, date of loss to follow‐up, or the end of the study (18 April 2018), whichever occurred first. In multivariable analysis, three models were applied; model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; model 3 was further adjusted for marital status, educational level, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, dyslipidemia, family history of type 2 diabetes, and history of cardiovascular disease. In a sensitivity analysis, we assessed if the associations persisted after adjustment for HOMA‐IR among a subsample of participants with available insulin data (n = 3,075). Additionally, we performed a subgroup analysis with testing for interaction by including multiplicative interaction terms in the Cox regression model. To account for the potential reverse causality bias, we repeated the analysis after excluding participants with incident composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes or type 2 diabetes, as appropriate, at the subsequent visit after baseline recruitment. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 14 SE (StataCorp, TX, USA), and a two‐tailed P < 0.05 was considered significant.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective cohort study involving Iranian adults and spanning over a decade of follow‐up, we sought to investigate the association between the difference in 2 h‐PG and FPG levels with the occurrence of type 2 diabetes and prediabetes/type 2 diabetes among individuals with NFG and NGT. Following adjustment for well‐known type 2 diabetes risk factors, a higher risk of incident composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes and type 2 diabetes were observed in individuals whose 2 h‐PG levels were greater than their FPG levels by 9 mg/dL or more. Importantly, we found that among individuals with a baseline FPG <90 mg/dL, those with ≥9 mg/dL difference had a higher risk of composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes. Additionally, the association of the high post‐load category (fourth quartile: ≥25 mg/dL) with the composite outcome of prediabetes/type 2 diabetes, compared with low post‐load and medium post‐load categories, was significantly higher among women than men.

In our study, over a quarter of participants (27.8%) had 2 h‐PG equal to or lower than their FPG, with significant variations by sex and age. Women were less likely to fall into the low post‐load category, and this group tended to be younger. These findings align with a previous study of young Japanese women, where only 23% exhibited a higher insulin response to meals and a lower post‐challenge glucose level than their FPG concentration 19 . The FPG reflects the balance between basal insulin, glucagon, and their effects on endogenous glucose production and disposal 20 , 21 . Following an OGTT, blood glucose levels increase due to carbohydrate absorption in the proximal small intestine 22 . The time it takes for the 2 h‐PG level to return to or fall below the FPG level after glucose ingestion depends on the insulin response during the OGTT and both peripheral and hepatic insulin sensitivity. The faster the 2 h‐PG level declines to the FPG level, the more efficient the person is in disposing of the glucose load 23 . A high post‐load level likely indicates insulin resistance or reduced insulin production, predisposing individuals to a higher risk of future prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. It is important to note that increased postprandial glucose levels are always accompanied by insulin resistance, aggravating oxidative stress, and inflammation, which play a major role in the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes 24 , 25 . The associations in our study remained significant even after adjusting for HOMA‐IR, indicating that factors beyond insulin resistance may contribute to the link between the high post‐load glucose gap and increased risk of prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. However, we lacked data on oxidative stress and inflammatory markers.

In the present study, we found out that the difference between 2 h‐PG and FPG may have the potential to help identify individuals with NFG and NGT at risk for developing composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes and type 2 diabetes. Previously, Abdul‐Ghani et al. 6 , in The San Antonio Heart Study, revealed that individuals with a greater 2 h‐PG compared with FPG had a significantly higher risk of progression to type 2 diabetes; nonetheless, the association reached non‐significant levels following adjustment for insulin secretion and insulin resistance. In support of this, it was found that those with a higher 2 h‐PG compared with FPG had a worse β‐cell function and more severe insulin resistance compared with those with a lower 2 h‐PG compared with FPG 6 . In contrast, in our study, adjustment for HOMA‐IR did not alter the results, indicating the independent association of the difference of 2 h‐PG and FPG with the risk of developing hyperglycemia. Most recently, Vivek et al. 12 found a significant association between a high post‐load (highest quartile of difference: ≥16.2 mg/dL) and incident type 2 diabetes (HR, 95% CI; 1.56, 1.03–2.37), compared with low post‐load (≤0 mg/dL), but not incident prediabetes in the full adjusted model among normoglycemic individuals of the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) cohort study. However, in contrast to our study, the authors did not detect a significant association between medium post‐load (the difference of 0–16.2 mg/dL) with incident type 2 diabetes or prediabetes. Lastly, they concluded that among those with NGT and NFG, a difference between the 2 h‐PG and the FPG level of ≥ 16.2 mg/dL can prospectively predict a greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes 12 . In our study, fewer values of difference (≥9 mg/dL) were associated with a higher risk of both incident composite of prediabetes/type 2 diabetes and type 2 diabetes. In the current analysis, we also observed a greater risk of composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes for the difference of ≥9 mg/dL among those with low normal FPG levels.

Our study found that the high post‐load category was associated with a significantly higher risk of developing dysglycemia in women compared with men. Specifically, women in our study exhibited higher 2 h‐PG levels (99.8 mg/dL vs 95.1 mg/dL) and lower FPG levels (86.3 mg/dL vs 87.9 mg/dL) than men. Premenopausal women typically have greater insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscle and the liver, as well as higher insulin secretion following glucose intake, which results in lower FPG levels compared with men 26 , 27 . However, despite this advantage, women often show higher 2 h‐PG levels, which could be due to prolonged glucose absorption in the gastrointestinal tract 28 . It has been shown that among those with normal glucose tolerance, women generally exhibit higher 2 h‐PG levels than men across all body surface area categories 29 . Consequently, women, despite their lower FPG, may be more prone to postprandial hyperglycemia and the subsequent development of dysglycemia. This is consistent with the broader epidemiological pattern, where women more often present with IGT, whereas men are more frequently diagnosed with fasting hyperglycemia 30 . Additionally, women experience a greater burden of metabolic risk factors, such as changes in BMI, blood pressure, FPG, and lipid levels before the onset of type 2 diabetes, which put them at higher risk 31 , 32 . The difference could also be attributable to sex hormones, as it was shown that menopausal treatment with estrogen reduces FPG and impairs glucose tolerance 33 .

A number of research studies have been conducted to assess glucose metabolism and glucose tolerance‐related risks in individuals with NGT 6 , 34 , 35 , 36 . A cross‐sectional study assessed glucose metabolism in coronary heart disease (CHD) patients with NGT and found that those without any stenosed vessels had a mean 2 h‐PG of 110 mg/dL, while those with three stenosed vessels had a mean 2 h‐PG of 130 mg/dL. Therefore, the authors concluded that an increase in 2 h‐PG levels, even within the normal range, was accompanied by increased severity of CHD 36 . In another study, Ning et al. found that normoglycemic individuals whose 2 h‐PG was greater than their FPG level had an increased risk of CHD and ischemic stroke 37 . Altogether, these observations, along with our findings, provide evidence that the extent of return to fasting state after an OGTT has the potential to identify individuals at heightened risk of developing clinical outcomes.

The major strengths of this study include long‐term follow‐up, a sufficiently large cohort size, and the ability to assess the risk of composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes and type 2 diabetes based on the difference between 2 h‐PG and FPG levels, both overall and when stratified by baseline FPG levels. Also, in the current study, we showed that the difference between 2 h‐PG and FPG can be utilized as a diagnostic tool for predicting prediabetes/type 2 diabetes among individuals with NFG and NGT; however, it is of importance to conduct further studies to confirm these findings in other populations. Another strength of the study is the large number of events for the primary composite outcome (prediabetes/type 2 diabetes), which allowed us to detect effect sizes as small as 1.06, reflected in the narrow confidence intervals. However, our study has a number of limitations; the first limitation of our study is the absence of 30 min and 1 h post‐load glucose measurements, which could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of glucose metabolism. According to the TLGS protocol, in a large population‐based study, we only measured 2 h‐PG, with no data available on other plasma glucose parameters during the OGTT. Emerging evidence suggests that 30 min and 1 h post‐load glucose levels are significant predictors of prediabetes and type 2 diabetes 38 , 39 , and may even outperform the conventional 2 h‐PG measurement 40 , 41 . Future research should explore the clinical relevance of these additional measurements in predicting prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. Second, we lacked HbA1c data, so we could not use it to diagnose type 2 diabetes or prediabetes. However, our study relied on the robust indicators of FPG and 2 h‐PG, consistently measured during triannual visits over more than a decade, minimizing the risk of misdiagnosis. Third, the TLGS only contains urban adults in Tehran and may not be generalizable to rural areas. Finally, similar to other cohort studies initiated at the time of the TLGS project, certain baseline characteristics – such as marital status, educational level, smoking status, family history of diabetes, history of cardiovascular disease, and medication use – were self‐reported by participants 42 , 43 , 44 . Although we used a standardized questionnaire and had trained interviewers to collect this data, the possibility of information bias in these covariates cannot be entirely ruled out.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, in this prospective cohort study among individuals with normal glucose metabolism, those whose a 2 h‐PG that did not return to their FPG levels during an OGTT had a significantly higher risk for future prediabetes and type 2 diabetes compared with those whose 2 h‐PG was equal to or lower than their FPG levels. Greater 2 h‐PG values of ≥9 mg/dL than FPG levels were associated with a higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes and composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes. Specifically, among normoglycemic individuals with a FPG of < 90 mg/dL, a higher 2 h‐PG level than the FPG level of as low as 9 mg/dL can identify those at higher risk of developing composite prediabetes/type 2 diabetes.
