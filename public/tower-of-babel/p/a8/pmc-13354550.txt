Comments on misuse of iron-57 transmission Mössbauer spectroscopy for surface characterization in Nature Communications 16, 5989 (2025)
Authors: Fernande Grandjean, Gary J Long
Published: 2026

arising from W. Lian et al. Nature Communications 10.1038/s41467-025-60919-7 (2025).

In disagreement with the claim in ref. 1, the presence of surface FeIV = O on FeS2, synthetic pyrite, upon reaction with chlorite is not supported by the room temperature Mössbauer spectra shown. These spectra have been reanalyzed herein with a simpler model involving fewer parameters—a reanalysis that agrees with the philosophy of Occam’s razor, see Wikipedia. These results are consistent with the well-known insensitivity of 57Fe transmission Mössbauer spectroscopy to surface iron detection and study. Additional problems in ref. 1 are delineated in the supplementary information.

We have used the experimental Mössbauer spectral data for supplementary Fig. 38 published in ref. 1 and provided by the Editor. All three spectra have been fitted over the entire velocity range of ±12 mm/s but the results are shown in Fig. 1 over the relevant velocity range of ±4 mm/s.

Our first fitting attempt of this spectrum used the parameters reported in supplementary Table 5 in ref. 1 or in Table 1 in Lian et al.’s reply to this comment, the latter table contains at least some uncertainties. Only two parameters, the baseline and the total spectral absorption area have been fitted herein and a χ2 of 0.964 has been obtained, indicating a satisfactory fit. Subsequently, the isomer shift and quadrupole splitting, and finally the linewidth have been fitted. The result of this fit is shown in the top of Fig. 1 and the five adjusted parameters with their uncertainties are given in Table 1 as shown below. This fit and these parameters agree with those presented for FeS2, A, in Table 1 in Lian et al.’s reply to this comment.

Unfortunately, a comparison of the obtained isomer shift and quadrupole splitting for FeS2 with the literature values is made difficult, because the isomer shift reference material is not reported in ref. 1. Isomer shifts relative to α-iron at room temperature of 0.31(2) mm/s at 294 K and 0.40(2) mm/s at 78 K were reported2 for a natural sample of pyrite. Hence, it appears that the room temperature isomer shift of 0.166(6) mm/s given in Table 1 is not referred to α-iron at room temperature but most likely to room temperature rhodium3. Quadrupole splittings, ΔEQ, of 0.61(2) mm/s at 294 K and 0.62(2) mm/s at 78 K were reported2 for a natural sample of pyrite and agree well with the value at room temperature of 0.63(1) mm/s given in Table 1 herein.

Next, our first fitting attempt of spectrum B in supplementary Fig. 38 in ref. 1 for R-FeS2-ClO2, where R means reacted, with two symmetric doublets, one major doublet assigned to FeII in FeS2 and a minor doublet, with the parameters quoted in supplementary Table 5 in ref. 1 or Table 1 in the reply to this comment, gave a preliminary fit with a χ2 of 1.121. Subsequent fits with adjustments of both isomer shifts and both quadrupole splittings, followed by fitting both linewidths, and finally, fitting the percent area of the minor doublet, gave a good fit with a χ2 of 1.124, a fit which is shown in the middle-left panel of Fig. 1 and whose nine parameters with their respective uncertainties are given in Table 1 herein.

A fitting procedure similar to that used for R-FeS2-ClO2, was followed for R-FeS2 spectrum C in supplementary Fig. 38 in ref. 1. The resulting fit is shown in the bottom left panel of Fig. 1 and its parameters with their respective uncertainties are given in Table 1 herein.

The comparison of the spectral parameters for R-FeS2-ClO2 and R-FeS2, given in Table 1 herein with those given in Table 1 in Lian et al.’s reply to this comment, shows good agreement for the isomer shift, quadrupole splitting, and linewidth of the major doublet. However, the adjustment of both linewidths and the percent area of the minor doublet affected its isomer shift and quadrupole splitting, slightly beyond the uncertainties. The percent area of the minor doublet found herein agrees within uncertainties with those given in Table 1 in Lian et al.’s reply to this comment. In brief, the spectral parameters given in the lower part of Table 1 herein show that, when all nine parameters of the model are refined, the isomer shift and quadrupole splitting of the minor doublet tend to approach the isomer shift and quadrupole splitting of the major doublet. Further, the linewidth of the minor doublet is smaller than that of the major doublet, without any physical reason for this difference, and even becomes smaller than the Heisenberg linewidth for iron-57 of 0.195 mm/s4. These two observations indicate that the presence of the minor doublet is highly dubious and questionable.

The room temperature isomer shifts of 0.05(7) and 0.08(14) mm/s relative to an unreported reference material, quoted in ref. 1 and the reply to this comment, are below the limit of 0.20 mm/s, relative to α-iron at room temperature5, needed to unquestionably conclude that the doublet is indicative of high-spin FeIV, see Fig. S1 in the supplementary information. Further, the discussion of the difference between 0.05 and 0.08 mm/s that appears on page 3 right column in ref. 1 is simply wrong and the so-called “regularity of isomer shift value change in related literature” supplementary text 2 in Lian et al.’s reply to this comment contains no physical basis for this discussion. If Lian et al. believe that 0.05(7) mm/s is significantly smaller than 0.08(14) mm/s, a belief unsupported by the uncertainties, the only physically sound analysis of this difference could go as follows. The smaller isomer shift results from a larger s-electron density at the iron nucleus6, a larger density that could result from the 3d-electron probability density extending further from the iron nucleus and, we guess, in the mind of Lian et al. towards the electronegative coordinated Cl. Of course, we do not even believe the FeIV component is present in the spectra and hence, this discussion is purely philosophical.

Because of the small relative area and the large uncertainties on the parameters of the minor doublet in both Mössbauer spectra of R-FeS2-ClO2 and R-FeS2, and because only a small asymmetry is observed in the two doublets shown in the middle and bottom panels in Fig. 1, the adequacy of fits with only one asymmetric doublet, as a result of texture in the absorber, was investigated. Successful fits of the room temperature Mössbauer spectra of R-FeS2-ClO2 and R-FeS2 with only six parameters and respective χ2 values of 1.107 and 1.182 are shown in the middle and bottom right panels of Fig. 1 and the corresponding parameters are given in the top portion of Table 1. Note that the FeIV doublet is simply not needed and that both χ2 values are not significantly larger than those obtained with a two symmetric doublet fit and the residuals do not show any significant misfit.

The isomer shifts and quadrupole splittings are slightly different from those observed for the original FeS2 synthetic pyrite sample, most likely as a result of the preparative treatment of R-FeS2-ClO2 and R-FeS2. There seems to be an increasing trend, perhaps accidental, in isomer shift and quadrupole splitting from FeS2 to R-FeS2-ClO2 and then to R-FeS2. The linewidth, see Table 1, is smaller than is observed for the original FeS2 sample fitted herein, and may indicate a better crystallinity of the reacted samples. In the fitting program used herein, the texture parameter is defined as the relative area, 2, of the ΔmI = 0 transitions in an 57Fe Mössbauer sextet of a random powder absorber. Hence, a symmetric doublet has a texture parameter of 2 and a departure from 2 in the fitted texture parameter indicates a non-random powder absorber. The texture parameters of 2.10(4) and 2.09(3) given in Table 1 are barely significantly different from 2 and thus both absorbers are very close to random powders.

In the original Mössbauer spectral data provided by the Editor, the number of counts in the baseline of the three spectra is given. It is worth noting that the number of counts in the baseline for Mössbauer spectrum A is about 713,000, in spectrum B is about 42,000, and in spectrum C is about 80,000. These numbers do not appear to fit the experimental conditions described in the supplementary text 1 in Lian et al.’s reply to this comment. These authors indicate: “we put pure FeS2 into the detection area for a short [never specified] detecting time” and this short detection time yielded 713,000 counts in the baseline. They continue: “The detection time for PA≡FeIV = O [on R-FeS2-ClO2] … PS≡FeIV = O on R-FeS2 was elongated” but this longer counting time gave small number of counts, 42,000 and 80,000, in the baseline and a huge 25 percent absorption in the spectra, see Fig. 1 herein, middle and bottom panels. These small number of counts in the baseline and the huge percent absorption may only result from using thick to very thick absorbers. This very thickness of the absorbers makes totally impossible the observation of eventual changes at the surface and in the first ten atomic layers below the surface that Lian et al. claimed1 to observe. The contribution of 10 atomic layers to the overall absorption area may be estimated to be 0.02 %, well below the typical detectable minimum 1% area in Mössbauer spectroscopy.

Another new, somewhat puzzling, information in Lian et al.’s reply to this comment, is: “the Mössbauer spectra of R-FeS2 and R-FeS2-ClO2 were in-situ characterized by adding corresponding solutions onto the FeS2”. This addition of solutions suggest that the FeS2 absorber was horizontal and hence that the drive of the Mössbauer spectrometer was operating in vertical mode. This is possible but unusual and surely should be mentioned if it is the case. Further, in view of the change in thickness of the absorbers for the acquisition of the three spectra, the form and the thickness in mg of Fe per cm2 of the absorbers must be reported. Without this information, this research cannot be reproduced as it should be.

In conclusion, from the fits with one and two doublets of the room temperature Mössbauer spectra of R-FeS2-ClO2 and R-FeS2, the presence of a minor doublet assigned to surface FeIV = O cannot be confirmed. The one doublet fits are perfectly adequate and of the same quality as the two doublet fits. The hyperfine parameters obtained for the FeII doublet show only small changes from those of pyrite. Because transmission Mössbauer spectroscopy is not a surface technique, the conclusion drawn above applies to the bulk of the samples. Only conversion electron Mössbauer spectroscopy7 could provide some information about the presence of FeIV on the surface of the grains in the samples.

Finally, the presence of ≡FeIV = O in R-FeS2 and R-FeS2-ClO2 is supported by peaks at 920.7 and 929.2 cm–1, respectively, in their in-situ Raman spectra shown in Fig. 1a and in supplementary Fig. 39 in1. These 920.7 and 929.2 cm–1 bands are far above the typical 750–860 cm−1 values for the Raman signature of ≡FeIV = O, involving a strong, characteristic stretching band. The 920.7 and 929.2 cm–1 values are actually higher than the highest reported value of 885 cm–1 for the stretching band of ≡FeIV = O in zeolites8, a high value that was explained by the absence of a trans-axial ligand. The 920.7 and 929.2 cm–1 values suggest a short FeIV = O bond, but unfortunately, the EXAFS analysis shown in Fig. 1f, g in ref. 1 does not include the FeIV = O bond. The authors of1 must provide an explanation for these large values of 920.7 and 929.2 cm–1 and compare with their theoretical calculations.

The seven-split EPR signal shown in ref. 1 in supplementary Fig. 6 is obtained in solution and in conditions so different from the powder absorber used in Mössbauer and Raman spectroscopy that any attempt of comparison is worthless.

In addition to the scientific problems outlined above1, contains additional editorial and scientific problems that are detailed in the supplementary material associated with this comment and in a posted comment on PubPeer.com.

Observations and conclusions: The room temperature 57Fe transmission Mössbauer spectra of R-FeS2-ClO2 and R-FeS2 may be perfectly well fit with a single quadrupole doublet assigned to low-spin FeII, see the right portion of Fig. 1, in spite of claims by the authors that a second doublet attributed to high-spin FeIV is required to fit the spectra, see Fig. 1d in ref. 1 and the left portion of Fig. 1 herein. Further, the two spectra do not provide any indication that either FeII or FeIV is present on the surface of the FeS2 treated pyrite. This latter observation is completely consistent with the well-known fact that 57Fe transmission Mössbauer spectra are not suitable for the study of surface iron sites. Thus, the present authors are forced to conclude that paper1 has provided no Mössbauer spectral knowledge of any “planar asymmetric surface FeIV = O synthesis with pyrite and chlorite for efficient oxygen atom transfer reactions.”
