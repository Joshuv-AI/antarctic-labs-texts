Autocrine role of senescent cardiac fibroblasts-derived extracellular vesicles
Authors: Yusei FUJIOKA, Kosuke OTANI, Tomoko KODAMA, Muneyoshi OKADA, Hideyuki YAMAWAKI
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCellular senescence is a highly stable state associated with cell cycle arrest, that is elicited in response to various stresses. The accumulation of senescent cells in tissues drives age-related diseases. Recent studies have shown that the cellular senescence enhances an extracellular vesicles (EV) secretion. EV are lipid-bilayer-capsuled particles released by various cells mediating cell-to-cell communication. It was recently reported that EV secreted by the senescent cells had several functions such as cancer cell proliferation and immune cell activation. In the present study, we investigated whether senescent cardiac fibroblasts-derived EV play an autocrine/paracrine role in the heart cells. Neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (NRCFs) were treated with doxorubicin (DOX) to induce cellular senescence. EV were isolated from NRCFs culture media. The vehicle-treated NRCFs-derived EV (D0-EV, 72 hr) increased a living cell number in NRCFs, which was attenuated by DOX (1,000 nM)-treated NRCFs-derived EV (D103-EV, 72 hr). While D0-EV did not affect protein concentration in NRCFs, D103-EV decreased it. Furthermore, D103-EV significantly increased a ratio of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3)-II to LC3-I in NRCFs, indicating an induction of autophagy. In addition, D103-EV increased phosphorylation of adenosine monophosphate-activated kinase (AMPK) α in NRCFs. In neonatal rat cardiomyocytes, however, NRCFs-derived EV (72 hr) had no effect on the living cell number, protein concentration, and ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I. In conclusion, we for the first time revealed that DOX-induced senescent NRCFs-derived EV induce autophagy in NRCFs perhaps partly through the activation of AMPKα.Keywords: autophagy, cardiac fibroblast, cellular senescence, doxorubicin, extracellular vesicle

Cellular senescence is a highly stable state associated with cell cycle arrest that is elicited in response to various stresses, such as radiation, oxidative stress, and chemotherapy, which mainly cause DNA damage [13, 33]. It is recognized an important mechanism for preventing the proliferation of certain cancer cells [26]. The senescent cells are known to increase a secretion of inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, chemokines, and extracellular matrix proteases. Then, the condition has been called a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [7]. The senescent cells are cleared by the immune cells that are recruited due to the inflammatory cytokines and chemokines secreted as part of the SASP [6]. On the contrary, it was reported that an inflammation caused by the SASP may play a causal role in age-related diseases, such as atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus, and cancer [6].

Recent studies have shown that the cellular senescence not only increases an expression of secretory proteins by SASP, but also enhances an extracellular vesicles (EV) secretion [30]. EV are enclosed by a lipid bilayer membrane and are secreted by the cells [34]. EV carry a rich cargo of DNA, mRNA, microRNA (miRNA), proteins, lipids, and metabolites reflective of their cellular origin, and are released into the extracellular space [14, 34]. The released EV affect cellular functions via a signal transduction through binding to the cell surface receptors and/or the delivery of the cargo by phagocytosis, pinocytosis, and membrane fusion [21, 34]. Therefore, EV are now recognized as the active particles mediating cell–to–cell communication. It was recently reported that EV secreted by the senescent cells had several functions such as cancer cell proliferation and immune cell activation [30].

Cardiac fibroblasts are main cell components in heart and contribute approximately 60% of all cardiac cells in rats [3]. Cardiac fibroblasts are involved in the maintenance of myocardial tissue structure not only through regulating extracellular matrix remodeling, but also through producing autocrine/paracrine factors such as cytokines, growth factors, and matrix metalloproteinases [4]. It was recently reported that senescence induction of cardiac fibroblasts can be beneficial in the setting of chronic wound healing following myocardial infarction, or detrimental in the setting of myocardial fibrosis during aging [5]. However, the relationship between EV and cellular senescence in heart has not been examined in a detailed manner. Therefore, in the present study, we investigated whether the senescent cardiac fibroblasts-derived EV play an autocrine/paracrine effect in the heart cells.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Reagent sources were as follows: doxorubicin hydrochloride salt (LC laboratories, Woburn, MA, USA) and rapamycin (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Doxorubicin hydrochloride salt was dissolved in purified water. Rapamycin was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; final concentration was 0.1%).

Antibody sources were as follows: anti-Alix (No. 12422-1-AP) (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), anti-TSG-101 (No. ab125011) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3; No. 2775), anti-phospho-adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase α (AMPKα; No. 2535), anti-total-AMPKα (No. 2603) (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA), and anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; No. 016-25523) (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan).

Animals

All animal experiments were approved through the judgement of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kitasato University (Approval No. 20-015). Male and female Wistar rats (CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) were cared in accordance with the guideline for animal care and treatment of the Kitasato University. For the isolation of neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (NRCFs) and neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs), 1–3-day-old rats were used.

Isolation of NRCFs and NRCMs

NRCFs and NRCMs were isolated from neonatal Wistar rats as described previously [28, 29]. The hearts were harvested from neonatal Wistar rats (1–3-day-old) and transferred immediately into the dish containing phosphate buffered saline (PBS) with 20 mM 2,3-butanedione monoxime (BDM) on ice. Then, the hearts were minced into small pieces and washed in a wash solution (Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution with 0.08% trypsin and 20 mM BDM) for 2 hr at 4°C with stirring. Next, they were incubated in collagenase solution (Leibovitz’s L15 medium with 0.15% collagenase and 20 mM BDM) for 30 min at 37°C. After the tissue suspension was filtrated for the removal of tissue fragments, it was centrifuged (100 × g, 5 min, 4°C). The pellet was resuspended in low glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic mixed solution (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). The cell suspension was pre-plated for 60 min twice to allow attachment of NRCFs. After the non-attached NRCMs were collected and resuspended in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic mixed solution, and 100 μM 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine, they were cultured on culture dishes. Passage 2–4 NRCFs and passage 1 NRCMs were used in this study. NRCFs and NRCMs that were grown to more than 90% confluent were starved for 24 hr in serum-free low- or high-glucose DMEM, respectively.

Induction of cellular senescence

For the induction of senescence, the starved NRCFs were treated with doxorubicin (DOX 10–1,000 nM) for 24 hr. The cells were used for total RNA extraction, and the culture media were stored at −80°C and used for EV isolation.

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

qRT-PCR was performed as previously described [24, 28]. Total RNA was extracted from NRCFs by using the TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center, Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA). The first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the ReverTra Ace qPCR master mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) at 65°C for 5 min, 37°C for 15 min, and 98°C for 5 min. The PCR amplification was performed using the THUNDERBIRD Next SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo) with a pair of gene-specific primers. Real-time analysis was performed using the QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After an initial activation at 95°C for 30 sec, 40 cycles of amplifications at 95°C for 5 sec and 60°C for 10 sec were done. Melting curve was analyzed from 60 to 95°C. Expression level of rat p16 (Cdkn2a) and p21 (Cdkn1a) mRNA was measured by ΔΔCT method (relative to GAPDH). The primer sequences were as follows: rat p16, forward 5′-GTCGTACCCCGATACAGGTGAT-3′ and reverse 5′-TACCAGAGTGTCTAGGAAGCCC-3′, rat p21, forward 5′-TGTTCCACACAGGAGCAAAG-3′ and reverse 5′-AACACGCTCCCA GACGTAGT-3′, rat GAPDH, forward 5′-GAGAATGGGAAGCTGGTCAT-3′ and reverse 5′-GAAGACGCCAGTAGACTCCA-3′.

EV isolation from culture media

NRCFs-derived EV were isolated from the media of NRCFs cultured at the similar cell density (~90%) by using Total Exosome Isolation (TEI) Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The culture media (4 mL) were centrifuged (2,000 × g, 30 min, 4°C) to eliminate cell debris, and supernatant was collected. To precipitate EV, 4 mL of the purified culture medium supernatant was incubated with 2 mL of TEI Reagent overnight at 4°C. Then, it was centrifuged (10,000 × g, 1 hr, 4°C) and the precipitant was resuspended in 200 μL of PBS or 2 mL of serum-free DMEM for Western blotting and transmission electron microscopy or cell treatment experiments, respectively. To remove contaminant, the EV suspension for cell treatment experiment was filtered using 1.0 μm filter (Membrane Solutions, Plano, TX, USA).

Protein extraction from EV

Protein extraction from EV was performed using a trichloroacetic acid (TCA)-induced precipitation and radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer-induced extraction method. Briefly, protein lysates of EV were extracted using a RIPA buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton-X, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 1% protease inhibitor mixture, and 1% phosphatase inhibitor mixture (Nacalai Tesque) at 4°C for 10 min. Next, the protein lysates were treated with 10% TCA for 30 min on ice. The precipitated proteins samples were pelleted down by a centrifugation (12,000 × g, 20 min, 4°C). The pellets were washed with an ice-cold acetone, followed by a dissolving in 20 μL of RIPA buffer.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

All procedures were carried out at room temperature. EV resuspended in PBS were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde (Nacalai Tesque) for 30 min. The fixed EV were loaded on a copper grid (EMJapan, Tokyo, Japan), which was hydrophilized using the PIB-10 apparatus (VACUUM DEVICE, Ibaraki, Japan), and incubated for 30 min. The excess EV on the grids were removed by contacting the grid edge with a filter paper. Then, the grid was washed three times with purified water for 2 min. Next, the EM stain solution (Nissin EM, Tokyo, Japan) diluted 4-fold was loaded on the grid, and it was incubated for 30 min. After the excess stain solution on the grid was removed, the grid was dried overnight. The morphology of EV was observed using the TEM apparatus (HT-7650, Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) at 80 kV.

Living cell counting

Living cell number was counted by a cell counting kit 8 (CC8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) as described previously [17, 32]. After NRCFs or NRCMs were treated with NRCFs-derived EV for 72 hr, they were washed twice with Tris-Buffered Saline (TBS). Next, CC8 solution (25 μL/0.5 mL medium) was added to the culture media at 37°C for 1 hr. Ten μL of 0.1 N HCl stop solution was added to 90 µL of the CC8-containing media, and an absorbance of the media at 485 nm was measured using a microplate reader (TriStar LB941, Berthold, Bad, Wildbad, Germany).

Measurement of protein concentration

Protein concentration of NRCFs and NRCMs was determined using a bicinchoninic acid method (Pierce, Rockfold, IL, USA). After counting the living cell number, NRCFs and NRCMs were washed twice with TBS on ice. Then, the Cell Lysis Buffer (Cell Signaling Technology) was added to them, and protein lysates were obtained by homogenizing them on ice. The composition of the Cell Lysis Buffer was as follows: 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM Na2EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1% Triton-X, 2.5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1 mM Na3VO4, and 1 μg/mL leupeptin with 0.1% protease inhibitor mixture (Nacalai Tesque).

Western blotting

The protein expression in NRCFs-derived EV, NRCFs, and NRCMs was determined by Western blotting as previously described [23, 25]. After equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE (7.5–14%, 80–120 V, 1.5 hr), they were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Pall Corp., Ann Arbor, MI, USA) (400 mA, 1.5 hr). After being blocked with 0.5% skim milk (for total protein) or 3% bovine serum albumin (for phosphorylated proteins) in TBS with 0.1% tween 20 (TBS-T), the membranes were incubated with a primary antibody (1:500 dilution in TBS-T, 4°C, overnight). They were detected by using a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution in TBS-T, 1 hr) and the chemiluminescent reagent (EZ-ECL kit; Biological Industries, Kibbutz Beit Haemek, Israel or Trident femto Western HRP Substrate; San Antonio, TX, USA). The bands were visualized by an ATTO light capture system (AE-6972: ATTO, Tokyo, Japan). The results were analyzed using CS analyzer 3.0 software (ATTO).

Determination of an autophagosome formation by an immunofluorescence staining

Autophagosome formation was examined by an immunofluorescence staining using a specific antibody against LC3 as described previously [15]. After treatment with NRCFs-derived EV for 72 hr, NRCFs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 at 37°C for 1 min. After being blocked for 1 hr with 5% normal goat serum, the cells were incubated with LC3 antibody (1:250 dilution) at 4°C overnight, which was visualized using a fluorescent-dye-conjugated secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The images were taken using a fluorescent microscope (BX-51; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital camera (DP74; Olympus).

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as means ± standard error of mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s test. Values of P<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

DOX induced cellular senescence in NRCFs

To establish an in vitro cellular senescence model, NRCFs were treated with DOX (10–1,000 nM, 24 hr). Cellular senescence is characterized by a highly stable cell cycle arrest, resulting in an alteration in cell morphology and an increased expression of cellular senescence-associated genes [13]. DOX (1,000 nM, 24 hr) changed the morphology of NRCFs to swelling one (white dashed line in Supplementary Fig. 1A). p16 and p21 are well-established markers of cellular senescence that regulate the cell cycle. DOX (1,000 nM, 24 hr) significantly increased the mRNA expression of p16 and p21 in NRCFs (Supplementary Fig. 1B). From the above results, we concluded that the in vitro cellular senescence model was successfully established by a treatment with DOX (1,000 nM, 24 hr) in NRCFs.

Confirmation of a successful isolation of EV from culture media of NRCFs

In Western blotting, the protein expression of Alix and TSG-101 (well-known marker proteins of EV) was observed after treatment with DOX (0–1,000 nM, 24 hr) (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Cup-shaped particles were observed in NRCFs-derived EV by using TEM (arrow in Supplementary Fig. 2B). The arrowhead indicates a spike control with a diameter of 110 nm. The size of EV was reported to be approximately 50–150 nm in diameter [34]. Therefore, the particles observed in this experiment show the size, which is consistent with the previously reported one.

Effects of senescent cell-derived EV on cell number and protein concentration in NRCFs

We first examined the effects of NRCFs-derived EV (72 hr) on cell number in NRCFs. D0-EV tended to increase the cell number compared with Control. On the other hand, D103-EV tended to decrease the cell number compared with D0-EV (Fig. 1A). We next examined the effects of NRCFs-derived EV (72 hr) on protein concentration in NRCFs. D0-EV had no effect on protein concentration compared with Control. On the other hand, D103-EV tended to decrease protein concentration compared with Control (Fig. 1B).

Effects of senescent cell-derived EV on autophagy in NRCFs

Since the protein concentration of NRCFs tended to be decreased by D103-EV, we next examined whether senescent cell-derived EV (72 hr) induced autophagy, a highly conserved intracellular process for protein degradation in eukaryotic cells, in NRCFs. D0-EV had no effect on the ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I compared with Control. On the other hand, D103-EV significantly increase the ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I compared with Control (Fig. 2A). When autophagy is induced, intracellular fluorescent dot (punctate LC3) can be observed by an immunofluorescence staining using a specific antibody against LC3 [20]. No puncta formation was observed in Control, while puncta formation was observed after treatment with D103-EV (Fig. 2B). We confirmed that the puncta formation was observed after treatment with rapamycin (1 μM, 24 hr), a known inducer of autophagy (Fig. 2B).

Effects of senescent cell-derived EV on AMPKα signaling in NRCFs

Since it is known that autophagy is induced by the activation of AMPKα (phosphorylation of AMPKα), we next examined whether senescent cell-derived EV (72 hr) activate AMPKα. D0-EV had no effect on phosphorylation of AMPKα compared with Control. On the other hand, D103-EV tended to increase phosphorylation of AMPKα compared with Control (Fig. 3).

Effects of senescent cell-derived EV on autophagy in NRCMs

To investigate whether NRCFs-EV induce autophagy in other cardiac cell types, we examined the effects of NRCFs-derived EV (72 hr) on autophagy in NRCMs. NRCFs-derived EV (D0–D103) had no effect on cell number in NRCMs compared with Control (Fig. 4A). NRCFs-derived EV (D0–D103) had also no effect on protein concentration in NRCMs compared with Control (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, NRCFs-derived EV (D0–D103) had no effect on the ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I in NRCMs compared with Control (Fig. 4C).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we for the first time revealed that the senescent NRCFs-derived EV induced autophagy in NRCFs perhaps partly through the activation of AMPK (Fig. 5). Since NRCFs-derived EV induced autophagy in NRCFs but not NRCMs, it seems likely that the senescent cardiac fibroblasts-derived EV play an autocrine role in the heart cells.

Summary of the present results. Senescent neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (NRCFs)-derived extracellular vesicles (EV) induced autophagy in NRCFs at least partly via activation of adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) α. On the other hand, senescent NRCFs-derived EV did not induce autophagy in neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs). LC3: microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3.

We showed that the senescent NRCFs-derived EV induced autophagy in NRCFs. Of note, NRCFs-derived EV tended to decrease protein content without decreasing cell number. It was reported that rapamycin treatment decreased the total protein content per cell in human osteosarcoma U2OS cells [9]. Therefore, it is assumed that the decrease in the protein concentration due to the treatment with NRCFs-derived EV is not a specific phenomenon but is generally induced by autophagy. It was reported that EV-cargo was changed by a state of cellular senescence and aging. For instance, miR-183-5p in bone marrow-derived EV increased with aging. MiR-183-5p was reported to suppress the proliferation of bone marrow stromal cell, inducing stem cell senescence [8]. In addition, EV sorting of EphA2 (Eph receptor tyrosine kinase) was increased in the senescent TIG-3 cells. EV-associated EphA2 was reported to bind to ephrin-A1, that is highly expressed in several types of cancer cells, promoting cell proliferation [31]. The senescent cells also increase the expression of secretory proteins, which is called SASP. Moreover, a previous study showed that the stress-induced cellular senescence altered the expression of intracellular miRNA [18]. In that study, the induction of cellular senescence increased the expression of intracellular miR-200c. Another report showed that miR-200c induced autophagy in PC-3 cells, a human prostate cancer cell line [27]. Furthermore, miRNA such as miR-15a/16 and miR-100 was reported to induce autophagy [10]. Therefore, it is suggested that the induction of cellular senescence by the treatment with DOX (1,000 nM, 24 hr) increased autophagy-regulating miRNA such as miR-200c, miR-15a/16, and miR-100 in NRCFs-derived EV. In addition, EV are known to contain the parent cell-derived molecular cargo, such as functional proteins and non-coding RNA other than miRNA [1]. We then need a comprehensive analysis of the expression of functional proteins and non-coding RNA including miRNA in the senescent NRCFs-derived EV to elucidate the detailed mechanisms of autophagy induction by EV.

We showed that the senescent NRCFs-derived EV induced autophagy in NRCFs perhaps partly through the activation of AMPKα. Similarly, it was reported that the umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived EV activated AMPK/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)-mediated autophagy pathway, alleviating viral myocarditis [11]. The mesenchymal stem cell-derived EV also reduced myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury by inducing cardiomyocyte autophagy via AMPK/mTOR and Akt/mTOR pathways [19]. However, these studies did not clarify the detailed mechanisms including the specific EV cargo that activated AMPK. AMPK is one of the intracellular energy sensors, which is also known as a positive regulator of autophagy [12]. It is a heterotrimeric protein comprised of a catalytic subunit (α) and 2 regulatory subunits (β and γ). Of the 2 isoforms of the α-subunit, the α2 isoform was shown abundant in cardiomyocytes [2]. Therefore, it is suggested that the AMPK-autophagic activity in cardiomyocyte might be higher than that in cardiac fibroblasts. In the present study, however, the autophagy induced by the senescent NRCFs-derived EV was observed only in NRCFs, but not in NRCMs. Thus, it is assumed that the differences in the expression of AMPKα2 may not be responsible for the difference in the effects of EV between cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts. The EV internalization in recipient cells has been reported to occur through multiple routes that involve endocytosis and pinocytosis [21]. The EV taken up by the recipient cells via endocytosis released their cargo into an intracellular space by a membrane fusion, otherwise they were targeted to lysosome for degradation [21, 22]. Many studies have not assessed the end point of EV internalization such as cargo delivery or EV degradation. Thus, the molecular mechanisms that determine whether the cargo transfer or degradation occurs remain unclear [21]. It seems likely that the expression levels of endocytosis-associated proteins (clathrin, caveolin, etc.) do not differ between cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts. On the other hand, it seems likely that the expression level of Fyn kinase, an upstream suppressor of AMPK, in cardiac fibroblasts is higher than that in cardiomyocytes [16]. Therefore, it is suggested that miRNA targeting Fyn kinase is one of the molecules which are responsible for the difference in the effects of EV between cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts.

In conclusion, it was for the first time revealed that the senescent NRCFs-derived EV induced autophagy only in NRCFs perhaps partly through the activation of AMPKα. Since the present study did not clarify whether the autophagy induction by EV was protective or promotive in cardiac pathology, further researches are needed to elucidate this question.
