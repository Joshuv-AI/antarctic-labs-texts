Rice E3 ubiquitin ligases: From key modulators of host immunity to potential breeding applications
Authors: Yuqing Yan, Hui Wang, Yan Bi, Fengming Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To combat pathogen attacks, plants have developed a highly advanced immune system, which requires tight regulation to initiate robust defense responses while simultaneously preventing autoimmunity. The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), which is responsible for degrading excess or misfolded proteins, has vital roles in ensuring strong and effective immune responses. E3 ligases, as key UPS components, play extensively documented roles in rice immunity by modulating the ubiquitination and degradation of downstream substrates involved in various immune signaling pathways. Here, we summarize the crucial roles of rice E3 ligases in both pathogen/microbe/damage-associated molecular pattern-triggered immunity and effector-triggered immunity, highlight the molecular mechanisms by which E3 ligases function in rice immune signaling, and emphasize the functions of E3 ligases as targets of pathogen effectors for pathogenesis. We also discuss potential strategies for application of immunity-associated E3 ligases in breeding of disease-resistant rice varieties without growth penalty. This review provides a comprehensive and updated understanding of the sophisticated and interconnected regulatory functions of E3 ligases in rice immunity and in balancing immunity with growth and development.

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa) is a fundamental food crop worldwide, with production that significantly influences the stable expansion of the global economy. During its lifespan, rice is frequently confronted with numerous pathogenic microorganisms, resulting in substantial losses of yield and quality (Liu et al., 2021a). To counteract these threats, rice, like other plants, has developed a dual-layered innate immune system that includes pathogen/microbe/damage-associated molecular pattern (P/M/DAMP)-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI) (Ngou et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2024). PTI, the first line of defense, relies on the recognition of P/M/DAMPs governed by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and results in a comparatively weak immune response that limits the colonization of all potential pathogenic microorganisms (Yuan et al., 2021a; Zhang et al., 2023). Evolutionarily adapted pathogens have evolved diverse pathogenicity-associated molecules, including effectors, to evade or suppress PTI, leading to effector-triggered susceptibility (ETS) (Zhou and Zhang, 2020). To counter ETS, the second line of defense, ETI, is initiated upon recognition of pathogen effectors by the archetypal nucleotide-binding domain, leucine-rich-repeat-containing receptor (NLR) proteins (Yuan et al., 2021b; Nabi et al., 2024). Although PTI and ETI are triggered by distinct activation mechanisms involving various early signaling components, the two-layered innate immune responses ultimately converge on or share similar downstream signaling pathways, including the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades, calcium fluxes, genome-wide transcriptional reprogramming, and phytohormone signaling (Peng et al., 2018; Thulasi Devendrakumar et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2024). Because of their vital roles in safeguarding plants against pathogen attacks, PTI and ETI are tightly controlled at diverse levels under homeostasis conditions. In general, immune regulators need to be eliminated in a timely fashion to activate effective immune response upon pathogen infection and to restore immune homeostasis once immune responses are complete, thereby balancing plant growth–immunity trade-offs (Copeland and Li, 2019). Deactivation of diverse immune regulators can be achieved through protein ubiquitination and degradation mediated by the ubiquitin–26S proteasome system (UPS) (Yin et al., 2019).

The protein ubiquitination process is achieved by an integrated three-step conjugation cascade involving an E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme, an E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme, and an E3 ubiquitin ligase. The substrate proteins of the ubiquitin system may be (1) monoubiquitylated and multi-monoubiquitylated, in which a single ubiquitin molecule is added to one or multiple sites, or (2) polyubiquitylated, in which additional ubiquitin molecules are added to the first ubiquitin molecule, forming a polyubiquitin chain on the substrate site (Liu et al., 2024). In general, monoubiquitination or multi-monoubiquitination regulates protein–protein interactions, activation, or localization, whereas polyubiquitination is a prerequisite needed to facilitate protein recycling through the UPS (Langin et al., 2023). Within the ubiquitin system, E3 ligases are primarily responsible for specifically recognizing substrates and ensuring the selective ubiquitination process (Liu et al., 2024). On the basis of their structures, E3 ligases can be divided into four major types, including three monomeric families (homology to the E6-associated protein C terminus [HETC] E3 ligases, plant U-box [PUB] ligases, and really interesting new gene [RING] ligases) and one multimeric family, the Cullin–RING E3 ligases (CRLs) (Vierstra, 2009). The rice genome harbors >1000 predicted genes encoding putative E3 ligases (Chen and Hellmann, 2013), and significant breakthroughs have been made in characterizing the functions of E3 ligases in rice immunity during the past two decades (Ning et al., 2016; Kong et al., 2021). Notably, most of the reported studies have focused on the E3 ligase-mediated polyubiquitination of downstream substrates and their degradation in rice immunity.

In this review, we provide a comprehensive update on the functions of E3 ligases in rice immunity and highlight the regulatory mechanisms of E3 ligases in rice immune signaling. We also focus on the regulation of E3 ligases in rice immunity and the involvement of E3 ligases as targets of pathogen effectors for susceptibility. Characterizing the immune functions of E3 ligases and revealing the underlying mechanisms of E3 ligase-regulated immune responses will facilitate the development of innovative strategies for breeding and creation of novel rice varieties with improved broad-spectrum resistance.

Functions of E3 ligases in rice immunity

E3 ligases in rice PTI

PTI signaling relies on the direct recognition of P/M/DAMPs by cell-surface receptors, particularly PRRs, including receptor-like kinases (RLKs) and receptor-like proteins (RLPs) (Boutrot and Zipfel, 2017). Upon binding of microbial ligands, RLKs and RLPs recruit coreceptors to form receptor complexes that phosphorylate receptor-like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs). RLCKs then activate a range of substrates, resulting in significant physiological changes such as an ROS burst, MAPK activation, defense hormone production, and transcriptional reprogramming (Tang et al., 2017a; Zhang et al., 2020a).

A growing body of evidence suggests that E3 ligase-mediated ubiquitination is involved in rice PTI against multiple pathogens (Table 1). Most of the identified rice RING-finger type E3 ligases are positive immune regulators. Specifically, BLAST AND BTH-INDUCED 1 (OsBBI1) modulates broad-spectrum resistance against multiple races of Magnaporthe oryzae, the causative agent of rice blast, through the modification of cell-wall defense responses (Li et al., 2011). The RGLG RING-type ligase OsRGLG5, Decreased grain size 1 (DGS1), and AvrPiz-t interacting protein 10 (APIP10) are positively involved in flg22- or chitin-triggered ROS bursts, defense gene expression, and resistance against M. oryzae (Park et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a). Zinc-finger C3H2C3-type domain-containing protein 1 (OsZFP1, also known as OsUBC26 Interact Protein 2, UCIP2), APIP6 (also named IPA1 interactor 7 [IPI7]), and ideal plant architecture 1 interactor 1 (IPI1; also named ROD1 interacting protein 1 [RIP1]) promote plant resistance against M. oryzae, Rhizoctonia solani, or Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) (Park et al., 2012; Gao et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021b; Shi et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2024). Similarly, OsRING113 enhances rice immunity against M. oryzae and Xoo, which is accompanied by chitin-elicited MAPK activation, an ROS burst, and upregulated defense gene expression (Zhang et al., 2024a). RING-H2 finger E3 ubiquitin ligase 2-10 (OsRFPH2-10) facilitates antiviral defense against rice dwarf virus (RDV) infection (Liu et al., 2014), and Microtubule-associated C4HC3-type E3 ligase (OsMEL) positively regulates broad-spectrum resistance to rice stripe virus (RSV), M. oryzae, and Xoo (Fu et al., 2022). To prevent overactivation of the immune system, plants have developed distinct mechanisms to maintain immune homeostasis, which involve the functions of diverse negative immune regulators, including some E3 ligases. The RING-finger type E3 ligase OsRFPH2-6, RING-finger E3 ubiquitin ligase interacting with WRKY1 (OsREIW1), Enhanced blight and blast resistance 1 (EBR1), the Arabidopsis Tóxicos En Levadura (ATL) subfamily member OsATL32, and the Jumonji C domain-containing protein OsJMJ715 contribute negatively to rice immunity against pathogens or insect pests (You et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2021a; Bi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023a; Yan et al., 2024a). OsREWI1 and OsRFPH2-6 inhibit the chitin-induced ROS burst and weaken rice immunity against M. oryzae (Bi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023a). Among the PUB-type E3 ligases, spotted leaf 11 (SPL11) and OsCERK1-interacting-E3 1 (OsCIE1) have redundant and negative roles, whereas OsPUB15 functions positively in plant cell death (PCD) and rice immunity against M. oryzae and Xoo (Zeng et al., 2004; Liu et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2015, 2024b). Specifically, spl11 and Oscie1 mutants demonstrated activated resistance to M. oryzae and Xoo, with an ROS burst and upregulation of defense genes, and the spl11/Oscie1 double mutant displayed more pronounced PCD and significantly elevated ROS levels (Liu et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2024b). OsPUB15 confers enhanced resistance to multiple isolates of M. oryzae, which is correlated with upregulated expression of defense genes and elevated accumulation of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (Wang et al., 2015). In addition, OsPUB41 positively regulates salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis, intensifies callose deposition, and upregulates defense gene expression, thereby enhancing immunity against Xoo (Kachewar et al., 2019). OsPUB44 facilitates chitin-triggered and peptidoglycan-triggered immune responses and Xoo resistance (Ishikawa et al., 2014), suggesting the involvement of OsPUB41 and OsPUB44 in enhancement of basal resistance against Xoo. OsPUB73 knockout mutants developed larger disease lesions and showed downregulation of defense genes upon M. oryzae or Xoo infection (Hao et al., 2022), and the PID2-interacting E3 (OsPIE3) mutant ospie3 displayed enhanced resistance and increased expression of defense genes upon M. oryzae invasion (Wang et al., 2023b), indicating a positive function for OsPUB73 and a negative role for OsPIE3 in basal resistance to M. oryzae and Xoo. Overexpression of P3-inducible protein 1 (P3IP1) led to enhanced susceptibility to rice grassy stunt virus (RGSV), indicating that P3IP1 plays a critical role in the susceptibility of rice plants to RGSV infection (Zhang et al., 2020b).

Multiple key components of multimeric Cullin–RING complexes have also been implicated in rice immunity. OsCUL3a, a component of Cullin3-based RING E3 ubiquitin ligases (CRL3s), DDB1 binding WD (OsDWD1), a substrate receptor for the CRL4 E3 complex, and F-box-type E3 ligase OsFBK16, a subunit of SCF E3 complexes, have negative roles in rice immunity. OsFBK16 and OsDWD1 contribute negatively to rice immunity against M. oryzae and Xoo, respectively, and OsCUL3a abolishes resistance to both M. oryzae and Xoo, accompanied by reduced ROS accumulation or suppressed SA and JA signaling (Liu et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022a). By contrast, coronatine-insensitive 1 (OsCOI1), EIN3-binding F-box protein 1 (OsEBF1), and OsEBF2, as components of SCF E3 ligase complexes, function positively in rice immunity against M. oryzae, Xoo, rice black-streaked dwarf virus (RBSDV), or BPH (Ye et al., 2012; He et al., 2017a; Ma et al., 2020, 2023).

E3 ligases in rice ETI

ETI signaling is initiated upon direct or indirect recognition of pathogen effectors by plant NLRs, resulting in a hypersensitive response and increased resistance (Yuan et al., 2021b; Jones et al., 2024). Notably, direct communication between plant NLRs and pathogen effectors is rarely observed; instead, numerous NLRs are thought to exploit plant self-proteins, which generally play crucial roles in plant immune signaling and are targeted by pathogen effectors for infection (Jones et al., 2016). Although the biochemical and molecular mechanisms by which NLR activation triggers downstream ETI signaling remain largely unknown, emerging studies have demonstrated that E3 ligases play indispensable roles in ETI by associating with plant NLRs or pathogen effectors (Liu et al., 2024) (Table 1). Upon recognition of a tyrosine-sulfated RaxX peptide secreted by Xoo, the pattern recognition receptor XA21, which confers gene-for-gene resistance to Xoo strains carrying RaxX (Pruitt et al., 2015), specifically induces the rice NLR gene XinN1, implying a potential role for XinN1 in XA21-mediated Xoo resistance (Moon et al., 2024). During Xoo infection, XA21 activates XA21 binding protein 3 (XB3), a RING-finger type E3 ligase, through transphosphorylation, which in turn controls the protein accumulation of XA21, thereby contributing to XA21-mediated ETI against Xoo (Wang et al., 2006). Despite its beneficial role in rice basal resistance to blast disease, APIP10 negatively effects ETI triggered by the NLR protein Piz-t by accelerating the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of Piz-t, the cognate NLR protein of the M. oryzae avirulence effector AvrPiz-t. This scenario is supported by the fact that knockdown of APIP10 in the Piz-t background attenuates PTI but strengthens resistance to a virulent M. oryzae isolate carrying AvrPiz-t (Li et al., 2009; Park et al., 2016). However, our current understanding of the involvement of E3 ligases in ETI is relatively limited.

Several immunity-associated E3 ligases, including APIP6, IPI1, OsMEL, EBR1, OsATL32, SPL11, and OsCIE1, modulate broad-spectrum resistance against multiple pathogens (Table 1). However, some E3 ligases, e.g., OsRGLG5, OsREIW1, OsPUB44, and OsPIE3, seem to play roles in immunity against a single disease (Table 1), and the functions of these E3 ligases, especially those with verified functions in PTI, in rice resistance against other diseases need to be clarified. These features reflect the complexity and diversity of the E3 ligases in rice immunity. Considering the critical and distinct functions of E3 ligases in both PTI and ETI, it is essential to investigate the sophisticated and intricate regulatory mechanisms of E3 ligases in these two layers of rice immunity.

Regulation of immune receptors

Upon the recognition of P/M/DAMPs, plant PRRs transmit immune signals that branch into MAPK cascades or an ROS burst by activating downstream RLCKs (Tang et al., 2017a; Zhang et al., 2023). To avoid the detrimental consequences of constitutively activated plant signal responses, PRRs and their downstream components are tightly controlled by ubiquitination (Copeland and Li, 2019). Accumulating evidence indicates that numerous RLKs function with E3 ligases to coordinate immune responses in rice (Trenner et al., 2022). For instance, OsPIE3 directly targets the B-lectin RLK PID2, modifying its translocation for subsequent degradation via the UPS and modulating PID2-initiated immune responses against M. oryzae (Zeng et al., 2004). SPL11, a negative regulator of PCD and resistance, ubiquitinates SDS2, an S-domain receptor-like kinase, and effects the activation of the RLCKs OsRLCK118 and OsRLCK176, thereby controlling defense responses (Fan et al., 2018). OsCIE1 and SPL11 restrict the kinase activity of OsCERK1, an essential RLK receptor that recognizes fungal chitin, through a ubiquitination process to prevent excessive immune responses during homeostasis. Upon chitin perception, the ligase activity of OsCIE1 is suppressed by OsCERK1 through direct phosphorylation, providing a mechanism by which OsCIE1 acts as a ligand-released brake controlling OsCERK1-mediated immune signaling (Wang et al., 2024b). Moreover, XB3 directly ubiquitinates RLK XA21, a key regulator for specific ETI against Xoo, thereby modulating XA21-mediated resistance (Wang et al., 2006). Collectively, these findings shed light on a general mechanism by which plasma membrane-associated RLKs associate with the E3 ligase-mediated protein degradation process during rice immune signaling.

Regulation of the ROS burst

ROS, including H2O2, superoxide anion (O⋅−), hydroxyl radical (⋅OH), and singlet oxygen (1O2), are critical signaling molecules broadly involved in plant growth, development, and environmental responses (Mittler et al., 2022). Among PTI signaling events, the ROS burst plays a central regulatory role in activating plant immune responses (Kou et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2023). ROS homeostasis is tightly controlled, primarily by plasma membrane-localized NADPH oxidases for generation and by ROS-scavenging antioxidant enzymes, including catalases and superoxide dismutases, for scavenging (Waszczak et al., 2018; Liu and Zhang, 2022). Recent studies have revealed the regulation of ROS through a ubiquitination process in which E3 ligases influence ROS homeostasis to modulate rice immune responses.

Cytosolic Ca2+, a prominent second messenger integrated into early immune signaling, is elevated upon pathogen infection (Köster et al., 2022). The Ca2+ sensor RESISTANCE OF RICE TO DISEASES 1 (ROD1), a global regulator of rice immunity, associates with catalase CatB to facilitate its H2O2 scavenging activity, forming a ROD1–CatB complex that counteracts the Ca2+-induced ROS burst and functions as a hub for immune suppression (Yuan et al., 2017a; Gao et al., 2021). The M. oryzae avirulence effector AvrPiz-t structurally mimics ROD1 to manipulate the ROD1-mediated ROS-scavenging cascade, thereby enhancing virulence and suppressing host immune response (Gao et al., 2021). However, the stability of ROD1 is precisely regulated by APIP6 and IPI1, which are required for inducing ROD1 destruction and sustaining ROS production, thereby guaranteeing reliable initiation of the immune response (Gao et al., 2021). The rice peroxisome receptor protein OsPEX5, an integral component of peroxisomes, interacts with the negative immune regulator catalase OsCATC and negatively affects blast resistance (You et al., 2023). OsPEX5 is ubiquitinated by APIP6, influencing the subsequent import of matrix proteins and peroxisome homeostasis (Wang and Subramani, 2017; You et al., 2023). Interestingly, APIP6 directly targets OsCATC for degradation and reduces OsCATC-mediated ROS scavenging to promote immune responses (You et al., 2022), implying that it functions in rice immunity by modulating ROS scavenging in the peroxisomes. Collectively, these findings characterize a regulatory network by which APIP6 functions in rice immunity by directly or indirectly modulating the catalase-mediated ROS-scavenging cascade (Yuan et al., 2017b; Wang et al., 2022b). APIP6 targets the M. oryzae avirulence effector AvrPiz-t for degradation to restrict infection, similar to OsRGLG5-mediated protein turnover of the M. oryzae effector AvrPi9 (Park et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2023). In addition, APIP6 and IPI1 form hetero-oligomers to synergistically modulate the degradation of the early-flowering 3 (ELF3) paralogs OsELF3-1 and OsELF3-2, which work together to regulate immunity-associated genes and influence plant defense, thereby positively modulating blast resistance (Lu et al., 2012; Ning et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2024). Therefore, it is likely that APIP6 may fine-tune rice growth and immunity by precisely regulating the degradation of its substrates, including ROD1, OsPEX5, OsCATC, OsELF3s, and AvrPiz-t (Table 2), making it a promising gene for disease-resistance breeding.

Small guanosine triphosphatases (GTPases) act as molecular switches for multiple signaling pathways in rice, and Rho GTPase-activating (RhoGAP) proteins are engaged in catalyzing the conversion of small GTPases from the GTP-bound state into the inactive guanosine diphosphate (GDP)-bound state (Stöckle et al., 2016). Upon recognition of PAMPs or pathogen effectors, OsRac1, a small GTPase, is activated by PRR and NLR receptors, respectively, resulting in the stimulation of ROS generation by coupling with the plasma membrane-localized OsRbohB, the primary NADPH oxidase responsible for ROS production, and thus transducing immune signaling in both PTI and ETI (Wong et al., 2007; Kawano et al., 2010; Akamatsu et al., 2013; Nagano et al., 2016). SPL11 interacts with and mediates the degradation of the RhoGAP protein SPIN6, which converts the GTP-bound OsRac1 to the inactive GDP-bound state and negatively modulates OsRac1-mediated immune signaling. SPIN6 connects the immune signals from SPL11 to the small GTPase OsRac1, thereby activating rice immune responses (Liu et al., 2015). Similarly, OsATL32 directly targets an ROS-producing module that consists of the small GTPase OsRac5 and the NADPH oxidase OsRbohB for degradation, thereby undermining rice immunity against M. oryzae (Yan et al., 2024a). These findings directly connect E3 ligases with the ROS production cascade mediated by the small GTPase–OsRboh module in rice immune responses. Furthermore, SPL11 ubiquitinates RLK SDS2, affecting the activities of downstream RLCKs in phosphorylating OsRbohB to induce the ROS burst (Fan et al., 2018), a finding that offers novel insights into indirect regulation of the ROS burst by SPL11. DGS1 and OsMEL are required for the regulation of ROS homeostasis in rice immune responses through mediation of the ubiquitination and degradation of their substrates, aquaporin OsPIP2;1 and serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT1), respectively (Fu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024a). Collectively, E3 ligases appear to have prominent functions in fine-tuning the ROS burst by directly or indirectly targeting distinct regulators of ROS homeostasis for ubiquitination and degradation during rice immune responses.

Regulation of MAPK cascades

Upon perception of chitin elicitors, the chitin elicitor-binding protein OsCEBiP associates with OsCERK1 to form a receptor complex that phosphorylates OsRLCK185 to activate MAPK cascades (Tang et al., 2017a). The MAPK signaling module is composed of the MKKK–MKK–MPK cascade, in which MKK kinases (MKKKs) activate the MPK kinases (MKKs) to phosphorylate MPKs (Zhang et al., 2018; Van Gerrewey and Chung, 2024). Notably, the OsMKK10.2–OsMPK6 module is necessary for resistance against M. oryzae mediated by the transcription factor OsWRKY45, in which OsMKK10.2 activates OsMPK6 to phosphorylate and activate OsWRKY45 (Ueno et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2017). OsPUB44-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of OsPUB44 INTERACTING 1 (PBI1) prevents OsWRKY45 from PBI1 suppression (Ichimaru et al., 2022). The perception of chitin activates both the OsMKK10.2–OsMPK6 module and OsPUB44-mediated PBI1 degradation, which cooperatively regulate the activity of OsWRKY45, ultimately contributing to the rapid initiation of immune responses (Ichimaru et al., 2022). Similarly, OsWRKY31 can be phosphorylated by the OsMKK10.2–OsMPK3 module, acting as a downstream regulator in OsMKK10.2-mediated blast resistance (Wang et al., 2023a). OsWRKY31 is directly ubiquitinated by OsREIW1, but its ubiquitination can be attenuated by its phosphorylation status, suggesting the competitive functions of OsREIW1-mediated ubiquitination and OsMKK10.2–OsMPK3-involved phosphorylation in regulating the stability and activity of OsWRKY31 (Wang et al., 2023a). Collectively, these findings highlight novel regulatory mechanisms by which MAPK-cascade-mediated phosphorylation and ubiquitination-based degradation cooperatively control the activities of rice immune regulators such as OsWRKY45 and OsWRKY31. Interestingly, MEL exhibits high similarity to the N-terminal region of human MEKK1, and overexpression of plant MEKK1 (homologous to human MEKK1) resulted in continuous activation of MAP kinase (Yang et al., 2021), suggesting a potential role for MEL in directly initiating plant MAP kinases. Given the crucial roles of E3 ligases in MAPK-cascade-mediated rice immunity, it will be very interesting to untangle the intertwined interplay between MAPK signaling and E3 ligase-mediated ubiquitination.

Regulation of hormone signaling

Phytohormones, such as JA, SA, ethylene (ET), abscisic acid (ABA), and brassinosteroid (BR), have been extensively investigated for their involvement in plant immunity (Nasir et al., 2018; Nolan et al., 2020; Ding et al., 2022). Exogenously applied JA induced significant resistance against M. oryzae and Xoo, underscoring the pivotal role of JA in rice immunity (Mei et al., 2006; Yamada et al., 2012). Strikingly, OsCOI1, a component of the SCF E3 ligase complex, acts as the principal JA receptor (Sheard et al., 2010). In response to elevated JA levels, the OsCOI1-based SCF E3 complex is initiated to degrade OsJAZ8, a transcriptional repressor of JA signaling, thus leading to rapid activation of JA responses and JA-dependent immune responses (Yamada et al., 2012). Moreover, OsJMJ715 negatively regulates the BPH-induced biosynthesis of ABA, JA, and JA-Ile, and OsEBF1 and OsEBF2 target OsEIL1, the key regulator of ET signaling, for degradation, promoting rice resistance to BPH by synergistically modulating JA and ET signaling pathways (Ma et al., 2020, 2023; Zhang et al., 2021a).

Rice non-expresser of pathogenesis-related genes 1 (OsNPR1) represses JA responses by modulating SA signaling, and SA–OsNPR1 signaling potentially contributes to XA21-conferred resistance against Xoo (Spoel et al., 2003). OsNPR1 has positive roles in rice PCD and immunity, as OsNPR1-overexpressing lines exhibited increased Xoo resistance, upregulated expression of defense genes, and benzothiadiazole-induced PCD (Chern et al., 2005; Yuan et al., 2007; Bai et al., 2011). Both OsCUL3a and OsDWD1 interact with and facilitate the degradation of OsNPR1, suggesting their involvement in OsNPR1-dependent immune responses (Liu et al., 2017; Choi et al., 2022). OsWRKY45, fine-tuned by E3 ligase-based ubiquitination and the MAPK cascade, plays an essential role in SA-mediated defense responses (Shimono et al., 2007; Ichimaru et al., 2022). Intriguingly, rice Xoo resistance is partially governed by SA signaling, with JA and SA signaling compensating for each other in the rice–Xoo interaction (Ding and Ding, 2020; Hou and Tsuda, 2022). Overexpression of OsPUB41 led to enhanced expression of SA and JA biosynthetic genes and increased Xoo resistance, revealing the involvement of OsPUB41 in hormone-regulated immune responses against Xoo (Kachewar et al., 2019).

In addition to its crucial roles in plant growth and development, BR signaling is also involved in rice resistance against diverse pathogens (Ortiz-Morea et al., 2020). Exogenous application of BR analogs induced rice resistance to M. oryzae and Xoo (Nakashita et al., 2003) but reduced resistance to RBSDV and the root oomycete Pythium graminicola (De Vleesschauwer et al., 2012; He et al., 2017a). Among the identified BR signaling regulators, several E3 ligases, including DGS1, OsATL32, and OsPUB73, have been demonstrated to participate in rice immunity (Hao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024a; Yan et al., 2024b). DGS1, a negative regulator of rice blast resistance, directly ubiquitinates glycogen synthase kinase 3 (OsGSK3), a BR signaling suppressor, thus playing a dual role in both BR signaling and immunity (Wang et al., 2024a). OsATL32 serves as a node connecting BR signaling and immunity by forming a loop with the Kelch-like repeat-domain-containing phosphatase OsPPKL2 and the glycogen synthase kinase OsGSK2, in which OsATL32 ubiquitinates and promotes the degradation of OsPPKL2, which is involved in dephosphorylating OsGSK2, a key inhibitor of BR signaling, and reducing its activity (Yan et al., 2024b). OsPUB73 targets the VQ-motif protein OsVQ25, which suppresses the transcriptional activation of downstream defense and BR signaling genes by OsWRKY53, establishing an E3 ligase–VQ protein–transcription factor module that fine-tunes rice growth and immunity (Tian et al., 2017; Hao et al., 2022). However, only some E3 ligases, including DGS1, OsATL32, OsCOI1- and OsEBF1/OsEBF2-based SCF E3 ligase complexes, the OsCUL3a-containing CRL3 E3 ligase, and the OsDWD1-based CRL4 complex, have been integrated in hormone-signaling-regulated immune responses through direct ubiquitination of OsJAZ8, OsEIL1, OsNPR1, OsPPKL2, and OsGSK3 (Table 2), key regulators of JA, ET, SA, and BR signaling pathways, respectively (Ye et al., 2012; He et al., 2017a; Liu et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2020, 2023; Choi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024a; Yan et al., 2024b). These findings provide novel perspectives on the role of E3 ligase-mediated ubiquitination in modulating key immune-related hormone signaling in rice immunity, although the detailed mechanisms of the interplay between hormone signaling and E3 ligase-mediated signaling in rice immunity need to be clarified.

Regulation of transcriptional reprogramming

As an integral part of plant immunity, the immune transcriptome is determined by the coordinated control of various transcriptional regulatory components, such as transcription factors (TFs), mediator complex, DNA and RNA modifiers, and chromatin remodelers (Aerts et al., 2022). TF-governed transcriptional reprogramming is one of the most important events in activating plant immune response upon pathogen infection by decoding the regulatory information in diverse immune signaling pathways (Birkenbihl et al., 2017; Aerts et al., 2022). The DnaJ chaperone protein OsDjA6 associates with and facilitates OsZFP1, forming a regulatory module that attenuates blast resistance by modulating the expression of OsWRKY45 and other defense genes (Shimono et al., 2012; Zhong et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2021b). APIP5, a bZIP TF, is ubiquitinated by OsRING113 to alleviate its transcriptional inhibition of the Bowman–Birk trypsin inhibitor (BBTI) family genes OsBBTI5 and APIP4, thereby attenuating their trypsin inhibitor activity and weakening broad-spectrum disease resistance against M. oryzae and Xoo (Zhang et al., 2024a). APIP10 directly ubiquitinates the TFs VASCULAR PLANT ONE–ZINC-FINGER 1 (OsVOZ1) and OsVOZ2 and the NLR protein Piz-t for degradation. APIP10, OsVOZ1, and OsVOZ2 synergistically modulate the abundance of the NLR protein Piz-t at both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels (Park et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021), revealing a novel mechanism through which E3 ligase associates with TFs to regulate NLR-mediated immunity. Moreover, a recent study demonstrated that IPI7-mediated K29 polyubiquitination is required for phosphorylated ideal plant architecture 1 (IPA1), a TF, to transactivate OsWRKY45 for initiating rice immune responses (Shi et al., 2024). These studies indicate that E3 ligase-mediated ubiquitination plays an essential role in rice immunity by modulating the degradation of immune-related transcriptional regulatory machineries to regulate pathogen-induced transcriptome reprogramming. Furthermore, several lines of evidence support the involvement of E3 ligases in plant immunity through a coordinated network with epigenetic mechanisms. For instance, the E3 ligase P3IP1 targets and accelerates the degradation of OsNRPD1a, an ortholog of the largest subunit of the plant-specific RNA polymerase IV required for RNA-directed DNA methylation, thereby enhancing rice susceptibility to RGSV (Zhang et al., 2020b). In Arabidopsis, the E3 ligase SUPPRESSOR OF GENE SILENCING 3 (SGS3)-INTERACTING PROTEIN 1, which is activated by a feedback loop consisting of heat-induced HEAT SHOCK TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR A2 and the H3K27me3 demethylase RELATIVE OF EARLY-FLOWERING 6, ubiquitinates SGS for degradation, thereby inhibiting the biosynthesis of trans-acting small interfering RNA, promoting early flowering and decreasing disease resistance (Liu et al., 2019). However, whether E3 ligases can directly target components of the epigenetic machinery to regulate rice immune responses remains to be clarified.

Regulation of other immunity-related processes

Rice E3 ligases have also been shown to modulate rice immunity by regulating the stability or accumulation of other major immune regulators (Table 2). OsBBI1, a positive contributor to blast resistance, ubiquitinates and mediates the degradation of the eukaryotic translation elongation factor OseEF1A, which confers negative effects on rice immunity against M. oryzae (Wang et al., 2024c). By contrast, EBR1 performs negative functions in rice PCD and immunity by targeting OsBAG4, a Bcl-2 associated athanogene (BAG) protein involved in autoimmunity and broad-spectrum disease resistance (Li et al., 2016; You et al., 2016). OsFBK16 contributes to the degradation of multiple phenylalanine ammonia lyases (OsPALs), which are key players in broad-spectrum resistance, thereby exerting negative effects on rice immunity (Zhou et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019, 2022b). Collectively, this compelling evidence demonstrates that E3 ligases perform varied functions in rice immunity by mediating the protein levels of multiple key immune regulators.

It is worth noting that several E3 ligases, including APIP6, APIP10, IPI1, DGS1, OsATL32, and SPL11, have been shown to target diverse substrates to modulate rice immune responses through distinct regulatory mechanisms, highlighting the intricate and multifaceted functions of E3 ligases in rice immune responses (Table 2 and Figure 1). Hence, further characterization of the potential substrates of immunity-associated E3 ligases will aid in deciphering the global molecular regulatory network of E3 ligases in rice immunity.

Regulation of E3 ligases in rice immune responses

Given the crucial and diverse roles of E3 ligases in rice immunity, it is necessary to intricately modulate their activities to ensure prompt and precise immune responses. It has been well documented that E3 ligases are modulated at different levels through translational or post-translational modifications (PTMs) that alter their protein stability or enzymatic activity (Liu et al., 2024).

Ubiquitination and phosphorylation are well-characterized PTMs in eukaryotes, and their interplay and crosstalk have also been extensively investigated (He et al., 2017b; Gough and Sadanandom, 2021). Phosphorylation typically serves as a regulatory switch that modulates the ubiquitination and degradation process, ultimately affecting plant immunity by diverse mechanisms (Yin et al., 2019; Zhang and Zeng, 2020). Upon the recognition of PAMPs, the plasma membrane-located RLKs PID2, SDS2, and OsCERK1 recruit the E3 ligases OsPUB15, SPL11, OsCIE1, and OsPUB44, respectively, for phosphorylation (Wang et al., 2015, 2024b; Fan et al., 2018; Ichimaru et al., 2022). PID2-dependent phosphorylation is required for OsPUB15 to release E3 ligase activity and perform a positive function in immunity (Wang et al., 2015). Phosphorylation of SPL11 and OsCIE1 is mediated by the RLKs SDS2 and OsCERK1, respectively, which inhibit their ligase activities and suppress the negative effects of SPL11 and OsCIE1 on immune responses (Fan et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2024b). Notably, OsPUB44 is phosphorylated and activated in an OsCERK1-dependent manner, which differs from the OsCERK1-mediated inhibition of OsCIE1 via phosphorylation (Ichimaru et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024b). Infection of M. oryzae induces the rice PHOSPHATASE AND TENSIN HOMOLOG (OsPTEN)-dependent dephosphorylation of OsATL32, which leads to the degradation of OsATL32, thus reducing its negative effect on blast resistance (Yan et al., 2024a). Moreover, the XA21–XB3 complex is activated by XA21-based transphosphorylation upon Xoo infection, triggering the XB3-dependent ubiquitination and degradation of putative negative immune regulators (Wang et al., 2006).

Whereas E3 ligases affect substrate stability, their homeostasis is tightly regulated to activate prompt and accurate immune responses. A recent study reported that IPI1 stabilizes APIP6 and inhibits its self-ubiquitination, leading to rapid degradation of APIP6 substrates and enhancement of immune responses (Yi et al., 2024). IPI1 has been proposed to regulate the protein stability and ligase activity of APIP6 in an E3 activity-independent manner, providing insight into the roles of E3 ligases in rice immunity beyond their typical E3 ligase functions. In addition, the protein stability or ligase activity of E3 ligases is manipulated by several pathogen effectors. The M. oryzae effectors AvrPiz-t and AvrPi9 interact with APIP6, APIP10, and OsRGLG5, respectively, to promote their degradation; conversely, APIP6, APIP10, and OsRGLG5 can ubiquitinate these two M. oryzae effectors, establishing a feedback regulation between rice E3 ligases and M. oryzae effectors (Park et al., 2012, 2016; Liu et al., 2023). However, AvrPiz-t facilitates OsATL32 homeostasis, as infection of M. oryzae strain RB22 promotes the degradation of OsATL32, which maintains stability in rice plants infected with M. oryzae strain RB22AvrPiz-t (Yan et al., 2024a). The potential role of OsATL32 in regulating M. oryzae AvrPiz-t and its cognate NLR protein Piz-t is currently unknown, and further investigation of its involvement in Piz-t-conferred ETI may expand our understanding of the molecular mechanism of OsATL32 in rice immunity.

E3 ligases are also regulated at the transcriptional level by a variety of regulators. The NAC TF ONAC083 plays a negative role in rice immunity against M. oryzae and activates the transcription of downstream genes including OsRFPH2-6, which encodes an E3 ligase that negatively regulates blast resistance (Bi et al., 2023). Expression of P3IP1 is induced by the RGSV-encoded P3 protein, thereby playing a negative role in antiviral defense (Zhang et al., 2020b), consistent with the upregulated expression of another E3 ligase gene, OsJMJ715, elicited by gravid BPH females (Zhang et al., 2021a).

Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the regulation of E3 ligases at both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels in rice immunity.

E3 ligases as targets of pathogen effectors for susceptibility

Pathogens have developed multiple strategies to suppress or overcome host immunity, including the delivery of effectors to target and inhibit host PTI, leading to ETS (Martel et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024); accordingly, plants use NLRs to recognize specific pathogen effectors and initiate robust ETI to counteract ETS (Zhou and Zhang, 2020). Over 500 NLR genes have been identified in rice, and more than 38 major NLR genes have been functionally characterized (Wang et al., 2024d). At least 14 Avirulence (Avr) genes have been identified in M. oryzae on the basis of gene-for-gene relationships (Devanna et al., 2022). Among them, AVR-Pik, AVR1-CO39, AVR-Pi54, and AVR-Pia directly interact with their cognate R proteins to trigger ETI; conversely, the Avr-R pairs AVR-Pii with Pii, AvrPi9 with Pi9, AvrPib with Pib, and AvrPiz-t with Piz-t, undergo indirect interactions (Devanna et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Kou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024d). Intriguingly, recent findings have revealed that the M. oryzae effector AVR-Pita is recognized by the unconventional resistance protein PTR to confer Pita-mediated resistance, whereas the previously reported NLR Pita is dispensable for AVR-Pita detection (Xiao et al., 2024). Most M. oryzae effectors suppress rice immunity and facilitate pathogenesis by interfering with several immune regulators. For instance, AVR-Pita and AVR-Pii diminish the ROS burst by modulating the activity of the cytochrome c oxidase assembly protein OsCOX11 and NADP-malic enzyme 2, key regulators of mitochondrial ROS metabolism, respectively (Singh et al., 2016; Han et al., 2021). Accumulating evidence suggests that E3 ligases are crucial pathogenicity targets during the rice–M. oryzae interaction (Figure 2). For instance, AvrPi9 helps M. oryzae to evade rice immunity by accelerating the degradation of two ubiquitination-related positive immune regulators, OsRGLG5 and deubiquitinase PigmR-interacting and chitin-induced protein 1, while limiting the degradation of AVRPI9-INTERACTING PROTEIN 1, a ubiquitin-like domain-containing protein with negative effects on basal resistance (Zhai et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2023). AvrPiz-t aids in M. oryzae invasion by targeting and affecting the stability or activity of several key components that control host immune responses, including the three E3 ligases APIP6, APIP10, and OsATL32 (Park et al., 2012, 2016; Wang et al., 2016; Tang et al., 2017b; Shi et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020c, 2022; Yan et al., 2024a). Specifically, AvrPiz-t accelerates the degradation of APIP6 and APIP10, and APIP6 and APIP10 can also ubiquitinate AvrPiz-t (Park et al., 2012, 2016). Notably, APIP10 plays distinct roles in cognate R protein Piz-t-mediated ETI by suppressing the protein accumulation of Piz-t (Park et al., 2016). A growing body of evidence suggests that E3 ligases are targeted or exploited by bacterial pathogens or destructive viruses during infection (Figure 2). Xoo secretes numerous effectors through its type III secretion system to interfere with the rice immune machinery (Timilsina et al., 2020). The Xoo effector XopP directly interacts with and inhibits the activity of OsPUB44, thus facilitating pathogen invasion (Ishikawa et al., 2014). Intriguingly, several Xoo effectors act as either functional E3 ligases or components of rice SCF E3 complexes during infection (Qin et al., 2018; Ji et al., 2020). XopK directly ubiquitinates and disrupts the activity of rice somatic embryogenic receptor kinase 2 (OsSERK2), a crucial signaling regulator in rice immunity, to subvert PTI (Qin et al., 2018). The F-box type effector XopI mimics the adapter of the SCF E3 complex OSK1–XopI–OsTrxh2, resulting in degradation of the thioredoxin protein OsTrxh2 and repression of downstream OsNPR1-dependent signaling (Ji et al., 2020).

Pathogenicity factors encoded by viral pathogens also target rice E3 ligases for successful infection (Figure 2). Among the specific factors encoded by RSV, protein N53 inhibits the E3 ligase activity of OsMEL by competitive binding to its substrate recognition site, leading to accumulation of the OsMEL substrate SHMT1 and suppression of immune responses (Wang et al., 2023c). Similarly, RBSDV uses the P5-1 factor to interfere with rice SCF E3 ligase complex-based ubiquitination of the OsJAZs, key JA signaling repressors, resulting in repression of JA signaling and promotion of RBSDV infection (He et al., 2020). By contrast, the RGSV-encoded P3 protein activates P3IP1 to ubiquitinate rice OsNRPD1a and promotes disease development (Zhang et al., 2020b). OsRFPH2-10 interacts with RDV-encoded P2 protein and accelerates its degradation, thereby playing a pivotal antiviral role at the beginning of RDV infection (Liu et al., 2014).

Taken together, these findings reveal the intricate and intertwined crosstalk between pathogen factors and rice E3 ligases during the early stages of infection, broadening our understanding of the regulatory network of E3 ligases in rice immunity.

Engineering of E3 ligases for improved immunity in rice

Because robust immune responses often come with growth penalties in crops, the “growth–immunity trade-off” is widely regarded as one of the fundamental principles of “plant economics” that enables plants to adapt growth and defense mechanisms in response to environmental conditions (Ning et al., 2017; He et al., 2022). In the past two decades, there have been significant advances in our understanding of the regulatory functions of E3 ligases in rice immunity, which have potential applications in crop-breeding programs aimed at achieving a balance between plant growth/development and immunity. For instance, IPI1 uses an effective mechanism to control the trade-off between plant immunity and flowering by targeting the ELF3 ortholog OsELF3-1, which positively regulates photoperiod and flowering but negatively modulates blast resistance (Xu et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2024). DGS1 facilitates the degradation of OsGSK3, a negative regulator of grain size, and aquaporin OsPIP2;1, which is negatively involved in rice PTI, suggesting a regulatory role for DGS1 in balancing grain development and immunity (Wang et al., 2024a). Similarly, OsCOI1 plays vital roles in plant growth/development and immunity by modulating JA signaling (Inagaki et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b). EBR1 and its substrate OsBAG4 form a module that fine-tunes immune homeostasis and defense–fitness trade-offs, probably through the dual roles of OsBAG4 in regulating plant growth and immunity (You et al., 2016). A recent study demonstrated that OsATL32, a negative immune regulator of basal resistance and BR signaling, has potential roles in the regulation of grain development, suggesting it as a putative target for disease-resistance breeding (Yan et al., 2024a, 2024b). The OsMKK10.2–OsMPK3/6–OsWRKY45/OsWRKY31 module serves as a node for rice growth–immunity trade-offs, and the stability or activity of OsWRKY45 and OsWRKY31 are modulated by OsPUB44 and OsREIW1, respectively (Ichimaru et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a). Similarly, OsPUB73-targeted OsVQ25 confers broad-spectrum resistance without a growth penalty by modulating the downstream expression of OsWRKY53, a key regulator of immunity and growth-related BR signaling (Hao et al., 2022). Therefore, it is worth exploring the potential applications of immunity-associated E3 ligases for breeding disease-resistant rice varieties through advanced and efficient approaches such as overexpression, RNA interference, and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and its accompanying protein (Cas)-based genome editing (Figure 3).Figure 3Strategies for engineering E3 ligases to improve rice immunity.(A) A flowchart showing genetic strategies for improving rice immunity using immunity-associated E3 ligase genes.(B) Improvement of rice immunity by activating immune-positive E3 ligase genes through overexpression, transcriptional activation, and promoter-editing approaches.(C) Improvement of rice immunity by repressing immune-negative E3 ligase genes through CRISPR-Cas9-mediated targeted mutagenesis and promoter-editing approaches.(D) Improvement of rice immunity against fungal, bacterial, and viral diseases by modifying the expression of immunity-associated E3 ligase genes.

For those E3 ligases with positive immune functions, overexpression through conventional transgenic technology is a common approach to augmenting gene function. A prime example is that overexpression of DGS1 in rice enhances plant disease resistance in addition to positively affecting rice yield (Wang et al., 2024a). Directed activation of positive E3 ligase genes against pathogen attack using the CRISPR-activation system is also a potential strategy for improving rice immunity; in this case, the Cas9 mutant dCas9 is combined with a transcriptional activator to achieve specific upregulation of E3 ligase gene expression (Zhang et al., 2021b). Given that transgene expression is often affected by the chosen promoter–terminator combination (Brooks et al., 2023), the use of suitable terminators and promoters with various strengths and specific expression patterns, including constitutive promoters, responsive/inducible promoters, and tissue-specific and growth/developmental stage-specific promoters, will enable genome engineering of E3 ligase genes for appropriate expression to precisely improve rice immunity. The 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs) and leader introns can also modulate gene expression by affecting mRNA biogenesis (Brooks et al., 2023). Therefore, genome editing at the 5′ or 3′ UTRs of immunity-associated E3 ligase genes may be an optional strategy for manipulating gene transcription and/or translation to improve rice immunity.

Complete or partial loss-of-function modification of host susceptibility genes and negative immunity regulators is an effective strategy to improve plant resistance (Tao et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022; Gupta et al., 2023). Importantly, loss-of-function mutation in susceptibility genes has no adverse effect on overall plant health and development (Wang et al., 2024e). The CRISPR-Cas9 system has become a highly effective genome-editing tool for modifying negative immune regulators and generating target-specific knockout mutations to create stable disease resistance (Zaynab et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022). For instance, a deletion of 29 bp in RESISTANCE TO BLAST1 (RBL1) resulted in enhanced M. oryzae and Xoo resistance, and a targeted genome-edited allele of RBL1, named RBLΔ12, confers broad-spectrum resistance without yield penalty (Sha et al., 2023), demonstrating the efficiency of genome editing for creation of broad-spectrum resistance in rice. The CRISPR/Cas9-edited OsPUB9 gene mutant lines exhibited enhanced Xoo resistance (Kim et al., 2024). Several E3 ligase genes with negative immune functions, including EBR1, OsATL32, SPL11, and OsCIE1, have also been shown to confer broad-spectrum resistance in rice (You et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2024b; Yan et al., 2024a; Zeng et al., 2004). One promising approach for their breeding application is to knock down or knock out these E3 ligase genes through a multiplexing genome-editing strategy that targets multiple sites. This will enable selection of an allele that provides high-level broad-spectrum disease resistance without detrimental effects on key agronomic traits.

Recent studies have shifted toward the development of novel CRISPR-Cas9-based strategies that create mutations in the promoter region with the aim of generating various cis-regulatory alleles to quantitatively regulate target gene expression (Zeng et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024). The CRISPR-Cas9-based promoter-editing approach has produced considerable improvements in rice grain quality, panicle structure, and amylose contents, as well as heading date (Zeng et al., 2020; Song et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024). In view of these results, promoter editing of pathogen-responsive cis elements in the promoters of E3 ligase genes emerges as a potential strategy for enhancing the pathogen-induced expression of immune-positive E3 ligase genes to achieve improved resistance. This approach may also be applicable for eliminating the pathogen-induced expression of E3 ligase genes with negative immune roles, disrupting their regulatory functions while maintaining minimal expression for normal growth and development. In addition, translational regulation for conditional expression of immune-related E3 ligases is recognized as a promising approach for molecular design and breeding of improved rice immunity in the future (Gao et al., 2024). Multiple valuable E3 ligase genes and their immune functions have been well characterized, paving the way for potential manipulation of E3 ligases using genome-editing approaches in rice breeding programs.

Concluding remarks and perspectives

In this review, we provide a comprehensive update on the functions of E3 ligases in rice immunity and the underlying mechanisms of the rice immune signaling network at multiple levels. However, some rice E3 ligases are targeted by pathogen effectors to facilitate pathogenesis. Thus, it is likely that E3 ligases have become major players in the struggle between rice and its pathogens, used by rice to activate immune responses and by pathogens to promote infection.

Considerable advances have been made in understanding the functions of E3 ligases in rice immunity through direct manipulation of immune receptors or modulation of various immune signaling events, including the ROS burst, MAPK cascade activation, transcriptional reprogramming, and hormone signaling. However, the comprehensive regulatory network of rice immunity, including the intricate interplay of diverse signaling pathways involving E3 ligases, is not fully understood. This is partly due to a lack of information on E3 ligase substrates, making it difficult to integrate the E3 ligases into the rice immune signaling network. Furthermore, the rice genome contains 48 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes, each of which may interact with multiple E3 ligases (Bae and Kim, 2014). However, only a limited number of E2s and E2–E3 pairs have been characterized in rice immunity. For instance, the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme OsUBC26 collaborates with the E3 ligases APIP6 and OsZFP1 to ubiquitinate downstream substrates and subsequently modulate rice immunity (Liu et al., 2021b). DGS1 and OsCIE1 work in conjunction with the E2 enzymes OsUBC45 and OsUBC8, respectively, to form E2–E3 pairs and facilitate the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of their substrates, thereby influencing rice yield and immunity (Wang et al., 2023d, 2024a, 2024b). Future investigation of the immune functions of E3 ligases, identification of E3-paired E2 enzymes, and characterization of their target substrates will expand our understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which E3 ligases influence rice immunity.

Pathogen effectors exploit rice E3 ligases to suppress host immunity at the early stage of invasion, facilitating ETS and infection. Several effector-targeted E3 ligases have evolved a feedback mechanism to influence their corresponding effectors, and only a few of them are involved in cognate NLR-conferred immunity. For instance, the M. oryzae effectors AvrPiz-t and AvrPi9 directly interact with the E3 ligases APIP6, APIP10, and OsRGLG5, which in turn accelerate the turnover of their corresponding effectors. However, APIP10, the sole E3 ligase targeted by an M. oryzae effector, has been demonstrated to play dual roles in both PTI and ETI. Therefore, it will be very important to reveal the mechanisms by which pathogen effectors manipulate rice E3 ligases to facilitate invasion and to characterize the potential roles of E3 ligases in effector–NLR interactions. Given that the majority of the reported roles of E3 ligases are involved in the rice–M. oryzae interaction, further investigations are necessary to reveal the molecular mechanisms by which E3 ligases participate in rice resistance against bacterial and viral diseases to obtain a global view of the regulatory network of E3 ligases in rice immunity.

Effective regulation of the immune system is critical for plant health and relies on balancing the plant growth–immunity trade-off. Given their regulatory functions in both immunity and growth-related processes, several E3 ligases show great potential for use in breeding programs to fine-tune rice growth and immunity. Furthermore, E3 ligases targeted by pathogen effectors are also promising targets for genetic engineering in breeding programs. Future identification of novel E3 ligases as regulators of both basal resistance and NLR-mediated immunity will provide new genetic resources for the development of disease-resistant rice varieties through biotechnological approaches such as genome editing.

Although substantial progress has been made in understanding the regulatory functions of E3 ligases in rice immunity, several key questions remain to be answered. These open questions include the following. How does E3 ligase-involved distinct signaling coordinate in rice immune responses? How does E3 ligase-mediated ubiquitination cooperate with other PTMs in regulating the rice immune response upon pathogen infection? How do pathogen effectors recognize and modulate host E3 ligases, and are other cofactors involved? Addressing these questions will enable a deeper understanding of the functions and regulatory network of E3 ligases in rice immunity.
