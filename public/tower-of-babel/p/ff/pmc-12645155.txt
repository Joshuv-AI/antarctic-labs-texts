Subjective sleep characteristics in non‐demented adults: domain‐specific associations with multimodal Alzheimer's disease biomarkers
Authors: You‐Yi Tu, Liang Cui, Zhen Zhang, Min Wang, Yi‐Hui Guan, Yue‐Hua Li, Fang Xie, Qi‐Hao Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSleep problems are early features of Alzheimer's disease (AD). INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia worldwide and is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques, tau‐related neurofibrillary tangles, and progressive neurodegeneration. 1 Mounting evidence suggests that the pathological cascade begins years before clinical symptoms emerge, highlighting the importance of identifying modifiable risk factors in preclinical and prodromal stages. 2

Sleep problems are increasingly recognized as an early feature of AD, with bidirectional interactions between disrupted sleep–wake regulation and the accumulation of Aβ and tau proteins. Sleep facilitates the clearance of neurotoxic proteins, whereas sleep disruption accelerates Aβ deposition and tau propagation. Conversely, AD‐related pathological changes can impair sleep architecture and circadian rhythms, creating a self‐reinforcing cycle. 3 , 4 Beyond core pathology, poor sleep quantity and quality have also been linked to astroglial activation, axonal injury, and alterations in functional network organization, all of which may contribute to cognitive decline. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Most prior studies have relied on objective sleep measures or composite sleep scores. 9 However, subjective perceptions of sleep quality and specific sleep dimensions – such as sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep latency, and daytime dysfunction – may have distinct effects on cognitive and neurobiological outcomes. 10 , 11 Few studies have simultaneously examined, within the same cohort, the associations of different dimensions of subjective sleep with multidomain cognitive performance, large‐scale brain network connectivity, and multiple imaging‐ and blood‐based AD biomarkers. Understanding these multidimensional relationships could improve the accuracy of sleep‐based risk stratification in older adults.

In this study, we leveraged a large, well‐characterized cohort of non‐demented older adults across the cognitive continuum to systematically examine how individual components of subjective sleep relate to cognitive performance, resting‐state functional connectivity, Aβ deposition, and plasma biomarkers reflecting tau pathology, axonal damage, and astroglial activation. By integrating cognitive, neuroimaging, and biochemical measures, we aimed to provide a comprehensive characterization of sleep–brain–pathology associations, offering potential targets for early detection and intervention in AD.

METHODS

Participants and recruitment

This observational study utilized data from the Chinese Preclinical Alzheimer's Disease Study (CPAS) cohort. 12 Our study enrolled 849 non‐demented older adults between March 2022 and April 2025 through recruitment at the Cognitive Disorder Clinic of Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital and from community sources. The sample consisted of 198 cognitively normal controls (NCs), 224 individuals with subjective cognitive decline (SCD), and 427 participants diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

Eligibility criteria required individuals to be aged 50 years or older and free of dementia based on the National Institute on Aging‐Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) 2011 criteria. 13 We excluded those with psychiatric illness (including major depressive disorder and other psychiatric conditions that could cause secondary insomnia), significant neurological diseases, or clinically diagnosed non‐primary sleep disorders with identifiable causes (e.g., restless legs syndrome, rapid eye movement [REM] sleep behavior disorder, or severe obstructive sleep apnea), as well as other major medical conditions likely to affect cognition or sleep.

All 849 participants underwent standardized cognitive testing, clinical interviews, sleep assessments, amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), and resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs‐fMRI) examinations. Blood samples for plasma biomarker measurement were collected from 768 participants, and analyses involving these biomarkers were performed on all available cases without imputation. SCD was diagnosed based on subjective cognitive complaints without measurable cognitive deficits, while MCI was diagnosed using Petersen's criteria, characterized by objective cognitive impairment and preserved independence in daily activities. Diagnosis was established through consensus by a panel of experienced clinicians and neuropsychologists.

All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital (approval number 2019‐041), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cognitive and sleep assessment

Neuropsychological and functional assessments were administered to evaluate multiple cognitive domains and daily functioning. Global cognitive function was assessed using the Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination III (ACE‐III), which evaluates attention, memory, language, verbal fluency, and visuospatial abilities. Memory performance was evaluated by the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT), including both long‐delay recall (LDR) and recognition (RE) scores. Executive function was assessed using the Shape Trail Test Parts A and B (STT‐A and STT‐B), while language ability was measured with the Animal Fluency Test (AFT) and the 30‐item Boston Naming Test (BNT). Functional status in daily life was evaluated using the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ).

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We searched PubMed and Google Scholar for studies on subjective sleep and AD, focusing on associations with cognition, neuroimaging, and biomarkers. Most prior studies relied on global sleep scores or objective measures such as polysomnography, and few examined how different dimensions of subjective sleep related simultaneously to cognition, functional brain networks, and multimodal biomarkers in a single cohort. Evidence on domain‐specific associations, especially with plasma p‐tau217, NfL, and GFAP, remains limited. Interpretation: In a large, well‐characterized non‐demented cohort, we found that adequacy‐ and function‐related sleep dimensions (sleep quality, efficiency, and daytime dysfunction) were more strongly linked to cognitive decline, altered functional connectivity, and Aβ/tau pathology. In contrast, sleep duration and fragmentation showed paradoxical associations with plasma biomarkers. These results indicate that different types of sleep disturbances may involve distinct biological underpinnings and pathways of influence in the AD continuum. Future directions: Future work should integrate longitudinal assessments and objective sleep measures to clarify temporal and causal relationships between sleep alterations and AD pathology. Validation across diverse cohorts will be essential to determine whether multidimensional sleep profiling can serve as a practical tool for early detection, risk stratification, and intervention in preclinical and prodromal stages of AD.

Subjective sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), 14 a widely used self‐report questionnaire comprising seven component scores: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is rated on a scale of 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep. In this study, we focused on the seven component scores rather than the global PSQI score, in order to capture domain‐specific associations between different dimensions of sleep and cognitive as well as biological outcomes.

Neuroimaging acquisition and preprocessing

Neuroimaging was conducted on a 3.0T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) at Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital. High‐resolution T1‐weighted structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were acquired using a 3D MPRAGE sequence with 0.8‐mm isotropic resolution. Rs‐fMRI scans were obtained using a multiband echo‐planar imaging sequence (TR = 800 ms; voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm3). Participants were instructed to remain still, stay awake, and keep their eyes closed during scanning.

All rs‐fMRI data preprocessing and connectivity analyses were performed using toolkits implemented in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), including Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12, Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, UCL, London, UK), RESTplus toolbox (http://restfmri.net/forum/restplus), and GRETNA toolbox (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna).

Rs‐fMRI data were preprocessed following standard procedures. The first 30 volumes were discarded to allow for signal equilibrium. Subsequent steps included realignment for head motion correction, spatial normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, nuisance regression (white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, global signal, and Friston‐24 motion parameters), temporal bandpass filtering (0.01 to 0.08 Hz), and spatial smoothing using a 6‐mm full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Participants with excessive head motion (>3 mm translation or >3° rotation) were excluded. High‐resolution T1‐weighted images were processed primarily to support anatomical referencing and quality control.

The posterior default mode network (pDMN) was selected as the primary region of interest (ROI) based on prior evidence showing its high vulnerability to both sleep‐related alterations and AD pathology. 15 , 16 In addition, between‐network connectivity analyses focused on the limbic network (LN) due to its critical role in sleep‐dependent memory consolidation and emotional regulation, as well as its previously reported sensitivity to sleep variability and aging‐related functional changes. 17 , 18

For ROI‐based connectivity analysis, functional coupling within the pDMN was examined. Specifically, we assessed the connectivity between the inferior parietal cortex (IPC), lateral temporal cortex (LTC), and the precuneus (PRC) in both hemispheres. Each ROI was defined as an 8‐mm‐radius sphere centered at standardized MNI coordinates derived from prior literature. 19 , 20 , 21 Mean time series were extracted from each region, and pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between IPC–PRC and LTC–PRC nodes bilaterally. The resulting correlation values were Fisher z‐transformed and used for statistical analyses.

For large‐scale network‐level connectivity, we assessed internetwork functional coupling between the LN and six canonical resting‐state networks, including the visual network (VN), somatomotor network (SMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), ventral attention network (VAN), frontoparietal control network (FCN), and default mode network (DMN). Brain networks were defined using the Schaefer 400‐parcel functional atlas in combination with the Yeo 7‐network parcellation framework. 22 The mean time series of each network was derived by averaging signals across constituent parcels, and internetwork connectivity was calculated as the Fisher z‐transformed correlation between pairs of network signals.

PET acquisition and processing

Cerebral Aβ deposition was assessed using [18F]‐florbetapir PET. Imaging was performed on a Siemens Biograph mCT FlowMotion PET/CT scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. Following intravenous injection of the radiotracer, PET scans were acquired within a standard uptake time window, with participants at rest during image acquisition.

Image preprocessing and quantification were conducted using SPM12. PET images were realigned, co‐registered to each subject's structural T1‐weighted MRI, normalized to MNI space, and smoothed using an 8‐mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. To improve quantification accuracy by accounting for signal spillover from adjacent tissues, partial volume effect (PVE) correction was applied during preprocessing. Regional tracer uptake was then quantified as standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR), using the whole cerebellum as the reference region.

Plasma biomarker assessment

Peripheral blood samples were collected using standardized venipuncture procedures. Plasma concentrations of tau phosphorylated at threonine 217 (p‐tau217), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) were quantified using a fully automated light‐initiated chemiluminescence immunoassay platform (Chemclin LiCA 800 analyzer, Chemclin Diagnostics, Beijing, China). This homogeneous assay utilizes laser‐triggered singlet oxygen transfer to initiate chemiluminescence, enabling wash‐free detection with high sensitivity, a wide dynamic range, and strong resistance to interference. All measurements were performed under blinded conditions following the manufacturer's standardized protocols. Data for all three biomarkers were included in the current analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Descriptive statistics were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons of PSQI component scores among NC, SCD, and MCI groups were conducted using Kruskal–Wallis tests. To explore associations between sleep components and neuroimaging and plasma biomarker outcomes, general linear models (GLMs) were constructed, adjusting for age, sex, and years of education. All seven PSQI components were included simultaneously in each model to estimate the independent contribution of each sleep domain while controlling for shared variance. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed).

Cognitive performance also differed markedly among groups. Median scores on ACE‐III, AVLT long‐delay recall, and AVLT recognition showed decline from NC to MCI (all H > 200, p < 0.001). Executive function, assessed by STT‐A and STT‐B, showed progressively longer completion times across the continuum (both H > 50, p < 0.001). Language performance (AFT and BNT) and functional status (FAQ) also demonstrated gradual worsening from NC to MCI (all H > 19, p < 0.001).

For biomarkers, group differences were observed in global amyloid SUVR (H = 7.21, p = 0.027), p‐tau217 (H = 15.29, p < 0.001), NfL (H = 11.79, p = 0.003), and GFAP (H = 20.52, p < 0.001), with values generally increasing from NC to MCI.

Group differences in subjective sleep characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the subjective sleep characteristics across diagnostic groups. Significant group differences were observed in several PSQI components. Subjective sleep quality (PSQI_A) showed a progressive increase in scores across the continuum from NC to SCD to MCI (H = 17.06, p < 0.001), indicating a trend of worsening perceived sleep quality. Sleep latency (PSQI_B) and sleep disturbances (PSQI_D) also differed significantly among groups (H = 12.35, p = 0.002; H = 7.42, p = 0.024), with higher scores suggesting longer time to fall asleep and more frequent sleep interruptions along the cognitive continuum.

Daytime dysfunction (PSQI_E) displayed a modest but significant difference (H = 6.23, p = 0.044), showing a trend of increased daytime impairment with worsening cognitive status. In contrast, sleep duration (PSQI_C) did not differ significantly among groups (H = 3.92, p = 0.141). Significant differences were also observed in sleep efficiency (PSQI_F, H = 7.85, p = 0.020) and use of sleep medication (PSQI_G, H = 26.55, p < 0.001), with higher scores seen in participants with cognitive symptoms.

These findings indicate that poorer subjective sleep quality, longer sleep latency, more frequent sleep disturbances, greater daytime dysfunction, reduced sleep efficiency, and increased use of sleep medication are more prominent with the emergence of cognitive impairment.

Daytime dysfunction exhibited the most consistent associations across multiple cognitive domains. Higher scores in daytime dysfunction were significantly associated with lower global cognition (ACE‐III: β = −0.10, p = 0.002), poorer functional ability (FAQ: β = 0.24, p < 0.001), reduced auditory memory performance (AVLT RE: β = −0.08, p = 0.049), and slower executive function (STT‐A: β = 0.09, p = 0.022; STT‐B: β = 0.10, p = 0.005).

In addition, use of sleep medication was positively associated with higher global cognition (ACE‐III: β = 0.11, p = 0.003) and better memory recall (AVLT LDR: β = 0.09, p = 0.016) but showed no significant associations with executive or language domains.

Subjective sleep quality was modestly associated with functional impairment (FAQ: β = 0.10, p = 0.045), suggesting that worse perceived sleep quality may relate to reduced daily functioning. Other PSQI components, including sleep latency, duration, efficiency, and disturbances, were not significantly associated with most cognitive measures.

Associations between subjective sleep characteristics and pDMN functional connectivity

We investigated the associations between individual sleep components and resting‐state functional connectivity within the pDMN, adjusting for age, sex, and education (Table 3). Significant associations emerged for three sleep components. Higher PSQI sleep efficiency scores, reflecting poorer subjective sleep efficiency, were positively associated with bilateral IPC–PRC connectivity (L_IPC–PRC: β = 0.12, p = 0.019; R_IPC–PRC: β = 0.12, p = 0.020). Higher PSQI subjective sleep quality scores, indicating poorer perceived sleep quality, were negatively associated with LTC–PRC connectivity in both hemispheres (L_LTC–PRC: β = –0.14, p = 0.007; R_LTC–PRC: β = –0.11, p = 0.032). Daytime dysfunction showed a positive association with LTC–PRC connectivity (L_LTC–PRC: β = 0.09, p = 0.029; R_LTC–PRC: β = 0.10, p = 0.011), which may reflect compensatory or dysregulated engagement of posterior cortical networks.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we addressed whether different subjective sleep dimensions showed distinct links with cognition, brain networks, and AD biomarkers. By integrating cognitive, imaging, and plasma measures in a large non‐demented cohort, we provided evidence that sleep problems were not uniform phenomena but may reflect heterogeneous biological processes.

Sleep difficulties are increasingly recognized as early indicators of neurodegenerative diseases, and specific features may provide greater discriminative value than global measures. 9 In our study, subjective sleep quality, latency, disturbances, daytime dysfunction, efficiency, and use of sleep medication showed significant group differences, generally worsening across the cognitive continuum. These results suggest that problems in initiating or maintaining sleep, impaired daytime function, and greater reliance on sleep aids may appear in the early stages of decline, consistent with reports that poor sleep relates to cognitive deterioration from midlife. 9 , 10 In contrast, sleep duration did not differ, implying that qualitative aspects of sleep may be more sensitive than total time. Potential mechanisms include mediation by systemic inflammation 23 , 24 or by pathways linking cognitive frailty and depressive symptoms. 25 Taken together with the results of prior studies, these findings underscore the value of evaluating specific sleep dimensions in at‐risk older adults.

This study identified daytime dysfunction as the most consistent sleep‐related correlate of decline, associated with impairments in global cognition, memory, executive function, and daily activities. Evidence suggests that daytime dysfunction is not only a consequence of poor sleep but also contributes to cognitive vulnerability. 26 Declines in alertness and vitality have been linked to measurable impairments in daytime performance. 27 Clinical and subclinical burnout, marked by fatigue and reduced flexibility, has similarly been associated with poorer attention and working memory under demanding conditions. 28 , 29 These findings highlight the clinical importance of daytime dysfunction as a behavioral marker of vulnerability to cognitive decline and suggest it may signal early neurocognitive changes related to sleep disruption.

Interestingly, sleep medication use was associated with better global cognition and memory. While often viewed as a marker of poor sleep, this finding suggests that pharmacological aids may, under certain conditions, help preserve cognitive function in older adults. The relationship remains complex and may vary by ethnicity and medication class. 30 , 31 Some agents, including L‐theanine, dual orexin receptor antagonists, and low‐dose trazodone, have been suggested to improve sleep without cognitive impairment and in some cases may even enhance memory consolidation through effects on sleep architecture and arousal regulation. 31 , 32 , 33 These results raise the possibility that targeted pharmacological interventions could benefit cognitive health in aging. However, given the heterogeneity of sleep medications and the observational design, further studies are needed to establish causality and clarify mechanisms.

Our findings showed that different sleep components were differentially associated with pDMN connectivity. Poorer sleep efficiency was linked to stronger IPC–PRC connectivity, whereas poorer subjective sleep quality was related to reduced LTC–PRC connectivity. Greater daytime dysfunction was associated with enhanced LTC–PRC connectivity, resembling patterns of insufficient DMN deactivation reported in insomnia. 8 , 34 , 35 These results suggest that reduced efficiency and negative sleep perceptions may reflect distinct vulnerabilities of posterior DMN circuits. Consistent with our cognitive findings, DMN alterations were primarily associated with sleep quality rather than duration. 36 One explanation is that DMN activity is selectively modulated across sleep stages, with REM‐related deactivation reflecting state‐dependent control. 37 This may account for the closer link of DMN connectivity with sleep quality and architecture rather than total time.

The LN, which integrates memory‐ and emotion‐related circuits, is known to reorganize early in cognitive decline, but its relationship with sleep has been less studied. 38 , 39 In our data, shorter sleep duration was associated with reduced connectivity between the LN and multiple large‐scale networks. Recent evidence likewise shows that poor or variable sleep relates to widespread LN disruptions and reduced coupling with cortical and subcortical regions. 17 , 40 In contrast, greater daytime dysfunction was associated with increased LN connectivity to the DAN and FCN, possibly reflecting compensatory or dysregulated recruitment under reduced alertness. 41 , 42 Similar patterns have been observed in the DMN, where shorter or disturbed sleep reduced segregation from control networks. 43 We also found that poorer efficiency and longer latency were associated with stronger LN–VN and LN–FCN connectivity, indicating network‐specific sensitivity to distinct sleep characteristics.

We found that poorer sleep efficiency was associated with greater amyloid burden, and more severe daytime dysfunction was correlated with higher p‐tau217 and NfL levels. These results align with evidence that disrupted sleep continuity and reduced daytime alertness are linked to early AD pathology. A bidirectional relationship between sleep and AD pathology has been proposed, 44 whereby sleep disruption accelerates Aβ and tau accumulation while their deposition further impairs circadian rhythms and sleep architecture. 4 , 45 , 46

Although plasma p‐tau217 is considered one of the most promising early pathological biomarkers of AD, to our knowledge, no prior studies reported its association with sleep measures. Our results extend the literature by suggesting that daytime dysfunction may serve as a behavioral correlate of tau phosphorylation and axonal injury in the early stages of AD. Mechanistically, the glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic systems contribute to the clearance of AD‐related biomarkers, including NfL and GFAP, and this clearance is tightly coupled with sleep. Consistent with this, previous studies reported diurnal fluctuations in plasma biomarkers such as NfL, 47 and higher NfL concentrations have been linked to poorer sleep quality. 48 Animal studies have shown that obstructive sleep apnea is associated with elevated GFAP levels, 49 and astroglial activation has been observed in sleep architecture disruptions associated with synucleinopathies. 50 Furthermore, patients with isolated REM sleep behavior disorder have been reported to show elevated plasma levels of both NfL and GFAP. Together, these findings are in line with our results, suggesting that sleep disruption may contribute to impaired metabolic clearance, tau propagation, neuroinflammation, and glial dysregulation.

Unexpectedly, shorter sleep duration and more frequent disturbances were associated with lower plasma p‐tau217, NfL, and GFAP. These counterintuitive findings may reflect subjective–objective discrepancies, stage‐dependent dynamics, or residual confounding and should be interpreted with caution. Subjective reports of sleep often diverge from objective measures: Individuals may overestimate or underestimate sleep depending on restriction or insomnia type, 51 , 52 and self‐reports primarily capture perceived efficiency rather than objective depth. In cognitively impaired populations, fewer complaints can paradoxically coexist with more disrupted sleep, and poorer self‐reported quality has even been linked to lower CSF tau when mediated by memory performance. 53 , 54 Another possibility is disease‐stage variation, as lower efficiency and fragmentation predicted cortical thinning in A–T– but thicker cortices in A+T+ individuals. 55 Together, these observations suggest that paradoxical associations between sleep complaints and AD biomarkers may reflect complex interactions among reporting biases, disease stage, and pathophysiology.

Finally, these paradoxical associations underscore the need to clarify which subjective sleep dimensions reliably track AD‐related processes and which are more vulnerable to reporting biases or network‐level disruptions. Shorter reported sleep duration, for instance, was consistently related to widespread reductions in LN coupling with higher‐order cortical networks, suggesting that some dimensions (e.g., perceived duration or disturbances) may primarily reflect network‐level dysregulation of interoceptive monitoring and metacognitive appraisal rather than direct biochemical pathology. Such network‐driven perceptual biases could partly explain the counterintuitive associations observed with plasma markers. These considerations highlight the importance of distinguishing stable, pathology‐linked sleep dimensions from those more affected by perceptual or network‐related biases.

Considering both the consistency and breadth of associations across cognitive, neuroimaging, and biomarker domains, daytime dysfunction and sleep efficiency emerged as the most robust and translationally meaningful dimensions. Daytime dysfunction encapsulates the functional consequences of sleep problems, reflecting associations with both cognitive and functional performance and AD‐related network, neuronal, and tau pathology, whereas sleep efficiency reflects physiological alterations linked to amyloid burden and network decoupling. Together, these two dimensions may serve as practical entry points for early screening and sleep‐based interventions in the context of AD.

In contrast, other subjective sleep dimensions may be distorted by network dysfunction or cognitive impairment. Future longitudinal studies integrating objective sleep measures with functional imaging will be essential to disentangle perception‐driven from pathology‐driven components of sleep–brain relationships.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precluded causal inference, making it impossible to determine whether sleep alterations were a consequence of or a contributing factor to AD pathology. Second, sleep parameters were derived from self‐reported measures, which are subject to recall bias and may diverge from objective sleep indices, particularly in cognitively impaired individuals. Third, although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, unmeasured variables such as comorbid medical conditions, medication use, or lifestyle factors could still introduce residual confounding.

CONCLUSION

In summary, different dimensions of subjective sleep exhibited distinct associations with cognition, brain network connectivity, and AD‐related biomarkers in non‐demented older adults. Dimensions related to perceived adequacy and functional impact, such as sleep quality, sleep efficiency, and daytime dysfunction, were more strongly associated with cognitive performance, network alterations, and Aβ/tau pathology. In contrast, sleep duration and disturbances showed paradoxical associations with plasma biomarkers, pointing to possible discrepancies between subjective reports and underlying biology or network‐level alterations. Together, these findings indicate that different types of sleep problems may involve distinct biological underpinnings and pathways influencing the AD continuum.
