Remimazolam Preserves Oxygen Reserve and Improves Sedation Safety Compared to Propofol in Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography
Authors: Jee Hoon Kim, Kyuho Lee, Sung Jin Lee, Galam Leem, Hee Seung Lee, Jeong Youp Park, Seungmin Bang, Seung Woo Park, Namo Kim, Moon Jae Chung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRemimazolam is a short‐acting benzodiazepine with less cardiorespiratory depression compared with propofol. The Oxygen Reserve Index (ORi) reflects oxygenation status in the mild hyperoxic range and can detect subtle respiratory depression induced by sedatives.ObjectiveWe compared remimazolam and propofol in patients undergoing endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and assessed the ORi to evaluate the impact of these sedatives on oxygen reserve.MethodsPatients scheduled for ERCP were enrolled and randomly assigned to either the remimazolam or the propofol group. They received 0.1 mg kg−1 remimazolam or 1.0 mg kg−1 propofol, with bolus injections of either 2 mg remimazolam or 20 mg propofol added as required, according to the group allocation. The primary outcome was the incidence of oxygen reserve depletion, defined as an ORi reduction to 0.00.ResultsAmong the 102 patients, oxygen reserve depletion was more frequent in the propofol group (70.6% vs. 41.2%, odds ratio 0.29, 95% confidence interval 0.13–0.66, p = 0.003). The time from sedative injection to endoscope insertion, length of stay in the recovery room, and overall procedure time were comparable between the groups. Patients in the remimazolam group reported a lower incidence of procedural recall, fewer complaints of inadequate sedation, and higher satisfaction scores than those in the propofol group.ConclusionsRemimazolam effectively preserved the oxygen reserve compared with propofol, lowering the risk of hypoxia during sedation. Remimazolam was also associated with more favorable recovery profiles for patients undergoing ERCP, making it a safe and preferred sedative for this procedure.Trail Registration Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT06359834.Keywords: endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, endoscopic sedation, hypoxia risk, oxygen reserve depletion, oxygen reserve index, propofol, remimazolam, sedation safety

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is an advanced endoscopic technique performed to diagnose and treat pancreaticobiliary diseases [1]. Due to its complexity, sedation is required to attenuate patient discomfort and improve examination quality [2]. Nonetheless, sedation may be accompanied by unwanted side effects, such as hypotension and respiratory depression [3]. Furthermore, prone positioning is suggested to properly assess the bile duct during ERCP, which can exacerbate the risk of adverse events, as appropriate rescue interventions may become more challenging in prone‐positioned patients. With the increasing number of ERCP procedures being performed on older patients in Korea, ensuring patient safety during ERCP is of the utmost importance [4].

Currently, propofol and midazolam are the most favored agents for endoscopic sedation [5]. Propofol is favored due to its rapid onset and short duration; however, clinicians should be aware of the potential risk of severe cardiorespiratory depression associated with propofol use [6]. On the other hand, midazolam has minimal cardiorespiratory depressive effects, and the presence of its reversal agent, flumazenil, offers an additional advantage in terms of patient safety. However, midazolam has a slower onset and longer duration of action compared to propofol, which may slow the patient turnover rate in endoscopy units, and the potential risk of re‐sedation with active metabolites makes it less appealing [7].

Remimazolam is a short‐acting benzodiazepine approved for use in procedural sedation [8]. Similar to midazolam, remimazolam is known to minimally affect hemodynamics and can be reversed with flumazenil [9]. Furthermore, it is rapidly metabolized by liver carboxylesterases, which contributes to significantly shorter duration of action compared to midazolam [9]. Owing to these favorable aspects, previous studies indicated that remimazolam is comparably effective and associated with fewer incidence of respiratory depression, defined as peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) < 90%, when compared to propofol [10, 11]. However, recent evidence suggests that a SpO2 of < 94% may indicate a significant decrease in arterial oxygen tension (PaO2) below 60 mmHg [12], underscoring the need for a more precise assessment of the impact of sedatives on oxygenation.

The Oxygen Reserve Index (ORi) is a non‐invasive, continuous monitoring parameter which assesses the oxygenation status of the mild hyperoxic range of PaO2 100–200 mmHg with a unitless scale between 0.00 and 1.00 [13, 14]. It detects changes in venous oxygen saturation, which declines approximately 30 s faster than SpO2 during respiratory depression, providing clinicians with critical time to intervene [15]. Hence, ORi can be used to detect subtle respiratory depression induced by sedatives in preoxygenated patients, although no studies have yet investigated the use of ORi in patients undergoing ERCP.

This study aimed to compare the safety and efficacy of remimazolam and propofol in ERCP through a randomized controlled trial at a single center by assessing the ORi to evaluate the impact of these sedatives on the oxygenation status of patients.

Materials and Methods

This prospective randomized controlled study was conducted between April 2024 and August 2024. This study adhered to the applicable consolidated standards of reporting trials guidelines. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB no. 4‐2023‐1693) of Severance Hospital, Yonsei University Health System, in February 2024 and registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06359834) in April 2024. The patients scheduled to undergo ERCP were screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were as follows: [1] American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status class I–III, and [2] age between 20 and 80 years. The exclusion criteria were: [1] pregnancy [2] known hypersensitivity to benzodiazepine or propofol [3], history of adverse events related to previous sedation endoscopy [4], severe renal disease (serum creatinine ≥ 2 mg/dL) [5], severe heart disease (NYHA class III or IV) [6], baseline systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg, and [7] SpO2 < 90% in the room air or < 95% despite preoxygenation. Patients whose baseline ORi did not increase over 0.00 despite preoxygenation were excluded from the analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and the study was conducted in accordance with the Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects, as outlined in the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 (revised 2013).

All enrolled patients were allocated to either the remimazolam or propofol group by KL according to a randomized sequence generated through a website (www.randomizer.org). Patients were blinded to the group allocation. Routine monitoring, including electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, and non‐invasive blood pressure measurements, was initiated upon arrival at the endoscopy unit. To assess ORi, a Rainbow sensor (Masimo Corp, Irvine, CA, USA) was applied to the index finger and the index was continuously monitored. Preoxygenation at a flow rate of 2 L min−1 was initiated via a nasal cannula for 2 min before sedative injection [16]. After the ORi value plateaued for at least 30 s, patients received an intravenous injection of either remimazolam 0.1 mg kg−1 over 1 min or propofol 1.0 mg kg−1 according to group allocation.

Endoscopy was performed when the sedation level reached a Modified Observer's Assessment of Alertness/Sedation Scale (MOAA/S; Supporting Information S1: Table S1) score of 2 or 3. If required, 2 mg of either remimazolam or 20 mg of propofol was added to reach the targeted sedation level depending on the group. All ERCP procedures were performed using duodenoscopes from a single manufacturer (TJF‐260V, TJF‐Q290V, and JF‐260; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The following stents were used: Advanix double pigtail plastic stent (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA); Tannenbaum plastic stent, Zimmon double pigtail plastic stent, and Geenen pancreatic plastic stent (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA); Niti‐S Kaffes covered metal stent and Niti‐S Large Cell D‐type uncovered metal stent (Taewoong Medical, Gimpo, Korea); and EGIS double bare uncovered metal stent (S&G Biotech, Yongin, Korea).

When hypoxia, defined as SpO2 < 94%, occurred, airway interventions such as increasing the oxygen flow rate, supine positioning, or chin lift were considered. Hypertension was defined as a > 20% increase in mean blood pressure from baseline, and hypotension was defined as a > 20% decrease in mean blood pressure from baseline or < 60 mmHg. Tachycardia and bradycardia were defined as heart rates > 120 beats min−1 and < 50 beats min−1, respectively.

Procedure duration was defined as the time from sedative injection to endoscope withdrawal. The endoscopist marked his satisfaction score immediately after the completion of the procedure. Patients were transferred to the recovery room (RR) and assessed for nausea, dyspnea, and dizziness. Discharge from RR was determined based on the modified Aldrete score of 9 or higher (Supporting Information S1: Table S2). Before discharge, all patients were asked to report their sedation satisfaction scores. The overall procedure time, defined as the time from the initial sedative injection to RR discharge, was assessed.

Hemodynamic variables and the MOAA/S scale scores were measured at the following time points: before sedation (baseline), endoscopy start (T1), 5 min after T1 (T2), 10 min after T1 (T3), endoscopy end (T4), RR arrival (R1), 5 min after R1 (R2), 10 min after R1 (R3), and discharge from RR (R3).

The primary outcome was the incidence of oxygen reserve depletion, which was defined as a decrease in the plateau ORi to 0.00. Other outcomes included hemodynamic parameters, procedural‐ and sedation‐related outcomes, adverse events, and side effects.

The sample size was calculated from the results of our previous study in which ORi was assessed in patients who underwent diagnostic upper gastrointestinal endoscopy [17]. In this study, the incidence of oxygen reserve depletion was 65.7% in the propofol group and 38.2% in the remimazolam group [17]. G*Power (version 3.1.9.2, Brunsbüttel, Germany) estimated that 51 patients were required in each group, with a power of 80% and a significance level of 0.05. Assuming a dropout rate of 10%, 112 patients were enrolled in this study.

SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The paired Student's t‐test was used to analyze continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze variables that did not meet normality. Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests were used to compare categorical variables between the groups. Repeated variables were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA, including group, time, and the interaction between group and time as fixed effects, with post hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction for multiple within‐group comparisons. The effect size was calculated as follows: odds ratio (OR) for dichotomous categorical data, Cramér’s V with Haldane–Anscombe correction (if required) for multi‐category contingency tables, and Cliff's delta for variables not meeting normality assumptions. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A schematic of the study design is shown in Figure 1. Among the 122 eligible patients scheduled for ERCP, 10 patients declined to participate. The remaining 112 patients were randomly assigned to receive either remimazolam (n = 56) or propofol sedation (n = 56). Five patients in each group were excluded from the study because their ORi remained 0.00 despite preoxygenation. Hence, data from 102 patients were analyzed.

Patient enrollment.

The baseline characteristics of the patients were comparable between the groups (Table 1). The most common diagnoses were tumors, biliary stone disease, and biliary strictures. There was no significant difference in papilla status between the two groups. Data associated with the procedure and sedation are listed in Table 2. There was no significant difference in the procedure category performed between the two groups; stent change or insertion was the most common, followed by follow‐up endoscopy. However, the intended ERCP procedures could not be completed in six patients in the propofol group and seven in the remimazolam group. Specifically, in two patients (one from each group), the endoscope could not be advanced to the second portion of the duodenum due to duodenal strictures. Ten patients (five from each group) failed to achieve selective deep cannulation of the bile duct due to anatomical variations. Lastly, one patient experienced a paradoxical reaction following the administration of remimazolam. Nonetheless, these patients did not meet our exclusion criteria, and all the required parameters were successfully assessed, which allowed their data to be included in the analysis.

Subgroup analysis of patients who underwent follow‐up endoscopy, stent change, or insertion was performed. There was no difference between the two sedatives in the “follow‐up endoscopy” subgroup, except for oxygen reserve depletion, which was significantly less frequent in the remimazolam group (Supporting Information S1: Table S3). In the “stent change or insertion” subgroup, remimazolam exhibited lesser oxygen reserve depletion, shorter procedure duration, less procedural recall, no complaints regarding inadequate sedation, and higher patient satisfaction scores compared with propofol (Supporting Information S1: Table S4). Further subgroup analysis based on the median procedure duration (8 min) revealed that oxygen reserve depletion was less frequent with remimazolam sedation than with propofol sedation for procedures > 8 min (Supporting Information S1: Table S5). In procedures ≤ 8 min, the incidence of oxygen reserve depletion was comparable; however, the degree of procedural recall and complaints of inadequate sedation was more frequent in patients who received propofol sedation (Supporting Information S1: Table S6).

Discussion

This study was conducted to compare the efficacy of remimazolam and propofol in patients undergoing ERCP. The primary outcome was the incidence of oxygen reserve depletion, defined as a drop in the ORi to 0.00 during sedation. Secondary outcomes included changes in hemodynamic parameters, the frequency of interfering events, and satisfaction scores reported by both endoscopists and patients. Our results indicate that remimazolam maintained the oxygenation status in the mildly hyperoxic range more favorably than propofol. In addition, patients in the remimazolam group reported a lower incidence of procedural recall, fewer complaints of inadequate sedation, and higher satisfaction scores than those in the propofol group.

Accurate assessment of oxygenation status is essential for maintaining patient safety during procedural sedation. However, conventional pulse oximetry is limited in evaluating oxygenation status beyond a SpO2 of 100%, whereas ORi monitoring enables assessment within the mild hyperoxic range. Therefore, a decrease of ORi to 0.00 during sedation can be translated as an early warning signal of impending hypoxia [18], and the index has been applied in various clinical settings, including pediatric intubation, airway surgery, and cardiothoracic surgery [15]. In addition, a recent study reported that an ORi range of 0.00–1.00 correlates well with PaO2 levels of 100–200 mmHg [13]. This suggests that plateaued ORi decreasing to 0.00, defined as the depletion of oxygen reserve in our study, indicates a decrease in PaO2 below 100 mmHg. Although ORi is not yet widely adopted in endoscopic procedures, we anticipated that it would allow for a precise comparison of respiratory depression induced by different sedatives. Through ORi monitoring, we found that even small doses of propofol can significantly suppress respiration compared with remimazolam, which was consistent with the result of our previous study [17]. It should also be noted that we were able to clearly distinguish the respiratory effects of the two agents with relatively small sedative doses with reduced incidence of adverse events compared to other studies [11, 19]. Nonetheless, our results suggest that even small doses of propofol could induce potent respiratory depression, indicating a narrow therapeutic range of the drug. Notably, our subgroup analysis indicated that the effect of propofol on oxygen reserve depletion was more pronounced in longer procedures (Supporting Information S1: Table S5). In contrast, remimazolam showed a significantly lower incidence of oxygen reserve depletion and maintained ORi above 0.00 throughout the procedure, as shown in Figure 2.

While our initial sedative dose was sufficient to rapidly achieve the sedation target of MOAA/S 2–3, the low initial sedative dose in our protocol probably contributed to the requirement for additional sedatives during endoscopy. This was significantly more frequent compared to what was required in our previous study conducted in diagnostic gastroscopy patients [17]. We presume that this difference stemmed from the deeper advancement of the endoscope beyond the pyloric sphincter required for ERCP, which likely involved more intense stimulation compared to diagnostic gastroscopy. Although using higher doses of sedatives or adding opioids may reduce the need for repeated sedative dosing [11], we did not adopt this approach in accordance with our institutional protocol, which prioritizes the prevention of sedative‐related adverse events and the maintenance of hemodynamic stability.

According to our results, remimazolam had an onset time of less than 1 min, which was significantly faster than previously reported [20]. This discrepancy is likely due to our measurement of onset time at shorter intervals, defined as the time from sedative injection to endoscope insertion. In other studies, consciousness was assessed at minute intervals [21], while in our study, the endoscopist evaluated the MOAA/S every 10 s following sedative administration. This measurement method appeared to have contributed to the faster recorded onset time of remimazolam.

In this study, the procedure duration was relatively short compared with previous reports [10, 11]. We believe that several factors contributed to this finding. First, the proportion of patients with naive papilla was low (27.5% in the propofol group and 37.3% in the remimazolam group; Table 1). Additionally, the non‐negligible incidence of follow‐up endoscopy without therapeutic intervention may have further shortened the procedure times (13.7% in the propofol group and 19.6% in the remimazolam group; Table 2). Moreover, in the stent change or insertion subgroup—which accounted for the majority of patients—most underwent stenting for extrahepatic strictures (82.9% in the propofol group and 65.6% in the remimazolam group; Table 2), which are generally less complex and require shorter procedure times compared to hilar strictures. Furthermore, the high level of expertise among the endoscopists at our center, along with their efforts to minimize radiation exposure and sedation time, may have also contributed to the reduced procedure duration, as similarly short times have been reported in a previous study [22].

The relatively brief procedure times may, in turn, have contributed to the comparable incidence of interfering events observed between the two groups. Nonetheless, subgroup analysis offered a potential clue that remimazolam may facilitate smoother procedures with fewer interruptions, even in relatively complex cases. In the stent change or insertion subgroup (Supporting Information S1: 4), physical restraint was required in five cases in the propofol group but in none of the remimazolam group (p = 0.058). Notably, the procedure duration was also significantly shorter in the remimazolam group (p = 0.037), suggesting that the reduction in interfering events may have contributed to improved procedural efficiency.

Remimazolam sedation induced minimal recall, whereas significantly higher incidences of partial and full recall were observed in the propofol group. Considering that both groups achieved comparable levels of sedation during examination (Figure 2), we presume that the antegrade amnesia induced by remimazolam may have contributed to this outcome [23]. The antegrade amnesia also appears to have positively influenced patients' subjective sedation experience, as the patients who complained of inadequate sedation were significantly lower in the remimazolam group. Comprehensively, these factors likely contributed to higher patient satisfaction in the remimazolam group, which was particularly evident in more demanding ERCP procedures, such as stent changes or insertions. According to our subgroup analysis, patients who underwent simple follow‐up endoscopy reported comparable satisfaction regardless of the sedatives they received. However, in patients who underwent stent changes or insertions, which are relatively more complex than follow‐up endoscopy, the remimazolam group reported less procedural recall, fewer complaints of inadequate sedation, and ultimately higher satisfaction scores than the propofol group.

Discharge time is an effective indicator of patient recovery, especially in ambulatory endoscopy centers characterized by a high turnover of patients and limited capacity of the RR [24]. In this study, remimazolam sedation showed similar time from the end of endoscopy to fully alert, length of stay in the RR, and overall procedure time compared with propofol sedation, which was consistent with previous studies [17, 24]. Although we expected that the recovery time for remimazolam would be longer than that for propofol in ERCP patients whose potential hepatic impairment may negatively affect the clearance of remimazolam [25], our results indicate that remimazolam can be safely used in this population.

Nevertheless, it should also be noted that one patient in the remimazolam group experienced a paradoxical reaction after sedative injection. The prevalence of paradoxical reactions to benzodiazepines is estimated to be approximately 1% in the general population, and the pathophysiologic mechanisms underlying paradoxical reactions are not clearly elucidated [26]. Among several known predisposing factors, including old age, alcohol abuse, psychological disorders, and genetic variations in gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors [27], we discovered that the patient had a history of heavy alcohol consumption, with an estimated intake of approximately 200 g of alcohol per week. As alcohol abuse disrupts the GABAergic system [28], it seems plausible that the patient's response to GABA‐modulating sedatives may also have been impaired. Our case suggests that paradoxical reactions should be considered when administering sedatives to patients with a history of heavy alcohol consumption.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, this was a single‐center study with a small cohort. A multicenter study involving a larger population is required for a more definitive comparison between the two drugs. Second, blinding of the endoscopist was not feasible because of the different colors and preparation methods of the two sedatives. However, to ensure the complete blinding of the endoscopist, it is necessary to conceal not only the syringes containing the drug but also the intravenous lines connected to the patient, which is practically challenging to implement. Third, the relatively short procedure duration in our study limits the generalizability of our findings to more time‐consuming ERCP procedures. Nevertheless, even within this limited timeframe, significant differences in oxygenation status and procedural efficiency were consistently observed between remimazolam and propofol. These results suggest that the observed differences are primarily attributable to the intrinsic pharmacologic characteristics of the sedatives rather than the duration of sedation. Lastly, despite more frequent early warnings of hypoxia, they were not associated with a higher incidence of hypoxia in the propofol group. We speculate that the short procedural duration contributed to the comparable incidence of hypoxia, as previous studies have shown that nasopharyngeal oxygen insufflation following preoxygenation can maintain SpO2 > 94% for over 6 min of apnea [29]. Hence, a further study involving more time‐consuming ERCP procedures may reveal a significant difference in the incidence of hypoxia between the two sedatives.

In conclusion, remimazolam effectively preserved the oxygen reserve compared with propofol, reducing the risk of hypoxia during sedation. Remimazolam was also associated with more favorable recovery profiles for patients undergoing ERCP, making it a safe and preferred sedative for this procedure.
