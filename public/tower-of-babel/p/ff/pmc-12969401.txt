Pedicled distal-radius vascularized bone graft with shape-memory staple fixation for scaphoid nonunion: A modified technique
Authors: Andrea Cruciani, Silvia Pietramala, Alessio Greco, Lorenzo Rocchi, Gianfranco Merendi, Camillo Fulchignoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Scaphoid nonunion remains a challenging condition due to the scaphoid's retrograde vascular supply, its susceptibility to collapse, and the risk of progressive carpal malalignment and degenerative changes. Pedicled vascularized bone grafts harvested from the distal radius preserve intrinsic perfusion but require stable fixation to allow graft incorporation. This study describes a modified surgical technique combining a pedicled distal-radius vascularized bone graft based on the recurrent volar intercarpal artery with fixation using a shape-memory nitinol compression staple and reports early clinical and radiographic outcomes in a consecutive series. A retrospective analysis of 8 consecutive patients treated between January 2024 and January 2025 was performed. Clinical outcomes included wrist range of motion, pain assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS), the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH), and the Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire (MHQ). Radiographic union was evaluated using standard radiographs and computed tomography (CT). Bony union was achieved in all patients (100%) by six months. Mean QuickDASH scores improved from 36 preoperatively to 6 at final follow-up, while mean MHQ scores improved from 60 to 91. Mean clinical follow-up was 7 months. No implant-related complications or donor-site morbidity were observed. This modified technique appears feasible and was associated with early radiographic union and functional improvement in this small retrospective series. However, these findings represent preliminary results, and larger comparative studies with longer follow-up are required to determine its long-term clinical effectiveness and potential advantages over established fixation methods.

Introduction

Scaphoid nonunion remains a challenging clinical condition due to the scaphoid's retrograde vascular supply, its susceptibility to collapse, and the risk of progressive carpal malalignment and degenerative changes. Nonunion is commonly defined as the absence of radiographic healing beyond six months after injury and most frequently involves the scaphoid waist, particularly in young and active individuals.1 Although early cross-sectional imaging has improved diagnostic accuracy, delayed or missed fractures continue to account for a substantial proportion of nonunions.2,3

Traditional surgical management includes non-vascularized bone grafting techniques, such as the Matti–Russe procedure, often combined with headless compression screw fixation.4 While these methods may restore scaphoid geometry and provide mechanical stability, they rely on creeping substitution and may be less effective in cases with compromised vascularity, proximal pole involvement, or failure of previous fixation.5 In such scenarios, pedicled vascularized bone grafts harvested from the distal radius have gained widespread acceptance. Techniques described by Kuhlmann and later refined by Mathoulin, based on the recurrent volar intercarpal artery, preserve intrinsic graft perfusion and have demonstrated high union rates when combined with rigid internal fixation.6,7

Stable fixation is considered essential in vascularized scaphoid reconstruction to maintain graft position and allow graft incorporation. Conventionally, fixation has been achieved using headless compression screws, which provide strong interfragmentary compression but require a central transosseous trajectory across the scaphoid. Although effective from a mechanical standpoint, centrally placed screws may theoretically endanger the vascular pedicle or generate focal stress at the graft–host interface, potentially affecting graft perfusion. Despite these theoretical concerns, the interaction between fixation mechanics and pedicle safety has received limited attention in the literature.

Shape-memory nitinol compression staples have been increasingly used in scaphoid fracture fixation and nonunion management, offering continuous, temperature-activated compression determined by implant geometry rather than manual tightening.8, 9, 10, 11 Biomechanical and clinical studies have shown that staples can provide stable fixation with predictable compressive forces and a low-profile construct.8,9,12,13 However, reports describing their use in combination with pedicled vascularized bone grafts of the distal radius are scarce, and the potential advantages of distributed compression without transosseous hardware traversing the graft have not been systematically explored.

The purpose of this study is to describe a modified surgical technique that combines a pedicled distal-radius vascularized bone graft based on the recurrent volar intercarpal artery with fixation using a shape-memory nitinol compression staple, and to report early clinical and radiographic outcomes in a consecutive series of patients with scaphoid nonunion. This technique is intended as an alternative fixation strategy in selected cases where preservation of graft perfusion and pedicle safety are considered a priority.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This study is a retrospective analysis of a consecutive series of patients treated for scaphoid nonunion using a pedicled distal-radius vascularized bone graft combined with shape-memory nitinol staple fixation. All procedures were performed between January 2024 and January 2025 at a single tertiary referral center by the same surgical team. Inclusion criteria were symptomatic scaphoid waist nonunion confirmed by radiographs or computed tomography (CT), with or without previous conservative or surgical treatment. No eligible patients were excluded during the study period. Exclusion criteria included advanced scaphoid nonunion collapse (SNAC stages III–IV), active infection, or severe comorbidities that precluded surgery.

Anatomical considerations

The vascularized graft is based on an arterial anastomotic network located on the volar aspect of the distal radius. Anatomical studies have consistently identified a transverse volar carpal artery arising from the radial artery at the level of the radial styloid and coursing along the distal border of the pronator quadratus. This vessel anastomoses on the volar aspect of the distal radioulnar joint with branches of the anterior interosseous and ulnar arteries, forming a reliable vascular arcade that supports graft perfusion5˒.7

Surgical technique

Setup

All procedures were performed under regional anesthesia with a brachial plexus block. The patient was positioned supine with the affected limb placed on a radiolucent hand table. After sterile preparation and draping, a pneumatic tourniquet was applied to the upper arm.

Exposure

A longitudinal volar-radial incision was made following the Henry approach and extended distally along the flexor carpi radialis tendon sheath. Pedicled graft harvest

The recurrent volar intercarpal artery was identified between the palmar radial periosteum and the distal fibers of the pronator quadratus. A corticocancellous bone graft was harvested from the volar distal radius and elevated subperiosteally while preserving pedicle continuity. Graft inset and fixation with shape-memory staple

The vascularized graft was impacted into the prepared defect. Two temporary Kirschner wires were placed across the scaphoid to maintain reduction and act as centering guides.

Using the manufacturer's guide, pilot holes were drilled for the nitinol staple legs. The staple size was selected intraoperatively based on scaphoid measurements. Nitinol staples regain a preset configuration at body temperature and thereby provide controlled, sustained compression determined by implant geometry rather than manual tightening.

To avoid volar prominence, a shallow trough was fashioned between the pilot holes so the staple bridge could lie flush or slightly recessed. Implant position and reduction were confirmed fluoroscopically.

Throughout the procedure, the vascular pedicle was identified and protected; staple legs were deliberately oriented to avoid the pedicle course. Closure and immobilization

Capsulorrhaphy was performed to restore the radiocarpal capsule. After tourniquet release, hemostasis was achieved and pulsatile flow was verified within the pedicle. Soft tissues were closed in layers, and a below-elbow thumb-spica cast was applied. Postoperative management and rehabilitation

All patients remained under overnight observation. The wrist and thumb were immobilized in a below-elbow thumb-spica cast for approximately 30 days, followed by a removable splint worn intermittently for an additional month. Once the cast was discontinued, a standardized rehabilitation program was initiated and supervised by the treating surgeon.

Protected, load-free wrist motion was allowed during periods when the splint was off. Grip strengthening and weight-bearing activities were restricted for 90 days to protect the vascular pedicle and allow progressive incorporation of the graft. A standardized rehabilitation protocol was applied to all patients and supervised by certified hand therapists. No deviations from the rehabilitation protocol were recorded (see Fig. 6).

Outcome assessment

Clinical and functional evaluation

Clinical evaluation included wrist range of motion (flexion and extension), pain intensity assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS), grip strength measured using a Jamar dynamometer, and functional outcomes assessed using the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) and the Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire (MHQ).

Radiographic assessment and union criteria

Radiographic follow-up included standard posteroanterior and lateral wrist radiographs. Computed tomography (CT) scans were obtained in all patients at approximately 3 and 6 months postoperatively. Union was defined as the presence of bridging trabeculae across at least 75% of the graft–host interface on CT scans, independently assessed by one hand surgeon and one musculoskeletal radiologist, who performed their evaluations separately and were blinded to each other's assessments.

Statistical analysis

Given the descriptive nature of the study and the small sample size, results are reported as means with standard deviations and ranges. No formal power analysis was performed.

Clinical outcomes

Eight patients were included in the final analysis. Mean clinical follow-up was 7.4 ± 2.6 months (range, 6–13). Early radiographic graft incorporation was observed in all patients. At approximately 3 months postoperatively, CT demonstrated ≥75% trabecular bridging across the graft–host interface in all patients (8/8, 100%), meeting the predefined union criteria. At 6 months, CT confirmed complete consolidation in all cases.

Radiographic alignment parameters showed progressive correction following surgery. The mean scapholunate angle improved from 70.0 ± 4.7° preoperatively to 49.0 ± 3.9° at final follow-up, restoring carpal alignment to within the physiological range. The mean intrascaphoid angle decreased from 56.6 ± 8.5° preoperatively to 36.5 ± 3.5° at final follow-up, indicating correction of the humpback deformity. Mean scaphoid length increased from 23.9 ± 1.3 mm preoperatively to 26.4 ± 1.4 mm postoperatively, consistent with restoration of scaphoid anatomy. In addition, the mean carpal height ratio improved from 0.51 ± 0.01 preoperatively to 0.55 ± 0.01 at final follow-up, indicating restoration of global carpal alignment.

Mean wrist flexion improved from 36.3 ± 7.3° preoperatively to 67.9 ± 5.4° at final follow-up, while mean wrist extension improved from 30.6 ± 6.3° to 65.0 ± 4.1°. Mean QuickDASH scores decreased from 36.3 ± 5.2 preoperatively to 5.8 ± 2.6 at final follow-up, and mean MHQ total scores improved from 59.5 ± 7.8 preoperatively to 90.8 ± 5.2 at final follow-up. Mean VAS pain scores improved from 5.0 ± 0.8 preoperatively to 0.9 ± 0.7 at final follow-up. Mean grip strength improved from 62.4 ± 9.1% of the contralateral side preoperatively to 93.6 ± 5.8% at final follow-up, with all patients achieving at least 85% of contralateral grip strength. Return to work was achieved at a mean of 94.9 ± 52.1 days (range, 40–180 days), while return to sports involving moderate to high wrist loading occurred at a mean of 103.1 ± 13.6 days (range, 90–125 days).

No postoperative complications were observed, including infection, neurovascular injury, donor-site morbidity, hardware-related complications, or complex regional pain syndrome.

Discussion

This study describes a reproducible modification of distal-radius pedicled vascularized bone grafting for scaphoid nonunion, combining a graft based on the recurrent volar intercarpal artery with fixation using a shape-memory nitinol compression staple. The principal finding of this consecutive series was that early radiographic union and clinically meaningful functional improvement were observed in all patients with available follow-up.

Pedicled vascularized bone grafts from the distal radius have been widely reported as an effective option for scaphoid nonunion, particularly in cases with compromised vascularity, proximal pole involvement, or failed prior fixation. Kuhlmann and Mathoulin reported union rates approaching 90-96% when these grafts are combined with rigid internal fixation, most commonly headless compression screws.6˒7 The present series demonstrates encouraging early union rates using an alternative fixation strategy, suggesting that staple fixation may provide sufficient stability to support graft incorporation in selected cases. However, given the limited sample size and relatively short follow-up, these results should be interpreted as early findings rather than definitive evidence of clinical effectiveness. Although a 100% union rate was observed, the small cohort may lead to overestimation of treatment success, and these outcomes may not be generalizable to more complex or heterogeneous scaphoid nonunion patterns.

The rationale for using a shape-memory nitinol staple rather than a headless compression screw is primarily related to fixation mechanics and graft biology. Headless screws generate strong interfragmentary compression but concentrate force along a central transosseous trajectory, which may intersect or lie adjacent to the vascular pedicle of a pedicled graft. In contrast, nitinol staples provide continuous, distributed compression across the graft–host interface without traversing the graft itself. Biomechanical studies have shown that staple compression is determined by implant geometry and thermal activation rather than manual tightening, resulting in predictable and sustained compressive forces.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Although no direct biomechanical comparison was performed in the present study, the consistent early consolidation observed clinically supports the feasibility of this fixation concept. Nevertheless, the absence of a control or comparative group prevents any definitive conclusions regarding superiority or equivalence relative to headless compression screw fixation. The proposed mechanical and biological advantages of staple fixation should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence-based.

An additional practical advantage observed intraoperatively was the stabilizing effect of the staple bridge, which acted as a buttress to maintain graft position during correction of scaphoid alignment. Pedicled grafts harvested from the volar distal radius may be prone to extrusion during reduction maneuvers, particularly when correcting humpback deformity. The staple bridge provided containment of the graft without the need for transfixation, which could otherwise jeopardize graft integrity or pedicle perfusion. Although graft perfusion was visually confirmed intraoperatively, no objective postoperative assessment of vascularity (such as Doppler ultrasonography or contrast-enhanced MRI) was performed. Consequently, conclusions regarding enhanced biological preservation should be interpreted cautiously and remain theoretical in the absence of direct perfusion measurements.

Other fixation options include low-profile screw constructs and bioabsorbable compression devices. Nitinol staples may offer continuous compression with minimal hardware across the graft, but comparative data remain limited. Staple fixation may not be suitable in all scenarios. In cases with significant scaphoid deformity requiring strong interfragmentary compression along the central axis, or when rigid fixation across larger defects is necessary, headless compression screws may remain preferable. Biomechanical constraints, including potential limitations in compression magnitude and resistance to rotational forces, should be considered when selecting the fixation strategy. Furthermore, in very proximal scaphoid nonunions, where the proximal fragment is small and available bone stock is limited, insertion of a staple may be technically challenging or constrained by insufficient space. In such situations, screw fixation may provide a more practical and controlled option.

Future studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up, and comparative or biomechanical designs are warranted to better define the role of shape-memory staples in vascularized scaphoid reconstruction. Until such data are available, the described technique should be considered an adjunctive option for carefully selected scaphoid nonunions in which preservation of graft perfusion is a priority.

More broadly, this modification should be viewed as a technical alternative in selected cases of scaphoid nonunion treated with pedicled distal-radius vascularized grafts, rather than a replacement of established fixation strategies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged.

First, it represents a retrospective case series of only eight patients, with a relatively short mean follow-up of 7.4 months. While sufficient to document early radiographic union, this duration does not allow assessment of the durability of union, long-term maintenance of carpal alignment, or the development of late complications such as carpal collapse, degenerative changes, or hardware-related issues.

Second, the absence of a control or comparative group precludes meaningful statistical comparison with alternative fixation strategies and prevents definitive conclusions regarding relative efficacy.

Third, potential selection bias cannot be excluded, as only patients deemed suitable for this technique were included, which may limit generalizability.

Despite these limitations, the study provides a detailed technical description and early clinical evidence supporting the feasibility of the proposed technique.

Conclusion

In this preliminary retrospective series, pedicled distal-radius vascularized bone grafting combined with shape-memory nitinol staple fixation was associated with early radiographic union and functional improvement. While the technique appears feasible and safe in selected cases, the absence of comparative data and the limited follow-up preclude definitive conclusions regarding its long-term effectiveness or superiority. Further prospective and comparative studies are necessary to validate these early findings.
