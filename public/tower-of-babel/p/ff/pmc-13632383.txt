Association between serum uric acid levels and COPD: a meta-analysis and mendelian randomization study
Authors: Aiju Su, Yunpeng Xu, Xue Han, Qiuyu Chen, Jian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) poses a major global health burden, but its relationship with serum uric acid (SUA) remains controversial. This study combined meta-analysis and Mendelian randomization (MR) to evaluate the observational association and potential causal relationship between SUA and COPD.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang Data for observational studies comparing SUA levels between COPD patients and healthy controls published up to December 2025. Meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model to calculate mean differences (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), followed by subgroup analyses. This meta-analysis has been registered with PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420251234511. Two-sample bidirectional MR analysis was conducted using publicly available GWAS summary data, with inverse variance weighting (IVW) as the primary method, supplemented by weighted median, MR-Egger regression, and sensitivity analyses.ResultsThe meta-analysis included 22 studies, comprising a total of 3739 COPD patients and 2778 healthy controls. Results showed that SUA levels were significantly higher in stable COPD patients compared to healthy controls (MD: 1.19 mg/dL, 95% CI: 0.83–1.55, P < 0.00001). Patients with acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD) exhibited even higher uric acid levels (MD: 1.30 mg/dL, 95% CI: 0.36–2.24, P < 0.00001), and SUA levels were significantly higher in severe COPD patients compared to those with mild-to-moderate COPD (MD: 1.18 mg/dL, 95% CI: 0.76–1.60, P = 0.02). Bidirectional MR analysis revealed that genetically predicted higher SUA levels were causally associated with reduced COPD risk (IVW: OR: 0.883, 95% CI: 0.811–0.961, P = 0.004). Reverse MR analysis found no significant causal effect of COPD on uric acid levels (P > 0.05). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the MR findings.ConclusionObservational evidence indicated elevated SUA in COPD, especially during exacerbations, while genetically predicted higher SUA showed a modest protective effect. These findings suggest a complex, dual role of uric acid. However, observational associations may be confounded by reverse causality, and the MR estimates—derived from East Asian populations—are method-sensitive. Current evidence does not yet support altering uric acid management in COPD, and further prospective and interventional studies across diverse populations are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04477-8.Keywords: Serum uric acid, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Meta-analysis, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common systemic inflammatory disease characterized by chronic cough, sputum production, wheezing, and persistent airflow limitation. These respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation result from airway and/or alveolar abnormalities caused by significant exposure to toxic particles or gases [1]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 report indicates that Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is the third leading cause of death worldwide [2]. Existing evidence demonstrates that the development and progression of COPD are closely associated with oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation in the body. In the ongoing search for biomarkers for COPD diagnosis and prognosis, small-sample studies have found that uric acid levels in COPD patients are higher than those in healthy controls, suggesting it may serve as a novel biomarker [3–4]. However, other studies have reported no significant difference in uric acid levels between COPD patients and healthy controls, and some have even found lower uric acid levels in COPD patients compared to healthy controls [5–6]. Summaries of previous research have indeed revealed the dual role of uric acid in the development and progression of COPD. Uric acid is the final product of purine metabolism in humans, synthesized primarily through two pathways: endogenous de novo synthesis and exogenous salvage synthesis. Xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) is a crucial rate-limiting enzyme in the process of uric acid synthesis [7]. Alterations in uric acid metabolism also occur during the development and progression of COPD. With advancing research in recent years, increasing attention has been drawn to the dual nature of uric acid—both pro-oxidant and antioxidant. On one hand, in patients with long-standing COPD, high levels of uric acid can stimulate monocytes to release more inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor, leading to the accumulation of oxygen free radicals and thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response [8]. On the other hand, uric acid possesses significant antioxidant properties, enabling it to scavenge peroxynitrite and reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the body. This capacity suggests that uric acid may effectively inhibit oxidative stress during the course of COPD [9]. Furthermore, hyperuricemia often coexists with conditions such as metabolic syndrome, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and insulin resistance, indicating its involvement in the regulation of systemic metabolic-inflammatory networks [10]. Similarly, a growing body of research in recent years has found a close relationship between the development and progression of COPD and inflammation [11]. In summary, the relationship between COPD and uric acid remains inconclusive. Notably, a critical distinction should be made between acute reactive elevations of SUA during COPD exacerbations and basal, genetically determined SUA levels. During acute exacerbations, intensified systemic inflammation, hypoxia, and treatment-related factors can transiently raise SUA, making it a potential downstream marker of disease activity. In contrast, genetically instrumented SUA levels in Mendelian randomization reflect lifelong, constitutive exposure that is less susceptible to confounding by disease state and reverse causation. Clarifying this distinction is essential to understanding whether SUA plays a causal role in COPD pathogenesis or merely represents a secondary phenomenon. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies to quantify the association between SUA and COPD across different disease phases, and complemented this with bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis to explore the potential causal relationship between genetically predicted SUA levels and COPD risk.

Materials and methods

Meta-analysis

This study followed the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [12]. Additionally, the protocol for this study has been registered with PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420251234511 and can be accessed at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php? ID=CRD420251234511.

Literature search

We systematically searched the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang Data, Cochrane Library, CBM (Chinese Biomedical Literature Database), and VIP (China Science and Technology Journal Database) databases for articles published from their inception through December 09,2025. In addition to the systematic search, we performed citation searching as a supplementary approach to avoid missing potentially eligible studies. (The search strategies and results for each database are provided in the Additional file 1.)

Inclusion criteria

(1) Participants were definitively diagnosed with COPD according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines [13]; (2) Study types: randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, and case-control studies; (3) Primary outcome measure: serum uric acid levels; (4) The included COPD population was without significant confounding comorbidities, such as hyperuricemia.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Conference abstracts, editorials or commentaries, letters, notes; (2) Animal experiments; (3) Incomplete data; (4) Inability to obtain full text; (5) Duplicate or overlapping data; (6) Inappropriate control group setting, such as only comparing pre-treatment and post-treatment within the same group.

Data extraction and quality assessment

All articles retrieved from the systematic search were exported to EndNote X9 for deduplication. Two researchers independently screened the literature, extracted data, and cross-checked the results. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer, who made the final decision. The literature screening process involved an initial review of titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text assessment to determine the final inclusion of studies. The extracted data included elements such as the first author, publication year, country, sample size, study design, mean age, outcome measures relevant to this study, and risk of bias assessment. For literature quality evaluation, we used the 2014 version of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [14]. The NOS comprises three domains with a total of eight items: four items for the selection of cases and controls, one item for comparability, and three items for the assessment of exposure. Except for the comparability item, which can be awarded a maximum of two stars, all other items can be awarded a maximum of one star. The total score ranges from 0 to 9 stars. Generally, studies with a score of five stars or more can be included in the meta-analysis, with a higher number of stars indicating higher study quality. If means or standard deviations were missing or unclear, we attempted to calculate them from reported medians, interquartile ranges, or confidence intervals using established formulas; if calculation was impossible, the study was excluded from the corresponding meta-analysis. No imputation for missing data was performed.

Statistical analysis

This study primarily used Review Manager 5.4 to perform the main statistical analyses, and the Meta package in R software was utilized for Egger’s test. When selecting the statistical effect model, Cochran’s Q test and I² test were first performed on the data, and clinical and methodological heterogeneity were comprehensively considered. When substantial clinical heterogeneity existed among the included studies, an analysis was conducted to determine whether the source of heterogeneity was controllable. If clinical heterogeneity was difficult to control, a random-effects model was selected, even if the I² value was not large. When substantial heterogeneity was present, subgroup analysis or sensitivity analysis was used to explore the sources of heterogeneity. For studies with multiple intervention arms sharing a single control group (Gu 2024, Liang 2023, and Guo 2015), we split the control group proportionally to avoid double-counting participants, following Cochrane Handbook recommendations [15]. Specifically, for two intervention arms with sample sizes N₁ and N₂ sharing a common control (size Nc, mean Mc, SD SDc), the sample size allocated to each split control subgroup was calculated as : .The mean and SD of each split subgroup were retained as Mc and SDc, respectively. Fractional values were retained in variance calculations to avoid rounding error. These corrected estimates were then entered into the meta-analysis using the generic inverse-variance method, ensuring that each control participant contributed to only one pairwise comparison [16].( A detailed worked example for each affected study is provided in Additional file 4.) For studies reporting uric acid in mg/dL, values were converted to µmol/L using the conversion factor ×59.5, as per standard clinical laboratory conversion tables. Final results provided pooled data such as odds ratios (OR), mean differences (MD), and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for the included studies, and the pooled results were visualized through forest plots. For a result to be included in our meta-analysis for pooling and reporting, at least two studies needed to report non-overlapping data [17]. In this study, three main comparisons were conducted: comparison between COPD patients and healthy controls, comparison between stable COPD (SCOPD) patients and healthy controls, and comparison between severe COPD and mild-to-moderate COPD patients. Severe COPD was defined as patients with GOLD stage 3 or 4, and mild-to-moderate COPD as patients with GOLD stage 1 or 2. Additionally, based on COPD status, subgroup analyses were performed comparing SCOPD and acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD). Furthermore, based on SCOPD, a country-based subgroup analysis was conducted according to country.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each meta-analysis outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. Evidence from observational studies started as moderate certainty and could be rated down due to risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, or publication bias, or rated up due to large effect size, dose-response gradient, or residual confounding. The overall certainty was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Mendelian randomization analysis

Study design

This study adhered to the three core assumptions of Mendelian randomization analysis [18]. Assumption 1 requires that uric acid-related single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are strongly associated with uric acid levels. Assumption 2 requires that uric acid-related genotypes are not associated with confounding factors. Assumption 3 requires that the genotypes influence the outcome (COPD) only through the risk factor (uric acid levels).

Data sources

The data for this study were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) and the EBI GWAS catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). The GWAS IDs for the summary statistics used in this study were: COPD: GCST90399693; uric acid: bbj-a-57. SNPs that achieved genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were selected from the GWAS datasets for inclusion in this study. As this study utilized data from public databases, ethical approval was not required. A total of 275,699 participants of East Asian ancestry were included, comprising 109,029 participants with serum uric acid data and 166,670 patients with COPD. There was no overlap in the populations between the exposure and outcome GWAS datasets.

Both GWAS datasets were derived from East Asian populations. This choice had two justifications: (i) at the time of analysis, the largest publicly available SUA GWAS with complete summary statistics was from Biobank Japan; and (ii) restricting both exposure and outcome to a single ancestry group minimizes bias from population stratification and ensures genetic compatibility of instrumental variables. However, because the genetic architecture of uric acid—including allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium patterns—differs across ancestral groups, our causal estimates may not directly generalize to other populations. Trans-ancestry MR studies are warranted when comparable non-East Asian GWAS data become available.

To quantify the potential bias from using only East Asian GWAS data, we examined the 39 instrumental SNPs (Additional Table S1). First, we identified palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strand orientation (e.g., rs2075251), which are inherently vulnerable to cross-population LD differences. Second, we calculated the mean absolute difference in effect allele frequency (ΔEAF) between the exposure and outcome datasets (both East Asian); the small ΔEAF (mean 0.012, range 0–0.03) confirmed internal consistency. Third, we referenced published evidence from the Global Urate Genetics Consortium (GUGC) and 1000 Genomes, which shows that for urate-associated variants, ΔEAF between East Asians and Africans typically ranges 0.2–0.5, and LD r2 for the same index SNP can drop from > 0.8 to < 0.2 across populations [19]. These parameters indicate that the risk of non-portability, including potential reversal of effect direction, is non-negligible.

Selection of instrumental variables

SNPs are regarded as human genetic variants and can serve as instrumental variables (IVs) for traits in MR analysis [20–21]. Suitable SNPs were selected from the exposure-related GWAS according to the following criteria: (1) Genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10⁻⁸; All exposures yielded a sufficient number of SNPs at this threshold, therefore no relaxed threshold was applied. (2) F-statistic > 10. (3) Linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed using data from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 East Asian population as the reference panel, with parameters set at r² < 0.001 and a clumping distance window of 10,000 kb. Within each LD block, only the SNP with the smallest p-value was retained for subsequent analyses [22]. (4) SNPs potentially associated with confounding factors related to the outcome were removed. (5) SNPs associated with smoking-related confounders were excluded.

Bidirectional mendelian randomization analysis

This study employed a bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis to investigate the causal relationship between COPD and uric acid. For the forward MR analysis, uric acid levels were designated as the exposure and COPD as the outcome; for the reverse MR analysis, COPD was designated as the exposure and uric acid levels as the outcome. In this study, the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary method for MR analysis, as it is the earliest and most commonly used approach in the field [23]. Additionally, four other MR methods were applied: the weighted mode (WMode), weighted median (WM), simple mode (SM), and MR-Egger methods. The IVW method evaluates the effect of SNPs on the exposure and outcome under the assumption of no pleiotropy, after adjusting for heterogeneity. In contrast, the weighted median method provides a median-based causal estimate, the weighted mode method identifies the modal causal estimate, the simple mode method predicts the causal association without weighting, and the MR-Egger method is used to detect and adjust for pleiotropy. The combined use of these methods enhances the robustness of the results and provides a multi-perspective analytical view. In IVW analysis, it is assumed that all instrumental variables are valid; any violation of this assumption can affect the precision of the estimate. These methods, which account for different assumptions and potential biases, help to provide a comprehensive examination of the causal relationship. MR analyses were performed using the Two Sample MR package (version 0.6.24) and R software (version 4.5.2).

Heterogeneity and sensitivity analysis

The IVW method was used as the primary analysis to estimate the overall effect under the assumption of no heterogeneity [24]. In MR studies, the Cochran’s Q statistic was calculated to assess the presence of heterogeneity, with a p-value < 0.05 indicating statistically significant heterogeneity [25]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test whether the MR results remained robust under potential violations of the three core assumptions. The intercept term from MR-Egger regression was used to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy, with a p-value < 0.05 indicating its presence. If pleiotropy was detected, outlier SNPs were removed. Additionally, a leave-one-out analysis was performed to assess the potential influence of individual SNPs on the overall causal estimate.

Reporting guidelines

The systematic review and meta-analysis were reported according to the PRISMA 2020 statement (checklist provided in Additional file 2). The Mendelian randomization analysis was reported according to the STROBE-MR statement (checklist provided in Additional file 3).

Data availability

The GWAS summary data used in this study are publicly available from the IEU OpenGWAS and EBI databases. The SNP-level association data for the forward and reverse Mendelian randomization analyses are provided as Additional Table S1 and Additional Table S2. These tables include for each SNP: effect allele, other allele, beta coefficient, standard error, and p-value for both the exposure and outcome.

Results

Meta-analysis results

Literature search results, study characteristics and quality assessment

A systematic search of major Chinese and English databases was conducted to comprehensively collect relevant evidence. The initial search yielded 2900 records. After removing duplicates, 1548 records remained. Following initial screening based on titles and abstracts, 1497 clearly irrelevant records were excluded, leaving 51 articles for full-text assessment. After careful full-text review, 29 articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were ultimately excluded, including 13 lacking relevant outcome measures, 3 with duplicate or overlapping data, 11 with inappropriate control group settings, and 2 where the study population had definite confounding comorbidities. Ultimately, 22 studies meeting the pre-specified inclusion criteria were included in this meta-analysis [5–6, 26–45]. Detailed results are presented in Fig. 1. Baseline characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1.NOS was used to rigorously assess the quality of the 22 included observational studies. The evaluation results indicated that the overall quality of all included studies was moderate to high. Specifically, 16 studies received an NOS score of 7 stars, indicating clear case definitions, appropriate control group selection, and standardized data collection methods. The remaining 6 studies received 2 stars for the “comparability of cases and controls” item due to additional control for one or more key confounding factors such as smoking history, body mass index, or pulmonary function indicators, achieving a total score of 8 stars. This suggests that these studies were more rigorous in design and had a stronger capacity to control for confounding bias. It is worth noting that all studies performed well on items reflecting the risk of information bias, such as “case definition and diagnosis” and “consistency of exposure measurement methods” with no obvious deficiencies identified. No studies with scores below 6 stars were included in this evaluation, indicating that the evidence synthesized in this meta-analysis is generally reliable and that the risk of results being influenced by individual low-quality studies is low. This provides methodological assurance for the validity and robustness of subsequent pooled analyses. Results are presented in Table 2.

Meta-analysis of the association between serum uric acid levels and COPD

Comparison of uric acid levels between COPD patients and healthy controls

A total of 22 studies were included in this analysis, comprising 3739 COPD patients and 2778 healthy controls. Subgroup analyses were performed based on COPD status (SCOPD and AECOPD). Due to the extremely high statistical heterogeneity I²=98, which exceeds the threshold for meaningful inferential pooling, the random-effects model is used only to generate a descriptive summary rather than a formal statistical estimate. A random-effects model was selected to estimate the pooled effect size, taking into account both clinical and methodological heterogeneity as well as statistical heterogeneity.The results of the random-effects meta-analysis showed that uric acid levels were higher in SCOPD patients compared to healthy controls (MD: 1.19 mg/dL, 95% CI: 0.83–1.55, P < 0.00001). However, given the high heterogeneity, this summary value should be interpreted as a descriptive average rather than a precise inferential effect. The elevation was even more pronounced in AECOPD patients (MD: 1.30 mg/dL, 95% CI: 0.36–2.24, P < 0.00001). Detailed results are presented in Fig. 2.Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method revealed that the pooled effect size ranged from 0.92 to 0.97 mg/dL after sequentially omitting each study, indicating minimal variation. The statistical significance and heterogeneity remained consistent throughout, suggesting that our primary findings are robust. However, the I² value remained at 98% in each iteration of the analysis, indicating that the high heterogeneity stemmed from general differences across studies rather than from any single outlier study. Therefore, we present these findings as a consistent directional pattern in which all studies show elevated uric acid in COPD, along with a descriptive range of effect magnitudes, without claiming a statistically precise pooled effect.

Subgroup analysis of uric acid levels in COPD patients by country

Previous research has suggested that differences in dietary patterns across regions may influence baseline uric acid levels in the general population. Furthermore, studies have indicated that genomic differences between East Asian and European populations may contribute to variations in uric acid levels. Therefore, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on country. A total of 21 studies were included and categorized into two groups: the China group (11 studies) and the non-China group (10 studies), comprising 3333 patients in the SCOPD subgroup and 2712 healthy controls. Considering the unavoidable clinical heterogeneity and the presence of statistical heterogeneity, due to persistently high heterogeneity within each stratum (China: I2 = 98%; non-China: I2 = 94%), formal meta-analytic pooling remains inappropriate; the following summary estimates are presented descriptively, a random-effects model was used for pooled analysis. The results showed that in the Chinese population, uric acid levels in the SCOPD group were significantly higher than those in the healthy control group, with a pooled effect size of 1.50 mg/dL (95% CI: 0.94–2.06, P < 0.00001). In the non-Chinese studies, the pooled effect size was 0.83 mg/dL (95% CI: 0.39–1.28, P = 0.0003). Detailed results are presented in Fig. 3. The test for subgroup differences yielded a P-value of 0.07, which, given the high within-group heterogeneity, should not be interpreted as a formal test of interaction; instead, this p-value reflects only a descriptive trend. However, this trend still suggests that geographical region or dietary structure may influence uric acid levels in COPD patients.To assess the robustness of the subgroup analysis results, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed. In the China subgroup, after sequentially excluding each study, the pooled effect size ranged from 1.24 to 1.49 mg/dL. In the non-China subgroup, the pooled effect size ranged from 0.81 to 1.00 mg/dL. All 95% confidence intervals excluded 0 and were consistent with the main analysis, indicating that the results of this analysis are robust. However, the I² values remained consistently high at 98% and 94% in the two subgroups, respectively, suggesting that heterogeneity arose from general differences across studies, potentially attributable to variations in baseline characteristics of the included populations, baseline uric acid levels, and medications used during treatment. Thus, country-level stratification does not substantially resolve the heterogeneity, and other unmeasured factors, such as medication use, comorbidities, or laboratory methods are likely responsible.

Comparison of uric acid levels between patients with mild-to-moderate and severe COPD according to GOLD staging

To investigate the association between COPD severity and serum uric acid levels, this study compared uric acid levels between patients with GOLD stage 3 or 4 (defined as severe COPD) and those with GOLD stage 1 or 2 (defined as mild-to-moderate COPD). A total of 4 studies were included, comprising 276 patients with severe COPD and 166 patients with mild-to-moderate COPD. A random-effects model was selected for pooled analysis considering clinical and statistical heterogeneity. The results of the meta-analysis showed that serum uric acid levels were significantly higher in patients with severe COPD compared to those with mild-to-moderate COPD (MD: 1.18 mg/dL, 95% CI: 0.76–1.60, P = 0.02). The direction of effect was consistent across all included studies, suggesting that uric acid levels increase with increasing COPD severity. The overall effect was clearly statistically significant. Due to the small number of studies, the estimation of I2 is imprecise; however, the consistent direction of effect across all included studies provides descriptive support for an association between COPD severity and uric acid levels. Detailed results are presented in Fig. 4.

Publication bias assessment

The certainty of evidence was assessed according to GRADE for observational studies, starting from low certainty. Due to the extremely high inconsistency, I2 = 98%, we downgraded by two levels from low to very low for any quantitative pooling. However, the consistent direction of effect across all 22 studies, all showing higher uric acid in COPD, and the large minimum observed mean difference > 0.5 mg/dL warrant an upgrade by one level. No other serious limitations were identified, including risk of bias, indirectness, and publication bias, with Egger’s P = 0.152. Therefore, the final certainty is downgraded to low. We conclude that while there is a consistent directional association between serum uric acid and COPD, the exact magnitude remains highly uncertain due to extreme heterogeneity; results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Mendelian randomization analysis results

The results of forward MR

The F-statistics for 39 instrumental variables ranged from 19.65 to 42.35 (mean = 25.74), all exceeding the conventional threshold of 10, indicating no weak instrument bias; see Additional Table S1 for details. Heterogeneity among the instrumental variables was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. For the forward MR analysis, the IVW method also revealed substantial heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q = 187.6, df = 38, P = 4.2 × 10⁻¹⁸, I² = 83.5%), supporting the use of a random-effects model. MR analysis was performed using the Two Sample MR package in R, with COPD as the outcome variable and uric acid as the exposure variable. The overall forest plot of the analysis results is shown in Fig. 5, with emphasis on the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method. The MR-PRESSO method was used to detect and remove smoking-related confounding SNPs (rs79105258 and rs1260326), as these might simultaneously influence both uric acid levels and COPD development.The IVW method revealed a negative association between genetically predicted higher uric acid levels and COPD risk (β = -0.125, P = 0.004), which was statistically significant. Specifically, each unit increase in uric acid level was associated with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.883 for COPD risk (95% CI: 0.811–0.961). The weighted mode method supported this finding (P = 0.032; 95% CI: 0.848–0.990). Other sensitivity analysis methods, including the weighted median method (β = -0.054, P = 0.254), MR-Egger regression (β = -0.106, P = 0.087), and the simple mode method (β = -0.136, P = 0.110), all showed effect estimates consistent in direction with the IVW method (all negative), although they did not reach conventional statistical significance (detailed results are presented in Table 3; Fig. 5).Different MR methods have varying assumptions regarding the validity of instrumental variables (IVs). The IVW method assumes that all IVs are valid (no pleiotropy) and provides the highest statistical power. The weighted median method allows up to 50% of IVs to be invalid, while the MR-Egger method can detect and correct for directional pleiotropy, though it typically has lower statistical power [23]. In this study, the leave-one-out analysis demonstrated robust results, and neither the funnel plot nor the MR-Egger intercept test (P>0.05) revealed significant horizontal pleiotropy, further supporting the reliability of the primary IVW findings.

The results of reverse MR

The F-statistics for the 27 instrumental variables used in the reverse MR analysis ranged from 29.84 to 2517 (meand = 204.7), all exceeding the conventional threshold of 10, indicating no weak instrument bias; see Additional Table S1 for details. Heterogeneity among the instrumental variables was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. For the reverse MR analysis, the IVW method revealed substantial heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q = 124.7, df = 26, P = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁴, I² = 79.2%), supporting the use of a random-effects model. To exclude the potential interference of reverse causation (i.e., COPD influencing the expression levels of uric acid-related genes) on the study conclusions, we performed a reverse Mendelian randomization analysis (with COPD as the exposure and uric acid as the outcome). The analysis showed that across all five MR methods, genetically predicted COPD had no statistically significant causal effect on serum uric acid levels (all P-values > 0.05) (detailed results are presented in Table 3; Fig. 6). These findings strongly support the directionality of the causal relationship, suggesting that the association is more likely to reflect an effect of uric acid levels on the risk of COPD development and progression, rather than the reverse.

Sensitivity and heterogeneity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis

To assess the potential excessive influence of individual instrumental variables on the overall effect estimates, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for both the forward and reverse causal relationships. In the forward analysis of uric acid on COPD, after sequentially removing each of the 39 SNPs, the pooled effect estimates of the remaining SNPs remained highly stable (detailed results are presented in Fig. 7). All effect estimates after removal, along with their 95% confidence intervals, clustered tightly around the overall pooled effect (IVW estimate, β = -0.125) and were all located to the left of the null line, with no change in the direction of the effect. This indicates that the results of the forward MR analysis are not dependent on any single instrumental variable, and the primary findings are highly robust.In the reverse analysis of COPD on uric acid, the leave-one-one analysis results (detailed results are presented in Fig. 8) similarly showed that after removing any single SNP, the confidence intervals of the pooled effect estimates were wide and crossed the null value, which is entirely consistent with the main analysis finding of no significant effect. This further confirms that the conclusion of no reverse causal relationship is robust and not influenced by specific instrumental variables (Fig. 9).

Assessment of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy

To assess potential heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy in the instrumental variables, we generated funnel plots and performed the MR-Egger regression intercept test. The funnel plot analysis revealed that, in the analysis of uric acid on COPD, the scatter points representing the effect estimates (βIV) and precision (1/SEIV) of each instrumental variable were approximately symmetrically distributed around the pooled effect line of the inverse-variance weighted method in the high-precision region. The fitted trend line of the MR-Egger regression largely overlapped with the IVW line, showing no significant separation. This symmetrical pattern suggests the absence of significant directional horizontal pleiotropy among the instrumental variables in this study, implying that the genetic variants influence COPD risk primarily through the exposure pathway of uric acid, rather than via other unknown confounding pathways. The intercept term of the MR-Egger regression was not statistically significant in the forward analysis (p > 0.05), providing quantitative support for “no horizontal pleiotropy” (see Table 3 for details). Furthermore, although some dispersed points were observed in the low-precision region of the funnel plot, suggesting potential mild heterogeneity, given the symmetrical distribution of high-precision instrumental variables and the robust results from the leave-one-out analysis, such heterogeneity is unlikely to pose a substantive threat to the validity of the main effect estimates.

Discussion

A structured causal framework to disentangle observational associations

Two key findings emerge from this triangulation. First, the robust observation is the consistent directional association: across all 22 studies, SUA levels were higher in COPD patients than in healthy controls, with particularly marked elevations during acute exacerbations and in more severe disease. This pattern is highly consistent despite substantial between-study heterogeneity. Second, the uncertain elements include: (i) the exact magnitude of the effect, given I² = 98% and inability to perform meta-regression due to incomplete reporting of covariates; (ii) the role of unmeasured confounding, particularly medication use (corticosteroids, diuretics), renal function, and comorbidities; and (iii) the generalizability of MR findings, which were derived exclusively from East Asian populations and require confirmation in other ancestry groups.

To better understand why observational studies show a positive association between SUA and COPD while MR suggests a protective causal effect, we constructed a directed acyclic graph (detailed results are presented in Figure 10). The DAG explicitly maps the potential sources of non-causal association in observational data. As illustrated, the observational SUA–COPD association can arise from at least four non-causal pathways:

Non-causal DAG for uric acid-COPD

(1) reverse causation – COPD-induced chronic hypoxia, systemic inflammation, and tissue catabolism accelerate ATP degradation and xanthine oxidase activity, leading to secondary hyperuricemia; (2) medication confounding – corticosteroids and diuretics, commonly used in COPD, especially during acute exacerbations, directly raise SUA by increasing purine catabolism or reducing renal excretion; (3) severity or indication bias – patients with more severe COPD are more likely to receive these drugs and also have worse hypoxemia, both of which elevate SUA; and (4) comorbidity confounding – metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease frequently coexist with COPD and independently influence SUA levels. Our reverse MR provided no evidence that genetic predisposition to COPD causally increases SUA, which argues against reverse causation as the sole explanation. Instead, the strong positive association observed in meta-analysis, particularly the marked elevation during acute exacerbations, is likely driven by a combination of acute-phase medication use (high-dose steroids/diuretics) and disease severity (severe hypoxemia, intense inflammation). These factors are time-varying and not captured by genetic instruments, explaining why MR – which is unaffected by such dynamic confounders – yields a different direction of effect (protective). Future studies with longitudinal medication records and detailed severity adjustment are needed to quantify the relative contribution of each pathway.

Below we discuss each pathway in detail, integrating pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical evidence.

Pathway (1): Reverse causation – COPD-induced secondary hyperuricemia

From a pathophysiological perspective, reverse causality is highly plausible. COPD is characterized by chronic systemic hypoxia, respiratory distress, and tissue catabolism, all of which are well-established drivers of secondary hyperuricemia. Hypoxia accelerates ATP degradation, 28 leading to increased purine turnover and uric acid production, while impaired renal function and medication effects further reduce uric acid clearance [46]. This interpretation is supported by the negative results of our reverse Mendelian randomization analysis, which provided insufficient evidence for a causal effect of COPD on genetically predicted SUA levels. The meta-analysis indicated that the increase in SUA was more pronounced in patients with AECOPD than in those with stable disease. This may relate to the intensification of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress. Prolonged tissue hypoxia and intense inflammatory responses can induce ATP degradation and overactivate xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR), thereby accelerating purine catabolism and ultimately elevating uric acid levels [47]. Inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and macrophages release pro-inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species [48] which may directly or indirectly influence uric acid metabolism [49]. On one hand, inflammatory cytokines may inhibit renal urate transporters such as URAT1 and GLUT9, reducing uric acid excretion. On the other hand, under inflammatory and hypoxic conditions, cellular energy metabolism becomes dysregulated, leading to accelerated ATP degradation into hypoxanthine and xanthine, which are converted to uric acid by xanthine oxidase (XO). XO expression and activity may also be upregulated by inflammatory stimuli. Furthermore, hypoxemia—a common feature of moderate to severe COPD—promotes uric acid production through multiple pathways [47] stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) can upregulate XO expression; cells shift to less efficient glycolysis, increasing ATP degradation products; and renal blood flow redistribution during hypoxia may alter tubular handling of uric acid. Thus, SUA levels may indirectly reflect the severity of hypoxia in COPD patients.

Pathway (2): Medication confounding – corticosteroids and diuretics

The observed surge in SUA during AECOPD should be interpreted with caution. Patients hospitalized for acute exacerbations frequently receive high-dose systemic corticosteroids and diuretics as part of standard emergency management. Corticosteroids promote protein catabolism and fluid retention, indirectly raising SUA, while diuretics reduce renal urate excretion [50]. It is therefore possible that the marked elevation of SUA partly reflects these acute-care confounders rather than a purely disease-driven metabolic response. Future studies with detailed medication records are needed to disentangle the relative contributions of exacerbation-related pathophysiology and therapeutic interventions.

In clinical practice, long-term systemic use of glucocorticoids in COPD patients may indirectly affect uric acid metabolism by promoting protein catabolism, causing fluid and sodium retention, and influencing renal function [51]. Although we attempted to exclude studies with confounding comorbidities during literature screening, completely isolating medication effects in observational studies using real-world clinical data is extremely challenging.

Pathway (3): Severity/indication bias

Patients with more severe COPD (higher GOLD stage or frequent exacerbations) are more likely to receive medications that raise SUA (corticosteroids, diuretics) and also experience more severe hypoxemia and systemic inflammation. This creates a severity/indication bias: the positive association between SUA and COPD severity may be driven by the fact that sicker patients have both higher SUA (due to hypoxia and drugs) and worse outcomes. Our meta-analysis showed that SUA levels increased incrementally with disease severity (GOLD stages), consistent with this bias. The reverse MR finding of no causal effect of COPD on SUA further supports that the observed gradient is likely non-causal.

Pathway (4): Comorbidity confounding

Undoubtedly, changes in uric acid levels are also inevitably influenced by comorbidities associated with COPD and medications used during treatment. Due to factors such as limited physical activity, systemic inflammation, and potential use of medications including diuretics, low-dose aspirin, and certain bronchodilators, COPD patients have a significantly higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease compared to the general population. Consequently, COPD frequently coexists with a range of metabolic and cardiovascular comorbidities, forming a multimorbidity syndrome. Studies by Adnan et al. have confirmed that uric acid levels are independently associated with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome components such as central obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension [46]. As indicated by our meta-analysis results, we found a close association between the occurrence and prognosis of COPD and serum uric acid levels, but this association is likely confounded by these comorbidities.

Protective causal effect of SUA on COPD: insights from forward MR

The findings from the forward Mendelian randomization analysis, suggesting that uric acid is a protective factor for COPD, have prompted further consideration. Extensive previous research has demonstrated that uric acid is one of the most abundant endogenous water-soluble antioxidants in human blood, accounting for approximately 60% of the total antioxidant capacity of plasma [52]. It effectively scavenges various reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, including peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), hydroxyl radicals, and singlet oxygen, thereby preventing oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA [53–54]. Under physiological pH conditions, uric acid exists primarily as the urate anion, which is the predominant form responsible for its antioxidant effects. A genetically determined lifetime elevation in SUA levels essentially provides the body with a continuously enhanced systemic antioxidant buffering capacity. Circulating uric acid can diffuse into the alveolar lining fluid, directly neutralizing inhaled oxidants or ROS released by inflammatory cells, thus protecting alveolar epithelial cells and capillary endothelial cells from injury. Some studies suggest that soluble uric acid, in its non-crystalline state, may exert inhibitory effects on the NLRP3 inflammasome, which plays a significant role in airway inflammation in COPD. This may represent another facet of its anti-inflammatory properties. Additionally, uric acid can participate in redox cycling with other antioxidants such as vitamin C, regenerating these antioxidant molecules and synergistically enhancing the overall systemic antioxidant network [55]. Therefore, changes in uric acid levels accompany the entire process of COPD development and progression and play an important role in this context.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. It is the first to integrate observational meta-analysis with bidirectional MR to examine the relationship between SUA and COPD. This triangulation of evidence leverages the complementary strengths of both approaches: the meta-analysis captures real-world associations across different disease phases, while MR uses genetic instruments to mitigate confounding and reverse causation, thereby strengthening causal inference. By incorporating subgroup analyses for stable COPD, acute exacerbations, and different GOLD stages, the study offers a comprehensive description of SUA dynamics throughout the disease course. The bidirectional design further enables a balanced assessment of whether elevated SUA is more likely to play a causal role or to represent a downstream metabolic response, a distinction that is important for clinical interpretation.

Several limitations should be taken into account. First, the exclusion of patients with clinically diagnosed gout from the meta-analysis, while intended to reduce disease-related confounding, may have implications for the interpretation of the findings. Because gout is typically accompanied by markedly elevated SUA levels, this exclusion could result in a restricted range of SUA values in the analyzed population. Consequently, the observed association between SUA and COPD may be somewhat conservative, and its applicability to COPD patients with comorbid hyperuricemia may warrant further evaluation. Second, as with any synthesis of observational studies, the potential for residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Factors such as renal function, the use of diuretics or systemic corticosteroids, and metabolic comorbidities can simultaneously influence SUA levels and COPD outcomes. Although efforts were made to address these factors through study selection, complete adjustment across all included studies was not feasible. The considerable heterogeneity observed in some meta-analyses is likely to reflect, at least in part, these unmeasured or variably controlled confounders. Third, the generalizability of the MR findings is limited by the ancestral background of the study populations. The GWAS summary statistics were derived exclusively from East Asian cohorts, and the meta-analysis predominantly comprised participants of East Asian and European ancestry. Whether similar causal estimates would be observed in other populations, such as those of African or South Asian descent, remains an open question that requires further investigation. Fourth, substantial heterogeneity was present in certain pooled analyses, and the planned subgroup and sensitivity analyses could not fully account for its sources. This heterogeneity is likely attributable to differences in study design, covariate adjustment, and participant characteristics, and suggests that the pooled effect estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Fourth, the meta-analyses exhibited extremely high statistical heterogeneity, with an I² value of 98% in the main pooled analysis. This level of heterogeneity exceeds the threshold for meaningful inferential pooling; therefore, the random-effects summary estimate should be interpreted as a descriptive average rather than as a precise statistical effect. We were unable to perform meta-regression to explore sources of heterogeneity due to incomplete reporting of potential modifiers across studies, such as medication use, renal function, or laboratory methods. The planned subgroup and sensitivity analyses could not fully account for the sources of this heterogeneity, which is likely attributable to differences in study design, covariate adjustment, and participant characteristics. Consequently, the primary finding is the consistent directional pattern across studies, that is, all studies showed elevated uric acid in COPD, rather than the exact magnitude, and the pooled effect estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Fifth, as summarized in Table 1, most of the included studies did not report key baseline characteristics such as age, sex, BMI, smoking status, or medication use. The authors made every effort to contact the original study investigators to obtain the missing data, but unfortunately no additional information was provided. This lack of essential covariates has two major implications. First, it directly affects the quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, particularly the comparability domain, because we could not determine whether studies controlled for these critical confounders in their design or analysis. Second, it increases the risk of residual confounding in the meta-analysis, as we were unable to adjust for these potential modifiers at the study level. The substantial heterogeneity observed across studies may partly stem from these unreported or unevenly distributed baseline factors. Readers should therefore interpret the pooled estimates with awareness that unmeasured confounders could influence the observed associations. Finally, because currently available instrumental variables do not permit stratification by sex, medication use, or disease duration, we were unable to explore potential effect modification by these factors. Future studies incorporating individual-level data with detailed clinical phenotyping will be valuable for disentangling the specific contributions of medications, renal function, and comorbidities. Validating these findings in ancestrally diverse populations and integrating multi-omics data with mechanistic experiments will also be essential to further clarify the molecular pathways linking uric acid metabolism to COPD pathogenesis.

Conclusion

By integrating meta-analysis with bidirectional Mendelian randomization, this study offers a multifaceted assessment of the relationship between SUA and COPD. The meta-analytic evidence indicates that SUA concentrations are significantly elevated in individuals with COPD, most notably during acute exacerbations, whereas forward Mendelian randomization analysis suggests a potential protective effect of genetically predicted higher SUA levels on COPD risk, and reverse Mendelian randomization did not support a causal effect of COPD on SUA. Collectively, these observations point to a complex and likely dual role of uric acid in COPD pathophysiology. Nonetheless, these findings should be interpreted with circumspection. The observational associations, although generally robust, remain susceptible to residual confounding and reverse causality, while the Mendelian randomization estimates—though less vulnerable to these biases—are drawn exclusively from East Asian populations and their generalizability to other ancestral groups awaits confirmation. Moreover, the causal effect estimates are modest in magnitude and show sensitivity to analytical methods, and the unexplained heterogeneity across analyses further tempers confidence in a uniform causal interpretation. Consequently, the current body of evidence does not yet provide sufficient support for modifying clinical practice regarding uric acid management in COPD, and any consideration of SUA as a therapeutic target would be premature. Well-designed prospective cohort studies with extensive phenotypic characterization across diverse populations, complemented by targeted intervention trials, will be essential to delineate the specific contribution of uric acid to COPD progression and to establish whether modulating SUA levels may confer clinical benefit. Such evidence is a prerequisite before recommendations for risk stratification or urate-directed strategies can be responsibly contemplated. Specifically, the observational signal—marked elevation of SUA during acute exacerbations—likely reflects disease activity, medication effects, and hypoxic stress, whereas the genetically predicted protective effect suggests a potential antioxidant role of constitutive SUA. This duality implies that SUA should currently be viewed as a complex biomarker reflecting both disease burden and host defense capacity, rather than as a straightforward therapeutic target.
