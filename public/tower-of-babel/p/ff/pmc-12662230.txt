SAI: A Python Package for Statistics for Adaptive Introgression
Authors: Xin Huang, Simon Chen, Josef Hackl, Martin Kuhlwilm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdaptive introgression is an important evolutionary process, which can be identified with widely used summary statistics, such as the number of uniquely shared sites and the quantile of the derived allele frequencies in such sites. However, these as well as more recently developed statistics such as D+ and Danc, still lack accessible implementations. Here, we present SAI, a Python package for computing these statistics along with a new statistic, DD, and demonstrate its application on 2 datasets. First, using the 1000 Genomes Project data, we replicated previously reported candidate regions and identified additional ones, including a region detected by studies using supervised deep learning. Second, we investigated bonobo introgression into central chimpanzees and identified candidate genes, finding one region that overlaps a high-frequency Denisovan-introgressed haplotype block reported in modern Papuans. This is an intriguing co-occurrence across divergent lineages, underscoring the role of adaptive introgression in evolution.

Adaptive introgression refers to the transfer of genetic material between distantly related lineages through gene flow, increasing the fitness of the recipient lineage. To detect candidate genomic regions of adaptive introgression, some studies primarily utilized ad hoc approaches—first applying methods to detect natural selection and introgression separately, then identifying their intersection as candidate regions (Fijarczyk and Babik 2015; Nye et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2023a). Furthermore, supervised machine learning methods, such as genomatnn, MaLAdapt, and ERICA, have been introduced specifically for detecting loci of adaptive introgression (Gower et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023a, 2023b). Meanwhile, dedicated summary statistics for this purpose have been proposed by Racimo et al. (2017) (Table S1), such as the number of uniquely shared sites (U statistic) and the quantile of the derived allele frequencies in such sites (Q statistic). These statistics remain invaluable, particularly in cases where the demographic model is unknown (Gower et al. 2021; Romieu et al. 2025).

In addition, several summary statistics such as df, Danc, D+, and fd were originally developed under neutral introgression scenarios and rely on shared derived alleles between source and target populations (Martin et al. 2015; Pfeifer and Kapan 2019; Fang et al. 2024). However, except for fd, which Racimo et al. (2017) showed can distinguish neutral from adaptive introgression, their performance under adaptive introgression remains unclear. Moreover, although these statistics are based solely on allele frequencies and thus are not restricted to phased diploid genomes, currently available tools such as Dsuite can only compute df and fd for diploid, phased data, while Danc and D+ are implemented by custom scripts tailored to specific datasets (Malinsky et al. 2021; Huang et al. 2025). This may limit the application of these statistics to a wider range of genomic data, including unphased and polyploid genomes.

To address these gaps, we developed SAI (version 1.1.0), a Python package that implements these statistics and provides a robust framework for studying adaptive introgression, thereby improving the accessibility and reproducibility of research concerning this aspect of evolutionary biology. We also introduce a new statistic, DD (the average difference of sequence divergence), to replace the RD statistic proposed by Racimo et al. (2017), as the implementation of RD may result in zero-denominator cases. Removing such observations could introduce an undesired bias (Supplementary material).

We evaluated the performance of different statistics with simulated data (Supplementary material). Our results (Fig. 1) indicate that all statistics can almost perfectly distinguish genomic regions under nonintrogression or neutral introgression from those under adaptive introgression when the strength of natural selection is strong (s = 0.01 or 0.1). This is consistent with the evaluation of the fd, U, and Q statistics by Racimo et al. (2017). However, under weak selection (s = 0.001), these statistics show limited ability to distinguish between the scenarios. In this case, the Q95 statistic performs best, achieving the largest area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Table S2).

The dashed line indicates the baseline at 0.2%, reflecting the fact that only 0.2% of the instances in the data correspond to adaptive introgression.

In empirical data, the prevalence of adaptive introgression may vary, and in most cases of introgression, it will be a rare event across the genome (Horta et al. 2025); hence, class imbalance could be a concern (Huang et al. 2022). Therefore, it is more appropriate to evaluate the performance of these statistics using precision–recall (PR) curves, which are more informative for imbalanced datasets (Saito and Rehmsmeier 2015). Our results (Fig. 1) show that all statistics can reasonably detect adaptive introgression when natural selection is strong, with df, fd, and Q95 performing best as measured by the average precision (Table S2). However, under weak selection, these statistics show only limited ability to detect adaptive introgression when it is rare in the data. We also examined how ancestral allele misspecification affects the performance of these statistics using PR curves. Our results indicate that such misspecification can reduce the performance of some statistics, particularly the U statistic, whereas the D+ and DD statistics remain unaffected (Figures S1 to S4).

We then applied SAI to the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 dataset (The 1000 Genomes Project Consortium 2015), as previously reported in Racimo et al. (2017) (see Supplementary material). Here, the Altai Neanderthal and Denisovan genomes were used as source or donor populations, that is, the populations presumed to have contributed introgressed material (Huang et al. 2025). We focused on the U50 and Q95 statistics because this choice follows Racimo et al. (2017) and, importantly, these are the only statistics among those implemented that allow for multisource introgression, as they can be defined conditional on whether a given variant is present in both sources or restricted to only one of them. Candidate regions were identified based on the overlap of outliers from both the U50 and Q95 statistics, computed using derived allele frequencies (Table S3). These regions were then annotated using ANNOVAR (version 2022Oct05), with annotations from RefSeq, dbNSFP v4.2c, and dbSNP build 150 (Sherry et al. 2001; Wang et al. 2010; O’Leary et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2020). We successfully replicated several previously reported candidate regions, including those in BNC2, a gene associated with human pigmentation and possibly subject to natural selection in modern Europeans (Huang et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2022). We also identified additional candidate regions, including one on chromosome 20 that was not reported by Racimo et al. (2017), but which was later detected using supervised deep learning (Gower et al. 2021). However, we did not recover the region chr17:18880001–18920000 (hg19 coordinates), which Racimo et al. (2017) identified as a candidate region of Denisovan-specific introgression.

We observed discrepancies in U50 and Q95 values between our results and those reported by Racimo et al. (2017) within the same genomic regions (Table S3). To validate our SAI results, we compared allele counts computed by our implementation with those obtained using BCFtools (version 1.21; Danecek et al. 2021) and found them to be consistent (Supplementary material). We also noted that the original definition of the U statistic is not specifically based on the derived allele (Table S1). Therefore, we extended our implementation to compute these statistics regardless of ancestral state assignment by using whichever allele (coded as 0 or 1 in the input) meets the required conditions (Supplementary material). Despite this adjustment, our results (Table S4) still did not align with those of Racimo et al. (2017), even after further filtering of low-quality variants in the archaic hominin genomes (Tables S5 and S6). Although the original definition of the Q statistic is based on the derived allele, Racimo et al. (2017) did not provide explicit details on how the ancestral states were inferred when analyzing real data. In MaLAdapt (Zhang et al. 2023a), the U and Q statistics were used as input features for its machine learning algorithm (Extra-Trees classifiers) with an explicit definition based on the derived allele; however, the source of ancestral allele information is not specified. Since the code or detailed data processing steps for Racimo et al. (2017) are not publicly available, the precise causes of these discrepancies remain unresolved.

Finally, we explored bonobo introgression into central chimpanzees using a curated great ape genome dataset (Han et al. 2025) with western chimpanzees as nonintrogressed reference population (Supplementary material). We applied all implemented statistics to the polarized data, and only the DD, U, and Q statistics to the unpolarized data, as these 3 can be computed without ancestral allele information. Our results show that the Q statistic identifies many candidate genes (Tables S7 and S8), which may reflect the substantial number of shared variants between central chimpanzees and bonobos (de Manuel et al. 2016), as well as the relatively small sample size of the dataset, leading to alleles appearing fixed in the target population. To reduce false positives, we only consider candidate genes detected by at least 2 statistics (Tables S9 and S10). Among these candidate regions (Tables S9 and S10), we found 2 genes—ADGRL4 and GALNTL6—that had been previously reported as candidates for positive selection within bonobo-introgressed regions (Nye et al. 2018). Interestingly, we also identified TOMM20L, TIMM9, and KIAA0586 as candidate genes (Table S10), which were previously shown to harbor high-frequency Denisovan-introgressed haplotype blocks in modern Papuans (Jacobs et al. 2019). Previous studies also suggested that KIAA0586 was under positive selection in chimpanzees and rhesus macaques (Cagan et al. 2016; Nye 2018; Szpiech et al. 2021). Mutations in this gene have been linked to Joubert syndrome, a rare neurodevelopmental disorder, highlighting its functional importance (Bachmann-Gagescu et al. 2015; Roosing et al. 2015). The recurrent signal of positive selection on KIAA0586 across multiple primate species warrants further investigation and may be related to its role in neurodevelopment.

Our study highlights the importance of terminological precision, such as whether allele frequencies are calculated using ancestral or derived alleles, and technical transparency, such as how ancestral alleles are inferred. Ambiguous definitions can lead to inconsistent interpretations and the absence of publicly available implementations hinders reproducibility and independent validation. When methodological descriptions are unclear, a well-documented implementation would be a helpful complement, such as a workflow built with Snakemake (Mölder et al. 2021). As interest grows in applying advanced computational methods, particularly machine learning and deep learning (Schrider and Kern 2018; Christin et al. 2019; Andermann 2020; Lürig et al. 2021; Morimoto et al. 2021; Borowiec et al. 2022; Callier 2022; Korfmann et al. 2023; Pichler and Hartig 2023; Yelmen and Jay 2023; Huang et al. 2024; Mo et al. 2024; Wu 2024), to evolutionary biology, it is essential to ensure both rigorous theoretical formulations and accessible, reproducible implementations to uphold the reliability and integrity of scientific research (Huang 2024).
