Association of anterior communicating artery location with rupture in small intracranial aneurysms less than 5 mm: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Shin Heon Lee, Kyoung Min Jang, Taek Kyun Nam, Hyun Ho Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite intracranial aneurysms smaller than 5 mm are generally considered to have a low risk of rupture, management of those located at the anterior communicating artery (ACoA) remains a significant clinical challenge. This study examined the association between ACoA location and rupture tendency in aneurysms smaller than 5 mm. We retrospectively reviewed patients diagnosed with intracranial aneurysms at our institution from January 2015 to September 2022. After excluding unruptured aneurysms, re-ruptures at previous surgical sites, and aneurysms with fusiform, dissecting, or blood blister morphology, 381 cases were analyzed. Clinical and radiological data were collected, with subgroup comparisons based on aneurysm size (< 5 mm vs. ≥5 mm). Logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with rupture in small aneurysms. The ACoA was the most frequent rupture site (31.2%). The diameter at the ACoA was also significantly smaller than that at other locations (5.6 ± 2.5 mm vs. 6.5 ± 3.5 mm; P = 0.008). Subgroup analysis confirmed that ACoA location was associated with a disproportionately elevated rupture rate among smaller aneurysms (P = 0.030). Logistic regression identified ACoA site as an independent predictor of rupture in aneurysms smaller than 5 mm (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 1.646; 95% CI, 1.030–2.629; P = 0.037). In this cohort of ruptured aneurysms, ACoA location was associated with a higher likelihood of rupture among small (< 5 mm) aneurysms, suggesting a potential site-related vulnerability.

Introduction

Intracranial aneurysms are pathological dilatations of cerebral arteries that carry a substantial risk of rupture, often resulting in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), a life-threatening event [1]. The widespread adoption of advanced neuroimaging has increased incidental detection of unruptured intracranial aneurysms, intensifying the need for evidence-based management strategies [2]. Identifying reliable predictors of aneurysm rupture is therefore a clinical priority to facilitate timely intervention and optimize patient outcomes.

Beyond aneurysm size, previous studies have identified morphological and anatomical characteristics associated with increased rupture risk, such as location, shape, and the presence of daughter sacs [3, 4]. Although the anterior communicating artery (ACoA) is a well-established site for aneurysm development, its specific role as a rupture risk factor for aneurysms less than 5 mm remains insufficiently defined [5].

Managing small intracranial aneurysms is further complicated by a relatively high rate of periprocedural adverse events [6]. Accordingly, this study investigated the relationship between ACoA location and the occurrence of rupture among small intracranial aneurysms, aiming to elucidate whether ACoA location may predispose small aneurysms to rupture and thereby contribute to refining current treatment decision-making.

Materials & methods

Study population

From January 2015 to September 2022, 1,085 patients underwent surgical or endovascular treatment for intracranial aneurysms at our institution. Patients with unruptured aneurysms, re-ruptures at previously treated sites, and aneurysms with fusiform, dissecting, or blood blister morphology were excluded. The final cohort comprised 381 patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Patients were stratified by aneurysm size into two groups: those with aneurysms less than 5 mm and those with aneurysms 5 mm or larger. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate clinical and anatomical differences between these groups.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (approval No. 2508-031−19591). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design.

Clinical trial number

Not applicable.

Data collection

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients, extracting data from electronic medical records using a standardized institutional data collection form. Baseline demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), were obtained. Comprehensive medical and social histories were reviewed, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, alcohol use, and history of cerebrovascular disease. Information on prior medications, specifically antiplatelet and anticoagulant agents, was also documented.

Clinical and aneurysm data, as well as treatment details, were collected. All patients underwent digital subtraction angiography, with assessment of aneurysmal configuration and parent vessel anatomy performed using cerebral angiography and rotational 3D reconstruction. Imaging was acquired using either the AXIOM Artis dBA or AXIOM Artis Q systems (Siemens AG, Forchheim, Germany).

Aneurysm characteristics, including maximum diameter, location, shape, multiplicity, and treatment modality (surgical or endovascular), were evaluated. Maximum diameter was defined as the longest axis measured across the aneurysm dome. In patients with multiple aneurysms, the ruptured lesion was identified by integrating CT findings of hemorrhage distribution and hematoma proximity with angiographic features. The angiographic features included aneurysm size, morphology, presence of vasospasm, and hemodynamic indices such as aspect and size ratios, as well as the presence of irregularity or bleb formation. The final determination was reached by consensus among a multidisciplinary neurovascular team composed of two independent specialists (a neurosurgeon and a neuroradiologist). In surgically treated patients, intraoperative observations, such as wall disruption, fibrin cap formation, local clot adhesion, or active bleeding, served as confirmatory evidence of the rupture site.

Aneurysm locations were categorized as follows: (1) anterior communicating artery, (2) middle cerebral artery, (3) posterior communicating artery, (4) internal carotid artery, (5) anterior cerebral artery, and (6) posterior circulation (including posterior cerebral artery, basilar artery, and vertebral artery). A board-certified neurosurgeon and a neuroradiologist independently assessed aneurysm size and location, blinded to all clinical data.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages; group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Intergroup comparisons for normally distributed continuous variables used the Student’s t-test, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Logistic regression was performed for two groups based on an aneurysm size threshold of 5 mm. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

However, the distribution of rupture sites differed significantly between subgroups. The proportion of ruptured aneurysms at the ACoA was significantly higher in the < 5 mm group compared to the ≥ 5 mm group (38.2% vs. 27.0%; P = 0.03), indicating a disproportionate representation of ACoA aneurysms among small ruptured cases. In contrast, ruptured aneurysms at the posterior communicating artery were significantly less frequent in the < 5 mm group (19.4% vs. 30.0%; P = 0.03). Lobulated aneurysm morphology (11.1% vs. 27.0%; P < 0.001) and the presence of daughter sacs (21.5% vs. 51.1%; P < 0.001) were both markedly less common in aneurysms < 5 mm than in those ≥ 5 mm (Table 2).

Factors associated with ruptured aneurysms less than 5 mm

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of 381 ruptured intracranial aneurysms, ACoA location was identified as independently associated with rupture occurrence among aneurysms smaller than 5 mm. While prior studies have emphasized aneurysm size as the primary determinant of rupture risk [7], our findings underscore the critical role of aneurysm location—particularly at the ACoA—in modifying risk, even in small aneurysms traditionally considered low-risk.

This observation aligns with previous reports. Joo et al. found that the ACoA harbored the smallest mean rupture size among all sites [8], and Forget et al. reported that approximately 94% of ruptured ACoA aneurysms were small, with 44% less than 5 mm [9]. In our cohort, nearly half (46.2%) of ruptured ACoA aneurysms were < 5 mm, whereas the proportion at non-ACoA locations was 34.0%, supporting a disproportionate vulnerability at this site.

The International Study of Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysms (ISUIA) reported a low rupture risk for small aneurysms (< 7 mm) in patients without a prior history of SAH [7]. The Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysm Study (UCAS) in a Japanese cohort subsequently confirmed these results [10]. However, the traditional conservative management for small aneurysms (< 5 mm) has been increasingly questioned, as numerous ruptured aneurysms fall within this size range [11, 12]. Multiple studies have identified a substantial proportion of small aneurysms among ruptured cases, prompting a re-evaluation of size-based risk stratification [13, 14]. Notably, the International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial (ISAT) found that more than half of treated ruptured aneurysms were < 5 mm, underscoring the clinical significance of small aneurysm rupture [15].

Our multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that ACoA location was significantly associated with rupture in aneurysms < 5 mm (adjusted OR, 1.646; 95% CI, 1.030–2.629; P = 0.037). This increased rupture propensity may result from the complex hemodynamic and anatomical characteristics of the ACoA region [16]. In particular, hypoplasia or aplasia of the A1 segment, frequently observed in patients with ACoA aneurysms, leads to asymmetric flow dynamics. These alterations may predispose the aneurysmal wall to elevated biomechanical stress and early rupture before morphological changes are detectable on imaging [17, 18].

In our cohort, morphological features classically associated with rupture risk, such as lobulated dome shape and daughter sac formation, were significantly less frequent in ruptured aneurysms < 5 mm than in those ≥ 5 mm. This suggests that traditional morphological indicators may be less applicable to small aneurysms and that early rupture may occur before such features develop. One hypothesis is that certain aneurysms rupture shortly after formation, before wall remodeling or stabilization occurs [2, 19]. This is further supported by clinical observations indicating that rupture risk is highest within the first year after diagnosis and decreases thereafter [19].

These findings have important clinical implications. Although management of unruptured intracranial aneurysms must consider procedural risks—reported to be up to 10% in the ISUIA study [7]—our data support reconsideration of current risk stratification strategies. Small unruptured aneurysms located at the ACoA may warrant earlier intervention, even when the lesion is < 5 mm. In addition, current risk prediction models such as the PHASES score [20], while already recognizing ACoA aneurysms as part of the high-risk anterior cerebral artery (ACA) group, may not fully capture the elevated rupture propensity observed in small ACoA aneurysms. In our study, 72.3% of ruptured ACoA aneurysms measured ≤ 7 mm, reinforcing the need for more refined, location- and size-specific refinements to existing risk models.

In recent years, advances in endovascular treatments have continued to reshape the management paradigm of small unruptured aneurysms [21, 22]. Traditionally, aneurysms smaller than 5 mm were managed conservatively, as the procedural risks of treatment were considered to outweigh the natural rupture risk of such aneurysms [23, 24]. However, the introduction of flow-diverting stents allows safe and effective treatment of small aneurysms that were previously deemed risky for interventional treatment [21, 22, 25]. These innovations are gradually shifting the traditional risk–benefit balance, favoring treatment in carefully selected cases [21, 22, 25]. Recent evidence also indicates the importance of morphological orientation of ACoA, highlighting the need for morphology-based individualized endovascular management [26]. Therefore, based on the results of the current study, treatment for ACoA aneurysms should be considered even if they are small, with an appropriate treatment modality balancing procedural and patient-specific risks.

This study had several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results. Second, although imaging assessments were independently performed by a neurosurgeon and a neuroradiologist, subtle measurement differences and interobserver variability cannot be fully excluded. Third, the cohort consisted exclusively of patients with ruptured aneurysms. While this design effectively highlights that small aneurysms at the ACoA were disproportionately represented among the rupture cases, it does not directly quantify the rupture risk of unruptured ACoA aneurysms. Therefore, the observed association between ACoA location and rupture may be relatively overrepresented within the ruptured population and should not be interpreted as an absolute risk. Finally, in the absence of histopathological correlation, direct evaluation of aneurysm wall integrity or inflammation was not possible. Despite these limitations, our study’s strength lied in its focused evaluation of a well-defined cohort with consistent imaging protocols and comprehensive clinical data.

In summary, our findings indicate that the ACoA is disproportionately represented among small ruptured intracranial aneurysms (< 5 mm), underscoring the relevance of aneurysm location—particularly at the ACoA—in rupture occurrence. Future prospective, multicenter studies, incorporating both ruptured and unruptured aneurysms as well as advanced hemodynamic modeling and vessel wall imaging, are needed to validate and expand upon these findings. Clinicians should remain vigilant in evaluating small ACoA aneurysms and consider proactive treatment strategies for appropriately selected patients, even when lesion size alone does not indicate high risk.

Conclusion

Our findings, based on an analysis of ruptured aneurysms, indicate that ACoA location is associated with a significantly higher likelihood of rupture among small aneurysms (< 5 mm) than other locations. These findings suggest that ACoA location may represent a potential site-related vulnerability, even in small aneurysms that are traditionally regarded as low-risk aneurysms.
