From association to attribution: intraoperative hypotension and hypoalbuminemia as partial pathways linking comorbidity to postoperative kidney injury
Authors: Ki Tae Jung
Published: 2026

Postoperative acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common complication after major noncardiac surgery and is closely associated with both short-term postoperative complications and long-term adverse outcomes, including chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular events, and mortality [1,2]. Older age and comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease and diabetes are established risk factors for postoperative AKI [1,2]. Additionally, a high preoperative comorbidity burden, often reflected by a high American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA-PS) score, is associated with increased risk [1,2]. Finally, intraoperative hypotension (IOH), with longer durations spent below progressively lower mean arterial pressure (MAP) thresholds, also correspond to a greater risk of AKI [3]. However, a more clinically relevant question is how much of the risk is attributable to intraoperative events and at which point during the perioperative course the intervention may be most effective.

Observational studies have consistently demonstrated an association between IOH and postoperative AKI; however, randomized trials of stricter intraoperative blood pressure management have not convincingly demonstrated a reduction in AKI incidence [3]. A recent meta-analysis comparing lower (MAP ≤ 60 mmHg) and higher (> 60 mmHg) intraoperative MAP targets found no clear increase in major adverse outcomes with a lower MAP strategy [4], suggesting that the relationship between IOH and postoperative organ injury may be more complex than previously assumed. This uncertainty has significant clinical implications. If IOH directly contributes to the pathway linking comorbidity burden and AKI, intraoperative hemodynamic management may meaningfully reduce the risk of postoperative kidney injury. Alternatively, if IOH primarily reflects the underlying comorbidity burden of high-risk patients, a greater opportunity for risk reduction may lie in preoperative optimization. These observations raise an important question regarding the extent to which the association between the preoperative comorbidity burden and postoperative AKI is attributable to potentially modifiable intraoperative conditions, including hypotension.

In this issue of the Korean Journal of Anesthesiology, Jo et al. [5] addressed this question by shifting the focus from association to mediation. In a single-center cohort of 79 788 adults undergoing noncardiac surgery under general anesthesia, the authors applied inverse probability of treatment weighting to balance the measured confounders, followed by structural equation modeling-based mediation analysis to partition the overall effect of a high ASA-PS score on postoperative AKI into direct effects and indirect effects operating through the following three candidate mediators: IOH (cumulative duration of MAP < 60 mmHg), hypoalbuminemia, and anemia. This approach allowed the authors to estimate the extent to which the observed association was transmitted through each potential pathway [6]. As patients with a greater comorbidity burden may be more likely to develop hypotension or low albumin levels during surgery (which may in turn promote kidney injury), part of the excess risk would reach the kidney indirectly by passing through these intermediate conditions, whereas the remainder would reflect a more direct effect of the comorbidities that these conditions cannot explain. Mediation analysis estimates the relative contribution of each pathway to the overall risk. This conceptual reframing distinguishes the current study from most previous studies, which have treated comorbidity burden and IOH as separate risk factors for postoperative AKI. In this study, however, the authors examined how these factors may be linked along the pathway leading to AKI. By estimating how much of the comorbidity-related risk is mediated through intraoperative conditions, the study shifted the focus from identifying high-risk patients to an understanding of where perioperative risk may develop.

In the primary model, approximately half of the excess AKI risk associated with a high ASA-PS score was statistically significantly mediated. IOH and hypoalbuminemia each contributed significant and similar proportions, whereas anemia did not. These findings provide further insight into whether hypotension functions primarily as a marker or mediator of postoperative AKI risk. IOH and hypoalbuminemia appeared to act as partial mediators, suggesting that intraoperative management may influence only a portion of the overall risk. Importantly, the authors interpreted these findings as statistically mediated pathways rather than as proven causal mechanisms. They also provided an anonymized INSPIRE-derived sample dataset together with the analysis code [7], contributing to transparency and reproducibility.

These findings have several clinical implications. Because a substantial proportion of the excess AKI risk appeared to be mediated through intraoperative conditions, management during surgery may influence outcomes in high-risk patients. Notably, hypoalbuminemia contributed a mediated proportion comparable to that of hypotension. This observation may help explain why randomized trials of blood pressure management alone have not consistently reduced AKI, as hypotension accounted for only part of the mediated risk, and suggests that perioperative strategies may need to extend beyond blood pressure management to include attention to albumin. Simultaneously, a considerable proportion of the excess risk was not mediated by the measured intraoperative factors, emphasizing the continued importance of preoperative optimization. Unlike previous studies that have examined comorbidity burden and hypotension as separate risk factors, this analysis quantified the extent to which the comorbidity-related risk operated through each intraoperative pathway, offering a more integrated view of where that risk arises. Therefore, these findings suggest that reducing postoperative AKI risk in high-risk patients will likely require both preoperative and intraoperative approaches.

However, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Mediation analysis identifies statistically mediated pathways rather than proven causal mechanisms. Moreover, the estimated proportions are not exact values. In this study, the mediated fraction changed substantially when the ASA-PS score was analyzed as a continuous rather than a binary exposure, and the results may also vary according to the blood pressure threshold. Therefore, the estimates are best viewed as approximate and context-dependent, rather than precise effect sizes.

In conclusion, Jo et al. [5] have extended the discussion beyond the well-established association between a high ASA-PS score and postoperative AKI by examining the extent to which this risk may be mediated by intraoperative conditions. Although a mediation analysis cannot prove causality, it provides a useful framework for identifying potentially modifiable pathways and generating clinically relevant hypotheses. Further prospective studies with more detailed physiological data are required to determine whether these pathways can be effectively targeted in perioperative care.
