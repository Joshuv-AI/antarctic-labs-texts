“The Renal Trail Reveals Masked Face” – A Unique Case Series
Authors: S. Nivetha, C. Tinky Bose, M. M. Mini, V. A Chinnu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic renal disease (CRD) is a progressive, irreversible loss of renal mass and function, posing a significant health concern. Secondary hyperparathyroidism caused by hypokalcemia in CRD often leads to renal osteodystrophy (ROD), a bone pathology resulting from alterations in mineral metabolism. ROD typically occurs early in renal failure and progresses as kidney function deteriorates. Marked jaw enlargement remains a rare but severe complication of the ROD, causing significant facial disfigurement. This case series highlights the four cases of ROD with extensive orofacial involvement, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Chronic renal disease (CRD) is a progressive and irreversible deterioration of renal function that ultimately leads to end-stage renal disease, a condition associated with substantial systemic and skeletal complications. Long-standing renal dysfunction disrupts calcium–phosphate homeostasis and impairs Vitamin D metabolism, resulting in secondary hyperparathyroidism and a spectrum of bone alterations collectively termed renal osteodystrophy (ROD).[1] ROD, a component of CRD-mineral and bone disorder, stands out as a significant complication in chronic kidney disease (CKD).[2]

The uniqueness of the present article lies in documenting multiple pretransplant patients with CRD presenting with extensive orofacial involvement due to ROD – a presentation rarely reported in the literature. Unlike most published cases that describe isolated or posttransplant manifestations, this series provides a comprehensive clinico-radiologic and biochemical correlation of jaw involvement in the early phase of systemic disease. It further emphasizes the pivotal role of oral medicine specialists in recognizing oral and maxillofacial signs as early indicators of systemic bone pathology, thereby facilitating timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary management.

Case Reports

Case 1

A 21-year-old male presented with progressive facial swelling over 8 months, associated with dyspnea and dysphagia. He had a 3-year history of CRD and was on hemodialysis with secondary hyperparathyroidism under the medical management. Clinical examination revealed facial asymmetry, a depressed nasal bridge, widened nares, and diffuse maxillomandibular enlargement. On palpation, the swelling was bony hard and nontender [Figure 1a]. Intraoral examination showed significant bony expansion of the maxilla and mandible, obliterating the buccal and lingual sulci with generalized spacing of teeth [Figure 1b]. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) of the skull revealed an altered trabecular pattern with a “salt and pepper” appearance. Bicortical expansion with a ground-glass pattern and multiple osteolytic lesions were noted in both the maxilla and mandible, along with generalized loss of the lamina dura [Figure 2a]. On diagnostic assessment, elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels with low serum calcium were observed [Figure 3]. The main diagnostic challenge was differentiating ROD from fibrous dysplasia. Therapeutic Intervention: Oral calcitriol 0.25 μg/day and phosphate binders (sevelamer carbonate 800 mg three times daily). Follow-up and outcome: After 6 months, serum calcium levels improved, and the facial swelling remained stable. No adverse events were reported. Patient Perspective: The patient reported embarrassment due to facial disfigurement but expressed relief following stabilization of the condition [Table 1].

Case 2

A 43-year-old female patient presented with a swelling in the upper gum region for the past 2 years. She had a history of CRD and had been on hemodialysis for 12 years. She also had secondary hyperparathyroidism and was under medical management. Pallor was noted on general examination. Extraoral examination revealed a mild, diffuse, and bony-hard swelling in the middle third of the face with obliteration of the nasolabial fold [Figure 1c]. Intraoral examination showed mild bony expansion of both the maxilla and mandible [Figure 1d]. CBCT imaging demonstrated a ground-glass appearance with bicortical expansion of both jaws. Multiple osteolytic lesions with generalized loss of the lamina dura were observed [Figure 2b]. On the Diagnostic Assessment, PTH and ALP were elevated. No parathyroid adenoma detected [Figure 3]. Therapeutic intervention: Vitamin D analogs and phosphate restriction were prescribed. Follow-up and outcome: after 8 months, bone changes stabilized and biochemical parameters showed improvement. Patient perspective: The patient reported a better understanding of the chronic nature of his illness [Table 2].

Case 3

A 47-year-old female patient presented with facial swelling for the past 2 years, along with a history of dyspnea and dysphagia. She had a 9-year history of CRD and was undergoing hemodialysis. She also had secondary hyperparathyroidism associated with a parathyroid adenoma. Extraoral examination revealed facial asymmetry with diffuse enlargement of the maxilla and mandible, widened nares, and a depressed nasal bridge [Figure 1e]. Intraoral examination showed significant bony expansion of both the maxilla and mandible [Figure 1f]. MRI of the face revealed low signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images in the facial bones and cranium, with expansion suggestive of a late sclerotic stage [Figure 2c]. Diagnostic assessment: Laboratory investigations showed markedly elevated PTH levels and hyperphosphatemia [Figure 3]. The prognosis was considered guarded due to adenoma-induced hyperparathyroidism. Therapeutic intervention: The patient underwent subtotal parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation, followed by postoperative administration of oral calcitriol 0.5 μg/day. Follow-up and outcome: At a 6-month follow-up, marked biochemical improvement was noted, along with partial regression of bone changes. Patient perspective: The patient reported significant relief in breathing difficulty and improved overall comfort following surgery [Table 3].

Case 4

A 35-year-old male patient presented with facial swelling for the past 1 year, associated with dyspnea and dysphagia. He had a history of CRD and had been on hemodialysis for 4 years. He also had secondary hyperparathyroidism and was under medical management. The patient exhibited short stature, abnormal gait, and pallor. Extraoral examination revealed facial asymmetry with widening of the nostrils, a depressed nasal bridge, and enlargement of both the maxilla and mandible with obliteration of the nasolabial fold [Figure 1g]. Intraoral examination showed significant bony expansion of the maxilla and mandible with obliteration of the buccal and lingual sulci [Figure 1h]. Panoramic imaging demonstrated a ground-glass appearance involving both jaws with generalized loss of the lamina dura [Figure 2d]. Diagnostic assessment: Laboratory investigations revealed elevated PTH and ALP levels, along with low serum calcium [Figure 3]. Therapeutic intervention: The patient was treated with phosphate binders and Vitamin D supplementation. Follow-up and outcome: The facial swelling remained stable during follow-up, with no further progression. Patient perspective: The patient expressed the awareness of the long-term nature of his condition and continued the treatment correctly [Table 4].

Discussion

CRD is associated with multiple disturbances in mineral metabolism, including hyperphosphatemia, hypokalcemia, and reduced production of active Vitamin D (calcitriol). These imbalances stimulate PTH secretion, resulting in secondary hyperparathyroidism and abnormal bone turnover known as ROD.[1] The four main types include Osteitis fibrosa cystica, adynamic bone disease, osteomalacia, and mixed uremic osteodystrophy.[3] Brown tumor are often associated with primary hyperparathyroidism and secondary hyperparathyroidism.[4] The characteristic maxillofacial findings – diffuse bony expansion of the maxilla and mandible, widening of the nasal bridge, and increased interdental spacing – reflect hyperostotic remodeling and marrow fibrosis secondary to chronic PTH stimulation. Upper airway obstruction, speech impairment, dysphonia, dysphagia, and cranial nerve compression due to foraminal stenosis in the skull base may also occur.[5] It lead to severe esthetic and functional disabilities, including upper respiratory tract compression.[6] Radiographically, the patients exhibited cortical thinning of multiple bones, coarsened trabecular patterns, osteolytic lesions, typical ground-glass and “salt and pepper” appearances, consistent with generalized bone demineralization and trabecular coarsening.[7] In our case, most of the radiographic features typically seen in this condition were evident. These imaging features, when correlated with biochemical alterations, are highly suggestive of secondary hyperparathyroidism in CRD patients. As a hybrid imaging technique, 18F-NaF positron emission tomography/computed tomography shows great potential in accurately evaluating bone turnover and vascular calcification. PTH is still the main biomarker for assessing bone turnover.[8] The elevated levels of serum PTH and ALP, along with reduced serum calcium and phosphate retention, were consistent across all cases, confirming metabolic etiology rather than a neoplastic process.[7] The differential diagnosis of ROD with other expansive bone pathologies, such as brown tumor, cystic fibrous osteitis, cherubism, central giant cell lesion, and ossifying fibroma, should always be considered.[9] Therapeutically, management is aimed at correcting the underlying metabolic imbalance rather than surgical excision of the jaw lesions. High phosphate, low calcium, abnormal PTH, and Vitamin D indicate the need for diet and lifestyle changes and the administration of therapeutic agents, such as phosphate binders, calcimimetic drugs, calcitriol/calcitriol analogs, or surgical intervention, that is, parathyroidectomy.[10] Despite the available treatment options, there is no standardized protocol for managing this condition.[9] In our series, all patients responded favorably to medical therapy, and one patient underwent surgical parathyroidectomy with marked improvement. Early identification and treatment of ROD are crucial, as delayed management may lead to irreversible facial deformity, pathological fractures, or poor transplant outcomes. Next-generation sequencing tools have allowed researchers to identify the key genes and pathways involved in bone metabolism and mineralization in CKD patients with ROD.[11]

Strengths and limitations

The present case series underscores the rare occurrence of ROD presenting with significant orofacial manifestations in pretransplant patients, a finding that is seldom documented in the existing literature. Each case was comprehensively evaluated using radiologic modalities such as CBCT and MRI, along with biochemical investigations, thereby strengthening diagnostic accuracy and ensuring reliable clinico-radiologic correlation. This report also highlights the pivotal role of dental professionals in identifying systemic bone disease through oral and facial changes, facilitating early referral and multidisciplinary intervention. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Histopathological confirmation was not performed, as the diagnosis was established based on the consistent biochemical and radiologic findings. In addition, the small sample size restricts the generalization of the results. Furthermore, therapeutic response was evaluated primarily through clinical and radiographic improvement, without quantitative assessment of bone turnover markers. Despite these limitations, the case series contributes valuable insight into the maxillofacial spectrum of ROD and reinforces the importance of interdisciplinary management in such systemic disorders.

Conclusion

Early recognition and management of ROD is critical to prevent skeletal complications and improve the quality of life for patients with CRD. Systemic patient evaluation should be done before diagnosing the oral lesions.
