Redefining Uric Acid and Cardiovascular Risk
Authors: Sunao Kojima, Kensuke Nishimiya, Ichiro Hisatome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urate-lowering trials have not consistently demonstrated benefits in preventing coronary events, revealing an interventional gap and challenging the causal role of uric acid in cardiovascular disease. Advances in dual-energy computed tomography have revealed monosodium urate crystal deposition within vessel walls and atherosclerotic plaques, leading to the concept of vascular gout. Persistent hyperuricemia, particularly in Asian populations characterized by urate underexcretion, may facilitate crystal deposition and innate immune pathway activation. These inflammatory processes may promote vascular inflammation, plaque instability, and cardiovascular events. Xanthine oxidase inhibition effectively lowers serum uric acid levels and reduces monosodium urate crystal burden in articular tissues. However, whether urate-lowering therapy reduces monosodium urate crystal deposition and crystal-driven inflammation within the vascular wall remains unknown. Therefore, we aimed to reframe uric acid from a metabolic marker to a mediator of urate crystal-driven vascular inflammation and discuss future therapeutic strategies targeting crystal burden and inflammation in Asian populations.

Introduction: An Old But Evolving Theme: Uric Acid and Cardiovascular Disease

Since the Framingham Study, whether serum uric acid levels determine cardiovascular disease risk remains controversial. Numerous observational studies have demonstrated associations between hyperuricemia and hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke, and all-cause mortality,1, 2, 3, 4 giving rise to the long-standing clinical perception that uric acid is an indicator of cardiovascular risk. However, hyperuricemia frequently coexists with obesity, insulin resistance, renal dysfunction, metabolic syndrome, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, and hypertension,1,5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 raising a fundamental question that remains unresolved: is uric acid an independent causal factor in the development of cardiovascular disease, or just an epiphenomenal marker reflecting underlying metabolic, renal, and vascular pathology?11,12

Growing experimental and clinical evidence indicate that urate can induce endothelial dysfunction through oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling. Therefore, the hypothesis emerged that active urate lowering might prevent cardiovascular events. This concept prompted a series of large-scale randomized controlled trials conducted in Western countries and Japan, primarily evaluating xanthine oxidase (XO) inhibitors, such as allopurinol and febuxostat, as urate-lowering therapies.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20

However, the results of these interventional trials did not consistently meet expectations in terms of preventing coronary events. In the ALL-HEART (Allopurinol vs Usual Care in United Kingdom Patients With Ischemic Heart Disease) trial, allopurinol therapy failed to significantly reduce major adverse cardiovascular events, including cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, and stroke, among patients with ischemic heart disease.19 Similarly, in FREED (Febuxostat for Cerebral and Cardiorenovascular Events Prevention Study) targeting patients with asymptomatic hyperuricemia, febuxostat significantly reduced a composite endpoint encompassing cerebrovascular, cardiovascular, and renal events; however, the primary benefit in FREED appeared to be largely attributable to renal outcomes rather than a consistent reduction in coronary events.17 Collectively, these findings highlight a critical “interventional gap”: urate-lowering therapy appears beneficial for renal outcomes; however, meaningful reductions in coronary-centered cardiovascular events have not been achieved consistently. This discrepancy has prompted renewed scrutiny of the clinical significance of uric acid and its role in cardiovascular pathophysiology.

Recently, vascular gout has been proposed as a novel pathophysiological concept likely underlying this interventional gap by focusing on urate deposition within the vascular wall.21, 22, 23 This concept is based on the hypothesis that monosodium urate (MSU) crystals deposited in the vascular wall may trigger chronic inflammation by activating innate immune pathways, thereby promoting atherosclerotic plaque instability. Advances in dual-energy computed tomography (DECT) have enabled the in vivo visualization of MSU deposition within the vascular wall,22,24,25 and accumulating evidence indicates that therapeutic strategies targeting innate immune-mediated inflammation can reduce coronary events.26, 27, 28 In particular, low-dose colchicine significantly reduced major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with recent myocardial infarction (COLCOT [COLchicine Cardiovascular Outcomes Trial]) and in those with chronic coronary artery disease (LoDoCo2 [Low-Dose Colchicine for Secondary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]), further supporting the concept that inflammation is a key therapeutic target in atherosclerosis.27,28 Consequently, renewed recognition of the urate underexcretion phenotype, which is particularly prevalent among Asian populations,29 has further heightened the clinical relevance of this concept. Emerging reports have suggested the potential use of uricosuric agents with mechanisms distinct from XO inhibition.30, 31, 32 Notably, selective urate reabsorption inhibitors (SURIs) have been shown to lower serum urate levels and potentially attenuate key pathological processes, including apoptosis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis.33, 34, 35 Collectively, these observations suggest alternative therapeutic axes for cardiovascular event prevention that extend beyond conventional XO inhibition, highlighting the need to redefine urate management strategies, including those for asymptomatic hyperuricemia (Central Illustration).Central IllustrationConceptual Shift in UA-Related Cardiovascular RiskThis Central Illustration summarizes a conceptual shift in understanding UA-related cardiovascular risk. Hyperuricemia and Cardiovascular Events: Epidemiological Evidence From Asian and Western Populations

The epidemiological characteristics of hyperuricemia and its association with cardiovascular events vary across regions or ethnic populations. Notably, Asian and Western populations differ in dietary patterns, lifestyle behaviors, genetic backgrounds, distribution of comorbid conditions, health care systems, and clinical practice patterns. Consequently, the prevalence of hyperuricemia, its clinical significance, and its contribution to cardiovascular outcomes vary across these regions. Epidemiological investigations that explicitly account for such regional heterogeneity are essential to accurately delineate the relationship between uric acid and cardiovascular events and to inform the development of appropriate, population-specific therapeutic strategies.

Hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk in Asian populations

The prevalence of hyperuricemia has increased steadily across Asian countries. Reportedly, approximately 20% to 25% of adult men in Japan have hyperuricemia (Table 1). Patients with hyperuricemia frequently exhibit a high burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities, including hypertension, obesity, impaired glucose tolerance, and dyslipidemia. These findings have proven hyperuricemia to be closely associated with a wide spectrum of cardiovascular end-organ damage, such as atherosclerosis, stroke, ischemic heart disease, and heart failure.29,36, 37, 38, 39 Similar trends have been observed in other Asian regions, including China, Korea, and Taiwan, where the prevalence of hyperuricemia and gout has increased significantly in recent decades (Table 2).40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45 These epidemiological shifts have been attributed to the westernization of dietary habits and lifestyle behaviors, and genetic susceptibility. In particular, Asian populations exhibit a higher frequency of polymorphisms in urate transporter–related genes, including ABCG2, which predispose individuals to a urate underexcretion phenotype. According to large-scale cohort studies conducted in Japan and Korea, hyperuricemia is strongly associated with major cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome, obesity, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). In addition, it contributes to an increased risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.3,49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57 Furthermore, population-based cohort studies in Japan have demonstrated that higher serum uric acid levels are associated with an increased risk of incident hypertension, suggesting that hyperuricemia may function as a concomitant condition and a contributing factor in the development of hypertension.58 Furthermore, large database studies across multiple Asian countries have shown that even asymptomatic hyperuricemia is associated with a significantly elevated risk of future cardiovascular events and incident CKD.59, 60, 61 A large cohort study of Japanese patients with acute myocardial infarction identified serum uric acid as an independent prognostic determinant of cardiovascular mortality, with a stepwise increase in risk observed across higher uric acid categories.3 The prognostic value of uric acid was preserved after adjusting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors and renal function, supporting the notion that uric acid may be more than just an epiphenomenal marker and may be directly involved in disease pathogenesis.

Similarly, large Japanese registry studies on heart failure have demonstrated that hyperuricemia is an independent predictor of heart failure hospitalization and mortality.62, 63, 64 Elevated serum uric acid levels also have been associated with adverse outcomes in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.64 Asian populations are characterized by advanced aging, a high prevalence of obesity, and a substantial burden of hypertension; hence, the clinical relevance of uric acid may be particularly pronounced in these populations.

Hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk in Western populations

Large-scale epidemiological studies, including the Framingham Heart Study and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, have investigated the association between serum uric acid levels and cardiovascular risk in Western countries.65,66 Cohort studies in the United States have demonstrated a stepwise increase in cardiovascular mortality risk with higher serum uric acid levels, and this association reportedly persists after adjusting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors, such as age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia.66 Similar findings have been observed in analyses of hypertensive populations, indicating that elevated uric acid may serve as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular events.67

Meta-analyses predominantly based on Western studies have shown that, in the general population, hyperuricemia is significantly associated with increased risks of cardiovascular and all-cause mortalities; however, these associations are modest.68,69 In contrast, stronger associations between uric acid and cardiovascular events have been reported in populations with CKD or diabetes mellitus, suggesting that underlying comorbidities may modify the clinical significance of uric acid.70,71

Conversely, several studies have reported that the independent association between uric acid and cardiovascular events is attenuated or abolished after rigorous adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors and renal function indices.11,12 In heart failure, elevated serum uric acid has traditionally been regarded as a powerful surrogate marker reflecting increased XO activity, heightened oxidative stress, and reduced exercise capacity,72,73 rather than a direct causal factor.

More recently, numerous Mendelian randomization studies leveraging genetic variants as instruments for lifelong uric acid exposure have attempted to clarify the causal relationship between uric acid and cardiovascular disease. However, their results have been inconsistent. Asymptomatic hyperuricemia: uric acid trajectories and cardiovascular risk

Recent analyses focusing on the temporal patterns of serum uric acid levels—referred to as uric acid trajectories—have gained attention as a novel framework for cardiovascular risk stratification in patients with asymptomatic hyperuricemia. Large-scale database studies from Korea and Taiwan have demonstrated that even in the absence of a history of gout flares, individuals with asymptomatic hyperuricemia exhibit significantly higher risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality than their normouricemic counterparts.57,79 Moreover, studies evaluating long-term uric acid trajectories, rather than single time-point measurements, have reported that individuals with persistently elevated or increasing uric acid trajectories face a significantly increased risk of future stroke and coronary artery disease.80, 81, 82

These findings underscore the need for sustained or progressive uric acid exposure in shaping cardiovascular risk. Collectively, emerging evidence suggests that hyperuricemia should be viewed as a state of prolonged or cumulatively increasing urate burden that is pathophysiologically relevant to cardiovascular risk rather than a transient biochemical abnormality. In this context, hyperuricemia represents not merely a snapshot serum marker but a cumulative exposure of vascular and renal tissues to uric acid over time, with its temporal integration potentially serving as a key determinant of cardiovascular event risk.

Pathophysiology: Is Uric Acid a Marker or a Mediator?

Multiple mechanisms have been proposed to explain the involvement of hyperuricemia in cardiovascular pathophysiology, including increased oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, renal tubular injury, and inflammatory and immune response activation. Circulating uric acid exerts antioxidant effects in the extracellular compartment and likely accounts for a substantial proportion of plasma antioxidant capacity, comparable to that of vitamin C. In contrast, once transported into cells, urate promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species by activating nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase and impairing mitochondrial function.83,84 Consequently, the expression and activity of endothelial nitric oxide synthase are suppressed in endothelial cells, leading to impaired endothelium-dependent vasodilation—a phenomenon demonstrated in experimental animal models and human studies.85,86

As evidenced by experimental hyperuricemia models and pathological studies of gouty nephropathy, hyperuricemia has been shown to induce tubular epithelial injury in the kidney through intraluminal deposition of urate crystals, promoting inflammatory cell infiltration and interstitial fibrosis.87, 88, 89 Such urate-associated renal injury may progress from potentially reversible changes to irreversible CKD. Conversely, several meta-analyses have suggested that urate-lowering therapy with XO inhibitors may mitigate the decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate and reduce renal events.90, 91, 92 These findings imply that lowering serum uric acid may provide clinically meaningful benefits, particularly in patients with hyperuricemia complicated by early to moderate CKD.29

Beyond soluble urate, MSU crystals are recognized as potent activators of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome93,94 and as modulators of macrophage polarization and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation.95, 96, 97 Experimental studies have shown that MSU-induced NETs and M1-polarized macrophages mutually amplify NLRP3-dependent interleukin (IL)-1β production during the acute phase of gouty inflammation,93,96, 97, 98 highlighting the central role of urate crystals in propagating innate immune–mediated inflammatory responses.

These pathophysiological insights suggest that hyperuricemia should not be regarded merely as an epiphenomenal marker that coexists with other cardiovascular risk factors. Rather, urate may function as a mediator that actively promotes pathological processes affecting the vasculature, kidneys, and heart, particularly in the presence of urate crystallization. Nevertheless, the magnitude of its contribution may vary substantially according to patient characteristics, comorbid conditions, disease stage, and the pattern of urate burden (sustained vs cumulative exposure). Consequently, urate-lowering therapy may not confer uniform prognostic benefit across all patient populations. Such heterogeneity in the pathophysiological role of uric acid may represent a key factor underlying the inconsistent cardiovascular outcomes observed in previous interventional trials such as ALL-HEART and FREED.17,19

Vascular Gout: A Pathophysiological Concept of Crystal Deposition and Inflammation in the Vascular Wall

The inconsistent findings from epidemiological studies and interventional trials examining the association between serum uric acid levels and cardiovascular events have highlighted the need to redefine uric acid not merely as a metabolic biomarker but through a pathophysiological framework centered on tissue deposition and inflammation. Within this context, increasing attention has been directed toward vascular gout, which posits that MSU crystals are deposited within the vascular wall and induce chronic inflammatory responses.

In this review, we propose “vascular gout” as a pathophysiological construct rather than a distinct clinical entity. Vascular gout refers to a condition in which MSU crystals are deposited within the vascular wall and/or atherosclerotic plaques, where they may trigger innate immune activation and sustained inflammation, particularly through the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1β signaling pathways.93,99,100

Importantly, vascular gout should not be interpreted as a separate disease category, but rather as a mechanistic phenotype or spectrum within atherosclerosis, particularly in patients with hyperuricemia or gout. This concept integrates crystal deposition biology with inflammation-driven atherogenesis.26,100

At present, no validated diagnostic criteria for vascular gout exist. However, a conceptual diagnostic framework may include: 1) biochemical assessment of hyperuricemia; 2) imaging evidence suggestive of MSU deposition (eg, dual-energy computed tomography); and 3) markers of inflammatory activation. Emerging imaging modalities and histopathological studies may further refine this framework in the future.

Importantly, the vascular gout hypothesis remains largely unproven in terms of prospective interventional evidence. Although indirect support is provided by studies of urate-lowering therapies and anti-inflammatory interventions, no randomized clinical trials have specifically tested whether targeting vascular MSU deposition or crystal-driven inflammation reduces cardiovascular events. Therefore, this concept should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires validation in future studies.

Studies in patients with gout have shown that MSU deposition detected by coronary computed tomography colocalizes with atherosclerotic plaques, particularly at sites with morphological features characteristic of vulnerable plaques, such as enlarged lipid cores and fibrous cap thinning.24,25,106,107 Detailed analyses of coronary or peripheral arterial atheroma specimens using polarized light microscopy and high-resolution optical coherence tomography have identified structures suggestive of MSU crystals within plaques. These findings frequently coexist with macrophage infiltration and heightened inflammasome activity, indicating an inflammatory plaque microenvironment.108, 109, 110 These observations increase the probability that inflammatory processes analogous to those seen in articular gout may persist chronically within the vasculature. Consistent with this notion, clinical studies employing DECT to evaluate MSU deposition within coronary atheromas have reported significantly higher rates of major adverse cardiovascular events among patients with detectable MSU deposition, suggesting that intravascular MSU accumulation is not just an incidental imaging finding but may represent a pathophysiologically and prognostically relevant substrate.23

Silent MSU crystal deposition also has been detected by DECT in joints and tendons of patients with asymptomatic hyperuricemia and no history of gout flares.111,112 Moreover, DECT signals consistent with MSU deposition have been observed within coronary atherosclerotic plaques in patients with gout and those with hyperuricemia alone, often colocalizing with sites of atherosclerosis.23,25,113 These findings challenge the conventional assumption that the absence of clinical symptoms indicates the absence of crystal deposition and suggest that asymptomatic hyperuricemia may represent a subclinical stage of vascular gout.

Vascular gout viewed through the natural history of gout: the central role of the NLRP3 inflammasome and priming

Gouty arthritis is classically understood as a stepwise process in which prolonged asymptomatic hyperuricemia causes the total body urate pool to expand, subsequent MSU crystal deposition in joints, the development of acute gout flares, and eventual progression to tophaceous gout and gouty nephropathy through recurrent cycles of remission and relapse.114

When this natural history is extrapolated to the vascular system, a stage exists in which MSU crystals form deposits in joints and within the vascular wall, corresponding to vascular gout. Similar to articular gout, MSU crystals deposited in the vascular wall may activate innate immune responses through the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to IL-1β production and sustained low-grade inflammation within the vessel wall, promoting the progression of atherosclerosis.93

MSU crystals induce inflammation through a 2-step requirement for IL-1β production. The priming signal (signal 1) is commonly mediated, at least in part, by Toll-like receptor–dependent activation of nuclear factor-κB, leading to upregulation of pro-IL-1β and NLRP3 expression; this process also may be facilitated by hyperuricemia, as soluble urate promotes intracellular oxidative stress and nuclear factor -κB activation, thereby enhancing the priming state.115,116 In the vascular system, such priming may be further promoted by aging, obesity, CKD, and preexisting atherosclerotic lesions.99,117, 118, 119 In contrast, the second signal (signal 2) is triggered by MSU crystal uptake and promotes NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and caspase-1 activation, leading to the maturation and release of IL-1β.93,120 Importantly, MSU crystal internalization is considered a multifactorial process involving multiple receptors and opsonization mechanisms, rather than a single defined pathway, and the precise mechanisms of crystal recognition and uptake remain incompletely understood.116,121 Collectively, these findings support a pathophysiological model in which asymptomatic hyperuricemia may function as a priming signal, while MSU crystals act as a secondary trigger, synergistically enhancing IL-1β production and thereby contributing to vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis (Figure 3).122, 123, 124, 125, 126, 127Figure 3Urate Priming and MSU-Triggered IL-1β Production in AtherosclerosisHyperuricemia acts as an inflammatory priming signal and amplifies IL-1β–dependent inflammation through MSU crystal–mediated activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting atherosclerosis progression and the development of cardiovascular events. Consistent with this model, recent epidemiological studies have reported poorer outcomes in patients with gout whose hyperuricemia is inadequately controlled compared with those achieving optimal urate management, highlighting the importance of asymptomatic hyperuricemia as a priming factor in vascular gout.78,128 Moreover, in older patients with asymptomatic hyperuricemia and a history of cardiovascular disease, treatment with febuxostat has been associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality.129 These findings suggest that when hyperuricemia coexists with priming conditions such as advanced age and established atherosclerosis, urate-lowering therapy may translate into meaningful improvements in clinical outcomes.

Interaction between cholesterol crystals and MSU crystals in atherosclerosis

In addition to MSU crystals, cholesterol crystals are well-established drivers of inflammation in atherosclerosis. Cholesterol crystals are present in atherosclerotic plaques from early stages and are known to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to IL-1β production and downstream inflammatory cascades.99,100 Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that MSU crystals may coexist with cholesterol crystals within the same plaque environment. A recent study using cross-polarized micro-optical coherence tomography demonstrated that both MSU and cholesterol crystals are detectable in human coronary plaques and are increased in patients with gout, with a significant correlation between their respective burdens.110 Given their shared ability to activate innate immune pathways such as the NLRP3 inflammasome,93 MSU crystals may act as an additional or synergistic stimulus, potentially amplifying crystal-driven inflammation within plaques.

These observations suggest that vascular inflammation may be driven by a spectrum of crystalline stimuli, rather than a single dominant crystal species. In this context, the concept of “vascular gout” should be considered as an extension of crystal-induced inflammation in atherosclerosis, rather than a replacement for the established role of cholesterol crystals.

Redefining the therapeutic targets of urate-lowering therapy from the perspective of vascular gout

The fundamental objective of urate-lowering therapy in vascular gout should be redefined beyond merely achieving serum uric acid levels within a predefined target range. Rather, the primary therapeutic goal should be to reduce systemic MSU crystal deposition, including within the vasculature and kidneys, and to control the chronic inflammation driven by such crystal burden. XO inhibitors lower serum uric acid levels by suppressing urate production; however, whether these agents can effectively dissolve preexisting MSU crystals within the vascular wall over a short time remains uncertain. Even when serum uric acid levels are reduced to target ranges, dissolution equilibrium may not be readily established within the vascular microenvironment, and MSU crystals internalized within macrophages or vascular smooth muscle cells may persist.

If such “residual crystal burden” continues to act as a chronic inflammatory stimulus within the vessel wall, this mechanism could plausibly explain the limited cardiovascular event reduction observed in previous interventional trials, despite effective lowering of serum uric acid levels. From this perspective, the vascular gout paradigm provides an important theoretical framework for re-examining therapeutic targets and strategies in urate management (Central Illustration).

Structural Determinants of the Association Between Uric Acid and Cardiovascular and Renal Outcomes: Why Are the Findings Inconsistent?

Numerous observational studies have investigated the association between serum uric acid levels and cardiovascular and renal outcomes; however, the findings have not been consistent. Notably, some studies have demonstrated an approximately linear relationship between serum uric acid and clinical outcomes; however, others have reported nonlinear associations, including J-shaped or U-shaped relationships, which may appear contradictory at first glance (Figure 4). These discrepancies do not necessarily reflect a lack of reproducibility. Clinical implications: from a single threshold to organ-specific optimal uric acid targets

The relationship between serum uric acid levels and clinical outcomes is dynamic; hence, deriving a uniform optimal uric acid target solely from observational data is challenging. Nevertheless, numerous epidemiological studies have consistently supported the principle that cardiovascular and renal risks increase with a greater urate burden. Accordingly, in settings in which hyperuricemia persists, the need for interventions to lower serum uric acid levels cannot be dismissed.

In contrast, the indiscriminate application of a “the lower, the better” strategy to all patients is not necessarily rational. Excessive urate lowering may fail to translate into prognostic benefit and may reflect or exacerbate adverse conditions, such as malnutrition and frailty, especially among high-risk populations with established cardiovascular disease or CKD. Thus, in urate management, the quantitative question of how far to lower serum uric acid should be reconsidered based on individual patient characteristics and organ-specific vulnerability.

Current clinical guidelines recognize hyperuricemia as the etiological basis of urate deposition disorders, including gouty arthritis and urate-related kidney disease. They also indicate that MSU crystallization becomes more likely when serum uric acid levels exceed approximately 7.0 mg/dL.29,143,144 Conversely, complications such as urolithiasis and exercise-induced acute kidney injury have been described in conditions such as renal hypouricemia, leading to recommendations that serum uric acid levels should not be excessively reduced and that levels below approximately 2.0 mg/dL should generally be avoided.145 These considerations underscore the need to recognize upper and lower bounds in urate management and highlight the importance of defining optimal uric acid targets through organ- and disease-specific interventional trials.

The FREED trial, a prospective randomized controlled study conducted in high-risk older patients treated with febuxostat, demonstrated a significant reduction in renal events and provides an illustrative example.17 In a subsequent subanalysis, the relationship between achieved serum uric acid levels and renal outcomes followed a J-shaped pattern, with the lowest incidence of new-onset or worsening proteinuria observed among patients in whom uric acid levels were reduced to approximately 5 to 6 mg/dL.146 Notably, most prior uric acid research has relied on observational associations between baseline uric acid levels and outcomes; however, this subanalysis of the FREED trial offers important insight by directly examining the relationship between achieved uric acid levels following intervention and organ-specific outcomes.

Uricosuric Agents in Vascular Gout: Crystal Burden Reduction as the Primary Therapeutic Goal

XO inhibitors effectively lower serum uric acid levels and are widely used as first-line therapy for gout. Urate-lowering therapy, including XO inhibitors, is well established to dissolve MSU crystals in articular tissues and to reduce gout flares.147 However, whether similar crystal dissolution occurs within the vascular wall remains unknown. Unlike synovial tissues, vascular structures differ substantially in microenvironment, blood flow dynamics, and local immune responses, which may influence MSU crystal formation and clearance. Therefore, the potential effect of urate-lowering therapy on vascular MSU deposition and crystal-driven vascular inflammation remains an important unresolved question.

To date, XO inhibitors have played a central role in urate-lowering therapy. However, in Asian populations, including Japanese patients, urate underexcretion is the predominant pathophysiological phenotype of hyperuricemia. In this context, uricosuric agents that inhibit urate reabsorption in the proximal tubule, particularly SURIs, are clinically relevant.148, 149, 150, 151, 152, 153 In addition, impaired extrarenal urate excretion, exemplified by reduced ABCG2 function, is highly prevalent among Asian individuals. In this population, the coexistence of renal and extrarenal underexcretion is a key pathophysiological substrate of hyperuricemia.151,152

Classical uricosuric agents, such as benzbromarone and probenecid, have long been used in clinical practice.148,154 However, concerns regarding severe hepatotoxicity led to the withdrawal or strict restriction of benzbromarone use in many Western countries.155,156 In contrast, its clinical utility has been preserved in Japan by incorporating regular liver function monitoring in real-world practice.157 Based on this, newer uricosuric agents with enhanced selectivity and safety profiles, namely SURIs, have been developed and are increasingly being adopted in clinical practice.

The clinical significance of SURIs extends beyond their ability to lower serum uric acid levels. In hyperuricemia predominantly driven by urate underexcretion, SURIs can achieve efficient urate lowering at relatively low doses. For example, dotinurad has demonstrated dose-dependent urate-lowering efficacy starting from low doses in Japanese patients with hyperuricemia, with noninferiority to benzbromarone confirmed in comparative trials.153 In patients with multiple comorbidities and polypharmacy, where drug–drug interactions and tolerability often limit therapeutic options, the ability to reach treatment targets with minimal effective dosing represents a meaningful clinical advantage.

Moreover, by enhancing urate excretion and reducing the total body urate pool, uricosuric therapy lowers serum uric acid levels, which is the primary determinant of MSU crystal formation and persistence. In articular gout, sustained urate-lowering therapy leads to time-dependent disappearance of MSU crystals from synovial fluid, supporting crystal burden reduction as the central therapeutic principle.158 Accordingly, from the perspective of vascular gout, the primary goal of SURIs also should be understood as reducing vascular MSU crystal burden through effective urate lowering over time.

Beyond this established urate-lowering rationale, preliminary experimental data suggest that dotinurad also may modulate inflammatory signaling. In macrophage models, dotinurad has been shown to suppress MSU crystal–induced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, including reductions in caspase-1 activation and IL-1β secretion.33 In addition, experimental studies have suggested potential URAT1-related effects on cardiac inflammation and metabolic stress responses.34 However, these findings remain preclinical and should be considered hypothesis-generating. At present, there is insufficient clinical evidence to regard such direct anti-inflammatory effects as the principal advantage of SURIs. Thus, in vascular gout, SURIs should be viewed primarily as urate-lowering agents capable of reducing crystal burden, particularly in patients with an underexcretion phenotype; any additional anti-inflammatory effects require further validation.

Lessons From Anti-Inflammatory Trials in Atherosclerosis: Implications for Vascular Gout

Several landmark clinical trials, including CANTOS (Canakinumab Antiinflammatory Thrombosis Outcome Study), COLCOT, and LoDoCo2, have established the central role of inflammation in atherosclerosis and demonstrated that targeting inflammatory pathways can reduce cardiovascular events. Importantly, these trials were conducted in general populations with coronary artery disease and were not designed to specifically investigate uric acid–related inflammation.26, 27, 28 Nevertheless, these studies provide important mechanistic insights into inflammation-driven atherosclerosis, particularly with respect to pathways such as IL-1β signaling and innate immune activation. These pathways are also implicated in MSU crystal–induced inflammation, suggesting that the lessons from these trials may have translational relevance for the concept of vascular gout.

Proof of the atherosclerotic inflammation hypothesis: lessons from CANTOS, COLCOT, and LoDoCo2

In the CANTOS trial, administration of canakinumab (an IL-1β inhibitor) to patients with a prior myocardial infarction significantly reduced major adverse cardiovascular events, accompanied by a marked decrease in high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), without affecting low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels.26 This finding represented a paradigm shift in cardiovascular medicine by demonstrating that targeting inflammation per se, independent of lipid lowering, can improve outcomes in patients with atherosclerotic disease.

Similarly, the COLCOT and LoDoCo2 trials showed that low-dose colchicine significantly reduced cardiovascular events in patients with recent acute coronary syndromes or chronic coronary artery disease, respectively.27,28 Notably, colchicine has long been used in treating gout flares and is well established as a prototypical therapy for suppressing MSU crystal–induced inflammation.

The cardiovascular benefits observed with IL-1β inhibition and colchicine should not be interpreted simply as nonspecific anti-inflammatory effects. Rather, insights from crystal-induced inflammation suggest that these therapies may influence the local inflammatory behavior of crystal-rich lesions. In gout, IL-1β is a key effector cytokine released in response to MSU crystal–driven inflammasome activation, and the inflammatory activity of a tophus appears to depend not only on crystal burden itself but also on the local balance between pro-inflammatory cytokine production and endogenous mechanisms of resolution. Recent work suggests that unstable or remodeling tophi may represent an “irritable” inflammatory state, in which local IL-1β activity contributes to recurrent flares; in this context, IL-1β blockade may stabilize the crystal deposit by interrupting this cytokine-driven amplification loop.159

Similarly, colchicine may exert protective effects not only through broad suppression of leukocyte trafficking, but also by modulating crystal-induced inflammatory microenvironments. Experimental studies show that colchicine inhibits NET formation, reduces IL-1β production, and alters the inflammatory properties of MSU crystal deposits and tophi, in part through effects on F-actin dynamics. These findings raise the possibility that the cardiovascular benefit of colchicine could, at least conceptually, involve stabilization of crystal-driven inflammatory activity within atherosclerotic plaques, although this remains unproven in human vascular tissue. Thus, the relevance of CANTOS, COLCOT, and LoDoCo2 to vascular gout lies not in their direct study of uric acid–related disease, but in demonstrating that local innate immune and IL-1β–centered inflammatory pathways are actionable therapeutic targets in atherosclerotic disease.160,161

Limitations of urate-lowering therapy: lessons from a FREED subanalysis that failed to achieve inflammation control

In a subanalysis of the FREED trial, evidence suggesting that inflammation was not adequately controlled provides an important insight into why urate-lowering therapy does not necessarily translate into cardiovascular event reduction. In that analysis, febuxostat significantly lowered serum uric acid levels; however, hsCRP did not differ between the treatment and nontreatment groups, and no correlation was observed between the magnitude of urate reduction and changes in hsCRP. Moreover, even among patients with residual inflammatory risk—defined by elevated hsCRP—febuxostat neither reduced inflammatory markers nor reduced cardiovascular events.162 These observations suggest that febuxostat-mediated urate lowering does not necessarily attenuate vascular inflammation; in other words, “lowering uric acid” may not be synonymous with “suppressing inflammation.” Consequently, urate-lowering therapy that fails to achieve inflammation control may have inherent limitations in preventing cardiovascular events, unlike inflammation-targeted therapies exemplified by CANTOS, COLCOT, and LoDoCo2.

From this perspective, the lack of consistent cardioprotective effects in prior febuxostat trials should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that uric acid is irrelevant as a cardiovascular risk factor. Rather, these findings may reflect that therapeutic strategies can sufficiently suppress the relevant inflammatory pathways that had not been fully achieved.

Risk Stratification and Timing of Therapeutic Intervention in Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia

Determining the optimal timing for initiating pharmacological therapy in patients with asymptomatic hyperuricemia remains a critical and unresolved issue in contemporary clinical practice. Western guidelines do not recommend routine pharmacological treatment for asymptomatic hyperuricemia in the absence of symptomatic manifestations, such as gout flares or urolithiasis. Rather, lifestyle modification is emphasized as the cornerstone of management.143,144 In contrast, the Japanese guidelines for managing hyperuricemia and gout suggest initiating urate-lowering therapy in addition to lifestyle intervention in patients with serum uric acid levels ≥8.0 mg/dL who also have cardiovascular risk factors or CKD.29 These divergent recommendations highlight substantial regional differences in therapeutic philosophy.

This discrepancy may be explained by the recognition that uric acid is not just a metabolic marker but is intricately linked with diverse pathophysiological conditions, including hypertension, obesity, insulin resistance, and renal dysfunction.1,5, 6, 7, 8 Consequently, future cardiovascular risk is unlikely to be uniformly distributed among individuals with asymptomatic hyperuricemia. Interventional trials on patients with asymptomatic hyperuricemia have shown that cardiovascular events are not uniformly reduced despite successful urate lowering, underscoring the limitations of a strategy that applies pharmacological intervention indiscriminately to all such patients. These observations favor a risk-based approach that stratifies patients by anticipated cardiovascular risk to optimize the timing and intensity of intervention rather than relying on a single uric acid threshold.

When the concept of vascular gout is applied clinically, asymptomatic hyperuricemia should be regarded as a spectrum of risk phenotypes warranting staged and tailored therapeutic strategies, rather than a homogeneous entity. First, in patients with long-standing persistent hyperuricemia, a rational approach involves urate-lowering therapy as a foundational intervention, coupled with simultaneous comprehensive risk management optimization—including blood pressure control, metabolic regulation, and preservation of renal function.

Second, growing evidence indicates that DECT can visualize MSU deposition within the coronary arteries and aorta, and corroborative pathological reports support these findings.24,163 Therefore, patients with demonstrable vascular MSU deposition may have a phenotype harboring heightened cardiovascular risk. If such deposits are reversible, a strategy aimed at long-term maintenance of lower uric acid targets to promote crystal debulking, monitored using imaging biomarkers, may be envisioned.164

Third, adjunctive intervention targeting inflammatory pathways—beyond urate lowering alone—may contribute to cardiovascular event reduction in patients exhibiting persistently elevated inflammatory markers, such as hsCRP or IL-6.28,164 This concept provides a theoretical basis for evaluating combined strategies of urate reduction and anti-inflammatory therapy in the chronic inflammatory milieu associated with gout and hyperuricemia.

Additional epidemiological evidence supports the systemic relevance of gout-related inflammation. A retrospective cohort study conducted in the United Kingdom reported that patients with gout had increased risk of cardiovascular events—including acute myocardial infarction and stroke—during the periods 0 to 60 days and 61 to 120 days following a gout flare, with adjusted ORs of 1.93 and 1.57, respectively.165 These findings suggest that gout flares should not be viewed as merely localized joint events but as time-dependent systemic inflammatory triggers that can precipitate cardiovascular events.166 Accordingly, preventing gout flares through appropriate hyperuricemia management may represent an additional avenue for mitigating cardiovascular risk.

Effective clinical implementation of the vascular gout concept will require explicit stratification of asymptomatic hyperuricemia into distinct risk categories, including: 1) long-standing persistent hyperuricemia; 2) phenotypes characterized by DECT-detectable vascular MSU deposition; and 3) phenotypes with sustained low-grade inflammation. Prospective studies incorporating tailored uric acid targets, treatment duration, and adjunctive therapies—such as intensive risk factor control or anti-inflammatory interventions—are required to determine whether such stratified strategies can reduce cardiovascular events. This precision-oriented approach can be supported by validation of surrogate endpoints, including imaging markers and inflammatory biomarkers. Conclusions: Redefining Uric Acid From a Numerical Marker to a Deposition- and Inflammation-Driven Vascular Risk

Epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated an association between serum uric acid levels and cardiovascular events; however, interventional trials of urate-lowering therapy have not uniformly shown clear benefits in reducing coronary events. This discrepancy cannot be fully explained by the traditional view of uric acid as a mere serum biomarker.

Uric acid is a multifaceted molecule whose role extends beyond just a soluble metabolic indicator. Through crystal deposition and the ensuing inflammatory responses, urate can influence vascular function and the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis. Vascular gout offers a framework for redefining uric acid–related disorders as not just joint disease or metabolic abnormalities, but as vascular risk conditions centered on deposition and inflammation within the vessel wall (Central Illustration).114

Of note is the consistent epidemiological observation that the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke transiently increases in the relatively early period following a gout flare. A gout flare, likened to a “fuse” leading to a cardiovascular event, represents a state in which the fuse has already been ignited, leaving only a short window before the “explosion”—the onset of a cardiovascular event. Accordingly, the opportunity for effective intervention at this stage is inherently limited. From this perspective, the most fundamental strategy for primary prevention is to appropriately control urate burden before the fuse is lit, especially at the stage of asymptomatic hyperuricemia. Nevertheless, the occurrence of a gout flare does not render subsequent intervention futile. During the acute phase, prompt suppression of inflammation, followed by sustained parallel management of inflammation and uric acid levels during remission, remains crucial for reducing long-term cardiovascular risk. This conceptual approach is consistent with existing clinical evidence demonstrating that therapies directly targeting inflammation can lead to cardiovascular event reduction.

In summary, a single measurement of serum uric acid at one point in time does not reflect uric acid–related cardiovascular risk, rather the cumulative burden of long-term urate exposure, crystal deposition, and the resultant chronic and acute inflammatory responses poses significant risks. Future clinical research should operationalize the vascular gout concept and prospectively evaluate the preventive cardiovascular benefits of appropriately managing uric acid at the asymptomatic hyperuricemia stage.
