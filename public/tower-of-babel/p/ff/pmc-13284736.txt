Bridging the diagnostic gap: Expanding dementia care navigation for timely diagnosis
Authors: Lucille Carriere, Morgan Minyo, David Bass, Katherine L Possin, Rafael Samper‐Ternent, Joel Salinas, Nicole Deaner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver half of dementia cases remain undiagnosed, with persistent disparities across racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic groups. Dementia care navigation (DCN) has demonstrated value in post‐diagnosis settings. If implemented earlier, DCN could address diagnostic delays and care gaps. We define the diagnostic window as the period from initial symptom awareness through diagnosis and early care planning. Drawing on deliberations from the Alzheimer's Association Dementia Care Navigation Roundtable, we present a pre‐diagnosis DCN framework organized across six domains and describe navigator roles across three phases: pre‐evaluation, diagnostic assessment, and immediate post‐diagnosis and transition. We address special considerations, including people without a care partner and those with unmet care needs. The framework complements federal initiatives such as the Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) Model and the National Alzheimer's Project Act and identifies existing reimbursement pathways for pre‐diagnostic navigation activities. Generating evidence to refine these models across diverse settings will be essential to inform policy action and system‐level integration.Keywords: care navigation, dementia diagnosis, GUIDE model, health equity, mild cognitive impairment, pre‐diagnosis

BACKGROUND

The pathway to dementia diagnosis usually starts with recognition of subjective cognitive decline or functional changes perceived by the person, family members, or healthcare or social service providers. 1 However, symptoms may go unnoticed for years or be wrongfully attributed to normal aging. 1 Even when changes are recognized, people may delay evaluation due to fear of a serious diagnosis, social stigma, and the potential loss of personal autonomy, including driving privileges and independent living. 2 , 3 On the provider side, barriers include clinician discomfort with cognitive assessment, perceived limited benefits of early detection, insufficient time for screening, limited reimbursement for cognitive testing and post‐diagnostic care, and lack of clear referral pathways to specialists. 4 Consequently, dementia diagnosis is often delayed, with an average interval of 3.5 years from symptom recognition, and 4.1 years in early‐onset cases. 5 Only 8% of expected mild cognitive impairment (MCI) cases receive timely diagnosis, 6 and about 60% of dementia cases remain undetected in the United States, 7 reflecting systemic gaps in screening and evaluation. These gaps disproportionately affect racial and ethnic minorities, with 72% of non‐Hispanic white persons diagnosed at early disease stages compared to only 63% of non‐Hispanic Black and 59% of Hispanic persons. 8 Socioeconomic disparities and clinical factors, including better preserved cognitive function and fewer comorbidities at symptom onset, further influence diagnostic timeliness. 8 , 9 Geographic proximity to clinical diagnostic resources, such as living in urban areas or regions with robust healthcare infrastructure, favors earlier diagnosis. 10

The consequences of delayed diagnosis are substantial. When dementia remains undetected, people are more likely to experience fragmented care, increased acute healthcare utilization, preventable hospitalizations, medication errors, and episodes of delirium. 4 The emergence of disease‐modifying therapies (DMTs) for early dementia, including United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) ‐approved lecanemab and donanemab, creates the demand for timely diagnosis and treatment access, particularly among historically underserved populations. While Medicare coverage formally extends DMT access to all eligible beneficiaries who meet clinical criteria, substantial barriers persist in practice. Without navigation support and care system reforms to facilitate timely diagnosis and specialist referrals, DMTs risk reaching primarily patients already connected to well‐resourced healthcare systems, thereby perpetuating existing disparities in dementia care. 11 Beyond facilitating access to DMTs for eligible patients, timely diagnosis of cognitive impairment enables people to engage in advance care planning when cognitive capacity allows informed decision‐making. 12 It also provides care partners with essential resources and education before crises develop. 13 At the same time, early evaluation remains crucial for identifying and treating reversible causes of cognitive impairment. 14

Dementia Care Navigation (DCN) has emerged to address fragmented care during the pre‐diagnostic phase. 15 By validating symptoms, coordinating cognitive screening, educating about modifiable risk factors, and facilitating specialist referrals, navigators bridge the gap between symptom recognition and diagnosis. 15 , 16 , 17 Navigators facilitate evidence‐based diagnostic evaluations in primary care and enable ongoing follow‐up after abnormal screening results. 15 , 16 , 17 Screening programs using navigators in underserved communities have shown promise for reducing healthcare disparities. 18 Entry into pre‐diagnosis DCN may occur through multiple pathways, including community‐based identification, self‐ or family‐initiated concerns, and referrals from clinicians. Because many people with undiagnosed dementia symptoms already receive healthcare or community services for other chronic conditions, 9 including care navigation, these providers represent entry points for extending DCN into the pre‐diagnosis phase.

Networks such as Dementia Friendly America have made important progress sensitizing professionals and lay people to warning signs and connecting people to diagnostic and support resources. 19 Emerging pilot programs also show promise. The Davos Alzheimer's Collaborative Healthcare System Preparedness (DAC‐SP) program, a global initiative to improve early detection of cognitive impairment, has implemented its Brain Health Navigator (BHN) model across six healthcare systems in the United States. 15 Across these pilot sites, BHN positions were established and staffed within 6 months of program launch, and over 250 participants were enrolled throughout 2025, with positive claims payment supporting initial financial viability. 15 Norton Healthcare, one of the DAC‐SP pilot sites, reported high acceptability of its BHN program among patients and care partners and no significant barriers to reimbursement. 17 Quantitative outcomes have also been reported by independent BHN programs, including one implemented across seven clinics within a single health system in Indiana. 16 In that program, patients with abnormal results on a digital cognitive assessment received a referral to the navigator for evaluation. Those who completed the navigator visit had a significantly higher likelihood of receiving a new diagnosis of MCI and of having orders placed for diagnostic testing and referrals to neurology and neuropsychology than patients who did not. 16 Together, these data support the feasibility of navigator‐assisted diagnostic pathways. However, DCN protocols for pre‐diagnosis remain underdeveloped, and most literature focuses on post‐diagnosis support. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25

National health policy reforms create unprecedented opportunities for systematic change. Beginning in 2025, hospitals participating in Medicare's Hospital Inpatient Quality Reporting Program must report on Age‐Friendly Hospital Measures, which include attestation regarding cognitive screening and other age‐friendly practices. 26 While hospitals are not required to have all practices in place, failure to report can result in reimbursement penalties, and attestation responses are publicly available through Centers for Medicaire and Medicaid Services (CMS) Care Compare. 26 Separately, federal policy includes cognitive assessment for Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 and older during Annual Wellness Visits, 27 though implementation remains limited, with one‐third receiving this benefit. 28

Community‐based organizations (CBOs) play a critical role in pre‐diagnosis DCN by extending reach beyond traditional healthcare settings. CBOs address cultural and social factors affecting care, reduce access barriers, and connect people to clinical diagnostic pathways and local support resources. Partnerships with local CBOs promote health equity, and successful DCN models consistently collaborate with community‐based services representative of populations served. 20 However, community‐based resources are underutilized, with only one‐fourth of unpaid care partners taking advantage of supportive services. 20

This perspective paper focuses on DCN during the pre‐diagnosis period for people experiencing cognitive or functional changes that raise concern for possible dementia. We acknowledge the pathway from cognitive concern to formal diagnosis exists along a continuum—from subjective cognitive concerns to MCI to dementia—with boundaries often unclear in primary care settings. We use “pre‐diagnosis DCN” to refer to navigation activities supporting people and families from symptom recognition through the process of determining whether there is a diagnosis of MCI or dementia, including initial care planning, but prior to enrollment in post‐diagnosis care management programs (Box 1). Our framework complements other emerging policy initiatives, particularly the Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) model of the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), 29 and resonates with international efforts such as the DAC‐SP program. 15 As implementation models for pre‐diagnosis navigation emerge in specific healthcare settings, a unifying conceptual framework is needed to support adaptation across diverse contexts and inform consistent evaluation of these programs. Drawing upon deliberations from the Dementia Care Navigation Roundtable (DCNR) Workgroups from the Alzheimer's Association, we outline evidence‐informed pre‐diagnostic navigation activities designed to accelerate diagnosis, improve equity, and enhance outcomes for people facing cognitive decline and their families. This framework builds on prior DCNR work articulating foundational DCN principles, navigator roles and responsibilities, and competency‐based training frameworks [ 20 and unpublished results].

CONCEPTUALIZING THE DIAGNOSTIC WINDOW AND MODEL FOUNDATIONS

For most people, the path to a cognitive diagnosis, including MCI and dementia, begins well before formal evaluation. Early changes in memory, thinking, or daily functioning are often first noticed by the person, family members, or close contacts. 1 Cognitive impairment may manifest through various symptoms, the most common being apathy, depression, loss of short‐term memory, difficulties recalling names or words, impaired concentration and attention, problems managing finances, difficulties with problem‐solving and calculations, and confusion with time or place. 2 , 30 , 31 Clinical presentation varies by disease severity, with people potentially experiencing one or multiple symptoms at varying degrees of intensity, ranging from mild to severe. 2 Close relatives frequently play a central role at this stage, interpreting symptoms, encouraging help‐seeking, and navigating uncertainty about whether observed changes warrant medical attention. 1 In other cases, healthcare providers detect cognitive impairment during the Annual Wellness Visits, 27 while community‐based providers may identify early signs through their ongoing contact with patients and families. 32

The diagnostic window is defined as spanning the period from initial symptom awareness through diagnosis and early care planning. Although this process is often described in phases, including pre‐evaluation, diagnostic assessment, and immediate post‐diagnosis and transition, progression is rarely linear. People may delay evaluation, disengage, or cycle between steps depending on symptom severity, access to care, and competing priorities. Care partner involvement often intensifies over time, particularly as systems become more complex. As people and families move through these phases, navigation needs to evolve accordingly.

Most patients with Alzheimer's disease receive their first evaluation in primary care settings, where the primary care physician (PCP) evaluates cognitive concerns and determines whether referral to specialty care is warranted. 2 Whereas most cases of severe Alzheimer's disease are diagnosed by a PCP, MCI due to Alzheimer's disease tends to be more frequently diagnosed by neurologists. 2 In addition, among Medicare beneficiaries, non‐dementia specialist physicians initiate the majority of MCI and dementia diagnoses, including Alzheimer's disease. 33 , 34 However, the diagnostic profile differs by provider type: among beneficiaries who do not see a dementia specialist within 1 year of diagnosis, approximately one‐third receive a diagnosis of “unspecified dementia,” whereas those evaluated by a specialist are more likely to receive an etiology‐specific diagnosis, such as Alzheimer's disease. 33 Referral to specialty care may be prompted by atypical clinical presentations, early‐onset dementia, rapidly progressive cognitive decline, diagnostic uncertainty, complexity in clinical presentation, challenging behavioral or neuropsychiatric symptoms, significant social risk factors that complicate care management, or when families require additional support navigating complex care needs. 35 The emergence of anti‐amyloid DMTs has introduced an additional driver for specialist referral, as eligibility assessment, treatment initiation, and ongoing safety monitoring typically require coordination with neurology or memory specialty services. 36 However, access to screening or specialty care may be limited. 3

After initial assessment, the main factors for diagnostic delays include systemic hurdles such as the time needed to coordinate follow‐up consultations, finalize specialist referrals, and arrange for required diagnostic procedures. 2 These challenges are shaped by geography, health system capacity, and social context, contributing to persistent inequities in diagnostic timing and experience. Diagnostic procedures routinely integrate behavioral and cognitive assessments alongside general laboratory tests and qualitative reports from patients and their families regarding medical history. Most diagnostic cases include structural imaging, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Until recently, biomarker‐based testing was used in only a minority of cases. 2 However, broader Medicare coverage of amyloid‐positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and the increasing availability of plasma‐based biomarkers are expected to expand the use of biomarker confirmation in clinical practice.

Models of service utilization, such as the Behavioral Health Model, 37 provide a useful lens for understanding how people access and use dementia care services during the diagnostic journey based on predisposing factors (demographics, cultural values, knowledge), enabling factors (income, informal support, community resources), and need factors (cognitive/functional impairment, behavioral symptoms). This conceptual foundation emphasizes alignment with real‐world care pathways rather than adherence to a rigid sequence of steps.

NAVIGATOR ROLES, RESPONSIBILITIES, AND HANDOFFS

During the diagnostic window, pre‐diagnosis DCN involves practical, time‐bound activities that support people and families as they move from initial concern through evaluation and early post‐diagnostic planning (Table S1). Because navigation functions are distributed across community and clinical settings rather than residing in a single role, coordinated workflows and clear handoffs between providers are essential to continuity of care. We propose a pre‐diagnosis DCN framework organized in six domains: assessment, care planning and coordination, information and education and support for the person and care partner, communication, person‐centered care, and safety (Table 1 and Table S1). These domains apply throughout the diagnostic journey, though the specific activities within each vary by phase. The following activities align with established DCN principles 20 and are organized across the three phases of the diagnostic window.

Pre‐evaluation. During the pre‐evaluation phase, navigators consult with families to better understand symptoms, functional impairment, and immediate safety concerns. They assess readiness to pursue diagnostic services, identify and engage available care partners, and clarify roles related to medical power of attorney, decision‐making, benefits, finances, and support needs. Navigators provide early education about dementia symptoms, potential causes, and the benefits of early evaluation. They explain the diagnostic process, what to expect from testing and specialty referrals, and assist families in locating appropriate providers and scheduling services. Navigation activities during this phase frequently include addressing logistical barriers, such as transportation, insurance questions, or fragmented referral pathways, and connecting families to relevant healthcare and community‐based resources. 38 Within their scope of practice, navigators in community‐based settings listen for concerns about cognition, observe for signs and symptoms of cognitive impairment, and note changes that occur over time. They communicate these observations appropriately and encourage people and families to discuss concerns with their physician. 32 In some models, clinicians may also directly refer patients to navigation services following recognition of cognitive concerns or abnormal screening results. 15 This bidirectional referral pathway can strengthen coordination between clinical and community‐based services and support timely follow‐through on evaluation. Navigators in community‐based settings maintain up‐to‐date lists of local memory assessment centers and physicians who can provide diagnostic evaluations and ideally partner with healthcare systems to establish effective referral procedures. 32

Diagnostic assessment. Once evaluation begins, navigation shifts toward coordination. Navigators facilitate referral completion, help schedule and prepare families for testing, and ensure communication across providers. Standardized assessment tools and safety evaluation guides can support navigators under appropriate supervision in conducting structured evaluations across domains, including cognition, function, behavioral symptoms, medication management, and home safety. 39 Within their scope of practice and with appropriate training, supervision, and standardized protocols, navigators with clinical backgrounds, such as registered nurses, may review medical charts to ensure that recommended testing has been completed or ordered, summarize assessment findings to support the PCP in diagnostic decision‐making, and provide guidance on whether specialist referral may be warranted. 40 Care partner engagement intensifies at this stage: navigators support care partners in observing and tracking symptoms, facilitate their active participation in clinical encounters, and identify barriers to service use. For people whose clinician has recommended evaluation, navigators encourage follow‐through by discussing the importance of completing the process. 32 Navigators also support shared decision‐making by connecting diagnostic steps with the person's values and preferences.

Immediate post‐diagnosis and transition. In this period, navigation activities continue, often reinforcing education, providing emotional support, and supporting people and families who do not fully understand their diagnosis by encouraging them to talk with the diagnosing clinician and offering print and online sources of additional information. 32 They initiate early care planning and coordinate linkage to treatment, ongoing DCN, or management programs, including DMT clinics where appropriate and post‐diagnostic models such as GUIDE. Warm handoffs at this stage ensure that assessment findings, goals, and care preferences are communicated across settings and that families are prepared for the next steps following diagnosis.

Without defined scope, accountability, and payment structures, navigation activities risk duplication or inconsistent delivery. An ecosystem approach requires coordinated integration across CBOs (including Area Agencies on Aging [AAA], social service agencies, and community health worker programs), primary care, and specialty services with clear roles, coordinated workflows, and intentional handoffs. Embedding navigators as connectors within interdisciplinary teams can reduce diagnostic gaps, but this requires alignment across workforce roles, care settings, and policy frameworks.

SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS AND POPULATIONS

Pre‐diagnosis care navigation must account for people who are at risk of delayed or missed evaluation due to social, structural, and contextual factors. One population of particular concern includes people undergoing evaluation for suspected MCI or dementia who do not have care partners. Many diagnostic pathways implicitly rely on the presence of another person to observe cognitive or functional changes, provide contextual history, support decision‐making, and assist with navigating care processes. When such support is absent, the person may face additional barriers to initiating or completing a diagnostic evaluation.

In these circumstances, pre‐diagnosis care navigation necessarily differs from models that assume involvement of a care partner. Navigation relies more on direct engagement with the person and may require additional outreach, follow‐up, and alternative approaches to gathering relevant information, supporting informed decision‐making, and coordinating referrals and next steps. Explicit recognition of people without a care partner is essential to ensure that navigation models do not inadvertently reinforce practices that limit access to evaluation and follow‐through.

Beyond care partner status, several population‐level factors can influence engagement with the diagnostic process and warrant consideration in pre‐diagnosis navigation. These include people living alone, people with language access needs or limited English proficiency, people from culturally diverse backgrounds who may experience stigma, mistrust, or differing expectations related to cognitive evaluation, and people residing in rural or resource‐limited settings, where access to specialty care and diagnostic services may be constrained. Insurance coverage, funding limitations, and broader system‐level barriers may further shape pathways to evaluation. Taken together, these considerations underscore the importance of navigation approaches that are flexible and responsive to varied social contexts and health system capacities, rather than predicated on uniform access to care partners, resources, or services.

POLICY ALIGNMENT

Navigation during the diagnostic window aligns with emerging federal dementia policy, including broader federal initiatives under the National Alzheimer's Project Act (NAPA). 41 Enacted in 2011, NAPA established a coordinated national strategy to address Alzheimer's disease and related dementias through its National Plan, which includes goals to enhance public awareness and engagement and to improve care quality and efficiency. Pre‐diagnosis navigation directly supports these objectives by promoting early recognition, facilitating timely evaluation, and strengthening community engagement before formal diagnosis.

Federal implementation of the NAPA is coordinated across agencies through collaborations led by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, including the National Institute on Aging, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the CMS. 42 The CDC's Healthy Aging Program and related public health initiatives emphasize brain health promotion, risk reduction, and community‐based partnerships, reinforcing the role of early engagement strategies. 43 The Alzheimer's Association has played a formal advisory and advocacy role within NAPA, advancing public awareness, workforce development, and access to care and supports across the continuum. 44

Pre‐diagnosis navigation can support timely and appropriate entry into post‐diagnostic reimbursement models, including GUIDE, 29 by helping people and families move efficiently through evaluation, reducing delays between symptom recognition and diagnostic confirmation, and preparing care partners for engagement in longitudinal dementia care. By the time a diagnosis is established, earlier navigation may facilitate a smoother transition to the GUIDE program by clarifying care goals, supporting care partner readiness, and strengthening connections to clinical and community‐based resources. Pre‐diagnosis navigation is also consistent with broader policy trends exploring value‐based care models, team‐based primary care redesign, and expanded use of non‐physician roles such as navigators and care managers to extend clinical capacity and reduce fragmented care. 45 These functions complement, rather than duplicate, GUIDE‐covered services, strengthening the pathway from symptom recognition to post‐diagnostic care.

At the same time, policy gaps remain. Because GUIDE reimbursement begins only after a dementia diagnosis is established, organizations face limited financial support for navigation earlier in the diagnostic process. Funding for navigation is often distributed across healthcare systems, aging services, and public health programs, creating structural barriers to coordination. Workforce capacity and training requirements also vary across regions, limiting consistent implementation of pre‐diagnostic navigation programs.

Existing care management and navigation‐related billing pathways may partially cover activities during the diagnostic period (see Table S2), although reimbursement options remain limited and variably utilized before a confirmed dementia diagnosis. Notably, the Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 99483 (Cognitive Assessment and Care Planning) applies to suspected cognitive impairment and does not require a confirmed dementia diagnosis, which makes it directly relevant to pre‐diagnosis navigation activities. 46 The Principal Illness Navigation (PIN) codes represent an additional potential pathway for reimbursement of navigation services during cognitive impairment assessment. 17 Contracting arrangements with AAA and other CBOs can help bridge clinical and community services during the diagnostic period. 47 Furthermore, partnerships between clinicians and AAAs can lead to significant cost savings and decreased medical utilization among older adults. 48 Public health awareness and early identification initiatives offer additional platforms to test navigation models that connect symptom recognition to evaluation and eventual enrollment in GUIDE‐supported care. 49 Together, these approaches can inform future policy refinements supporting pre‐diagnosis navigation.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND RESEARCH AGENDA

Although evidence supports the value of DCN overall, research specific to navigation before diagnosis remains limited. Most studies examine navigation after a diagnosis is established, with relatively little attention to what happens earlier, when people and families are first trying to make sense of symptoms and determine whether evaluation is warranted. As a result, there is limited empirical guidance on whether navigation during this period meaningfully shortens time to diagnosis, changes patterns of service use, or alters the experience of patients and care partners. The success of early navigation in oncology, 50 which has effectively reduced the time to diagnosis and treatment, suggests a scalable framework that could be replicated to improve the diagnostic pathway for cognitive impairment.

Introducing navigation earlier also raises practical questions that have not been well studied. Workforce roles, training expectations, and supervision models vary widely across programs, and it is not clear which competencies are most critical during the diagnostic phase as opposed to later stages of care. Navigators often come from different professional backgrounds, and organizations differ in how responsibility is shared between navigators, clinicians, and community partners, including community health workers, community outreach specialists, health educators, and other professionals within CBOs. For example, nurses may be best suited for a navigation role that supports diagnostic evaluation and requires understanding of the patient's medical conditions and tests. In contrast, community health workers may play a key role in outreach, education, and culturally responsive engagement during early identification and referral, and social workers may be well‐suited for post‐diagnostic care planning.

Implementation will also depend heavily on context. Access to specialty care, availability of community‐based services, and health system infrastructure differ substantially across regions. In some settings, navigation may focus on helping families overcome long wait times because of limited workforce capacity or lack of local resources, while in others, the primary challenge may be fragmented referral pathways. These variations suggest that a single model is unlikely to be effective across all settings.

We recommend that future research prioritize studies that examine how navigation provided during the diagnostic period can support subsequent integration into existing care frameworks, including DMT clinics for MCI or early dementia and the GUIDE Model for established dementia cases. This includes pragmatic implementation studies, trials that assess navigation earlier in the diagnostic pathway, and designs that explicitly examine equity‐related outcomes. Analyses of cost and scalability will also be important, particularly for informing payment and policy decisions. Together, this work can help determine when, where, and for whom pre‐diagnosis navigation adds the greatest value.

CONCLUSION AND CALL TO ACTION

The diagnostic window represents a consequential but underdeveloped phase of the dementia care continuum. By facilitating early recognition, timely referral, and coordinated follow‐up, pre‐diagnosis DCN can help shift the trajectory toward earlier diagnosis and more appropriate, proactive, and equitable care, potentially reducing avoidable complications and inefficient healthcare use.

Strengthening navigator roles, building workforce capacity, and complementing services with policy initiatives such as the GUIDE Model can help connect people and families to coordinated care at an earlier point. These efforts also have the potential to support more effective enrollment into longitudinal dementia care programs and to increase the overall impact of value‐based reforms.

Policy‐makers, healthcare systems, and CBOs should act now to integrate navigation into the diagnostic phase of care using existing policy levers and delivery structures. At the same time, researchers must continue to generate evidence to refine these models and assess their effectiveness across diverse settings. Coordinated action during this period of policy momentum is necessary to reduce diagnostic delays and advance more timely and equitable dementia care.
