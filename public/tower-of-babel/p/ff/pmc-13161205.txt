Germ cells are essential for testicular morphogenesis and functional reconstruction in a porcine xenograft model
Authors: Min-Gi Han, Yoseop Jeon, Hyoyoung Maeng, Donghyeon Kim, Jeong Tae Do, Kwonho Hong, Youngsok Choi, Hyuk Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReconstruction of functional testis tissue can provide new opportunities for in vitro spermatogenesis and fertility restoration in livestock. Although testis reconstruction has been reported in several species, the germ cell dependent mechanisms underlying de novo testicular morphogenesis remain insufficiently defined in large animals. In this study, we investigated the contribution of porcine spermatogonial stem cells (pSSCs) to testis morphogenesis and spermatogenesis using a xenograft model. Testicular cells containing approximately 10% germ cells combined with a somatic-enriched fraction (SEF) (GF group) or SEF only (FO group) were transplanted subcutaneously into immunodeficient mice and examined after six months. GF tissues developed well-organized seminiferous tubules and showed multiple spermatogenic stages, whereas FO tissues remained fragmented, poorly organized, and lacked stable tubular architecture. Immunofluorescence analyses revealed appropriate localization of germ cells and maturation-associated features of Sertoli cells in GF tissues, whereas FO tissues exhibited aberrant Sertoli cell localization and marker expression patterns. Transcriptomic profiling further showed enrichment of cell cycle– and spermatogenesis-related pathways in GF tissues, while FO tissues exhibited upregulation of coagulation-, inflammation-, and early developmental pathways, consistent with impaired tissue organization. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that pSSCs are indispensable for initiating and stabilizing testicular morphogenesis in xenograft condition and underscore the utility of this model for advancing reproductive biotechnology in livestock.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44916-4.Keywords: Xenograft, Testicular morphogenesis, Spermatogonial stem cells, Testis reconstruction, Porcine testisSubject terms: Biotechnology, Cell biology, Developmental biology

Introduction

Climate change has emerged as a major threat to global livestock productivity, with rising temperatures and environmental stressors impairing animal fertility and reproductive efficiency1. To address these challenges, the development of reproductive technologies capable of forming testes and producing functional sperm in vitro has become increasingly critical, representing a significant area in reproductive biotechnology2. In vitro spermatogenesis not only provides a powerful tool for investigating fundamental processes of germ cell development and testicular biology, but also holds considerable promise for both clinical and agricultural applications3,4. In vitro testis reconstitution, when integrated with long-term spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) culture, may contribute to continuous breeding strategies, preservation of superior genetic lines, fertility management with impaired testicular function5,6. Furthermore, in vitro-derived sperm can serve as a platform for advanced assisted reproductive technologies, including gene editing and delivery in valuable livestock breeds7,8.

Recent advances in testicular tissue engineering have demonstrated that dissociated testicular cells can reorganize into testis-like structures when transplanted into immunodeficient mice9,10. Several studies using testicular cells from rodents or large animals have reported the formation of tubular structures and, in some cases, partial or complete spermatogenesis11–13. In particular, xenotransplantation models provide a powerful platform for studying in vivo testicular morphogenesis under physiologically relevant conditions. More recently, studies have shown that growth factors such as epidermal growth factor (EGF) and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) contribute to the formation of in vitro testis-like structures14,15. Furthermore, the use of testis-derived cells from various mammals in combination with extracellular matrix components such as Matrigel has been shown to enhance tubule formation and tissue integrity by promoting three-dimensional aggregation and maintaining cell polarity16,17. Despite these advances, the reconstruction of functional testicular structures and complete spermatogenesis in large animal models such as pigs remains a considerable challenge due to developmental timing differences, variations in tissue composition, and limited availability of relevant materials.

Although the function of germ cells for the completion of the spermatogenic process has been thoroughly investigated, their role during the early stages of testicular tissue formation remains unclear. Traditionally, testis morphogenesis has been considered a somatically driven process, orchestrated primarily by Sertoli cells and peritubular myoid cells18. However, emerging evidence suggests that germ cells may play a more active role beyond merely occupying the seminiferous cords19. Germ cells may influence the spatial organization, vascularization, and maturation of the somatic environment itself20. Attempts to reconstruct functional testes in vitro frequently encounter the challenge of germ cell depletion, where spermatogonial stem cells undergo apoptosis or fail to integrate into the developing microenvironment. This suggests that germ cells may be essential not only for spermatogenesis but also for initiating and stabilizing testis-like architecture. This raises important questions: Can testis-like structures self-organize in the absence of germ cells? If so, how do the resulting structures differ morphologically and functionally from those formed in the presence of germ cells? Addressing these questions is critical for optimizing in vitro testis reconstruction protocols and resolving challenges associated with testicular tissue engineering, particularly in translationally relevant large-animal models such as pigs.

In this study, we evaluated whether testis-like structures and the spermatogenic process could be induced under defined conditions, and investigated the impact of germ cell absence on testicular architecture and function under in vitro testis reconstitution using xenotransplantation model. These findings provide new insights into germ cell–dependent testis development and criteria for assessing functional seminiferous tubules, supporting the development of in vitro spermatogenesis in large animal models.

Methods

Experimental design

To systematically investigate the cellular requirements for in vitro testis reconstruction, we employed a xenotransplantation model using porcine testicular cells. Specifically, two experimental groups were established: (1) GF tissues, composed of both porcine spermatogonial stem cells (pSSCs) enriched germ cells and testicular somatic-enriched fraction (SEF); and (2) FO tissues, containing SEF only and lacking germ cells. The FO group was designed to mimic a germ cell-depleted condition, which may arise from developmental defects or environmental damage during testis-like structure formation. This comparative design enabled us to evaluate whether the presence of germ cells is critical for the formation and maturation of testis-like tissue under defined conditions. Unlike rodent models, in which primordial germ cells (PGCs) migration can be genetically manipulated20, such interventions are technically unfeasible in large-animal species like pigs. Therefore, the use of xenotransplantation offered a practical and reproducible approach to assess testicular morphogenesis through histological, immunofluorescence, and transcriptomic analyses.

In vitro testicular cell isolation and colony formation

The animal experimental procedures used in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Konkuk University (KU23097). All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Porcine testes were collected from 5-d-old, crossbred piglets (Landrace and Large White Yorkshire) sourced from Sam-Woo Livestock (Yang Pyeong, Korea). Porcine total testicular cells (pTTCs) were isolated as previously described21. The tunica albuginea was removed, and the chopped testicular tissue was digested using five-fold volume (v/w) of enzyme mixture solution containing 0.01 mg/mL DNase Ⅰ (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 0.5 mg/mL collagenase Ⅳ (Gibco, CA, USA), 0.5 mg/mL hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.1 mg/mL and soy bean trypsin inhibitor (Gibco) in Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS) at 37℃ for 15 min. After DPBS washing, second digestion (5 mg/mL collagenase IV, 0.01 mg/mL DNAse I, and 0.1 mg/mL soybean trypsin inhibitor) were conduct at 37℃ for 15 min. The digested tissue was filtered through a 40-µm nylon cell strainer (Corning, NY, USA). The collected cells were treated with an equal volume of RBC lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min to eliminate red blood cells.

Dissociated pTTCs were cultured for 1 day at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After this attachment step, adherent SEF cells were designated feeder cells, and the suspension cell population containing pSSCs was collected by gentle pipetting. Fresh SEF feeder cells were harvested and cryopreserved to use subsequent injections and for continued support of pSSC colony culture.

Suspended cells were plated at 2 × 105 cells per well on 0.1% (w/v) gelatin-coated 12-well plates and maintained at 34 °C with 5% CO₂ in StemPro-34 medium (Gibco) as previously described22, supplemented with 1% ITS (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA; Gibco), 1% MEM vitamin solution (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco), 0.1 mM vitamin C solution (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 µg/mL lactic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), 30 ng/mL β-estradiol (Sigma-Aldrich), 60 ng/mL progesterone (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.2% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich), 20 ng/mL mouse EGF (PeproTech, NJ, USA), 10 ng/mL basic FGF (PeproTech), 10 ng/mL GDNF (R&D Systems, MN, USA), and 103 U/mL LIF (Millipore, MA, USA).

To isolate pSSC colonies before overgrowth of SEF feeder cells, loosely attached colonies were gently dislodged by pipetting and transferred onto gelatin-coated 12-well plates that had been pre-seeded with 0.5 × 105 fresh SEF feeder cells per well. Colonies were expanded for at least two passages. After colony formation, colonies were separated from SEF feeder cells by pipetting. Subsequently, a pre-plating step was performed on 0.1% gelatin-coated 100-mm dishes: cells were incubated for 1 h, twice in succession, discarding newly adherent cells each time by gentle pipetting. The non-adherent, colony-forming cells were collected and used for downstream experiments. No proliferation inhibitors (e.g., Mitomycin C) were used at any step of the cell culture procedures.

Flowcytometry

For flow cytometry analysis, colonies were first dissociated into single cells. This was achieved by incubating them in 0.25% Trypsin at 37 °C for 5 min, with gentle pipetting every minute to facilitate the process. The enzymatic reaction was subsequently neutralized using DPBS supplemented with 5% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). All subsequent washing and antibody steps utilized a buffer consisting of DPBS supplemented with 1% (w/v) Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA-100; Bovogen, VIC, Australia). Cells were washed in this buffer for 5 minutes and then stained with PE Mouse Anti-Human CD90 (BD Pharmingen™, catalog 561970). The primary staining was carried out for 1 hour at 4°C. Following the primary incubation, cells were washed three times (5 min per wash) using the same BSA buffer. DAPI (1:1000) was utilized for nuclear counterstaining for 5 minutes, followed by three final 5 min washes. Samples were analyzed using a CytoFlex Flow Cytometry Analyzer (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA), and the resulting data was processed with CytExpert 2.6 (Beckman Coulter) software.

RNA extraction, reverse transcriptase-PCR, and quantitative-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from pSSCs and SEF cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) with an On-Column DNase Set (Qiagen). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1000 ng of total RNA using the M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) with an oligo dT primer mix. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of target genes was performed with 35 cycles at 95℃ for 30 s, 57℃ for 20 s, and 70℃ for 20 s. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using a 20 μL reaction mixture containing 20 ng of cDNA and 10 μL AccuPower® 2X Greenstar™ qPCR Master Mix (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea). The qPCR conditions were set as follows: initial enzyme activation and DNA pre-denaturation at 95℃ for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95℃ for 10 s, 57℃ for 10 s, and 72℃ for 20 s. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were normalized based on the expression of the porcine β−2-microglobulin (B2M) gene. A list of primer sets is presented in Table 1.

Immunocytochemistry

Two-passaged colonies of pSSCs were cultured on glass cell culture slides (31404, SPL, Pocheon, Korea). After washing with DPBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Immunofluorescence (IF) buffer, containing 0.05% Triton X-100 in DPBS, was used to permeabilize the cell membrane, while 2% BSA was used to prevent nonspecific protein binding. All primary antibodies—mouse anti-human UCHL1 IgG (7863-1004; Bio-Rad, CA, USA), rabbit anti-SOX9 IgG (ab185966; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and normal rabbit IgG (GTX35035, GeneTex, CA, USA) and mouse IgG (GTX35009; GeneTex) were treated for negative control—were diluted at 1:500 in IF buffer and applied to the fixed samples for overnight incubation at 4℃. Subsequently, the samples were washed twice with DPBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 594 anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) secondary antibodies in IF buffer. The IF buffer was applied without primary antibodies, following the same procedure as a negative control. The samples were incubated with 1 μg/mL 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Thermo Fisher) for 10 min to achieve nuclear staining. Finally, samples were mounted in an aqueous mounting medium (DAKO, CA, USA), and fluorescence was observed under a confocal microscope (LSM 700; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Recipient mouse and xenograft

Four 3-week-old Balb/c immunodeficient nude male mice (DBL Co., Eumseong, Korea) were purchased and allowed a 1-week acclimatization period. Non-castration four-week-old mice were used as two recipient groups for xenotransplantation. Each mouse received four injections per group (n = 8). Mice were anesthetized with alfaxalone administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 80 mg/kg prior to cell transplantation. A total of 5×106 testicular cells suspended in 50 μL of StemPro-34 media and 50 μL of high-concentration Matrigel (growth factor reduced, 354263, Corning) were mixed in ice and administered into the dorsal skin using a 1-mL syringe with a 23-gauge (23-G) needle. The GF group was injected at a ratio of 1:9, consisting of 0.5×106 pSSC-colony cells and 4.5×106 fresh SEF feeder cells. All mice were maintained at 22 ± 2 °C with unrestricted access to food and water. Six months after the xenograft, immunodeficient mice were euthanized using a CO2 chamber to collect the tissue. No specific inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied. Humane endpoints were set, including weight loss, appetite, weakness, and moribund state, but none were observed. All animals and data points were included in the analysis. The collected tissues were subjected to histological analysis.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

The reconstituted tissues were washed with DPBS and fixed overnight at 4℃ with 4% paraformaldehyde. The tissues were progressively dehydrated using a series of ethanol solutions (70–100%), immersed in xylene, and finally embedded in paraffin. Sections of the tissue, 5-μm thick, were cut using a rotary microtome (Thermo Fisher) and placed on glass slides. Subsequently, the sections were deparaffinized with 100% xylene and rehydrated using serial ethanol solutions (100–70%).

The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological analysis and observed under a microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For the quantitative analysis of H&E-stained tissue images, a minimum of three non-overlapping fields containing clusters of tubular structures were selected and analyzed from distinct tissue sections. Qupath software (v. 0.5.1) was used to count all tubule structures to determine the total number of tubules. Among these, those characterized by the presence of cells in the germinal epithelium, a distinguishable basement membrane, and an inner array of Sertoli-like cells were classified as ‘testis-like tubules’. Tubule diameter measurement was conducted by measuring the narrowest part of the tubular structure in different non-overlapping tubule-clustered sections, based on the scale bar, and the final counts of the classified tubules were recorded for subsequent statistical analysis.

Additionally, antigen retrieval was performed by boiling the sections in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min for immunohistochemical analysis. The primary antibodies (Table 2) and same dilution of normal rabbit and mouse IgG for negative control were diluted in IF buffer and applied to the sections overnight at 4℃. Sections were then rinsed with DPBS and incubated with secondary antibodies (diluted 1:1000: Alexa 488 anti-mouse (A32723), Alexa 568 anti-mouse (A11004), Alexa 594 anti-rabbit (A11012), and Alexa 488 anti-rabbit (A11008) for 1 h at room temperature (24–26℃). Subsequently, 1 μg/mL of DAPI was applied to the sections for 10 min to stain the nuclei, followed by washing with DPBS. The sections were then mounted using an aqueous mounting medium (DAKO) and examined under an LSM 700 confocal microscope. For quantitative analysis of immunohistochemical analysis, images were captured by tile capture method for merging whole section and counted using Qupath software.

Tissue RNA isolation, library preparation, and sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from 5-d-old (5D) testes (only displayed in additional file 3), GF and FO tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation System (Agilent Technologies, USA), and RNA quantification was performed using a Qubit (Thermo Fisher). A library was constructed using the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Lexogen, Austria) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for control and test RNAs. In brief, total RNA from each sample was prepared. An oligo-dT primer containing an Illumina-compatible sequence at its 5’ end was hybridized to the RNA, and reverse transcription was performed. After degradation of the RNA template, second strand synthesis was initiated by a random primer containing an Illumina compatible linker sequence at its 5’ end. The double-stranded library was purified using magnetic beads to remove all reaction components. The library was then amplified to add complete adapter sequences required for cluster generation, and the final library was purified from the PCR components. High-throughput single-end 75 sequencing was performed using a NextSeq 550 (Illumina, USA).

Data analysis

Quality control of the raw sequencing data was performed using Fast QC23. QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq reads were aligned using the Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) program24. STAR indices were generated from either the genome assembly sequence or representative transcript sequences for alignment to the genome and transcriptome. The alignment file was used to assemble transcripts, estimate their abundance, and detect differential gene expressions (DEGs).

DEGs were determined based on counts from unique and multiple alignments using coverage from HTSeq counts25. The Read Count (RC) data were processed based on the Trimmed Mean of M-values + Counts per million (TMM + CPM) normalization using the EdgeR method and DEseq2 (v1.42.0)26. Gene classification was based on searches performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID; http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/) and Medline databases (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Data mining and graphic visualization were performed using ExDEGA (Ebiogen, Seoul, Korea) and Rstudio (v4.3.1). The heatmap and Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) plot were constructed using ComplexHeatmp (v2.16.0) and ClusterProfiler (v4.8.2).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism was used for data analysis. All data are presented as the mean ± standard errors of the mean (SEM). A t-test was used for statistical testing between the control and the sample. The null hypothesis was rejected, when the probability was < 0.05.

Results

Characterization of pSSCs and SEF feeder cells used for xenograft

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) was performed to characterize pSSCs and SEF feeder cells before injection. The pSSC colonies were positively stained for UCHL1 and DDX4, while SOX9 expression was observed in SEF feeder cells but not in the colonies (Fig. 1a). After isolating pSSC colonies from SEF feeder cells, RT PCR and qPCR were performed to verify their characteristics and separation efficiency. UCHL1 was observed in both pSSCs and SEF feeder cells. The gene expression of the SSC marker CD90 and GFRα1 were not identified in SEF feeder cells but were observed in pSSCs (Fig. 1b). However, the expressions of WT1 and SOX9, which are markers of Sertoli cells, were observed mainly in SEF feeder cells, while lower expression was identified in pSSCs (Fig. 1b). In addition, qPCR data revealed a significant increase in CD90 (p < 0.001) expression in pSSCs, whereas notable differences in WT1 (p < 0.001) and SOX9 (p < 0.001) expression were observed in SEF feeder cells, with fold changes of 3.7 and 6.4, respectively (Fig. 1c). To validate the characteristics of SEF feeder cells and pSSC colonies, gene expression relative to pTTC was assessed by RT- and qPCR. In SEF feeder cells, bands of PDGFRA (stromal cell marker), CD31 (PECAM1; vascular endothelial cell marker), ACTA2 (α SMA; peritubular myoid cell marker), STAR (Leydig cell marker), HSD3B1 (Leydig cell marker), SOX90, and WT1 were observed, but PDGFRA, CD31, STAR, HSD3B1 were relatively lower than pTTCs (Fig. 1d). In qPCR, expression of genes representing testicular somatic cell components showed the following patterns: PDGFRA, no significant difference; CD31, significantly decreased (p < 0.05); ACTA2, no significant difference; Leydig cell–related genes STAR and HSD3B1, significantly decreased (p < 0.001); SOX9, no significant difference; WT1, significantly decreased (p < 0.05; Fig. 1e). In pSSC colonies, UCHL1 (p < 0.001), CD90 (p < 0.001), CD9 (p < 0.001), and GFRA1 (p < 0.05) were significantly increased relative to pTTC (Fig. 1f). Additionally, verification of the absence of differentiation during in vitro culture of germ cells is presented in additional file 1d. Flowcytometry further showed that the CD90-positive population in pSSC colonies was approximately 75%, significantly higher than in pTTC (Fig. 1g), gating strategy is presented in additional file 2. These cells were subsequently injected into the dorsal skin of 4-week-old BALB/c nude mice.

Formation and histological characterization of xenograft tissues

Six months after testicular cell transplantation into immunodeficient mice, reconstructed tissues were extracted from the dorsal skin. Recovery rates of GF and FO tissues were listed in Table 3. GF tissues showed the development of blood vessels (red arrows) between the mouse skin and the reconstructed tissues (Fig. 2a and b). Although FO tissues also exhibited development of blood vessels, it was less extensive (Fig. 2a and b). Particularly, the GF tissues displayed a three-dimensional spherical morphology, whereas the FO tissues had a flattened and oval configuration (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, GF tissues formed a single cohesive structure (white arrows) following cell injection, whereas FO tissues formed two separate structures (yellow arrows) after each injection (Fig. 2a). Additionally, blood vessels persisted on the surface of GF tissues following isolation and washing but were fewer on FO tissues (Fig. 2b).

H&E-stained GF tissues revealed prepubertal and mature testicular organization, featuring seminiferous tubule-like structures of various sizes. These tubules were clearly distinguishable from the surrounding interstitial area (Fig. 2c and d). A group of these seminiferous tubules, along with their interstitial areas, was demarcated by tunica albuginea-like tissue (TAL; Fig. 2c). In addition, Sertoli cells were aligned along the basement membrane, similar to the typical testis structure, as shown at high magnification (Fig. 2c and d). Among the different tubule sizes, the most dilated tubules exhibited a mature-like structure, including a well-developed lumen within the germinal epithelium. These tubules consisted of a complex germinal epithelium and a thick basement membrane (Fig. 2d).

FO tissues showed a more heterogeneous structure than GF tissues, with simple tubular development (Fig. 2e-g). The central region of the FO tissues exhibited other types of tissues in addition to the tubular shape (Fig. 2e-g). Moreover, none of the circular structures formed a pattern of normal seminiferous tubules or interstitial areas (Fig. 2e-g). The outer lining of the simple tubules was composed of cells that lacked the basement membrane typically found in seminiferous tubules. In addition, the inner mass of these tubules appeared to be separated from the outer lining (Fig. 2e-g).

At 100× magnification, counts of tubule clusters indicated that GF tissues contained more total tubules than FO tissues (p < 0.001; Fig. 2h). Both small (<100 µm diameter) and large (≥100 µm) tubular structures were also significantly more frequent in GF (p < 0.01). Although the absolute number of testis-like tubules was higher in GF, the proportion of tubules with empty lumina did not differ between groups (Fig. 2i). Mature-like tubules with well-defined lumens were composed of about 24% (Fig. 2i).

Comparison between GF tissues and normal testis

A broader interstitial area containing interstitial cells was observed in 5D testes. Here, Sertoli cells and germ cells were attached to the basement membrane of the tubules (Fig. 3a). Notably, spermatogonia were located on both the basement membrane and germinal epithelium (Fig. 3a). Similar to the 5D testes, a wide range of interstitial areas with interstitial cells was also observed in GF tissues. Sertoli cells along with spermatogonia were identified within the basement membrane, with spermatogonia further located in the germinal epithelium (Fig. 3b). The expanded tubules and interstitial area were relatively reduced, and fully integrated spermatogonia located between Sertoli cells were identified on the basement membrane in both the 90-d-old (90D) testes and GF tissues (Fig. 3c and d). Some GF tissue sections exhibited mature seminiferous tubule morphology. Compared with fully mature 150-d-old (150D) testes, GF tissue showed an increase in the cell population within the germinal epithelium; spermatogonia were positioned along the basement membrane (Fig. 3e and f). Moreover, both differentiating germ cells and round spermatids were observed within the germinal epithelium, while elongated spermatids were detected at the boundary between the germinal epithelium and lumen in both the 150D testis and GF tissue (Fig. 3e and f).

Discussion

A previous study reported that a high germ cell proportion (3–25%) in Matrigel promoted seminiferous tubule formation, with no added benefit at higher ratios (> 25%) (14). Based on this, we used a germ cell proportion of 10% of total cells, which lies within the optimal range. In this study, pSSC-enriched colony cells showed increased mRNA levels of UCHL1, CD90, CD9, and GFRA1 relative to pTTCs, and flowcytometry confirmed a high frequency of CD90-positive cells, indicating enrichment of pSSC features. In line with previous reports, colony-formed pSSCs were enriched to obtain cells with high self-renewal capacity and stemness, as indicated by elevated expression of UCHL1, PLZF, OCT4, NANOG, and GFRA127,28. Compared with freshly isolated germ cells, colony-forming pSSCs engage more robustly with testicular somatic cells and constitute a more homogeneous population, which may improve the reproducibility and efficiency of testicular tissue reconstruction. Characterization of SEF feeder cells showed no CD90 and CD9 expression, indicating that germ cells were not included in this fraction. Testicular somatic cells have been described as comprising Sertoli cells, peritubular myoid cells, Leydig cells, and stromal/interstitial cells29. To define these cellular properties, expressions of PDGFRA, CD31, ACTA2, STAR, HSD3B1, SOX9, and WT1 were detected in the SEF feeder cells. Because adherent cells were selectively collected during the attachment step, relative enrichment of stromal cells, vascular endothelial cells, and Sertoli cells and decrease of Leydig cells were observed. Accordingly, transplantation in this study was performed under somatic-cell conditions characterized by relatively fewer Leydig and endothelial cells, with relative enrichment of stromal cells, peritubular myoid cells, and Sertoli cells. A modified StemPro34 medium, optimized for the maintenance of porcine spermatogonial stem cells, was used as the basal medium to enhance germ cell survival16,27. This differs from earlier studies that employed DMEM or DMEM/F12 as the basal medium9,10,14,17. Further, the medium was supplemented with EGF, FGF2, LIF, and GDNF to support the survival of spermatogonia and facilitate early aggregation. Notably, Recent studies have shown that these growth factors, when combined, facilitate the initiation of testis cord formation during the early stages of reconstruction15,30.

The reconstructed GF tissues exhibited active vascularization and formed large, spherical grafts with well-organized tubule-like structures, indicating that germ cells promote testicular morphogenesis. This aligns with earlier findings that immature testicular cell grafts promote vascular ingrowth31. In contrast, FO tissues showed limited vascularization and developed into fragmented, small grafts (14 grafts from 8 injections) composed of simple or pseudo-tubules. DEG analysis revealed strong enrichment of coagulation pathways, which showed the highest NES among FO-associated signatures and are typically linked to tissue injury, inflammatory repair responses, and progression toward fibrosis. FO tissues also exhibited selective upregulation of ECM-related genes compared with 5-d-old testes (Additional file 3D). This likely reflects the high baseline angiogenic activity of neonatal testes, where vascular- and angiogenesis-related pathways are already maximally active, making ECM remodeling the predominant detectable change. Morphologically, FO grafts resembled Sertoli Cell-Only Syndrome (SCOS) testes32, and germ cell–depleted mouse testes33, as their transcriptome profiles revealed upregulation of inflammation- and apoptosis-related genes34. Collectively, these results underscore the indispensable role of germ cells to somatic coordination required for proper angiogenesis and testicular morphogenesis.

Although several studies have described the morphological reconstruction and partial spermatogenesis following dorsal skin transplantation of testicular cells9,12,14, evidence for sequential spermatogenesis remains limited. In this study, GF tissues displayed organized seminiferous tubules with appropriate positioning of spermatogonia, and germ cells spanning spermatocytes to spermatids were identified. The spatial distribution of germ and Sertoli cells resembled that of intact porcine testes. Strong DDX4 expression supported ongoing spermatogenic progression from undifferentiated spermatogonia to mature spermatids35,36. Detection of ACR, a marker of spermiogenesis, further indicated the presence of spermatids undergoing Golgi, cap, and acrosome phases37. Furthermore, PRM2, a key component involved in the final stage of spermiogenesis38, were detected in GF tissue. These findings validate the functional reconstruction of the xenografted tissue and highlight the essential role of pSSCs in establishing a competent testicular microenvironment.

To investigate the formation of a functional germ cell-Sertoli cell niche, Sertoli cell characterization was conducted between GF and FO tissues. FO tissues exhibited pronounced defects in Sertoli cell arrangement, polarity, and function, highlighting the challenges of maintaining testicular architecture. Although WT1, an early gonadal marker known to support NR5A1 expression for testis development39–41, was still expressed in both GF and FO tissues, other key regulators of Sertoli cell identity such as SOX9, AR, DMRT1, and NR5A142–45 showed abnormal localization or diminished expression in FO tissues. This is consistent with earlier reports of disorder of sex development (DSD) showing that loss of germ cell-Sertoli cell interactions during early testis development results in disorganized tubule formation and reduced Sertoli cell specialization46,47. Furthermore, this observation is consistent with germ cell-depleted mouse models, in which loss of germ cells alters Sertoli cell transcriptional programs and produces an abnormal, non-cycling Sertoli state distinct from that in germ cell-sufficient testes33,48,49. Specifically, Similar to germ cell–deficient mouse models in which PGC specification is impaired Blimp1/Prdm1 or Prdm14 mutants50,51, the FO xenografts in this study represent a germ-cell–free testicular environment from the outset. The most notable results were FOXL2 and ESR1 in FO tissues, Ectopic FOXL2 expression in FO tissues further supports a shift toward a feminized somatic fate, as FOXL2 upregulation in XY gonads or postnatal testes is a hallmark of male-to-female reprogramming following disruption of DMRT1 or SOX9 function52,53. To our knowledge, this appears to be the first report of FOXL2 expression in the absence of germ cells in porcine species. In parallel, ESR1 expression in FO tissues suggests increased sensitivity to estrogen signaling, which has been shown to be mediated by nuclear ESR1 in Sertoli cells and to modulate Sertoli transcriptional activity54,55.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis corroborated the morphological findings. GF tissues showed strong enrichment of cell cycle and spermatogenesis-related pathways, and stage-specific germ cell signatures were evident in the heatmap. CNET analysis confirmed high expression of mature spermatid genes (ACR, ACRBP, PRM1, PRM2), consistent with active spermatogenesis56. PCA revealed dispersed GF samples and tightly clustered FO samples, indicating developmental progression versus uniform arrest (Additional file 3a). Correlation analysis further positioned 5D testes between GF and FO tissues (Additional file 3c). Notably, GF1 and GF4 of RNA samples, which displayed less advanced tubule morphology, also occupied intermediate transcriptomic positions, suggesting partial rather than complete maturation. These results indicate that reconstruction of functional testis-like tissues depends not solely on transplantation duration, but on the early establishment of an appropriate germ cell-Sertoli cell niche formation.

GOBP analysis revealed that GF tissues were significantly enriched for Sertoli cell-specific biological processes, including those involving NR5A1, AMH, AR, and DMRT1 This enrichment reflects the successful activation of male developmental pathways and the establishment of a well-organized testicular architecture. In contrast, FO tissues lacked such testis-related enrichment and instead showed upregulation of genes involved in early developmental pathways. Notably, genes such as BMP2, BMP4, WNT5A, TGFBR3, ACVR1, FOXL2 and ACVR2B, which are typically repressed during male gonad differentiation57,58, were prominently expressed in FO tissues, indicating a failure to suppress developmental pathways incompatible with testis formation. This dysregulation is closely linked to the absence of germ cells, which are known to promote SOX9 activation via FGF signaling, suppression of feminization pathways through DMRT1, and inhibition of TGFβ/activin signaling55,59. These molecular features reinforce the conserved role of germ cells in orchestrating Sertoli cell maturation and testis formation across species20, highlighting their indispensable function in establishing a male-specific gonadal environment. Collectively, these observations provide a critical molecular criterion for distinguishing true testis-like structures from simple or pseudo-tubular formations in in vitro testis generation.

Collectively, preserving and incorporating spermatogonia into xenograft models will therefore be essential for producing functional, transplantable testis-like structures and for enabling scale-up in future applications. In this context, we expect that our findings will provide useful insights for overcoming current challenges in testis reconstruction for in vitro spermatogenesis, including incomplete seminiferous tubule formation and germ cell loss. This was achieved by combining pre-conditioned pSSCs with an optimized medium containing high-concentration Matrigel. These results have important implications for livestock biotechnology, particularly for advancing in vitro spermatogenesis techniques that support reproductive efficiency, preservation of superior genetic traits, and the generation of transgenic large animals for agricultural and biomedical purposes.

Nevertheless, the partial success in testis morphogenesis and spermatid development observed in this study, several limitations remain. Although our data are statistically significant, the relatively small sample size necessitates cautious interpretation when extrapolating the results. Leydig cell immunostaining was attempted; however, the signals were inconsistent, possibly due to antibody compatibility with xenografted porcine tissue. Detailed characterization of spermatid subtypes was not conducted, consistent control over developmental stages was not achieved, and the fertilizing ability of the sperm, as well as their capacity to support embryo development, has yet to be confirmed. Further studies are also needed to clarify how germ cell deficiency contributes to gonadal dysgenesis, which will be crucial for optimizing germ cell-somatic cell interactions in future testis reconstruction platforms.

Conclusion

During the xenotransplantation experiments, the combination of porcine testicular cells containing colony-forming pSSCs with modified StemPro-34 (+EGF, FGF, GDNF, LIF) and concentrated Matrigel resulted in the successful assembly of tissue that closely resembled the native porcine testis. Germ cells played an indispensable and central role in influencing the alignment of somatic cells and the formation of seminiferous tubules. Without the pSSCs, the grafts consistently failed to organize like SOCS or DSD models, clearly demonstrating the fundamental dependence of organized in vitro testis formation and development on germ cell input. The xenograft model successfully reproduced key structural and cellular patterns observed in vivo, thereby providing clear criteria to distinguish true testis-like structures from pseudo-tubules, making it a practical framework for studies in livestock reproduction. This work establishes a practical framework for studies in livestock reproduction and could be used to address challenges such as fertility recovery, preservation of superior genetic lines, and the generation of genetically modified animals.
