Effect of Contextual Information on Speech-In-Noise Perception among Young and Older Adults with Normal Hearing Sensitivity: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Ritik Roushan, Mohan Kumar Kalaiah, Usha Shastri, Kaushlendra Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:This study aimed to investigate the effect of contextual information on speech-in-noise perception among young and older adults with normal hearing sensitivity.Method:A comprehensive search was carried out in electronic databases, that is, Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and Embase, to identify relevant studies exploring the effects of context on speech-in-noise perception among younger and older adults. Studies comparing the perception of high- and low-context sentences in noise between young and older adults were included. Twenty studies involving 840 participants were included in the analysis. A mixed-method appraisal tool was employed for quality appraisal.Results:Multitalker babble was found to be more challenging than other background noises. In high- and low-context conditions, young adults outperformed older adults. However, older adults benefited more from contextual cues. Meta-analysis showed that at moderate signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs), contextual information more significantly improved speech-in-noise perception in older adults compared with young adults (Cohen’s d = 0.30, 95% CI [0.09, 0.51]. Effect sizes were larger but non-significant at low (Cohen’s d = 0.66, 95% CI [–0.30, 1.61] and high (Cohen’s d = 1.34, 95% CI [–0.64, 3.33] SNRs because of high heterogeneity.Conclusion:This systematic review revealed many studies reporting greater contextual benefit for older adults and few studies showing comparable or reduced benefit for younger adults. The meta-analysis findings revealed greater benefits at moderate SNRs among older adults.

INTRODUCTION

Speech-in-noise perception is a “complex process that incorporates detecting spectro–temporal cues for speech identification and separating signals from background noise.”[1] It is affected by several factors, namely, age, hearing sensitivity, hearing loss type, auditory processing ability, cognitive abilities, redundancy or contextual information in speech, noise type, noise level, and reverberation.[23] Aging negatively influences speech perception, especially in the presence of noise. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed that age-related structural changes in the auditory cortex and executive-control regions significantly contribute to reduced speech-in-noise perception, even when peripheral hearing thresholds are normal.[4] The poor speech perception in older adults has been attributed to their poor suprathreshold auditory processing and cognitive deficits[3] and is well documented across different noises.[5678] Research comparing energetic masker and informational masker found that the latter introduces more perceptual challenges due to energetic overlap and linguistic interference.[49] This effect underscores the complexity of real-world listening.[9] In the presence of noise, older adults require a greater signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) to match the performance of young adults.[1011] Cognitive abilities also influence speech-in-noise perception among older adults.[121314] Cognition plays a critical role: working memory (WM) supports speech comprehension under energetic masking, while processing speed and neural connectivity facilitate the handling of informational masking.[15] Studies revealed good speech-in-noise perception abilities among individuals with superior cognitive functions.[12161718] Contextual information or redundancy in speech is useful for understanding speech in adverse listening conditions such as noise and reverberation.[19] The facilitatory effect of contextual information on speech perception has been widely reported.[82021]

Context is a general term for cues that include linguistic elements such as meaning, vocabulary, sentence composition, speech rate, and emotional content. It helps people understand speech by narrowing the search space in the lexicon,[12] predicting missing information,[22] accelerating word retrieval,[23] mitigating noise interference,[12] and addressing lexicon uncertainty.[22] The extent to which a person can utilize contextual information for speech-in-noise perception depends on their auditory–cognitive abilities, which include peripheral hearing,[24] spectro–temporal precision,[25] WM/short-term memory,[26] semantic long-term memory,[14] vocabulary knowledge as a component of crystal intelligence,[27] background noise, and SNR.[2829] Studies investigating the effect of contextual information on speech perception applied varying degrees of contextual information available in sentences to measure its effect on speech perception.[11] According to the extent of context, sentences could be of high or low context.

Pichora–Fuller[10] investigated how age affects the perception of predictable and unpredictable final words in sentences presented with babble noise among young adults with normal hearing and older adults with near-normal hearing or hearing loss. The results showed that older adults benefited more from contextual cues than younger adults, possibly due to the former’s greater reliance on cognitive resources during speech-in-noise perception. Several studies explored the influence of cognitive processes on older adults’ speech-in-noise perception and observed that vocabulary and linguistic knowledge, provided by crystallized intelligence to form and employ context, are conserved and even enhanced with age.[17] Thus, older adults are adept at using context to compensate for communication challenges in everyday listening. Conversely, utilizing context involves cognitive abilities such as WM,[12] which may decline with age. WM is a limited-capacity process that helps in storing, processing, and manipulating the information required to complete complex tasks.[30] It supports listeners by using context to refine the interpretation of speech semantics and speed up semantic integration[31] and helps compensate for the increased processing demands when the quality of the speech signal deteriorates.[121314]

Many studies investigated the effect of contextual information on speech-in-noise perception among young and older adults.[82021] Comparison of findings across investigations revealed mixed results.[14] Several studies found that older adults derive greater benefit from contextual information for speech-in-noise perception than young adults,[3233] whereas a few reported the opposite.[8] Others stated that young and older adults derive similar benefits from contextual information. These discrepancies in the findings could arise from differences in methodologies across investigations, such as differences in stimuli, noise type, and SNR. Despite the growing interest, a systematic effort to synthesize these findings and resolve the inconsistency is lacking. To our knowledge, no prior review has quantitatively examined how contextual information influences speech perception in older adults across noise conditions and SNR levels. Thus, the present systematic review and meta-analysis were carried out to address this gap and compare the benefit of contextual information for speech-in-noise perception between young and older adults.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This systematic review was carried out in accordance with the guidelines outlined by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).[34]

Search Strategy

A systematic search of literature in electronic databases PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase was conducted in May 2024 to identify eligible studies investigating the effect of contextual information on speech perception in young and older adults. Search terms related to contextual information and speech perception were combined to optimize the results. Keywords included speech, perception, recognition, understanding, noise, context, predictable, and adult. The search string used was (“speech” AND (“perception” OR “recognition” OR “understand”) AND (“noise”) AND (“context” OR “predictable”) AND (“Adult”). No date restrictions were applied. The search was not limited to studies published only in the English language. Grey literature and unpublished sources were not included. Additionally, the reference lists of the included studies were manually searched to identify additional articles relevant to the study. The review protocol was not preregistered in PROSPERO or any other similar platforms. However, all methodological decisions, such as inclusion and exclusion criteria, screening process, and data analysis plans, were made prior to and followed throughout the study to maintain transparency and reproducibility.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were based on the PECO format.[35]

Participants

Studies that selected young and older adults with hearing sensitivity within normal limits as participants were included. The age criteria for young and older adults were 18–40 years and >55 years, respectively. Defining older adults with a broad age range allowed for the inclusion of a wide range of studies and ensured adequate representation of older adults across different methodologies. Studies recruiting young or older adults alone were also included. Those focusing on children only, adults with hearing impairment only, and hearing aid users or cochlear implant users only, and those combining young and older adults into a single undifferentiated group (e.g., 18–75 years) were excluded. However, studies that separately reported data for young and older adults were included.

Exposure

Studies investigating the perception of sentences in noise at one or more SNRs were included. Noise refers to any disruptive sound that masks speech, such as white noise, speech-shaped noise (SSN), filtered noise, speech babbles, and competing speech. Studies measuring perception of speech in noise using degraded speech, such as vocoded speech and jittered speech, were excluded.

Comparator

Studies comparing the perception of high- and low-context sentences were included. High-context sentences are semantically and syntactically accurate, whereas low-context sentences are semantically inaccurate and syntactically accurate. Studies comparing the perception of high- and low-context sentences in at least one SNR were also included.

Outcome

Studies reporting speech-in-noise perception findings as correct identification scores or SNR-50 were included. These outcome measures were reported separately for high- and low-context conditions among young and older adults.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

All studies identified across the databases were uploaded into Rayyan software (Rayyan Systems Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA), where duplicate entries were identified and removed. Two researchers (Ritik Roushan and Mohan Kumar Kalaiah) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all the studies to assess their relevance for this review. Disagreements were resolved through discussion between the researchers (Ritik Roushan and Mohan Kumar Kalaiah). If consensus could not be reached, a third researcher (Usha Shastri) was consulted. Studies deviating from the objective of this review were excluded. The full text of the remaining studies was then reviewed, and suitable studies were selected in accordance with the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria comprised original articles reporting perception of high- and low-context sentences in noise, studies involving adults with hearing sensitivity within normal limits as participants, and studies written in English. However, relevant articles were not found in other languages. The exclusion criteria included case reports, case series, review articles, questionnaire-based studies, studies involving only individuals with hearing impairment as participants, studies using stimuli such as vocoded sentences or jittered sentences only, and studies lacking speech perception scores for high- and low-context sentences. The data extraction form was created in Microsoft Excel, version 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and prepared specifically to meet this review’s objective. From each article, general information related to the study (author, year, and country), study characteristics (study design and sample size), and data related to PECO were initially extracted by the first author (Ritik Roushan). The extracted data were then cross-verified by the second author (Mohan Kumar Kalaiah), who also participated in the title and abstract screening. Any discrepancies were resolved together through discussion, and a third author (Usha Shastri) was consulted when necessary. Additional information extracted from the included studies comprised the age of participants, number of participants, test material used for speech-in-noise perception, type of background noise, SNRs, identification score in high and low contexts, SNR-50 in high and low contexts, and mean difference (MD) among high and low contexts. The mean and standard deviation of the outcome measure were extracted from each study. When data were presented graphically, the necessary numerical data were extracted from the graphs using WebPlot Digitizer (https://automeris.io/WebPlotDigitizer/). The data extracted from the studies are presented in tables in the results section.

Quality Appraisal

The mixed methods appraisal tool (MMAT) was used to determine the quality of the studies included in this systematic review. It consists of a five-category framework, with each category containing five methodological quality criteria. Each criterion is assessed as either “yes” (met), “no” (not met), or “cannot tell” (insufficient information for judgment).[36] The key aspects assessed by the tool include the relevance of the sampling strategy, representativeness of the sample, measurement of variables, risk of non-response bias, and appropriateness of statistical analysis. Two researchers (Ritik Roushan and Mohan Kumar Kalaiah) appraised all the selected studies using this checklist. The MMAT results were used to describe the overall methodological quality of the included studies, but not to exclude any studies from the synthesis or meta-analysis.

Data Synthesis

A total of 1248 studies were identified through the initial literature search across various databases. After 666 duplicate entries were removed, 582 studies were available for title and abstract screening. Following this screening, 486 studies were excluded, and the remaining 96 studies were selected for full-text review. Finally, 22 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in this systematic review. Figure 1 illustrates a summary of the study selection process employed in this review. Qualitative analysis was performed on the 22 studies that met the inclusion criteria. It included a descriptive summary of the included studies in tables, which are organized into studies investigating the effect of context on speech-in-noise perception among young and older adults. The tables include the place of the study, age of the participants, type of sentence material used, background noise, SNRs, context-specific scores, and MD scores.

Data Analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted to compare the effect of contextual information on speech-in-noise perception between young and older adults. The SNRs across the studies were grouped into three categories: low (−5 dB), moderate (0 dB), and high SNR (+5 dB). The scores of participants at −5 and −4 dB were included in the low SNR category, 0 dB in the moderate SNR category, and +4 and +5 dB in the high SNR category. Of the 22 studies considered for the systematic review, five met the criteria for meta-analysis. Studies reporting speech identification scores for high- and low-context sentences at one or more SNRs for young and older adults were eligible. PLT software (BTNRH PLT) version 5.16 was used to generate the figure. The meta-analysis was carried out using Jamovi software version 2.6.25 using the random effects model. The MD with a 95% confidence interval (CI) for continuous data was estimated using the MAJOR module in Jamovi. The effect size (Cohen’s d) of each study was calculated using the practical meta-analysis effect size calculator[37] based on the mean and standard deviation for speech identification score in high and low contexts for young and older adults. The effect size was categorized as trivial (<0.2), small (0.2–0.49), medium (0.5–0.79), and large (≥0.8). Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic and the I2 test. The Q test evaluates whether the variation in observed effect sizes is greater than expected by chance, and I2 quantifies the percentage of total variability due to true heterogeneity rather than sampling error. On the basis of the heterogeneity, the random-effects model was applied to estimate pooled effect sizes, 95% CIs, and P-values. Owing to the small number of studies included in the meta-analysis, the publication bias test was not formally assessed using funnel plots or statistical tests (e.g., Egger’s test). The studies included in the meta-analysis employed two types of noise, that is, speech babble and steady-state noise, as the background noise. A meta-analysis for different high and low SNR conditions could not be carried out because of the small number of studies having similar SNRs.

RESULTS

Study Characteristics

All studies included in this review used a cross-sectional study design and were published between 1988 and 2024. Fifteen studies were conducted in the United States, three in Canada, three in Europe, and one in China. The majority of the studies measured speech identification score in noise, and three used SNR-50 as an outcome measure. Out of the 19 studies measuring speech identification scores, nine included only young adults as participants, two included only older adults as participants, and the remaining eight included both as participants in separate groups. Among the studies measuring SNR-50, one included only young adults, and two included young and older adults as participants. Additionally, the studies used different sentence materials, background noise, and SNRs to examine the effect of contextual information on speech-in-noise perception. The sentence materials used across the studies include revised speech perception in noise test (R-SPIN), German SPIN, basic English lexicon sentences, Boothroyd and Nittrouer’s sentences, repeat–recall test, Hagerman sentences, Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) sentences, hearing in noise test (HINT) sentences, nonsense sentences, and unrelated sentences. However, the most frequently used sentence material was R-SPIN (n = 8). Commonly used noise was speech babble (n = 13), followed by SSN (n = 5), modulated SSN (n = 2), Gaussian noise (n = 2), and high-pass noise (n = 1). The SNRs used were between −6 and +20 dB. Tables 1–4 display the characteristics of the studies included in this review.

1 shows a list of studies where only young adults served as participants. The SNRs used across these studies ranged from −5 dB to +20 dB. Regarding background noise, five studies used speech babble, and two studies each used white noise and SSN. The results of all the studies showed better speech identification scores for high-context sentences compared with low-context sentences across background noises and SNRs. In the low-context condition, the identification scores ranged from 9.5% to 75.63% across the studies; in the high-context condition, the scores were between 19.5% and 99.2%. Synthesis of Qualitative Findings

Qualitative analysis of the 22 studies revealed that high-context sentences consistently led to better speech-in-noise perception than low-context sentences across young and older adults. The degree of improvement varied according to background noise type and SNR. Speech babble was identified as a more challenging masker than steady-state or Gaussian noise, but also allowed for greater use of contextual cues. Contextual benefit was the highest at moderate SNRs (e.g., 0 to +4 dB) and tended to decline at very low or high SNRs. Although young adults generally performed better in both contexts, older adults showed relatively greater benefit from context, supporting the idea that top-down processing compensates for age-related auditory decline. However, discrepancies across the studies arise due to methodological variability, such as differences in stimulus type, SNR conditions, and outcome measures (e.g., identification score vs. SNR-50).

Among the 22 studies selected for systematic review, only eight consisted of young and older adults as participants in separate groups. Among them, one study did not have sufficient data to calculate the effect size, and two studies used very high SNRs ≥16 dB. Thus, only five studies were included for the meta-analysis and used as the basis for the quantitative analysis. Tests of publication bias were not performed since few studies were available for the meta-analysis.[57]

Meta-Analysis

The benefit of context on speech-in-noise perception score was compared at three SNR categories (i.e., low, moderate, and high). A meta-analysis was carried out separately for these three SNR categories. The results showed that contextual information significantly improved speech-in-noise perception in older adults compared with that in young adults, but only at moderate SNRs (Cohen’s d = 0.30, 95% CI [0.09, 0.51], P = 0.005). Furthermore, medium and large effects were present at low (Cohen’s d = 0.66, 95% CI [−0.30, 1.61], P = 0.177) and high (Cohen’s d = 1.34, 95% CI [−0.64, 3.33], P = 0.185) SNRs. Table 6 shows the meta-analysis findings, and Figure 3 presents the forest plots. Heterogeneity test results showed no heterogeneity in effect sizes across the studies at moderate SNRs (I2 = 0.02%), but Cochran’s test was significant (Q (4) = 10.87, P = 0.028), suggesting possible between-study variability. By contrast, high heterogeneity in effect size across the studies was observed at low (I2 = 93.13%, Q (4) = 22.47, P < 0.001) and high (I2 = 97.52%, Q (2) = 19.66, P <0.001) SNRs, indicating inconsistency in study outcomes. The forest plot showed that the high heterogeneity could be due to the large effect size in two studies[2252] at low SNRs [Figure 3a], and one study[22] at high SNRs [Figure 3c].

Subgroup Analysis

The studies reported a wide range of methodological differences, possibly leading to the high heterogeneity at low and high SNRs. Thus, separate meta-analyses were conducted wherever possible to understand the influence of the type of background noise and studies with small sample sizes on the benefit of context for speech-in-noise perception.

1. Effect of Noise Type

A meta-analysis was performed separately for the studies reporting the effect of context on speech perception in SSN (low SNR). However, similar analysis could not be done in the presence of babble noise (high and low SNRs) and SSN at high SNRs because of the limited number of studies. For SSN, the result showed a moderate effect [112152] (d = 0.745), which was not significant (P = 0.33), and heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 95.1%). This finding suggests that noise type alone did not lead to the observed variability, and other methodological differences have possible contributions.

2. Effect of Sample Size

A meta-analysis was conducted to understand the effect of small sample sizes on low SNRs. The first analysis excluded Pichora–Fuller’s[10] study (n = 20) and showed a reduced effect size (from d = 0.66 to 0.455) and slightly increased heterogeneity (I2 = 94.36%). Similarly, excluding the Carter[52] study (n = 18) resulted in further reduction in effect size (d = 0.214) and heterogeneity (I2 = 81.97%). When both studies were removed simultaneously, the effect size became negligible and non-significant (d = 0.0264, P = 0.838), and the heterogeneity reduced drastically (I2 = 14.74%). These results suggest that the high heterogeneity could be attributed to the small sample size. Overall, the findings emphasize the influence of study characteristics, particularly sample size and noise type, on understanding the benefit of context for speech-in-noise perception.

DISCUSSION

This review was carried out to compare the benefit of contextual information for speech-in-noise perception between young and older adults. The studies demonstrated that contextual information improves speech-in-noise perception for young and older adults with normal hearing. Young adults showed higher overall speech perception ability than older adults. However, older adults derived a greater benefit from contextual information than young adults, particularly at moderate SNRs. Qualitative synthesis revealed that speech babble presented a greater challenge to speech perception compared with other noise types, yet it facilitated a greater use of contextual information. These findings suggest that top-down linguistic knowledge plays a compensatory role in supporting speech understanding in older adults under adverse listening conditions.[58]

Context Benefit Between Groups

For the effect of context, data across the studies showed good speech-in-noise perception in the high-context condition for young and older adults. The results of several studies comparing the performance of young and older adults revealed that older adults derived greater benefit from context information when sentences were presented in noise. Other studies showed a similar or slightly less benefit in older adults. The greater benefit observed in older adults has been attributed to top-down processing during speech-in-noise perception.[58] In adverse listening conditions, older adults tend to compensate for degraded acoustic input by relying heavily on their linguistic knowledge, experience, and contextual cues to facilitate understanding.

This finding aligns with the research on top-down processing during speech-in-noise perception.[58] One reason for this greater contextual benefit may lie in crystal intelligence. Crystal intelligence, which provides vocabulary and linguistic knowledge to construct and utilize context information, is preserved and even improves with age.[27] Another reason for the greater benefit of context for older adults is that they are quite adept at using context because they rely on it to solve their daily communication difficulties.[59] This benefit from context supports the interactive compensatory hypothesis proposed by Stine–Morrow et al.,[60] which suggests that reliance on contextual information might be high among older adults to compensate for age-related cognitive declines.[60]

Noise Type and Context Benefit

The comparison of context-related benefit across background noise types revealed that the benefit was greater in speech babble than in steady-state noise for young and older adults. The improvement in sentence identification score in speech babble ranged from 3% to 44% for young adults and 7% to 52% for older adults across SNRs. In SSN, the improvement ranged from 2% to 27% for young adults and 6% to 27% for older adults across SNRs. In high-pass noise, the benefit was 7% for young adults and 2.9% for older adults.[53] These findings suggest that the benefit from context is higher in fluctuating noise than in steady noise. However, the findings of the two studies that used steady noise and fluctuating noise showed similar benefits in both noise conditions. The greater benefit of context in fluctuating noise could be attributed to the glimpsing of speech. In fluctuating noise, listeners can access brief acoustic glimpses of speech, facilitating the use of contextual cues during speech-in-noise perception. These findings show that the acoustic and linguistic features of the masker might affect the benefit derived from context during speech-in-noise perception.

Effect of SNR

The comparison of benefits obtained from context across SNRs showed a greater benefit at moderate SNRs compared with that at high and low SNRs for young and older adults across noise types. The benefit was similar at high and low SNRs. In speech babble, the improvement was 3%–44% for young adults and 7%–52% for older adults at moderate SNRs, 3%–19.5% for young adults and 2%–22.2% for older adults at high SNRs and 13%–24% for young adults and 4%–21% for older adults at low SNRs across the studies.[101149] Similarly, in SSN, the benefit was 6%–27% for young adults and 9%–22% for older adults at moderate SNRs, 2% for young adults and 6% older adults at high SNRs, and 9%–22% for young adults and 10%–27% for older adults at low SNRs.[101149] The low benefit at high SNRs could be due to the ceiling effect, leaving minimal scope for further improvement in scores due to context. At high SNRs, the speech is already clear and intelligible due to a favorable SNR; therefore, listeners primarily depend on bottom-up processing, and the added value of context is minimal.[1149] At high SNRs, the contribution of contextual information diminishes because the listener does not rely on top-down processing. Meanwhile, the low benefit at low SNRs could be due to the poor audibility of the speech. At low SNRs, the quality of the acoustic signal deteriorates to the extent that even fundamental speech elements such as phonemes or familiar words become hard to perceive. Under these conditions, contextual information cannot sufficiently compensate for the poor bottom-up input, leading to minimal gains in speech recognition, even in high-context conditions. Pichora–Fuller et al.[10] observed that when background noise masks speech to near unintelligibility, older adults showed a reduced ability to benefit from contextual information, largely because of the insufficient linguistic input to support top-down processing. Thus, the performance remains low regardless of context at low SNRs because the signal is too difficult to access.

Although this review focuses on individuals with normal hearing sensitivity, the findings also have implications for people with hearing impairment. Individuals with hearing loss may also derive greater benefit from contextual information in speech because of poorer audibility of speech and poorer auditory processing abilities. Future studies should investigate whether hearing-impaired populations exhibit comparable patterns of context-related benefit, which could help clinical interventions and rehabilitation strategies.

Meta-Analysis

Meta-analysis showed a small but significant effect for the improvement of speech identification score (i.e., benefit) between groups at moderate SNRs. The effect size was medium and large at low and high SNRs, respectively, but it was not significant. The greater effect size at high and low SNRs compared with that at moderate SNRs was not expected because the magnitude of improvement in speech identification score was low at low and high SNRs. This greater effect size could be due to the large heterogeneity in study outcomes. Additionally, the lack of significance for effect size could be attributed to the substantial heterogeneity in sample size, background noise, speech stimuli used, and study outcomes across the studies. Heterogeneity varied across SNR conditions. While I2 was negligible at moderate SNR, the Q test was statistically significant, indicating subtle variability among the studies. By contrast, I2 and Q were high at low and high SNRs, suggesting true differences in effect sizes across the studies. At low SNRs, listeners may rely on top-down processing, which can increase the benefit from context. However, the high variability in individual performance and study methods makes it difficult to detect consistent effects. At high SNRs, while context may still play a role, the ease of the listening task may reduce the difference between high- and low-context conditions. Overall, our analysis shows that older adults derived greater benefit from the contextual information present in speech compared with young adults; however, the benefit was significantly different only at moderate SNRs. The findings indicate that moderate SNRs provide the most reliable conditions for observing age-related differences in contextual processing during speech-in-noise perception. Subgroup analysis indicated that the studies with small sample sizes contributed substantially to the observed variability. Specifically, removing the studies with small sample sizes significantly reduced heterogeneity and varied the effect size. However, further subgroup analysis was not feasible because of the limited number of studies in the high SNR category. These findings underscore the importance of methodological consistency and sufficient sample size in studies examining the benefit of contextual information for speech perception. Additional studies are required to understand the effect of context on speech-in-noise perception at favorable and unfavorable SNR conditions among young and older adults.

Although this review provides a valuable understanding of the effect of contextual information on speech-in-noise perception, some limitations must be noted. Firstly, direct comparisons across the studies were challenging because of variations in their methods, that is, test materials, background noise types, sample size, study outcomes, and SNRs. This variability led to heterogeneity, which made it difficult to draw firm conclusions across the studies. For the assessment of context effects, future research should be planned to establish a standardized protocol that could help make good and easy comparisons. The second limitation is that publication bias could have also contributed to the heterogeneity. However, publication bias analysis was not performed because of the small number of studies available for the meta-analysis. Additionally, the exclusion of grey literature may have led to the omission of relevant research, potentially introducing publication bias. Future cross-sectional studies examining the cognitive mechanisms underlying age-related differences in the use of contextual information could provide deep insights and guide the development of targeted interventions.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis show how contextual information influences speech-in-noise perception among young and older adults with normal hearing. High-context sentences were consistently found to improve speech-in-noise perception for both age groups compared with low-context sentences. However, the extent of benefit varied depending on the SNR and type of background noise. Speech babble was found to be more challenging than other background noises.

Meta-analysis findings further confirmed that older adults derived significantly more benefit from contextual cues at moderate SNRs (0 dB), with a small but statistically significant effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.30). While large effect sizes were observed at low and high SNRs, they were not statistically significant because of the great variability and heterogeneity in study characteristics. These findings support the idea that top-down processing and cognitive processes, such as linguistic knowledge and crystal intelligence, help older adults understand speech in adverse listening conditions.
