Comment on “Association between the generation of cephalosporins for perioperative prophylaxis and postoperative surgical site infections in open fractures: a prospective cohort study”
Authors: Zimin Xiang, Lingzhi Ding, Zhaobo Zhang
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The soft tissue envelope in subsequent wounds exposes the fracture hematoma to contamination, making the management of open fractures an unsolvable challenge for orthopedists. High-energy trauma accounts for the majority of open fractures. Surgical site infection (SSI) is a concerning complication that commonly necessitates surgical revision and is a major cause of nonunion and loss of function following open fractures. About 0–70% of open fractures have bacterial contamination at the time of trauma[1,2]. In terms of economic costs, prior research found that during the 3- to 6-month period, after open fractures of the lower extremities, the average hospitalization costs for infected cases were higher, and 17% of nosocomial infections are SSI.

Currently, there are conflicting recommendations from numerous major guidelines regarding which generation of cephalosporin should be applied for the prevention of SSI. Yang et al[3] carried out a prospective cohort study to examine association between postoperative surgical site infections in open fractures and the production of cephalosporins for perioperative prophylaxis. There were 3582 eligible patients in all. Higher-generation cephalosporin usage during surgery was not linked to a decrease in SSIs following open fractures. The following are some of the factors that can explain this. First, higher-generation cephalosporins have poor efficacy in preventing SSI than first-generation cephalosporins because they theoretically have a narrower antibacterial range against gram-positive bacteria, which cause between 37.8% and 62.5% of all infections in open fractures. Second, in open fractures, bacteria exhibiting low sensitivity or even antibiotic resistance to high-generation cephalosporins accounts for about 27.3–82.6% of gram-negative infections. This stands in stark contrast to the antibiotic sensitivity of the same strains that do not exhibit resistance in the laboratory, indicating that the antibacterial spectrum verified in the laboratory is not reliable in actual clinical practice. Third, intravenous antibiotics are administered to tissues that have an adequate blood supply; however, with open fractures, this pathway is more likely to be disrupted in severely damaged blood vessels or tissues. In this case, the cephalosporins’ poor blood supply prevents them from achieving their theoretical antibacterial effect, even if the serum concentration of drug is significantly higher than the minimum inhibitory concentration.

There are several limitations on this study. First, because of inherent differences within each generation, the percentage of cephalosporins was not evenly distributed across generations, which could influence the precision of interpreting the exposure–outcome relationship. Second, there could be a number of unidentified or unmeasured factors that contribute to residual confounding. Third, the statistical power might be diminished owing to the small sample size. Finally, this single-center design of the study might restrict its applicability to different populations and medical center. To validate the current findings and clarify the pathophysiological or biological mechanisms, more high-quality trials are required.
