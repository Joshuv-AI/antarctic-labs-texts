Mobilization Techniques for Acute and Chronic Retracted Pectoralis Major Tears for Primary Repair
Authors: Ali Ahmadi Pirshahid, Steven Villani, Marie-Eve LeBel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPectoralis major (PM) tears are uncommon yet increasing in incidence in the athletic population. Previous research has favored operative management of both acute and chronic PM tears. Primary repair is indicated for both acute and chronic tears, when possible, to improve functional outcomes and cosmesis. Often, lack of viable tissue and inadequate tendon length make primary repair challenging, especially with increased chronicity and increased retraction. Autograft and allograft tendon use is described to bridge a residual gap between tendinous tissue and the footprint or to augment tissue quality. However, to our knowledge, the literature is scarce regarding detailed technical tips and tricks on how to specifically mobilize a chronically torn and/or markedly retracted PM. This article clearly describes and depicts the step-by-step approach and surgical technique for mobilizing a markedly retracted and/or chronically torn PM. It also describes and shows how to use and position an allograft patch if needed.

Pectoralis major (PM) tears are uncommon but are seen increasingly. They usually occur with forceful eccentric muscle contraction.1, 2, 3, 4 They can present with bony avulsion or intra-muscular tear but are more frequently encountered at the tendon-muscle junction or intratendinous at the humeral insertion.5, 6, 7

For better outcomes, surgical repair remains the prime treatment for acute tears (<6 weeks) in the active population.1,3,8,9 Several studies have shown superiority of surgical outcomes for cosmesis, pain relief, return to activity, and overall satisfaction.2,4,10,11

Most chronic tears (≥6 weeks) respond well to primary tendon repair without needing autografts or allografts.1, 2, 3,12 In the current literature, the specific technical points for tensionless tissue mobilization to avoid graft use are poorly described. In this article, we detail tips and tricks for achieving excellent tissue mobilization and describe the surgical technique to primarily repair chronic PM tendon tears. This technique allows for primary repair or augmentation with graft if the tissue quality is poor and for bridging a residual gap after mobilization is completed.

Surgical Technique

The senior author (M-E.L.) developed extensive expertise in chronic PM repairs in an academic tertiary setting. In PM ruptures surgically treated around 10 to 12 weeks from injury, a useful layer of scar tissue forms along the torn tissue edge that is consistently reliable to hold sutures without requiring grafting once extensive tissue mobilization is completed. An allograft is considered if there is a residual gap after mobilization or in revision repairs.

Perioperative planning is based on a thorough history and physical examination. Imaging studies (radiography, chest magnetic resonance imaging) are performed to confirm the diagnosis and location of injury. In chronic tears over 1 year from injury, magnetic resonance imaging indicates the presence or absence of any fatty atrophy. In our experience, most patients take steroids and other muscle-enhancing drugs or supplements. These are discontinued at the initial consultation and may be restarted only after 3 months postoperatively.

Prior to surgery, the patient undergoes regional and general anesthesia in the beach-chair position with a pneumatic arm holder (Video 1). The arm, shoulder, and entire PM are exposed. A short deltopectoral approach is used, from the lateral edge of the coracoid to the axillary fold, with elevation of flaps and blunt dissection subsequently performed to identify the cephalic vein (Fig 1). This vein may or may not be present because it might have been damaged at the time of the rupture. The vein (if present) is kept intact and retracted laterally (Fig 2).

The PM may or may not be recognizable easily, depending on the type of rupture. The superficial anterior and subcutaneous aspect of the PM is bluntly dissected and liberated with long Metzenbaum scissors up to the sternum (Fig 3). Then, the distal aspect of the muscle belly is dissected from distal/medial to lateral/proximal (from the “known” normal anatomy to the “unknown” scarred and/or torn area). A large hole is usually found in the torn area, and a gap as large as 10 to 15 cm (from the humerus) can be identified where the distal aspect of the PM muscle used to be (Fig 4).

A large cavity is typically encountered at the level of the pectoralis major rupture. Left shoulder, close-up anterolateral view, beach-chair position. The star indicates the patient’s head.

Medially to this gap, the PM muscle tissue is commonly scarred down and adhered. Care must be taken to preserve and maintain large amounts of scar tissue with the muscle, where sutures will be placed. This is beneficial to the repair, providing a more solid suture-anchoring point compared with the easy-to-tear muscle tissue alone. Once the scarred area of the lateral edge is dissected or freed, the undersurface dissection is carried out. The muscle tissue should be freed off the ribs and pectoralis minor using blunt dissection. The 2 neurovascular bundles (superior and inferior) must be kept intact. The dissection can be carried almost all the way to the sternum and clavicle, fully liberating the muscle belly. In cases of a single ruptured head, splitting between the 2 heads may be required, ensuring more complete mobilization. Thorough mobilization of the torn muscle tissue provides the much-needed length required to complete a tensionless repair. In rare cases in which there is an isolated clavicular head tear, the dissection may be more challenging. The surgeon may work underneath the sternal head to reach and mobilize the scarred clavicular head separately.

Thereafter, the deltoid is retracted laterally, and the normal footprint of the PM tendon insertion is visualized, lateral to the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT). Arm positioning may be modified (abduction, internal or external rotation) with the arm holder to release deltoid tension to access the humeral shaft effortlessly. Commonly, no tendon is left at the insertion site: Only scarred tissues are visible there. If the mobilized PM does not reach the footprint and additional length is needed, the repair can be either medialized along the medial border of the LHBT groove or bridged with a graft to the anatomic footprint. Medialization of the insertion point allows 1 to 2 cm in length to be gained. It also allows repair of the tendon with reduced tension and without the risk of strangulating the biceps and inducing pain after repair. Next, 3 unicortical drill holes are made 1 cm apart for the insertion of 3 double-loaded titanium headless anchors. As the bone is particularly dense in this location, increased torque is needed to insert the anchors. An anchor tap may be used. The anchors can also be sequentially advanced and retracted incrementally (to gradually create the bony tracks for the anchor threads), a few threads at a time, until fully seated, just below the cortical surface. Six suture limbs are used for Mason-Allen stitches to anchor down the mobilized scarred PM muscle tissue (Fig 5).

Six Mason-Allen stitches (from 3 double-loaded anchors in the humerus) are passed within the lateral pectoralis major for primary repair: drawing (A) and clinical photograph (B). Left shoulder, anterior view, beach-chair position. The arrow points toward the patient’s head.

If the scar tissue proves inadequate in retaining the sutures without tearing, an allograft (dermal acellular augmentation patch, 4 × 8 cm) can be used, augmenting the construct. This involves sandwiching the PM tissue’s superficial and deep surfaces with two portions of the patch and sewing the construct together (Fig 6) with 6 Mason-Allen stitches. Thorough irrigation and layered closure are completed. We then place the arm in a Velpeau sling in internal rotation (forearm against abdomen) reducing the repair’s tension. Postoperative Protocol

The postoperative protocol is similar for all repairs, including patch augmentation. All patients use a Velpeau sling for 6 weeks. Elbow, hand, and wrist range of motion (ROM) starts immediately, with the arm kept at the side and the palm facing toward the back (no external rotation). Pendulum exercises start at 2 weeks, and active-assisted ROM starts at 6 weeks, when sling use is discontinued. Active ROM starts after 8 weeks, and strengthening starts at 3 months postoperatively.

Discussion

PM tears are usually rare injuries and they can occasionally be missed. Chronic tears are generally defined as those present 6 weeks or more after the injury.13 Chronic PM tears present with weakness in humeral adduction, flexion, and internal rotation despite adequate rehabilitation and residual insertional gapping, resulting in chest asymmetry.14 Surgical repair of chronic tears is sometimes required. Technique Pearls and Pitfalls

PM, pectoralis major.

This technique offers several advantages (Table 2) for repairing chronically (and acutely) retracted tears. First, with significant medial retraction of the PM, superficial and deep dissections of the muscle belly (without compromising the neurovascular bundles) increase mobility, allowing for primary repair without requiring allograft. Second, medialization of humeral anchors allows effective reduction of the distance (up to 2 cm) and tension needed to affix the PM for increased healing and a lower failure risk. Additionally, medialization reduces the risk of strictures over the LHBT. Third, the described technique allows reinforcement of the distal PM for a stronger repair and still allows bridging if required. Finally, the suture anchors’ placement can be moved more distally or proximally to accommodate more proximal or more distal injuries.

Disadvantages of this technique (Table 2) include the risk of neurovascular bundle injury with too vigorous or forceful mobilization. This risk is reduced by performing manual blunt dissection of the muscle’s undersurface and feeling the gradual release, without pulling forcefully. In addition, a dermal patch may be more expensive than an autograft or allograft. Furthermore, medialization of the footprint could theoretically reduce the PM’s internal rotation strength. In our clinical experience, this is well tolerated by patients, given that internal rotation is also achieved by other muscles. Finally, in cases of significant atrophy or chronicity, the surgeon must remember that the described technique may be insufficient to achieve appropriate length and tension. In such cases, the surgeon would have to plan for possible graft options to bridge any potential residual gap.

In conclusion, we have presented a detailed, step-by-step PM tissue mobilization and repair technique using anchors for large chronically (or acutely) retracted tears. This allows a tensionless repair, even in the setting of a chronically retracted tear with a large gap. Additionally, it can be used with or without allograft to reduce gaps and reinforce poor-quality tissue.
