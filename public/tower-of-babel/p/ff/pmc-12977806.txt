Tissue-specific epigenetic regulation of fat metabolism in pigs through integrated analysis of DNA methylation and gene expression networks
Authors: Do-Young Kim, Byeonghwi Lim, Rajesh Kumar Pathak, Woncheoul Park, Jong-Eun Park, Jun-Mo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFat metabolism in pigs is controlled by tissue-specific molecular mechanisms that ultimately affect growth performance and meat quality. Understanding how epigenetic modifications interact with gene expression across key metabolic and fat-depositing tissues is essential for identifying regulatory processes and potential biomarkers to improve pork quality traits. Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate tissue specific epigenetic regulation of fat metabolism by integrating DNA methylation and gene expression profiles from liver, backfat, and loin (longissimus dorsi) tissues at two physiologically developmental stages (10 and 26 weeks), representing the early post-weaning growth phase and near-market weight, respectively. By explicitly comparing these ages and tissues, the study was designed to capture the transition from muscle-dominated growth to increased lipid deposition and to identify tissue- and stage-specific regulatory signatures that may serve as biomarkers for pork quality.ResultsGenome-wide DNA methylation exhibited weak clustering by tissue, whereas gene expression showed clear tissue separation. The liver harbored fewer genes with differential methylation across stage and tissue but a greater number of genes with differential expression than backfat and loin, suggesting distinct regulatory modes. Integrative analysis of the overlap genes between methylation and expression signals highlighted epigenetically mediated regulation of extracellular matrix organization, lipid metabolism, and muscle development pathways. Furthermore, weighted gene co-expression network analysis revealed distinct tissue-specific correlations between co-methylated and co-expressed modules, with enrichment in cholesterol biosynthesis, muscle contractility, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Together, these findings suggest that methylation changes are more subtle than transcriptional shifts, yet they are aligned with key functional pathways, consistent with a role for methylation as a fine-tuning mechanism that shapes tissue-specific transcriptional networks during growth.ConclusionsAcross liver, backfat, and loin, DNA methylation modulates transcriptional programs in a tissue-dependent manner, prioritizing pathways central to lipid handling, extracellular matrix remodeling, and muscle function. Background

The pig industry plays a vital role in global livestock production, providing a reliable source of animal protein and making a significant contribution to agricultural economies. Modern pig production aims to strike a balance between rapid growth performance and consistent meat quality, thereby ensuring economic efficiency and consumer satisfaction [1]. Genetic improvement through selective breeding and crossbreeding has resulted in substantial advancements in growth rate, feed efficiency, and carcass traits in commercial pigs [2, 3]. However, meat quality is influenced by more than genetics alone; environmental factors, such as feeding strategies, nutritional content, and rearing conditions, also play essential roles in shaping pork characteristics by affecting intramuscular fat (IMF) deposition and fat distribution [4, 5]. These factors play crucial roles in shaping meat quality by influencing body fat distribution and intramuscular fat content, highlighting the importance of both genetic and environmental factors in determining meat quality.

In pigs, the liver, backfat, and loin (longissimus dorsi) tissues are intimately involved in lipid metabolism and play crucial roles in meat quality development [6–9]. De novo fatty acid synthesis occurs primarily in adipose tissue, especially in backfat, which serves as a major site for lipid storage and acts as an endocrine organ through the secretion of adipokines and other bioactive molecules [10, 11].​ The liver plays a central regulatory role in whole-body lipid metabolism by coordinating fatty acid uptake, triglyceride synthesis, and lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion, thereby influencing fat deposition in peripheral tissues such as backfat and muscle [12–14]. The loin (longissimus muscle) contributes to meat quality traits such as tenderness, marbling, and flavor, due to its intramuscular fat content and muscle fiber composition [4, 15, 16].​ Understanding how these tissues interact to regulate fat metabolism at different developmental stages is therefore crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying growth and pork quality [17–20].

In addition to transcriptomic analysis, the study of epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, has become a crucial area of research for understanding the regulation of various metabolic diseases [21–24]. Epigenetic modifications are linked to major lipid and fat metabolic pathways that control fat accumulation and influence the expression of genes involved in lipogenesis, lipolysis, and energy homeostasis [25–29]. Integrating epigenetic research with multi-omics data, such as the epigenome and transcriptome, provides a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms regulating gene expression and how these modifications influence metabolic processes [30, 31]. For example, a study investigating adipocyte fractions from retroperitoneal adipose tissue in obese pigs identified differentially methylated regions linked to metabolic signaling pathways, including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and insulin signaling, shedding light on the epigenetic regulation of fat metabolism [32]. These studies have demonstrated the utility of integrating epigenetics with multi-omics approaches to deepen our understanding of metabolic regulation in livestock.

Here, we tested the hypothesis that tissue-specific DNA methylation functions as a fine-tuning mechanism coordinating transcriptional networks underlying lipid metabolism in the liver, backfat, and loin during growth. To capture the dynamics of this regulatory layer, two critical developmental stages were selected based on physiological relevance: 10 weeks, representing the early post-weaning growth phase characterized by rapid skeletal muscle development and establishment of adipose tissue depots [33–35], and 26 weeks corresponding to near market weight when fat deposition patterns are well established [36–40]. This developmental window encompasses the transition from muscle-dominated growth to enhanced lipid accumulation, providing an opportunity to identify both conserved epigenetic signatures and developmentally dynamic regulatory changes [4, 29]. To address this hypothesis, we integrated methylated DNA-binding domain sequencing (MBD-seq) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from crossbred pigs (Korean native pig × Yorkshire) at 10 and 26 weeks. Specifically, we aimed to (i) compare tissue- and stage-specific methylation and gene expression patterns; (ii) identify genes showing both differential methylation and expression (DM–DE overlap); and (iii) construct co-methylation and co-expression modules to uncover pathways associated with fat metabolism and pork quality. This integrative approach enhances our understanding of context-dependent epigenetic regulation and identifies candidate regulatory networks and markers relevant to meat quality improvement.

Methods

Animal materials and ethics statement

This study included a total of 16 crossbred pigs (Korean native pig × Yorkshire, F1 generation), as described in previous studies [41–43]. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institute of Animal Science, South Korea (NIAS2016-848). Pigs had unrestricted access to feed and water, and the diet composition is detailed in previous studies [41].​ The pigs were randomly assigned to two groups: 10 weeks (n = 8; 4 males, 4 females) representing the early post-weaning period, and 26 weeks (n = 8; 4 males, 4 females) representing an average body weight at slaughter of approximately 90 kg. The animals were sacrificed at the end of the growth stage (10 and 26 weeks) and samples were collected. All tissues were collected by an experienced technician to minimize variation. For each animal, samples of liver, backfat, and loin (longissimus dorsi) were obtained from anatomically consistent locations as follows: liver from the left lateral lobe, backfat from the dorsal midline directly above the eighth rib, and loin muscle from the central part of the longissimus dorsi located between the tenth and eleventh thoracic vertebrae. Tissue samples were consistently collected across all animals, cut into 1 cm3 pieces, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Sample extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Whole-genome methylation analysis was conducted using MBD-seq, whereas RNA-seq was used for transcriptomic analysis. For MBD-seq, genomic DNA was quantified from tissue samples using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). Then, genomic DNA (1 µg) was fragmented and enriched for methylated regions using MBD proteins with a MethylMiner Methylated DNA Enrichment Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The enriched methylated DNA was processed into libraries using a TruSeq Nano DNA Library Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and libraries were prepared for RNA-seq. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, with thorough library quantification and quality control prior to sequencing.

Sequencing data processing and analysis

For both MBD-seq and RNA-seq analyses, the quality of raw sequencing data was initially assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1) [44]. Subsequent data cleaning involved removing low-quality reads and adapter sequences using TrimGalore (v0.6.6) [45] for MBD-seq data and Trimmomatic (v0.38) [46] for RNA-seq data. After quality control, both datasets were aligned to the Sscrofa11.1 reference genome. Bowtie2 (v3.5.1) was used for MBD-seq alignment [47], and Hisat2 (v2.1.0) was used for RNA-seq alignment [48]. Samtools (v1.9) was used for file format conversions for both datasets, including duplicate removal from the MBD-seq data using the markup function [49]. For RNA-seq analysis, gene quantification was performed using feature counts (v2.0.0) [50].

For MBD-seq, MEDIPS and edgeR were used to identify the differentially methylated regions (DMRs) [51, 52]. The genome was divided into 500 bp windows, and differential methylation between the two stages was computed using fold change (FC) values and P-values derived from edgeR. Regions meeting the absolute log2FC ≥ 1 and P-value < 0.001 criteria were considered significant DMRs. These DMRs were annotated to gene features such as exons, introns, and promoters, with promoters defined as 2,000 bp upstream of the transcription start site. Differentially methylated genes (DMGs) were identified based on the proportion of DMR coverage across features. For RNA-seq, gene expression data were normalized using the trimmed mean of M values (TMM) method in EdgeR. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the absolute log2FC ≥ 1 and a false discovery rate < 0.05 criteria, based on the Benjamini–Hochberg multiple testing correction. Samples with significant discrepancies in library size, despite TMM normalization, were excluded from the final DMG and DEG analyses to ensure the robustness of the results.

To compare the DMGs and DEGs among the three tissues, we used Venn diagrams and upset plots in the R package to visualize the overlap between each omics gene set. For functional enrichment, we used the database annotation visualization integrated discovery (DAVID) web tool to perform Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses to identify biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components, and enriched pathways [53–55]. A P-value threshold of 0.01 was used to indicate the significance of functional annotation selection criteria.

Integrated DNA methylome and transcriptome analysis

We performed integrated analysis of DNA methylation and gene expression data to investigate epigenetic regulation of gene expression. The shared gene sets between DMGs and DEGs were clustered based on their regulatory status. Four shared clusters (Hypo-Up, Hyper-Up, Hypo-Down, and Hyper-Down) were identified based on the methylation and expression patterns.

Next, we conducted a weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to examine the correlation between the two omics datasets [56]. This approach enabled us to identify co-methylation and co-expression modules across tissues, which helped clarify the regulatory relationships. WGCNA was chosen because of its ability to reveal complex module relationships between large-scale datasets, making it well-suited for integrative multi-omics analysis. We then profiled the co-expression and co-methylation modules from each omics dataset and analyzed the correlations between these modules using the Pearson method. Modules with high correlations were further investigated by identifying the functional roles of the gene sets within each correlated module. Finally, Cytoscape was used to construct gene interaction networks for highly correlated modules [57].

Results

DNA methylation and expression clustering

RNA-seq and MBD-seq data were generated from backfat, liver, and loin tissues at 10 and 26 weeks of age. The average number of raw reads was 36,983,389 for transcriptomic data and 11,351,613 for DNA methylation data. After quality trimming, the average reads were 36,706,627 and 11,242,492, respectively. The unique mapping rates averaged 75.50% for RNA-seq and 56.90% for MBD-seq, while the overall mapping rates were 92.88% and 93.82%, respectively (Table S1).

Clustering of methylation profiles (Fig. S1A) showed weak tissue and time points separation, whereas expression profiles (Fig. S1B) showed clear tissue-specific clustering, with liver forming a distinct cluster across stages. Different development stage related differences were also evident within liver and loin expression profiles, while methylation profiles were comparatively conserved. While DNA methylation profiles remained relatively conserved, the gene expression data suggested more dynamic changes.

Identification of differential methylation patterns during growth

A comparative analysis of DMGs across backfat, liver, and loin revealed tissue-specific and shared methylation patterns (Fig. 1A). Backfat and loin shared more DMGs than liver, which shared fewer with backfat and some with loin. Loin displayed 7,534 DMGs (6,772 unique), backfat 1,425 (695 unique), and liver 98 (58 unique) (Table S2, Fig. S2A). Hypermethylated genes predominated in backfat and loin, whereas liver DMG counts were limited, precluding inference of a predominant direction. DMGs function annotations based on GO biological processes and KEGG pathway databases revealed tissue-specific and common biological functions (Fig. 1C, Fig. S3, Table S3). Backfat samples exhibited structural integrity (cell adhesion, DNA repair, and extracellular matrix), signaling pathways (GTPase, adhesion, integrin, and phosphorylation), and immune modulation (T-cell polarity and vesicle).

In the loin, hypermethylated genes were associated with the cell cycle, protein phosphorylation, and intracellular transport, which support the fundamental biological functions required to maintain muscle function and structure. Conversely, hypomethylated genes were associated with protein localization, actin filament dynamics, mitochondrial functions, and autophagy. Upon examining the comprehensive gene set, processes related to cytoskeletal organization, protein processing, and intracellular signaling pathways were found to be significantly enriched. Furthermore, MAPK signaling and insulin pathways were identified as playing a role in muscle development, energy balance, and cellular responses.

In contrast, methylation in liver tissue was more selective, with minimal widespread trends, and was primarily related to transcriptional regulation and hemostasis. The results of these analyses highlighted that backfat, loin, and liver tissues exhibit distinct methylation-associated biological functions and physiological roles. Backfat is mainly involved in immune regulation and cellular transport functions, while loin is more involved in muscle signaling and structure maintenance. In contrast, the functional contribution of DNA methylation in the liver is limited.

Profiling of gene expression dynamics during growth

Gene expression profiling identified DEGs in all three tissues (Fig. 2A). The liver displayed the highest numbers of unique DEGs at down- and up-regulation (4,131 and 3,535, respectively), with fewer in backfat (1,310 and 732) and loin (874 and 497) (Table S4, Fig. S2B). UpSet plots partitioned DEGs into up- and down-regulated sets, highlighting tissue-specific patterns: liver showed a larger proportion of up-regulated genes, while backfat and loin exhibited more down-regulated genes (Fig. 2B).

This analysis highlighted the distinct physiological roles of backfat, loin, and liver tissues, as evidenced by their unique functional enrichment of gene expression (Fig. 2C, Fig. S4, Table S5). Backfat was significantly enriched in lipid metabolism and energy regulation pathways, particularly fatty acid beta-oxidation and the positive regulation of cholesterol efflux. The upregulation of pathways such as retinol metabolism and hormone metabolic processes further emphasized the role of backfat in metabolic and endocrine functions, aligning with its primary function in fat storage and signaling.

The loin showed enrichment of pathways such as proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic processes and intracellular signal transduction, which are broadly involved in muscle protein turnover and signaling regulation. In addition, pathways related to energy homeostasis, calcium signaling, and vascular smooth muscle contraction were enriched in the loin transcriptome, reflecting general mechanisms of muscle contraction, calcium handling, and energy balance that support muscle functionality and growth.

Although the liver showed fewer enriched pathways than backfat and loin, it displayed notable upregulation in the regulation of RNA splicing and metabolic pathways, reinforcing its central role in systemic metabolism. Pathways such as calcium ion transmembrane transport and calcium ion import across the plasma membrane were also enriched, indicating that the liver contributes to calcium homeostasis. Taken together, these results illustrated both shared and unique physiological roles across tissues. Shared processes, such as metabolic pathways, reflect common functions, while specialized enrichment underscores tissue-specific roles, such as lipid metabolism in backfat, muscle function in the loin, and metabolic regulation in the liver.

Unified functional enrichment through common genes among DMGs and DEGs

Based on shared genes between DMGs and DEGs in each tissue, we aimed to analyze the direct regulatory interaction between DNA methylation and gene expression. The number of shared DMG–DEG genes identified were 71 (backfat), 230 (liver), and 18 (loin) (Fig. 3A–C, Fig. S5). Shared genes were grouped into four regulatory clusters by methylation–expression pairing: C1/C4 showed inverse relationships (hyper ↔ low, hypo ↔ high), whereas C2/C3 showed positive relationships (hyper ↔ high, hypo ↔ low) (Fig. 3D–F). Loin was enriched for C2 and C4 cluster genes, while backfat and liver were more evenly distributed across clusters. Integrating DNA methylation and gene expression data provided insights into the epigenetic interactions that may underlie tissue-specific and growth-stage-dependent regulation (Fig. 4C). Integration of module eigengenes revealed associations between expression (black/green/red/blue/pink/yellow) and methylation (green/grey) modules. Module based data integration across multi-omics layers

The functional gene enrichment network and associated regulatory mechanisms of each WGCNA module, integrating expression and DNA methylation data, present unique functional and core gene profiles (Fig. 5, Table S7). The yellow–green module played a minor role, with a limited number of genes associated with primary metabolic functions and stress responses (Fig. 5A). The blue–green integration module was significantly enriched in pathways related to extracellular matrix organization, collagen biosynthetic processes, and cell signaling. It included COL1A1, COL5A1, and ENSSSCG00000032761, which are essential for tissue structure and cell communication (Fig. 5B). The integration module (expression–methylation) indicated an association with backfat (positive–non-significant), liver (negative–positive), and loin (non-significant–negative), suggesting a targeted effect involved in connective tissue and extracellular matrix dynamics in pig growth. The black–green module functions related to oxygen transport and hydrogen peroxide catabolic processes were prominent (Fig. 5C). This module contained three to four genes with specific functions in the stress response and metabolic regulation. The primary functional process within the blue–grey integration module involved the ECM and collagen fibril organization, which are essential for tissue growth and repair (Fig. 5D). Genes such as COL1A1, COL26A1, and TNXB are involved in connective tissue function. Notably, the integration module showed associations with backfat (positive–negative) and the liver (negative–non-significant), suggesting a potential inhibitory mechanism in the liver tissue while promoting backfat development, with no significant relation to loin tissue. This pattern implied that adipose tissue development may involve demethylation processes that activate genes associated with tissue formation. Gene function analysis of the green–green integrated module indicated that it regulates various physiological processes, including muscle development and intercellular signaling (MYH7, ACTN2, PLEC, MYOZ1, ALPK2, and PDLIM3) and lipid and cholesterol metabolism (ABCA4, ABCA5, ABCA8, ABCA9, VPS13B, ENSSSCG00000015069; Fig. 5E). The negative correlation between gene expression and methylation levels in the liver (negative–positive) and loin (positive–negative) mediated tissue-specific functional regulation. The pink–grey module contributed to translation (ribosomal protein large/small subunit gene families), cell division (CDC20, CCNB1, and CCNB3), DNA replication (minichromosome maintenance gene families), and DNA repair mechanisms, indicating its high level of involvement in fundamental cellular processes (Fig. 5F). The red–green module focused on lipid metabolism, cellular proliferation, and viral responses (Fig. 5G). It included pathways such as cholesterol biosynthesis, homeostasis, metabolic processes, and the regulation of cell population proliferation, underscoring its role in maintaining the lipid balance, which is essential for energy storage and cellular function in the growth stage to the completion of development.

Discussion

Tissue-specific epigenetic regulation of fat metabolism

In this study, we analyzed DNA methylation and transcriptome data to clarify the regulatory mechanisms underlying fat metabolism in the liver, backfat, and loin muscle tissues during pig development. Our results indicated distinct tissue-specific methylation and gene expression patterns, highlighting the complex regulatory interactions essential for lipid metabolism and pork quality traits. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, suggesting that epigenetic modifications play a significant role in metabolic regulation in pigs [26, 29, 32]. DNA methylation clustering analysis revealed relatively weak tissue specificity compared with gene expression clustering. This suggested that, while DNA methylation serves as a regulatory mechanism, transcriptional regulation may be more dominant in defining tissue-specific metabolic functions. Furthermore, DNA methylation was not evenly distributed across tissues. While backfat and loin muscle exhibited more extensive methylation changes, the liver demonstrated a limited range of DMGs, indicating significant epigenetic plasticity in tissues directly associated with fat storage and muscle growth, reinforcing its primary role as a metabolic hub that coordinates systemic lipid metabolism [12, 14].

Gene expression profiles and functional specialization

Although the liver had fewer DMRs, it had the highest number of DEGs. This suggested that hepatic gene regulation during development is less dependent on methylation dynamics and more influenced by trans-acting factors and signaling pathways. This finding is consistent with the observations of Wang et al. [25] who demonstrated that stable, yet functionally relevant methylation plays a role in regulating lipid metabolism in the liver [25]. Liver DEGs were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways, RNA splicing, and calcium homeostasis. This finding highlighted the importance of the liver in regulating overall lipid and energy metabolism [12, 14, 27]. In contrast, DEGs in backfat were significantly involved in lipid metabolism and endocrine signaling, which supports their recognized role in fat accumulation and metabolic regulation [7, 8, 20]. The loin exhibited DEGs enriched in muscle functionality, protein turnover, and energy balance, emphasizing its essential function in pork tenderness and marbling [6, 58]. These results are consistent with those of previous studies, indicating that muscle and adipose tissues display tissue-specific transcriptomic signatures linked to their physiological functions [59]. Overall, the broader diversity of enriched methylation and expression signatures in backfat and loin suggests more extensive developmental and functional remodeling in these tissues compared with the liver.

Functional characteristics of genes with common differential methylation and expression

Integrative analysis of DNA methylation and transcriptomic data revealed genes that were both differentially methylated and expressed, highlighting metabolically regulated pathways under direct epigenetic control. In our dataset, approximately 70% of the annotated DMGs were located within gene body regions, whereas the remaining 30% were identified in promoter regions. This distribution suggests that, while promoter methylation typically serves a repressive function, gene methylation may have broader or alternative roles [60, 61]. Although promoter methylation is traditionally associated with transcriptional repression, gene body methylation has been linked to alternative splicing, transcriptional elongation, and even activation, depending on the genomic and chromatin contexts [62, 63]. Of note, many hypermethylated genes were also DEGs during the integrative analysis. This suggested that methylation and gene expression are not always inversely related, particularly in tissues with complex developmental programming, such as fat and muscle tissues. Previous studies in pigs and other livestock support this finding, indicating that gene body methylation can mark actively transcribed regions and correlate with tissue-specific gene functions [9, 64–66].

To clarify the functional impact of DNA methylation on gene regulation, we categorized the shared DMGs/DEGs based on tissue specificity (backfat, liver, and loin) and correlation patterns (positive, negative, or whole). Functional enrichment analysis revealed distinct biological themes within each tissue, emphasizing the specificity of epigenetic regulation. In backfat tissue, genes such as TNXB (ENSSSCG00000023611), ITGA8 (ENSSSCG00000011046), and ITGA5 (ENSSSCG00000000293) are commonly associated with the negative regulation of ECM organization, cell–matrix adhesion, and cytoskeletal remodeling. TNXB encodes tenascin-X, a glycoprotein involved in matrix stabilization that has been shown to influence ECM remodeling during adipose tissue expansion [67]. Similarly, ITGA, an integrin subunit, mediates fibroblast-ECM interactions and has been implicated in tissue stiffness, thereby affecting preadipocyte differentiation [68]. In contrast, positively regulated genes in backfat, such as PLEC (ENSSSCG00000039094) and FLNC (ENSSSCG00000016578), promote sarcomeric cytoskeleton integrity and mechanical sensing in differentiating adipocytes, possibly reflecting functional convergence between muscle-like cytoskeletal pathways and adipocyte morphology [69, 70]. In loin muscle, negatively regulated genes, such as MSMO1 (ENSSSCG00000008857) and LMNB2 (ENSSSCG00000026784) are frequently involved in cholesterol biosynthesis, nuclear architecture, and collagen biosynthesis, respectively. MSMO1 plays a crucial role in sterol biosynthesis and is closely linked to lipid accumulation and cellular membrane integrity in intramuscular adipocytes [71]. In contrast, positively regulated genes in the loin were enriched for muscle fiber structure (LMNB2—ENSSSCG00000026784) and collagen biosynthesis (COL1A1—ENSSSCG00000036135), facilitating active muscle development and integrity during later growth stages. COL1A1, a type I collagen complex, is essential for ECM stiffness and structural support during muscle development, and is often co-expressed with COL1A2 in skeletal-muscle-related traits [72–74]. These patterns highlight that methylation-expression correlations differ by function and tissue type. In both backfat and loin, several genes exhibited changes in expression despite hypermethylation, emphasizing the complexity of regulation, particularly in the context of gene body methylation. Importantly, key pathways such as the PI3K-Akt signaling and cholesterol biosynthesis pathways emerged in both positive and negative contexts, reflecting stage-dependent epigenetic reprogramming rather than static repression.

Integration of epigenetic regulation network and tissue specificity through module correlation

The module-based integration of DNA methylation and gene expression provides deeper insights into how large-scale gene networks are regulated in a tissue- and growth-specific manner during pig growth. Unlike individual gene-level comparisons, the WGCNA-based approach allowed us to uncover how co-methylated and co-expressed gene clusters act as coordinated functional units, which may better reflect the underlying biological architecture of complex traits, such as fat deposition and muscle development [56]. The green methylation module, which showed a strong correlation with the liver (positive), backfat (negative), and loin (negative) phenotypes, emerged as a key epigenetic driver module. Its significant association with expression modules such as the black, red, and green modules, which were enriched for cholesterol biosynthesis, cell proliferation, and oxidative metabolism, suggests that DNA methylation may act as an upstream regulatory mechanism orchestrating liver-specific metabolic functions [25, 32, 62]. Of note, the directionality of the correlation between methylation and expression was not uniform across tissues, and both negative and positive relationships were observed. This variability supports previous findings that DNA methylation, especially in gene body regions, does not always suppress gene expression, and may instead reflect transcriptional activity or chromatin accessibility [60, 64].

Furthermore, the identification of tissue-specific module correlations, such as the blue and yellow expression modules correlating with backfat traits or the green-green module showing opposing regulation between the liver and loin, raises important questions about the mechanisms by which epigenetic states are established and maintained in a context-dependent manner. From a functional perspective, the identification of collagen-related genes (COL1A1, COL26A1, and TNXB) and cholesterol metabolism regulators (MSMO1 and HMGCR) within highly correlated modules underscores the dual role of epigenetic regulation: one mediates structural remodeling in fat and muscle and the other coordinates metabolic homeostasis. Our findings suggest that their activity may be shaped by the methylation-driven reprogramming of module-wide transcription, which may serve as a fine-tuning mechanism for aligning tissue-specific functions with systemic metabolic demands. This perspective aligns with recent models that propose that epigenetic states modulate transcriptional response ranges rather than absolute on/off switches. Several of these genes also appeared in the DEG-DMGs that overlapped with the first integration, reinforcing their central role. The convergence of these findings from distinct analytical strategies lends robustness to their biological relevance and indicates their potential as biomarkers or targets in breeding programs focused on meat quality traits. Moreover, the tissue-specific nature of correlated modules suggests that epigenetic mechanisms are not uniformly deployed, but are tailored to support each tissue’s unique functional requirements. For example, the contrasting patterns observed in the liver and backfat modules imply a context-dependent role for DNA methylation in modulating metabolic gene networks. These findings echo prior observations in both livestock and human studies, where environmental cues, developmental timing, and tissue physiology jointly shaped epigenetic landscapes. Finally, it is worth noting that although WGCNA provides a powerful framework for identifying correlated gene clusters, it cannot confirm causal relationships. Although biologically suggestive, the observed correlations require further validation through experimental perturbation studies, such as CRISPR-based epigenetic editing or methylation inhibition. However, due to the limited amount of biological material and the comprehensive multi-tissue, multi-omics nature of this study, additional functional assays (e.g., protein-level or gene editing experiments) could not be performed. We acknowledge this as a limitation of the present work. Nonetheless, our findings represent a critical first step in establishing modular epigenetic networks as key regulators of tissue development and metabolic specialization in pigs. These results provide a valuable resource and experimental foundation for future targeted functional validation. Furthermore, epigenetic regulation operates through a multilayered machinery that involves not only DNA methylation but also histone modifications and chromatin architecture, and this strategy appears to be conserved across diverse biological systems. For example, in bacteria, DNA methylation and nucleoid-associated proteins cooperatively regulate gene expression and virulence, while in other livestock species such as cattle and sheep, the interplay between histone marks and chromatin accessibility has been shown to fine-tune developmental gene programs [75, 76]. Together, these examples, ranging from prokaryotes to mammals, highlight that DNA methylation is functionally intertwined with higher-order chromatin states. Therefore, future studies in pigs should integrate histone profiling and chromatin conformation analysis (e.g., Hi-C) with DNA methylation and transcriptomic data to capture a more comprehensive landscape of epigenetic regulation governing fat metabolism.

Potential environmental influences and comparative perspectives

Although all pigs were managed under uniform housing, feeding, and husbandry conditions, residual environmental factors (e.g., microenvironment, health status, early life influences) may still have contributed to the observed epigenetic and transcriptomic variation. Previous studies in livestock have shown that nutrition, thermal stress, pathogens, and other management-related factors can induce stable changes in DNA methylation and gene expression, thereby modulating growth, metabolism, and product quality [77–80]. These potential environmental influences should be considered when extrapolating our findings and motivate future experiments designed to explicitly manipulate and measure such factors.​

Tissue and development stage specific epigenetic patterns similar to those observed here in porcine backfat, loin, and liver have been reported in other livestock species. In cattle and sheep, DNA methylation and transcriptome studies in subcutaneous and intramuscular adipose depots have linked depot-specific methylation signatures in lipid metabolic genes to differences in fat deposition, fatty acid composition, and carcass quality, paralleling the enrichment of lipid metabolism pathways detected in pig backfat [26, 29]. Likewise, comparative analyses of skeletal muscle methylomes in cattle and poultry have shown that methylation changes in myogenic, cytoskeletal, and energy metabolism genes accompany growth, fibre-type composition, and age-related transitions, which is consistent with the muscle-regulatory and energy homeostasis pathways enriched in the porcine loin across developmental stages [81–83].​ The studies in livestock have shown that epigenetic regulation of hepatic genes is associated with systemic energy balance, feed efficiency, and responses to nutritional or environmental challenges. For example, DNA methylation changes in bovine have been linked to divergent residual feed intake and to differences in nutrient metabolism and metabolic stress across diets or thermal environments [84, 85]. Similar work in humans further indicates that epigenetic modifications in the liver contribute to the regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism and broader metabolic homeostasis [86]. Moreover, recent cross‑species comparative analyses of DNA methylation in brain, liver, and skeletal muscle across multiple mammals, including cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, and mice, support the idea that modular epigenetic regulation of key metabolic tissues is a conserved feature underlying complex traits such as growth, feed efficiency, and adaptation in domesticated animals [87].​

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive multi-omics overview of tissue-specific epigenetic regulation of fat metabolism in pigs. Through the integration of DNA methylation and gene expression data from the liver, backfat, and loin, we revealed that methylation exerts a fine-tuning role rather than a dominant regulatory effect. While the liver exhibited fewer methylation changes but extensive transcriptional variation, backfat and loin showed greater epigenetic plasticity linked to adipogenesis and muscle structure. Integrative network analysis identified tissue-dependent co-regulated modules, emphasizing extracellular matrix remodeling, cholesterol metabolism, and muscle fiber development as key pathways influenced by methylation–expression interactions. While these findings advance our mechanistic understanding, several limitations temper immediate translational applications: (i) causality requires experimental validation; (ii) protein-level confirmation is needed; and (iii) generalizability across breeds remains to be established. The identified epigenetic markers provide a foundation for future hypothesis-driven studies and may eventually inform breeding strategies once validated in larger populations under diverse conditions.
