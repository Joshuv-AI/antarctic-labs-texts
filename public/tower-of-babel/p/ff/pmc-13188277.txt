Cognitive impairment, depression, anxiety, and health-related quality of life in fibrotic ILD: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Elif Yelda Ozgun Niksarlioglu, Mehmet Atilla Uysal, Ramazan Eren, Neval Alagoz, Oznur Yıldız, Sule Gul, Senay Aydın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComorbidities such as systemic hypertension, coronary artery disease, and pulmonary hypertension are common in interstitial lung diseases. These conditions may significantly affect treatment choice, outcomes, and health-related quality of life (HRQL). Few studies have investigated cognitive impairment (CI) in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and data on CI in hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) are lacking. We aimed to evaluate CI in a cohort of patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease and to examine associated factors, including depression, anxiety, and their effects on HRQL.MethodsNinety-four patients with F-ILD were included in the study, and cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Turkish version of Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-TR). Depression and anxiety were evaluated using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). HRQL was evaluated using the Short Form-36 (SF-36).ResultsOf the 94 patients included, 61 (64.9%) were male, with a mean (standard deviation) age of 63.6 (SD: 10.6) years, and 62.8% had a history of smoking. The cohort included 45 (47.9%) with IPF and 49 (52.1%) with fibrotic HP patients. The mean FVC% predicted, and DLCO% were 72.2% (SD: 18.6) and 55.7% (SD: 20.7), respectively, and the mean 6-minute walk test (6MWT) distance was 350 (SD: 111) m.The mean MMSE and MoCA-TR scores were 22.3 (SD: 5.5) and 18.9 (SD: 5.7), both below established cutoff values. CI was identified in 51.1% of patients using MMSE and in 66.0% using MoCA-TR. Notably, MMSE-defined CI was more frequent in females, nonsmokers, patients with higher BMI, and those with anxiety, and was associated with lower physical functioning scores on SF-36. In contrast, when CI was defined by MoCA-TR, only a shorter 6MWT distance was related to CI. Depression and anxiety were present in 35 (37.2%) and 21 (22.3%) patients, respectively. Patients with anxiety were older than those without anxiety (68.9 (SD: 8.4) vs. 62.1 (SD: 10.7), p = 0.009), and anxiety was more frequent in females as well. Although BMI, DLCO%, 6MWT distance, and SF-36 subdomain scores demonstrated weak associations with cognitive scores, these findings help us better understand patient variability.ConclusionCI was observed in more than half of patients with F-ILD and was associated with functional limitations, anxiety, and impaired HRQL. Awareness of CI in this patient population is essential for healthcare professionals, as it may facilitate appropriate assessment, individualized patient-centered approaches, and the implementation of supportive care strategies.

Introduction

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders affecting the lung parenchyma, arising from environmental and occupational exposures, connective tissue–associated conditions, or idiopathic processes, characterized by progressive fibrosis of the lung interstitium. Many ILDs, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), and nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), may progress despite appropriate treatment and are therefore classified as fibrotic interstitial lung diseases (F-ILDs). Although ILDs have traditionally been considered diseases primarily affecting the lungs, they are frequently accompanied by multiple comorbidities, including coronary artery disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, pulmonary hypertension, osteoporosis, obstructive sleep apnea, depression, anxiety, and cognitive dysfunction [1, 2]. Importantly, coexisting comorbidities may further complicate diagnosis, prognosis, therapeutic decision-making, and health-related quality of life (HRQL).

Cognitive dysfunction has been investigated in several chronic respiratory diseases, particularly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). An estimated 54% of patients with COPD have cognitive impairment (CI), attributed mainly to chronic hypoxemia and systemic inflammation [3]. The reported prevalence of CI in COPD is approximately 20%–30%, and associated factors include age, cough, forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), partial arterial oxygen pressure (PaO2), and depression [4]. More recent studies suggest that CI may occur in COPD with or without hypoxemia, and that more advanced COPD is associated with poorer CI [5, 6]. Chronic hypoxemia may contribute to CI via reduced cerebral oxygenation, and systemic inflammation may promote microvascular brain injury—mechanisms that may also be relevant in ILDs [7, 8].

A recent review identified several risk factors for CI in ILD. In IPF, reduced diffusion capacity of carbon monoxide (DLCO), impaired FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio, shorter 6-minute walk test (6MWT) distance, hypoxemia, and post-exercise arterial oxygen tension were associated with CI [7]. CI may manifest as memory problems, learning difficulties, and reduced concentration in ILD, potentially affecting functional evaluation (pulmonary function tests (PFTs), DLCO, 6MWT) and treatment adherence [9, 10]. However, evidence regarding the frequency of CI and its impact on clinical characteristics, functional parameters, and HRQL in patients with F-ILDs remains limited. We aimed to evaluate CI in a cohort of patients with F-ILDs and investigate associated factors, including depression/anxiety, and their effects on HRQL.

Methods

Patient selection

This was a cross-sectional, observational study conducted between August 2023 and December 2024, 94 patients with F-ILD attending the pulmonary medicine department of a tertiary hospital were evaluated consecutively during a stable period. F-ILD was diagnosed according to the ATS/ERS/ALAT IPF guideline and PPF guideline [11]. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (FHP) share several clinical, functional, and radiological characteristics, particularly with respect to the usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern on imaging. Therefore, the study population was restricted to patients with IPF and FHP. Patients with other fibrotic interstitial lung diseases—such as connective tissue disease–associated interstitial lung involvement, sarcoidosis, and fibrotic nonspecific interstitial pneumonia—were excluded to reduce disease-related heterogeneity and potential confounding, ensuring a more homogeneous study population for the assessment of cognitive dysfunction. The study population consisted of native Turkish-speaking patients. Patients with illiteracy or without formal education in Turkish were excluded to ensure appropriate comprehension and validity of the cognitive assessment tools. Patients with previously diagnosed psychiatric and/or neurological disease, traumatic brain injury, current delirium, dementia, stroke, malignancy, insufficient formal education in Turkish or illiteracy, severe visual problems, and acute exacerbation were also excluded. Patients with an acute exacerbation, as defined by the ATS/ERS criteria, were excluded from the study [12]. Acute exacerbation was considered an acute worsening of dyspnea and clinical deterioration, characterized by the presence of new ground-glass opacities and/or consolidation on chest computed tomography, after excluding alternative causes such as cardiac failure and fluid overload. Furthermore, patients who had experienced fever or increased sputum volume and purulence within the preceding 4 weeks were excluded.

Demographic data, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, respiratory symptoms, ILD medications, and radiological findings on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) were recorded. Functional status was assessed using PFTs, DLCO, 6MWT, and the GAP score. Whenever possible, PFT’s, DLCO, and 6MWT measurements were performed on the same day as the cognitive function tests. The GAP score includes sex, age, forced vital capacity (FVC) % predicted, and DLCO % predicted; higher scores indicate increased mortality risk [13].

Cognitive assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Turkish Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-TR), administered by a neurologist. The MMSE is an 11-item test assessing orientation, registration, attention, calculation, recall, language, and visuospatial skills [14]. Turkish validation of the MMSE-TR was by Güngen et al. in 2002, with scores from 0 to 30 and a cutoff of 24 for CI [15].

MoCA is a brief (~ 10-minute) test assessing attention/concentration, memory, language, executive functions, visuospatial skills, abstraction, calculation, and orientation. Scores range from 0 to 30; <26 is commonly used for mild CI and < 18 for severe impairment [16]. In the Turkish version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-TR), the cutoff for cognitive impairment is 21 points, as reported in the Turkish validation study [17]. Although a higher cutoff value was proposed in the original MoCA validation study, population-specific validations have demonstrated variability in optimal cutoffs across countries, likely due to differences in cultural, linguistic, educational, and demographic characteristics. In particular, the cutoff value was set at 21 in the MoCA-TR validation and was therefore used in the present study.

Depression/anxiety and health-related quality of life

HRQL was evaluated using the Short Form-36 (SF-36) [18], validated in Turkish by Koçyiğit et al. [19]. The SF-36 consists of 36 items grouped into eight domains: physical functioning (PF), role limitations due to physical problems (RP), role limitations due to emotional problems (RE), bodily pain (BP), general health perceptions (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), and mental health (MH). Domain scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health status and quality of life. Norm-based scoring (mean 50, SD: 10) was not applied because country-specific norm-based reference values are not available for the Turkish population. The Turkish version of the SF-36 questionnaire was administered to all patients to assess HRQOL. Depression and anxiety were evaluated using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). The Turkish validation study was published in 1997 [20]. The scale consists of 14 items, comprising two subscales: anxiety (HAD-A) and depression (HAD-D), each containing seven items. Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), and cutoff values were ≥ 8 for depression and ≥ 10 for anxiety in the Turkish version of HADS-TR 19 [20].

To minimize potential interviewer bias, all questionnaires and cognitive assessments were administered on the same day for each participant. No additional measures were taken to address diagnostician bias. The study was approved by the Health Sciences University, Yedikule Chest Diseases and Thoracic Surgery Training and Research Hospital Review Board and Central Ethics Committee (approval number: 2023 − 379). All participants provided written informed consent.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation), whereas non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median and 25th–75th percentiles (Q1-Q3). Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and between-group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate analyses to identify variables associated with cognitive impairment were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the 115 patients screened, 21 were excluded for the following reasons: diagnoses other than IPF or FHP (n = 9; sarcoidosis, n = 3; nonspecific interstitial pneumonia, n = 3; systemic sclerosis-related interstitial lung disease, n = 2; rheumatoid arthritis-related interstitial lung disease, n = 1), incomplete questionnaire or cognitive assessment data (n = 5), non-native Turkish speaker (n = 2), recent acute exacerbation (n = 3), and withdrawal of consent (n = 2). The final study population consisted of 94 patients (Fig. 1). Of the 94 patients included, 61 (64.9%) were male, and the mean (standard deviation) age was 63.6 (10.6) years. A smoking history was present in 59 patients (62.8%), with a mean exposure of 36.7 (SD: 21.4) pack-year. The most common comorbidities were hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease (Table 1). Dyspnea and cough were the most frequently reported symptoms. Among the cohort, 45 patients (47.9%) had IPF and 49 (52.1%) had FHP. The mean FVC% predicted was 72.2 (SD: 18.6), the mean DLCO% predicted was 55.7 (SD: 20.7), and the mean 6MWT distance was 350 (SD: 111) meters.

A total of 115 patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease (F-ILD) were initially screened; however, due to the exclusion criteria detailed in the Fig. 1, the final analysis included 94 patients. A post hoc sample size calculation, based on the observed effect sizes of the most significant variables (gender and BMI; both p = 0.008), with a 1:1 allocation ratio, 80% power, and α = 0.05, indicated that approximately 102 patients would be required. Our study included 94 patients, resulting in an achieved power of approximately 77%. Although this is slightly below the conventional 80% threshold, it reflects the inherent challenges of conducting research in rare diseases with limited patient availability.

Discussion

In our study, CI, depression/anxiety, and quality of life were evaluated in F-ILD cases. CI was common in patients with F-ILDs, as assessed by MMSE (51.1%) and MoCA-TR (66%). MMSE-defined impairment was associated with female sex, nonsmoking status, higher BMI, longer follow-up, and anxiety, whereas MoCA-TR defined impairment was associated with lower 6MWT distance. Cognitive impairment was also associated with poorer HRQL, notably reduced SF-36 physical functioning. In the HADS assessment, anxiety was more prevalent among older patients, females, never-smokers, and those with MMSE-defined CI.

In a recently published study by Vastag et al., neurocognitive dysfunction was assessed using the MMSE in patients with F-ILD and non-ILD, and cognitive dysfunction was identified in approximately one-third of patients with F-ILD. Cognitive dysfunction was reported to be more frequent, particularly in patients with reduced DLCO and higher GAP stage. Unlike our study, that investigation included patients with radiological patterns of usual interstitial pneumonia as well as nonspecific interstitial pneumonia, sarcoidosis-associated pulmonary fibrosis, and bronchiolocentric interstitial pneumonia [21]. In contrast, our study focused exclusively on patients with IPF and fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis exhibiting a UIP pattern. In our cohort, a weak correlation was observed between MoCA-TR scores and DLCO, and no significant differences were identified between groups in terms of cognitive impairment assessed by either MMSE or MoCA-TR. These discrepancies may be attributable to differences in the patient populations included in the studies.

In contrast, the presence of any comorbidity showed a borderline association with depression, based on HADS. Regarding correlations, MoCA-TR scores were weakly correlated with DLCO and baseline SpO₂ during the 6MWT and moderately correlated with 6MWT distance. MMSE scores showed a weak negative correlation with BMI, and weak correlations were observed between MMSE and 6MWT distance, as well as selected SF-36 domains (PF, RP, MH, and SF). There are limited studies in the English literature evaluating CI in patients with IPF, and data in fibrotic ILD—particularly FHP—remain scarce. Therefore, we believe our study provides novel evidence and represents one of the earliest reports on CI in a well-defined F-ILD cohort.

Evidence regarding CI in ILD, and particularly in fibrotic HP, remains limited. Prior studies in COPD link CI to dyspnea, hypoxemia, reduced lung function, and obstructive sleep apnea 20 [22]. In idiopathic interstitial pneumonias, CI has also been reported in 26.6% of patients, with multifactorial contributors including disease severity and comorbidities [23]. Other studies reported that disease severity, comorbidities, dyspnea, and depression might be risk factors for CI in ILD patients [9, 24]. Our findings extend the existing literature by demonstrating a high prevalence of CI in a cohort restricted to fibrotic phenotypes. Mechanistically, CI in F-ILD is likely multifactorial, involving hypoxemia/hypercapnia, systemic inflammation-related increased cardiovascular risk, and dyspnea-related reductions in physical activity, which may also contribute to depression/anxiety and impaired HRQL [24].

In their review, Annaka H et al. noted that, unlike in COPD, in ILD cases, DLCO, post-exercise hypoxia, and post-exercise increased heart rate can be used to detect CI for early IPF diagnosis [7]. In our study, we observed that the walking distance at the 6MWT was shorter in CI cases identified by MoCA-TR, and that both MMSE and MoCA-TR points correlated with 6MWT walking distance. Therefore, the detection of a decline in 6MWT in the follow-up of F-ILD cases may be an alarming finding in terms of neurocognitive impairment. Similarly, in the study by Giannouli et al., it was commented that walking distance, post-exercise heart rate, oxygen saturation, and DLCO can predict neurocognitive performance [10]. In our study, no relationship was found between the basal SpO2 level before 6MWT or desaturation during the test and CI. This difference may be due to the differences in the cases included in these studies. While our study included IPF and F-HP cases, the other study included cases of IPF, sarcoidosis, nonspecific interstitial pneumonia, unspecified ILD, cryptogenic organizing pneumonia, and pulmonary histiocytosis. Due to the limited number of studies investigating risk factors for cognitive impairment in FHP cases, we are not comparing FHP cases in our study.

Dyspnea, a significant symptom of ILD patients, occurs as a result of ventilatory impairment caused by impaired gas exchange and decreased lung compliance [25]. Increased dyspnea can lead to reduced exercise capacity, limitations in physical activity, and, consequently, impaired quality of life. Therefore, cognitive capacity, especially concentration, memory, and executive function, can be impaired in patients with significant dyspnea [24, 26]. In our study, dyspnea was observed in 89.4% of cases, regardless of CI. This may be because all cases were in the F-ILD class, i.e., a relatively advanced stage patient group. Further research examining the relationship between dyspnea and cognitive impairment in early and advanced-stage ILD cases can be planned.

Studies of IPF cases have shown an increase in CI with age, which is attributed to physiological aging and increased comorbidities and polypharmacy [27]. In our study, when evaluated with both MMSE and MoCA-TR, no age difference was found between the two groups. This may be because the F-ILD group is generally older. In a 2023 review, no association between gender and cognitive function was reported among ILD patients [24]. In our study, however, the frequency of CI using MMSE was higher in women than in men. This may be due to differences in patient selection. A large number of cases in the ARIC Study showed that a restrictive pattern of pulmonary function test was correlated with impaired cognitive function [28]. In our study, however, no correlation was found between cognitive impairment detected by MMSE or MoCA-TR and pulmonary function tests, FVC% predicted, DLCO% predicted, and GAP score. This can be explained by the relatively small number of patients in our study.

Although cardiopulmonary reserve indicators such as the 6-MWT, desaturation after the 6-MWT, and post-exercise heart rate have been shown to predict CI in ILD cases, no intergroup differences were observed in factors other than the 6-MWT in our study. Since all cases included in our study were F-ILD and there was no control group, it is not possible to make further interpretations [10]. Furthermore, some studies have identified the presence of OSA as a risk factor for cognitive impairment in IPF and COPD patients. In our study, however, no patients with OSA diagnoses were identified in either IPF or FHP cases, and the presence of OSA symptoms, such as witnessed apnea, daytime sleepiness, and snoring, was not investigated [29].

Although the MoCA test was not explicitly developed for IPF patients, studies report that its psychometric properties are sufficient for this patient group [30, 31]. A review of the English-language literature shows that the MMSE and MoCA have been used in studies of IPF patients [32]. However, no publications examining cognitive impairment in HP cases were found in the literature. Therefore, in our research, we preferred the MMSE and MoCA-TR tests, which have Turkish validation, have been used previously in COPD cases, and are quick and easy to perform [33]. On the other hand, it is not clearly determined which tests should be used for cognitive assessment in groups with more advanced disease stages, such as F-HP and IPF cases [32]. Based on our results, it seems appropriate to use both tests together to assess cognitive impairment and related factors, especially given the observed correlations with different parameters. Our study may motivate the development of new cognitive impairment tests specific to ILD groups in the future.

In contrast to previous studies investigating cognitive impairment in ILD, we observed that patients with MMSE-defined CI had a significantly higher BMI (29.3 vs. 26.9, p = 0.008). Furthermore, MMSE scores demonstrated a weak but significant negative correlation with BMI. To date, similar findings have not been reported in IPF-focused studies, suggesting that this association may be influenced by differences in disease phenotype, particularly the inclusion of patients with FHP in our cohort. While this observation should be interpreted with caution, it represents a potentially novel finding that warrants further investigation in larger, phenotype-specific, and longitudinal studies to elucidate its underlying mechanisms and clinical implications.

Previous studies have shown that depression and anxiety occur in 14%–49% and 21%–60% of patients with ILD, respectively, and are associated with impaired HRQL, poor treatment adherence, and increased exacerbations and hospitalizations [34–36]. In a study by Shaheen et al., depressive symptoms were observed in all patients with IPF and were reported to be associated with hypoxemia [37]. In our cohort, depression and anxiety were identified in 37.2% and 22.3% of patients with F-ILD, respectively. Variability in prevalence across studies may be related to differences in assessment tools and study populations. Previous studies—particularly in COPD and IPF—have reported no significant association between MoCA defined CI and anxiety or depression assessed by HADS [29, 38, 39]. In contrast, in our study, anxiety was more frequent among patients with MMSE-defined CI; however, no significant correlations were observed between anxiety or depression scores and MMSE or MoCA-TR results, suggesting that psychological symptoms and cognitive impairment may coexist without a direct linear relationship.

Previous studies in ILD populations have evaluated the association between depression and anxiety assessed by HADS and disease-related symptoms [40]. In our cohort, cough and dyspnea—key manifestations of ILD and markers of disease burden—were reported by the majority of patients, reflecting the advanced fibrotic phenotype. In contrast, data from the Australian IPF Registry (516 patients) demonstrated lower prevalences of depression (10.8%) and anxiety (13.5%) [40]. Although patients in that registry were older (mean age 71.3 years), their disease severity was substantially lower, as indicated by a mean GAP score of 1.8, compared with our F-ILD cohort, which exhibited more advanced disease (mean GAP score 3.38). Taken together, these findings suggest that greater disease severity and symptom burden may contribute to the higher prevalence of depression and anxiety observed in our study.

Previous studies have shown that ILD adversely affects HRQOL, with patients with severe IPF exhibiting worse SF-36 scores than both moderate IPF and control groups, particularly in the physical health and level of independence domains [9, 41, 42]. In our study, SF-36 physical functioning (PF) scores were lower in patients with MMSE-defined CI, whereas no comparable HRQOL differences were observed when CI was defined using MoCA-TR. MMSE scores showed weak-to-moderate correlations with PF, RP, MH, and SF, consistent with previous reports suggesting that the modest association between CI and SF-36 domains may reflect the subjective nature of HRQOL instruments [42]. Therefore, objective measures such as the 6-minute walk test may provide more robust insights into the clinical impact of CI. Overall, our findings indicate that CI in F-ILD may be associated with impaired quality of life, although furtherstudies are needed to characterize this relationship better.

This study has several limitations. First, despite being conducted in a tertiary referral center, it was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability. Second, the sample size was modest, which may have limited statistical power for some associations. Third, this study included only patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis. Although this approach enhanced cohort homogeneity and internal validity, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to other fibrotic interstitial lung diseases. Lastly, potential confounding factors that may influence cognitive function, such as obstructive sleep apnea and educational level, were not systematically assessed or adjusted for in the analysis. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal associations between disease course and cognitive decline.

Conclusion

CI was observed in more than half of patients with F-ILD and was associated with functional limitations, anxiety, and impaired health-related quality of life. Awareness of CI in this patient population is essential for healthcare professionals, as it may facilitate appropriate assessment, individualized patient-centered approaches, and the implementation of supportive care strategies. In patients with ILD, cognitive assessment may help identify individuals who could benefit from tailored educational and supportive interventions incorporated into routine clinical practice.
