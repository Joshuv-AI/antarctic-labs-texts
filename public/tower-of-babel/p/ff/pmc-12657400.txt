Update on the impact of lipid and glucose control on diabetic wound healing
Authors: Rui Sun, Yang Xu, Zhenjun Ji, Xingxing Li, Zaixiao Tao, Weichen Luo, Yuyu Yao, Lijuan Chen, Genshan Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review summarizes current insights into the molecular mechanisms by which hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia contribute to chronic diabetic wounds. It discusses the roles of key metabolic pathways, including the hexosamine biosynthetic and polyol pathways, along with the significance of inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress in this process. In addition, emerging therapeutic strategies are evaluated, such as the use of metformin to activate AMPK, PPAR agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists, which hold promise for modulating the inflammatory microenvironment and restoring cellular function. Combination therapies, advanced wound dressings, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT), and regenerative approaches such as stem cell therapies and bioengineered skin substitutes are also reviewed as integrated strategies that target both systemic metabolic dysregulation and local wound-specific challenges. These findings underscore the critical role of improved glucose and lipid control in optimizing diabetic wound healing and suggest that personalized, multimodal therapeutic approaches may offer enhanced clinical outcomes for patients with diabetic ulcers and other chronic wounds.Keywords: Diabetic wound healing, Glucose metabolism, Lipid dysregulation, Inflammation and oxidative stress, Metabolic therapeutic strategies

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. It affects millions of individuals globally, with an estimated 537 million adults aged 20–79 living with diabetes in 2021, a number projected to rise to 783 million by 2045 according to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [1]. The prevalence of diabetes is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries, where access to healthcare resources may be limited. In China, the prevalence of diabetes also reached alarming rates, with approximately 11.6 % of the adult population affected [2]. The rapid increase in diabetes prevalence globally underscores the urgent need for effective management strategies [3]. DM is associated with numerous microvascular and macrovascular complications, including neuropathy, retinopathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular diseases. Among these complications, impaired wound healing is a significant clinical concern, particularly in individuals with poorly controlled diabetes. Chronic wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), are prevalent and contribute substantially to morbidity, healthcare costs, and reduced quality of life [4,5].

Wound healing is a complex and dynamic process involving hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. In diabetic patients, this process is often disrupted more seriously due to excessive inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, reduced fibroblast function, and compromised immune responses [6,7]. These factors might increase the risk of persistent wounds, wounds infections, and even the need of amputations in DM patients. Understanding the underlying mechanisms of impaired wound healing in diabetes is thus essential for developing targeted preventive and therapeutic strategies to mitigate these challenges.

Emerging evidence highlights the critical pathogenic roles of glucose and lipid metabolism in wound healing processes. Dysregulated glucose metabolism might lead to enhanced advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) formation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, forming a vicious cascade of further impaired wound-healing [8]. Concurrently, altered lipid metabolism, characterized by dyslipidemia and lipid peroxidation, could also exacerbate inflammation and delay tissue repair [9,10]. The interplay between these metabolic pathways creates a hostile microenvironment that hinders effective wound resolution in diabetic patients. This review summarizes research progress on the intricate relationship between glucose and lipid metabolism and the intervention impact of glucose and lipid management on the wound-healing process in diabetes, providing hints for future efficient therapeutic intervention options on attenuating diabetic wound injury and improving the wound healing management in diabetic patients [11].

To provide a coherent narrative, we organize this review into three themes: (i) pathophysiological mechanisms (redox/AGE–RAGE, HBP/polyol, NF-κB/JNK, ferroptosis), (ii) therapeutic interventions (metformin, GLP-1RA, SGLT2i, PPAR agonists, lipid-lowering strategies), and (iii) emerging mechanisms and platforms (e.g., GPR132, OTUD3, microneedles, electrospun scaffolds). Each section concludes with explicit knowledge gaps and translational priorities.

Molecular mechanisms of glucose metabolism in wound healing

Hexosamine biosynthetic pathway

One of the key consequences of hyperglycemia in diabetic wound healing is the activation of the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP). This pathway, when upregulated by elevated glucose levels, leads to the production of UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), which is used for the O-linked glycosylation of proteins [12]. The modification of proteins through this pathway has significant consequences for cellular function, particularly in the regulation of transcription factors and signaling proteins involved in wound healing [13]. The accumulation of these modified proteins can impair cellular functions such as migration, proliferation, and ECM remodeling, all of which are essential for wound healing.

Moreover, the accumulation of O-GlcNAc-modified proteins has been shown to interfere with the normal function of key signaling molecules involved in cellular survival and growth. For example, O-GlcNAc modification of transcription factors such as Sp1 and Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells(NF-κB) can lead to altered gene expression profiles that favor inflammation over healing, thus impairing the resolution of the wound healing process [14,15].

Polyol pathway

The polyol pathway, also known as the sorbitol pathway, becomes increasingly activated in states of chronic hyperglycemia. In this pathway, excess glucose is reduced to sorbitol by the enzyme aldose reductase, leading to the accumulation of sorbitol and fructose in cells [16]. This accumulation leads to osmotic stress, which can damage cellular structures and disrupt normal cellular functions. Additionally, the activation of the polyol pathway increases the production of ROS, contributing to oxidative stress. [17]. The increased oxidative stress also impairs endothelial function, contributing to the dysfunction of blood vessels and poor tissue oxygenation, which are crucial for wound healing.

Hyperglycemia and NF-κB activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release

NF-κB is a central transcription factor involved in the inflammatory response. In diabetic patients, persistent hyperglycemia leads to the activation of NF-κB, which in turn promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. These cytokines disrupt the wound healing process by prolonging the inflammatory phase and inhibiting the transition to the proliferative and remodeling phases [18,19]. Moreover, NF-κB activation leads to the upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that degrade ECM proteins, further impairing tissue repair. In chronic wounds, this dysregulated inflammatory response exacerbates tissue damage and delays wound closure [20].

GPR132-mediated macrophage reprogramming

A novel mechanism emerging in the context of diabetic wound healing involves the receptor GPR132, which plays a role in the reprogramming of macrophages to resolve inflammation. GPR132, a G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), has been shown to modulate macrophage polarization, promoting a shift from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [21]. This shift is crucial for the resolution of inflammation and the promotion of tissue repair.In diabetic wounds, targeting GPR132 to modulate macrophage polarization holds potential as a therapeutic strategy for improving wound healing by enhancing inflammation resolution [22].

OTUD3

OTUD3 (OTU deubiquitinase 3) has recently been identified as a key regulator of glucose metabolism in various tissues. OTUD3 plays a role in maintaining glucose homeostasis by regulating insulin signaling and modulating the ubiquitination of key metabolic enzymes [23]. In diabetic wounds, OTUD3 appears to be crucial for maintaining the balance between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in fibroblasts and keratinocytes, ensuring the energy demands of wound healing are met. Loss of OTUD3 function can impair cellular metabolism, leading to reduced energy production and delayed wound healing. Therefore, OTUD3 represents a promising target for therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring normal glucose metabolism and improving wound repair in diabetic patients [24].

Polyol and hexosamine/OGT flux, AGEs–RAGE signalling, PKC activation and ROS-centred oxidative stress converge on NF-κB/JNK-mediated inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and impaired extracellular-matrix remodelling, collectively suppressing angiogenesis and re-epithelialisation. Molecular mechanisms of lipid metabolism in wound healing

Dyslipidemia

Dyslipidemia, particularly increased levels of triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), along with reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL), are common in diabetes and contribute to metabolic disturbances that impair wound healing [25]. Elevated lipid levels lead to endothelial dysfunction, reduced nitric oxide production, and impaired blood flow, all of which hinder tissue repair and regeneration. Recent studies suggest that dyslipidemia promotes a pro-inflammatory state, exacerbating both cardiovascular and microvascular complications in diabetic patients, thus prolonging the healing process [26].

Lipotoxicity

Lipotoxicity, characterized by the accumulation of lipids like ceramides and diacylglycerols (DAGs), plays a significant role in diabetic wound healing impairment. Ceramides interfere with insulin signaling by disrupting Akt activation, impairing glucose uptake in key cell types such as fibroblasts and keratinocytes [27]. Similarly, DAGs activate protein kinase C (PKC), further exacerbating inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are detrimental to the healing process [28]. In diabetic conditions, this dysregulation of lipid metabolism impairs the ability of cells to migrate, proliferate, and differentiate properly during wound healing.

Dysregulated macrophage polarization (M1/M2 imbalance)

Macrophages play a pivotal role in wound healing by switching between pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes. Dyslipidemia results in an imbalance favoring M1 macrophages, which release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β [26]. This skewed polarization leads to chronic inflammation and delayed tissue repair. In contrast, M2 macrophages are essential for resolution of inflammation and tissue regeneration [22]. The shift towards an M1 phenotype in the diabetic environment significantly contributes to poor wound healing outcomes.

Beyond isolated glucose toxicity, lipid derangements amplify tissue injury through ceramides, DAG/PKC signalling and mitochondrial stress, with ferroptosis further locking wounds in an inflammatory, non-resolving state. These glucose–lipid axes converge on common effectors that blunt keratinocyte and fibroblast function and restrict neovascularisation. Interplay between glucose and lipid metabolism in wound healing

Pathophysiological mechanisms

Chronic inflammation mediated by shared pathways

Chronic inflammation is a hallmark of impaired wound healing in diabetic individuals, driven by dysregulated glucose and lipid metabolism. Both elevated glucose and lipids activate common inflammatory pathways, notably the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling pathways [29,30]. NF-κB, a master regulator of inflammation, is activated by AGEs formed during hyperglycemia, while lipid mediators, particularly pro-inflammatory eicosanoids and ceramides, also contribute to NF-κB activation [19]. Similarly, JNK is activated by oxidative stress and inflammatory signals, further amplifying tissue damage and delaying wound healing.

Oxidative stress amplification through ROS production from both pathways

Oxidative stress is a crucial player in the delayed healing of diabetic wounds. Both glucose and lipid metabolic dysregulation increase ROS production. In hyperglycemia, ROS generation is enhanced through the polyol pathway, hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Lipotoxicity, characterized by excessive ceramides and DAGs, similarly induces ROS production through activation of PKC and sphingolipid signaling [27,28]. The accumulation of ROS from both pathways further damages cellular structures, exacerbates inflammation, and impairs cellular functions essential for wound repair, such as fibroblast migration, collagen synthesis, and angiogenesis.Polyol flux contributes to the redox load of diabetic skin; for ROS-focused mechanisms and network interactions, see §I.

Therapeutic interventions

Impact of glucose control on wound healing

Glycaemic management exerts multi-level effects on the wound microenvironment—dampening systemic inflammation, improving endothelial function, and normalizing cellular stress pathways. Here we synthesize signals across metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and tirzepatide, separating direct wound data from indirect metabolic benefits. Safety nuances and agent-level heterogeneity are noted, and each subsection closes with an evidence tag to indicate wound-relevant strength (A/B/C). The practical aim is to align individualized glucose targets with best-practice foot care to create a repair-permissive milieu.

Metformin

Recent clinical and translational studies have demonstrated that metformin, through its activation of AMPK, not only improves glycemic control but also significantly enhances wound healing in diabetic patients. By activating AMPK, metformin improves cellular energy metabolism, reduces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and promotes angiogenesis and collagen synthesis—key processes that facilitate tissue repair [31]. For example, a randomized controlled trial by Riaz et al. (2021) showed that metformin treatment accelerated the healing of diabetic foot ulcers, which was attributed to enhanced AMPK phosphorylation and subsequent upregulation of VEGF in wound tissues [32]. Furthermore, a comprehensive review by summarized current clinical evidence and demonstrated that when metformin is combined with standard wound care protocols, it significantly reduces healing time and improves tissue regeneration in diabetic wounds [33]. These findings underscore metformin's dual role in bridging systemic metabolic control with local tissue repair, thereby offering promising new avenues for the management of diabetic wounds.

PPAR agonists

PPAR agonists, including PPAR-α, PPAR-β/δ, and PPAR-γ, play a significant role in diabetic wound healing by regulating inflammation, promoting angiogenesis, and enhancing fibroblast function. They help resolve inflammation by shifting macrophages to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, stimulate blood vessel formation, and improve extracellular matrix remodeling, all critical for effective tissue repair [34]. Clinical studies have shown that PPAR-α agonists, such as fenofibrate, improve endothelial function and promote angiogenesis, which is essential for diabetic wound healing [35]. Additionally, PPAR-γ agonists have been demonstrated to enhance fibroblast migration, collagen synthesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling, facilitating wound closure and strength [36]. Clinical evidence suggests that PPAR agonists can complement existing treatments and offer synergistic benefits in accelerating wound healing [37]. However, challenges such as side effects and potential drug interactions remain, and further clinical trials are needed to fully assess their safety and efficacy in diabetic wound care.

SGLT2 inhibitors

Sodium-dependent glucose transporters 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i,such as canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, and empagliflozin) are a relatively new class of antihyperglycemic agents that work by inhibiting renal glucose reabsorption, thereby lowering blood glucose levels independently of insulin secretion. In addition to their glycemic benefits, these drugs have demonstrated favorable cardiovascular and renal outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes. However, their influence on diabetic wound healing has been a subject of considerable debate, as early reports—particularly with canagliflozin—raised concerns about an increased risk of lower-limb amputations [38].

Improved glycemic control is fundamental to wound healing in diabetic patients. SGLT2 inhibitors lower plasma glucose levels by blocking the reabsorption of filtered glucose in the proximal tubule. By reducing hyperglycemia, these agents help decrease the formation of AGEs and reduce oxidative stress—both of which are detrimental to wound repair. Lower blood glucose levels lessen the “glucotoxic” environment that impairs the function of key cells involved in wound healing.Recent evidence suggests that SGLT2 inhibitors may exert direct anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [39]. Preclinical studies have indicated that these agents can reduce the expression of proinflammatory cytokines and decrease reactive oxygen species ROS production. By attenuating chronic inflammation and oxidative stress at the wound site, SGLT2 inhibitors might indirectly support the wound healing process [40].SGLT2 inhibitors are known to induce mild diuresis and natriuresis, which can reduce blood pressure and improve vascular function. Improved endothelial function and microcirculation may benefit the wound-healing environment by enhancing the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to the damaged tissue. However, excessive diuresis and volume depletion in some patients could theoretically impair perfusion if not carefully monitored.Some experimental studies suggest that SGLT2 inhibitors may modulate cellular energy metabolism and improve mitochondrial function [38]. Enhanced mitochondrial efficiency in skin cells such as keratinocytes and fibroblasts could promote cell survival and proliferation during the wound-healing process.

Concerns about lower-limb amputation with SGLT2 inhibition originated from CANVAS (canagliflozin), but subsequent large outcome trials did not reproduce a class-wide excess risk, indicating heterogeneity across agents, populations, and event ascertainment. Accordingly, contemporary practice favors individualized limb-risk appraisal (peripheral artery disease, prior amputation, advanced neuropathy, active infection, foot deformity) rather than blanket avoidance. With respect to wound healing, any benefit of SGLT2 inhibitors is likely indirect—through improved glycaemic control, attenuation of systemic inflammation, optimization of volume status and endothelial function—while wound-specific randomized endpoints remain sparse. In high-risk limbs, therapy should be embedded within best-practice foot care, including rigorous off-loading, adequate hydration/volume management, and close surveillance for early signs of ischaemia or infection.

GLP-1 receptor agonists

Originally introduced as glucose-lowering agents for type 2 diabetes management, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists(GLP-1 RAs) have attracted increasing attention for their pleiotropic effects in tissue repair and wound healing. Both preclinical studies and emerging clinical data indicate that GLP1R activation can improve the wound-healing process in diabetic patients by modulating inflammatory responses, enhancing angiogenesis, and promoting cell proliferation.

GLP-1 receptors belong to the G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family. Upon ligand binding, these receptors primarily couple with the Gs protein, leading to an increase in intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) [44]. Elevated cAMP levels activate protein kinase A (PKA), which inhibits the NFκB signaling pathway. This inhibition results in reduced transcription of proinflammatory cytokines.Because chronic inflammation is a key factor contributing to delayed wound healing in diabetes, the anti-inflammatory effects of GLP1RAs are critical for creating an environment conducive to tissue repair.GLP1RAs also stimulate the phosphatidylinositol 3kinase (PI3K)/Akt signaling cascade [45]. Akt activation increases the expression and activity of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), which boosts nitric oxide (NO) production. NO is essential for vasodilation and improves local blood flow to the wound area. In addition, NO promotes endothelial cell migration and proliferation, which are key steps in angiogenesis. Improved angiogenesis enhances oxygen and nutrient delivery to the wound bed, thus accelerating healing [46].Efficient re-epithelialization, which is critical for wound closure, depends on the migration and proliferation of keratinocytes. Studies have demonstrated that GLP1RAs (such as exendin4 and liraglutide) stimulate the PI3K/Akt pathway in keratinocytes [47]. This stimulation enhances their motility and proliferation, thereby promoting faster re-epithelialization and wound closure.Diabetic conditions are marked by elevated blood glucose levels that lead to increased ROS and oxidative stress. GLP1RAs have been shown to activate AMPK. AMPK activation also indirectly supports eNOS activity, further contributing to NO production and improved vascular function. By lowering oxidative stress, these drugs protect wound site cells from damage, supporting effective healing.A persistent proinflammatory environment in diabetic wounds can hinder the healing process [48]. GLP1RAs help modulate the immune response by promoting the polarization of macrophages from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. M2 macrophages secrete various growth factors that are essential for tissue remodeling and angiogenesis. This shift not only reduces harmful inflammation but also supports the release of factors that facilitate wound repair.

Impact of lipid-lowering therapy on wound healing

Beyond glycaemia, lipid modulation addresses vascular and inflammatory drivers that shape tissue repair. We appraise statins/ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, inclisiran, and HTD1801 with an emphasis on where evidence is cardiovascularly robust yet wound-specific endpoints remain sparse. Mechanistic plausibility (endothelial support, anti-inflammation) is summarized alongside key safety considerations, and evidence tags clarify that several conclusions are indirect for wound closure. The goal is judicious positioning of lipid-lowering within comprehensive wound care—useful, but not overstated.

Tirzepatide

Tirzepatide and Semaglutide have significant differences in lipid regulation, mainly due to their different mechanisms of action and targets. Tirzepatide is a dual agonist that acts on both glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptors and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptors. This dual mechanism of action allows it to have a positive effect on lipid metabolism while regulating blood glucose. Clinical studies have shown that Tirzepatide has a weight loss effect of 22.5 % after 72 weeks of administration, showing significant lipid regulation ability. In contrast, semaglutide is a selective GLP-1 receptor agonist that regulates blood glucose and body weight primarily through the activation of GLP-1 receptors, and its effect on lipid metabolism is mainly achieved indirectly through weight loss and improved insulin sensitivity. The study showed that semaglutide was 14.9 % weight loss after 68 weeks of administration, and its lipid regulation effect was mainly attributed to weight loss and blood sugar control [38].Therefore, Tirzepatide may have greater potential in directly regulating lipid metabolism due to its unique dual receptor agonist mechanism, while the lipid regulation effect of semaglutide is more the result of its comprehensive metabolic improvement. ⤎

The mechanism of action of Tirzepatide at the molecular level is quite complex. It regulates insulin secretion and blood glucose levels not only by simultaneously activating GLP-1 and GIP receptors but also exerts extensive effects on lipid metabolism. Firstly, Tirzepatide promotes the conversion of white adipose tissue to brown adipose tissue, a process accomplished by upregulating the expression of genes associated with thermogenesis and energy expenditure, thereby increasing the body's basal metabolic rate and reducing fat accumulation. Secondly, it regulates the AMP-cAMP-ATF2/DUSP4 signaling pathway, which plays a key regulatory role within adipocytes, enhancing the thermogenic capacity of cells while alleviating the inflammatory state in adipose tissue by reducing the release of inflammatory factors. At the same time, Tirzepatide can significantly boost the secretion of adiponectin, which, as an important adipokine, not only enhances insulin sensitivity but also plays a pivotal role in lipid metabolism, anti-inflammation, and cardiovascular protection. Through the synergistic action of these multiple molecular targets, Tirzepatide provides a comprehensive therapeutic strategy for improving diabetes and related metabolic disorders.

Inclisiran

Inclisiran is a small interfering RNA (siRNA) drug that reduces low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels by inhibiting the expression of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) in hepatocytes. Its uniqueness lies in requiring only two subcutaneous injections per year to continuously and effectively lower LDL-C levels. In patients with diabetes, dyslipidemia is a common comorbidity that increases the risk of cardiovascular disease. The lipid-lowering effects of Inclisiran may provide potential benefits for these patients. In July 2023, Novartis announced that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the expansion of Inclisiran's indication as an adjunct therapy to diet and statin therapy for lowering LDL-C levels in patients with primary hypercholesterolemia at increased risk for heart disease. This expanded indication includes patients with comorbidities, such as hypertension and diabetes, who have not yet experienced a cardiovascular event. Clinical studies have shown that Inclisiran significantly reduces LDL-C levels [39]. However, more data is needed regarding its specific efficacy and safety in patients with diabetes. Therefore, in clinical practice, the use of Inclisiran in diabetic patients should be considered under the guidance of healthcare professionals, weighing the potential benefits and risks based on individual circumstances.

Evolocumab

Evolocumab is a PCSK9 inhibitor that has been shown to have significant lipid-lowering and cardiovascular protective effects in diabetic patients. Studies have shown that the addition of evolocumab further reduces low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels in patients with type 2 diabetes treated with the maximum tolerated dose of statin. For example, a randomized, placebo-controlled study showed an average 54.3 % reduction in LDL-C after 12 weeks of Evolocumab treatment, compared to only 1.1 % in the placebo group [40].

In addition, the use of evolocumab in patients with diabetes mellitus has also shown a reduction in the risk of cardiovascular events. In a large clinical trial, evolocumab significantly reduced cardiovascular risk in patients with or without diabetes. Specifically, for the primary endpoint event, the hazard ratio of evolocumab in patients with diabetes and non-diabetic patients was 0.83 and 0.87, respectively.

Of note, evolocumab did not increase the risk of new-onset diabetes and did not exacerbate glycemic control. One study showed that evolocumab did not increase the risk of new-onset diabetes and did not worsen glycemic levels in patients with metabolic syndrome. This finding is also supported by another study, showing that evolocumab has no negative effect on glucose metabolism while lowering LDL-C.

Ursodeoxychoberine

Ursodeoxychoberine (HTD1801) is a new oral drug consisting of ionic salts formed from ursodeoxycholic acid and berberine, which have anti-inflammatory and metabolic modulatory effects. Recent studies have shown that HTD1801 has shown significant efficacy in the management of blood glucose and lipid metabolism in patients with type 2 diabetes.

A double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trial published in JAMA Network Open evaluated the efficacy and safety of HTD1801 in 113 patients with type 2 diabetes. The results showed that patients treated with HTD1801 had a significant decrease in glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels at week 12, and most patients achieved the goal of less than 7 % HbA1c. In addition, HTD1801 significantly reduced fasting blood glucose and 0.5-h postprandial blood glucose levels in a dose-dependent manner [41].

In terms of lipid metabolism, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and non-HDL cholesterol levels were significantly reduced between weeks 4 and 12 in the HTD1801 treatment group, while no significant changes were observed in the placebo group.

In addition, HTD1801 exhibit potential hepatoprotective effects. At the 4th week of treatment, the levels of alanine aminotransferase, aspartate transferase and γ-glutamyl transferase in the HTD1801 group showed a downward trend, suggesting that they may improve liver function.

Emerging mechanisms & platforms

New biology is reshaping the field: ferroptosis, GPR132-linked lactate signaling, and OTUD3-mediated stress responses point to testable nodes, while human skin–humanized mouse models, microneedle delivery, and electrospun scaffolds provide translational tooling. We highlight hypotheses that are promising but largely preclinical, specify the knowledge gaps, and outline validation steps that bridge platform readouts to patient-relevant endpoints (closure, time-to-heal, recurrence). A companion table maps each model to clinical outcomes to guide study design and prioritization.

Combination therapies targeting both glucose and lipid metabolism

In diabetic wound healing, combination therapies that concurrently target both glucose and lipid metabolism offer synergistic benefits by correcting hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia—two interrelated metabolic disturbances that hinder tissue repair. These therapies work by normalizing glycolytic flux to restore cellular energy balance and reducing glucotoxicity, which limits the formation of AGEs and subsequent oxidative stress. At the same time, they modulate lipid metabolism by regulating de novo fatty acid synthesis, cholesterol biosynthesis, and fatty acid oxidation, thereby improving endothelial function and promoting angiogenesis through enhanced nitric oxide (NO) production. This dual modulation not only shifts the inflammatory microenvironment—by encouraging the polarization of macrophages from a proinflammatory M1 to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype,but also supports keratinocyte migration and proliferation, which are essential for re-epithelialization. Clinical observations further support these mechanisms; for example, retrospective studies and pilot trials have demonstrated that combination regimens such as metformin plus statins or innovative dual-responsive topical hydrogels accelerate wound closure and reduce diabetic foot ulcer incidence, while nutritional interventions like intermittent fasting have been shown to improve both glycemic and lipid profiles, thereby enhancing overall wound healing outcomes.L-carnitine not only has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, but also improves cellular energy metabolism. Its effects in accelerating wound healing suggest that it may become an effective means of adjuvant treatment for diabetic ulcers. In addition, L-carnitine also has anti-glycation effects, inhibiting the formation of AGEs, which may have potential benefits in preventing diabetes-related microvascular complications and neuropathy[53]. In summary, extending the application of L-carnitine from aging wound healing to diabetic wound repair can not only improve the healing process of diabetic ulcers but may also alleviate other diabetes-related complications, showing important clinical translational prospects. Together, these findings provide a strong rationale for integrated therapies that address both glucose and lipid dysregulation to create a more favorable wound microenvironment and ultimately improve healing in diabetic patients.

Novel agents targeting specific receptors in wound healing

Recent advancements in diabetic wound healing have focused on novel agents targeting specific receptors to enhance tissue repair. One promising approach involves the use of small molecule stabilizers of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), which play a crucial role in cellular responses to low oxygen levels. Stabilizing HIF-1α has been shown to accelerate diabetic wound healing by promoting angiogenesis and improving tissue regeneration [42]. Another strategy targets MMPs, enzymes that, when overactive, can impair wound healing by degrading extracellular matrix components. Natural products have been identified that modulate MMP activity, thereby promoting a more balanced environment conducive to healing [43]. Additionally, the application of recombinant human epidermal growth factor (rhEGF), such as nepidermin, has been explored to stimulate cell proliferation and migration, essential processes in wound repair. Nepidermin acts as an agonist of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), facilitating the healing of diabetic foot ulcers [44]. These innovative therapies represent significant progress in targeting specific molecular pathways involved in diabetic wound healing, offering potential for improved clinical outcomes.

Translational and clinical insights

Translational research in diabetic wound healing has identified critical pathophysiological mechanisms—such as chronic inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, defective extracellular matrix remodeling, and altered cellular responses under hyperglycemic conditions—that hinder normal repair processes. These insights have driven the development of innovative clinical interventions including advanced wound dressings, negative pressure wound therapy(NPWT), hyperbaric oxygen therapy(HBOT), and the application of growth factors, as well as regenerative strategies like stem cell therapy and bioengineered skin substitutes. By bridging basic science and clinical practice, these translational advances are informing more targeted and effective treatments to improve healing outcomes and reduce complications in diabetic patients.

Advanced wound dressings

Advanced wound dressings play a pivotal role in the management and healing of diabetic wounds. In diabetic wound healing, these dressings are designed not only to protect the wound from external contamination and mechanical trauma but also to maintain a moist environment that facilitates cellular migration, angiogenesis, and granulation tissue formation [45]. Modern dressings, including hydrocolloids, hydrogels, foam dressings, alginate dressings, have been developed to address specific challenges associated with diabetic ulcers, such as excessive exudate, infection, and impaired healing due to chronic inflammation and poor vascularization. Some dressings are further enhanced with incorporated antimicrobial agents, growth factors, or even stem cells to actively modulate the wound microenvironment, reduce bacterial load, and promote tissue regeneration [46]. By optimizing moisture balance, absorbing exudates, and delivering bioactive molecules, these advanced dressings can accelerate wound closure, reduce complications, and improve overall healing outcomes in diabetic patients.

Negative pressure wound therapy in diabetes wound healing

NPWT has emerged as a valuable adjunct in the management of diabetic wounds, particularly diabetic foot ulcers. By applying controlled suction to the wound bed, NPWT effectively removes excess exudate and reduces edema, thereby improving local blood flow and oxygenation. This mechanical action not only helps in reducing the bacterial load but also promotes the formation of granulation tissue and accelerates wound contraction [47]. In the context of diabetes, where wound healing is often impaired due to chronic inflammation, poor perfusion, and neuropathy, NPWT creates a moist environment conducive to healing and can significantly shorten the time to closure when combined with appropriate debridement, infection control, and optimized glycemic management [48]. Clinical research have demonstrated that NPWT can improve healing outcomes, reduce the incidence of infection, and ultimately lower the risk of amputation in diabetic patients.

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy

HBOT involves the delivery of 100 % oxygen at elevated atmospheric pressures, significantly increasing the oxygen dissolved in the blood and subsequently the oxygen available to tissues. In the context of diabetic wound healing, especially for diabetic foot ulcers, HBOT addresses the common issue of local tissue hypoxia resulting from poor blood flow [49]. Enhanced oxygenation promotes angiogenesis, collagen synthesis, and immune function, all of which are critical for wound repair. Clinical studies have demonstrated that HBOT can accelerate wound closure, reduce infection rates, and lower the risk of amputation when used as an adjunct to standard care—which typically includes debridement, infection management, and glycemic control [50]. Although not a standalone treatment, HBOT is increasingly recognized as a valuable component in comprehensive diabetic wound management strategies.

Stem cell therapy and bioengineered skin substitutes

Stem cell therapy and bioengineered skin substitutes are two innovative strategies that have shown promise in enhancing diabetic wound healing. Stem cell therapy—particularly using mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)—leverages the cells’ ability to secrete a rich array of growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines, which help modulate inflammation, promote angiogenesis, and stimulate tissue regeneration [51]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that MSCs can accelerate granulation tissue formation and improve overall wound closure in diabetic foot ulcers. In parallel, bioengineered skin substitutes, which can be either living skin equivalents incorporating keratinocytes and fibroblasts or acellular dermal matrices, provide a biocompatible scaffold that supports cell migration and extracellular matrix deposition [52]. These substitutes not only protect the wound from infection and dehydration but also actively promote healing by delivering growth factors and mimicking the natural dermal environment. Together, these therapies offer a multifaceted approach to overcoming the impaired healing seen in diabetic wounds, though further large-scale clinical trials are needed to refine treatment protocols, ensure safety, and confirm long-term efficacy.

Future directions

Advancing wound healing in diabetic patients requires identifying novel molecular targets that address the interplay between glucose and lipid metabolism. Research is increasingly focused on mechanisms such as the role of AGEs in exacerbating inflammation and oxidative stress. Emerging targets include molecules like SIRT6, GPR132, and Nogo-B, which regulate inflammation, lipid metabolism, and angiogenesis. For example, therapies targeting SIRT6 can potentially modulate glucose homeostasis and reduce oxidative stress, improving cellular function in wounds. Future research must prioritize mechanisms that integrate systemic metabolic improvements with local wound-specific effects to ensure effective therapeutic outcomes.

Diabetes and its complications, including chronic wounds, present with significant interpatient variability, driven by genetic, environmental, and metabolic factors. Personalized medicine approaches hold immense promise in tailoring treatments to individual needs. Integrating patient-specific data, including genetic predispositions, comorbidities, and metabolic profiles, can enhance therapeutic precision. For instance, biomarker-based stratification of patients, such as measuring levels of adiponectin or VEGF, can help determine the most effective therapy, whether pharmacological, dietary, or lifestyle interventions. This precision approach would also mitigate adverse effects while optimizing wound healing outcomes.

Omics technologies, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, are transforming our understanding of complex biological processes involved in wound healing. These high-throughput platforms enable the identification of key pathways and molecular networks altered in diabetic wounds. For example, transcriptomics studies have revealed dysregulated inflammatory and angiogenic pathways in chronic wounds, while proteomics has identified potential biomarkers like MMPs and TIMPs. The application of multi-omics approaches can uncover new therapeutic targets, provide insights into treatment resistance, and facilitate the development of more effective interventions tailored to patient-specific metabolic and molecular profiles.

Regenerative medicine and bioengineering are revolutionizing the treatment of diabetic wounds. Innovations such as bioengineered skin substitutes, growth factor-releasing scaffolds, and advanced biomaterials have shown promise in promoting tissue regeneration and improving wound healing. Recent advances in 3D bioprinting and nanotechnology have enabled the development of customized scaffolds that can deliver therapeutic agents, such as insulin, VEGF, or antimicrobial peptides, directly to the wound site. Additionally, stem cell-based therapies, including the use of mesenchymal stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells, have demonstrated potential in modulating the wound microenvironment, reducing inflammation, and enhancing angiogenesis and tissue repair.

Conclusion

Diabetic wound healing is profoundly influenced by the interplay of glucose and lipid metabolism, with dysregulation in these pathways contributing to delayed healing, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress. Effective control of glucose and lipid levels has been shown to mitigate these effects, highlighting the critical role of metabolic management in improving wound outcomes. Strategies such as AMPK activation, modulation of PPAR signaling, and interventions targeting AGEs offer promising avenues for addressing these metabolic disruptions.

An integrated approach is essential for improving outcomes in diabetic wound care. This involves combining pharmacological therapies with non-pharmacological strategies, such as dietary interventions and exercise, to address both systemic metabolic dysregulation and local wound-specific factors. Emerging therapies, including personalized medicine approaches, combination drug regimens, and regenerative medicine techniques, hold great potential for transforming clinical outcomes.

Despite significant advancements, gaps remain in our understanding of the molecular mechanisms linking glucose and lipid metabolism to wound healing. Continued research is necessary to identify novel molecular targets, develop predictive biomarkers, and optimize therapeutic approaches. Integration of omics technologies and bioengineering innovations will be pivotal in bridging these knowledge gaps, paving the way for precision medicine in diabetic wound care.
