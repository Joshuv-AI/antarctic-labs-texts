Risk factors for COVID-19 symptom severity across clinical categories among hospitalised patients in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia: a prospective cohort study

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to quantify how patient risk factors relate to COVID-19 severity across categories 1–5 in a prospective, hospital-based cohort. We hypothesised that greater severity would be associated with higher odds of intensive care unit (ICU) admission and in-hospital mortality. Secondary aims were to assess associations with age, viral variants, symptom clusters, lymphocyte count, fasting blood glucose and cytokine profiles.DesignProspective cohort study.SettingA secondary-care/tertiary-care hospital and linked community settings in Cheras, Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia.ParticipantsThis study was nested within the COVGEN project, a prospective COVID-19 cohort conducted at Hospital Canselor Tuanku Muhriz UKM (HCTM), Cheras Health Clinic and the Bandar Tun Razak COVID-19 Assessment Centre in Cheras, Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, from 1 August 2021 to 31 October 2022. 2532 participants were enrolled at baseline. Eligible participants were Malaysian citizens aged 12–18 years (paediatric/adolescent) or ≥18 years who had reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction–confirmed COVID-19 at recruitment and resided in Kuala Lumpur or Selangor. Patients who had a clinically unstable condition and those who declined participation (personally or via a next-of-kin or legal representative) were excluded. This analysis included 559 patients hospitalised at HCTM; after excluding five with incomplete questionnaires, 554 remained for analysis (413 admitted to general wards and 141 to ICUs). Categories 3–5 comprised hospitalised patients, whereas categories 1–2 included hospitalised individuals and a subset recruited from community settings.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes included disease severity (categories 4–5 vs 1–3), ICU admission and in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included associations with age strata, viral variant (delta vs omicron), symptom clusters, lymphocyte count, fasting blood glucose and cytokines: interferon gamma-inducible protein 10, interferon gamma, interleukins 8, 10, 2, 6 and 7 and tumour necrosis factor alpha.Results141 of 554 (25.5%) patients required ICU care. Compared with milder categories, category 5 was associated with markedly higher odds of ICU admission (OR 204.50; 95% CI 37.54 to 1114.18; p<0.001) and death (OR 13.20, 95% CI 3.97 to 43.86; p<0.05). Older age was associated with greater severity: >55 versus <36 years for categories 3 (OR 4.575; 95% CI 1.472 to 14.218; p=0.009) and 5 (OR 18.003; 95% CI 1.139 to 284.601; p=0.040) and 36–55 versus <36 years for category 5 (OR 18.412; 95% CI 1.094 to 309.951; p=0.043). Delta accounted for 47.7% of infections compared with 17.9% for omicron, with a higher proportion of category 4 presentations among delta cases. Systemic, pulmonary/respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms, lymphopenia, hyperglycaemia and elevated cytokines were each associated with increasing severity (p<0.05).ConclusionsAn increasing clinical severity category was strongly associated with ICU admission and mortality. Age, delta infection, specific symptom clusters, lymphopenia, hyperglycaemia and pro-inflammatory cytokines identified higher-risk patients, supporting risk-stratified management and prioritisation for enhanced monitoring.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the novel coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, became one of the most significant global health challenges in recent history. The disease, first identified in December 2019 in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China, quickly spread worldwide, leading to millions of infections and significant loss of life.1 2 Malaysia, like many other nations, experienced multiple waves of COVID-19 outbreaks following its first confirmed case on 25 January 2020.3 As case numbers increased, various preventive measures were implemented, including movement restrictions, border controls, widespread COVID-19 testing and accelerated vaccination efforts to curb transmission and protect public health.4

SARS-CoV-2 belongs to the coronavirus family, which is known to cause respiratory illnesses in humans.5 It not only shares similarities with the previously identified coronaviruses, SARS-CoV and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus, but also possesses unique characteristics that contribute to its distinct epidemiology and clinical presentation. The virus is primarily transmitted through respiratory droplets generated when an infected individual coughs, sneezes, talks or breathes, with close contact and enclosed spaces posing higher risks of transmission.1 5

COVID-19 presents with a wide range of clinical manifestations, from mild or moderate respiratory symptoms to severe pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).6 It can also affect multiple organ systems, leading to complications such as cardiovascular abnormalities, thrombosis, renal impairment and neurological manifestations.7 Symptom severity varies among individuals, with certain populations, including older adults and those with underlying comorbidities, at higher risk of developing severe illness and adverse outcomes.

Intense efforts by governments, healthcare systems, the scientific community and individuals were undertaken to understand, mitigate and control viral spread, including in Malaysia. Despite significant progress in combating the pandemic, challenges persisted, including the emergence of new viral variants. Therefore, continued research remained crucial in the ongoing response to COVID-19.

As of March 2024, Malaysia had reported more than 5 million confirmed COVID-19 cases, including more than 37 000 deaths.8 During this period, up to 33.0% of patients were on ventilation, 65% were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and 70.0% were hospitalised (data as of seventh March 2024). Given that COVID-19 spans a broad clinical spectrum, from asymptomatic infection to severe disease requiring ICU admission, it is crucial to identify patients at risk of progressing to severe illness. This would enable earlier intervention and improved clinical management to reduce mortality.

This cohort study included laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 patients from Hospital Canselor Tuanku Muhriz UKM (HCTM), Cheras Health Clinic (KK) and the Bandar Tun Razak COVID-19 Assessment Centre (CAC), Cheras, Kuala Lumpur. During the pandemic, HCTM was designated as one of the hospitals in Malaysia responsible for receiving and treating COVID-19 patients. This study aimed to determine risk factors associated with infection severity across categories of hospitalised COVID-19 patients and describe the cohort’s clinical outcomes and laboratory findings. The data enabled identification of predictive factors that distinguish COVID-19 severity, providing prognostic markers to guide targeted interventions and closer monitoring across severity categories.

This study employed a prospective cohort design encompassing the full clinical spectrum of COVID-19 (categories 1–5), in contrast to most local studies, which have been retrospective and largely restricted to hospitalised or ICU populations. Prospective enrolment using International Severe Acute Respiratory and Emerging Infection Consortium (ISARIC)–WHO category definitions minimised misclassification and enabled formal analyses across the severity gradient. Recruitment uses a mixed hospital–community framework: categories 1–2 were enrolled from both hospital and community settings, whereas categories 3–5 were enrolled from hospital care only. This approach enabled characterisation of the transition from mild to severe disease within a unified analytic framework. The recruitment period aligned with the local epidemiological curve and spanned the surge and post-peak inflection (1 August 2021–3 October 2022), allowing direct comparison of clinical outcomes during delta predominance and early omicron circulation within a single protocol.

Beyond routine demographic and comorbidity data, clinical, laboratory and immunological phenotyping were integrated, including lymphocyte counts, fasting blood glucose and a targeted cytokine panel: interferon gamma-inducible protein 10 (IP-10), interferon gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin 8 (IL-8), interleukin 10 (IL-10), interleukin 2 (IL-2), interleukin 6 (IL-6), interleukin 7 (IL-7), and tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α). This provided biologically grounded risk stratification that is rarely reported in Malaysian cohorts. To move beyond simple associations, Bayesian network directed acyclic graph (BN-DAG) modelling was applied to cytokine profiles in hospitalised patients to infer putative causal structure, directional dependencies and potential mediating pathways. Effect sizes were reported transparently, with ORs, 95% CIs, exact p-values and absolute event rates for ICU admission and mortality, addressing common reporting gaps where relative measures are presented without baseline risks. Age effects were examined using age-banded severity models (<36, 36–55 and >55 years), clarifying how age modifies the severity gradient rather than treating age as a single continuous covariate. Eligibility criteria, reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) confirmation across all categories, pre-specified outcomes and standardised data abstraction were applied throughout, strengthening internal validity and reproducibility compared with heterogeneous record-review datasets.

Methods

Recruitment and selection criteria

This study was derived from the COVGEN project, a prospective COVID-19 cohort conducted at HCTM, KK and CAC, Cheras, Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. The study was conducted from 1 August 2021 to 31 October 2022 and successfully enrolled 2532 participants at baseline.

Inclusion criteria

Malaysian citizens aged 12‒18 years (paediatric and adolescent cases) and adults aged ≥18 years who tested RT-PCR–positive for COVID-19 at recruitment (for those recruited at HCTM, KK and CAC).

Malaysian citizens aged 12‒18 years (paediatric and adolescent cases) and adults aged ≥18 years who tested RT-PCR–positive for COVID-19 at recruitment (for those recruited at HCTM, KK and CAC).

Residing in the Kuala Lumpur and Selangor areas to facilitate serial follow-ups.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with clinically unstable conditions at the time of recruitment.

Study sample

This analysis involved a subset of 559 participants who were hospitalised at HCTM during the recruitment period. After excluding five participants with incomplete questionnaires, 554 remained for analysis, comprising 413 individuals admitted to general wards and 141 admitted to ICUs.

Categories 3–5 comprised hospitalised patients. Categories 1–2 included hospitalised individuals and a subset recruited from community settings (KK and CAC). Patients in categories 1 and 2 were admitted primarily for non-COVID comorbidities (online supplemental table 1). SARS-CoV-2 positivity was confirmed by RT-PCR and classified as asymptomatic (category 1) or symptomatic without pneumonia (category 2).

The sample was not intended to be representative of all COVID-19 patients during the study period, as convenience sampling was used. Participants were recruited voluntarily if they fulfilled the inclusion criteria and resided in Kuala Lumpur and Selangor to ensure logistical feasibility for follow-up visits. Eligible patients were identified and approached by the study team at participating sites. The overall follow-up response rate was 35.7% (899/2520).

Clinical definitions

The target population comprised individuals with laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 receiving care at HCTM during the recruitment window. Each case was categorised according to clinical severity: category I, asymptomatic case; category 2, symptomatic without pneumonia; category 3, pneumonia without hypoxia; category IV, pneumonia with hypoxia requiring oxygen supplementation therapy; and category V, critically ill with or without organ failure.9 10 For this study, disease severity was defined by the worst clinical progression (ie, the highest severity category experienced) during hospitalisation and as designated at discharge. Disease complications were defined according to the ISARIC–WHO Case Report Form.11 12

Sample collection

Four tubes of fasting venous blood were collected: one serum separator tube, two ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes and one fluoride tube (Becton Dickinson, USA). At least 15 mL of urine sample and a nasal swab sample were also collected.

Data collection

Clinical data were managed by the Department of Diagnostic Laboratory Services at HCTM and retrieved from HCTM’s Order Medical Systems. Sociodemographic characteristics (sex, age, ethnicity and marital status), medical history (including comorbidities), pregnancy status and admission/discharge details were obtained from participants’ medical records after consent. COVID-19-related information, including date of infection, symptoms during acute infection and vaccination status (number of doses, dates and vaccine types), was also extracted. In addition, the medical record number and contact numbers for participants and their next-of-kin were recorded to facilitate future medical record retrieval and follow-up assessments. Data collection was conducted within the ethics approval period (1 August 2021 to 28 July 2023).

Laboratory test

Biochemical tests

Fasting blood sugar, fasting lipid profile and renal profile were analysed using the Cobas 6000 analyser (Roche, Switzerland). Haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was measured using the Variant II Turbo haemoglobin testing system (Bio-Rad, USA).

Neutralising antibody test

Neutralising antibodies against the wild-type Wuhan SARS-CoV-2 were measured using the cPASS SARS-CoV-2 Neutralising Antibody Detection Assay (GenScript, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cPASS assay measures, semi-quantitatively, neutralising antibodies that inhibit the interaction between the receptor-binding domain (RBD) and the cell surface receptor angiotensin-converting enzyme-2 (ACE2).13 Briefly, serum samples were heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min, diluted 1:10 with serum diluent, mixed with RBD-Horse Radish Peroxidase (HRP) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Next, 100 µL of serum/RBD-HRP mixture was added to a 96-well plate pre-coated with purified human ACE2 protein and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. After washing, tetramethylbenzidine substrate was added, followed by the stop solution. Absorbance was read immediately at 450 nm using a plate reader. Results were expressed as percent inhibition; samples with >30% inhibition were considered positive for anti-SARS-CoV-2 neutralising antibodies.13 All tests were conducted in duplicate.

Cytokine test

Eight serum cytokines and chemokines were measured using a fluorophore bead–based multiplex immunoassay according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Human XL Cytokine Premixed Kit, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA) and run on Luminex MAGPIX System. The analytes measured were IP-10, IFN-γ, IL-8, IL-10, IL-2, IL-6, IL-7 and TNF-α. Briefly, magnetic microparticles pre-coated with cytokine-specific capture antibodies and embedded with fluorophores were incubated with 50 µL of serum. A cocktail of biotinylated detection antibodies was then added to bind the captured analytes. Streptavidin–phycoerythrin conjugate was subsequently added, and fluorescence was measured by the Luminex analyser to determine cytokine concentrations. Concentrations were quantified and analysed using Luminex xPONENT software (V1.5.13).

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean and SD, whereas skewed variables are reported as median and IQR. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare groups, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and the χ2 test was used to assess differences between groups. Univariable multinomial logistic regression was performed to evaluate association between individual risk factors and COVID-19 severity.

For regression analyses, categories 1 and 2 were combined to represent the mild group, because both categories exhibited minimal symptoms and had small sample sizes, limiting the stability of OR estimates. χ2 tests were used alongside ORs to assess associations between categorical variables. Although ORs quantify the strength of association, χ2 tests provide complementary evidence of statistical significance. Variables with p<0.05 in univariable analyses were included in the multivariable multinomial logistic regression model. Forest plots were generated to summarise individual ORs and 95% CIs for the associations between symptom domains and severity categories; these plots were used for visualisation only and not to derive pooled estimates. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistical Software V23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Causal cytokine model

The BNstruct R package (V.1.4.10) was utilised to construct a BN-DAG to characterise causal relationships among seven cytokines: IL-2, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-10, TNF-α and IP-10.14 IFN-γ was excluded from BN construction because a large proportion of values were undetectable (93.9%), which hindered the algorithm’s ability to learn an optimal network structure, even with imputation. The max-min-hill-climbing (MMHC) algorithm was employed to establish the initial network structure and orient edges using Bayesian score–based algorithms.15 Since the BN model could only accommodate discrete variables, each cytokine was classified into ‘high’ or ‘normal’ before model development, based on the reference ranges presented in table 1.

Results

Between August 2021 and October 2022, a total of 2532 participants enrolled in the COVGEN project at the baseline time point. Of these, 559 individuals were hospitalised during the recruitment period. However, for this study, only 554 participants were eligible for analysis; 5 were excluded due to incomplete questionnaires. Risk factors associated with infection severity were examined across categories of hospitalised COVID-19 patients. Online supplemental table 1 provides an overview of the demographic and clinical characteristics of these patients.

Patients were distributed as follows: category 1 (n=28), category 2 (n=97), category 3 (n=123), category 4 (n=237) and category 5 (n=69). Overall, 413 (74.5%) patients were admitted to the general ward, whereas 141 (25.5%) were admitted to the ICU. Among ward admissions, most cases were from categories 3 and 4, with 120 (97.6%) and 183 (77.2%) patients, respectively. In contrast, among ICU admissions, categories 4 and 5 accounted for the highest numbers of cases, with 54 (22.8%) and 65 (94.2%) patients, respectively, indicating more critical illness. Statistical analysis showed a highly significant association (p<0.001) between admission status (ward vs ICU) and symptom severity across the five categories. As expected, higher categories were associated with a greater likelihood of severe symptoms.

Of the total analysed population, 307 (55.4%) were male and 247 (44.6%) were female. No statistically significant association was found between sex and symptom severity (p=0.870). Most participants were ≥55 years, comprising 62.8% of the analysed population; among them, the majority were in category 4 (n=161; 67.9%). A significant association was observed between age and symptom severity (p<0.001), indicating that older individuals were more likely to experience severe symptoms than younger participants (<55 years).

A significant association was also observed between ethnicity and COVID-19 symptom severity (p=0.005, online supplemental table 1). Malays constituted the largest proportion of the cohort (57.2%), followed by Chinese (34.7%) and Indian (6.7%), with other ethnic groups collectively comprising 1.4%. Both Malay and Chinese participants had the highest prevalence in category 4 (56.1% and 39.2%, respectively), whereas Indian participants and those from other ethnic groups showed a higher prevalence in category 3 (13.0% and 2.4%, respectively).

Most participants were married (76.9%). Among female participants, most were not pregnant (95.1%). However, neither marital status nor pregnancy status was significantly associated with the category of COVID-19 symptom severity (p=0.236 and p=0.072, respectively).

Regarding comorbidities, all patients had at least one pre-existing condition. The most prevalent comorbidities were hypertension (58.5%), diabetes mellitus (45.7%), dyslipidaemia (26.0%), cardiovascular disease (24.2%) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) (16.8%) (online supplemental table 1). The prevalence of these comorbidities varied across severity categories, with most peaking in category 4. However, statistically significant differences were observed only for hypertension (p=0.025) and CKD (p=0.001). These findings suggest that certain comorbidities may influence COVID-19 severity, highlighting the importance of monitoring and managing these conditions in affected COVID-19 patients.

A large proportion of participants had been vaccinated (n=436; 78.7%). Most had received at least a second dose (52.3%), and 21.8% had received a booster dose. Vaccination dose status was significantly associated with the COVID-19 severity category (p=0.001). Notably, despite having received at least a second dose, hospitalised patients exhibited the highest prevalence in category 4. The lower prevalence in the booster group may suggest a benefit of booster doses (online supplemental table 1). Overall, these findings emphasise the importance of completing the full vaccination regimen, including booster doses, to potentially reduce disease severity.

Further analysis showed a significant association between vaccine type and COVID-19 severity category for the first dose, second dose and the combination of both doses (all p<0.001). Among vaccinated patients, those who received the inactivated whole-virion SARS-CoV-2 (Sinovac) vaccine either as first, second or both doses showed a higher prevalence of severity (category 4). In some cases, vaccine type was unavailable because patients were critically ill. For the booster dose, no significant association was found between the booster vaccine type and severity category (p=0.787). However, because many patients did not receive a booster (52.3%), booster-related analyses may be underpowered.

Analyses of COVID-19 timing relative to vaccination indicated significant associations between severity and the timing of first and second doses (p=0.001). Among patients who contracted the infection before receiving the first dose, 32 (13.5%) had severe symptoms (category 4). In contrast, among those who became infected after receiving the first dose, lower proportions were observed, with 20 (8.4%) in category 4, 13 (10.6%) in category 3 and 16 (16.5%) in category 2. Patients who had not received either the first or second dose showed a higher prevalence of severe symptoms (category 4), with approximately 30 (12.7%) patients. These findings should be interpreted in light of data availability.

The association between outcome status and COVID-19 severity category was significant (p<0.001). Overall, 367 (66.2%) patients were recorded as alive, with most in categories 2 (n=86; 88.7%), 3 (n=91; 74.0%), 4 (n=162; 68.4%) and 5 (n=14; 20.3%). Tragically, 187 of 554 (33.8%) patients died; among deaths, 75 (31.6%) were in category 4, and 55 (79.7%) were in category 5. The body mass index category was not significantly associated with severity.

Variant analysis showed that the delta variant was the most common (n=264; 47.7%), whereas omicron was identified in 99 (17.9%) patients. In category 4, delta accounted for 57.4% of cases (n=136), whereas omicron accounted for 14.8% (n=35). Variant status was unavailable for 190 (34.3%) patients, and one (0.2%) had a non-variant status of concern and was excluded from the statistical analysis. Variant type was significantly associated with severity category (p=0.002), underscoring the importance of ongoing variant surveillance for prognostication.

Symptom analysis showed several significant associations with COVID-19 severity category (online supplemental table 2), including systemic symptoms such as fever (p<0.001) and lethargy (p<0.001); cardiovascular symptoms such as chest pain (p=0.001); musculoskeletal symptoms such as myalgia (p=0.040) and arthralgia (p=0.018); and ear, eye, nose and throat symptoms including sore throat (p=0.004), nasal congestion (p=0.014), runny nose (p=0.044) and sore eyes (p=0.035). Pulmonary and respiratory symptoms such as shortness of breath (p<0.001) and cough (p<0.001) were also significant, as were gastrointestinal symptoms, including vomiting (p=0.015) and loss of appetite (p<0.001), and neurological symptoms such as dizziness (p=0.003).

Blood group was also analysed (online supplemental table 3), but no significant association with symptom severity was found (p=0.106). Several laboratory parameters were significantly associated with severity category (online supplemental table 3), including elevated creatinine (Cr>104 µmol/L; p=0.024), elevated alanine aminotransferase (>50 U/L; p=0.012), low total protein (TP<65 g/L; p<0.001), low albumin (ALB<40 g/L; p<0.001), elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH>243 IU/L; p<0.001), lymphopenia (LYM<1.1×109/L; p<0.001), reduced neutrophils (<1.8×109/L; p<0.001), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP>4 mg/L; p=0.003), prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (>40 s; p=0.007) and elevated D-dimer (D-D>0.55 mg/L; p<0.001). In contrast, elevated total bilirubin (>21 µmol/Lp), creatine kinase (CK>171 IU/L), procalcitonin (>0.05 ng/mL), prothrombin time (PT>13 s), fibrinogen (>4 g/L), and troponin-I>40 pg/mL were not significantly associated with severity.

Biochemistry parameters showed significant associations with severity category for most measures, except for triglycerides (online supplemental table 3). Significant parameters included fasting blood glucose (p<0.001), HbA1c (p=0.023), total cholesterol (p<0.001), high-density lipoprotein (HDL; p<0.001), low-density lipoprotein (LDL; p<0.001), sodium (p<0.001), potassium (p=0.011), urea (p<0.001), calcium (p<0.001), phosphate (p=0.017) and magnesium (p<0.001).

Cytokine levels were examined across severity categories (figure 1). IL-2, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, TNF-α, IP-10 and IFN-γ differed significantly across categories (p<0.05), with median concentrations increasing with severity and peaking in category 5. In contrast, IL-10 did not differ significantly across categories (p>0.05). Antibody status (p=0.557) and viral load (p=0.107) were also not significantly associated with symptom severity (online supplemental table 4).

Additionally, category 3 patients had higher odds of delta infection than omicron infection (OR 2.317; 95% CI 1.026 to 5.224; p=0.043), whereas no significant association was observed for categories 4 and 5.

Pulmonary/respiratory symptoms also exhibited significant associations, with increasing odds across categories. Category 3 had an OR of 4.232 (95% CI 2.127 to 8.420; p<0.001), category 4 had an OR of 2.981 (95% CI 6.525 to 25.825; p<0.001) and category 5 had an OR of 8.858 (95% CI 2.417 to 32.455; p=0.001) (figure 3).

Likewise, gastrointestinal symptoms were associated with higher odds of more severe categories. Category 3 had an OR of 3.337 (95% CI 1.727 to 6.450; p<0.001), category 4 had an OR of 2.204 (95% CI 1.176 to 4.128; p=0.014) and category 5 had an OR of 3.587 (95% CI 1.197 to 10.746; p=0.023).

Laboratory abnormalities, including lymphopenia and elevated fasting blood glucose, were also linked to severity. Category 5 had higher odds of lymphopenia (LYM<1.1×109/L; OR 4.708; 95% CI 1.426 to 15.545; p=0.011). Category 4 had higher odds of elevated fasting blood glucose (OR 1.130; 95% CI1.028 to 1.242; p=0.011) (figure 3).

Cytokine levels also showed associations, with category 3 having lower odds of high IL-7 (OR 0.939; 95% CI 0.905 to 0.974; p=0.011) and category 4 having lower odds of high TNF-α (OR 0.987; 95% CI 0.977 to 0.998; p=0.020) (figure 2B). Further details on adjusted and crude ORs are provided in online supplemental tables 5 and 6, respectively.

The BN model was trained on baseline cytokine data and demonstrates probabilistic relationships among seven cytokines (IL-2, IP-10, IL-10, IL-7, TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-8) involved in the immune response to COVID-19. The BN-DAG topology suggests that all cytokines depend on IL-2, which has the highest connectivity (figure 4). The BN model identified three main components of the COVID-19–related immune response, mediated separately by TNF-α, IP-10 and IL-7. Notable dependencies were also observed between IL-8 and TNF-α, either directly or indirectly via IL-6. Discussion

This study presents an overview of the clinical characteristics, laboratory findings and clinical outcomes of 554 laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 patients admitted to HCTM, categorised by disease severity. This study identifies predictive risk factors that distinguish between severity categories of COVID-19, providing prognostic parameters to guide targeted interventions and close monitoring across patient groups. The increased risk of developing severe symptoms among hospitalised COVID-19 patients highlights the importance of promptly managing and treating those who require heightened medical attention and support during hospitalisation. Although the pandemic may be over, these findings provide valuable insights for future public health events.

The significant disparity in the admission rates to hospital wards or ICUs across severity categories emphasises the critical need for timely and appropriate medical intervention for patients with severe COVID-19 symptoms. In our cohort, both sexes showed comparable susceptibility to COVID-19, with no notable discrepancy in severity. This contrasts with reports from other studies that have documented greater severity among men compared with women in their populations.16 17

Age consistently emerged as a significant risk factor for COVID-19 severity, consistent with previous studies and epidemiological data.18 19 This finding indicates increased vulnerability to more severe symptoms among older patients (aged >55 years). With advancing age, immune function can change, reducing the ability to mount an effective response against the virus.20 In addition, older individuals are more likely to have pre-existing medical conditions (eg, cardiovascular disease, diabetes and respiratory disorders), which can further increase the risk of severe outcomes. Age-related differences in COVID-19 severity may also be influenced by social and behavioural factors. Older adults may experience different levels of exposure because of differences in living arrangements, occupation and adherence to preventive measures. Moreover, access to healthcare and timely medical intervention can affect outcomes, and older individuals may face additional challenges to seeking and receiving appropriate care. These observations emphasise the importance of targeted interventions and tailored care for this age group. Prioritising vaccination among older adults, similar to Malaysia’s vaccination strategy, is a commendable targeted preventive measure, particularly in settings with high proportions of older individuals (eg, nursing homes). Beyond vaccination, mitigating the impact of COVID-19 in older populations also requires early detection and management of comorbidities, promotion of preventive behaviours and provision of appropriate healthcare support. By addressing the specific needs of this age group, healthcare systems can better protect those most susceptible to severe outcomes.

Ethnicity is another factor that may influence the risk and severity of COVID-19.21 22 In our cohort, Malays constituted the largest proportion of the total sample, followed by Chinese and Indians. This reflects Malaysia’s population distribution, where Malays account for 61.8%, Chinese 21.4% and Indian 6.4% of the total population.23 Malays and Chinese showed similar patterns, with higher prevalence of severe symptoms (category 4), whereas Indians had a higher prevalence of moderate symptoms (category 3). The discrepancies in symptom severity among ethnic groups may be influenced by multiple factors, including genetic predispositions, cultural practices, adherence to preventive measures, access to healthcare and socio-economic conditions. Addressing ethnicity-related disparities in COVID-19 outcomes, therefore, requires a comprehensive approach that considers the specific needs and circumstances of different ethnic communities.

Comorbidities (pre-existing medical conditions) have been identified as significant risk factors for COVID-19 severity.24 25 Our data highlight associations between selected comorbidities and symptom severity, although not all differences were statistically significant. Among the comorbidities examined, hypertension and CKD were associated with a higher likelihood of severe COVID-19. The mechanism underlying these associations is multifactorial. Individuals with hypertension may have chronic inflammation and immune dysfunction,26 27 which can weaken antiviral defences and increase susceptibility to infections. Hypertension also commonly coexists with other cardiovascular conditions (eg, heart disease and stroke),28 which can damage blood vessels and impair blood flow, including to the lungs. In the context of COVID-19, impaired perfusion and reduced oxygenation may contribute to respiratory complications and increase the likelihood of severe disease.29 Hypertension is also prevalent among individuals with other comorbidities, such as diabetes and obesity,30 further compounding the risk of severe outcomes. The combination of these underlying conditions creates a complex interplay of factors that heighten the vulnerability to severe symptoms.

CKD is also a recognised risk factor for severe COVID-19. CKD can compromise immune function and increase susceptibility to infections.31 In addition, individuals with CKD often have comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes,24 25 which independently increase the risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes. CKD may exacerbate these underlying conditions, contributing to a more severe course of illness following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Furthermore, patients with CKD often require regular medical care (eg, dialysis or transplantation follow-up), which may increase exposure risk during outbreaks. Physiological changes associated with CKD, such as impaired renal function and immune dysregulation,32 may also contribute to a heightened inflammatory response, leading to more severe disease. These findings emphasise the importance of prioritising individuals with CKD for vaccination and implementing targeted preventive measures to reduce exposure risk. Healthcare providers should also monitor patients with both CKD and COVID-19 closely, as they may be more prone to severe complications requiring prompt medical intervention.

Overall, hypertension and CKD may increase COVID-19 severity through overlapping mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, immune dysfunction, cardiovascular injury, impaired respiratory function and metabolic disturbances.25 27 Recognising and addressing these risk factors can inform preventive measures and clinical interventions, reducing the likelihood of severe disease and improving overall patient outcomes.

Our findings regarding vaccination provide insights into the association between vaccination status and COVID-19 severity, including the number of doses received and vaccine brands. These observations should be interpreted cautiously, considering potential confounders such as vaccine types, timing of vaccination, number of doses, circulating variants and underlying risk factors. Breakthrough infections can occur among vaccinated individuals, although they typically result in milder symptoms than in unvaccinated ones. The type of vaccine used for the primary series and subsequent doses may influence disease severity. In this dataset, the inactivated whole-virion SARS-CoV-2 vaccine (Sinovac) was associated with a higher incidence of severe symptoms compared with BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech) and ChAdOx1 nCOV-19 (Oxford - AstraZeneca). However, direct comparisons between vaccine brands are challenging because of multiple factors, including emerging variants,29 inter-individual immune responses,30 comorbidities20 26 and other risk factors that can independently influence severity and may confound observed associations.

Notably, even among patients who received at least a second dose, hospitalised patients had the highest prevalence in category 4, whereas the booster group showed a lower prevalence. This pattern may suggest a benefit of booster doses, although interpretation is limited by the available data and the observational nature of the analysis. Although a first dose provides some protection, completing the recommended vaccination schedule is crucial for optimal effectiveness. Importantly, any association between vaccination and COVID-19 severity in this dataset is contingent on data completeness and potential residual confounding.

Disease severity substantially affected mortality, with patients in the most severe category facing a higher probability of death than those in mild categories. This highlights the importance of identifying and closely monitoring patients in category 5, prioritising resources and implementing timely interventions to reduce mortality. However, clinical status alone does not fully explain differences in severity. Other variables, such as age and comorbidities, also contribute to the observed outcomes.

The distribution of COVID-19 variants in our cohort, particularly delta and omicron, has important epidemiological and clinical implications. Delta accounted for 47.7% of cases, substantially exceeding omicron at 17.9%, indicating delta’s dominance during the study period despite the emergence of omicron. Variant distribution across severity categories suggests potential differences in clinical impact. A higher proportion of delta cases was observed in category 4, suggesting an association with more severe disease, consistent with prior reports of higher transmissibility and severity for this variant.33 34 In contrast, omicron showed a lower overall prevalence, and its distribution from mild to severe categories suggests a weaker association with severe outcomes than delta. Although omicron cases were also present in category 4, the proportion was lower (14.8%), consistent with a potentially milder clinical course. The predominance of delta in severe cases highlights the continued importance of vaccination and preventive measures to reduce transmission and healthcare burden. The emergence of variants such as omicron also underscores the need for adaptable public health strategies to respond to evolving viral dynamics.

The distribution of symptoms across severity categories provides insights into clinical presentation and disease progression. Systemic symptoms, including fever and lethargy, were significantly more common in higher severity categories, consistent with the systemic nature of COVID-19 and its multiple organ systems.35 Systemic, pulmonary/respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms appeared to be associated with increased risk of severe disease. Fever,17 shortness of breath,36 cough37 and comorbidities24 25 have also been consistently reported as key features in other studies. These findings should nevertheless be interpreted in light of the study’s limitations. The analysed data may not capture the full spectrum of symptoms and reported symptom severity may be subjective. Systemic symptoms (eg, fever, lethargy and myalgia) may reflect the systemic inflammatory response characteristic of severe illness.35 Respiratory symptoms (eg, cough, dyspnoea and chest pain) reflect pulmonary involvement and may progress to ARDS in severe cases.38 Importantly, because disease severity in this study was partly defined by respiratory manifestations (including pneumonia, hypoxia and oxygen supplementation), comparisons of respiratory symptoms across severity categories should be interpreted cautiously. The overlap between symptoms and severity-defining criteria may introduce bias and reduce the independence of these associations. Gastrointestinal symptoms, such as vomiting and loss of appetite, commonly reported elsewhere, may reflect gastrointestinal involvement and potential complications.39

Laboratory abnormalities across severity categories provide further insights into systemic involvement. Elevated Cr (>104 µmol/L) in category 4 suggests possible renal dysfunction with disease progression, emphasising the need to monitor renal function in severe cases. Lower levels of TP (<65 g/L) and ALB (<40 g/L) were significantly associated with higher severity, suggesting a relationship between hypoalbuminaemia and disease severity. ALB supports immune function and overall health; therefore, depletion may contribute to worse outcomes.40 Elevated LDH (>243 IU/L) and CRP (>0.4 mg/dL) levels were more common in severe disease, reflecting tissue injury and heightened inflammation. CRP elevation may also reflect an intense inflammatory response and potential cytokine storm in severe cases. Elevated D-D levels (>0.55 µg/mL) were significantly more prevalent in severe categories, suggesting a hypercoagulable state and increased thrombotic risk; elevated D-D has also been identified as a risk factor for severe disease.41 Collectively, these findings highlight the systemic nature of COVID-19 and the importance of monitoring laboratory parameters to guide clinical decision-making. These markers reflect inflammation, organ dysfunction and immune dysregulation, which contribute to disease severity.17 19 35 LYM patterns across severity categories further highlight immune dysregulation. Lymphopenia is a well-documented feature of severe COVID-19 and is associated with dysregulated immune response and worse prognosis.42 This supports the clinical utility of monitoring LYMP counts to assess severity and guide management, including consideration of immunomodulatory strategies in severe cases. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanism driving lymphopenia and their implications for outcomes.

Biochemical profiles also suggest metabolic alterations associated with COVID-19 severity. The highest fasting blood glucose levels were observed in category 4, indicating a possible association between hyperglycaemia and disease severity. In our analysis, fasting blood glucose was significantly higher in category 4 than in mild categories (OR 1.162; 95% CI 1.093 to 1.242; p<0.011), whereas the difference in category 5 was not statistically significant. This suggests a complex relationship in which factors beyond fasting blood glucose alone may influence severity, warranting further investigation into interactions between metabolic parameters and outcomes. HbA1c, a marker of long-term glycaemic control, was also significantly elevated in category 4, suggesting that chronic hyperglycaemia may exacerbate disease severity. Dyslipidaemia was observed across severity categories, with elevated total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol and reduced HDL cholesterol, which may contribute to cardiovascular complications in severe disease. Electrolyte imbalances (including hyponatraemia and hypokalaemia) were prevalent, potentially reflecting renal involvement and electrolyte disturbances. Renal markers (urea and creatinine) were significantly elevated in severe cases, suggesting renal involvement and possible acute kidney injury. Alterations in calcium, phosphate and magnesium further emphasise the systemic impact of COVID-19 on metabolic homeostasis. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring and managing metabolic parameters, especially in severe cases where metabolic dysregulation may contribute to adverse outcomes and where mechanistic studies could inform targeted therapies.

Cytokine pattern across severity categories provides additional insight into immune dysregulation. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-2, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, TNF-α, IP-10 and IFN-γ), particularly in category 5, suggest an exaggerated inflammatory response associated with severe COVID-19 outcomes. This dysregulated immune response may contribute to cytokine storm, tissue damage and organ dysfunction. The progressive increase in IL-7 with severity suggests a potential role in disease progression, possibly through effects on T-cell activation and regulation. Elevated levels of IP-10 in category 5 indicate potential utility as a severity marker, given its role in recruiting immune cells to inflamed tissues. IFN-γ showed a distinct pattern with the highest levels in category 5, consistent with its role in antiviral immunity in severe disease. Further work is needed to clarify the mechanisms and therapeutic implications of cytokine dysregulation across severity levels.

BN analysis of cytokine profiles suggests a cascade of immune responses triggered by IL-2 following infection. IL-2, secreted by dendritic cells and natural killer cells, promotes T-cell and B-cell proliferation and CD8 memory T-cell differentiation.43 IL-2 also promotes IFN-γ secretion, which in turn induces IP-10 production, an important chemokine for recruiting CD4 and CD8 effector T cells involved in antiviral defence.44 45 IFN-γ-mediated macrophage activation increases TNF-α production, facilitating recruitment of lymphocytes, macrophages and polymorphonuclear cells to infection sites.46 This process also increases expression of adhesion molecules such as E-selectin and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1, enabling leucocyte rolling and migration across the endothelium.47 TNF-α produced by macrophages stimulates IL-8 production, which augments early innate immune response in the lungs and further promotes leucocyte migration. TNF-α may stimulate IL-8 directly or indirectly via an IL-6-mediated pathway, consistent with previous findings.48 IL-8 contributes to the early innate response by interacting with acute-phase chemoattractants such as leukotriene B4 and C5a49 and by activating neutrophil integrins, thereby enhancing leucocyte migration.50

A further aspect of the COVID-19 immune response involves development of longer-term adaptive immunity mediated by IL-7, which supports memory B-cell production. IL-7, derived from bone marrow and thymic stromal cells, promotes late thymocyte growth and maturation51 and supports rearrangement of T-cell and B-cell receptor genes48 and activation of memory B cells.52 These processes are important for sustained adaptive immunity following infection. Finally, IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine produced by regulatory T cells, plays a key role in immune regulation.53 IL-10 suppresses production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1 and IL-12, helping to prevent excessive inflammation and tissue damage. In our BN analysis and statistical analyses, IL-10 did not show a causal relationship with other cytokines, consistent with the absence of significant differences in IL-10 across severity categories.

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort did not include a non-COVID comparison group, limiting our ability to evaluate overall main effects or determine whether observed patterns differ from those in individuals without prior SARS-CoV-2 infection. Accordingly, the pairwise comparisons reflect relative differences between COVID-19 severity categories rather than absolute differences from a true baseline. Without an uninfected reference population, we cannot establish background risks or determine whether some outcomes are specifically attributable to COVID-19 as opposed to reflecting underlying population prevalence. This constraint also affects the interpretation of category-level comparisons, because the absence of a non-COVID group restricts assessment of whether differences between severity categories extend beyond within-cohort variation. Future studies incorporating non-infected controls would enable clearer assessment of main effects and strengthen the interpretability of pairwise comparisons across categories.

Conclusion

These findings highlight the multifaceted nature of COVID-19 and provide valuable insights into risk factors for disease severity in a Malaysian cohort. ICU admission, older age, infection with the delta variant, systemic/pulmonary/gastrointestinal symptoms, lymphocyte abnormalities and elevated fasting blood glucose, as well as cytokines such as IL-7 and TNF-α, were associated with greater severity, highlighting the value of symptom-specific, biomarker-informed assessment. Several of these associations (older age, delta predominance, lymphopenia, hyperglycaemia and respiratory/systemic symptom clusters) are consistent with prior Malaysian12 54 and international reports,5556 reinforcing the existing evidence base.

In addition, this study offers several novel contributions. We report a prospective, protocolised cohort spanning the full clinical spectrum (categories 1–5) with mixed recruitment (hospital and community for categories 1 and 2; hospital admitted only for categories 3–5), enabling directly comparable assessment from mild to critical disease within a single framework. We also integrate clinical, laboratory and immunological phenotyping that links a targeted cytokine panel quantitatively to severity categories. Furthermore, causal cytokine modelling using a BN-DAG was applied to explore directional dependencies among inflammatory mediators, and age-banded, category-specific risk estimates with absolute risks and denominators were presented to support aid clinical interpretation. Collectively, these methodological advances both reinforce established risk factors and extend the literature by providing mechanistically anchored and clinically actionable stratification for Malaysian patients.
