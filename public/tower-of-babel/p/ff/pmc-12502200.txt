Ultrashort wave therapy for chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps: protocol for a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial
Authors: Ling Kong, Jinlong Zhang, Chuanmei Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) is characterized by persistent sinonasal inflammation and substantially impairs quality of life. Current treatments, including corticosteroids, surgery, and biologics, are limited by side effects and high recurrence rates. Ultrashort wave (USW) therapy, a non-invasive modality with purported anti-inflammatory effects, lacks robust evidence for its efficacy in CRSwNP. MethodsThis single-center, double-blind, sham-controlled trial will randomize 150 patients with CRSwNP to receive either active USW therapy (50 mA, 27.12 MHz) or sham treatment for 20 min daily over 2 weeks, alongside standard medical care. The sole primary outcome is the change in the 22-item Sino-Nasal Outcome Test (SNOT-22) score from baseline to the 12-month follow-up. Key secondary outcomes include the change in the Lund-Kennedy endoscopic score at the 12-month follow-up. The primary outcome will be analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model, and sensitivity analyses will be conducted to handle missing data.DiscussionThis trial aims to address a critical evidence gap regarding the efficacy of USW therapy for CRSwNP. If effective, USW therapy could offer a non-invasive adjunct, potentially reducing the burden of corticosteroids, surgery, and biologics while improving long-term patient outcomes. The findings will provide crucial evidence to inform clinical guidelines and direct future research.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400092411). Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is an inflammatory condition of the sinonasal mucosa lasting over 12 weeks, clinically defined by at least two cardinal symptoms: nasal obstruction, nasal discharge, facial pain/pressure, or hyposmia, and confirmed by objective evidence from nasal endoscopy or imaging [1]. This complex disorder significantly impairs quality of life (QoL) due to its chronicity and therapeutic challenges [2] and is a common manifestation of systemic diseases like cystic fibrosis [3]. Globally, the burden is substantial, with over one million patients requiring surgical intervention for CRS annually [4]. CRS is broadly categorized into phenotypes such as CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP), and allergic fungal rhinosinusitis [5].

CRSwNP, predominantly driven by type 2 inflammation, presents a particular clinical challenge. Standard management includes intranasal corticosteroids, saline irrigation, and short-course oral corticosteroids [6]. While current guidelines recommend a stepwise approach, many cases prove refractory [7, 8]. Endoscopic sinus surgery, though effective at improving QoL, is associated with a long-term revision rate of 15–20% [9]. More recently, biologics have revolutionized the management of severe type 2 CRSwNP [10, 11], but their high cost [12], strict eligibility criteria, and potential side effects limit widespread access [13]. Consequently, there is an unmet need for effective, non-invasive, and cost-effective adjunctive therapies.

Precision medicine emphasizes the prevention of disease chronicity and the individualization of therapeutic strategies to minimize overall disease burden [14]. USW therapy, first introduced in the 1940s, has been employed in the management of CRS due to its noninvasive nature and hypothesized mechanisms of action, which may include vasodilation and enhancement of capillary microcirculation [15]. Although early studies suggested possible clinical benefits in CRS [16], robust evidence supporting its efficacy in CRSwNP is lacking. No recent clinical trials have evaluated USW specifically in CRSwNP. Recent experimental studies, for example, have demonstrated that USW therapy may promote neurological recovery after traumatic brain injury by suppressing neuroinflammation [17] and attenuate pulmonary inflammation in murine models [18], suggesting plausible anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair properties relevant to CRSwNP pathophysiology. These findings, while indirect, provide a scientific basis for further investigation in CRSwNP.

In addition to therapeutic applications, energy-based physical modalities have also been explored in CRS-related clinical settings. For instance, microwave disinfection of nasal irrigation bottles and irrigation fluid after endoscopic sinus surgery has been shown to reduce bacterial contamination rates to 0% under supervised protocols [19], with a 90-s microwave cycle recommended as the optimal decontamination procedure [20]. These examples highlight the expanding interest in the physical energy-based approaches in CRS management and underscore the rationale for exploring USW therapy as a safe and accessible modality in CRSwNP.

To date, no rigorous clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy and safety of USW therapy specifically for CRSwNP. Therefore, this study was designed to address this critical evidence gap by determining whether USW therapy, as an adjunct to standard care, can improve outcomes for patients with CRSwNP in a double-blind, sham-controlled setting.

Objectives

This trial therefore addresses two objectives: (1) to evaluate whether USW therapy can reduce nasal congestion, polyp size, and improve QoL using validated tools (e.g., SNOT-22), and (2) to assess its impact on physiological markers, such as peak nasal inspiratory flow (PNIF) and blood eosinophil counts, as well as its safety profile. This single-center, randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial will be conducted with CRSwNP patients at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, with a 6-month recruitment period, a 2-week intervention, and follow-up visits at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months (Fig. 1).

Methods

Trial design

This study is a single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind, parallel-group controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. It is designed within a superiority framework to evaluate whether the investigational intervention produces greater clinical benefit than a sham control. Participants meeting the eligibility criteria will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group.

Study setting

The trial will be conducted at the outpatient and inpatient Department of Otolaryngology-Head & Neck and Rehabilitation Medicine of West China Hospital, Sichuan University. All clinical assessments, interventions, and follow-up visits will be performed on-site. Eligible individuals will be identified during routine outpatient visits or via specialist referrals.

Interventions: administration and management

The USW therapy protocol will be administered using a calibrated DL-CLL device (Dajia Medical Equipment Co., Shantou, China) operating at a 27.12 MHz frequency. This specific frequency will be selected based on its established tissue penetration characteristics and international standardization for medical applications. The treatment parameters (50 mA intensity, 20 min per session, 10 sessions over 2 weeks) were established based on a comprehensive review of biophysical principles governing electromagnetic energy delivery to biological tissues and previous clinical applications demonstrating therapeutic efficacy in inflammatory conditions [21, 22].

Participant withdrawal from the study

Participants have the right to withdraw from the study entirely at any time, for any reason, without penalty. A participant may also be withdrawn from the study at the discretion of the investigator due to noncompliance with the study protocol or if they are lost to follow-up.

Participant timeline and schedule of assessments

The total duration of study participation for each individual will be approximately 13 months, comprising a screening period, a 2-week intervention phase, and a 12-month follow-up period. The participant flow is structured into the following phases:

Screening phase (up to 4 weeks prior to baseline)

Potential participants will undergo a screening process to confirm eligibility. During this phase, the study team will obtain written informed consent and will conduct medical history reviews, physical examinations, and baseline laboratory tests to ensure participants meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Baseline and randomization (visit 1/week 0)

Eligible participants who have provided consent will attend the baseline visit. At this visit, all baseline outcome assessments will be performed. Following the completion of these assessments, participants will be formally enrolled and randomly allocated (1:1) to either the intervention group (active USW therapy) or the control group (sham USW therapy).

Intervention phase (weeks 1 and 2)

Participants will receive their assigned intervention over a 2-week period. This consists of 10 treatment sessions, scheduled once daily for five consecutive days per week.

Follow-up phase (post-intervention)

Following the completion of the intervention phase, participants will attend follow-up visits for outcome assessment at the following time points: week 2 (immediately post-intervention), month 3, month 6, month 9, and month 12.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

The sole primary outcome is the change in the SNOT-22 total score from baseline to the 12-month follow-up. The SNOT-22 is a validated, patient-reported measure of sinonasal symptom burden, scored from 0 to 110, with higher scores indicating greater severity. The 12-month timepoint has been designated as the primary endpoint because it best reflects the long-term, sustained efficacy of the intervention, which is particularly relevant for a chronic condition such as CRSwNP.

Additional assessments will be conducted at baseline, immediately post-intervention (week 2), and at 3, 6, and 9 months. These intermediate timepoints will be analyzed as secondary outcomes to evaluate short- and medium-term effects and to characterize the longitudinal trajectory of response. This approach minimizes the risk of type I error from multiple comparisons while preserving the ability to examine clinically relevant time-dependent patterns. The SNOT-22 has established validity in CRSwNP and incorporates a well-defined minimal clinically important difference (MCID), allowing differentiation between statistical and clinical significance (see Table 3).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes are designed to complement the primary endpoint by evaluating structural changes, nasal airflow, systemic inflammation, olfactory function, additional patient-reported symptoms, composite response dynamics, and safety. Unless otherwise specified, all assessments will be conducted at baseline, at the end of the intervention period (Week 2), and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months (as detailed in Table 3).

Anatomical and endoscopic measures

Lund-Kennedy endoscopic score (0–20): Quantifies the severity of intranasal mucosal disease, with higher scores indicating greater pathology. Examinations will follow standardized protocols, with photographic documentation for independent verification. All images will be independently evaluated by two blinded rhinologists. Bilateral ENPS (0–8): Rates polyp size in each nasal cavity, providing a direct, objective indicator of polyp burden.

Physiological function

PNIF(L/min): An objective measurement of nasal patency obtained using a PNIF meter, with higher values indicating better airflow.

Inflammatory biomarker

Peripheral blood eosinophil count (× 109/L): A laboratory measure of systemic eosinophilic inflammation, obtained from venous blood samples.

Olfactory function

UPSIT (0–40): A standardized assessment of odor identification ability, with higher scores reflecting better olfactory performance. Loss-of-smell score (0–3): Patient-reported severity of olfactory loss, with higher scores indicating greater impairment.

Additional patient-reported symptom measures

Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10 cm): Measures overall sinonasal symptom severity, with higher values denoting more severe symptoms. Nasal congestion/obstruction score (0–3): Rates severity of nasal blockage as reported by the patient.

Composite and temporal measures

Time to response: Interval from baseline to the first achievement of the SNOT-22 MCID, defined as an improvement of ≥ 9 points. Sustained response: Maintenance of MCID-level improvement throughout the 12-month follow-up, as well as the proportion of participants achieving this threshold at each timepoint.

Safety and tolerability

Incidence, severity, and causality of all Adverse Events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs), including potential mild burns or transient discomfort at the intervention site, will be recorded throughout the study period.

Justification for surrogate outcomes

Scientific and practical rationale

The primary outcome for this trial—the SNOT‑22 total score—was selected as a surrogate endpoint because it is highly responsive to short‑ and medium‑term changes in CRSwNP and captures the patient-perceived impact of the disease on QoL. In contrast, definitive endpoints such as long‑term polyp recurrence or need for revision surgery would require years of follow-up to observe a sufficient number of events, making them impractical for an initial efficacy trial. SNOT‑22 allows for sensitive detection of clinically meaningful changes over the 12‑month follow‑up and enables efficient hypothesis testing within a feasible study timeframe.

The MCID for SNOT‑22 in CRS populations is approximately 9 points, providing a validated threshold for interpreting changes in a clinically meaningful context.

Safety assessment adequacy

Although surrogate endpoints focus on mechanistic and short-term clinical effects, the trial’s 12-month follow-up and adequate sample size enable detection of potential harms, including symptom worsening, polyp progression, or systemic inflammatory responses. AEs will be actively monitored at each follow-up visit, ensuring a balanced assessment of both benefit and risk.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was determined using PASS version 15.0 (NCSS, LLC) for a two-sample t-test with a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05. The calculation was based on detecting a clinically meaningful between-group difference in the SNOT-22 total score. Drawing from a published randomized trial involving a similar CRSwNP population and intervention modality [23], the expected mean difference was set to correspond to a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), which approximates the MCID for the SNOT-22 in this disease context. This effect size was selected to ensure the trial is powered to detect a change that is both statistically significant and clinically relevant.

With 80% power to detect this effect size at α = 0.05, the calculation indicated that 64 participants per group would be required. To account for an anticipated 15% attrition rate over the 12-month follow-up period, the final recruitment target was set at 75 participants per group (total N = 150). An interim analysis to estimate variance will be conducted after enrolling 20 participants to validate these assumptions according to the internal pilot approach [24], without unblinding treatment allocation.

Randomization procedures

Sequence generation

An independent statistician will generate a stratified 1:1 randomization sequence via Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0, stratified by baseline polyp severity.

Allocation concealment mechanism

Group assignments will be concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE), prepared and secured by the independent statistician. The envelopes will be tamper-evident and stored in a locked location. Upon enrollment, the recruitment physiotherapist will open the corresponding envelope to determine the participant’s group, ensuring allocation concealment is maintained.

Implementation

At the time of enrollment, the recruitment physiotherapist will open the envelope to determine group assignment (active or sham), thereby maintaining allocation concealment.

Blinding (masking)

Participants and outcome assessors will remain blinded to treatment allocation. Treating physiotherapists cannot be blinded due to the nature of device operation; however, identical electrode placement and standardized session protocols will be used to preserve participant blinding.

Procedures for emergency unblinding will be managed by an independent monitor and initiated only when knowledge of the assignment is essential for clinical management.

Harms and AEs

Potential AEs of mild thermal USW include transient warmth, skin erythema, and, rarely, superficial burns. To minimize these risks, all sessions will be delivered by trained physiotherapists, with continuous patient feedback and skin checks before, during, and after treatment. Sessions will be terminated or parameters reduced if any discomfort or adverse skin changes occur. Patients with impaired local sensation, active infection, pacemakers, or metallic implants in the head/neck region are excluded.

All AEs and SAEs will be recorded and graded according to the CTCAE. Participants will be encouraged to report any discomfort, side effects, or unexpected symptoms via a dedicated, study-specific telephone hotline.

SAEs will be reported to the Institutional Review Board (IRB) within 7 days of occurrence, and non-serious AEs within 14 days. In the event of any harm deemed related to the intervention, the study treatment will be discontinued immediately, and appropriate medical management will be provided.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be conducted according to the ITT principle unless explicitly stated otherwise. The primary comparison will be between the intervention (USW) and control cohorts, following hypothesis-driven analytical approaches. Statistical significance will be set at two-tailed p < 0.05, with accompanying 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analytical workflows will be preregistered on the Open Science Framework to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Comparative analysis of baseline and outcome data

Categorical variables will be summarized as counts and percentages and compared between groups using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts are < 5. Continuous variables will be reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians (interquartile ranges), as appropriate, and compared using the Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test otherwise. Normality will be assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.10) and visual inspection of quantile–quantile plots.

Primary and secondary outcome analyses

The primary outcome will be analyzed by comparing the mean change in SNOT-22 score from baseline to the 12-month follow-up between groups, using the most appropriate parametric or non-parametric test depending on distributional assumptions. This single primary timepoint was pre-specified to avoid inflation of type I error due to multiple testing. Analyses at other timepoints (2 weeks, 3, 6, and 9 months) will be treated as secondary outcomes and analyzed in the same manner, with adjustment for multiple testing where applicable. Both unadjusted and adjusted analyses will be performed, with adjusted models including predefined baseline covariates such as age, sex, smoking status, asthma/allergic history, previous sinus surgery, and concurrent medication use.

Longitudinal analyses

Changes over time across all repeated measures (baseline, 2 weeks, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months) will also be analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, including fixed effects for treatment group, time (as a categorical variable), and their interaction, with random intercepts for participants to account for within-subject correlation. This model allows for efficient use of all available data, accounts for correlation among repeated measures, and enables evaluation of the treatment effect trajectory. The primary conclusion will be based solely on the pre-specified 12-month contrast from this model.

Handling of missing data

Patterns of missingness will be examined using Little’s test for missing completely at random (MCAR) and visual inspection of missing-data heatmaps. If data are MCAR, single imputation will be performed using the mean for continuous variables and the mode for categorical variables. For data missing at random, multiple imputation by chained equations will be implemented with 20 imputations using predictive mean matching over 50 iterations, incorporating relevant baseline variables as predictors.

Subsequent verification of findings

Study data will be preserved for future confirmatory and external validation analyses. Planned activities include replication of primary analyses in independent patient cohorts and pooling with compatible datasets for meta-analysis of individual participant data, to assess reproducibility and generalizability.

Interim and pilot analyses

No formal interim analyses of efficacy outcomes are planned, to preserve the overall type I error rate and maintain blinding. Safety outcomes will be monitored on an ongoing basis by the study investigators, with immediate reporting and evaluation of any SAEs according to institutional and regulatory guidelines. An internal pilot analysis will be performed after the first 20 participants complete the 12-month follow-up, limited to estimating the variance of the primary outcome for confirming sample size assumptions. This analysis will be conducted without unblinding treatment allocation, will not assess efficacy, and will not be used as a stopping rule.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial is designed to rigorously evaluate the efficacy of USW therapy as a novel, non-invasive adjunctive treatment for CRSwNP. Despite available treatments, CRSwNP remains a challenging condition due to the limitations of chronic medication use and the risk of post-surgical recurrence, underscoring the need for safe and cost-effective complementary strategies.

Should our hypotheses be confirmed, patients receiving active USW therapy are expected to demonstrate significant improvements in both subjective and objective outcomes—including SNOT-22 scores, endoscopic polyp scores, nasal obstruction, and peak inspiratory flow—compared to the sham control group. These findings would support the role of USW therapy in alleviating core symptoms of CRSwNP and improving QoL.

The clinical implications of positive results would be substantial. USW could be integrated into stepped-care treatment algorithms, offering a non-pharmacological option to reduce dependence on corticosteroids and delay or prevent revision surgeries. This may enhance long-term disease control, improve adherence, and lower the economic burden of CRSwNP.

The potential therapeutic mechanisms of USW in CRSwNP are multifactorial. Preclinical evidence suggests that USW may suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines and modulate key inflammatory pathways [25]. Given the central role of inflammation in CRSwNP [26], this anti-inflammatory effect could ameliorate the local mucosal environment and reduce polyp burden [27]. Additionally, USW-induced vasodilation may enhance microcirculation [28], promoting tissue oxygenation [29] and edema resolution [30, 31], which could directly alleviate nasal congestion and obstruction [32, 33]. Furthermore, USW therapy may disrupt bacterial biofilms [34], which could enhance the efficacy of conventional medical therapies by reducing the persistence of infection [35–37]. USW might also influence immune cell activity [38], promote cellular proliferation [39], potentially fostering a more balanced immune response in the sinonasal mucosa [40], facilitating the repair and regeneration of damaged tissues [41]. In CRSwNP, this could translate to a healthier nasal mucosa and reduced polyp formation. While these proposed mechanisms align with aspects of CRSwNP pathophysiology, it is important to emphasize that they remain hypothetical in this specific disease context and require verification through targeted research.

Current treatment strategies for CRSwNP predominantly involve pharmacological therapies, such as intranasal corticosteroids [42], systemic corticosteroids [43], leukotriene antagonists [44], and antibiotics [45]. Surgical interventions, particularly endoscopic sinus surgery [46], also play an important role. These treatments, while often beneficial, have recognized limitations. Long-term use of corticosteroids can be associated with systemic side effects [47], and surgery, although initially successful in many cases, may be followed by polyp recurrence, sometimes necessitating repeated procedures [48].

In comparison, USW therapy represents a novel approach with a potentially distinct mechanism of action. Studies in other inflammatory and chronic conditions have suggested potential benefits. For instance, research in rheumatoid arthritis has indicated possible anti-inflammatory effects [49], and investigations in experimental lung injury models have demonstrated a potential reduction in inflammatory markers [18]. Some clinical applications have also reported analgesic effects in various conditions [50].

CRSwNP is characterized by local inflammatory responses in the nasal cavity [51], with eosinophilic inflammation implicated in its pathogenesis, severity, and treatment responsiveness [52]. Consequently, modulating the local inflammatory response in the nasal cavity of CRSwNP patients has become an important therapeutic goal [53]. However, research specifically investigating USW therapy in CRSwNP is notably sparse, highlighting the need for studies in this area. Our investigation aims to systematically evaluate the potential efficacy of USW therapy in CRSwNP, addressing an important gap in the current literature.

Previous studies on physical therapies for CRSwNP, including ultrasound therapy and photodynamic therapy, have yielded variable results [54, 55]. USW therapy may be distinguished by its combination of potential thermal and non-thermal effects, which might offer anti-inflammatory benefits without the thermal damage associated with certain other modalities. Additionally, if USW therapy can influence biofilm formation as hypothesized, this could address a microbial component of CRSwNP that often proves resistant to conventional treatments.

Future studies should investigate varying frequencies and dosages of USW therapy to determine optimal treatment protocols for CRSwNP. Exploring combinations of USW with other therapeutic modalities, such as corticosteroids or biologics, might provide insights into possible synergistic effects. Additionally, incorporating biomarker analyses in future research could help elucidate the mechanisms by which USW might modulate inflammation and immune responses in CRSwNP patients.

By comparing our findings with existing studies on other therapeutic approaches, this research may contribute to positioning USW therapy as a potential complementary treatment option for CRSwNP. If demonstrated to be effective, the integration of USW therapy could potentially enhance current treatment paradigms, offering an additional tool to managing this complex condition.

Confidentiality

Participant data will be de-identified using unique identifiers, and all personally identifiable information will be securely stored in a password-protected database accessible only to authorized personnel. Paper records, if any, will be kept in locked cabinets. Data sharing with external collaborators will comply with local and international data protection regulations, such as GDPR.

Ancillary and post-trial care

Participants will receive appropriate medical care for any trial-related AEs or injuries that are directly attributable to their participation in the trial. Such treatment will be provided at no cost to the participant. In the event of study-related harm, compensation will be offered in accordance with applicable laws, institutional policies, and ethical guidelines. Should ongoing medical management be required after trial completion, participants will be referred to qualified specialists to ensure continuity of care.

Dissemination policy

Final trial results will be communicated to participants, healthcare professionals, and the public through plain‑language summaries, presentations at national and international scientific meetings, and publication in peer‑reviewed journals without publication restrictions. Authorship will follow International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) criteria, and no professional medical writers will be used. The full trial protocol, anonymized participant‑level dataset, and statistical code will be made publicly available in an open‑access repository after publication of the primary results. Surrogate and target outcome data will be shared under the same open‑access conditions to enable future secondary research in accordance with applicable data protection regulations.
