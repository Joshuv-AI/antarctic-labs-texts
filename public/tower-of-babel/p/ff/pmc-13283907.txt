Simulation of long‐term lecanemab treatment effect on Alzheimer's disease progression
Authors: Troy Williams, Se Ryeong Jang, Krista L. Lanctôt, Ashley Kang, Lisa Bloudek, Guoqiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAnti‐amyloid therapies such as lecanemab have demonstrated statistically significant slowing of decline on the Clinical Dementia Rating‐Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) in patients with early Alzheimer's disease (AD) in pivotal trials. Converting treatment differences on CDR‐SB into time saved from disease progression may help convey clinical relevance for patients and caregivers more effectively.METHODSDisease progression models were developed using Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative and National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center data. A 37% treatment‐related time delay, derived from the Clarity AD trial, was applied to estimate long‐term efficacy of lecanemab.RESULTSNatural progression models estimated 11.5 to 13.7 years from mild cognitive impairment due to AD to severe AD. When starting treatment at CDR‐SB 3.2, lecanemab delayed progression to severe AD by 2.5 to 3.7 years when assuming patients remained on treatment and 2.0 to 3.0 years when accounting for treatment discontinuation. Results were consistent across different datasets.DISCUSSIONProjections suggest lecanemab substantially delays clinical progression of AD, preserving patients’ time in earlier stages of AD.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia in individuals aged 65 years and older. 1 In 2019, approximately 57.4 million individuals globally had AD or other dementias, and this number is projected to increase to 152.8 million cases by 2050. 2 Early pathology is characterized by amyloid beta (Aβ) accumulation, which triggers neurofibrillary tangles, neurodegeneration, and cognitive and functional deterioration. 3 , 4 , 5 As such, Aβ has been one of the most frequently targeted pathways in experimental drugs designed to impact the underlying pathophysiology of AD. 6

Prior to approval of anti‐amyloid treatments, AD was primarily managed with symptomatic treatments, non‐pharmacological prevention, and supportive care. Recent advancements in AD research have led to treatments that slow the underlying pathophysiology of AD in earlier stages of the disease (i.e., mild cognitive impairment [MCI] due to AD or mild AD dementia; collectively referred to as “early AD”) before the rate of disease progression accelerates rapidly. 7 , 8 Lecanemab received traditional approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as the first anti‐amyloid antibody to treat individuals with early AD after demonstrating improvement in clinical outcomes, health‐related quality of life, and biomarkers in the pivotal Phase 3 study. 7 Nonetheless, clinicians, patients, and families may find it difficult to interpret the clinical relevance of measures such as the Clinical Dementia Rating‐Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB), as these outcomes are not routinely used in clinical practice. 9 Time to disease‐related clinical progression may offer a more interpretable way to communicate clinical benefit. 9 , 10 , 11

A slope analysis of Clarity AD data estimated that lecanemab preserved approximately 5.3 months of worsening relative to placebo at 18 months (backward projection) and would not reach the 18‐month placebo level until 7.5 months later (forward projection), assuming a linear trajectory within this period. 12

It takes approximately 10 to 17 years for patients with early AD to progress to severe AD. 13 While the disease trajectory in early AD is relatively linear, the overall trajectory is not, with progression accelerating in later stages. 14 Longitudinal observational studies provide an opportunity to explore treatment effects across the AD continuum and assess the cumulative benefits of anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies beyond the clinical trial period. In this study, an AD disease progression model (DPM) was developed using non‐linear mixed‐effects methods to assess the potential long‐term treatment benefit of lecanemab.

METHODS

Data source and population

Patient‐level data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) 15 were used to model the natural progression of AD among patients with characteristics similar to those enrolled in the pivotal lecanemab trial (Clarity AD). 7 Included patients were aged 50 to 90 years with a baseline Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of ≥22, baseline Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) memory score of ≥0.5, and ≥1 post‐baseline CDR‐SB score in the database. The analysis was limited to amyloid‐positive patients, aligned with the definition of amyloid positivity used in the external ADNI cohort in the Clarity AD trial, 7 indicated by any one of the following: positron emission tomography (PET) standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR)AV45, FBP ≥1.11, PET SUVRPIB ≥1.47, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) total tau/Aβ1‐42>0.222. In total, 434 patients met these criteria: 266 with MCI due to AD and 168 with mild AD. MCI due to AD was defined as having a baseline diagnosis of “late mild cognitive impairment” (LMCI) and CDR global score of 0.5. Mild AD was defined as a baseline diagnosis of “AD” and CDR global score of 0.5 or 1.0. In the absence of a placebo arm in the open‐label extension phase of Clarity AD, a matched ADNI cohort was used to predict the natural progression of AD.

The main analysis was replicated in the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) dataset to compare results. 16 In the NACC database, a similar population was identified using selection criteria described in a previous study that had estimated progression of AD in amyloid‐positive individuals. 16 , 17 This analysis used data from 12 Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs) and included Uniform Data Set (UDS) visits conducted between October 2005 and January 2024. 16

ADNI and NACC datasets were selected for their longitudinal design and prior use in AD modeling studies. 10 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ADNI was used for the base case for two reasons. First, it includes more complete biomarker and clinical data to support matching to the Clarity AD patient cohort; second, it has more frequent longitudinal follow‐up (every 6 months for the first 2 years, then annually), providing a higher density of data points than the annual assessments in NACC. NACC data served as a secondary dataset. 17

Outcome measure

CDR‐SB was used to track disease progression in AD, as it is recognized as appropriate in assessing treatment effects in early AD by both the US FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA). 25 , 26 The CDR‐SB is a validated outcome measure derived from structured interviews with patients and care partners to assess cognitive and functional impairment across six domains, with total scores ranging from 0 (no impairment) to 18 (severe impairment); scores of 0.5 to 6 are generally indicative of MCI due to AD or mild AD dementia. 27 Its close reflection of clinical progression and validity across diverse cohorts make it a valuable measure for assessing treatment efficacy in early‐stage AD. 28

Natural progression of AD

To estimate the natural progression of AD, a non‐linear mixed‐effects DPM was implemented based on previously published methods. 21 Two separate DPMs – one assuming that the AD natural disease progression follows an exponential trajectory, another assuming a logistic trajectory – were fit to the selected data using the progmod package in R.

Exponential DPM

The exponential DPM used the following formulation: θt=l∗expt+sexpg+v.

The parameters l, g, s, and v are the basic model parameters that describe the shape of θ and were modeled as fixed effects. The l parameter is a scaling parameter and was modeled as a single free parameter. The g parameter represents a scaling parameter of time where baseline age, sex, race, apolipiprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, and MMSE were explored as covariates. The s parameter is a shift in observed time with baseline diagnosis as a covariate. In this model, s models disease time since the average baseline stage of MCI due to AD. Thus, the MCI due to AD group was assigned a value of 0 for s, and the final model predicted a shift in time for the mild AD group. Additionally, the s parameter was modeled as a random effect, and a time shift was predicted for each patient. The v parameter represents an intercept parameter.

Logistic DPM

The logistic DPM was based on the following formulation: θt=A+K−A1+exp−B∗t+sv

The parameters A and K represent the horizontal asymptotes of the curve and were set to 0 and 18, respectively, given CDR‐SB ranges from 0 to 18. The B parameter represents how fast the curve transitions between the two asymptotes where baseline age, sex, race, APOE ε4 status, and MMSE were explored as covariates. Like the exponential DPM, the s parameter represents a shift in observed time with baseline diagnosis as a covariate and is modeled as both a fixed and random effect. The v parameter describes the asymmetry of the curve.

Model assessment and covariate selection

Model fit was assessed using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better fit (Tables S1–S5). Variance components were extracted from each DPM to quantify between‐subject variability and residual error. Diagnostic plots for both ADNI DPMs included observed versus model‐predicted CDR‐SB values at the population level (fixed effects only) and individual level (fixed plus random effects), and Pearson residuals plotted against individual‐level fitted values were assessed (Figures S1–S14).

ADNI DPMs were compared with and without baseline MMSE as a covariate. In the exponential DPM, MMSE inclusion modestly increased AIC (8666.14 vs 8660.52) with no improvement in residual diagnostics. In the logistic DPM, MMSE inclusion caused a collapse of the random intercept variance, an almost doubling of the residual variance (6.24), and severe deterioration in fit (AIC = 10027.66 vs 8949.01). Final ADNI models therefore excluded MMSE. For NACC, MMSE was not included due to missing values for 80% of the cohort. Age was included as a covariate in the NACC exponential DPM but excluded from the logistic DPM, as including age produced near‐zero random intercept variance and implausible results.

Mean trajectory

Given differences in baseline age, sex, race, and APOE ε4 status between ADNI and NACC compared to the Clarity AD population (Table 1), each DPM was used to predict a trajectory for the average Clarity AD patient using g‐computation. Population‐level predictions were made across a fine grid of follow‐up time, fixing age and enumerating all combinations of categorical variables. A mean trajectory was estimated by taking a weighted average of stratum‐specific predictions, where weights reflected the baseline values of the total Clarity AD population. Trajectories were estimated based on the baseline mean age, proportion by sex, proportion by race, and proportion by APOE ε4 status. For DPMs using NACC, APOE ε4 included a level for missing values, as 16% of patients had missing baseline APOE ε4 status. Time in and time to health states were calculated and compared to digitized results from other published DPMs (Table S6). 20 , 21 , 22 , 29 Severity was defined by established CDR‐SB classifications: MCI due to AD (CDR‐SB 0.5 to 4.0), mild AD (CDR‐SB 4.5 to 9.0), moderate AD (CDR‐SB 9.5 to 15.5), and severe AD (CDR‐SB 16 to 18). 30

To derive confidence intervals (CIs) around the mean time to health states under natural progression, a bootstrap procedure was implemented. For each DPM, 10,000 bootstrap samples were estimated and the DPM was re‐fit on each resampled dataset. Natural progression times were estimated for each bootstrap replicate. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentile from the resulting bootstrap distribution were taken to represent the 95% CI for the natural progression times to each health state.

Delayed progression with lecanemab treatment

A relative treatment effect derived from Clarity AD was used to estimate long‐term progression in patients treated with lecanemab. The forward projection method estimated that an additional 6.7 months was needed for lecanemab to reach the placebo decline at 18 months, 31 corresponding to a 37% time delay relative to placebo. This time delay percentage was applied to the natural disease progression curve by delaying the natural progression time by 37% to estimate a long‐term lecanemab progression curve, starting at CDR‐SB 3.2 (the average baseline CDR‐SB score in Clarity AD). Mean time to progress to mild, moderate, and severe AD was calculated for the natural progression and lecanemab progression, with the difference presented as years delayed in progression due to lecanemab. A subgroup analysis was conducted in the ADNI population excluding patients with homozygous APOE ε4 status at baseline, aligned with the EMA‐approved population 25 ; the relative treatment effect for non‐carrier and heterozygous APOE ε4 corresponded to a 51% time delay (an additional 9.1 months). The APOE ε4 non‐carrier and heterozygous subgroup analysis was not performed using NACC data due to 16% missing baseline APOE ε4 status.

To derive a 95% CI around the difference in mean time to each health state, standard errors from the mixed models for repeated measures (MMRM) analysis in Clarity AD were used to calculate a standard error for the forward projection estimate using propagation of error. A 95% CI for the forward projection was estimated assuming a normal distribution (95% CI: 3.2 to 10.2 months). These bounds were converted to percentage time delay: 18% to 57% for the 37% time delay. The same approach was used for the non‐carrier or heterozygous APOE ε4 subgroup: 28% to 72% for the 51% time delay.

Treatment discontinuation

To account for the impact of treatment discontinuation on the long‐term effect of lecanemab, the percentage of patients on treatment was estimated from a time‐to‐treatment discontinuation curve from a previously published cost‐effectiveness model. 32 A linear effect waning was applied after treatment discontinuation. Separate mean trajectories were calculated for each monthly discontinuation scenario (assuming discontinuation at the end of each month), with effect waning beginning at discontinuation time. Each monthly trajectory was then weighted by the proportion of patients who discontinued each month to estimate a mean trajectory.

RESULTS

Natural progression

The estimated mean trajectory from each model is presented in Figure 1, along with estimated time shifts applied to the observed data. Similar total time from the start of MCI due to AD to the start of severe AD was predicted across models (exponential: 13.7 years; logistic: 13.3 years). However, the models predicted substantially different times spent in MCI due to AD and moderate AD (Table 2): The exponential DPM estimated 8.5 years in MCI due to AD, 3.1 years in mild AD, and 2.1 years in moderate AD; the logistic DPM estimated 4.4 years in MCI due to AD, 3.0 years in mild AD, and 5.9 years in moderate AD. The logistic model is bound above by CDR‐SB 18, creating a ceiling effect wherein progression slows as estimated values approach CDR‐SB 18, which may explain the longer time estimated in moderate AD. Conversely, the exponential is unbounded above and can predict values outside the CDR‐SB scale.

Individual‐ and population‐level predictions for the ADNI cohort across sex and APOE ε4 baseline groups are presented in Tables S8–S14. In the exponential DPM, homozygous (12.6 years), heterozygous (13.7 years), and non‐carrier (12.8 years) showed similar progression times to severe AD. The logistic DPM showed greater variability: homozygous (11.0 years), heterozygous (13.0 years), and non‐carriers (14.2 years). The exponential DPM showed similar progression times by sex (males: 13.1 years; females: 13.3 years), while the logistic DPM estimated slower progression for males than females (13.7 vs 12.0 years).

Secondary analyses using NACC data were generally consistent with the analyses using ADNI data. When using the NACC data, the exponential and logistic DPMs also showed notable differences in estimated time in MCI due to AD and moderate AD where the exponential DPM estimated more time in MCI due to AD and less time in moderate AD compared to the logistic DPM (Tables 2 and S15, Figures S13 and S14).

Delayed progression with lecanemab treatment

The estimated time to mild, moderate, and severe AD from a starting CDR‐SB score of 3.2 for lecanemab‐treated patients is presented in Table 3. The exponential DPM showed lecanemab to delay progression to mild, moderate, and severe AD by 6 months, 1.7 years, and 2.5 years, respectively, without treatment discontinuation (Figure 2). The logistic DPM showed delays of 4 months, 1.5 years, and 3.7 years, respectively, without treatment discontinuation (Figure 3). Using the lower 95% CI natural progression estimate, delays were reduced to 4 months, 11 months, and 1.2 years (exponential) and 4 months, 1.3 years, and 3.0 years (logistic). Accounting for treatment discontinuation and effect waning, delays to moderate and severe AD were further reduced by 2 months and 6 months (exponential) and 2 months and 9 months (logistic), respectively.

In the non‐carrier or heterozygous APOE ε4 subgroup, the delay to severe AD was approximately 8 months (exponential) and 1 year (logistic) longer than in the overall population, when accounting for discontinuation (Figures S15 and S16). This greater delay reflects the larger treatment effect for lecanemab in this subgroup relative to the overall population, which includes homozygous APOE ε4 patients.

DISCUSSION

Across all DPMs, lecanemab treatment may delay progression to mild AD by 4 to 7 months, to moderate AD by 1.3 to 1.9 years, and to severe AD by 2.5 to 3.7 years for patients initiating treatment at CDR‐SB 3.2 without treatment discontinuation. For those who naturally progress faster (lower bootstrap estimate) than average, delays to moderate and severe AD would likely be reduced by approximately 4 to 8 months. Treatment discontinuation would likely further reduce the delay to moderate AD by 2 months and to severe AD by 6 months (exponential) and 1.2 years (logistic).

The estimated delay in progression is consistent with predictions from another study, despite differences in modeling approach (natural cubic spline vs exponential/logistic models), how the time delay was estimated, and model inputs. 22 The natural cubic spline model estimated a slightly larger delay to severe AD (2.9 years) than the exponential model (2.5 years) and slightly shorter delay than the logistic model (3.7 years). Our analysis additionally incorporated estimates accounting for treatment discontinuation with effect waning and uncertainty in natural progression time via bootstrap analysis.

An additional study assessing anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibody therapies showed that, for patients with a baseline CDR‐SB of 2, lecanemab extended independence in instrumental activities for daily living by 10 months, where loss of independence in these activities, which included financial management, driving a motor vehicle, meal preparation, and remembering to take medication or keep appointments, was expected at CDR‐SB >4.5. 37 These findings, along with the results of our analysis, consistently show a benefit for patients being treated with lecanemab by delaying progression to more severe health stages.

This study explored exponential and logistic non‐linear mixed‐effects DPMs using two datasets (ADNI and NACC). The exponential‐ADNI, exponential‐NACC, logistic‐ADNI, and logistic‐NACC models estimated similar total time from MCI due to AD to severe AD (13.7, 13.3, 13.0, and 11.5 years, respectively). However, results were sensitive to model choice for time in MCI due to AD and moderate AD, which impacts estimated delays for lecanemab. The exponential model predicts more time in MCI due to AD and less time in moderate AD than the logistic model; in ADNI, the exponential model estimated 2.1 years in moderate AD versus 5.9 years for the logistic model. The logistic model has a ceiling effect, as the curve is bounded above by CDR‐SB 18. As predicted CDR‐SB values approach 18, progression slows, resulting in a longer tail and greater estimated time in moderate AD. This ceiling effect may also yield less reliable estimates for time to severe AD in the logistic DPM. While the logistic model demonstrated better statistical fit, the exponential form may better reflect the AD progression, as the apparent slowing in the logistic tail may represent a modeling artifact rather than true disease behavior.

The predicted estimates for the natural progression model were similar to previously published non‐linear mixed‐effects models, which was expected given the underlying methods and data were similar. 20 , 21 , 22 When compared against results from other published linear based models, 24 , 29 our estimates and other non‐linear models showed faster natural progression through the health states by approximately 1.5 years (natural cubic spline) and 3 years (exponential) until severe AD (Table S7).

Longitudinal studies have shown that quality of life for patients and caregivers decreases over time, with faster decline with progression to dementia, particularly in amyloid‐positive patients, and greater reduction among institutionalized patients. 38 , 39 Our analysis demonstrates that lecanemab may benefit patients and caregivers by prolonging time in less severe stages of AD, where patients retain greater independence and quality of life. The greatest delays in progression are observed in patients who remain on treatment.

Limitations

The results of this study are based on simulated long‐term estimates for disease progression that rely on underlying modeling assumptions rather than observed long‐term efficacy of lecanemab in the real world.

The ADNI cohort is predominantly White (96%), with 1.6% Asian patients, where 17% of the Clarity AD population was Asian. While race (White vs non‐White) was included as a covariate, the model's ability to capture race‐specific differences in progression is limited. Additionally, lecanemab estimates represent the average Clarity AD patient trajectory, which may not be generalizable to populations that differ substantially from those enrolled in Clarity AD.

In both ADNI and NACC cohorts, few observations were available for patients reaching severe AD. Approximately 19% of the ADNI cohort have records in the moderate AD health state and approximately 5% have records in the severe AD health state. Estimated time in severe AD is therefore driven primarily by the overall shape of the model and its fit in earlier health states, rather than observed severe AD data. Model‐derived estimates for time to mild and moderate AD are more robust than those for time to severe AD, which should be interpreted with caution. While one scenario accounted for all‐cause treatment discontinuation, mortality or other competing risks that are typically included in disease simulation models, such as cost‐effectiveness models, were not included.

The 95% CI for the percentage time delay was based on propagation of error, assuming a normal distribution, and may not represent a true 95% CI. Nevertheless, the scenario applying the lower CI of the percentage time delay to the lower bootstrap estimate of natural progression times was considered to reasonably capture uncertainty in the progression delay with lecanemab.
