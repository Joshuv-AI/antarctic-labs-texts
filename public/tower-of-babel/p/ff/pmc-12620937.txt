Identifying indicators and targets for the new World Health Organization Global Framework on Infection Prevention and Control: an e-Delphi study
Authors: G. Satta, P. Rogers, J. Bana e Costa, E. Tartari, A.F. Santos, P. Bischoff, J. Storr, L. Cihambanya, A.P. Coutinho Rehse, I. Heweidy, Z. Li, P. Ramon-Pardo, A.S. Shah, M.L. Grayson, B. Allegranzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe World Health Organization (WHO) recently developed the Global Action Plan (GAP) and Monitoring Framework (MF) for Infection Prevention and Control (IPC). An e-Delphi survey was conducted as part of a consultative process to achieve consensus on IPC MF indicators and targets.MethodsThe study was conducted as an e-Delphi consensus-seeking exercise, with two rounds of sequential surveys. In round 1, participants were asked to assess each indicator and target using a five-point Likert scale. In round 2, participants were asked to re-assess those indicators and targets that did not reach consensus, and to rank all indicators and targets in priority order. ‘Consensus’ was defined as when >70% of the responses fell within the two positive extreme ranges of the scale and, at the same time, <5% of the responses were within the two negative extreme ranges.FindingsIn total, 86 country representatives along with experts from 63 countries participated in the initial draft IPC MF development. The e-Delphi study invited 394 participants, with response rates of 36% (142/394) in round 1 and 69% (98/142) in round 2. Of the 134 indicators and 27 targets included in round 1, all but one achieved consensus, and 16 showed >5% level of disagreement. In round 2, three indicators were rejected, and participants ranked the top 10 global targets and top five national targets. The survey showed strong support for improving IPC surveillance, whilst research ranked lower due to other competing priorities.ConclusionA high level of agreement on the proposed indicators and targets to be monitored by all countries to track progress in IPC was achieved through a strong consensus building process. The WHO IPC GAP and MF was adopted by all countries during the 77th World Health Assembly in May 2024.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) represent a significant burden and safety concern for patients globally, although with variations across the world [1]. In acute care facilities in the European Union and European Economic Area, HAIs affect, on average, 8% of patients [2]. The frequency of HAIs is higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), and varies from approximately 10% in the Americas and Eastern Mediterranean regions to up to 27% in Africa. Furthermore, in intensive care units (ICUs), up to one-third of patients can be affected by HAIs [3]. Approximately one in four (23.6%) of all cases of hospital-treated sepsis are healthcare-associated, and this increases to almost half (48.7%) among cases of sepsis with organ dysfunction treated in adult ICUs [4,5]. A large proportion of HAIs can be prevented with improved infection prevention and control (IPC) practices and basic water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) services, both of which are also highly cost-effective to reduce antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in healthcare settings [6]. Data from the 2023–2024 World Health Organization (WHO) global IPC survey [3] found that while 71% of countries now have an active IPC programme, just 6% met all the WHO IPC minimum requirements at the national level [7].

Recognizing the need for strengthening IPC, all countries adopted the first Global Strategy on IPC (GSIPC) in 2022 [8], in accordance with a resolution from the 75th World Health Assembly [9]. The strategy encompasses eight strategic directions, serving as the overarching guiding framework for countries' actions to implement the GSIPC. These strategic directions are: (i) political commitment and policies; (ii) active IPC programmes; (iii) IPC integration and coordination; (iv) IPC knowledge of health and care workers and career pathways for IPC professionals; (v) data for action; (vi) advocacy and communications; (vii) research and development; and (viii) collaboration and stakeholders' support.

To support implementation of the strategy, a global action plan (GAP) and monitoring framework (MF) were developed and approved by the World Health Assembly in May 2024 [10]. This process involved extensive consultations with member states, the three operational levels of WHO (headquarters, regional office and country office), and leading international organizations in the field of IPC including members of the Global IPC Network (GIPCN) [11]. In May 2023, an international meeting was held in Geneva to start the development of the GAP and MF. The meeting brought together GIPCN members, ministry of health representatives and WHO professionals to identify key actions, indicators and targets for IPC at the global, national and facility levels, aligned with the eight strategic directions of the GSIPC. Consultations with experts during the meeting yielded a list of >50 actions with associated multiple IPC indicators and, in some cases, targets for the global and national levels (>100). Some of the 10.13039/100018534IPC indicators proposed during the international meeting already existed as part of other global initiatives and country capacity monitoring systems, such as: the International Health Regulation States Parties Self-Assessment Annual Report tool [12], the Tracking AMR Country Self-assessment Survey platform [13], the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals [14], and the WHO/United Nation's Children's Fund Joint Monitoring Programme for Water Supply, Sanitation and Hygiene [15]. Others were drawn from WHO IPC assessment tools developed to monitor the core components and minimum requirements for IPC programmes [16]. Some of the proposed indicators also overlapped with IPC standards used by international healthcare accreditation bodies under the umbrella of the International Society for Quality in Health Care (i.e. Joint Commission International and others). Following the WHO international consultation meeting, the draft actions, indicators and targets were discussed further in a global WHO online consultation with country representatives in September 2023.

As an additional critical step, the draft indicators and targets developed through the consultative process and the work of the WHO secretariat were included in an e-Delphi survey to achieve consensus on the content of the WHO MF for IPC. The e-Delphi study aimed to gather input and consensus to select, refine and prioritize indicators and targets from an initial list of >100 indicators, identified to track progress of the actions indicated in the GAP. The aim of the survey was also to identify a core set of top indicators and targets for monitoring at both the local and national levels. This paper describes the methodology and results of the e-Delphi study.

Methods

The study was conducted as an e-Delphi consensus-seeking exercise, with two rounds of sequential surveys (rounds 1 and 2) administered online between 27th September and 26th October 2023. The e-Delphi method is an online adaptation of the traditional Delphi method, allowing experts to provide their input online in their own time to reach a consensus [[17], [18], [19]].

Recruitment of participants

Participants in the e-Delphi surveys were recruited from the GIPCN and other WHO stakeholders' networks (including multiple international, national and professional organizations and societies working in the field of IPC and part of the GIPCN1), national IPC focal points and WHO personnel (not involved in the study management), representing a broad range of experts in IPC and its complementary areas of work, including antimicrobial resistance, occupational health, patient safety, public health emergencies, quality of care, WASH and healthcare waste management. As there is no standard method for calculating the sample size for Delphi processes [[17], [18], [19]], a purposive sampling technique was used, with the aim of convening an international group of participants from countries representing all six WHO regions and four World Bank income levels [20].

Study process

During round 1, eligible participants received an invitation via e-mail, including an explanation of the study objectives, instructions for participation and expected outcomes. Regular reminders were also sent. Participants were provided with the lists of the proposed IPC actions (N=59, not to be rated, but provided to give full context), indicators and targets (134 indicators and 27 targets to be rated, as identified during the initial consultations) for each of the eight strategic directions of the WHO GSIPC. The questionnaire was initially pilot tested with five independent testers (not included later as eligible participants) prior to launching round 1 of the surveys, and to verify the clarity of the instructions provided and usability of the platform. ‘Consensus’ was defined as being met if >70% of the responses fell within the two positive extreme ranges of the scale and, at the same time, <5% of the responses were within the two negative extreme ranges. This level of consent is consistent with recommendations published in the healthcare literature [[21], [22], [23]]. Participants were also given the opportunity to add free-text comments for each question.

Two additional questions were added at the end of round 1 to assess the general feasibility of all targets:1.Do you agree with the current and ambitious proposed target approach of 30% of countries by 2026, 75% by 2028, and 90% or 100% by 2030?2.Would you have a strong preference for a more realistic target approach of 30% of countries by 2026, 50% by 2028, and 80% by 2030?

In round 2, only participants that had completed round 1 in full were invited. Each participant was provided with the distribution of responses and comments from all participants in the previous round alongside their own response (but only for those IPC indicators and targets that did not reach consensus in round 1). Participants were then asked to consider the responses from the other participants and review their own, either confirming or changing them (Part A). They were also asked to rank the top indicators and targets (top 10 global targets and top five national targets) (Part B).

Data collection and analysis

Data collection was performed through the Welphi platform [28], an online survey platform that implements the e-Delphi method and allows the creation of questionnaires intended to capture the perspectives of geographically dispersed participants in an easy and efficient way. All experts received an automated e-mail with a unique URL link to access the questionnaire. The study was conducted in alignment with, and reported following the reporting standard for, Conducting and Reporting of Delphi Studies [27]. Data analysis was performed using the two-samples z-approach by comparing two percentage rates using an online statistical calculator based on Chi-squared.

Ethics

Ethical exemption was obtained from the WHO Ethics Review Committee (ERC) (ERC.0004002). Participants were included anonymously, and no personal and traceable information was collected. A separate consent form was deemed unnecessary, as instructions for participation (voluntary and anonymous) were provided on the online platform prior to commencing the survey.

Results

In total, 394 participants were invited, including all regional IPC leads (referred from here as ‘IPC focal points’) from the six WHO regional offices (African, Eastern Mediterranean, European, Americas, South-East Asia and Western Pacific), the national IPC focal points in the ministries of health and associated offices of 129 member states for which contact details were available (47 countries in the African region, 22 in the Eastern Mediterranean region, 22 from the European region, 23 from the Americas region, nine from the South-east Asian region and six from the Western Pacific region, representing all income levels) (Figure 1), 36 international IPC organizations and societies that were part of the GIPCN, plus various other stakeholders with an interest in IPC that are both internal and external to WHO. Other member states (i.e. UK represented by the UK Health Security Agency) participated through their GIPCN representation. Round 1

The response rate in round 1 was 36% (142/394), and participants were asked to rate 134 indicators and 27 targets (Supplementary Table 1). All but one indicator achieved the cut-off consensus level of 70%: the rejected indicator (68% consensus) belonged to Strategic Direction (SD) 7 (research and development) > facility level > number of research projects on IPC implemented. When also considering the negative part of the consensus rule with 5% level of disagreement, eight indicators (inclusive of the one mentioned above) and eight targets were included for re-voting in round 2 (Supplementary Table 2).

Overall, the various indicators and targets achieved an average level of consensus of 87%, ranging from 82% (in SD7) to 90% (SD5, data for action and surveillance), and a high level of agreement (≥90% consensus) was achieved in 50% (67/134) of the indicators (Figure 2). At the end of round 1, there was a consensus (72%) supporting more realistic targets (30% of countries achieving them by 2026, 50% by 2028, and 80% by 2030) compared with higher targets (30% of countries by 2026, 75% by 2028, and 90% or 100% by 2030) with 56% consensus (P=0.01).

Round 2

Only the 142 participants that completed round 1 were invited to take part in round 2. The response rate was 69% (98/142), and this was significantly higher compared with round 1 (P<0.00001). Due to the anonymized data, it was not possible to assess the total overall number of countries that participated in the entire process. In round 2, the 16 indicators and targets from round 1 were subjected to a re-vote (Part A), followed by a ranking of all 27 targets (not indicators) to select the top national and global targets (Part B).

Participants' comments

Many participants provided free-text comments, mostly expressing their support for those indicators that achieved high consensus, their concerns on the feasibility of the rejected indicators, and on the initial higher targets of 100% of countries achieving them by 2030. Other comments fell into the categories of general reflections, those suggesting clarifications and those provided to justify scoring. These comments added a qualitative dimension, including a specific focus on LMICs, and they were reviewed carefully by the WHO team and considered when finalizing the GAP and MF for submission to the WHO Executive Board and the World Health Assembly.

Discussion

To the authors' knowledge, this is the first and most comprehensive global consultation aimed at identifying a definitive set of IPC indicators and targets for use at global, national and facility levels, and that have been included in the final WHO IPC GAP and MF, thereby supporting the achievement of the GSIPC objectives. In total, 394 participants from all six WHO regions, including regional and national focal points from member states and key experts among the global network, were invited to participate, maximizing the effort to include as many interested stakeholders as possible.

The use of Delphi surveys has become common across health sciences research, and can be used to identify priorities, reach consensus on specific issues, and represent a form of expert opinion research that can address fundamental questions where the guiding evidence is limited [18]. Following round 2 of this e-Delphi study, 131 indicators and 27 targets were retained for further international consultation and inclusion in the new WHO Global Monitoring Framework for IPC.

The results of this e-Delphi survey also show the different levels of support for the eight strategic directions (Figure 2) contained in the GAP and MF. Indicators and targets in SD5 (data for action) achieved the highest consensus (with an average of 90%), highlighting the current gap and the need to establish or strengthen global and national data collection and tracking systems for IPC monitoring to support HAI and related AMR surveillance systems, including for early warning to detect outbreaks, epidemics and pandemic-prone pathogens, and for monitoring antimicrobial consumption. Conversely, research and development in the field of IPC (SD7) achieved the lowest consensus level among the various indicators (with an average of 82%). This likely reflects the pressing challenges faced by healthcare settings, particularly in LMICs, where there are immediate gaps in IPC that require urgent attention. Many front-line healthcare professionals and policy makers may prioritize establishing fundamental IPC measures – such as hygiene protocols, infrastructure improvements, and workforce training – before dedicating resources to advancing research. Among others, free-text comments included ‘research will be difficult for some countries and may distract them from implementation of IPC’, ‘this is too much to ask from countries that are still building their programmes’, and ‘consider that research mostly requires time and money and that LMICs will be struggling to achieve other goals’.

It is also important to note that among 131 indicators identified for the IPC MF through this e-Delphi study, 25 belong to areas of work that are critically complementary to IPC in reducing infections and AMR in health care, with two indicators related to appropriate use of antimicrobials, highlighting the synergistic effect of antimicrobial stewardship and IPC interventions, and the importance of implementing and monitoring them together. The selected indicators and targets also inherently reflect sustainability aspects of IPC, particularly through measures such as dedicated funding for WASH services, national policy integration, and long-term surveillance systems, reinforcing the importance of sustainable capacity-building in IPC.

Regarding the targets to be achieved by 2030, there was a strong indication from most participants to reduce the expected level of achievement from 100% to 80%. Thus, the decision was taken to follow this recommendation for most targets, except for those that reflect the WHO minimum requirements for IPC [7] which, by definition, should be met by all countries to provide minimum protection and safety to patients, healthcare workers and visitors to healthcare facilities. It is also critical to consider that while the proposed indicators and targets are undoubtedly ambitious, most of them are not new and have been recommended by WHO for many years through the guidelines on core components [29], the minimum requirements for IPC programmes [7], and other key monitoring systems (for example on AMR, epidemics and pandemics; occupational health; and WASH). WHO had also provided clear guidance on the implementation strategies to achieve these, indicating a step-wise approach for countries and healthcare facilities to focus on and prioritize actions based on the local situation and available resources [[30], [31], [32]].

This study has some limitations. The issues of sample size, consensus and representativeness are often major challenges for a survey at the global level; as such, this study focused on the three levels of WHO (headquarters, regional and country), and contacted all IPC focal points within member states and professional societies in the field. However, some countries were reluctant to share ministry of health contact details for survey purposes, and thus the authors were only able to invite focal points whose contact details were available in their database. Despite the authors' best efforts and multiple reminders, there was a low response rate (36%; 142/394) in round 1, considering a threshold acceptable level of ≥65% as an appropriate survey response rate, and some regions likely remained under-represented. However, once engaged in the process, participants were substantially (69%; 98/142) more likely to complete the subsequent round, but this could also have skewed the results in favour of the more responsive countries. Criticism of the Delphi method also includes the perception that it may force consensus, and it does not allow participants to fully express and discuss their concerns. The authors addressed the above limitations through the availability of free comments, that were shared with all participants across rounds, and the e-Delphi survey represented an additional step, complementary to the multiple global and regional consultations, to achieve a tangible and measurable consensus on the indicators and targets to be included in the final framework. Finally, the study was conducted in line with internationally recognized guidelines for the Conducting and Reporting of Delphi Studies [27].

In conclusion, an e-Delphi survey was undertaken successfully to determine suitable indicators and targets for IPC monitoring at the global, national and facility levels. The process resulted in a high level of agreement, enabling the identification of strong indicators and prioritization of preferred targets. Despite the advantages and disadvantages of the approach, the Delphi methodology remains highly effective in strategic decision-making and for policy development, particularly when a consultation process involving multi-level parties globally, the evaluation of numerous and potentially competing items, and a relatively short timeframe are required. The results of this study constituted a solid basis for further global and regional consultations with countries on the GAP and MF that led to the finalization and unanimous adoption of the WHO GAP MF by all WHO member states. The selected indicators and targets will be operationalized to improve monitoring and implementation of IPC globally and at the national and facility levels.
