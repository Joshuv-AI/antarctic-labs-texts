HNRNPC-Mediated m6A Epitranscriptomics Drives CD80-Dependent Tubular Dysfunction in Sepsis-Induced AKI
Authors: Chenxia Juan, Xiangling Zhao, Yuejuan Wang, Duqun Chen, Manshu Yu, Yan Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (S-AKI) is a critical condition characterized by renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis and abnormal cytoskeleton. This study aims to investigate the role of the m6A modification-dependent protein HNRNPC in regulating renal cell apoptosis and cytoskeleton in S-AKI. Dot blot analysis was employed to assess the total m6A levels. Cell viability, flow cytometry, and fluorescent phalloidin staining were used to evaluate the role of HNRNPC in CD80-dependent apoptosis and cytoskeletal remodeling. RNA sequencing and subsequent data analysis highlighted the involvement of the NF-κB signaling pathway. Luciferase reporter assays and Western blot were used to establish that HNRNPC transcriptionally promotes CD80 expression. Bioinformatics, EMSA, and ChIP assays further confirmed the role of NF-κB in regulating CD80. Additionally, MeRIP-qPCR and RNA m6A quantification demonstrated that HNRNPC facilitates apoptosis through m6A-dependent regulation of NF-κB. Induction of HNRNPC to a higher level can induce apoptosis and cytoskeletal deformation in renal tubular cells. Meanwhile, CD80 was essential for HNRNPC-induced tubular injury. Further experiments showed that HNRNPC regulated NF-κB mRNA dependent on m6A modification. Moreover, NF-κB acted as a transcription factor to promote CD80 expression. In vivo experiments further verified the relationships between HNRNPC, NF-κB, and CD80 and demonstrated the significance of HNRNPC in CD80-associated apoptosis. This study elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of S-AKI and highlights the potential of HNRNPC and CD80 as therapeutic targets to reduce renal damage in S-AKI.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-025-02349-7.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a clinical syndrome characterized by azotemia, electrolyte disturbances, acid–base imbalances, and systemic symptoms. In intensive care units (ICUs), AKI affects 22–67% of patients, with a poor long-term prognosis [1, 2]. Up to 46% of critically ill patients experience AKI, and approximately 20% require renal replacement therapy (RRT) [3]. Severe AKI requiring RRT is associated with high mortality rates (45%−70%) among hospitalized patients, with 13–32% requiring maintenance hemodialysis post-discharge [4, 5]. AKI is causally linked to subsequent chronic kidney disease (CKD) development [6, 7], posing a significant medical burden in China.

AKI can be triggered by various factors, with microbial infections, particularly those leading to sepsis, being predominant in ICU settings [8]. Understanding and intervening in sepsis-associated AKI (S-AKI) are urgent research priorities, involving hypotheses such as hemodynamic and inflammatory pathways [9]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying S-AKI require further elucidation [3].

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a prevalent RNA modification involved in mRNA processing and regulation in eukaryotes [10]. In AKI, m6A modifications play crucial roles. For instance, FTO-mediated m6A enhances PPAR-α-related inflammation in alcohol-induced renal injury [11], while METTL3 regulates inflammatory pain by modulating pri-miR-365-3p processing [12]. Dysregulation of m6A writers, readers, and erasers can alter downstream gene expression, contributing to disease pathogenesis [13, 14]. Recent studies highlight the"m6A switch,"where m6A levels regulate RNA structure, thereby influencing RNA–protein interactions crucial for biological regulation [15]. HNRNPC, an m6A-dependent RNA-binding protein, regulates gene expression through alternative splicing and translation enhancement [16].

Exploring downstream genes in AKI pathogenesis, CD80, traditionally a co-stimulatory factor in antigen-presenting cells, has garnered attention. CD80 overexpression on non-bone marrow-derived cells impairs function [17, 18]. In mouse models of S-AKI, anti-CD80 antibodies reduce leukocyte infiltration and improve renal function post-ischemia–reperfusion injury [19]. CD80 is upregulated in renal tubular epithelial cells during AKI, but its upstream regulation and cellular mechanisms remain unclear.

Our hypothesis proposes that under inflammatory conditions, altered m6A levels regulate CD80 via HNRNPC-mediated NF-κB signaling, promoting renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis in AKI pathogenesis. This study aims to investigate this hypothesis from molecular mechanisms to pathological implications, using both in vitro cell models and in vivo animal models. Understanding the role of m6A recognition protein HNRNPC in NF-κB/CD80-mediated renal epithelial cell apoptosis could provide new molecular insights for intervening in S-AKI pathogenesis and targeting therapies.

Materials and Methods

Reagents and Plasmids

Reagents and Plasmids

Reagents and Plasmids

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, #L2880) was procured from Sigma-Aldrich. 3-Deazaadenosine (DAA, #HY-W013332), cycloheximide (CHX, #HY-12320), and Z-VAD-FMK (#HY-16658B) were sourced from MedChemExpress (MCE). RNA interference constructs (sh-METTL3, sh-HNRNPC, sh-CD80, and sh-NF-κB p65) were acquired from Genepharma, and lentiviruses expressing sh-HNRNPC and nonsense controls (NC) were obtained from Tsingke. The NF-κB p65 overexpression plasmids were constructed using p-ENTER, and the CD80 overexpression ones were developed with pcDNA3.1.2.Animal Model of AKI

2.Animal Model of AKI

Animal Model of AKI

The animal study was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments at Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (2022NL-KS202). Male C57BL/6 J mice were sourced from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology and housed in a biosafety level-3 (ABSL-3) laboratory. Mice were randomly allocated to groups and subjected to cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery to induce septic AKI. In brief, after midline laparotomy, the cecum was Ligated. A 21-gauge needle was used to perform a single through-and-through puncture at the antimesenteric side of the ligated cecum. A small amount of fecal content was extruded to ensure patency, followed by cecal repositioning and abdominal closure. Lentiviral particles (Lv-HNRNPC, titer: 1 × 109 TU/mL) silencing the target gene were administered via tail vein injection 72 h prior to CLP surgery. Each mouse received 100 μL of viral suspension (equivalent to 1 × 108 TU) diluted in sterile PBS, with control groups receiving empty vector (Lv-NC) at the same dose. All procedures complied with Chinese regulations governing laboratory animal use.3.Cell Culture

3.Cell Culture

Cell Culture

HK2 cells from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences were cultured in DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. LPS was used to stimulate cells, and CHX was employed to block protein synthesis prior to LPS treatment. Z-VAD-FMK was applied to inhibit caspase-related apoptosis, while DAA was used to modulate m6A methylation levels.4.RNA Extraction, Sequencing and Subsequent Data Analysis

4.RNA Extraction, Sequencing and Subsequent Data Analysis

RNA Extraction, Sequencing and Subsequent Data Analysis

RNA was extracted utilizing the EZNA ® Total RNA Kit I (R6834, Omega Bio-Tek). High-throughput RNA and deep sequencing were conducted by Shenzhen Huada Genome Co., Ltd., followed by analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across groups. DEGs were identified using the criteria of Log2FC(JO/JA) ≥ 1 or ≤ −1, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were then conducted to identify biological process and pathway variations among samples.5.Renal Function and Histology Evaluation

5.Renal Function and Histology Evaluation

Renal Function and Histology Evaluation

The frozen kidney tissues were preserved at −80 °C for subsequent protein or mRNA detection. The blood of mice was centrifuged at 1500 r/min at 4 °C. Next, the separated serum was stored at −80 °C. Serum creatinine (Scr) was measured using commercial kit reagents to assess Renal function. Paraffin-embedded kidney tissues were sectioned and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining.6.Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Immunofluorescence Staining

6.Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Immunofluorescence Staining

Antigen retrieval for paraffin-embedded tissues was performed using microwave heating in a citrate solution prior to immunostaining. Kidney tissues underwent IHC incubation with antibodies targeting HNRNPC, NF-κB p65, and CD80. Fluorescent phalloidin was used to visualize F-actin in HK2 cells. DAPI was used to stain cell nuclei. Samples were visualized with a Leica Thunder microscope system following immuno-staining. Analysis of immunohistochemistry images was conducted in ImageJ.7.Dot Blot and Western Blot (WB) Analysis

7.Dot Blot and Western Blot (WB) Analysis

Dot Blot and Western Blot (WB) Analysis

Poly (A) RNA was isolated from total RNA using the DynaBeads mRNA Purification Kit (ThermoFisher) for dot blot analysis. Poly(A) RNAs were diluted to 250 ng/μl and 100 ng/μl concentrations. Each dilution was applied twice (2 µl) onto positively charged nylon membranes (Amersham Pharmacia). Poly(A) RNA membranes were crosslinked with a Stratalinker 2400 Crosslinker. The membranes were incubated in blocking buffer with diluted anti-m6A antibodies. Total m6A levels were measured using enhanced chemiluminescence following standard immunoblotting procedures with secondary antibody treatment.

WB was conventionally performed. The primary antibodies utilized included anti-HNRNPC (Proteintech, #11760–1-AP), anti-NF-κB p65 (Proteintech, #10745–1-AP), anti-CD80 (Abcam, #ab134120), anti-PARP (Proteintech, #13371–1-AP), anti-Bcl2 (Cell signaling technology, #3498), anti-Cleaved-Caspase3 (Proteintech, #68773–1-Ig), and anti-β-actin (Proteintech, #20536–1-AP). Quantification of WB bands was performed with ImageJ.8.RNA m6A Quantification and MeRIP-qPCR

8.RNA m6A Quantification and MeRIP-qPCR

RNA m6A Quantification and MeRIP-qPCR

The m6A content was analyzed using an RNA Methylation Assay Kit from Abcam. Following the manufacturer's protocol, 150 ng of extracted RNA and the appropriate solution were added to a 96-well plate. A microplate reader measured absorption at 450 nm to calculate the percentage of m6A content. The MeRIP-qPCR assay utilized a Dynabeads™ mRNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen) to isolate mRNA from total RNA.A third of the isolated samples served as an input control. The primary anti-N6-methyladenosine antibody or rabbit IgG (both from Abcam) was conjugated with Pierce™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Scientific).The isolated samples were combined and precipitated using glycogen, sodium acetate, and ethanol. m6A enrichment was assessed using qPCR, with results normalized to the input.9.Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

9.Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Mouse CD80 levels (CUSABIO, #CSB-EL004959MO), IL-6 levels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #KMC0061), TNF-α levels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #BMS607-3), and Scr levels (Jiancheng, #C011-2–1) in mice were measured following the provided instructions. Each sample was measured either four or six times.10.Real-Time Quantitative PCR (qPCR) and Cell Viability Analysis

10.Real-Time Quantitative PCR (qPCR) and Cell Viability Analysis

Real-Time Quantitative PCR (qPCR) and Cell Viability Analysis

For qPCR, cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript RT Master Mix kit (Takara, #RR036A). The relative level of mRNA expression was quantified with an SYBR Kit (Vazyme Biotech, #Q711).

Cell viability was assessed using the MCE cell viability assay (#HY-K0301). Briefly, HK2 cells were seeded into the plates and incubated with 10 ul CCK-8 per well. Optical density (OD) was detected at 450 nm using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).11.Transient Transfections and Luciferase Reporter Assays

11.Transient Transfections and Luciferase Reporter Assays

Transient Transfections and Luciferase Reporter Assays

For promoter analysis, the CD80 promoter was inserted into pGL3-basic plasmids (Promega). PCR products of both wild-type and mutant forms, featuring the NF-κB p65 binding motif in the CD80 promoter, were cloned into pGL3-basic plasmids. To investigate how HNRNPC stabilizes NF-κB p65 mRNA through the 3'-UTR region, adenosine (A) in the m6A motif was substituted with cytosine (C) and cloned into pmir-GLO reporter plasmids. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine-3000 (Invitrogen) according to the provided protocol. The dual-luciferase reporter gene assay kit (Promega) was applied to detect luciferase activities. The activities of firefly luciferase were normalized to those of Renilla luciferase.12.Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA) and Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

12.Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA) and Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA) and Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

GenePharma synthesized the Wt and Mut short oligonucleotide probes for EMSA. The probes were labeled at the 3’ end with biotin using the Biotin 3’ End DNA Labeling Kit (Thermo Pierce). EMSA was performed utilizing the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific. In the super shift assay, either anti-NF-κB p65 antibody (Proteintech, #10745–1-AP) or IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, #3900) was added. All mixtures were resolved using 6% native polyacrylamide and subsequently transferred to positively charged nylon membranes (Amersham Pharmacia). Chemiluminescence technique was used to detect the biotin-labeled DNA following crosslinking.

ChIP was conducted using Magna ChIP® A/G Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Kit from Merck. Protein-DNA complexes were incubated overnight with protein beads coupled to either anti-NF-κB p65 antibodies (Proteintech, #10745–1-AP) or IgG antibodies (Merck, #17–10086). Subsequently, DNA was purified and precipitated for PCR analysis utilizing Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme, #P222-01). The products were separated using a 2% agarose gel. ChIP DNA was quantified via qPCR with an SYBR Kit (Vazyme Biotech, #Q711) on the StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems).13.Bioinformatics

13.Bioinformatics

Bioinformatics

Database searches to find homologies between sequences were performed using the BLAST algorithm (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The NF-κB p65 binding site within the CD80 promoter was predicted by the JASPAR (https://jaspar.genereg.net/) and the UCSC (http://genome.ucsc.edu/) databases. We investigated the m6A site in NF-κB p65 mRNA using RMVar (https://rmvar.renlab.org/) and SRAMP (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp).14.Statistical Analysis

14.Statistical Analysis

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 software was used for statistical analyses and data visualization, with each point representing independent data. P values were determined using t-test, one-way and two-way ANOVA, along with Pearson analyses. Results are expressed as means ± SEMs from three independent experiments. P values less than 0.05 (*P < 0.05) and 0.001 (***P < 0.001) were deemed statistically significant, whereas N.S. indicates non-significance.

We investigated mRNA expression levels of m6A regulators in AKI using qPCR and identified elevated HNRNPC expression in CLP mice (Fig. 1B). Intracellular HNRNPC level was further measured through WB in CLP group (Fig. 1C). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis revealed a similar pattern in HNRNPC expression (Fig. 1D). To investigate HNRNPC's role in LPS-induced HK2 cells, shRNA was used to knock down the HNRNPC gene, and the suppression of its expression was confirmed by western blot analysis (Fig. 1H). The induction of HNRNPC in HK2 cells by LPS, as opposed to PBS, enhanced apoptosis, as evidenced by cell viability assays, apoptosis-related protein levels, and flow cytometry (Fig. 1I-K). HNRNPC knock-down enhanced the viability of LPS-stimulated HK2 cells (Fig. 1I). In sh-HNRNPC-expressing HK2 cells, PARP and cleaved Caspase3 levels decreased, whereas Bcl2 levels increased (Fig. 1J). Flow cytometry revealed the proportion of apoptotic cells reduced since the HNRNPC expression level was down-regulated (Fig. 1K). Furthermore, HK2 cytoskeleton was deformed and F-actin content was reduced when stimulated with LPS (Fig. 1L). And, the trend was reversed when HNRNPC was knocked-down. Our earlier research demonstrated that inhibiting CD80 can reduce apoptosis. We thereby hypothesized that HNRNPC-induced apoptosis is via targeting CD80. We then tested whether HNRNPC affects the expression of CD80 in HK2 cells.CD80 expression was elevated in CLP mice, as shown in Fig. 2E-G. Also, LPS stimulation up-regulated the CD80 expression in HK2 cells (Fig. 2H and I).

Despite LPS up-regulates CD80, whether the CD80 is required for HNRNPC-induced apoptosis in septic AKI remains unknown.CD80 was silenced in HK2 cells after HNRNPC induction (Fig. 2H and I). It was obvious that knocking down CD80 could partly promote cell viability (Fig. 2J) and abolish HNRNPC-induced apoptosis (Fig. 2K). Meanwhile, the LPS-stimulated reduce of F-actin content in HK2 cells was reversed when CD80 was knocked-down (Fig. 2L). The findings suggest that HNRNPC triggers apoptosis in septic AKI through CD80. Furthermore, at basal level, the overexpression of CD80 in sh-HNRNPC HK2 cells inhibited cell viability (Fig. 2M) and aggravated the degree of apoptosis and deformation of the cytoskeleton (Fig. 2N and O). To further enhance the reliability of the results, the apoptosis level was detected after the overexpression of HNRNPC along with the knockdown of CD80. Figure S1A confirmed the overexpression of HNRNPC and the knockdown of CD80. As shown in Fig. S1B, overexpression of HNRNPC and knockdown of CD80 resulted increased cell viability compared to the group overexpressing HNRNPC alone. Meanwhile, knocking down CD80 could change the apoptosis-related gene protein levels that was induced by the overexpression of HNRNPC (Fig. S1C). These results confirmed the importance of CD80 in the HNRNPC-induced apoptosis. CD80 is Transcriptionally Promoted by HNRNPC

CD80 expression was assessed in HK2 cells expressing sh-HNRNPC or sh-NC, revealing that HNRNPC is crucial for enhancing CD80 expression in septic AKI (Fig. 3A and B). The JASPAR and UCSC online tools were utilized to predict potential transcription factor binding in the region, identifying a p65 (RELA, a subunit crucial for the transcription activation potential of κB) motif (Fig. 4A). We mutated the NF-κB p65 motif to assess its necessity for CD80 promoter activity. As expected, overexpressing NF-κB p65 promoted CD80 promoter activity, while knocking it down reduced CD80 promoter activity (Fig. 4B). Altering the NF-κB p65 motif inhibited its impact, suggesting that NF-κB p65 is essential for CD80 promoter activity (Fig. 4B). Discussion

Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (S-AKI) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients, and its underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood. This study investigates the role of the m6A modification-dependent RNA-binding protein HNRNPC in regulating CD80 expression and promoting renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis during S-AKI. Our results indicate that HNRNPC, as an m6A"switch", modulates CD80 expression through NF-κB signaling, which exacerbates renal apoptosis under inflammatory conditions. These findings offer novel insights into the molecular pathogenesis of S-AKI and provide potential therapeutic avenues for its intervention.

HNRNPC as a Regulator of m6A-Dependent RNA Modification in AKI

The role of m6A modification in regulating RNA metabolism has been increasingly recognized in various diseases, including AKI. Studies have shown that m6A modifications influence gene expression by altering RNA splicing, stability, and translation, thus modulating cellular responses to stress and injury [20]. In AKI, several m6A"writers,""readers,"and"erasers"have been implicated in regulating inflammation and apoptosis. Our findings extend this knowledge by demonstrating that HNRNPC, a well-established m6A"reader,"is crucial in regulating CD80 expression in renal tubular epithelial cells during S-AKI.

Previous research has highlighted the involvement of HNRNPC in regulating RNA splicing and translation under stress conditions [21, 22]. In our study, we provide evidence that HNRNPC-mediated modulation of CD80 expression is dependent on m6A modification. This supports the concept of the"m6A switch,"where RNA-binding proteins like HNRNPC play a pivotal role in altering the fate of mRNA through differential splicing or translation in response to cellular stress signals [15, 23]. Moreover, our results align with recent studies that have demonstrated the critical role of m6A in regulating immune responses and inflammation, which are central to the pathogenesis of S-AKI [24].

CD80 and NF-κB Signaling in S-AKI

CD80, a co-stimulatory molecule traditionally associated with antigen-presenting cells, has recently been implicated in kidney injury and inflammation. Elevated expression of CD80 has been observed in renal tubular epithelial cells during AKI, and it has been proposed that CD80 may contribute to renal injury by promoting inflammatory responses [19]. Our study extends this knowledge by identifying CD80 as a downstream target of HNRNPC-mediated NF-κB activation. NF-κB, a key transcription factor in inflammation, has been shown to regulate the expression of a variety of pro-inflammatory genes, including CD80 [25].

The interaction between HNRNPC and NF-κB in regulating CD80 expression provides a novel molecular mechanism for renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis during S-AKI. Previous studies have shown that NF-κB activation is crucial for the inflammatory response in S-AKI, and inhibition of NF-κB signaling can protect against kidney injury in various animal models [26, 27]. Our findings suggest that HNRNPC not only acts as an m6A"switch"but also integrates NF-κB signaling to promote CD80-mediated renal apoptosis. These results are consistent with studies showing that NF-κB-induced CD80 expression is linked to immune cell activation and tissue damage during inflammation [28].

Implications for Therapeutic Targeting

The molecular pathways identified in this study suggest potential therapeutic targets for mitigating S-AKI. Inhibition of the m6A"reader"HNRNPC or blocking NF-κB signaling could provide promising strategies to reduce CD80-mediated renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis and inflammation in S-AKI. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of targeting NF-κB in treating inflammatory diseases and kidney injury. For example, several small molecules that inhibit NF-κB activation have shown efficacy in preclinical models of AKI [29]. Likewise, strategies to modulate RNA modifications, such as the use of small molecules targeting m6A regulators, are gaining attention as a therapeutic approach in various diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders [30].

Our findings suggest that the combined inhibition of m6A modification and NF-κB signaling could provide a dual approach to ameliorate renal injury in S-AKI. Further research is needed to explore the therapeutic potential of HNRNPC inhibitors and NF-κB modulators in clinical settings.

Limitations and Future Directions

While this study provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of S-AKI, there are several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, while we have demonstrated the involvement of HNRNPC in regulating CD80 expression in vitro and in vivo, the specific m6A-modified sites on CD80 mRNA remain to be identified. Future studies using high-throughput sequencing techniques, such as m6A RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-Seq), could provide a comprehensive map of m6A modifications in AKI and identify other potential target genes regulated by HNRNPC.
