Extracorporeal Photopheresis in Solid Organ Transplantation: Modulating B-cell Responses to Improve Graft Survival
Authors: Filipe Nogueira, S. Marieke van Ham, Anja ten Brinke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Solid organ transplantation remains the only curative treatment for end-stage organ diseases. A critical aspect of enhancing long-term graft survival is to prevent antibody-mediated rejection caused by donor-specific antibodies (DSAs). DSAs are formed when donor alloantigen-specific B cells differentiate into antibody-secreting cells. In this review, we explore what is known about the relationship between treatment with extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP) and its effects on undesired B-cell activation and DSA formation. Current preliminary evidence suggests that ECP, when used as an adjuvant therapy, displays significant benefits, including allograft survival, decreased circulating DSAs, and downregulated activation of the allogeneic immune response, possibly through expansion of regulatory B cells. Despite these promising findings, the precise mechanisms through which ECP affects B-cell fate remain incompletely understood. Further research into specific B-cell subpopulations is necessary to fully elucidate the role of ECP in modulating pathways involved in DSA formation, which might allow more effective management of antibody-mediated rejection.

DONOR-SPECIFIC ANTIBODIES AND ROLE IN TRANSPLANT REJECTION

Organ transplantation is a definitive treatment for patients with end-stage organ failure, providing the opportunity for long-term survival and improved quality of life.1-3 However, acute and chronic alloimmune responses against transplanted tissues remain a significant challenge. A critical component of this immune response is the formation of donor-specific antibodies (DSAs) by donor antigen-specific B cells that primarily target the highly diverse HLA.4 DSAs are a major barrier to long-term graft acceptance, as they cause acute and chronic antibody-mediated rejection (AMR). AMR results from the direct binding of DSAs to the allograft, leading to complement activation and recruitment of leukocytes, which causes injury to endothelial and parenchymal cells.5 In addition, transplantation between HLA-identical siblings can result in graft rejection, underscoring the importance of antibodies against non-HLA antigens.6

Lung transplant recipients who develop de novo DSAs (dnDSAs)—antibodies that arise after transplantation—tend to experience worse allograft survival, when sensitization occurs within the first-year posttransplant.7 dnDSA formation occurred in one-fourth of lung transplant patients within the first year; however, in one-third of these patients DSA was only transiently detectable.7 In kidney transplantation, development of dnDSAs is associated with an increased risk of acute AMR and allograft loss.8

Preformed DSAs resulting from previous exposure to foreign major histocompatibility complex, such as previous transplants, blood transfusions, or pregnancy, are a risk factor for AMR.9

Kidney transplant recipients with low titers of DSA detected in solid-phase assays, but a negative flow-cytometry crossmatch and negative complement-dependent cytotoxicity crossmatch, have a twofold higher risk of experiencing AMR compared with DSA− patients.10 However, outcomes at 4–5 y in kidney transplant patients who developed AMR were better for those who had low titers of DSA at transplantation compared with those who developed dnDSA after transplantation.11 This surprising result is partly explained by more intensive monitoring and earlier, more aggressive treatment in patients with preexisting DSA. However, a component of “graft accommodation” may also be involved, either because of reduced pathogenicity or clearance of preformed DSAs in patients under full immunosuppression, or as a result of the allograft acquiring resistance to antibody-mediated injury over time. DSAs exert direct effects on the graft endothelium by inducing endothelial cell activation and proliferation, thereby contributing to allograft injury. “Graft accommodation” may thus be determined by the endothelium’s capacity to resist the injurious effects of DSA binding.12 Furthermore, B-cell activity alongside fibrosis is detected 1 y after transplantation in patients who experienced acute kidney injury in the first instance. Although DSA formation in these chronic kidney injury patients seems primarily not initiated by a donor-recipient mismatch, it appears to result from a late immune response after dysfunctional tissue repair and induction of tissue fibrosis.13 Together this highlights the critical importance of obtaining more data on the role of donor-specific B-cell responses, B-cell populations, and plasmablast and plasma cells (PCs) that secrete DSA in solid organ transplant rejection.

B-CELL DIFFERENTIATION INTO MEMORY B CELLS AND ANTIBODY-SECRETING CELLS

Naïve B cells become activated on recognition of antigens via their B-cell receptor (BCR), which is the membrane-bound form of a secreted antibody. Some activated naïve B cells differentiate as part of an extrafollicular (EF) response, with limited T-cell help, into mainly low-affinity IgM and some class-switched immunoglobulin G (IgG) short-lived plasmablasts, resulting in the production of short-lived antibodies. Other activated naïve B cells enter into germinal center (GC) reactions in secondary lymphoid organs. This occurs after the antigen is bound to the BCR of the B cells and the B cell receives costimulatory signals and cytokines of specific T helper (Tfh) cells that have previously been activated by dendritic cells that have taken up the antigen (Figure 1). In the transition to the GCs, B cells may undergo class switch recombination and subsequently form a GC dark zone, where they proliferate and undergo somatic hypermutation. Subsequently, B cells move away from the dark zone into the light zone of the GC where they get exposed to antigen again and obtain help from cognate specialized CD4 Tfh cells, the follicular Tfh cells. B cells that have acquired higher affinity BCRs through the process of somatic hypermutation will more easily obtain antigen from the follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) and thereby obtain more help from Tfh for subsequent reentry to the dark zone. B cells with a lowered affinity for antigen will not receive Tfh help and die. Several rounds of affinity maturation will occur before B cells leave the GC and are released into circulation, giving rise to memory B cells (MBCs) and antibody-secreting cells (plasmablasts or PCs). Some PCs enter the bone marrow and reside there as long-lived PCs, which can produce antibodies for decades. MBC can on re-exposure of their antigen quickly differentiate mainly into plasmablasts and lead to a new wave of mostly short-lived antibodies.14

B-cell differentiation on antigen recognition occurs via 2 main pathways: the extrafollicular pathway, where CD11c+ B cells (DN2 and activated naïve B cells) and CD11c− B cells (DN3) differentiate into short-lived plasmablasts that secrete low-affinity IgM or IgG antibodies with limited SHM mutations, and the germinal center pathway, involving repeated cycling between the dark zone (proliferation and SHM) and the light zone (antigen re-encounter and selection with Tfh help), resulting in high-affinity switched B cells. ActB are cells recently activated via the germinal center pathway, displaying traits intermediate between MBCs and PCs. The main conventional human B-cell subsets in the blood, including naïve B cells, MBC, plasmablasts, and PCs are well defined. By specifically selecting antigen-specific B cells using fluorescently labeled antigens, progress has been made in distinguishing and delineating the developing B-cell response in greater detail.15,16 Our group and others demonstrated that “atypical” CD11c+ B cells, previously found enriched in autoimmunity and chronic exposure, are early precursors of a normal B-cell response. Of these, so-called double-negative 2 B cells (DN2: CD11c+CD27−CD21−IgD−) and age-associated B cells (CD11c+CD27+CD21−) expand shortly after antigen exposure and tend to differentiate into short-lived plasmablast and PCs.17-19 In addition, activated B cells (ActB) have been identified with CD27+CD71+CD95+CD21− phenotype that arises after recent antigen stimulation and have properties indicative of recent GC activity.15,17,19 This ActB compartment is heterogenous and also contains the CD11c+ B cells, which were formerly named age-associated B cells, and precedes the classical MBC and antibody-secreting cell compartments17-19 (Figure 1).

These recent insights into the B-cell stages that precede MBC and antibody-secreting cell formation, which might also distinguish EF and GC responses, are pertinent to our understanding of dnDSA development, especially why some DSA are transient, with no or little differentiation toward long-lived PCs, and others persistent, more GC output towards long-lived PCs. In addition, recognizing these B-cell subsets may help us understand the kinetics of DSA formation, particularly whether DSA can derive from the reactivation of DSA-specific MBCs that rapidly differentiate to short-lived PCs or whether preexisting DSAs are mainly derived from long-lived PCs that rest in the bone marrow.

DSA INDUCTION IN RELATION TO B-CELL SUBSETS

Transplant recipients who have been sensitized pose a particular challenge. These sensitized individuals possess memory T and B cells that can mount a rapid immune response on re-exposure to the same antigen. Alloreactive MBCs differentiate into antibody-secreting cells after antigen exposure, contributing to the quick appearance of DSAs, which can ultimately lead to AMR and graft failure. Indeed, it was shown that existing pretransplant HLA-specific MBC in kidney transplant patients correlated with posttransplant DSA persistence in circulation and development of AMR within the first year.20,21 Several studies investigated if the presence of specific B-cell subsets is predictive of the development of DSAs and/or AMR. It was found that switched (IgD−CD27+) MBC population pretransplant was higher in the group developing AMR.22 Furthermore, more circulating B cells with memory and antibody-secreting phenotype were detectable at the moment of DSA detection in pediatric kidney transplant recipients who later developed AMR in comparison with DSA+ patients not developing AMR.23

Using high dimensional profiling of B cells in a cohort of 96 kidney transplant recipients, of which 20 developed AMR, Louis et al24 revealed that patients who developed DSAs and progressed to AMR had expanded numbers of ActB in their circulation. These ActB expressed CD11c and T-bet and could be separated into 2 populations based on CD27 expression, a population that can be defined as CD11c+ ActB (CD11c+CD27+CD21−) and CD11c+CD27−CD21− B cells, comprising EF-formed B cells named activated naïve (IgD+) and DN2 (IgD−). RNA-Sequencing analysis of ActB in patients developing AMR revealed these cells to be poised for PC differentiation.24 Furthermore, the T-bet+/CD11c+ ActB correlated with levels of circulating Tfh (cTfh), plasmablasts, and DSAs, suggesting that CD11c+ ActB are critical in the immune response leading to graft rejection.24

A growing number of studies demonstrated that cTfh are present in higher numbers in the blood of patients developing DSAs compared with patients without DSAs (reviewed in Louis et al25). This suggests that Tfh–B-cell interactions also contribute to DSA generation. Recently, it was demonstrated that more proliferating cTfh (Ki67+ICOS+CXCR5+CD4+) coincide with bigger expansion of Ki67+ ActB (CD20+CD38lo) and plasmablasts (CD20+CD38hi) and levels of DSA generated in DSA+ AMR+ patients compared with patients having DSA without developing AMR, suggesting a stronger GC reaction in DSA+ AMR+ patients compared with DSA+ AMR- patients.25

Together, these data show that monitoring of the donor-specific Tfh cells and donor-specific B cells, which may differentiate into plasmablasts and PCs via the GC or EF route, before and after transplantation may give much-needed insights into the timing and mechanisms of formation of DSAs, which represent a significant risk factor for graft failure.26 Indeed, because antibody production by B cells requires T-cell help, an increase in serum DSA levels likely reflects the presence of a more mature T-cell alloimmune response. This association is further supported by evidence suggesting that, in kidney transplant recipients, T-cell-mediated rejection often precedes DSA-associated tissue injury.27

ECP INDUCES IMMUNE SUPPRESSION AND PREVENTS ALLOGRAFT REJECTION

Presently there are few strategies aiming to hamper B-cell activation and consequently prevent DSA formation by specifically manipulating Tfh–B-cell interactions.4,28

Furthermore, extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP) has recently reemerged as an interesting therapeutic possibility. ECP is a long-established and safe procedure used to treat acute and chronic graft-versus-host disease (GvHD), systemic sclerosis, and other primarily T-cell-mediated pathologies.29 ECP is now guideline therapy for chronic rejection in heart30 and lung transplant recipients and appears to be particularly useful in preventing or retarding the development of chronic lung allograft dysfunction.31

In essence, ECP is a form of apoptotic cell (AC) therapy in which autologous leukocytes are exposed to the photoactive compound 8-methoxypsoralen and ultraviolet A light before reinfusion into the patient.32 This procedure leads to double-stranded DNA breaks that inhibit transcription and cell replication, leading to cell inactivation and apoptosis.32 Apoptosis, a natural process of programmed cell death, plays a critical role in maintaining tissue health by removing nonfunctional cells in a noninflammatory manner. During apoptosis, cells release apoptotic bodies containing a range of biological molecules that can affect neighboring cells. The effectiveness of ECP depends on the ability of ACs to promote an anti-inflammatory response.33 This has been suggested to be primarily mediated by induction of tolerogenic antigen-presenting cells on uptake of ACs and migration to the lymph nodes where the T- and B-cell responses are mounted. This induces a shift in the immune response, increasing cytokines like interleukin (IL)-4, IL-10, and transforming growth factor-β, while decreasing IL-12, IL-1, interferon-α, and tumor necrosis factor-α. In consequence, tolerogenic DC drives the expansion of antigen-specific regulatory T cells (Treg) and IL-10-producing type 1 Treg cells, which play a pivotal role in downregulating immune reactions.34

EFFECTS OF ECP TREATMENT ON DSA AND B CELLS IN PATIENTS

The quality and volume of clinical or immunological evidence for ECP suppressing DSA in transplant recipients is limited.2,3 Baskaran et al35 first reported the effect of ECP on lung transplant patients diagnosed with bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome, a reflection of chronic allograft rejection and impaired lung function. In this study, antibody levels specific to lung-associated antigens and DSAs were measured after transplantation and before completing a 6-mo ECP regime. Only the patients who developed dnDSAs (27/88) were included in the final analysis investigating the effectiveness of ECP in antibody clearance. DSA levels did not increase in most patients (19/27; 70%), and total clearance was registered in 10% of them. Furthermore, a reduction in antibodies produced against self-antigens was observed after treatment. However, it is worth noting that all patients were also treated with IVIG, and some also with either Rituxan or antithymocyte globulin, which can facilitate the decrease of DSA levels, thus the sole effect of ECP could not be determined in this study.35

In a lung transplant study, ECP in combination with first-line treatment showed DSA clearance in 14 out of 16 patients included. However, during the follow-up, 8 patients developed either bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome or restrictive allograft syndrome, and 3 needed retransplantation. Importantly, the authors do not exclude the possibility of physiological DSA clearance, and underline the importance of adding a control group in future studies.36

In kidney transplant patients, similar trends were reported regarding the decrease in DSA levels after ECP cycles. DSAs were not detectable in 75% of ECP responder patients, defined as those whose estimated glomerular filtration rate decreased <20% from baseline value 12 mo after starting ECP treatment.37 In nonresponder patients, DSA levels did not change during ECP treatment but increased after ECP interruption.37

It is not yet clear how ECP exactly affects donor-specific B cells and antibody-secreting cell responses. In the kidney transplant study where decreased DSA levels were demonstrated after ECP,37 a decrease in circulating B cells was observed among other immune cell populations, namely T and Natural Killer cells in ECP responder patients.37 This trend was also noted at the transcriptomic level in a different cohort of patients with chronic antibody-mediated kidney rejection, mRNA expression of B-cell markers, including CD19 and PAX5, decreased after ECP.38

There are several ways by which ECP may affect donor-specific B-cell responses. Alteration of DC function by ECP products and subsequent alteration of Tfh activation or functionality might affect donor-specific B-cell responses and DSA generation. Indeed evidence suggests that ECP effects on B cells are mediated in the lymph nodes via DC and Tfh through the impairment of Tfh–B-cell interaction.34 It remains to be elucidated if ACs and soluble molecules generated by ECP exert direct effects on B cells. The latter may be effects that limit donor-specific B-cell responses or may involve the formation of regulatory B cells (Bregs).

Various B-cell subsets with regulatory capacities have been identified by assessing their ability to secrete IL-10, therefore different phenotypic signatures for Bregs are proposed. The distinct phenotypes reported in the context of organ transplantation include those that resemble transitional (CD24hiCD38hi), and MBCs (CD24hiCD27+; CD24hiCD27+CD39hi; CD73−CD25+CD71+). Consequently, which human B-cell subsets with regulatory capacity are most relevant for immune regulation is still unknown. Evidence shows that increased frequencies of IL-10 and IL-10-producing B cells are associated with better outcomes for organ transplant patients (reviewed in Elias et al39). The concept of Bregs is interesting in the context of DSA formation or maintenance. Classically, Bregs are considered to be tolerance-inducing via the tolerance-inducing effect of IL-10 on other cell types, like DCs, macrophages, and T cells. As such, Bregs are considered a natural counterpart of IL-10-secreting Treg. In B-cell responses however, IL-10 is a cytokine that drives B-cell responses and the formation of antibody-secreting cells.40 The question therefore remains whether Bregs indeed dampen dnDSA formation or reactivate preexisting donor-specific MBCs. Of specific interest here it the effect on the subclass of DSAs expressed, as both proinflammatory IgGs and anti-inflammatory IgG4 may be modulated by IL-10.41

Interestingly, together with the induction of Bregs ECP was also found to lead to a reduced CD19hiCD20hiCD38lo B-cell population which was found elevated in chronic GvHD.42 Although phenotyping of this population was limited, it warrants future phenotyping studies to demonstrate if these cells are ActB.

Limited data are available on the direct effect of ACs on B cells. Apoptosis induction leads to alterations in the plasma membrane, such as enrichment of phosphorylcholine, especially the innate like B-1a cells are highly enriched for binding to phosphorylcholine.43 B-1a cells have also been implicated as a precursor of the IL-10-producing Bregs.44 In mouse models, ACs have been shown to induce B cells with an IL-10-secreting regulatory phenotype, which protects against autoimmune inflammation45 and allograft rejection.44 In a mouse skin allograft model, self-reactive natural CD43+ B-1a cells isolated from the spleen responded to administration of ACs in vitro resulting in IL-10 production by these cells.44

In mice, a more detailed analysis of how ECP improves graft survival has been linked to the induction of Bregs.45 C57BL/6 mice were grafted with skin from BALB/c mice and infused with ECP-treated spleen cells from both strains, 7 d before skin transplantation, on the day of surgery, and 7 d posttransplantation. In mice that received ECP-treated cells, an upregulation of B cells producing IL-10 was observed, indicative of an increase in a regulatory-like B-cell phenotype compared with control groups.45 In humans, the effect of ECP on Bregs has also been explored in the context of GvHD. In patients with acute GvHD, the frequencies of CD24+CD38hi Bregs were significantly increased after ECP treatment.46

Other efforts have been made to identify markers on B cells and perform deeper B-cell profiling to predict whether GvHD patients benefit from ECP therapy.47,48 Patients with either moderate or severe chronic GvHD with higher proportions of immature CD19+CD21− B cells before starting ECP treatment did not show therapeutic response.47 Additionally, it was found that the proportions of memory CD19+CD27+ B cells before ECP treatment were not significantly different between responders and nonresponders.47 Moreover, the potential use of B-cell activator factor (BAFF) as a biomarker to predict ECP outcomes has been assessed in 46 chronic GvHD patients.48 Serum BAFF levels 1 mo after the start of treatment correlated with how patients would respond to the therapy at 3 and 6 mo, with elevated levels being associated with poorer outcomes, whereas patients with lower BAFF concentrations showed better responses.48

Overall, current studies lack detailed analysis of how ECP affects different B-cell populations and different B-cell differentiation routes, making it challenging to determine whether ECP has a preventing role in DSA formation especially as patients typically already present with DSA before undergoing treatment.35-38 Furthermore, patients are often treated simultaneously with immunosuppressant drugs, and a control group of patients is often lacking. Therefore, it is challenging to distinguish the direct effect of ECP on DSA levels. A natural reduction or even clearance of DSAs cannot be excluded without robust control groups. However, promising results have been found in both mice and humans regarding the therapeutic effect of ECP after lung and kidney transplantation and the effect on DSA levels. Future more controlled studies, applying more in-depth monitoring of donor-specific Tfh and B-cell subsets in combination with monitoring of DSA and clinical response are needed to obtain more insights if and how ECP treatment affects B cells and can prevent DSA induction and production. Likely this will also give more insights if ECP should be used to prevent dnDSA induction on transplantation or also treat patients who have already established (long-lived) DSA-producing cells.
