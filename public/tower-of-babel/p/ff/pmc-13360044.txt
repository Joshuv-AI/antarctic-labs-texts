Perioperative mortality in pediatric non-cardiac surgery in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sitotaw Tesfa Zegeye, Eshetu Tesfaye Dejen, Meseret Hulualem Nega, Belete Muluadam Admassie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative mortality in children continues to be a major concern, with rates significantly exceeding those observed in adults. This issue remains despite improvements in anesthesia, surgical, and perioperative management. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to generate evidence regarding perioperative pediatric mortality in Ethiopia.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in strict adherence to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A thorough literature search was undertaken to identify published and unpublished English-language studies. Electronic databases, including PubMed, African Journals Online, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL, were systematically searched to capture relevant studies for inclusion. The methodological quality of the selected studies was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools. Data analysis was performed using Stata version 17, whereby the pooled incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality was calculated, and potential predictors were examined.ResultsA total of 23 studies, comprising 7,059 participants, were included in this analysis. The pooled incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality was found to be 15.81% (95%CI: 11.82–19.79). Factors significantly associated with elevated perioperative pediatric mortality included neonatal surgery (pOR = 8.65; 95%CI: 4.06–18.45), pediatric trauma surgery (pOR = 8.07; 95%CI: 4.66–13.98), delayed presentation and surgical intervention following the onset of disease (pOR = 4.41; 95%CI: 2.02–9.62), prolonged hospital stays (pOR = 3.46; 95%CI: 2.10–5.68), and emergency surgery (pOR = 2.35; 95%CI: 1.80–3.08).ConclusionThe meta-analysis indicates that perioperative pediatric mortality in Ethiopia is elevated. Significant factors include pediatric trauma surgery, emergency surgery, delayed presentation and intervention following disease onset, prolonged hospital stay, and neonatal surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13741-026-00689-y.Keywords: Perioperative, Pediatric, Mortality, Ethiopia, Outcome

Introduction

Although there has been substantial progress in reducing mortality rates due to infectious diseases, there remains a relatively unaddressed but growing proportion of preventable mortality and morbidity in children due to surgical conditions, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Traditionally, child health programs in the world have focused on communicable and infectious diseases. As a result, people lacking access to safe, timely, and economical surgical and anesthetic care have been largely overlooked (Wright et al. 2018; Abbas and Samad 2020).

According to the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery, 5 billion people worldwide do not have access to safe, affordable, and timely surgical and anesthesia care when they need it, and 1.7 billion children do not have access to such care, contributing significantly to preventable mortality and morbidity (Meara et al. 2015). In most of the low-income regions, fewer than 3–8 percent of pediatric patients might receive the required surgical treatment (Abbas and Samad 2020).

Pediatric patients carry an especially increased burden of unmet surgical needs, in part because they make up a large and rapidly growing segment of the populations in LMICs. In some regions of sub-Saharan Africa, around 50% of the populations are under the age of 18 (Wright et al. 2021). This demographic fact implies that disparities in surgical care among pediatric patients have far-reaching implications on not only the health and survival rates of children, but also on the achievements of universal health coverage and the national health agenda (Wright et al. 2021; Epiu et al. 2018).

Surgical diseases are significant contributors to the world burden of disease and in cases where safe surgery is unavailable, children die and suffer permanent disability (Meara et al. 2015). For children in in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), lack of access is compounded by fragile health infrastructure, inadequate numbers of trained surgical and anesthesia providers, and limited perioperative capacity issues that clearly illustrate the inequity in global health systems (Epiu et al. 2018; Powell et al. 2023).

Even with the availability of surgical care, surgical outcomes still remain unacceptably low. A meta-analysis estimated that the peri-operative mortality rate among children in Africa was 17.9 per 1, 000, more than eight times that reported in Europe and North America (Taylor et al. 2024). These findings show dramatic gap in surgical safety and quality across global settings. The same patterns have been observed in the perioperative mortality rates of LMIC tertiary centers, such as in Ethiopia, where the evidence indicates that pediatric perioperative mortality is greater than higher income countries (Molla et al. 2024). Perioperative mortality rate is a tool to assess adequacy of surgical care and safety. It also aids in judicious allocation of scarce resources in terms of manpower and equipment (Talabi et al. 2021). Accordingly, the objective of this study is to generate a pooled incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality in Ethiopia, thereby establishing a national baseline and delineating key risk factors.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was conducted according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (Page et al. 2020). The items in the PRISMA checklist were addressed (Supplementary file 1).

The study selection process was documented across four distinct phases, as outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram (Moher et al. 2010). An initial literature search was performed to identify existing or planned similar studies and avoid duplication of effort. Furthermore, the complete methods section has been registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) under registration number CRD420251235793.

Search strategy and information sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect) and Google Scholar. The search strategy combined relevant keywords (e.g., mortality, pediatric, children, death, non-cardiac surgery) and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms (e.g., perioperative mortality, perioperative death, pediatric mortality) using Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT). Additionally, a manual search using targeted keywords and citation tracking (forward and backward) was performed in Google Scholar and PubMed to identify further relevant studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This review included both published and unpublished full-text articles conducted across all settings in Ethiopia. Eligible studies comprised observational designs (cross-sectional and cohort). Only English-language articles were considered, without any restriction on publication date. The final literature search was completed on December 30, 2025. Exclusion criteria included anonymous reports, editorials, conference abstracts, articles lacking full-text access despite efforts to contact authors, as well as those lacking both an abstract and a complete manuscript. The CoCoPop (Condition, Context, and Population) framework was employed to establish the eligibility criteria (Table 1).

Outcome of review

The primary endpoint of the study was to determine the pooled incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality. The secondary objective was to identify its associated factors.

Study screening and selection

Two independent reviewers (ST and BM) screened the titles and abstracts against the predefined inclusion criteria. Articles with uncertain eligibility at this stage underwent full-text review. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by consultation with additional reviewers (ET and MH). The entire selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram, outlining the number of records identified, included, and excluded at each stage, along with justifications for exclusion. The final set of eligible studies was then advanced for data extraction and synthesis (Supplementary file 2).

Data extraction

Upon the final selection of studies meeting the inclusion criteria, a rigorous data extraction phase was initiated. Extraction was performed using a standardized Excel template to systematically collect study characteristics (author, year, country, sample size, mortality rate (%), and predictors odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI)).

Quality assessment of studies

Studies satisfying the inclusion criteria underwent methodological quality appraisal by two independent reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool; any disagreements between reviewers were settled by consensus through discussion. This tool comprises nine items (Q1–Q9) designed to evaluate the methodological soundness of studies reporting prevalence data (Barker et al. 2023; Porritt et al. 2014). Studies that received affirmative responses ("yes") on more than 50% of the checklist items were considered to have a low risk of bias and were eligible for inclusion in the meta-analysis. Particular emphasis was placed on assessing study design, sampling methods, and data collection procedures, clarity of objectives, statistical analysis, and potential sources of bias (Supplementary file 3).

Statistical analysis

To ensure uniformity across the evidence base, all reported magnitudes were converted to a common denominator of 100, with results presented as pooled magnitude estimates. A random-effects meta-analysis model was employed a priori to account for anticipated heterogeneity across the included studies. Inter-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Higgins' inconsistency index (I2) statistic and classified according to established thresholds: negligible (0–40%), moderate (30–60%), substantial (50–90%), and considerable (75–100%) (López-Bueno et al. 2024). To explore sources of heterogeneity, we conducted univariate and multivariate random-effects meta-regression using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Covariates included publication year (centered at the mean), study region (Addis Ababa vs. other regions), and log-transformed sample size. Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed according to patient age and sample size.

To estimate the pooled effect of each factor on perioperative pediatric mortality, adjusted odds ratios (AORs) extracted from individual studies were log-transformed to normalize their distribution. These log-transformed effect estimates were subsequently pooled using a random-effects model with the DerSimonian and Laird method, implemented in Stata/MP 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Within this framework, study weights were calculated as the inverse of the sum of within-study variance and the estimated between-study variance component (τ2), thereby accounting for both sampling error and heterogeneity across studies. This approach was selected a priori to accommodate anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies, yielding more conservative estimates in the presence of between-study variability. The pooled log-odds ratios were then exponentiated to obtain final pooled odds ratios, presented with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Assessment of heterogeneity and publication bias

Heterogeneity was assessed using Galbraith radial plots, the I2 statistic, and Cochran’s Q test (significance threshold ≤ 0.1). Values of I2 exceeding 40% indicated substantial heterogeneity (Choi and Lam 2017). Sources of heterogeneity were investigated through subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis. Publication bias was assessed by visual examination of funnel plots and formal testing using Egger’s regression test.

Sensitivity and meta-regression analysis

To evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall pooled estimate, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed, wherein the meta-analysis was iteratively recalculated after sequentially omitting each study. Additionally, to explore potential sources of between-study heterogeneity, a multivariable random-effects meta-regression was conducted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation. Covariates included publication year (centered at the mean, 2019) and study region (categorized as Addis Ababa versus other regions).

Results

Study selection

A comprehensive search of major health and medical databases, along with additional relevant sources, identified 785 records. After removing 488 duplicates using EndNote, 297 records remained for screening. Following title and abstract review, 211 records were excluded, resulting in 86 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Of these, 63 studies were excluded, including those not reporting the outcome of interest (n = 7), focusing on pediatric in-hospital mortality (n = 19). lacking full-text accessibility (n = 12), or being conducted outside Ethiopia (n = 25). Ultimately, 23 studies were included in the final review (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

Twenty-three studies (Molla et al. 2024; Tamirat et al. 2025; Tarekegn et al. 2021; Mengistu et al. 2025; Shitaye and Dejene 2016; Firomsa et al. 2018; Moga et al. 2022; Menberu et al. 2025; Addisu and Wassie 2024; Negussie et al. 2018; Temesgen and Moges 2025; Ketema et al. 2017; Moges et al. 2023; Wubie et al. 2023; Temesgen et al. 2021; Zewde et al. 2024; Biluts and Admasu 2015; Mersha 2017; Gadisa et al. 2016; Tadesse and Gadisa 2014; Wassie et al. 2025; Tagesse et al. 2024; Tesfaye et al. 2022). The reported perioperative mortality rates across included studies ranged widely, from 1.3% (Ketema et al. 2017) to 75% (Moges et al. 2023). The studies employed various designs: 19 were cross-sectional, 3 were cohort studies, and 1 had a mixed design. Geographically, most studies (n = 14) were conducted in Addis Ababa, followed by the Amhara region (n = 5), Southern Ethiopia (n = 2), Harar (n = 1), and Oromia (n = 1). The sample size of included studies ranged from 53 to 1171 participants (Table 2).

Meta-analyses

The pooled incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality

The pooled incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality was 15.81% (95%CI: 11.82–19.79). Statistical assessment of heterogeneity using the Cochrane Q test and the I2 statistic revealed substantial heterogeneity among the included studies, with an I2 value of 98.3% and a p-value less than 0.001. This high level of variability indicates significant differences in the study outcomes, which may be attributed to factors such as variations in study design and sample size (Fig. 2). To evaluate whether the pooled estimate was disproportionately biased by any single constituent study, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was undertaken.

The pooled incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality in Ethiopia

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess whether any single study disproportionately influenced the pooled incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality. As shown in Fig. 3, the pooled estimate after sequentially omitting each study ranged from 15.31% (95% CI: 11.42–19.21%) to 16.62% (95% CI: 12.19–21.06%). These values closely approximate the original pooled estimate of 15.3% (95% CI: 11.3–19.3%). Therefore, no individual study had an undue influence, confirming the robustness of the overall pooled estimate (Fig. 3). This stability supports the robustness of the pooled estimate despite the presence of substantial statistical heterogeneity. Given the substantial heterogeneity observed, we conducted subgroup analyses to explore potential sources of variation.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis

Sub-group analysis and meta-regression

Subgroup analysis by patient age demonstrated a significantly elevated mortality rate in neonatal surgical procedures relative to non-neonatal pediatric surgical procedures. The mortality rate among neonates was 31.6% (95%CI: 13.8–49.4%), compared to 9.97% (95%CI: 7.02–12.92%) in older pediatric surgical patients (Fig. 4). In contrast, a subgroup analysis stratified by study sample size (< 200 vs. ≥ 200 participants) revealed no significant difference in mortality rates, with rates of 15.8% (95%CI: 9.22–22.42) and 15.97% (95%CI: 10.54–21.40) observed, respectively. The test for subgroup differences yielded a Q-statistic of 0.001 (p = 0.97), indicating no statistically significant heterogeneity (Fig. 5). In addition, multivariable random-effects meta-regression was conducted to explore sources of heterogeneity, with publication year (centered at 2019) and study region (Addis Ababa vs. other regions) as covariates. The model was statistically significant overall (Wald χ2 = 11.32, df = 2, p = 0.0035), explaining 30.9% of the between-study variance (R2 = 30.87%). Publication year was significantly associated with mortality estimates (β = 0.206, 95% CI: 0.063–0.349, p = 0.005; OR = 1.23 per year, 95% CI: 1.07–1.42), indicating increasing mortality odds in more recent studies. Studies conducted in Addis Ababa demonstrated significantly higher mortality odds compared to those from other regions (β = 1.500, 95% CI: 0.443–2.558, p = 0.005; OR = 4.48, 95% CI: 1.56–12.90). Despite these significant predictors, substantial residual heterogeneity remained (τ2 = 1.333, I2 = 99.92%), suggesting that unmeasured factors continue to contribute to between-study variability (Supplementary file 1: PRISMA 2020 statement (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis) checklist for Perioperative pediatric mortality and associated factors in Ethiopia. Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed asymmetry, with a discernible clustering of studies to the left of the pooled estimate (Fig. 6), raising the possibility of small-study effects or publication bias. To quantitatively evaluate this asymmetry, Egger's regression test was performed, which yielded a statistically significant result (intercept = −3.5047, 95% CI: −4.2920 to −2.7175, p < 0.001), indicating evidence of funnel plot asymmetry consistent with publication bias or other small-study effects. The precision of the effect estimates accounted for approximately one-third of the variance in effect sizes (R2 = 32.9%) (Supplementary file 5). In sensitivity analyses using trim-and-fill methodology, no studies were imputed, and the adjusted pooled effect estimate remained unchanged from the original (Supplementary file 6). In light of the substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 98.3%), the observed funnel plot asymmetry is more plausibly attributable to true clinical and methodological diversity across studies rather than to publication bias.

Factors associated with perioperative pediatric mortality in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis

This meta-analysis revealed several factors demonstrating significant associations with perioperative pediatric mortality in Ethiopia, including prolonged length of hospital stay. Prolonged hospital stays, defined as exceeding 7 days, were significantly associated with increased perioperative pediatric mortality across five studies included in this review. In the pooled analysis, prolonged hospital stays were associated with a significantly increased odds of perioperative pediatric mortality (pOR = 3.46, 95% CI: 2.10–5.68). Heterogeneity assessment demonstrated no substantial variation across the included studies, as evidenced by an I2 value of 0% (p = 0.604), reflecting homogeneity in the pooled estimates (Fig. 6: Funnel plot for the assessment of publication bias Figure. 7).

The pooled effect of Prolonged hospital stays on perioperative pediatric mortality

Additionally, Delayed presentation and surgical intervention after disease onset emerged as factors significantly associated with elevated perioperative mortality. Pooled analysis of two studies revealed that late surgical presentation was associated with a 4.41-fold increase in the odds of perioperative mortality (pOR = 4.41; 95% CI: 2.02–9.62), with negligible heterogeneity observed (I2 = 16.9%, p = 0.273) (Fig. 8).

Three studies explored the association between neonatal surgery and mortality. Neonates undergoing non-cardiac surgery demonstrated a substantially elevated risk of perioperative mortality relative to older pediatric patients, with a pooled odds ratio of 8.65 (95% CI: 4.06–18.45). This finding was characterized by negligible heterogeneity across the included studies (I2 = 0%, p = 0.471), reinforcing the robustness and consistency of the association between neonatal age and adverse perioperative outcomes (Fig. 9).

The pooled effect of neonatal surgery on perioperative pediatric mortality

Similarly, pediatric trauma surgeries as assessed by three studies were associated with a substantially increased risk of perioperative mortality compared with non-trauma cases, with a pooled odds ratio of 8.07 (95% CI: 4.66–13.98). No substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I2 = 21.8%, p = 0.278), supporting the consistency of this finding (Fig. 10).

The pooled effect of pediatric trauma surgery on perioperative pediatric mortality

Emergency surgical procedures were associated with a significantly increased risk of perioperative mortality compared with elective surgeries. Pooled analysis yielded an odds ratio of 2.35 (95% CI: 1.80–3.08), indicating a 2.35-fold increase in the odds of mortality for patients undergoing emergency procedures. Notably, the analysis revealed no significant heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 0%, p = 0.491), reflecting a consistent association across the included evidence (Fig. 11).

The pooled effect of emergency surgery on perioperative pediatric mortality

Although blood transfusion demonstrated a significant association with perioperative pediatric mortality in two individual studies, the pooled analysis revealed no statistically significant overall effect. This finding suggests that the observed associations in individual studies may be influenced by study-specific factors, such as variations in transfusion thresholds, case mix, or institutional practices, rather than representing a consistent independent risk factor across diverse clinical settings (Fig. 12).

Discussion

The review of perioperative mortality attributable to surgical or anesthetic causes in pediatric populations can serve as a key mechanism for driving quality improvement (Talabi et al. 2018). We therefore conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the incidence of perioperative mortality in pediatric patients after non-cardiac surgery and to identify potential predictors. The perioperative pediatric mortality rate in developed countries is low, occurring at an approximate rate of 1 per 10,000 cases (Cronje 2015).

Our analysis reveals a notably elevated pooled mortality rate, underscoring persistent challenges in pediatric surgical care, especially within resource-constrained environments. Nevertheless, the interpretation of these findings warrants caution given the pronounced heterogeneity and evidence of publication bias. The substantial between-study heterogeneity likely reflects variability in study populations, case mix, and surgical interventions across the included studies. Although a random-effects model was employed a priori to accommodate such variability, residual heterogeneity persists and merits careful consideration when interpreting the pooled estimate.

Despite the considerable statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 98.3%), both the sensitivity and subgroup analyses support the robustness of the pooled estimate. The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated that no single study unduly influenced the overall pooled incidence, with estimates after sequential omission of each study ranging narrowly from 15.31% to 16.62%—values closely approximating the original pooled estimate of 15.81%. This stability indicates that the high I2 value reflects variability in the magnitude of effect sizes across studies rather than the influence of any single outlier study. Subgroup analyses further clarified the sources of heterogeneity. Stratification by patient age revealed a marked difference in mortality rates between neonates (31.6%) and older pediatric patients (9.97%), suggesting that age composition across studies is a major contributor to the observed heterogeneity. Conversely, stratification by sample size (< 200 vs. ≥ 200 participants) yielded nearly identical pooled estimates (15.8% vs. 15.97%), with a non-significant test for subgroup differences (p = 0.97), indicating that sample size did not explain the between-study variance.

The meta-regression revealed higher mortality odds in studies conducted in Addis Ababa and in more recent publications. This may be explained by the fact that Addis Ababa serves as a national referral center, managing complex surgical cases referred from regional states. Similarly, more recent studies may reflect an increasing proportion of high-risk and complex surgical procedures, contributing to the observed rise in mortality estimates over time.

The pooled incidence of perioperative mortality among pediatric patients was 15.81%, which is notably higher than the rates reported in previous studies. Torborg A et al. reported a mortality rate of 1.1% in South Africa (Torborg et al. 2019), the African Surgical Outcomes Study (ASOS-Paeds) reported a 2.3% mortality rate in their multicenter investigation (Torborg et al. 2024), Nwankwo EP et al. 3.61% in Nigeria (Nwankwo et al. 2025) and Newton et al. found a 3% rate in Kenya (Newton et al. 2020). In Brazil perioperative mortality was low as compared to ours (0.1%) (Gonzalez et al. 2014),in Iran pediatric mortality was found to be 0.358% (Gharavifard et al. 2022).This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that those findings arise from individual studies, whereas our analysis synthesizes data across multiple studies through a meta-analytic approach, potentially capturing a broader and more varied patient population and clinical settings.

Key factors associated with mortality included neonatal age at surgery, intraoperative blood transfusion, trauma indication, emergency surgery, prolonged hospitalization (> 7 days), and delayed presentation to surgery following disease onset. The meta-analysis demonstrated that neonatal age (less than 30 days) is strongly associated with an increased risk of perioperative mortality compared to older pediatric patients undergoing surgery. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Australia, Nigeria, Sub-Saharan Africa, and the Netherlands (Talabi et al. 2021; Griend et al. 2011; Wright et al. 2021; Bruin et al. 2015). The elevated risk may be attributed to the higher likelihood of prematurity among neonates, which contributes to increased perioperative morbidity and mortality (Skertich et al. 2020). Furthermore, many neonatal surgical procedures are complex and often involve congenital anomalies, particularly congenital cardiac defects, which are independently linked to poor surgical outcomes (Collaborative 2016; Livingston et al. 2015; Cheung et al. 2019). The issue is intensified in resource-limited settings due to the insufficient availability of pediatric surgeons, as well as the lack of neonatal intensive care units equipped with advanced technology, comprehensive parenteral nutritional support and extra corporeal membrane oxygenation (Tayade et al. 2022).

Emergency surgery was found to be associated with higher risk of perioperative mortality. The perioperative mortality rate was 2.35 times higher than elective pediatric surgeries. This result was consistent with previous studies (Talabi et al. 2021; Gonzalez et al. 2012; Haider et al. 2015).This is attributed to the fact that emergency surgeries are associated with complications as compared with elective surgeries (Kassahun et al. 2022; Rehné Jensen et al. 2024; Mullen et al. 2017).

Prolonged length of hospital stays is associated with postoperative mortality in pediatric surgical patients. Pediatric surgical patients in low- and middle-income countries experience extended hospital stays attributable to malnutrition, surgical site infections, and postoperative stress (Wondemagegnehu et al. 2023). Admission for surgery alone is identified as one of the factors contributing to prolonged length of stay (PLOS) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (Marfil-Garza et al. 2018), which, consequently plays a significant role in increasing costs, morbidity, and mortality (Marfil-Garza et al. 2018). This may be explained by the association of prolonged hospital stays in children with elevated risks of hospital-acquired infections (HAIs), malnutrition, postoperative stress, and postoperative infections (Nesha et al. 2025; Gibbs et al. 2021). In interpreting these findings, prolonged hospital stay should be viewed as a marker of postoperative course rather than a preoperative risk factor. The observed association with mortality likely reflects greater illness severity, postoperative complications, or delayed recovery.

Delayed presentation to hospital after the onset of disease and intervention is also associated with perioperative pediatric mortality. Delayed hospital presentation and emergency surgery are significantly associated with increased major morbidity and/or mortality (Butterworth et al. 2023). Outcomes are adversely affected in several African LMICs where surgical interventions are delayed due to significant barriers in accessing surgical care for pediatric patients (Yousef et al. 2019).

Trauma represents a significant global health challenge, leading to considerable mortality and disability across all age groups. It has been projected that by 2030; trauma will become the third leading cause of death worldwide (Gomes et al. 2008; Krug et al. 2000). The management of critically injured pediatric trauma patients necessitates meticulously coordinated multidisciplinary care delivered by various pediatric surgical subspecialties (Acierno et al. 2004; Cooper et al. 2013). However, improving surgical outcomes for pediatric trauma patients remains challenging in contexts where access to multidisciplinary surgical care is delayed or lacking (Yousef et al. 2019). Perioperative mortality in pediatric trauma patients was also found to be 8 times higher than patients undergoing non-trauma surgical procedures.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed an incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality in Ethiopia of 15.81% higher than the worldwide and SSA averages. Several factors demonstrated consistent positive associations with increased mortality odds, including neonatal age, pediatric trauma surgery, delayed presentation and surgical intervention following disease onset, prolonged hospital stay, and emergency surgical procedures. Given that these pooled estimates are derived exclusively from observational study designs with inherent potential for residual confounding, the identified associations should not be interpreted as causal predictors of perioperative mortality. Rather, they represent robust empirical correlates that may inform hypothesis generation for future prospective research and guide targeted quality improvement initiatives in resource-limited healthcare settings.

Limitations and strength

To the best of our knowledge, this meta-analysis constitutes the first quantitative synthesis examining the incidence of perioperative pediatric mortality and its associated predictors in Ethiopia. The robustness of our pooled estimates is supported by heterogeneity exploration, sensitivity analysis, and comprehensive publication bias assessment. Nonetheless, the findings should be interpreted with caution considering certain limitations. Significant heterogeneity among the included studies, the lack of procedure-specific mortality rates, and the diversity of populations studied—ranging from neonate-specific to mixed surgical cases—may have constrained the generalizability of the results. Thus, these factors warrant careful consideration in the interpretation of the overall findings. Finally, we recommend that future research prioritize addressing these limitations by assessing procedure-specific mortality risks and promoting greater homogeneity among study populations.
