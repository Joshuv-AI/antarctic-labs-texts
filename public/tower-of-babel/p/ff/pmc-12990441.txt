Emergency use of a covered metal stent for life-threatening hemobilia from an arterio-biliary fistula: a case report
Authors: Rawan Daboul, Bilal Kassoumeh, Saddam Alsayd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArterio-biliary fistula (ABF) is a rare, life-threatening cause of hemobilia, most often resulting from iatrogenic injury during hepatobiliary procedures such as ERCP or laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Its delayed and nonspecific presentation poses a significant diagnostic challenge. When massive bleeding occurs, conventional endoscopic hemostasis often fails, necessitating urgent alternative interventions.Case presentationA 39-year-old woman with a history of laparoscopic cholecystectomy presented with obstructive jaundice. Initial ERCP with sphincterotomy and plastic stent placement was performed. She subsequently developed massive hematemesis and hemorrhagic shock. Emergency endoscopy revealed torrential arterial bleeding from the common bile duct, suggestive of an ABF. Balloon tamponade failed. As a lifesaving measure, a fully covered self-expandable metal stent (FC-SEMS, 8 mm x 8 cm) was immediately deployed, achieving complete hemostasis and hemodynamic stabilization. Subsequent computed tomography angiography (CTA) confirmed a right hepatic artery pseudoaneurysm communicating with the biliary tree. The patient underwent successful coil embolization two days later. The metal stent provided effective bridging hemostasis with no rebleeding. The patient recovered fully.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that the emergency placement of a covered metal stent can be a rapid, effective, and lifesaving bridging endoscopic intervention for controlling catastrophic hemobilia from an ABF, serving as a crucial temporary measure until definitive transarterial embolization can be performed.

Background

Hemobilia, defined as bleeding into the biliary tree, is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening condition [1]. Among its etiologies, arterio-biliary fistula (ABF)—an abnormal communication between the hepatic arterial system and the biliary tract—represents a rare but severe cause of hemorrhage [1, 2]. Most cases are iatrogenic, occurring after hepatobiliary surgery or endoscopic interventions such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) [3, 4]. During ERCP, arterial injury may result from guidewire manipulation, sphincterotomy, or stone extraction, leading to pseudoaneurysm formation and subsequent fistulization into the bile duct [4, 5]. Clinical presentation is often delayed and nonspecific, and the classic Quincke’s triad is present in fewer than 40% of cases, contributing to diagnostic difficulty [1, 2].

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) is considered the preferred diagnostic modality in suspected vascular hemobilia, while transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) remains the definitive first-line therapy in most cases [6, 7]. However, in situations of massive bleeding with hemodynamic instability, immediate endoscopic intervention may serve as a temporary bridging measure prior to definitive angiographic treatment.

We report a case of life-threatening hemobilia caused by an arterio-biliary fistula in which a fully covered self-expandable metal stent (FC-SEMS) was successfully deployed as an emergency bridging intervention before definitive embolization.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old woman presented with a one-week history of progressive jaundice, right upper quadrant pain, and low-grade fever. Her medical history was significant for laparoscopic cholecystectomy performed 15 months earlier. On admission (Day 0), she was hemodynamically stable (blood pressure 110/70 mmHg; heart rate 89 bpm). Physical examination revealed scleral icterus and mild right upper quadrant tenderness.

Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated obstructive jaundice (total bilirubin 5.4 mg/dL; direct bilirubin 4.1 mg/dL) and anemia (hemoglobin 8.8 g/dL). Coagulation parameters were not consistently available due to inter-facility transfer.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) revealed mild intra- and extrahepatic biliary dilatation (common hepatic duct diameter 9.9 mm) with non-visualization of the distal common bile duct, suggestive of obstruction, possibly related to intraductal debris or technical limitation. No vascular abnormality was identified.

Given suspected choledocholithiasis, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was performed on Day 0. A 1-cm pigment stone was extracted following sphincterotomy, and an 8.5-French plastic biliary stent was placed to ensure drainage.

Later that evening, the patient developed melena. Upper endoscopy demonstrated blood emerging from the major papilla. At that stage, the bleeding was initially suspected to be post-sphincterotomy or papillary in origin rather than arterial hemobilia. Local adrenaline injection was performed. Due to ongoing bleeding and need for advanced management, she was transferred to a tertiary referral center on Day 1.

On Day 1, repeat ERCP was performed. After removal of the plastic stent and evacuation of intraductal clots, torrential arterial bleeding occurred from the biliary orifice, resulting in hemorrhagic shock (blood pressure 80/30 mmHg). Immediate resuscitation was initiated, and the patient received packed red blood cells and fresh frozen plasma according to transfusion protocol. Balloon tamponade failed to control the hemorrhage. Therefore, a fully covered self-expandable metal stent (8 mm × 8 cm) was deployed across the papilla to provide mechanical tamponade. Immediate hemostasis was achieved, and hemodynamic stability was restored within two hours. After hemodynamic stabilization following covered stent placement, contrast-enhanced CT angiography demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm of the right hepatic artery with contrast extravasation into the common hepatic duct, consistent with an arterio-biliary fistula (Fig. 1). Definitive transcatheter arterial embolization was subsequently performed on Day 2, resulting in complete exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm. No technical difficulty related to the metallic stent was encountered during angiography.The covered stent was removed endoscopically on Day 3 following confirmation of vascular control, without complications. No rebleeding occurred.

Discussion and conclusions

Arterio-biliary fistula (ABF) is a rare but potentially fatal cause of hemobilia, most commonly occurring as an iatrogenic complication following hepatobiliary surgery or endoscopic interventions such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy and ERCP [1, 8]. ERCP-related vascular injury may result from guidewire manipulation or sphincterotomy, leading to pseudoaneurysm formation and subsequent fistulization into the biliary tree [4, 5]. Delayed presentation, as observed in our patient 15 months after cholecystectomy, is thought to result from gradual pseudoaneurysm development with eventual rupture [5, 9].

Diagnosis remains challenging because clinical manifestations are often nonspecific. Although Quincke’s triad is classically described, it is present in fewer than 40% of cases [1]. Current guidelines recommend early contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) in cases of severe or recurrent post-procedural bleeding, as it reliably identifies visceral artery pseudoaneurysms and active extravasation [6, 7]. In retrospect, earlier angiographic evaluation in our patient might have expedited definitive management.

Transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) is considered the definitive first-line treatment for hepatic artery pseudoaneurysms, with reported success rates exceeding 90% and lower morbidity compared with surgery [7, 9]. In this case, TAE achieved definitive vascular control; however, a period of uncontrolled hemorrhage necessitated immediate endoscopic intervention prior to angiography.

Fully covered self-expandable metal stents (FC-SEMS) provide sustained radial force and mechanical tamponade while preserving biliary drainage. Although not established as standard first-line therapy in current guidelines, several reports describe their use as a temporary bridging strategy in catastrophic hemobilia when definitive embolization is delayed or not immediately available [8, 9]. Compared with plastic stents, FC-SEMS offer stronger compression but require timely removal to minimize complications.

In our patient, the FC-SEMS provided effective temporary hemostasis without rebleeding and allowed safe subsequent embolization. This case highlights the importance of early vascular imaging, multidisciplinary coordination, and the potential role of covered metal stents as a bridging measure prior to definitive transcatheter arterial embolization.
