Relationship between cortical thickness and serotonin 1A receptor binding in bipolar disorder
Authors: Martin J Lan, Elizabeth Bartlett, Michael F Schmidt, Maria A Oquendo, Francesca Zanderigo, R Todd Ogden, J John Mann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeficits in cortical thickness have been a replicated finding in BD. Greater brain serotonin 1 A receptor (5HT1AR) binding has also been reported in BD, consistent with a disruption in serotonin signaling. Serotonin can cause a trophic effect on the cerebral cortex. 31 depressed participants with BD and 49 healthy volunteers (HVs) were included. PET imaging with [11C]WAY100635 was obtained with a metabolite-corrected arterial input function. Radiotracer binding potential (BPF) values were quantified within cortical regions. T1-weighted MRI scans were processed through Freesurfer 7.1.1, and cortical thickness values were quantified within a right caudal middle frontal region that had previously been shown to have deficits in BD. The previous results were replicated here, with lower cortical thickness in BD relative to HV’s in the region. Cortical thickness in that region was inversely associated with BPF of [11C]WAY100635 in BD (F = 4.77; df = 1, 28; p = 0.037), but no association was found in HVs (F = 0.62; df = 1, 46; p = 0.44). Whole brain exploratory analyses were performed to examine vertex-wise associations between BPF values and cortical thickness. Within BD participants, BPF of [11C]WAY100635 was inversely associated with right superior parietal cortical thickness, and positively associated in the right posterior cingulate cortex and the left entorhinal cortex. Mean cortical thickness values in the significant clusters did not differ between BD and HV groups. Within HVs, no clusters were associated between cortical thickness and BPF of [11C]WAY100635. These data are consistent with a model wherein disruptions in serotonin signaling lead to a loss of trophic effect on cortical tissue in BD. Associations between 5HT1AR binding and cortical thickness in BD are not limited to areas of cortical thickness deficits.Subject terms: Bipolar disorder, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Bipolar Disorder (BD) has a substantial public health impact. It is one of the top ten sources of disability worldwide [1]. A BD diagnosis has been associated with more than 10 fewer years of life [2–4]. It has a death rate by suicide that is about 20 times higher than the general population [5, 6], and has higher rates of co-morbid medical conditions [7]. Despite its public health importance, the pathogenesis of BD remains largely unknown, limiting our capacity to develop needed, bettertreatments.

A number of neuroimaging findings have been reported in participants with BD when compared to healthy volunteers (HVs) that may relate to its pathogenesis. Less cortical thickness, quantified using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), has been consistently reported in BD when compared to HVs [8]. We previously reported less cortical thickness in participants with BD when compared to HVs, with the largest deficit in the right caudal middle frontal cortex [9]. The cortex was also thinner in that region in BD when compared to participants with unipolar major depressive disorder (MDD). The result also remained after correcting for potential confounders, including past substance use history [9]. This study therefore used the same caudal middle frontal cortex region to further understand the cortical thickness deficits associated with BD. The caudal middle frontal region is subsumed by the area of lower cortical thickness found in the ENIGMA study, a mega-analysis of structural brain scans from multiple research sites, in BD when compared to HVs [10]. That study reported large effects in frontal regions, and no areas of increased thickness in BD. Neither the clinical nor pathophysiological significance of cortical thickness deficits in BD is known. Past studies have not found an association between cortical thickness and symptoms of BD, including measures of depression severity [11].

Molecular imaging studies have also indicated higher serotonin 1A receptor (5HT1AR) binding in BD when compared to HVs, consistent with altered brain serotonin signaling [12, 13]. In the cerebral cortex, the 5HT1AR is one of the main post-synaptic serotonin receptors. In the raphe nuclei, the 5HT1AR functions as an inhibitory autoreceptor, regulating serotonin release. A previous PET study found an inverse correlation between a marker of serotonin synthesis, and levels of 5HT1AR in the human brain, consistent with a model in which the 5HT1AR is upregulated in response to lower serotonin signaling [14]. Our group reported higher levels of 5HT1ARs in the brain in BD when compared to HVs with two different radiotracers, [11C]WAY100635 and [11C]CUMI-101 [12, 13]. The elevations in 5HT1AR binding were found across multiple regions of the brain, including ten cortical regions that are known to have substantial 5HT1AR levels. These cortical regions will be used here as an index of cortical 5HT1AR levels. Binding of [11C]WAY100635, a 5HT1AR antagonist, was not found to be correlated with depression severity or other clinical symptoms in BD participants [12]. However, binding of [11C]CUMI-101, a 5HT1AR partial agonist, was inversely associated with depression severity [13]. The current study focused on previously acquired [11C]WAY100635 data due to the limited number of [11C]CUMI-101 scans available for analysis.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated neurotrophic properties to serotonin signaling [15, 16], providing a model whereby a disruption of serotonin signaling may induce a cortical thickness deficit in BD. Because higher [11C]WAY100635 binding and lower cortical thickness were reported in BD relative to HV, we hypothesized that these two processes would have an inverse association with each other. Such an association would indicate a unified pathophysiological process between serotonin signaling disruptions and cortical thickness deficits. This is the first study to quantify the relationship between 5HT1AR levels and cortical thickness in BD. For this reason, a whole cortex exploratory analysis was also conducted to identify clusters where 5HT1AR and cortical thickness levels were associated outside of the primary region of interest.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited for studies with other scientific aims [12, 17, 18], and these data therefore represent a secondary analysis. 31 participants who were currently experiencing a major depressive episode and met criteria for either bipolar I or II disorder, as defined by DSM-IV, were retrospectively included. BD participants had 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale value greater than 15 on the day of screening [19]. BD participants were free from both antidepressant and mood stabilizer medications for at least 14 days (42 days for fluoxetine), and from oral antipsychotics for 21 days before PET scans. Short-acting benzodiazepines were permitted, only until 72 h before the PET scan. 49 HV participants were also retrospectively included, as defined by not meeting criteria for any axis I disorders as determined by DSM-IV. HVs were excluded if they had any first-degree family history of mood or psychotic disorder, including a history of suicide. Masters or PhD-level psychologists performed interviews for DSM-IV Axis I Disorders Patient Edition (SCID-I/P) to determine the diagnosis, and their assessment was confirmed by a research psychiatrist [20]. Both BD and HV participants were excluded if they had significant medical conditions, as determined by history, physical examination, and blood and urine tests, including urine toxicology. History of alcohol or substance use disorder during their lifetime was an exclusion for HVs, and a history within six months of screening was an exclusion for BD participants. Lifetime exposure to 3,4 methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA) was also an exclusion.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the research. Study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the New York State Psychiatric Institute, Health and Human Service registration number IRB00000488. All research procedures were in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

PET imaging

The [Carbonyl-C-11]WAY100635 radiotracer [N-(2-(4-(2-methoxyphenyl)-1-piperazinyl)ethyl0-N-(2-pyridinyl) cyclohexane carboxamide] was prepared, and arterial input function, metabolites, and radiotracer free plasma fraction (fp) were obtained, as previously reported [12, 21, 22]. Image acquisition parameters employed have been previously described [12, 21, 22].

PET data processing

Subject motion correction was applied as reported previously [12]. Cortical regions of interest from our in-house atlas that have been used in previous 5HT1AR analyses were used, including the parahippocampal, temporal, anterior cingulate, cingulate, dorsolateral prefrontal, medial frontal, orbitofrontal, insular parietal, and occipital cortices (PET ROIs). Cylindrical areas were drawn in the cerebellar white matter as a reference region. Total distribution volumes of [11C]WAY100635 were calculated for each region with a two-tissue constrained compartment (2TCC) model with a metabolite-corrected arterial input function as previously described to estimate the binding potential BPF [12, 21, 22] as the outcome measure. For whole brain analyses, basis pursuit voxel-wise BPF images were generated [23].

MRI imaging

63 T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired on a 1.5 T GE scanner with 124 1.5 mm thick slices, each slice at 0.8 mm×0.8 mm resolution (GE Genesis Signa, 0.005 s echo time, 0.034 s relaxation time, 45 degree flip angle). The remaining 16 MRI scans were acquired on a 3 T scanner with 1.0 mm isotropic resolution (10/16 in a GE Signa Excite with 0.00133s–0.00206 s echo time, 0.00504 s relaxation time, and 11 degree flip angle; 6/16 on a GE Signa HDx with 0.002856 s echo time, 0.00702 s relaxation time, and 9 degree flip angle). Cortical thickness measurements have been found to be reliable despite differences in MRI scanner field strength [24].

MRI processing

FreeSurfer 7.1.1 standard processing (reconall) was run on each scan to generate gray/white cortical and gray-CSF surfaces, and the data were registered to a spherical atlas [25]. Quality assurance and review was assisted with FreeSurfer 7.2.0’s QATools scripts. Cortical thickness measures in the regions of interest were aggregated for each scan.

Statistics

Region of interest analyses were conducted using a linear mixed model in which the outcome was [11C]WAY100635 BPF. We applied the log transform to the BPF data before analysis in order to stabilize variance and to allow for proportional differences across ROIs. Fixed effects included ROI, age, sex, and total intracranial volume, and the caudal middle frontal cortical thickness, and the random effect was subject. Significance was defined as p < 0.05 and tests were two sided. Statistical analyses of continuous data were performed using partial correlation analysis, and categorical data using either student’s t-test or Fisher’s exact test. R version 4.0.4 (www.R-project.org) was used for calculations.

Exploratory Whole Brain Analysis

T1-weighted space voxel-wise BPF maps were first natural log-transformed to remedy skewness in the outcome measure as has been performed in previous analyses with [11C]WAY100635 [12, 26]. The maps were then resampled onto the Freesurfer fsaverage surface using each participant’s Freesurfer 7.1.1 run and smoothed with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Cortical thickness surface maps were then prepared for whole-brain exploratory statistical analysis by smoothing with a Gaussian Kernel of 6 mm full-width half maximum. General linear models were run to examine vertex-wise associations between BPF and cortical thickness, covarying for age, sex and total intracranial volume. Multiple comparisons correction was performed via cluster-wise correction (cluster forming threshold p < 0.001, familywise error alpha<0.05) [27, 28].

Replication of Caudal Middle Frontal Cortical Thickness Findings

The subset of participants in this study who were not included in the previous study of cortical thickness (n = 21 BD and n = 47 HV), had less cortical thickness in right caudal middle frontal compared with HVs, covarying for sex, age and intracranial volume (F = 10.24, dF = 1,63, p = 0.0021). The estimated standard deviation for error was 0.0697 for the HV group and 0.0507 for the BD patients. Mean cortical thickness in that BD group was 3.4% less than in the HV group after accounting for other variables.

Regional Association between cortical thickness and BPF of [11C]WAY100635

Considering all BD participants with [11C]WAY100635 scans (n = 31 BD), the right caudal middle frontal cortex cortical thickness values had an inverse association with BPF of [11C]WAY100635 within PET ROIs (F = 4.77; df = 1,28; p = 0.037; Fig. 1A). Conversely, within the HV group (n = 49), there was no association between cortical thickness and BPF of [11C]WAY100635 within PET ROIs (F = 0.62, dF = 1,46, p = 0.44; Fig. 1B). These analyses were repeated after cortical thickness data was corrected for scanner differences using ComBat, and the results did not significantly change (Data not shown) [29]. Similar inverse associations were found when considering regions that had been derived from clusters of cortical thickness deficits from the previous study that were apart from our a priori ROI (Supplemental Figure S1).

Relationship between cortical thickness and BPF of [11C]WAY100635 within region of interest.

A Cortical thickness values in BD were found to be inversely associated with BPF of [11C]WAY100635 within a cluster of cortex that was previously found to be lower in BD relative to HV. B BPF of [11C]WAY100635 was not significantly associated with cortical thickness in same cluster within the HV group, consistent with a bipolar specific association.

Vertex-wise Whole Brain Exploratory Analysis

Given the association between cortical thickness and BPF of [11C]WAY100635 in the BD group, a vertex-wise, whole brain exploratory analysis was conducted to determine if associations were present outside of our initial regions of interest. Within the BD group, BPF of [11C]WAY100635 was inversely associated with cortical thickness in right superior parietal cortex (cluster area 126.6 mm2, p = 0.003; Fig. 2), and positively associated with cortical thickness in right posterior cingulate cortex (cluster area 106 mm2, p = 0.008; Fig. 2) and left entorhinal cortex (cluster area 146.9 mm2, p = 0.001; Fig. 2). In the whole-brain exploratory analysis across the HV group, no regions of association were found between BPF of [11C]WAY100635 and cortical thickness.

Post hoc Analysis of clusters between participants with BD and HV groups

Within the clusters where 5HT1AR binding was associated with cortical thickness from the vertex-wise analyses, mean cortical thickness values did not differ between BD and HV groups (right superior parietal, p = 0.34; right posterior cingulate, p = 0.56; left entorhinal, p = 0.89). However, mean BPF of [11C]WAY100635 within those clusters was elevated in the BD group compared with HVs (right superior parietal p < 0.001; right posterior cingulate p = 0.018; left entorhinal, p = 0.0071). Also, within the HV samples, mean BPF of [11C]WAY100635 and mean cortical thickness from those clusters showed no significant correlations (right posterior cingulate, R = 0.256, p = 0.086; right superior parietal, R = −0.27, p = 0.074; left entorhinal, R = 0.11, p = 0.48). Of note, the right posterior cingulate and right superior parietal cortex regions showed statistical trends in the same direction to the BD group.

Discussion

Overall Interpretation

In the right caudal middle frontal cortex, a region previously shown to have lower cortical thickness in BD compared with HV’s [9], cortical thickness in BD was inversely associated with levels of cortical 5HT1ARs, as measured by [11C]WAY100635 PET imaging. This finding is consistent with a model in which cortical thickness deficits in BD are associated with disruptions of serotonin neurotransmission. Plausible mechanistic explanations for the association, including a lack of neurotrophic support, are discussed below. Exploratory vertex-wise analyses identified other regions where cortical 5HT1AR levels were associated with cortical thickness in BD, one region with inverse association and two with positive associations. The cortical thickness values in these regions did not differ between BD and HV groups, however, indicating an association between the two that is unrelated to disease-specific cortical thickness deficits.

Implications for Cortical Thickness Deficits in BD

The etiology of cortical thickness deficits in BD remains unknown. Past studies in HVs have indicated that cortical thickness can vary depending on the activity of the region over time, as demonstrated through motoric challenges [30]. Regional cortical thickness deficits in BD may therefore occur due to a sustained lack of activity within those regions. Cortical thickness deficits also occur in multiple neurodegenerative disorders, thought to be part of the neuropathological process [31–33]. Although BD does not show signs of gross neurodegeneration, increased ventricular size, lower cortical thickness and lower white matter integrity have been found in BD relative to HVs, consistent with a subtle neurodegenerative process [11, 34, 35]. The data presented here indicate that the cortical thickness deficits, whether they are due to a subtle neurodegenerative process, a difference in activity, or another etiology entirely, are associated with changes in serotonergic signaling.

The relationship between postsynaptic 5HT1AR levels and serotonin signaling levels in the human brain has not been extensively studied. One PET study found an inverse correlation between the rate of serotonin synthesis and levels of postsynaptic 5HT1ARs [14]. Multiple PET studies have shown positive correlations between levels of raphe 5HT1ARs, which inhibit serotonin release, and levels of postsynaptic 5HT1ARs [12, 36, 37]. Together, these data are consistent with a process by which postsynaptic 5HT1ARs are upregulated to compensate for lower serotonin signaling. Assuming that interpretation to be correct, the data here are consistent with a model in which cortical thickness deficits in BD are associated with a paucity of serotonin signaling.

The findings would plausibly be explained by a model where lower serotonin signaling decreases neurotrophic support to the cortex, causing deficits in particularly vulnerable cortical regions. This model is consistent with a number of preclinical studies that have indicated that serotonin signaling has neurotrophic effects. Knockout mouse studies have highlighted the importance of serotonin signaling in central nervous system development, including disruptions to the thalamocortical fiber organization in both MAOA and SERT knockout mice [15]. In cell culture, serotonergic antidepressants demonstrated neuroprotective properties, and withdrawal of serotonin had the opposite effect [38]. Serotonergic antidepressants have been shown to induce adult neurogenesis in the hippocampus, a neurotrophic effect that did not occur in 5HT1AR knockout mice [39]. Serotonergic antidepressants may work in part due to their neurotrophic effects [16, 40, 41]. Preclinical studies have indicated a role for serotonin in regulating prefrontal cortical activity [42], and serotonin neurons are known to project throughout the cortex. Therefore, a long-term deficit of serotonin signaling may induce cortical atrophy in BD.

The cross-sectional design of this study cannot directly address directionality of the association between cortical thickness and serotonin signaling, however. Deficits in frontal cortical activity, in fact, have been shown to induce decreases in serotonin signaling originating from the raphe nucleus [43, 44]. It is therefore possible that deficits in activity in the caudal middle frontal region, reflected by decreased cortical thickness, may cause a hypo-serotonergic state. A feedback loop between decreased activity in the frontal cortex and lower serotonin activity is also possible. Alternatively, cortical thickness deficits and changes in serotonin signaling may be caused by an independent pathophysiological process. For example, it is plausible that greater brain inflammation induces both a cortical thickness deficit and a hypo-serotonergic state [45, 46], or that early life stress that affects both serotonin signaling and cortical thickness patterns [47–49].

Other Regions of 5HT1AR and Cortical Thickness Association

The exploratory, vertex-wise analysis in BD identified clusters outside of our a priori ROI’s where 5HT1AR levels and cortical thickness were associated with each other. Because the post hoc analyses of those clusters did not find that the cortical thickness values differed between BD and HV groups, those results are not relevant to our understanding the cortical thickness deficits in BD. However, the interaction between serotonin signaling and cortical morphology may prove to have implications for the pathogenesis of BD.

Relevance to Default Mode Network Activity

Cortical thickness has been found to increase in brain regions that are repeatedly stimulated during cortical activity, consistent with an increase in synaptogenesis [50]. For example, motor tasks induce cortical thickness increases in the motor cortex [50]. The long-term effect of resting state network activity on cortical thickness has not been studied. However, there is no reason that increased default mode network activity would not similarly cause an increase of cortical thickness within regions of the network.

The posterior cingulate cortex is a central node in the resting state default mode network, and the entorhinal cortex connects the hippocampus to the cortical nodes of the default mode network [51–53]. The positive association between 5HT1AR levels and cortical thickness in those regions may therefore be explained by an association between lower levels of serotonin signaling and greater activity of the default mode network [54]. Resting state activity was not acquired here to test this interpretation directly, but this model is consistent with other studies that have reported an association between serotonin signaling and default mode network activity. Acute tryptophan depletion, which lowers serotonin levels, was associated with either a corresponding increase in default mode network functional connectivity [55], a disruption in default mode network connectivity to other regions [56], or a greater resting functional amplitude of low frequency fluctuations in the posterior cingulate cortex in HVs [57]. Conversely, antidepressants altered default mode network connectivity patterns in HVs, and an alteration was associated with levels of serotonin transporter occupancy [58–60]. A normalization of default mode network functional connectivity from serotonergic medications in clinical populations has also been reported [61, 62]. Therefore, an interaction between serotonin neurotransmission and activity levels of the default mode network may prove to be important in the pathogenesis of BD.

Previous Data on Posterior Cingulate Cortex

A positive association between 5HT1AR levels and cortical thickness in the posterior cingulate cortex region has been noted in HVs in two past studies [54, 63]. One of those studies also reported this association in participants with MDD [63]. We also found a trend positive association within that region in HVs. Together, these data suggest that the positive association in the posterior cingulate cortex region is not specific to BD. However, the results indicate that altered default mode network activity in the pathophysiology of BD may be associated with altered 5HT1AR signaling.

Consideration of Radiotracer Quantification Methods

Estimation of the PET outcome measure BPF at the ROI level was obtained with a different approach from estimation at the voxel level. At the ROI level, a two tissue compartment constrained (2TCC) model was used to estimate the radiotracer total distribution volume (VT) [21], and the binding potential BPF then calculated in each ROI as (VT – VND)/fP, where VND is the tracer non-displaceable distribution volume, estimated using the VT in the reference region, the cerebellar white matter [64]. At the voxel level, BPF was instead estimated using a data-driven basis pursuit strategy [23], which determines in each voxel a parsimonious model (not necessarily the 2TCC) consistent with the measured data via selection of a family of basis functions. Once the VT parametric images were obtained for each subject, corresponding BPF images were generated by subtracting in each voxel the average (across voxels within the ROI) VT of the cerebellar white matter, and dividing for the subject measured fP. Given the different levels of signal-to-noise ratio in the PET time activity curves at the ROI vs. voxel level, the different approaches used for estimation of VT (model-based vs. data-driven), and the different approach for determination of VND, estimates at the ROI vs. voxel level are expected to be correlated, but potentially biased with respect to each other [23, 65]. Analyses at the ROI and voxel level present distinct advantages and disadvantages: the ROI level approach reduces the number of multiple comparisons and increases power at the expense of reduced anatomical detail, while the voxel level analyses provide increased anatomical detail at the expense of reduced sensitivity due to having to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Potential Partial Volume Effects

Given that partial volume effects would be in the opposite direction to the current findings, i.e., BPF may be lower in thinner regions if partial volume effects were present, the findings are unlikely to be confounded by partial volume effects. Partial volume effects were further minimized by performing surface-based smoothing of the [11C]WAY100635 BPF maps, a step that reduces bias and inter-subject variance, and therefore boosts statistical power, relative to volume-based smoothing. Surface-based smoothing avoids contamination of the grey matter BPF signal by solely smoothing along the cortical ribbon instead of across white matter and other tissue classes [66]. Of note, there is no gold standard for partial volume correction, particularly in dynamic PET scans [67].

Limitations

This study was limited by its sample size. The sample set was also one of convenience, and therefore formed a secondary analysis of data from studies with separate aims, although recruited and evaluated and scanned by the same research team [12, 17, 18]. Due to scanner upgrades over time, the MRI scans utilized here were performed on different scanners and field strengths. The whole brain exploratory analysis did not identify the inverse association of cortical thickness with 5HT1AR binding in the caudal middle frontal cortex from the analysis, and it is unknown whether the heterogeneity of scans limited the ability to detect that or other regions where the two are associated.

Future Studies

As this is the first study to measure associations between 5HT1AR binding and cortical thickness in BD, it requires replication. Studies with a longitudinal or interventional design could help elucidate the directionality of the association between cortical thickness deficits and 5HT1AR levels in BD. Future studies could investigate how an interaction between serotonin signaling and posterior cingulate cortical thickness, or default mode network activity, plays a role in the pathogenesis of BD and its symptoms.
