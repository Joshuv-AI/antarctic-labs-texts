Multivariate structure and time-segmented exposure characteristics of indoor air quality in childcare centers
Authors: Joon-sig Jung, Hyung-jin Jeon, Min-jeong Ko, So-young Lee, Jong-hee Jang, SungChul Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding indoor air quality (IAQ) in childcare environments is critical for safeguarding young children, who are physiologically vulnerable to airborne pollutants due to higher inhalation rates and developing respiratory systems. This study characterized daypart-specific IAQ patterns and their multivariate structures by using high-resolution monitoring at seven childcare centers in Korea. Continuous 1-min measurements of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, total volatile organic compounds (TVOC), CO₂, radon, temperature, and relative humidity were collected and aggregated into 5-min averages for analysis. PM₂.₅ (8.9–13.9 µg·m⁻³), PM₁₀ (18.4–25.8 µg·m⁻³), CO₂ (445–1,029 ppm), and radon (16–191 Bq·m⁻³) largely complied with domestic and WHO guidelines. In contrast, TVOC concentrations exceeded recommended reference ranges at all facilities and showed substantial inter-facility variability. Pollutant levels varied clearly by time of day: particles increased during active classroom periods and declined afterward; CO₂ temporarily exceeded 1,000 ppm during peak occupancy; TVOC showed activity-related peaks with evening persistence; and radon increased in one facility before morning ventilation. Exploratory principal component analysis (PCA) showed that the three components accounted for 83.5% of the total variance, reflecting increases in activity-related particulates, ventilation-dependent gaseous accumulation, and temperature-related variations in TVOC levels. K-means clustering grouped similar daypart profiles across centers, indicating recurring multivariate IAQ patterns beyond the daily average concentrations. The pollutant peaks did not occur simultaneously. Particulate matter aligned with occupancy, whereas gaseous pollutants accumulated under limited ventilation. These findings suggest that IAQ in childcare settings is better interpreted in relation to operational time segments rather than daily averages alone, and that ventilation strategies should consider both timing and pollutant-specific behavior when managing exposure in sensitive indoor environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45168-y.Keywords: Indoor air quality, Childcare center, Time-segmented exposure, PCA, Cluster analysisSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Childcare centers represent primary indoor environments where young children spend most of their daily time; therefore, indoor air quality (IAQ) in such facilities critically influences their health and developmental outcomes 1,2. Compared with adults, young children have approximately twice the respiratory ventilation rate per unit body weight, and their immune and detoxification systems are physiologically immature, making them more vulnerable to indoor air pollutants3. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), total volatile organic compounds (TVOC), carbon dioxide (CO2), and radon are recognized as high-priority indoor contaminants that can accumulate depending on building characteristics, ventilation efficiency, occupancy density, and proximity to traffic emissions4–6. Recent field investigations in childcare facilities have reported that multiple indoor pollutants frequently exceed the recommended guideline values, raising concerns regarding their potential respiratory and developmental impacts on children7.

Previous studies have identified building age, ventilation mode, occupancy density, surrounding roadway pollution, and construction material characteristics as key determinants of school IAQ, while highlighting the dominant role of airtightness and ventilation efficiency in shaping indoor pollutant levels4,5. Korean studies have also reported that outdoor traffic emissions and ventilation frequency significantly influence IAQ in childcare centers8. That radon and TVOC concentrations differ markedly depending on structural conditions, such as the presence of underground levels and ventilation status3.

However, most existing IAQ studies have focused on single pollutants or specific periods and have relied on low-resolution (hourly or daily) datasets, limiting their ability to characterize short-term peak exposure patterns or multi-pollutant interactions within real childcare environments9,10. Low-frequency measurements may fail to capture short-term fluctuations associated with transient ventilation changes, occupancy behaviors, or pollutant accumulation during unoccupied nighttime periods11. Recent improvements in real-time sensor technology have enabled continuous, high-resolution measurements at sub-minute intervals. Such data allow for the examination of pollutant levels over time and support the use of multivariate statistical methods to explore co-occurring patterns in indoor environments10,12.

Despite these methodological advances, multivariate research on IAQ in childcare facilities remains limited. In particular, studies simultaneously evaluating PM(2.5 and 10), TVOC, CO2, and radon using time-segmented patterns and dimensionality-reduction techniques remain scarce. The combined application of principal component analysis (PCA) and k-means clustering offers an integrated analytical approach for examining time-structured IAQ behavior, extending beyond conventional concentration-focused evaluations.

Therefore, this study aimed to characterize IAQ in seven childcare centers in South Korea by measuring PM2.5, PM10, TVOC, CO2, radon, temperature, and relative humidity using high-resolution continuous monitoring. Time-segmented (occupied vs. unoccupied) multi-pollutant interactions were analyzed using PCA, and exposure pattern classification was performed using K-means clustering. By interpreting IAQ as a time-variable multi-pollutant system, this study adopted a time-segmented perspective that may inform tailored ventilation strategies and IAQ management policies.

Materials and methods

Study area and monitoring period

This study was conducted at seven childcare centers in Jeollanam-do Province, Republic of Korea. Continuous indoor air quality monitoring was conducted for approximately six weeks, from May 26 to July 7, 2025. Minor variations existed in the installation and retrieval dates of the instruments due to logistical factors; however, all sites were monitored within the same seasonal and temporal period to ensure comparability (Fig. S1). Identical high-resolution real-time monitoring devices were installed at each facility, and data were collected at one-minute intervals throughout the monitoring period.

Instrumentation and data processing

Concentrations of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, total volatile organic compounds (TVOC), radon, and CO₂, as well as air temperature and relative humidity, were continuously measured using the ArimAir RA300W Indoor Air Quality Monitor (Arim Inc., Republic of Korea). The selection of indoor parameters was guided by the objective of characterizing time-segmented exposure structures and dominant indoor exposure mechanisms in childcare environments rather than assessing compliance with individual pollutant guideline values.

The instrument integrates multiple sensing modules, including a pulsed-ionization chamber for radon detection, a non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) sensor for CO₂, a laser-scattering sensor for particulate matter, a digital temperature sensor, and a capacitive humidity sensor. All sensors were factory-calibrated before deployment in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Monitoring devices were installed in the central area of each main activity room at approximately 1.0–1.5 m above the floor, corresponding to the practical breathing-zone height in childcare environments. This height range was selected to reflect the dynamic activity patterns of young children, who frequently alternate between sitting, standing, and moving, rather than maintaining a fixed static posture. Similar installation heights have been adopted in previous indoor air quality studies conducted in daycare centers and childcare facilities, as well as in international health guidelines for educational environments1,3,8.

To minimize the potential measurement artifacts, the first 24 h of data following instrument installation were excluded to allow for sensor stabilization and environmental adaptation. Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures were applied to remove power-related measurement interruptions, missing values, instrument error signals, and extreme outliers exceeding the mean ± 3 standard deviations.

Raw one-minute measurements were aggregated into 5-minute averaged values to reduce short-term noise and improve data comparability across facilities. To examine the indoor air quality variations associated with occupancy and daily activity patterns, the monitoring period was divided into five categories: occupied-AM (08:01–12:00), occupied-PM (12:01–17:00), occupied-late (17:01–20:00), unoccupied-ve (20:01–24:00), and unoccupied-night (00:01–08:00). This preprocessing framework enabled a standardized multivariate comparison of time-segmented IAQ patterns across seven childcare centers.

Air temperature and relative humidity were included as contextual variables because they affect pollutant emissions, particle resuspension, ventilation performance, and indoor accumulation, including these parameters allowed multivariate analyses to consider not only pollutant concentrations but also environmental conditions that may contribute to time-related variability in IAQ.

Total VOC (TVOC) concentrations were measured using a metal-oxide-semiconductor (MOS) sensor and reported as ethanol-equivalent values. Accordingly, comparisons with the guideline values were interpreted as indicative of exceedance levels rather than compound-specific toxicological thresholds.

Indoor ozone (O₃) and nitrogen oxides (NOx) were not included in the present analysis. In childcare settings, indoor levels of these pollutants are generally lower than those of primary indoor contaminants due to limited indoor sources and rapid surface reactions. Nevertheless, O₃ and NOx can contribute to secondary reactions indoors, particularly through interactions with VOCs and indoor surfaces, leading to the formation of additional by-products. Although these species were not measured in this monitoring campaign owing to instrument constraints and the focus on the main time-segmented pollutants, their potential relevance is addressed in the Discussion.

Although the nominal accuracy of PM sensors may appear high relative to the observed concentration levels, the present study focused on relative temporal variability and pattern identification rather than on absolute concentration–based compliance assessment. To support data reliability, short-term fluctuations were mitigated through 5-minute averaging, QA/QC procedures were applied to remove artifacts and extreme outliers, and standardized values were used in multivariate analyses. Accordingly, PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ results are interpreted in terms of time-segmented trends and multivariate exposure patterns rather than precise absolute concentrations.

Principal component analysis and cluster analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and k-means clustering were applied as exploratory multivariate techniques to identify the multivariate structure of indoor air pollutants and classify time-dependent IAQ patterns across childcare centers.

PCA was used to summarize the covariance structure among pollutants and to extract latent variables that capture joint variations across multiple IAQ parameters. This analysis was conducted as an exploratory tool to identify dominant exposure structures rather than to establish causal or predictive relationships. The seven variables (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, TVOC, radon, CO₂, temperature, and relative humidity) based on 5-minute averaged data were standardized using z-score normalization to eliminate scale-related distortions. A correlation matrix was employed, which is appropriate when variables are measured in different units and when deviations from strict normality are expected in environmental datasets.

Principal components were retained based on a combination of eigenvalue magnitude, scree plot inspection, and interpretability of loading patterns rather than strict reliance on the Kaiser criterion alone, resulting in a cumulative explained variance exceeding approximately 70%. Although the eigenvalue of PC3 was slightly below 1.0, this component was retained because it captured a distinct thermal–TVOC interaction pattern that was consistently observed across multiple facilities and activity periods. The PCA was conducted using 35 aggregated observations derived from seven childcare centers and five day-part categories, yielding a sample-to-variable ratio of approximately 1:5, which falls within the commonly cited thresholds for exploratory PCA applications. The PCA findings summarized how the measured pollutants varied across time segments without implying causal or predictive modeling, and the resulting component scores were used to explore similarities among IAQ patterns through cluster analysis.

K-means clustering was applied to group similar IAQ patterns based on the variability in pollutant profiles and microenvironmental conditions. The PCA-derived scores (PC1–PC3) were used as input variables to reduce dimensionality and avoid multicollinearity. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method and the silhouette coefficient to balance intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster separation. Clusters were interpreted based on aggregated pollutant characteristics, temperature, and humidity, and on their distribution across occupancy-based time segments (Occupied-AM, Occupied-PM, Occupied-Late, Unoccupied-Eve, and Unoccupied-Night). In this study, clustering was intended to provide a structured representation of time-segmented IAQ behavior patterns, complementing temporal variation analysis, rather than serving as a stand-alone classification framework. Using this exploratory clustering approach, similar time-segmented IAQ profiles were grouped to aid interpretation of recurring exposure patterns rather than to establish fixed categories.

Statistical analysis and correlation assessment

To evaluate pollutant-to-pollutant associations and differences in pollutant concentrations across dayparts and childcare facilities, correlation analysis and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted. Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) were calculated to examine linear associations among the major indoor air pollutants. Correlation analyses were performed for the overall dataset and separately for each day (Occupied-AM, Occupied-PM, Occupied-Late, Unoccupied-Eve, and Unoccupied-nights) to compare the co-variation patterns under different occupancy and ventilation conditions. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed tests (p < 0.05). Detailed correlation results for the total dataset and each time segment are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Correlation analysis showed that several variables shared common patterns of variation. In particular, PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ were strongly correlated, while moderate correlations were observed among selected gas-phase pollutants. Given these intervariable relationships, principal component analysis (PCA) was used as an exploratory approach to summarize the time-segmented IAQ variability. All variables were standardized using z-scores before the PCA to minimize scale differences, and the analysis was performed based on the correlation matrix.

Differences in pollutant concentrations among the five daypart categories were evaluated using one-way ANOVA. Normality and homogeneity of variance were examined using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. When assumptions were satisfied, ANOVA was applied; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Significant results were followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc comparisons.

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.11 (pandas, scipy.stats, statsmodels, scikit-learn) and IBM SPSS Statistics 12.0. PCA was implemented with the scikit-learn PCA module, and clustering was performed with the k-means algorithm. PCA loadings and scores were visualized as biplots, and cluster characteristics were interpreted by comparing the mean pollutant concentrations, microenvironmental parameters (temperature and relative humidity), and distribution of daypart categories within each cluster.

The radon concentrations showed substantial interfacility variability, ranging from 16.46 to 190.56 Bq·m⁻³. CC-3 recorded the highest concentration (190.56 Bq·m⁻³), exceeding both the WHO reference level (100 Bq·m⁻³) and the U.S. EPA action level (148 Bq·m⁻³), whereas all other centers remained below these thresholds.

TVOC concentrations (ethanol-equivalent) ranged from 211.16 to 474.33 ppb and exceeded both the Korean recommended limit (100 ppb) and the WHO reference range (75–125 ppb) at all facilities. The highest concentration was observed in CC-6 (474.33 ppb), corresponding to approximately 380–630% of the WHO reference range.

CO₂ concentrations ranged from 445 to 1,029 ppm, with an overall mean of 554 ppm. Most facilities remained below 1,000 ppm, although CC-3 slightly exceeded the WHO school environment guideline (1,000 ppm).

Air temperature and relative humidity ranged from 24.1 to 28.6 °C and 58.9 to 71.0%, respectively, and generally fell within recommended comfort ranges.

A one-way ANOVA indicated statistically significant inter-facility differences for several parameters (Table 2). In particular, radon and CO₂ concentrations differed significantly among childcare centers (p < 0.01), with post-hoc Tukey tests confirming that CC-3 exhibited significantly higher radon and CO₂ levels compared with most other facilities. Significant differences were also observed for PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, and TVOC concentrations in selected pairwise comparisons (p < 0.01), notably involving CC-6, which showed elevated particulate and TVOC levels relative to multiple centers. Temperature and relative humidity also demonstrated significant variability across the facilities (p < 0.01).

PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, and CO₂ concentrations generally met domestic and international guideline values, whereas TVOCs consistently exceeded recommended reference ranges across all facilities, and radon exceeded WHO and EPA thresholds at CC-3. Statistically significant interfacility differences suggested that IAQ variability may be associated with facility-specific factors, including ventilation performance, structural conditions, and material-related emissions. Among the measured pollutants, TVOCs showed the most consistent exceedance, indicating the importance of ventilation management and source control in childcare environments.

Activity-based analysis of indoor air pollutants

Tables S2–S8 and Fig. 1 summarize indoor air pollutant concentrations across the seven childcare centers by activity-related time segments (dayparts). PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ concentrations satisfied both the Korean Indoor Air Quality Standards (PM₂.₅ ≤ 35 µg·m⁻³; PM₁₀ ≤ 100 µg·m⁻³) and the WHO 24-hour guideline values across all centers. Nevertheless, PM₂.₅ levels at CC-3 and CC-6 approached the upper WHO limit during the Occupied-PM period (12:01–17:00). Clear diurnal patterns were observed, with PM concentrations increasing during the Occupied-AM (08:01–12:00) and Occupied-PM periods and decreasing by approximately 15–25% during the Unoccupied-night period (00:01–08:00). The PM₁₀/PM₂.₅ ratio ranged from 1.8 to 2.3, consistent with indoor particle size distributions.

Daypart-averaged radon concentrations ranged from 17 to 68 Bq·m⁻³ and remained below both the WHO and Korean guideline values at all centers. However, high–time-resolution data revealed a short-term radon peak of 265 Bq·m⁻³ at CC-3 during the Occupied-AM period, indicating transient accumulation before ventilation.

The TVOC concentrations (ethanol equivalent) showed the greatest temporal variability, ranging from approximately 190 ppb to values exceeding 1,000 ppb, and exceeded the WHO and domestic reference ranges at all facilities. Several peak periods exceeded the instrument’s upper detection limit (1,000 ppb), indicating sensor saturation. For this reason, TVOC exceedances are interpreted here as relative indicators of emission intensity and accumulation rather than precise absolute concentrations. TVOC peaks occurred most frequently during the occupied-late period (17:01–20:00), particularly at CC-4 and CC-6, and elevated concentrations (150–400 ppb) persisted during the unoccupied-Eve period (20:01–24:00).

CO₂ concentrations ranged from 450 to 1,360 ppm and remained below 1,000 ppm at most centers, although CC-2 and CC-3 reached 1,100–1,400 ppm during afternoon occupancy peaks. CO₂ levels declined to 450–500 ppm overnight, consistent with reduced occupancy and enhanced ventilation.

Air temperature and relative humidity ranged from 24.0 to 28.7 °C and 58–72%, respectively, with relatively elevated thermal and moisture conditions observed at CC-4, CC-6, and CC-7.

Across the centers, particles showed clear alignment with occupied periods, whereas gas-phase pollutants displayed delayed reduction or short-term accumulation when ventilation was limited. VOCs exhibited the widest temporal spread among the measured parameters, with repeated late-day and evening elevations. Daypart-specific differences within each childcare center were evaluated using a one-way ANOVA (Tables S2–S8). The analysis revealed that PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, and radon concentrations were significantly higher during the morning period, whereas CO₂ concentrations peaked in the afternoon (p < 0.01). In contrast, TVOC levels were significantly elevated during the late evening hours, and temperature and relative humidity increased significantly during the late evening and night (p < 0.01).

Principal component analysis of indoor air-quality patterns

A Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using seven indoor air quality indicators (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, radon, TVOC, CO₂, temperature, and relative humidity) across the seven childcare centers (CC-1 to CC-7). The analysis was based on the mean values of five time-segmented activity periods (days), yielding 35 observation units (seven centers × five dayparts).

The three principal components explained 83.5% of the total variance, with contributions of 42.8% (PC1), 27.6% (PC2), and 13.1% (PC3) (Table 3; Fig. 2). The factor loadings indicated that PC1 was primarily influenced by positive contributions from PM₂.₅ (0.420), PM₁₀ (0.409), relative humidity (0.491), radon (0.350), and CO₂ (0.184), while temperature (–0.490) showed a strong negative loading. PC2 showed high positive loadings for radon (0.484), CO₂ (0.524), and TVOC (0.352), whereas particulate matter variables loaded negatively. PC3 represents a thermal–TVOC dimension, with positive loadings for TVOC (0.638) and temperature (0.452) and a negative loading for relative humidity (–0.390).

Clustering analysis of activity-based IAQ patterns

To classify time-segmented indoor air quality patterns across the seven childcare centers, K-means clustering was conducted using PCA scores (PC1–PC3) derived from the 35 observation units (7 centers × 5 dayparts: Occupied-AM, Occupied-PM, Occupied-Late, Unoccupied-Eve, and Unoccupied-Night). All variables were standardized using z-scores before PCA, and clustering was performed using the resulting principal component scores.

The number of clusters was evaluated by comparing solutions for = 2–5 based on silhouette coefficients and inter-cluster separation. The three-cluster solution (k = 3) provided clear separation within the PCA score space (PC1–PC2, PC1–PC3, PC2–PC3 plots) (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Discussion

Dominant mechanisms driving IAQ variability

Particulate matter concentrations generally met the national and WHO guideline values. However, noticeable peaks occurred during occupied hours, particularly in the morning and afternoon. At CC-6, PM₂.₅ approached the WHO 24-hour guideline during active classroom periods. Concentrations declined during nighttime hours when activities ceased, suggesting that resuspension from movement and classroom activities played a substantial role. Similar diurnal patterns have been reported in preschool settings, where occupancy intensity and ventilation practices influence indoor particle levels13–15. In the present dataset, these daytime increases were reflected in higher PC1 scores and were frequently grouped within cluster 0.

TVOC levels showed greater variability than the other pollutants. Although the measurements represent ethanol-equivalent values rather than individual compounds, repeated increases were observed during the Late period (17:01–20:00), with elevated levels sometimes extending into the evening. These patterns are consistent with emissions from cleaning activities and material off-gassing under reduced ventilation16,17. The association between higher temperatures and increased TVOC levels during certain periods suggests that indoor thermal conditions may influence the emission intensity. This tendency corresponds to the PC3 dimensions and characteristics of cluster 2.

Radonconcentrations were mostly below the reference thresholds; however, short-term increases were observed at CC-3 in the morning hours before routine ventilation began. This pattern indicates that radon accumulation can occur overnight when the air exchange is limited. Unlike CO₂, which is directly linked to occupant presence, radon levels appear to be more sensitive to ventilation timing and building characteristics. Comparable behaviors have been reported in educational facilities, where structural features and pressure differences influence indoor radon levels and temporal variations18.

CO₂ concentrations generally remained within recommended limits. Afternoon increases were evident in several centers during peak occupancy, followed by declines once ventilation resumed. These changes are typical of metabolically generated pollutants and reflect the balance between the occupant density and air exchange rates. Similar observations have been made in previous preschool field studies14,15,19.

Temporal patterns differed across pollutants. Particulate concentrations increased quickly during classroom activities, whereas CO₂ rose more gradually with occupancy and declined once ventilation resumed. Radon tended to build up overnight and decrease after the morning air exchange, whereas TVOC showed both short-term spikes and prolonged elevations under limited ventilation. These contrasting responses indicate that IAQ variability in childcare centers reflects the combined influence of occupancy, ventilation timing, building characteristics, and indoor environmental conditions, consistent with established concepts in indoor air chemistry and exposure science20.

Multivariate IAQ behavior and pattern-based classification

The PCA results showed that most of the dataset’s variability could be described by three major components, which together accounted for 83.5% of the total variance. The observed correlation structure among variables supports this multivariate approach. Strong PM₂.₅–PM₁₀ correlations indicate shared particulate sources and resuspension processes, whereas moderate radon–CO₂ and TVOC–CO₂ correlations during evening and night periods are consistent with ventilation-dependent gas accumulation. PC1 was primarily associated with particulate matter during occupied periods; PC2 reflected the accumulation of radon and CO₂ under reduced ventilation; and PC3 captured the relationship between temperature and TVOC levels. These components summarize the co-occurring pollutant responses observed across the occupancy and ventilation phases, rather than representing independent source categories.

The clustering results are broadly consistent with these patterns. Similar IAQ profiles were grouped based on their PCA scores, and the differences between facilities became more apparent when identical time segments were compared. For example, some centers exhibited stronger particle-related signatures during occupied hours, whereas others displayed more sustained gas-phase accumulation in the evening. Such contrasts were particularly evident among centers with comparable occupancy schedules but differing evening ventilation practices. These facility-level differences suggest that operational behavior and building characteristics influence pollutant responses across time segments.

Practical implications and research significance

The time-segmented patterns identified in this study illustrate how pollutant behavior aligns with the occupancy and ventilation schedules in childcare facilities. Particulate concentrations increased during active daytime periods, whereas radon and TVOC showed delayed reduction or persistence when ventilation was limited. This distinction indicates that pollutant control strategies may need to be aligned with specific operational phases, rather than applied uniformly throughout the day.

Ventilation immediately following periods of high activity may help reduce short-term particulate elevation. Maintaining adequate air exchange during the evening and nighttime hours can reduce gas-phase accumulation. Under warmer indoor conditions, temperature management may also influence TVOC levels, particularly in facilities where material emissions are evident.

These findings suggest that the daily averages do not fully capture short-term changes in exposure. Examining data by day helps to clarify when pollutant accumulation is most likely and which parameters tend to respond under similar operational conditions. The use of high-resolution time-series monitoring enables simultaneous observation of these patterns across multiple pollutants in active childcare settings. Overall, the results highlighted the importance of occupancy schedules and ventilation practices in influencing IAQ variability in sensitive indoor environments.

Limitations and future perspectives

This exploratory study is based on 35 aggregated observations (seven facilities × five dayparts). Although sufficient to describe recurring patterns within the monitored centers, the sample size limited statistical generalization beyond the study population. Therefore, the PCA components and clusters summarized the patterns observed in this dataset and should not be interpreted as universal classifications.

Only seven childcare centers were included, and the ventilation rates, architectural layout, and material-specific emission characteristics were not directly measured. These factors are likely to contribute to the interfacility variability. The outdoor particulate concentrations were not included in the analysis. Because indoor PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ reflect both indoor activity and outdoor infiltration, the relative contribution of these sources was not separated. Accordingly, the particulate matter results should be viewed as representing combined indoor–outdoor influences rather than source-resolved behavior.

Additionally, low-cost particulate sensors have inherent limitations in terms of accuracy. Therefore, PM findings are more reliable than precise absolute quantification for identifying relative temporal patterns.

Indoor ozone (O₃) and nitrogen oxides (NOx) were not measured due to instrument constraints during the monitoring period. Although the indoor concentrations of these pollutants are typically lower than those of the major indoor contaminants, their role in secondary indoor chemistry warrants further consideration. Recent research indicates that reactions between indoor oxidants and VOCs can enhance the formation of secondary products, such as formaldehyde and secondary organic aerosols, potentially increasing modeled health impact estimates relative to primary emissions alone21. Reviews have further linked indoor ozone and its reaction products (ozone reaction products, ORPs) with adverse cardiorespiratory and dermal outcomes, while emphasizing the need for clearer exposure–response characterization in indoor environments22. Although these secondary processes were not directly evaluated in this study, their potential influence should be considered when interpreting temporal variations in IAQ.

Future work should expand the number of facilities, incorporate paired outdoor measurements and direct ventilation assessments, and examine the seasonal variability. Such extensions would allow the time-dependent IAQ patterns identified in this study to be evaluated across a broad range of operational conditions.

Conclusions

This study examined time-segmented indoor air quality patterns in seven childcare centers using high-resolution monitoring of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, TVOC, CO₂, and radon. Clear temporal differences were observed across the pollutants. Particulate matter increased during the occupied classroom period and declined overnight. Gas-phase pollutants, including radon and CO₂, were sensitive to ventilation timing, particularly during the evening and early morning. The TVOC levels varied substantially and were influenced by both activity patterns and indoor thermal conditions.

Although most pollutant concentrations complied with national guidelines, short-term peaks were evident. PM₂.₅ approached WHO 24-hour limits during active periods in some centers. The TVOC concentrations exceeded the recommended reference ranges across all facilities and showed repeated increases in the late afternoon. CO₂ temporarily exceeded 1,000 ppm during high occupancy, while radon showed morning elevations before ventilation in one facility.

Multivariate analyses showed consistent co-variation patterns across the time segments. Instead of being independent, pollutants respond to overlapping influences from occupancy intensity, ventilation performance, building characteristics, and indoor environmental conditions.

From a practical perspective, the results indicate that ventilation strategies should consider both the timing and the overall air exchange rate. Increasing ventilation after high-activity periods, maintaining adequate air exchange during the evening hours, and managing indoor thermal conditions may help reduce short-term pollutant accumulation, particularly for VOCs. In addition, the observed differences in pollutant behavior among the facilities suggest that IAQ management may benefit from a facility-specific approach. High-resolution monitoring can provide the information needed to tailor ventilation and control strategies according to the dominant pollutant patterns in each childcare center.

This study was limited by the small number of facilities and the use of TVOC as a composite indicator. Nevertheless, the time-resolved and multivariate approaches applied in this study provide insights into how IAQ varies within operational childcare environments. Further research involving larger samples and multi-season monitoring would strengthen our understanding of time-dependent IAQ variability in similar settings.
