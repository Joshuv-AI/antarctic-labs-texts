Ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis versus dry needling in patients with chronic low back pain a double-blind randomized clinical trial
Authors: Manuel Saavedra-Hernández, Inmaculada C Lara-Palomo, Cristian Sánchez-Ferre, Rubén Fernández-García, Juan José González-Gerez, Adelaida M Castro-Sánchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this randomized clinical trial was analysed the effects of percutaneous electrolysis versus electrical dry needling on pain, functionality, fear of movement and quality of live and sleep in patients with chronic low back pain. A total of 64 patients with chronic low back pain (age 46.45 ± 10.5) were randomly assigned to percutaneous electrolysis on the lumbar nerve root of L3 to L5, or electrical dry needling on the trigger points. Both groups received once a week for 6 weeks. The main outcome measures were pressure pain threshold and disability were the main, all outcomes were assessed at baseline, at 3, 6 and 10 weeks. The ANOVA test showed statistically significant differences for disability: F = 5.70, P = 0.005; pain: F = 7.42, P = 0.001; fear of movement: F = 4.24, P = 0.019; finger-to-floor distance test: F = 19.30, P < 0.001; and for daytime sleep: F = 3.14, P = 0.050; bodily pain: F = 5.03, P = 0.010; and total anxiety: F = 3.71, P = 0.030. In conclusion, the ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis on the lumbar nerve root of L3 to L5 was able to increase the pressure pain threshold, and decrease the pain intensity, disability, fear of movement, and finger-to-floor distance in individuals with chronic low back pain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45876-5.Keywords: Dry needling, Electrochemical techniques, Myofascial pain, Spine, Trigger point, Ultrasound imagingSubject terms: Diseases, Health care

Introduction

Chronic low back pain is one of the most common and costly forms of musculoskeletal pain in adults, and it has been considered one of the main causes of disability and decreased work productivity in people of working age (20–65 years) worldwide1–3. Approximately 85%of individuals seeking primary care do not have a specific identifiable pathoanatomical origin for their pain, and are therefore classified as having nonspecific low back pain4. Although the specific structures involved remain speculative, most lumbar structures have sensory innervation5; thus, many spinal tissues can contribute to pain when altered biochemically or damaged by degeneration or trauma. In recent years, chronic musculoskeletal disorders, including low back pain, appear capable of triggering central sensitization,6 characterized by reduced pain threshold, increased responsiveness, prolonged sequelae to noxious stimuli, and expansion of receptive fields allowing input from uninjured tissue to generate pain6–8.

Given the pivotal role of the nervous system in pain modulation, minimally invasive interventions such as dry needling and, more recently, ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis have emerged, targeting sensory relay systems using mechanical stimuli (needles) and galvanic current as a therapeutic medium9,10. Clinical evidence supports the application of puncture therapy to myofascial trigger points for reducing pain and disability11,12, yet evidence for electrolysis in non-specific chronic low back pain remains limited9,13. The percutaneous electrolysis involves delivering a galvanic electrolytic current through a solid filament needle directly to affected soft tissue, typically under ultrasound guidance14. Proposed mechanisms include mechanical tissue disruption, local electrochemical modulation, fibroblast activation, and neurophysiological effects, such as parasympathetic activation and pain threshold modulation9,14,15.

Emerging neuroimaging, electrophysiological, and biochemical studies provide further mechanistic insight16–18. Functional MRI and diffusion tensor imaging suggest that peripheral interventions can alter central pain processing networks17, while electromyography and local biochemical analyses indicate modulation of muscle activation patterns, neurotransmitter release, and inflammatory mediators18. Integrating these findings strengthens the rationale for percutaneous electrolysis as a method that may influence both peripheral and central pain mechanisms.

The main objective of this study was to analyse the efficacy of treatment through ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis applied up to the vicinity of the epineurium of the L3, L4, and L5 nerve roots versus electrical dry needling on myofascial trigger points in disability, pressure pain threshold, pain intensity, fear of movement, quality of life, sleep quality, mental health, trunk flexor endurance, and lumbar mobility in patients with non-specific chronic low back pain.

Methods

A randomized, double-blind, parallel-group clinical trial was conducted, with intention-to-treat analysis and both evaluator and statistical researcher blinded to treatment assignment. Study participants were randomly assigned to two groups with a 1:1 ratio: (i) patients with non-specific chronic low back pain treated with ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis at the lumbar nerve root, (ii) electrical dry needling on myofascial trigger points.

This trial was designed and conducted following the recommendations of the Declaration of Helsinki, and regulations for research in human beings, in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT)19. The study was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identification number NCT04290221, first registration 28/02/2020) and received approval from the ethics committee of the Andalusian Public Health System, Spain (UALBIO2019/037).

All participants were informed by one of the researchers about the study and their right to withdraw at any time without giving any explanations before signing the informed consent.

The sample size was calculated using Ene 3.0 software (Autonomous University of Barcelona, Spain). Based on the detection of a difference of 2.5 points on the Roland Morris questionnaire, assuming a standard deviation of 2.5 points, a two-tailed test, an alpha (α) of 0.05, and a desired power (beta) of 85%. The size per treatment group (G*Power, version 3.1.9.2) to increase statistical power, a target of nearly 70 participants was set.

The concealed allocation was carried out using a computer-generated random number table (Epidat 4.2) before the start of data collection by a researcher not involved in the recruitment or treatment of the patients. Individual and sequentially numbered cards were prepared with random allocation. The cards were folded and placed in sealed opaque envelopes. Another therapist, who was unaware of the initial examination, opened the envelope and proceeded with the treatment according to the group assignment.

During their first visit, participants were assessed for study eligibility according to the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria and were evaluated by a therapist blind to the interventions. After this in-person assessment, patients were randomly assigned to one of two groups and received the corresponding treatment administered by two researchers experienced in invasive physiotherapy who had more than 6 years of clinical experience. To be eligible, patients had to meet the following inclusion criteria: Non-specific chronic low back pain for three months or more; be between 30 and 67 years old; have a disability score for low back pain ≥ 4 on the Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire20; and not be receiving any other physiotherapy treatment. The exclusion criteria included: patients with sensory and/or coagulation disorders; history of spinal surgery; cardiac complications; severe concurrent disease of the central or peripheral nervous system; epilepsy; needle phobia; serious pathologies that could be the main cause of chronic low back pain (for example, presence of lumbar stenosis); or patients contraindicated or who had already received percutaneous electrolysis/electrical dry needling.

In addition, patients were required to have received only conservative treatments for chronic low back pain before enrollment (such as exercise therapy, manual therapy, analgesic or anti-inflammatory medication, or patient education), but not within the last 4 weeks prior to inclusion. None had undergone invasive procedures (e.g., intratissue percutaneous electrolysis, dry needling, infiltrations, or spinal surgery).

Finally, seventy patients aged between 30 and 67, diagnosed with non-specific chronic low back pain of more than 3 months duration and who were not receiving any type of treatment at that time, were recruited.

Firstly, participants provided demographic and clinical information and also completed a number of self-report measures and underwent a physical examination by an assessor blinded to the treatment allocation of the patients. Outcome measures were assessed before the first treatment session (baseline data), at three weeks (halfway through treatment), immediately post-treatment (one day after the final intervention) at 6 weeks, and one month after the last treatment session (follow-up) (Fig. 1).

Evaluation of myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) was performed with a 2-minute rest period between muscles. The diagnosis of MTrPs was carried out according to the criteria described by Gerwin et al.21 and Simons et al.22 using the following criteria: (1) the presence of a palpable taut band within a skeletal muscle; (2) the presence of a hyperirritable spot in the taut band; (3) a local twitch response elicited by snapping palpation of the taut band; and (4) the presence of referred pain in response to MTrP compression. MTrPs were classified as not present, latent, or active. MTrPs were considered active when local and referred pain produced clinical pain symptoms with manual compression, and latent when local and referred pain did not produce symptoms with a mechanical pain algometer (Kg/cm2) (Pain Diagnosis and Treatment Inc., Great Neck, NY, USA). The mean of the 3 trials (intra-examiner reliability) was calculated and used for the main analysis. A 30-second resting period was allowed between each trial.

The pressure pain threshold was assessed by an algometer (Wagner FPI 10 Algometer) applied in MTrPs, following the illustrations published by Travell and Simons22. The algometer consists of a rubber tip and a dial that measures the pressure applied to the MTrP in increments of 0.5 kg23. Three measurements were performed for each one of the selected trigger points: iliocostal muscle level L1; multifidus level L2, S1 and S4; quadratus lumbar deep superior, deep inferior, superficial superior and superficial inferior21. The average value of 3 repetitive measurements with intervals of 30 to 60 s was used for statistical analysis.20

The second instrument used in this study was the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) for assessing disability due to low back pain. The total score ranges from 0 points (no disability) to 24 points (maximum disability)20,24.

The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI)25 assessed the subjects’ limitations in activities of daily life in 10 dimensions. The overall score was expressed as a percentage: minimally disabled (0%-10%), moderately disabled (20%-40%), severely disabled (40%-60%), crippling back pain (60%-80%), or bedridden (80%-100%).

A Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)26 was used to assess the patients’ current level of pain. Study subjects situated their pain on a 100 mm horizontal line, where 0 mm indicates “no pain” and 100 mm indicates “the worst pain imaginable”.

The Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK)27 is a 17-item questionnaire developed to measure fear of movement and (re)injury. Ratings are summed to yield a total score (ranging from 17 to 68 points) where higher values reflect greater fear of (re)injury.

The SF-36 quality of life questionnaire28 assesses 8 domains including physical function, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. This scale ranges from 0 (lowest level of functioning) to 100 (highest level).

To test the isometric endurance of the trunk flexor muscles29, subjects were lying on their backs with their arms crossed over their chest, hands on the opposite shoulders, hips bent, and knees and feet apart. They were asked to lift their head and shoulders until the lower angle of the scapula lifted from the table and maintain the position for up to a maximum of 120 s.

Lumbar mobility in flexion was determined by measuring the finger-to-floor distance with a tape measure30. Participants performed a straight-legged forward trunk flexion from a standing position. The assessor measured the distance (cm.) from the tip of the third finger to the floor.

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index31 is a 10-item questionnaire with a total of 19 questions related to sleep habits in the previous month. The overall score ranges from 0 (no difficulty sleeping) to 21 points (severe difficulty sleeping).

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)32 assessed emotional distress. It consists of two subscales (HADA: Anxiety and HADD: Depression ) with seven items each that score from 0 (normal) to 3 (abnormal).

Patients underwent one session per week for a total of 6 weeks (one/week) at a clinic associated with the Faculty of Health Sciences at the University of Almería. Patients were required to complete 100% of their scheduled in-person treatment sessions to remain in the intention-to-treat analysis.

This group was treated with percutaneous electrolysis using a device specially designed and certified for this purpose (Directive EC 93/42/EEC). EPI Advanced Medicine®, Barcelona, Spain). The technique involved the use of ultrasound guidance to apply a continuous current through a puncture needle (0.25 × 40 mm, Agupunt® A1042P), performing 3 ultrasound-guided punctures of 3 s each in the proximal area of the epineurium of the posterior branches of the L3, L4, and L5 nerve roots, with an intensity of 3 mA33(Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6). The technique was applied bilaterally, first on the right side and then on the left. Ultrasound guidance was performed using a medically-certified diagnostic ultrasound device (General Electric LOGIQ E R9 TP), with a high-frequency linear array transducer (7.5–12 MHz; footprint 38–50 mm), providing high-resolution imaging of superficial musculoskeletal and neural structures. Real-time B-mode imaging allowed continuous monitoring of needle advancement and precise identification of the anatomical landmarks. Depth, gain, and focal zones were individually adjusted to optimize visualization of the proximal epineurium of the posterior branches of the L3, L4, and L5 nerve roots, ensuring accurate placement of the needle tip adjacent to the targeted neural structures while minimizing risk of injury (Video supplementary 1 to 3).

All patients in the dry needling group received bilateral electrical stimulation to active and latent myofascial trigger points in the following muscles11,22: iliocostalis lumborum (Level: L1), lumbosacral multifidus (Level: L2, S1, S4), and quadratus lumborum (Level: deep upper and lower, and deep medial and lateral; and superficial upper and lower, and superficial medial and lateral). Two sizes of sterilized, disposable stainless-steel acupuncture needles were used: 0.25 mm x 30 mm or 0.30 mm x 40 mm (Figs. 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11). The needle size was selected according to the patient’s physical constitution (i.e., muscle and/or connective tissue thickness). Each needle was inserted until it reached the active or latent myofascial trigger points or taut band, eliciting a local twitch response34(Video supplementary 4 to 8).

The needles were then connected to an electric current and left in place for 30 min (TensMed S82-Enraf Nonius)34. A low frequency current (2 Hz) was generated by a TENS device with a moderate pulse duration (250 microseconds) and a continuous biphasic waveform. The intensity was adjusted according to the patient´s perception, described as “mild to moderate”35,36.

Data were analysed using the IBM-SPSS software package (version 28.0). The significance threshold was set at 0.05 and the confidence interval limits at 95%. After a descriptive analysis, the normal distribution of the variables was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Baseline demographic and clinical variables were compared between the two groups using χ2 tests for categorical data and Student’s t-tests for continuous data. Repeated measures analysis of variance (2 × 3 mixed model ANOVA) was used to analyse time effects between both groups and group interaction effects for all outcome measures (disability, and pain as primary outcomes), between immediately after treatment and two months later. The group effect, time effect, and group x time interaction were measured by 95% confidence interval t-tests for paired or independent samples, as appropriate.

The minimum clinically significant difference (MCID) was calculated for the main outcomes analysed in this study. For functional disability, a change of 2–3 points in RMDQ or a relative reduction of 30%37, and an absolute change of 10–13 points in ODI were considered clinically relevant38. In addition, a cut-off point equivalent to 1.5–2 points on the 0–10 numerical scale was considered clinically relevant for the pain outcome (VAS). Regarding kinesiophobia (TSK), an approximate MCID of 4 points has been described as clinically significant39. For abdominal muscle strength and spinal flexion mobility, a change of at least 20–30% from baseline was considered a criterion of clinical relevance40.

Results

The most clinically relevant changes were observed in bodily pain (SF-36 Body Pain), social functioning (SF-36 Social Functioning), and anxiety (HADS Anxiety and Total Score). Some sleep domains showed small changes; only Daily Sleep Dysfunction reached clinical relevance at follow-up. Other SF-36 subscales, including sleep and depression, showed statistically minor and no clinically relevant changes.

Regarding adverse events, no significant complications or adverse effects of clinical significance occurred during the study. The only reported effects were transient local discomfort associated with the needling technique, such as pain or a feeling of heaviness in the treated area. These effects resolved spontaneously in all cases within a maximum of 72 h after the procedure. These symptoms were previously reported to the participants as part of the expected potential reactions to the procedure and are among the mild, self-limiting side effects commonly described in the literature on dry needling techniques.

Discussion

Regarding the clinical implications of the results, the authors interpret that ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis significantly improves pain, lumbar function, kinesiophobia, and several aspects of quality of life compared to dry needling. Six high-dose sessions (3 pulses of 3 mA for 3 s each) targeting the epineurium of the posterior branches of L3–L5 increased pressure pain threshold and reduced pain intensity, disability, fear of movement, and finger-to-floor distance, with short-term improvements in quality of life and sleep quality. Clinically, these results suggest that this technique can be an effective adjunct to conventional therapy, particularly for patients who do not fully respond to therapeutic exercise, providing meaningful physical and psychological benefits, although no significant effects were observed on anxiety and depression.

Different studies have demonstrated the efficacy of ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis in reducing pain and decreasing disability in musculoskeletal pain conditions such as patellar tendinopathy, epicondylitis, and temporomandibular disorders in the short, medium, and long term9,13. Nevertheless, this is the first study to evaluate the effects of ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis compared to the application of electrical dry needling in non-specific chronic low back pain.

Regarding the results in the pain pressure threshold, significant differences were found between the groups after treatment, and only the ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis group showed a significant improvement throughout the follow-up for active myofascial trigger points. These results could be explained by the additional effects that galvanic current may have when applied intratissue, inducing a controlled local inflammation that favours tissue repair, and produces analgesic effects in the central nervous system9. Recent advances in pain neuroscience suggest that percutaneous electrolysis may also exert effects through descending inhibitory pathways, neuroimmune modulation, and changes in cortical pain representation33,41. By activating peripheral nociceptors and simultaneously inducing a localized inflammatory response, the technique could promote both peripheral tissue regeneration and central desensitization processes42, which could partly explain the observed improvements in pain thresholds and disability. Different studies on percutaneous electrolysis have found effects on the modulation of pain perception43,44, Varela Calero et al.44 concluded that the application of percutaneous electrolysis induces significant short-term improvements in pressure pain threshold in active trigger points of the rectus femoris in patients with patellofemoral pain. However, it should be noted that the electrical dry needling group also improved, as the effects of the mechanical needle stimulation are compounded by the effects of TENS current, minimising the response of nociceptive nerve fibres45. Hernández et al.45 showed that although the application of this technique on the upper trapezius trigger points generated short-term beneficial effects on pain intensity, no significant changes in pressure pain threshold were found in patients with cervical myofascial pain. Which could explain the differences between the groups in the myofascial trigger points in this study.

Regarding self-reported measures and physical outcomes, significant differences were found between the groups in disability after treatment, and in pain and fear of movement during the follow-up. There is no doubt that studies on the application of ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis on tendons show clear benefits in terms of improvement of movement, flexibility, strength, and development of tendinous resistance46,47. However, evidence on the application of percutaneous electrolysis in chronic muscular pathologies is still limited9,43. The nociceptive effects of this therapy48 may have secondarily contributed to the optimization of muscle function, thereby reducing disability and enhancing patients’ physical performance13,49. Moreover, the observed improvements in functionality could also be attributed to a reduction in fear of movement. Indeed, Bordeleau et al.49 reported that decreasing kinesiophobia has positive effects in patients with chronic pain, leading to improvements in both range of motion and functional capacity.

According to different studies, the application of invasive techniques has shown to have positive effects on the quality of life, for example, a study conducted on athletes with myofascial pain syndrome demonstrated that dry needling therapy significantly influences quality of life and well-being, mental health, and vitality50. However, no evidence has been found that specifically links percutaneous electrolysis to an improvement in quality of life and sleep to date.

On the other hand, the absence of significant changes in depression and anxiety after the use of percutaneous electrolysis may be associated with the discomfort related to the application of galvanic current, making the procedure unpleasant for the patient. In fact, the most common adverse effects of percutaneous electrolysis, such as pain during and after the procedure, are similar to those observed in dry needling13,51. In addition, the use of needles as a therapeutic tool may cause a certain degree of apprehension in the patient, and fear of needles or pain could provoke negative emotions in the subject, affecting psychological outcomes51,52.

Despite the rigorous design of this randomized, double-blind, parallel-group trial, several limitations should be acknowledged. The primary limitation is the relatively short follow-up period of one month, which limits conclusions regarding the long-term effects of ultrasound-guided percutaneous intratissue electrolysis. Secondary limitations include the modest sample size, which, although adequately powered for the primary outcome, may reduce the generalizability of findings to broader populations. Additionally, although both evaluators and statisticians were blinded, the treating therapists were not blinded, which could introduce performance bias. Some outcome measures, particularly anxiety and depression, did not show significant changes, suggesting that psychological effects may require longer interventions or combined approaches. Another aspect that deserves consideration is the profitability of the technique. While ultrasound guidance and dedicated electrolysis equipment may increase resource requirements compared to conventional dry needling, the potential for faster clinical improvement and functional recovery may justify the investment in certain healthcare settings. Therefore, future studies should incorporate economic analyses to determine whether additional costs are offset by reduced disability, medication use, or treatment duration.

Future research should explore the long-term efficacy and safety of this intervention, investigate optimal dosing protocols, and evaluate its effects in combination with other conservative treatments. Moreover, studies with larger and more diverse populations are warranted to confirm the generalizability of these findings and to examine potential effects on broader psychological and functional outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that six high-dose sessions of ultrasound-guided percutaneous electrolysis(3 pulses of 3 mA for 3 s each) applied to the proximal epineurium of the posterior branches of L3, L4, and L5 significantly increased pressure pain threshold, and reduced pain intensity, disability, fear of movement, and the finger-to-floor distance in patients with nonspecific chronic low back pain. In addition, short-term improvements were observed in quality of life and certain aspects of sleep quality. However, no significant effects were found on anxiety or depression, suggesting that further research may be needed to evaluate the psychological impact of this intervention.
