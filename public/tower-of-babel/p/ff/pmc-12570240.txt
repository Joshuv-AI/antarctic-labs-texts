Navigating the development of social innovations: design of nature-based interventions in purpose driven SMEs
Authors: Päivi Juuso, Åsa Engström, Ossi Pesämaa, Jeaneth Johansson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite overall improvements in general health, the prevalence of mental illness continues to rise, underscoring the need for innovative approaches to health promotion. This study examines how purpose-driven small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) design and develop nature-based interventions (NBIs) as social innovations to promote mental health. Using a case study approach, the research draws on data from workshops, semi-structured interviews, and expert stakeholder discussions involving 28 SME practitioners, eight public actors from social and healthcare sectors, nine entrepreneurs, and four experienced stakeholders. The analysis reveals that the successful development of NBIs depends on five core dimensions: accessibility, acceptance, affordability, meaningfulness, and knowledge. In addition, the study identifies a range of strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats that SMEs encounter when navigating the complex landscape of NBI implementation. The findings highlight the potential of NBIs to complement traditional healthcare by fostering empowerment and supporting tailored recovery, while also offering strategic insights into the collaboration required for sustainable social innovation.KEYWORDS: Social impact, social innovation, mental illness, nature-based interventions, small and medium sized enterprises

Introduction

Although global health has improved in many respects, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and mental illness have both increased. NCDs can affect people of all ages, regions, and countries. While they are often associated with older age, approximately 18 million deaths occur before the age of 70. According to the WHO [1], NCDs cause more deaths among people under 70 than all other causes combined. Mental illness has also risen worldwide. The term mental illness – or mental health conditions – encompasses mental disorders, psychosocial disabilities, and other states associated with symptoms such as distress, impaired functioning, or risk of self-harm. In 2019, one in eight people globally lived with a mental disorder, with anxiety and depression the most common [2,3].

In this study, we focus on stress-related mental illness, particularly exhaustion disorder, which has become an increasing cause of sick leave [4,5]. Long-term exposure to stress without sufficient opportunities for recovery is the primary cause of exhaustion disorder. Common symptoms include physical and mental fatigue, often combined with depressive symptoms [6]. Traditional treatments for exhaustion disorder include cognitive behavioural therapy, which has been shown to reduce symptoms [7]. However, studies also show that nature-based interventions (NBIs) can effectively complement traditional treatments and support long-term recovery [8,9]. NBIs foster empowerment, expand knowledge, and provide practical strategies to improve daily life [10].

The primary goal of NBIs is to promote health and well-being by supporting recovery and empowerment. They are often delivered by purpose-driven small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) with expertise in nature-based services, typically in restorative outdoor settings such as farms and gardens. In practice, NBIs may include animal-assisted activities (e.g. feeding, caring for, or walking with animals), guided walks in forests, gardening, forest bathing, and other creative tasks in natural environments. These interventions are designed to provide structure, regularity, and opportunities for social interaction in supportive outdoor contexts. SMEs act as both service providers and facilitators of NBIs. They design and deliver activities in authentic work or farm environments, adapt them to participants' needs, and create the social and environmental context in which interventions take place. As such, SMEs play a dual role: on the one hand, they are entrepreneurs who must ensure the financial viability of their services; on the other hand, they are facilitators who provide tailored, health-promoting interventions in collaboration with healthcare professionals [9,11–13].

What is considered meaningful in an NBI varies across individuals. Participants commonly describe activities as meaningful when they support recovery, create a sense of purpose, or help re-establish daily routines. For example, end-users with stress-related exhaustion emphasised the value of simple, non-demanding activities such as walking with animals or gardening, which allowed them to experience calm, build self-confidence, and gradually return to structured routines [12,14,15]. Professionals, by contrast, considered interventions meaningful when they complemented treatment goals, ensured safety, and actively supported recovery processes [11,16].

Interest in using nature as a setting for health promotion and disease prevention is growing in research, practice, and policy [17,18]. Despite this, NBIs have not yet been fully integrated into public health systems in the Circumpolar North, where the field remains immature and emerging. This creates a demand for new social innovations [15]. Social innovations (SIs) aim to address unmet social needs through actions that drive the implementation of new solutions to social challenges. They may involve developing new services, improving existing processes, or adopting novel practices.

This paper focuses on SMEs developing new NBIs tailored to individual needs, with an emphasis on person-centred care models. Developing social innovations through NBIs presents significant challenges for purpose-driven SMEs, which must balance social impact with financial viability to survive and succeed. Previous research highlights the critical role of new collaborations in advancing such innovations. Building on this, the present paper explores the design of NBI service offerings and the development of SIs to promote mental health.

Social innovations aim to increase self-reliance and sustainability in communities and can be defined as ideas that address unmet social needs through collaborative actions and processes that invent and implement new solutions [19,20]. Importantly, SIs are not only about renewal but also about improving existing services.

Framework

Purpose driven social innovations for well-being

Studies reveal that individuals with mental illness face a heightened risk of social exclusion and isolation [21], often compounded by economic challenges that hinder social participation. Mental illness leads to personal suffering [22], loss of competence in organisations, and significant societal costs for healthcare and illness management [23]. Disease prevention and health promotion are crucial for fostering healthy choices and well-being. Aligned with Agenda 2030, particularly Goal 3 and sub-goal 3.4 on mental health, sustainable efforts are essential, as good health underpins individual potential and societal development while influencing and being influenced by economic, ecological, and social factors (Global Goals, [24]).

Health-promoting efforts focus on strengthening the individual's opportunities to experience health and well-being – regardless of whether the person lives with illness or not [25]. Achieving equitable health- and social care, requires addressing lifestyle diseases, stress, and reduced physical activity through disease-preventive and health-promoting efforts [26]. Nature-based interventions (NBI) promote health by providing activities in natural environments tailored to individual’s needs [12]. These interventions leverage restorative environments – such as gardens, parks, forests, and beaches – that foster recovery through stillness and peace without requiring active engagement [27]. Unlike traditional health-promoting efforts often conducted indoors, NBIs take place outdoors, offering new environments that enhance physical, psychological, and emotional well-being [28,29].

Exercise in nature has been shown to reduce stress and improve mental health [30]. NBIs also yield social benefits, particularly for vulnerable groups excluded from the labour market or societal safety nets, and support work integration [29,31]. Additionally, NBIs have demonstrated positive socioeconomic outcomes, including reduced healthcare utilisation for stress-related conditions and cost-effective rehabilitation [13,16]. While various terms describe these interventions, their core lies in utilising nature to promote holistic well-being [12]. Research on NBIs has predominantly focused on their impact on mental illness, with limited attention to how companies organise such solutions [32]. However, Ziegler [33] explored the organisational aspects of NBIs, emphasising their role across the entire innovation chain – from concept to practical application. Mapping of the social innovation reveals challenges such as insufficient funding, expertise, and organisational frameworks. These deficiencies often hinder implementation, while collaboration is seen as potential solutions [15,34].

Design/method

Design

We employed a case study design, which is appropriate when research questions seek to answer “how” or “why” a situation arises, functions, or affects participants while it is ongoing and observable, and when participants have direct experience of it [35,36]. A case study approach was particularly suitable here since the choices and behaviours of participants could not be manipulated without altering the phenomenon under investigation, and because the broader context was expected to shape how the situation was experienced [35].

To capture the complexity of nature-based interventions (NBIs) as a form of social innovation, we combined several methods of data collection. First, we reviewed relevant literature and reports within the field. Second, we organised workshops with different stakeholders, using Ripple-Effect-Mapping (REM) as inspiration. Third, we conducted qualitative semi-structured interviews in pairs, involving both small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and public actors. The use of multiple sources of evidence is recommended for strengthening the comprehensiveness and accuracy of case studies [36], and transparency in how such evidence is collected and analysed is essential [37].

Study setting

The study was conducted in northern Sweden, a region where the implementation of NBIs is still limited but gradually developing. The long, cold winters and short summers influence both the design and feasibility of interventions. Seasonality affects the types of activities that can be offered, the accessibility of intervention sites during harsh weather, and the additional resources required from SMEs to ensure participant safety and comfort. These contextual factors shaped not only the services developed by SMEs but also our methodological considerations related to recruitment, implementation, and interpretation of findings. Northern Sweden also shares many characteristics with other circumpolar regions, including low population density, long travel distances to healthcare services, reliance on natural environments and livelihoods, and elevated mental health challenges linked to isolation and limited access to care. This makes the region a particularly relevant context for studying how NBIs may be developed and implemented as complementary health-promoting services.

Participants and procedure

The recruitment targeted three stakeholder groups: SMEs, public actors, and stakeholders with experience in developing or implementing NBIs. SMEs were eligible if they had prior experience of providing nature-based services. Potential participants were identified through company websites and contacted via email, resulting in 28 SME representatives agreeing to participate (Table 1). Their enterprises included farming, gardening, tourism, and pet daycare. Public actors were eligible if they had experience supporting individuals with mental illness, and eight such participants, working as contact persons in social and healthcare organisations, were recruited via email invitations (Table 1).

Data collection

Different data collections were done by means of workshops, pair interviews and individual interviews. Workshops were conducted on three separate occasions with SMEs (n = 12 + 6 + 10 = 28) and public actors (n = 2 + 3 + 3 = 8). The workshops followed the REM approach, a participatory tool typically used in evaluation settings [38]. REM involves four key elements: appreciative enquiry, participatory group processes, interactive reflection, and mind-mapping. We applied the “theming and rippling” variant of REM [38].

At the start of each workshop, participants were informed of the study aim and the focus of discussions: their general experiences with NBIs, as well as their reflections on a specific NBI programme offered to individuals with mental illness. Participants discussed perceived strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats of NBIs. Following this, participants were divided into smaller mixed groups (including at least one representative from each stakeholder category). These groups used mind maps to document discussion outcomes. Results were then shared with the larger group, where collective reflections were added. In this phase, participants mapped the “ripples”, i.e. effects of NBIs on SMEs, public actors, and end users. Data consisted of recorded group discussions, mind maps, and collective reflections. All workshops were moderated by the research team.

The pair interviews were semi-structured [39] and were conducted with SME–public actor pairs who had collaborated on delivering NBIs. The interviews focused on their experiences of running NBI sessions (one to two times weekly, three hours per session, over five weeks) and included reflections on strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats of the programme for SMEs, public actors, and end users. Four pair interviews were conducted (two face-to-face, two via Zoom), each lasting 60–90 minutes. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Finally, also the stakeholder interviews were semi-structured and conducted digitally with four stakeholders who had experience in NBI implementation. These interviews addressed strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats of using NBIs as a complement to traditional healthcare. Each interview lasted 60–90 minutes and was transcribed verbatim.

Analytical approach

All workshop discussions, mind maps, and interview transcripts were analysed using qualitative content analysis with an inductive approach. Following Corbin and Strauss's [39] microanalysis method, we conducted detailed line-by-line readings of the material. Meaningful units of data were coded and compared across sources, and triangulation was used to identify similarities, differences, and recurring patterns. The analysis proceeded in three stages: (1) synthesis of workshop and interview data into a mind map focusing on Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats (SWOT), (2) development of five core dimensions of NBI offerings, and (3) validation and deepening of these dimensions through stakeholder interviews.

A mind map outlining SMEs development of NBI-services

In the first stage, codes were synthesised into a mind map to visually summarise the main domains of NBI as a form of social innovation (see Figure 1). The analysis was structured as a SWOT framework reflecting SMEs' perspectives on service development. The mind map highlighted several strengths, including the creation of social networks, the reduction of loneliness, improvements in health and well-being, enhanced productivity, and contributions to rural development. At the same time, weaknesses were identified, such as limited strategic frameworks, a shortage of trained personnel, and a lack of educational support. Threats included low awareness of NBIs, restricted prescribing authority among healthcare professionals, and the absence of financing and reimbursement structures. Opportunities were also evident, such as the empowerment of end-users, the use of local competencies, stronger collaboration between SMEs and public actors, the development of new services, contributions to sustainability, and the positioning of NBIs as complementary to traditional care. Overall, the mind map illustrated how SMEs attempt to navigate these conditions when designing strategies and business models for sustainable NBI offerings.

Five core dimensions of NBI design

Further line-by-line analysis across the data revealed five cross-cutting dimensions essential for designing NBI services and developing them as social innovations (see Tables 3 and 4 for illustrative quotations). The first of these was accessibility, as geographical remoteness, limited public transport, and the challenge of arranging participant travel repeatedly emerged as barriers. A second dimension was acceptance, where scepticism among healthcare organisations and professionals, combined with hesitation from some end-users, limited broader adoption. The third dimension concerned affordability, as rigid budget systems within healthcare organisations and the limited financial resources of end-users restricted uptake. The fourth, meaningfulness, highlighted the dual perspectives of professionals and end-users: while professionals valued NBIs that supported clinical progress, end-users emphasised the personal significance of activities such as gardening or animal care, which they described as motivating and enriching. Finally, knowledge emerged as a crucial factor, since a lack of awareness of NBI processes and outcomes hindered implementation, with both healthcare staff and potential users identified as needing more information and training. Together, these dimensions highlighted systemic challenges as well as the conditions under which NBIs could be designed to deliver sustainable value.

Stakeholder validation and deepened insights

In the final stage of analysis, interviews with stakeholders experienced in NBI implementation were used to validate and refine these five dimensions (see Table 5). Their perspectives underscored that accessibility requires evidence-based quality criteria relating to safety, environment, and interactions with animals and nature. They also emphasised that long implementation processes demand strong collaboration across organisational boundaries, with intrapreneurs inside healthcare systems playing a key role in legitimising NBIs. Affordability was described as dependent on SMEs aligning their pricing with both their own costs and the needs of healthcare organisations; however, a mismatch between SME enthusiasm for NBIs and healthcare organisations’ willingness to purchase them was seen as a risk to long-term viability. Meaningfulness was highlighted as requiring attention at multiple levels, from recovery-oriented interventions to more structured rehabilitation programmes. A tiered model, stakeholders argued, could allow SMEs to scale their offerings while ensuring that services remain evidence-based and health-promoting. Finally, knowledge gaps were consistently described as a central barrier, with systematic education of healthcare personnel, SMEs, and end-users identified as necessary to strengthen appropriate use and referrals.

Navigating NBI implementation

Bringing together the SWOT analysis, the five dimensions, and stakeholder validation, we developed a framework for understanding how SMEs navigate the design and implementation of NBI service offerings (see Figure 2). The findings highlight a persistent tension between end-user needs and structural constraints. SMEs must address logistical challenges to ensure accessibility, secure organisational buy-in to enhance acceptance, and develop sustainable financial models to safeguard affordability, while also ensuring that services are both meaningful and underpinned by sufficient knowledge. Collaboration among SMEs, healthcare organisations, and public actors emerged as a critical enabler in this process. However, scepticism among healthcare professionals and limited awareness of the benefits of NBIs continue to present substantial hurdles. Addressing these barriers requires targeted educational initiatives, systematic evidence-building, and stronger integration mechanisms to position NBIs as a legitimate complement to traditional healthcare.

Discussion

Findings from this study highlight the need for SMEs offering social innovations of NBI services to promote mental health to design solutions that are accessible, acceptable, affordable, meaningful, and understandable for both individuals with mental illness and institutional actors, such as healthcare organisations. However, effective communication with key actors who operate under different institutional logics may be challenging in navigating such design processes [40,41]. For example, what is considered affordable for healthcare organisations operating under tight budgets, in terms of both price and quality, may differ from what is feasible for individuals with mental health issues as potential users of NBIs. Similarly, the meaningfulness of activities varies depending on individual preferences. Studies [15,42] show that undemanding environments within NBIs support health and well-being from the end-user perspective, and Gittins et al. [42] further emphasise the necessity of sustainable funding and support for NBI projects to firmly establish their role in therapeutic and preventative care.

With respect to acceptance, one weakness is that healthcare professionals accustomed to indoor care settings may hesitate or resist referring patients to outdoor-based interventions. Bell et al. [43], studying services comparable to NBIs, demonstrated that a lack of evidence acceptable to decision-makers remains a weakness for implementation. Furthermore, if no best practice guidelines or standards are available within healthcare systems, services will not be equally accessible to all. Seasonal variation poses additional challenges in organising NBIs. Weather conditions such as cold, heat, darkness, or inaccessible terrain due to snow and ice can be obstacles to participation. In circumpolar regions, these contextual factors suggest the importance of complementary infrastructure, such as conservatories, to enable year-round interventions. Beyond physical access, other aspects of the natural setting – such as type of nature, safety, and perceived environmental quality – influence participants' willingness to attend [44]. Feeling safe and secure during an NBI is critical, and Johansson et al. [11] demonstrated that safety can be enhanced through the active support of facilitators, who are often the entrepreneurs offering NBI services. This creates further demands on SMEs to design activities adapted to individual needs. Shrestha et al. [44] also showed that immersive nature experiences can be especially beneficial for mental health outcomes but require regular and longer-term engagement from participants. Moreover, knowledge about the significance of the environment, as well as facilitator preparation, has been found to be important factors in fostering user confidence in NBIs [45].

SMEs offering NBIs therefore navigate a complex organisational landscape with significant opportunities for service innovation, but also substantial barriers. In line with Shrestha et al. [44], participatory co-design processes should be adopted to strengthen delivery and ensure that social, political, environmental, and economic dynamics are included in planning. Here, perspectives from implementation science may be particularly valuable, as frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [46] or the RE-AIM framework provide structured approaches for examining contextual factors, stakeholder engagement, and long-term sustainability [47]. Applying such frameworks could help identify mechanisms to integrate NBIs more systematically into healthcare systems, particularly in rural and circumpolar contexts.

Suggestions for future research

Our study has limitations, and we therefore propose five directions for future research to complement our findings. First, we did not examine the direct health effects of NBIs. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess the long-term health, social, and economic outcomes of NBI interventions. Such research would provide robust evidence to support the integration of NBIs as complementary treatments within healthcare systems. Second, to improve generalisability, comparative studies that examine NBIs alongside traditional healthcare approaches could help clarify the unique contributions of NBIs, particularly in relation to mental health and well-being. Third, our study did not investigate the dynamics of collaboration. Future research should therefore explore effective frameworks for partnerships between SMEs, healthcare organisations, and public actors. Understanding how such collaborations can bridge organisational silos and facilitate resource sharing will be crucial for successful implementation. Fourth, research should examine how SMEs can scale NBI services while maintaining affordability and accessibility. This includes developing sustainability frameworks that integrate environmental, social, and economic dimensions. Finally, further studies should identify effective strategies to address knowledge gaps among healthcare professionals and policymakers. Potential areas include methods for knowledge dissemination, training, and awareness-building to enhance the legitimacy and adoption of NBIs.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlights the need for purposeful SMEs to navigate the critical dimensions of accessibility, acceptance, affordability, meaningfulness, and knowledge in developing NBI services as social innovations. The findings align with broader discussions on social impact and social innovation, particularly the role of social ventures and hybrid organisations in addressing sustainability challenges. By focusing on sustainable value creation and managing complex stakeholder dynamics, SMEs offering NBI services can contribute to long-term, impactful solutions for mental health and well-being. Addressing the identified barriers through collaboration and further research will be essential to unlock the full potential of NBI as a meaningful and sustainable approach to care. These insights are especially relevant in circumpolar regions, where geographical distances, harsh climates, and limited access to traditional healthcare create both challenges and opportunities for the development of innovative, nature-based health services.
