Antenatal care service readiness and associated factors in health facilities across Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal: a comparative analysis
Authors: Mohammad Wazedul Islam, Mosiur Rahman, Mst. Sadia Afrin, Md. Kamal Hossain, Md. Nuruzzaman Haque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough antenatal care (ANC) is one of the key interventions for maternal and newborn health, simply providing ANC does not mean that facilities will be ready to provide quality care. Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal made progress in maternal health services but with much variation across the three countries as they have differing degrees of fragility of their health systems, governance and geography and service-delivery arrangements. However, comparative evidence using standardized facility-readiness indicators is scarce. This study assessed and compared ANC service readiness and its associated facility-level factors in health facilities across these three countries.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of nationally representative cross-sectional health facility survey data on ANC readiness indicators: Afghanistan Service Provision Assessment (2018–2019), Bangladesh Health Facility Survey (2017) and Nepal Health Facility Survey (2021). In the analytical sample, 95 facilities in Afghanistan, 1,495 in Bangladesh and 1,494 in Nepal provided ANC. The primary outcome was an equally weighted composite ANC readiness score ranging from 0 to 20, where higher scores indicate greater readiness to provide ANC. Negative binomial regression models including adjustments for complex survey design were fitted in each country. Findings are interpreted as country-specific survey-period estimates rather than pooled causal country effects, because surveys were carried out in different years and situations vary across health-systems.ResultsThe mean ANC readiness scores were Afghanistan (11.90 ± 3.81), Nepal (11.46 ± 2.50), and Bangladesh (9.87 ± 2.97). All these countries consistently showed that availability of ANC- providers and better basic amenities predict an increase in readiness. Facilities charging fixed user fees also showed higher readiness in Bangladesh (Incidence rate ratio [IRR] = 1.07) and Nepal (IRR = 1.07). In Afghanistan, public facilities had lower readiness (IRR = 0.56) than private, while government funding improved it (IRR = 1.35). Readiness also varied significantly by province. In Bangladesh, external supervision (IRR = 1.12) and client opinion review (IRR = 1.05) enhanced readiness, but compared with non-government organization clinics/hospitals, all other facility type demonstrated significantly lower readiness. In Nepal, primary health care centers were more ready than federal hospitals (IRR = 1.12), and routine quality assurance (IRR = 1.04) and management meetings (IRR = 1.05) positively influenced readiness.ConclusionANC-trained providers and basic amenities were common determinants of readiness in all three countries: however, context-determined the influence of managing authority, funding, supervision and facility type. Findings should be interpreted in context of the differing survey years and health-system settings. Context-specific interventions are needed to strengthen ANC readiness for better maternal health service delivery outcomes in South Asia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14928-x.

Introduction

Maternal mortality is a global public health priority and an important performance measure of health systems. While the global burden of maternal mortality is declining, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including south-Asian ones, still experience an unacceptably high rate of preventable maternal deaths along with poor access to quality care [1]. Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal have achieved important gains in maternal health in the past two decades but face a variety of constraints due to poor infrastructure, workforce shortages, weak diagnostic capacity, and facility non-readiness [2–4]. Hence, to achieve sustainable development goals (SDG) 3.1 on maternal health it is essential not only to increase coverage of maternal health services but also ensuring that health facilities are adequately ready to provide comprehensive and high-quality antenatal care (ANC) [5].

ANC stands out as a major nugget in the continuum of maternal-and-newborn health. It is recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) that the schedule ANC contacts should be at least eight for maximum outcome in pregnancy and lowered risk at birth for mothers and newborns [6]. However, the extent of effectiveness of ANC directly hinges on the readiness of health facilities to supply the essential services. This will include trained personnel; infrastructure; equipment; diagnostic tools; medicines and clinical guidelines [7]. These items, in fact, stand as irreplaceable requisites for effective authorization of ANC contact with health outcomes.

A variety of initiatives have been taken in Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal to strengthen maternal health systems, yet important challenges remain. The health system in Afghanistan has been greatly affected by ongoing conflict, political instability, and underinvestment, leading to inconsistent availability and quality of services [8]. Bangladesh has made significant gains through its community clinic network and expanded maternal health programs; however, disparities in access and quality persist across the regions, particularly in rural areas [9]. Nepal has improved service coverage and institutional deliveries following health sector decentralization reforms, but geographical and infrastructural barriers continue to limit access to quality care in many mountainous and remote areas [10].

This study specifically addresses the concern that women may attend facilities that offer ANC, but lack the minimum inputs required for quality service delivery. Facility readiness in the three countries may be influenced by very different health-system contexts: conflict-related disruption and inability to deliver services without contractors or external support in Afghanistan; rapid expansion of frontline and community-based facilities without sufficient infrastructures such as equipment, diagnostics, trained providers, and infection-prevention resources in Bangladesh; and decentralization, challenging geography and disaster vulnerability in Nepal. Thus, a cross-country comparative analysis based on standardized readiness indicators are warranted to identify common ANC readiness gaps from country-specific health-system constraints.

Facility-level factors such as managing authority (public or private), location (urban or rural), type of facility, availability of ANC care providers, and administrative practices like routine supervision and quality assurance are important determinants of service readiness [11]. Public facilities are often limited in resources while private facilities often have less regulation and are known to show aberration in quality and consistency of care [12]. In addition to inter-state or intra-state variations, workforce distribution, and cybernetic or external support from the donor or government sources can also impact the entire establishment’s capability to deliver effective essential maternal services [13].

There has been increased recognition internationally and regionally of the need for health facilities to be ready to deliver quality ANC, but comparative research across countries using standardized tools remains limited. Much of the literature is country-specific or based on broader multi-country samples. Service Provision Assessment (SPA) surveys provide a useful data source for this analysis. Undertaken by national authorities in collaboration with international agencies, SPA-type surveys collect standardized information on facility infrastructure, staffing, supplies, and readiness for service domains including maternal health and ANC [14, 15]. With standardized methodologies, these surveys support cautious cross-country comparisons of common readiness indicators.

The present study uses the recent SPA data collected from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal in evaluating differences in maternal health service readiness in health facilities with particular focus on ANC. The study also identifies facility- and system-level characteristics associated with readiness in the respective three countries. A better understanding of these factors is fundamental to informing national health strategies, guiding resource allocation, and highlighting good practices scaled or adapted with similar settings. This study augments the body of evidence on health systems strengthening and quality maternal care in LMICs. Based on identifying key gaps and enabling factors, this research will support creating more targeted interventions to reduce maternal mortality and achieve universal health coverage (UHC). Ultimately, it is necessary to guarantee that every woman-whoever she is, be it her socioeconomic or geographies-has access to quality maternal health services for health equity and sustainable development.

Methods

Study design and setting

This paper used a secondary cross-sectional comparative design based on nationally representative health facility survey data from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal. The analysis here did not involve collecting any new primary data. These three countries were selected, because they all faced similar challenges of service delivery for maternal-health in South Asian LMICs; however, their health-system contexts differ—to represent regional variation, with Afghanistan facing fragile and conflict-affected service delivery versus Bangladesh as a large pluralistic public–private/NGO system versus Nepal with decentralized geographically constrained system. The analysis compares patterns of ANC readiness with common WHO-SARA tracer indicators; therefore, we do not assume that the countries are alike nor that one country’s findings would be directly generalized to another. To facilitate cross-country comparison of availability and readiness service delivery elements, surveys were harmonized using the WHO -Service Availability and Readiness Assessment (SARA) framework [15].

Data sources

All three countries were represented in the research by publicly available data from nationally representative surveys of health facilities. For Afghanistan, data came from the Afghanistan Service Provision Assessment (AfSPA) 2018–2019 implemented by the Afghanistan Ministry of Public Health in partnership with ICF. The survey was conducted through 142 health facilities throughout seven provinces including hospitals, clinics and health posts in urban and rural settings. It gathered data on infrastructure, equipment, training of health providers, availability of medicines and maternal and child healthcare service delivery [16].

In Bangladesh, data source was the Bangladesh Health Facility Survey (BHFS) 2017 conducted by the National Institute of Population Research and Training (NIPORT), Associates for Community and Population Research (ACPR) and ICF with a funding support from the United States Agency for International Development (USAID). A nationwide survey is conducted in 1,524 health facilities consisting of public, private and NGO-run facilities at different levels of the health system with information collected on ANC guidelines, equipment, diagnostic capacity, medicines and commodities for ANC were majorly recorded some infection-prevention resources [9].

For Nepal, data sources were from the Nepal Health Facility Survey (NHFS) 2021 conducted by Ministry of Health and Population of Nepal, New ERA & ICF. This NHFS, which interviewed 1,576 health facilities across all seven provinces and facility types (health posts/primary health care centers/district/provincial/federal/private hospitals), included indicators on availability and readiness of facility-based maternal and newborn health services, including ANC, using a standardized framework for facility-assessment [10].

All three surveys used structured questionnaires administered to facility managers and health providers, direct observation for equipment and medicine availability, and reviews of facility records. Data collection teams were rigorously trained to assure consistency and data quality. These data sources, publicly accessible through the Demography and Health Survey (DHS) Program, provide a strong basis for comparing ANC service readiness and determinants among Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal.

Survey years vary as the analysis used the most recent publicly available facility survey for each country containing the necessary ANC readiness indicators at the time of analysis. Hence, differences observed should be seen not as contemporaneous countries’ effects but rather as differences between survey periods specific to each country. Taken together, these data-collection years were considered during interpretation and are acknowledged as a limitation.

Study sample

The analysis was restricted to health facilities that indicated providing ANC services in the respective survey years. We excluded facilities that either did not provide ANC or lacked the data required to compute the readiness score from our analytic samples, yielding Afghanistan: 95 facilities; Bangladesh: 1,495 facilities; Nepal: 1,494 facilities (Fig. 1). This has resulted in uneven sample sizes that do not equal sampling fractions between countries as the national surveys used independent sampling frames, and survey designs and numbers of ANC-providing facilities captured in each national survey. Providers were both public and private, as well as non-governmental facilities. Facility types were also broadly defined (hospital/clinic-level facilities, primary/frontline facilities and standalone/other), but the specific facility-type categories differ between countries, so these variables remain unmerged and were indeed kept in their country-specific form for the regression models.

Measures

Outcome variable: ANC readiness score

ANC readiness, the main outcome variable, was determined based on the WHO-SARA framework. We constructed the score based on 20 harmonized tracer items from five domains - (i) guidelines and trained staff: ANC guideline availability and at least one provider trained in ANC in the past 24 months, (ii) basic equipment: apparatus for measuring blood pressure, stethoscope to listen to lungs and heart sounds, adult weighing scale; fetal stethoscope and measuring tape; examination bed/couch (iii) diagnostics: capacity to perform hemoglobin tests, urine protein tests, urine glucose tests (iv) medicines and commodities: iron tablets (including combining agents), folic acid tablets (including combined agents), combined iron-folic acid tablets (v) ANC service-provision/infection-prevention resources: soap (liquid or bar), running water (piped/bottled borehole/spring/river); disinfectant (disinfecting hand rub), latex gloves, alcohol-based hand rub; waste receptacle. A coding of 1 was used to indicate when each item was available, while a coding of 0 indicated inability to provide that item. An overall score was calculated from the sum of equally weighted items; this total ranged from 0 to 20, with higher scores reflecting a greater readiness to provide ANC services [7, 15].

We only retained tracer items that were available and conceptually equivalent in all three surveys to facilitate comparability of measurement across datasets. Variable definitions and coding were harmonized prior to score construction. No formal measurement-invariance testing was conducted since the readiness score is a summative inventory of observed resources rather than a latent scale, although harmonization was used to ensure that each country-specific score reflects comparable tracer-item definitions and the same concept of service-readiness.

Explanatory variables

This study examined a wide range of facility-level independent variables to identify predictors of ANC service readiness in three countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal. These variables were chosen according to the WHO-SARA framework, the availability of conceptually comparable variables across surveys, and evidence from previous facility-readiness studies [7, 13, 15]. Because the health-system structures and facility nomenclature differ among Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal, the facility type was categorized according to country-specific service delivery levels. Thus, country-specific facility type was retained in the main models, with broad patterns interpreted, rather than assuming that facility labels are exactly equivalent across countries.

The managing authority of health facilities was classified as either public or private. Managing authority means ownership or administrative body governing facility. This variable is different from the revenue-source variable, which indicates whether a facility reported government revenue/ support or other/non-government sources. In some cases, especially in Afghanistan, government or publicly funded support of private or contracting based facilities, so public ownership and government finance should not necessarily be considered the same thing. Both variables are included to allow for the differentiation between administrative authority and financial support [17, 18].

External supervision visits and routine quality assurance activities were included as managerial factors that reflect oversight and quality improvement efforts known to enhance facility readiness and adherence to clinical guidelines [12, 19]. Similarly, management meetings and client feedback review mechanisms were assessed as indicators of facility governance and responsiveness, which contribute to improving service quality [20]. User fee structures were examined to understand how financial policies affect resource availability and accessibility. Facilities charging fees might have more funds to support service readiness, but on the other hand, might limit access for the disadvantaged populations [21, 22].

The availability of 24-hour services and inpatient capacity served as proxies for a facility’s operational capacity to manage emergencies and comprehensive maternal care [23]. Facilities offering continuous service and inpatient care are generally better equipped to provide quality ANC and manage complications. Human resources at facilities were captured by the number of ANC-trained providers, which is a critical determinant of quality ANC service delivery [12, 24]. We also considered the availability of basic amenities, such as electricity, water, sanitation, and privacy, as important infrastructural elements for safe and respectful maternal care [24, 25]. Statistical analysis

Background characteristics of health facilities in Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages, continuous variables calculated using means and standard deviations. This ANC readiness score was a non-negative integer ranging from 0 to 20. Count-model approaches were thus explored for estimating the association between facility-level characteristics and expected readiness score. Negative binomial regression was chosen because it allows for extra-Poisson heterogeneity across the facilities and is more flexible than a standard Poisson model in the presence of dispersion. Distributional diagnostics were investigated per country, including both mean-variance patterns and model‐based dispersion checks before further modeling. We also note that the readiness score is both artificially truncated at 20 and a composite score (not an unbounded count of independent events); hence, incidence rate ratios [IRRs] should be interpreted as relative differences in anticipated readiness scores rather than as incidence rates of independent events [26, 27]. The IRRs are differences in the expected composite readiness score associated with facility characteristics and do not measure structural readiness directly. Consequently, the regression results were viewed in conjunction with the descriptive tracer-item and domain-score tables that reflect specific structural readiness elements such as guidelines, trained staff, diagnostics, medicines and commodities, and service-provision resources.

We did not pool all facilities in one model because of the heterogeneous nature of facilities, unequal sample sizes and different survey years, therefore each country-based models were fitted separately for Afghanistan, Bangladesh & Nepal. Sampling weights provided in the survey datasets were used to adjust for unequal probabilities of selection and improve national representativeness. However, we did not adjust for primary sampling units (PSUs) or stratification because there was no consistent identifiable PSU/cluster and strata variable using the public datasets that were harmonized in this analysis. Thus, for the purpose of adjusting the estimates to account for the complex survey design, only available sampling weights were applied and were taken into consideration when interpreting statistical uncertainty. Statistical significance was evaluated at p < 0.05 and results are showed as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Since facility-type categories were not the same among countries, the original country-specific facility classifications were maintained for the primary analysis. This retains policy-relevant types within a health system but reduces the possibility for direct comparisons of facility type across countries. Cross-country findings were cautiously interpreted, and we attributed to common threads in widely relevant predictors, like trained ANC providers or availability of basic amenities.

We performed a sensitivity analysis using a harmonized binary facility-type variable to examine country-specific differences in facility-classification. Within each country, facility types were reclassified as hospital-level facilities or non-hospital/primary-level facilities. Hospital-level facilities (regional; national; provincial: special; and private hospitals in Afghanistan, NGO hospitals; private Hospitals; and District/upazila public facilities in Bangladesh; federal-province-local-private Hospitals in Nepal). Non-hospital facilities included private clinics in Afghanistan; community clinics, union-level public facilities, and maternal and child welfare center in Bangladesh; primary health care centers, health posts, community health units, urban health centers (UHC), and standalone HTC facility in Nepal. Finally, the negative binomial models were re-estimated as follows using this harmonized classification. The results are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Ethical considerations

We used publicly available, de-identified facility survey datasets from the DHS Program. The original surveys were ethically approved by relevant national authorities and the ICF Institutional Review Board, and respondents from facilities provided informed consent when data were collected. No individual-level identifiers were used for this secondary analysis and the analysis also received exemption from ethical review by the Ethical Review Board of the Institute of Biological Sciences, University of Rajshahi, Bangladesh [No. 293(13)/320/IAMEBBC/IBSc].

Most ANC-providing facilities in the analytic sample of Afghanistan were in urban areas; as only one Afghan facility remained in the analytic sample that was representative of rural facilities, findings comparing urban to rural facilities should be interpreted with caution. Facilities in Bangladesh were mostly rural, and over half of those in Nepal were urban (Table 1). Management Authority varied too: Afghanistan was dominated by privately managed facilities, whereas public facilities predominated in Bangladesh and Nepal. In Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal 24.8%, 14.8% and 23.6% facilities reported routine quality assurance activities respectively. Bangladesh and Nepal had more external supervision (92.6% and 94.4% respectively) than did Afghanistan (74.1%). With respect to user fees, 56.1% of Bangladeshi facilities and 74.7% of Nepali facilities had no user fees, while in Afghanistan, separate fees were reported by 79.5% of Afghan facilities. The availability of ANC trained staff and guidelines were limited across the three countries: in Afghanistan, only 17.6% of the facilities had ANC guidelines and 33.7% had trained ANC staff. Bangladesh had the highest availability of ANC guidelines (46.4%), although trained staff availability remained low (28.1%). Nepal had the lowest availability of both guidelines (10.6%) and trained staff (27.5%).

Core ANC equipment was relatively available in all three countries, such as blood pressure apparatus and stethoscopes, which exceeded 85% coverage in each setting. However, some equipment showed substantial cross-country variation. For example, fetal stethoscopes were widely available in Afghanistan (80.0%) and Nepal (87.5%) but were almost absent in Bangladesh (3.8%). Diagnostic capacity was also limited, although Afghanistan had comparatively higher availability of hemoglobin testing (46.6%), urine protein testing (46.6%), and urine glucose testing (41.5%) than Bangladesh and Nepal.

In terms of ANC-related medicines and commodities, combined iron and folic acid tablets were common in Nepal (90.8%) and Bangladesh (82.2%), but less available in Afghanistan (57.9%). Basic service and infection-prevention supply also differed by country. Nepal showed high availability of latex gloves (93.9%) and alcohol-based hand rub (90.6%), while Bangladesh had relatively low availability of disinfectant (38.1%), alcohol-based hand rub (22.5%), and waste receptacles (32.7%). Overall, the mean ANC readiness score was highest in Afghanistan (11.90 ± 3.81), followed by Nepal (11.46 ± 2.50), and lowest in Bangladesh (9.87 ± 2.97).

Sensitivity analysis using harmonized facility type

Sensitivity analysis using a harmonized hospital versus non-hospital facility classification is presented in Supplementary Table 2. The mean ANC readiness scores were higher among hospital-level facilities compared to non-hospital facilities in Afghanistan [12.49 v 11.47], Bangladesh [13.49 v 9.67] and Nepal [12.73 v 11.32] (Table S1). In the adjusted negative binomial models, however, hospital-level classification was not significantly associated with ANC readiness in Afghanistan (IRR = 1.10; 95% CI: 0.97–1.24), Bangladesh (IRR = 0.97; 95% CI: 0.91–1.03) or Nepal (IRR = 0.95, 95% CI:89 − 1·01). These results indicate that crude differences between hospital or non-hospital facilities were at least in part economically explained by other facility level characteristics included in the adjusted models.

Discussion

Variation in ANC readiness across countries and survey periods

This study highlighted the substantial heterogeneity of ANC service readiness in Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal during their respective survey periods. The mean readiness score was similar for Afghanistan and Nepal but higher than the score for Bangladesh; nevertheless, these differences should not be viewed as strictly attributable to country effects because different survey years were used, and the three countries have divergent health-system, geographic, and governance contexts. Consequently, the results boast evidence of ANC readiness during survey periods relative to each other rather than a ranking of pooled national health systems.

For country specific determinants, Afghanistan had lower readiness for the majority of provinces when compared with Balkh province, and public facilities demonstrated lower readiness than private facilities, whilst government revenue/support showed a positive association with propensity for readiness. In Bangladesh, external supervision and review of client voice were associated with higher readiness but this was not the case for community clinics nor other public facility levels as compared to NGO clinics/hospitals. Routine quality assurance and management meetings were associated with higher readiness in Nepal; primary health acre centers had higher readiness than federal level hospitals. These trends underscore the importance of country-specific policy response as opposed to a one-size-fits-all regional intervention model.

In terms of common determinants, the number of ANC-trained providers and the availability basic amenities were positively related to readiness in all three countries. These trends indicate that gains in ANC readiness observed across South Asian LMIC settings may be linked to the nature of investments in trained human resources, water, electricity, sanitation, privacy and infection-prevention infrastructure.

Sensitivity analyses comparing a harmonized hospital vs. non-hospital classification also revealed that while crude mean readiness scores for hospitals were higher than for non-hospitals, the adjusted hospital/non-hospital differences were not statistically significant, indicating that broad facility type alone did not independently influence ANC readiness after adjusting for other facility-level characteristics.

Novelty

This study provides new evidence on ANC service readiness in LMICs — following existing work which has largely focused on single country or broader LMIC samples. Previous work has documented gaps in facility-readiness (including, e.g., the availability of trained providers and essential commodities as well as basic amenities) for LMIC settings including Bangladesh, Nepal and elsewhere [7, 9, 13]. In contrast the current study uses a consistent WHO-SARA approach to select tracer-items in Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal distinguishing between shared determinants and context-specific factors. Commonly recommended ANC readiness inputs are assessed for direct comparability across countries, including availability of ANC guidelines and trained health staff, diagnostic capacity, medicines and commodities, and service-provision/infection-prevention resources. This enhances comparability without recognizing that facility type-structures are different from country to country.

Standardized indicators of readiness and robust regression modeling increase the validity and comparability of findings. Furthermore, the study identifies both universal and context-specific determinants, thus contributing to the evidence base to inform policy and programming targeted at improving ANC quality in varied health system environments. The findings add to the growing body of literature on readiness of health systems for maternal care and the importance of context-specific strategies in achieving SDG 3.1 on maternal health.

Comparison to other studies

This study’s findings corroborate and build upon existing literature on health facility readiness for ANC in LMICs, especially for the South Asian context. Like previous research, this study has shown that the availability of trained providers and infrastructure is crucial for the capacity of health facilities to provide quality maternal health services. For example, human resources, particularly the presence of skilled staff, are noted by studies [7, 12] to be key determinants of facility readiness and quality of care. In the same vein, systematic review and meta-analysis [28] assert that basic amenities such as water and sanitation are vital and are also found in our study to have positive associations with ANC readiness scores.

Our findings regarding facility type and managing authority resonate across several contextual settings. The higher readiness of hospitals and private facilities in Afghanistan mirrors findings of studies from Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia that show that better-equipped hospitals generally outperform primary care centers in regard readiness for service delivery because of greater availability of resources and specialized staff [17]. Yet, with a contrast to Bangladesh and Nepal, private management was almost exclusively represented in Afghanistan. This finding illustrates how governance models within health systems determine the shape of service delivery and allocation of resources, as has been affirmed by a study in LMICs 20 demonstrating this variability regarding ownership structures on readiness and service quality.

The role of external supervision and routine quality assurance in Bangladesh and Nepal aligns with the growing body of evidence supporting managerial oversight as a driver of service quality. For example, studies [18, 25] showed that regular visits for supervision and management meetings support compliance with clinical guidelines and improvement in facility readiness. The positive association of client feedback mechanisms with readiness, in the case of Bangladesh, finds support from another study involving LMICs [23], arguing that patient-centered feedback loops enhance responsiveness and quality.

Interestingly, this study highlights within-country geographic disparities, which have been well-documented in the literature. Lower readiness scores in certain provinces of Afghanistan and the relative urban-rural balance in Nepal, as noted in studies [3, 4] regarding infrastructural and workforce challenges disproportionately impacting rural and conflict-affected areas, are resonant with this study’s findings. Concentration of urban private facilities in Afghanistan, as noted in this study, concurs with a study conducted in Afghanistan [8] that describes the fragmentation of Afghanistan’s health system with an urban bias in service provision.

The relatively low availability in neonatal ANC guidelines and trained staff in Nepal, despite the country’s health sector decentralization reforms, which point to persistent challenges in human resource capacity and guideline dissemination, especially in rural and mountainous areas. In Bangladesh, the lower readiness of community health clinics discovered in this study reflects the increasing concern expressed in another study conducted in Bangladesh [29] about the capacity constraints facing frontline facilities, especially in the rural context, notwithstanding its extensive community health infrastructure.

Concerning medicines and diagnostics, our findings are compatible with earlier findings indicating that there is an equitable availability of essential commodities in LMICs [7, 9]. Availability of iron and folic acid supplements was found to be better in Nepal and Bangladesh as compared to Afghanistan, which can be attributed to the variations in supply chain robustness, and implementation of national maternal nutrition or ANC programs [9–10, 16]. The constrained access to diagnostic tests such as hemoglobin and urine analysis in Bangladesh and Nepal, however, stands in contrast to the much wider availability in Afghanistan. This may mean prioritizing certain services despite much broader systemic challenges.

In addition, the differential impact of user fee policies in Bangladesh and Nepal enhances the discussion on health financing and access within LMICs. Our finding of higher readiness among facilities charging fixed fees supports the view of the study conducted in Ghana [17] that such fees might bolster resource availability but must be weighed against global concerns regarding impediments to access.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study has several important strengths. Firstly, it uses nationally representative health facility data from three SA countries- Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal- and robustly comparative cross-country results can be drawn from standardized and validated SPA surveys. The harmonized methodology across countries as per the WHO’s SARA framework further supports consistency in measuring ANC service readiness and determinants and enhances the validity and comparability of findings. Secondly, the very large sample of surveyed facilities of various facility types that differ on management authority and geographical settings enriches the generalizability of results within each country. A number of facility-level determinants, including infrastructure, human resources, management practices, and financing mechanisms, could capture a full understanding of the factors affecting ANC readiness.

Thirdly, the negative binomial regression models utilize the advantages of being able to address over-dispersion in the data, which provides more reliable and unbiased estimates of associations than can be achieved with simpler modeling approaches. Increased confidence is gained in locating common as well as context-specific determinants for targeted interventions.

Finally, the findings provided evidence to health systems strengthening efforts in LMICs, more so in fragile and resource-constrained places such as Afghanistan, besides providing contextual comparisons toward guiding regional policymakers and global initiatives in maternal health.

Limitations

However, some limitations to consider are present despite these strengths. First, a limitation of this cross-sectional design is that it restricts causal inference with respect to facility characteristics and readiness scores. Second, secondary facility survey data limited variables to those that were consistently collected across the surveys; key contextual factors such as provider motivation, patient experience, demand-side barriers and local security/disaster conditions could not be fully quantified. Third, the survey years were not identical across countries (Bangladesh 2017; Afghanistan 2018–19; Nepal 2021). As a result, differences observed may reflect both differences in country-context variables and changes over time in the health system of countries; hence our findings should be interpreted as survey-period effects specific to each individual country rather than as contemporaneous effect by country.

Fourth, while this study examined readiness to provide ANC, it did not assess clinical quality of care, patient outcomes or how services were utilized. Readiness to deliver care is necessary but not sufficient; future research should relate facility readiness with measured quality of care and maternal and newborn outcomes.

Fifth, the unequal sample sizes and different facility-type structures preclude direct cross-country generalization. Because of country-specific survey frame and exclusion criteria, Afghanistan contributed fewer ANC-providing facilities than Bangladesh and Nepal, with just a single rural Afghan facility retained in the analytic sample and thereby rendering rural-urban comparisons for Afghanistan meaningless. To reflect these limitations, we employed country-specific models and controlled for type of facility and managing authority instead of assuming a single pooled model. Sixth, although sampling weights were applied, PSU/cluster and stratification variables were not consistently available across the harmonized public datasets; thus, variance estimation was unable to consider all complex survey-design features.

Seventh, although we used a harmonized hospital versus non-hospital classification for the sensitivity analysis, some residual non-equivalence may still exist because facility labels and service capacity are not perfectly comparable across all national survey systems. Lastly, the ANC readiness score is a bounded composite score based on related tracer items rather than an unbounded event count of the negative binomial model. Hence, IRRs ought to be interpreted cautiously as associations with the anticipated readiness score rather than incidence rates of independent events.

Conclusion

This comparative analysis of ANC service readiness in health facilities across Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal identifies both common measures but also determinants unique to the local context that likely impacts readiness at each survey time point. We found that for all three countries trained ANC providers and basic amenities consistently remained associated with higher readiness, suggesting the fundamental need to invest in human-resource supply and basic infrastructure. In contrast, facility ownership, funding sources, supervision, quality assurance and management practices varied across countries as did the type of facilities. Since these surveys were undertaken in different years and the countries exist in different health-system contexts, the findings from this work should be used as evidence to inform context specific strengthening of ANC readiness rather than direct pooled rankings. The availability of high-quality ANC for all women regardless of their socioeconomic or geographic background is still important for improving maternal and newborn health outcomes in South Asia.

Implications of policies

These results are important for policy in Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Nepal to improve ANC readiness. Firstly, resource allocation must be country-specific and based on results from the surveys that identify low-performing provinces, regions and frontline facility types. Ministries and partners also need to support those who work in ANC by creating training opportunities, improving retention rates and distributing skilled providers on the ground equitably. Third, Governance practices previously associated with readiness in Bangladesh and Nepal, such as supportive supervision, quality assurance, management meetings and client feedback mechanisms should be institutionalized and adapted to other settings. Fourth, user-fee policies need to be carefully monitored: fees may support facility resources but should not prevent poor or disadvantaged women from accessing care. Finally, because Afghanistan’s results indicate that the relationship between managing authority and government revenue/support is complex, public-private partnerships and contracted service arrangements should be coupled with meaningful accountability, regulation and quality standards.
