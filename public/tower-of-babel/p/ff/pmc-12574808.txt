Clinicopathological Features by Differentiation and Prognostic Factors in Anal Canal Carcinoma
Authors: Hajime Kayano, Seiichiro Yamamoto, Yasumitsu Saiki, Kazutaka Yamada, Kenichi Sugihara, Yoichi Ajioka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: In this study, we aimed to present the clinicopathological features of anal canal carcinomas based on differentiation and clarify whether differentiation affects prognosis. Methods: We included 597 patients with adenocarcinoma registered in the multicenter colorectal cancer registry data of the Colorectal Cancer Study Group. We also included 151 patients with squamous cell carcinoma enrolled by the 47 medical affiliates of the Colorectal Cancer Study Group in a multicenter cohort study conducted in Japan. The clinicopathological features by differentiation and prognostic factors of each histological type were examined retrospectively. Results: The clinicopathological features of adenocarcinoma were similar to those of rectal carcinoma by differentiation. In contrast, the clinicopathological features of squamous cell carcinoma did not differ according to differentiation. Differentiation was a prognostic factor in patients with adenocarcinoma (G3/4; hazard ratio: 1.91, 95% confidence interval: 1.22-3.01, p=0.0043) but not in patients with squamous cell carcinoma. Conclusions: Differentiation was a prognostic factor for adenocarcinoma in anal canal carcinoma; thus, differentiation should be considered in the treatment strategy. However, differentiation was not a prognostic factor in squamous cell carcinoma. Further studies should explore the involvement of differentiation in squamous cell carcinoma treatment. Keywords: anal canal carcinoma, differentiation, clinicopathological features, adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma

Introduction

Anal canal carcinoma (ACC) is a rare condition accounting for 1-2.6% of gastrointestinal cancers and 2-4% of anal and colorectal cancers[1]; however, its incidence is increasing globally. Histologically, the anal canal has a rectal mucosa, composed of columnar epithelium; the transitional zone epithelium is composed of columnar, cuboidal, and stratified squamous epithelia; and the anal epithelium is composed of stratified squamous epithelium. Although the anal canal is a short luminal organ, ACCs arising from it have diverse histology. Although most ACCs are squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), adenocarcinomas (ACs) are more common in some areas of the anal canal[2]. Contrarily, differentiation used for histological diagnosis is based on the degree of morphological deviation from normal structures or benign tumors, thereby describing the biological nature of the tumor and the characteristics of the cancer[3]. Differentiation correlates with tumor grade and has been associated with the prognosis of patients with malignant tumors arising from various organs, including gastrointestinal[3], respiratory[4], and male[5] and female[6] organ malignancies. Similarly, differentiation is prognostically relevant in colorectal cancer[7]. However, ACCs are rare and can be divided into two major histological subtypes, ACs and SCCs. To date, very few studies have investigated the clinicopathological features of ACCs by differentiation in a sufficient number of cases and the relationship between differentiation and prognosis. Hence, in this retrospective study, we aimed to divide ACCs into ACs and SCCs, present the clinicopathological features by grade, and characterize the effect of differentiation on ACC prognosis. Furthermore, we aimed to identify prognostic factors for each histological type of ACC.

Methods

Patient selection

For ACs, patients enrolled in the Multi-Institutional Registry of Large Bowel Cancer project of the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum (JSCCR) were included. Between 1991 and 2011, 908 patients were diagnosed with ACs. Of these, 16 cases of unknown differentiation; 118 of unknown tumor diameter; 60 of unknown depth and Tis, 18 of unknown lymph node metastasis, and 3 of unknown vascular invasion; 77 of local resection; 18 of unknown curability; and 1 of unknown progression were excluded. Overall, 597 patients with stages I-IV cancer who underwent radical resection with lymph node dissection were included (Figure 1A). Patients with SCCs were enrolled from 47 centers in Japan that are medically affiliated with the JSCCR. Overall, 435 cases of SCC diagnosed between 1991 and 2015 were included in the study. Of these, 14 cases of basal cell carcinoma, 7 cases of adenosquamous carcinoma, 199 cases of unknown differentiation; 45 cases of unknown tumor diameter, 12 cases of Tis and unknown depth, and 1 case of unknown lymph node metastasis; and 6 untreated cases were excluded. Overall, 151 patients with stage I-IV cancer treated with surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, or chemoradiotherapy were included in this study (Figure 1B). Differentiation was classified into three categories. For AC: G1 is the well-differentiated type of tubular and papillary adenocarcinoma; G2 is the moderately differentiated type of tubular adenocarcinoma; G3 is poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, and signet-ring cell carcinoma; and G4 includes undifferentiated carcinoma. For SCC: G1 is the well-differentiated type; G2 is the moderately differentiated type; and G3 is poorly differentiated.

Study flow chart for adenocarcinomas of the anal canal (A) and squamous cell carcinomas of the anal canal (B).

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Tokai University School of Medicine, Japan (23RC011), on October 23, 2023, and performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The JSCCR Ethics Committee approved the study protocol. Due to the retrospective study design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Analysis of clinicopathological features of AC and SCC

We examined patient factors, tumor factors, and treatment factors to clarify the clinicopathological features of AC and SCC. Patient factors included sex, age (over 75 years), and treatment period; tumor factors included lesion site, number of lesions, macroscopic type, tumor diameter (5 cm or more), TNM classification[8], distant metastasis site, venous invasion (severe invasion: V3 or higher), and lymphatic invasion (severe invasion: Ly3 or higher); and treatment factors included surgical procedures (anus-preserving surgery), combined resection, first line treatment method (chemoradiation therapy, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and surgery), colostomy (recurrence surgery), pre- and postoperative treatment, residual tumor classification, and treatment effect, which were compared according to differentiation. The number of lesions, venous invasion, lymphatic invasion, surgical procedure (anus-preserving surgery), combined resection, pre- and postoperative treatment, and residual tumor classification were factors for AC-only cases, whereas treatment, colostomy (recurrence surgery), and treatment effects were factors for SCC-only cases.

Relationship between differentiation and prognosis of AC and SCC

The 5-year overall survival (OS) for each AC and SCC stage was compared based on differentiation using the Kaplan-Meier method to examine the relationship between differentiation and prognosis.

Analysis of prognostic factors of AC and SCC

The prognostic factors for AC and SCC were analyzed using the previously described patient, tumor, and treatment factors. Furthermore, we analyzed cases from Stages I to III, excluding Stage IV.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi-square test. For comparisons among the three groups, the Steel-Dwass test was performed, and p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. OS was defined as the duration from the date of surgery to the date of the last visit or the date of death from AC and as the duration from the date each treatment started to the date of the last visit or the date of death from SCC. Survival curves were created using the Kaplan-Meier method. OS comparisons between the groups were evaluated using the log-rank test, and p-values were calculated. Cox proportional hazard models were used for the multivariate analyses of prognostic factors. p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The software used for the statistical analyses was JMP for Windows, version 13.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Table 1 presents the patient characteristics stratified by ACC histological type. AC was slightly more common in males than in females, and SCC was more common in females than in males. Sex differences were recognized depending on histological type. G2 differentiation was approximately 50% for both AC and SCC. Of the patients with AC, 20.9%, 32.6%, 27.8%, and 18.5% had stages I, II, III, and IV tumors, respectively.

Clinicopathological features

A comparison of clinicopathological features of ACC based on differentiation by AC is presented in Table 3. In the AC group, no differences were observed in patient factors based on differentiation. Regarding tumor factors, as the degree of differentiation decreased, the depth of invasion increased for the T-category and lymph node metastasis increased for the N-category. In addition, the lower the differentiation, the more common the distant metastasis; however, G2 was the most frequent in liver metastasis, and G3 was the most frequent in extra lymphatic metastasis. Regarding treatment factors, the rate of anal-sparing surgery decreased proportionally with the degree of differentiation. Further, the lower the degree of differentiation, the higher the residual tumor classification and frequency of postoperative treatment. In SCC, none of these factors differed significantly based on differentiation (Table 4).

OS rates

In AC, the 5-year OS rate by differentiation was not significantly different in patients with stage I cancer (Figure 4A). However, a significant difference was observed in the 5-year OS rate based on differentiation in patients with stage II, III, or IV cancer (Figure 4B-4D). In patients with SCC, no significant difference was observed in the 5-year OS rates based on differentiation (Figure 5A-5D).

Prognostic factors

Prognostic factors based on ACC histological type analyzed using the Cox proportional hazards model are presented in Table 5. The prognostic factors associated with AC were differentiation (G3/4; hazard ratio [HR]: 1.33, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02-1.72, p=0.04), age (>75 years; HR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.12-1.79, p=0.003), N-category (N2; HR: 1.49, 95% CI: 1.09-2.01, p=0.03), and residual tumor classification (R1; HR: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.27-2.53, p=0.001 and R2; HR: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.31-2.60, p<0.001). In patients with SCC, the prognostic factors were sex (male; HR: 5.41, 95% CI: 2.64-12.58, p=<0.001) and treatment method (chemotherapy; HR: 64.94, 95% CI: 2.49-1691.39, p=0.01). Differentiation was not a prognostic factor in SCC.

Discussion

Differentiation, used for histological diagnosis, is based on the degree of morphological deviation from normal structures or benign tumors and describes the biological nature of the tumor and thus the characteristics of the cancer[3]. Tumor cells with differentiation similar to that of normal cells have weak proliferative potential, whereas immature tumor cells have high malignant potential and proliferate rapidly, with frequent occurrences of metastasis and local destruction.

In this study, we examined the clinicopathological features of ACC, a rare disease, based on differentiation. Furthermore, prognostic factors were analyzed to improve treatment. ACC is divided into two histological subtypes, AC and SCC; each sub-type was analyzed separately.

This study revealed that AC was more common in males, whereas SCC was more common in females. The higher prevalence of AC in males aligns with the results of a previous study[9]. In most nations, women experience higher incidence rates of SCC[10] as human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a risk factor for SCC[11]. The virus also causes high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions and cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (HSIL/CIN) in the cervix[12] and vagina[13]. HSIL/CIN of the cervix and vagina are significant risk factors for anal HSIL/anal intraepithelial neoplasia owing to the bidirectional interactions of HPV infection between anatomical sites in the vagina and anus[14]. Regarding differentiation, both AC and SCC were more often moderately differentiated, as previously reported[15]. Regarding survival rates, SCC was associated with a favorable prognosis, consistent with previous findings[16]. In this study, as the tumor became more poorly differentiated, the number of invasive type cases increased from localized to macroscopic. In addition, the penetration depth (T-category) and degree of lymph node metastasis (N-category) advanced as the tumors became more poorly differentiated.

The frequency of distant metastasis also increased as the tumors became more poorly differentiated; however, in terms of metastasis type, hematogenous liver metastasis was most frequent in G2, and lymphogenous extra-regional lymph node metastasis was most frequent in G3. In colorectal cancer, lymph node metastasis is more common in poorly differentiated cancer types[17], whereas liver metastasis is more common in moderately differentiated ACs[18]. Regarding treatment factors, the rate of anus-preserving surgery decreased as the level of differentiation became poor and residual tumor classification advanced, resulting in the need for additional postoperative treatment. In SCC cases, no differences were observed in any patient, tumor, and treatment factors or other characteristics based on differentiation. Regarding the 5-year OS rates, patients with AC showed significant differences in survival based on differentiation. However, in SCC, no differences were observed in survival based on differentiation at any stage.

This study revealed that differentiation, age, N-category, and residual tumor classification were prognostic factors for AC. Differentiation[19] and N-category[20] have been reported as prognostic factors for rectal cancer. Residual tumor classification has also been previously reported as a prognostic factor[21]. Thus, the clinicopathological features by differentiation and prognostic factors for ACs in ACC were similar to those for rectal cancer. Since differentiation that can be diagnosed by biopsy before treatment is a prognostic factor, if a patient is diagnosed with a G3 differentiation, the patient should be given a positron emission tomography-computed tomography scan to detect metastases to areas other than the abdominal pelvis for accurate staging, considering that the frequency of extra-regional lymph node metastases is higher for G3 and that chemotherapy is usually the main treatment for positive extra-regional lymph nodes. Since the frequency of regional lymph node metastasis is also high, lymph node dissection should be performed thoroughly. Additionally, because there are many residuals, the primary tumor should be resected with sufficient margins. In addition, it is necessary to consider combined resection of other organs more than in other differentiation levels. Various guidelines[22-24] recommend adjuvant chemotherapy for stage II colorectal cancer with G3 poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma at risk of recurrence; this should also be considered for anal canal cancer.

In SCCs, the prognostic factors were sex and treatment methods. Differentiation was not a prognostic factor. In esophageal cancer, which is a type of SCC, many studies have reported that differentiation is a risk factor for lymph node metastasis[25], poor prognosis[26], and recurrence[27]. Both are gastrointestinal cancers; however, some differences exist. In cervical cancer, HPV infection was reported to be associated with tumor differentiation, and non-HPV infection is associated with more advanced keratinization[28]. Moreover, in cervical and vaginal cancers caused by HPV, differentiation is considered a prognostic factor[6]. As in this study, a previous study reported that differentiation is not a prognostic factor for the SCC subtype of ACCs[29]. There are reports that HPV infection affects tumor differentiation in ACCs[30], similar to cervical cancer, and that the presence or absence of HPV infection is an independent poor prognostic factor after chemoradiotherapy[31,32]. Additionally, patients with HPV infection reportedly have a better prognosis. Based on previous reports of ACCs and cancers of other organs, ACCs associated with HPV infection are classified as low-grade SCCs of the anal canal and are considered to have good responsiveness to chemoradiotherapy and a favorable prognosis. The prognosis may vary depending on the degree of differentiation. Nonetheless, it remains unclear whether carcinoma differentiation is a prognostic factor for SCC. It is necessary to conduct a comprehensive investigation of HPV infection, differentiation grade, and the efficacy of chemoradiotherapy.

In this study, men had a poorer prognosis than women. A study using the Cancer Outcomes and Services Dataset reported that women, compared to men, were more likely to have more advanced SCC[33]. Furthermore, a study examining the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program data revealed that women were at a higher risk of progression to an advanced cancer stage and death than that observed with men[34]. This suggests that differences in treatment methods exist based on socioeconomic disparities and sex differences in the early diagnosis of SCC through early consultation. However, generally, men have been reported to have lower OS[35], similar to that observed in this study. The reason for this poor prognosis in men remains unclear, and we were unable to clarify it in this study.

Regarding treatment modalities, according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines[36], chemoradiation is the first choice of treatment for SCC of the anal canal. In this study, no prognostic differences were observed between chemoradiotherapy and surgical therapy. Regardless of differentiation, chemoradiotherapy should be the treatment of choice for SCC of the anal canal owing to its ability to preserve anorectal function. Furthermore, in the present study, no difference was observed in prognosis between patients undergoing radiation therapy alone or chemoradiotherapy. Therefore, for older patients who cannot undergo chemotherapy, radiation therapy alone may be a viable treatment option. Chemotherapy was the least effective treatment for ACC; therefore, it is not the first choice of treatment in cases without distant metastasis.

This study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective study. Second, this study did not include HPV infection status as a patient factor, and molecular biological data, including genetic mutations, associated with the cancer as the data were not registered. The strength of this study lies in its examination of the clinicopathological features of ACC, a rare cancer with difficult case collection based on differentiation after stratifying the patients into two histological subtypes, AC and SCC.

In conclusion, in ACC, the clinicopathological features of AC by differentiation were similar to those of rectal carcinoma. Moreover, because differentiation was a prognostic factor, treatment strategies should be similar to those for rectal carcinoma. Conversely, SCC had no clinical characteristics based on differentiation, and differentiation was not a prognostic factor; thus, differentiation does not need to be considered during treatment. Further studies should explore the involvement of differentiation in SCC treatment.
