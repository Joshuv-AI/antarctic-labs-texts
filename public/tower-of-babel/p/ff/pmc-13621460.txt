Understanding eating behaviors in obesity: a DEBQ-based analysis of clinical and demographic factors
Authors: Yasemin Koçyiğit, Hatice Ayça Kaloğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a multifactorial chronic disease influenced by metabolic, genetic, behavioral, and psychosocial factors. This study aimed to assess eating behaviors in treatment-seeking individuals with obesity using the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ) and to explore the associations of its emotional, external, and restrained eating subscales with clinical and sociodemographic characteristics.MethodsThis study included 220 individuals with obesity who were followed at the Etlik City Hospital Obesity Center. Psychiatric diagnoses were assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders (SCID-5). Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a sociodemographic form and the DEBQ. Regression analyses were conducted as exploratory, hypothesis-generating models to identify variables associated with DEBQ subscale scores, without implying causal relationships.ResultsEmotional eating was positively associated with years of education, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and the presence of an eating disorder-related condition. The strongest association was observed for the presence of an eating disorder-related condition, which may partly reflect conceptual and measurement overlap with the emotional eating construct assessed by the DEBQ. Restrained eating was positively associated with duration of obesity and negatively associated with the presence of an eating disorder-related condition. External eating was positively associated with years of education and the presence of an eating disorder-related condition.ConclusionEating behaviors among treatment-seeking individuals with obesity appear heterogeneous and are associated with both psychiatric and sociodemographic factors. In particular, emotional eating was linked to depression, OCD, and the presence of an eating disorder-related condition; however, associations involving eating disorder-related condition should be interpreted cautiously due to potential construct overlap. These findings underscore the importance of assessing psychiatric comorbidities and eating behavior patterns in the individualized management of obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40337-026-01701-y.

Background

Obesity is a major public health problem due to its increasing global prevalence and associated health risks [1]. Its etiology cannot be explained solely by metabolic or endocrine factors; rather, it arises from complex interactions among biological, behavioral, environmental, and psychosocial influences [2].

Effective prevention and treatment require attention not only to metabolic factors but also to eating behaviors. Eating is shaped not only by physiological hunger but also by mood, stress, learned habits, and environmental cues, highlighting the important role of psychosocial and environmental influences [3]. Understanding the social, cultural, and psychological determinants of eating behavior is therefore essential for obesity management [4].

Van Strien et al. described three types of eating behavior based on their underlying triggers: emotional, external, and restrained eating [5]. This three-factor structure has been shown to be valid and reliable across different cultures [6, 7].

Emotional eating refers to eating in response to negative emotions rather than physical hunger, often disregarding satiety signals. It is associated with overeating, particularly during stress, anxiety, depression, and anger, and is reported by more than 60% of individuals with overweight or obesity [8]. Difficulties in emotion regulation may also hinder long-term weight management.

External eating is driven by environmental cues, such as the sight or smell of food, rather than internal hunger signals. Individuals with overweight or obesity may be more sensitive to such cues, which can promote overeating [9].

Restrained eating refers to the intentional restriction of food intake. However, strict dietary restrictions can paradoxically trigger overeating, complicating weight control, whereas flexible strategies are associated with more successful management [10].

Maladaptive eating behaviors are not merely contributors to excess weight but are significant behavioral barriers to long-term treatment success [11]. Identifying these patterns can guide individualized treatment and help detect individuals at risk for severe psychiatric or weight-related complications. In Turkiye, obesity prevalence exceeds the global average [12], yet research using validated instruments like the DEBQ remains limited, often relying on small samples or self-reported psychiatric histories [13, 14].

The present study extends the literature by examining emotional, external, and restrained eating patterns in a treatment-seeking clinical sample in Turkiye. By utilizing structured DSM-5-based psychiatric interviews rather than self-reports, this research contextualizes DEBQ associations within a real-world clinical setting.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess eating behaviors among patients at a Turkish obesity center and examine their associations with clinical and demographic characteristics. Given its cross-sectional design, the study explores these associations rather than causal relationships. These findings highlight the importance of integrating eating behavior and psychiatric assessments into routine obesity care.

Methods

Participants

Following ethical approval from the Etlik City Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision: 2024 − 871), this study recruited adults with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) who attended the obesity center between October 2024 and April 2025. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old, have at least a primary school education, and provide written informed consent.

Psychiatric comorbidities and formal eating disorder diagnoses were assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders (SCID-5), administered face-to-face by two experienced clinicians. This structured approach ensured diagnostic accuracy rather than relying on self-reported histories. In addition to DSM-5 eating disorder diagnoses (binge-eating disorder and bulimia nervosa), night eating syndrome was systematically assessed using the proposed DSM-5 research criteria for Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder (OSFED) presentations. Classification was based on predefined diagnostic criteria systematically applied during the clinical interview rather than on unstructured clinical judgment. To capture the broader spectrum of clinically significant disordered eating behaviors in this treatment-seeking population, participants meeting criteria for any DSM-5 eating disorder or night eating syndrome were combined into a single “eating disorder-related” variable. Although this composite variable is inherently heterogeneous and may limit the interpretability of disorder-specific associations, it was chosen to examine the overall association between clinically relevant eating pathology and DEBQ scores rather than to compare specific diagnostic categories; accordingly, throughout this manuscript, this variable is referred to as the “eating disorder-related” variable rather than “eating disorder diagnosis” to better reflect its heterogeneous nature.

Data collection further included a sociodemographic form and the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ), both administered during face-to-face interviews.

Scales used

Socio-demographic Data Form: A researcher-designed form was used to collect data on age, gender, education, employment status, and duration of obesity. It also assessed childhood obesity, family history of obesity, smoking status, and alcohol use.

Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ): Developed by Van Strien et al. [15], this 33-item scale assesses three psychologically based eating behaviors: emotional eating (eating in response to negative emotions), restrained eating (intentional food restriction for weight control), and external eating (reacting to the sight or smell of food). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “very often.” The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Bozan et al. [16]

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values (within ± 1.5) and was also evaluated within the groups compared in the variance analyses [17]. Group differences were evaluated via t-tests, ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U, or Kruskal–Wallis tests, with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons where appropriate. Bivariate associations were examined using Pearson or Spearman correlations.

Exploratory multiple linear regression analyses using the Enter method were performed to examine associations between the independent and dependent variables. Variables that showed statistically significant associations with the relevant dependent variable in the preliminary group comparison and correlation analyses were entered into the corresponding regression model as independent variables. Model assumptions were evaluated using Durbin–Watson statistics and tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values to assess autocorrelation and multicollinearity. In addition, the normality of residuals and homoscedasticity assumptions were examined through residual diagnostics and scatterplots of residuals versus predicted values. Potential outliers and influential observations were assessed using Cook’s distance values.

Results

Among the 220 participants, 86.4% were female (n = 190). The mean age was 42.07 ± 11.82 years, mean education duration was 10.85 ± 4.11 years, mean BMI was 42.54 ± 6.50 kg/m², and mean duration of obesity was 20.78 ± 10.75 years.

DSM-5 eating disorder diagnoses included binge-eating disorder (13.2%) and bulimia nervosa (11.8%). In addition, night eating syndrome (37.7%) was identified as an other specified feeding or eating disorder (OSFED)-related eating pattern during clinical assessment. Because some participants met criteria for more than one diagnosis or eating-related presentation, these categories were not mutually exclusive. Overall, 135 participants (61.4%) met criteria for at least one eating disorder-related condition. Most participants met criteria for a single eating disorder-related condition, with diagnostic overlap observed in only three participants (BED + NES). The distribution and overlap of specific diagnoses are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Mean DEBQ scores were 2.45 ± 0.70 for restrained eating, 2.93 ± 1.16 for emotional eating, and 2.99 ± 0.91 for external eating.

Correlation analyses (Table 1) showed that age was weakly positively associated with restrained eating and weakly negatively associated with emotional eating. Education was weakly positively associated with emotional and external eating. Duration of obesity was weakly positively associated with restrained eating, while BMI was weakly negatively associated with emotional eating.

DEBQ scores were also compared across psychiatric diagnoses. Emotional eating differed significantly between groups (p < 0.05), with higher scores in participants with depression compared to those without any psychiatric diagnosis (3.41 ± 1.03 vs. 2.79 ± 1.13).

Participants meeting criteria for an eating disorder-related condition had higher emotional and external eating scores than those who did not meet criteria for any eating disorder-related condition, whereas restrained eating scores were higher in participants without an eating disorder-related condition (p < 0.05 for all comparisons). In addition, restrained eating scores were higher in non-smokers than in smokers (2.55 ± 0.67 vs. 2.12 ± 0.72; p < 0.05). No significant differences in DEBQ subscale scores were observed according to alcohol use, childhood obesity, or family history of obesity (p > 0.05).

Regression analysis for emotional eating (Table 3) showed that the model was statistically significant (p < 0.001) and accounted for 27.8% of the variance in emotional eating scores. Years of education, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and the presence of an eating disorder-related condition were positively associated with emotional eating, whereas male gender was associated with lower emotional eating scores. The presence of an eating disorder-related condition showed the strongest association in the model; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to potential conceptual overlap between the eating disorder-related variable and the emotional eating construct. Anxiety disorder was included in the model but was not significantly associated with emotional eating.

Discussion

This study examined the multidimensional nature of eating behaviors in individuals with obesity and their associations with clinical and sociodemographic characteristics. Emotional, restrained, and external eating patterns were differentially related to psychiatric comorbidities and demographic factors. Given the cross-sectional and exploratory design, findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. The sample was predominantly female, likely reflecting gender-specific healthcare-seeking patterns rather than true population prevalence. Prior research indicates that women are more likely to seek professional help following an obesity diagnosis [18], and clinical samples frequently exhibit a high proportion of women [19, 20]. In line with these trends, women showed higher restrained and emotional eating scores, potentially related to gender roles, body image concerns, repeated dieting, and emotional coping mechanisms [6, 15, 21, 22]. Within the DEBQ framework, chronic restrained eating may also increase vulnerability to disinhibition and binge eating in response to emotional triggers [23]. However, these gender-based comparisons should be interpreted with considerable caution. With 86.4% of the sample being female, the male subsample was markedly small (n = 30), resulting in limited statistical power to detect gender differences reliably and increasing the risk of unstable estimates. Therefore, the observed gender differences should be regarded as preliminary and descriptive rather than conclusive; replication in samples with more balanced gender representation is needed before drawing firm conclusions about gender-specific eating behavior patterns in clinical obesity populations [24].

Age exhibited weak associations with DEBQ subscale scores: emotional eating decreased, while restrained eating increased with age. This aligns with previous DEBQ studies suggesting that age is mainly associated with emotional and external eating, including lower emotional eating in older populations, whereas restrained eating generally remains relatively stable, except for the restrained eating finding in the present study [6, 25, 26], although such behaviors are also modulated by depression, chronic stress, and broader psychosocial factors [22, 27]. The positive association between age and restrained eating may reflect accumulated dieting experience or heightened health concerns in this clinical population; however, the small effect size necessitates confirmation through longitudinal designs. Similarly, educational level typically treated as a descriptive variable [15, 28] was weakly associated with emotional and external eating. Rather than suggesting a direct causal link, education may function as a proxy for unmeasured environmental or occupational exposures, warranting further investigation into underlying mechanisms.

The observed heterogeneity of eating disorder diagnoses and behavioral presentations suggests that maladaptive eating in obesity is not a uniform clinical pattern. In particular, the frequent occurrence of binge-eating disorder and night eating syndrome may be associated with the elevated emotional and external eating scores, consistent with literature emphasizing the multidimensional relationship between obesity and disordered eating [22, 29, 30]. To clarify these relationships, exploratory regression findings for each subscale are discussed separately below.

Emotional eating

Emotional eating was significantly associated with depression, OCD, and the presence of an eating disorder-related condition, suggesting that this pattern may reflect broader psychiatric comorbidity rather than an isolated eating style. While the presence of an eating disorder-related condition emerged as the strongest correlate consistent with evidence that emotional eating is a core feature of binge-eating disorder and bulimia nervosa [31, 32] this finding likely represents clinical convergence rather than independent causality, given the conceptual overlap between these constructs. Subgroup analyses reaffirmed this link: participants with binge-eating disorder reported significantly higher emotional and external eating scores than those without an eating disorder, aligning with the view that binge-eating pathology is commonly associated with emotional and cue-based triggers [29–32]. Conversely, higher restrained eating scores among patients without an eating disorder suggest a more preserved capacity for deliberate dietary control within treatment settings.

The DEBQ identifies emotional eating as a response to negative affect. Although its predictive power for actual food intake remains debated [33], current evidence validates it as a robust indicator of psychological distress and emotion regulation deficits in obesity [34]. Our findings regarding depression mirror both cross-sectional and prospective studies showing associations between depressive symptoms and emotional eating and poorer weight outcomes [35, 36]. Emotional eating may be related to short-term coping responses to negative affect [37]; however, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about directionality. Previous studies have similarly linked depressive symptoms with higher emotional eating and weight-related outcomes [38, 39].

Recent clinical evidence also indicates that emotional eating is associated with psychiatric symptoms and obesity across different age groups [22, 40]. This underscores that caloric restriction alone may be insufficient for patients with comorbid depression; interventions targeting emotion regulation may be more clinically relevant. Furthermore, the association with OCD is consistent with a transdiagnostic framework in which compulsivity and rigid control mechanisms manifest in eating behaviors. This is consistent with clinical observations reporting associations between negative affect, compulsive urges, and eating-related difficulties in treatment-seeking individuals [41]. Collectively, these results suggest that emotional eating is associated with mood pathology and compulsivity, highlighting the importance of psychiatric screening in obesity management.

Restrained eating

The mean restrained eating score was higher than in some previous obesity studies [42], potentially reflecting the treatment-seeking status and weight-loss motivation of the sample. However, the fact that restraint scores were lower than emotional or external eating suggests that cognitive restraint reflects an ongoing but not fully effective control strategy rather than a successful regulatory pattern [6]. Longer duration of obesity was positively associated with restrained eating, consistent with a pattern of cumulative dieting efforts over time a pattern consistent with evidence linking cognitive restraint to higher body weight and persistent weight management attempts [43]. Male gender and smoking were negatively associated with restrained eating, which may suggest lower engagement in deliberate dietary control due to reduced body image preoccupation in men or the appetite-modulating effects of nicotine in smokers [29, 43].

The presence of an eating disorder-related condition was also negatively associated with restrained eating. This likely indicates a shift from planned cognitive restriction toward more dysregulated and impulsive eating patterns in individuals with comorbid eating disorders [29, 43]. Conditions such as binge-eating disorder, prevalent in clinical obesity populations, are characterized by impaired control rather than sustained restraint. However, given the diagnostic heterogeneity, these results should be interpreted as an expected clinical association rather than a causal effect, particularly as meta-analytic evidence suggests poorer clinical outcomes in this subgroup [30].

External eating

External eating scores were higher than in community-based samples but consistent with clinical obesity populations [6], suggesting greater responsiveness to environmental food cues over internal satiety signals [44, 45]. In contemporary food environments characterized by the over-availability of energy-dense foods, increased exposure may be associated with increased cue-driven eating, particularly in individuals with lower regulatory capacity [46]. External eating was also positively associated with the presence of an eating disorder-related condition, consistent with findings that obesity–eating disorder comorbidity involves heightened cue responsiveness and impaired appetite regulation [30, 47]. The observed co-occurrence of external and emotional eating further supports the interpretation that these dimensions are linked to broader self-regulation vulnerabilities [48]. External eating was also positively associated with retired employment status, which may partly reflect age- and lifestyle-related changes in eating behavior observed in older adults [7, 25].

While these associations suggest clinical convergence, they should not be interpreted as independent causal effects due to conceptual overlap with disordered eating behaviors. Nevertheless, individuals with elevated external eating may benefit from interventions focusing on stimulus control and mindful eating, although such implications remain hypothesis-generating given the exploratory design [49, 50]. The moderate explained variance further suggests that eating behaviors in obesity are modulated by additional biological and psychological factors not captured in the present models.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that DEBQ-assessed eating behaviors in individuals with obesity are differentially associated with psychiatric and sociodemographic factors. Emotional eating was associated with depression and OCD, while the presence of an eating disorder-related condition was associated with all DEBQ subscales. However, findings involving the presence of an eating disorder-related condition should be interpreted cautiously given the conceptual overlap with DEBQ constructs, particularly emotional eating. Overall, these results underscore the clinical importance of jointly assessing eating behavior patterns and psychiatric comorbidities in treatment-seeking populations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and eating behaviors may vary over time, highlighting the need for longitudinal studies. Second, the predominance of female participants limits generalizability to men. Third, although psychiatric diagnoses and eating disorder–related conditions were assessed using structured clinical interviews, the observed associations—particularly those involving eating disorder diagnoses and DEBQ emotional eating—may be influenced by conceptual and measurement overlap. Emotional eating as assessed by the DEBQ reflects eating in response to negative affect and overlaps clinically with core features of binge-eating disorder and bulimia nervosa; therefore, shared construct variance may have inflated the magnitude of some associations and regression coefficients and increases the risk of interpretive circularity when conceptually related variables are entered into the same model. Therefore, the observed relationships should be interpreted as associations between related clinical constructs rather than evidence of independent effects.

Future research using longitudinal designs and analytic strategies that partition shared versus unique variance (e.g., latent-variable approaches/structural equation modeling or sensitivity analyses by diagnostic subgroup) would help to better disentangle these constructs. Fourth, reliance on self-report measures for eating behaviors may introduce recall and social desirability bias; future studies would benefit from incorporating objective or real-time behavioral assessments. Finally, a notable limitation is the creation of a combined “eating disorder-related” variable that includes both formal DSM-5 eating disorder diagnoses and night eating syndrome. Although this variable was intended to reflect the broader clinical picture of disordered eating in treatment-seeking individuals with obesity, the resulting construct is heterogeneous. This heterogeneity may obscure important differences between specific eating disorder presentations and could have influenced the strength of the observed associations with DEBQ subscales.

In conclusion, obesity treatment may benefit from integrated approaches that extend beyond caloric restriction to include psychiatric evaluation and the assessment of individual eating behavior patterns. Although causal conclusions cannot be drawn, accounting for variability in eating behaviors may support the development of more targeted and sustainable treatment strategies.
