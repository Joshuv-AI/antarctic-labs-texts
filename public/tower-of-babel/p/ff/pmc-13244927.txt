Comparison of staff workload between percutaneous nephrolithotomy with and without intraoperative cone‐beam CT: an 18‐month postoperative analysis
Authors: Chris A. Suijker, Rosanne van Ee, Antoinette D. I. van Asselt, Inge M. van Oort, Riemer A. Kingma, Stijn Roemeling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo assess whether hybrid cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT)‐percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) reduces overall staff demands compared to standard PCNL.Patients and MethodsWe performed a retrospective study of a randomised controlled trial conducted in a tertiary Dutch urolithiasis centre. A total of 160 patients were randomised 1:1 to CBCT‐PCNL or standard PCNL. Staff time was estimated from preoperative assessment through 18 months of follow‐up, including both elective and unplanned care delivered by nurses, urologists, anaesthesiologists, intensivists, radiologists, and technical staff. Time estimates were derived from literature, standard times, and bottom‐up and top‐down calculations.ResultsHybrid CBCT‐PCNL required 36.3 staff hours per patient vs 43.1 h for standard PCNL (19% reduction), largely driven by a 47% decrease in unplanned care due to fewer complications and stone‐related events. Most care was attributable to nurses (61% in the CBCT‐PCNL group; 63% in the standard PCNL group), with time concentrated in hospital days (43%; 42%) and surgery (46%; 38%).ConclusionHybrid CBCT‐PCNL can improve clinical efficiency and reduce labour demand, freeing staff for other care needs. These findings highlight how innovative urological technologies can enhance care efficiency, which is especially important amid growing workforce pressures.

Introduction

The adoption of new surgical or medical innovations must consider not only clinical effectiveness and cost‐efficiency but also their impact on healthcare workforce demands [1, 2]. Expanding diagnostic and treatment options, combined with an ageing population, increase strain on an already uncertain workforce [3, 4]. Evaluating techniques for their effect on staff demands helps identify efficient care pathways, optimise staff capacity and enhance care availability.

In the Netherlands, a shortage of 360 000 healthcare workers is projected by 2030 (4% of the total workforce), escalating to 2 007 000 (21%) by 2060 [1]. Similar shortages across all medical specialties are reported globally [2, 5, 6]. Within the European Union, an estimated deficit of 1.2 million healthcare professionals was reported in 2023, projected to reach 4.1 million in 2030 [7, 8]. Workforce constraints may soon pose greater barriers to adopting new treatments than cost or effectiveness, highlighting the need to evaluate labour demands alongside clinical and economic factors. Nevertheless, analyses of staff workload remain limited, and standardised methodologies are largely lacking [1].

This study quantifies the effect of new treatment methods on staff demands by presenting the first comparative analysis of labour requirements between standard percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) and PCNL with intraoperative cone‐beam CT (CBCT). The analysis is based on data of a single‐centre randomised controlled trial (RCT) conducted in the Netherlands. Intraoperative CBCT, performed at the end of the procedure to detect and remove residual kidney stone fragments, improved ≤4 mm stone‐free rates by 15%, reducing stone‐related events (SREs) such as emergency department (ED) visits, re‐interventions, and hospital admissions [9, 10].

By comparing staff demands across the full care pathway, this analysis guides clinical decision‐making toward more sustainable treatment strategies that free staff capacity and improve care availability. Beyond urolithiasis, the framework provides a transferable approach for assessing workforce implications of medical innovations in urology and other specialties, supporting efforts to strengthen healthcare delivery.

Patients and Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Review Committee of the University Medical Center Groningen (approval number: 2019/375), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. An independent monitor oversaw the study and data collection. No external funding was received for this study.

Data were retrospectively collected from a two‐arm, parallel, non‐blinded, single‐centre RCT conducted at a tertiary referral centre specialising in complex urolithiasis in the Netherlands. The original trial is briefly introduced, followed by the detailed design of the present study.

Original RCT Procedures

The original RCT randomised patients intraoperatively during PCNL between undergoing intraoperative CBCT or concluding the procedure once fluoroscopic and endoscopic assessments indicated a stone‐free status. The rationale was that intraoperative CBCT can detect residual stone fragments missed by fluoroscopy and endoscopy, as urologists incorrectly assume a stone‐free status in 20–50% of cases [11, 12, 13]. Detecting these fragments allows for their removal during the same surgery. Patients who were not fluoroscopically and endoscopically stone free could not be randomised, as intraoperative CBCT would not be of added value in these patients.

Eligible patients were non‐pregnant adults (aged ≥18 years) indicated for PCNL. Randomisation was performed in a 1:1 ratio using a computerised model in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system with random block sizes to ensure allocation concealment. The primary outcome was the Grade C (≤4 mm) stone‐free rate on non‐contrast CT at 4 weeks postoperatively. Patients with bilateral urolithiasis requiring two separate procedures were randomised individually for each kidney. Details of operative procedures, CBCT protocols, and outcomes have been published previously [9, 14].

Study Design

This study utilised the randomised participants from the original RCT. All in‐hospital healthcare resources—covering elective (routine) care, and additional unplanned care (for complications and SREs) —that could be directly linked to labour input from various healthcare professionals were identified from the initial preoperative consultation up to 18 months (548 days) postoperatively. For each case, the number of procedures was recorded (including hybrid CBCT‐PCNL, standard PCNL, hybrid CBCT‐endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery [ECIRS], ECIRS, ureterorenoscopy, JJ stent placement and removal, and operative management of complications), along with hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) days, CT scans, ultrasounds, kidney‐ureter‐bladder radiographs, outpatient visits, telephone consultations, and ED visits. Only healthcare resources related to the randomised (ipsilateral) kidney were included.

Labour input for each healthcare resource was quantified in minutes, involving the following healthcare workers: urologists, anaesthesiologists, radiologists, intensivists, physicians (non‐specialist doctors working in urology or intensive care medicine), anaesthesiology technicians, radiological technicians, and department/scrub nurses. Due to the absence of standardised methods, a combination of bottom‐up and top‐down approaches was employed, as well as institutional standard values and previously published data from other centres, to estimate labour time per activity for each professional category [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, a detailed description of labour time allocation for each healthcare provider per activity is provided in Appendix S1, which also summarises the minutes allocated per activity by each healthcare worker.

Certain groups whose labour contributions are difficult to quantify, such as physicians in training and support staff (e.g., laboratory personnel, pharmacy staff, management, and information technology [IT] professionals), were excluded. In cases where intraoperative CBCT confirmed a stone‐free status, the labour input for the 4‐week postoperative CT scan was omitted, as this scan is not routinely performed outside of research settings. Two missed consultations, whether outpatient or by telephone, were counted as a single consultation.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

Data were extracted from electronic patient records by a single observer (R.v.E.) and independently verified by a second observer (C.A.S.). All data were pseudonymised and stored securely in a REDCap database, complying with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). For patients who received follow‐up or treatment at their local hospital, data were requested from these institutions. For patients lost to follow‐up at our centre, we attempted to obtain medical records from regional centres. If no additional records were found, it was assumed no healthcare activities had occurred.

Labour input was categorised per individual healthcare worker, and mean labour input per case was calculated across categories. To facilitate comparison, healthcare workers were grouped into four occupational categories: medical specialists, physicians, nurses, and technicians. Care was divided into elective care and unplanned care (care related to complications and/or SREs). Differences in labour input between standard PCNL and hybrid CBCT‐PCNL were assessed. A calculation tool is provided in Appendix S2. Labour input is reported in whole minutes or in hours with one decimal, although calculations were performed using exact values.

Statistical analyses were conducted according to the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) principle. Data are presented following standard statistical reporting guidelines. To assess uncertainty around differences in labour input, a non‐parametric bootstrap analysis with 1000 iterations was performed. All analyses were carried out using R Studio (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The study was closed at interim analysis after 160 randomisations (80/group), as pre‐defined superiority criteria were met. These 160 randomisations included 149 patients, 11 of whom underwent two PCNL procedures for bilateral urolithiasis. In total, 222 participants signed informed consent, of whom 62 were not randomised due to endoscopic and/or fluoroscopic evidence of residual stones. The present analysis solely compares the 160 randomised patients.

Procedures were performed between January 2020 and July 2023. A Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram up to 18 months postoperatively has been published previously [9, 10]. During the 18‐month follow‐up period, nine patients (11%) in the CBCT‐PCNL arm and seven patients (9%) in the control arm were lost to follow‐up. All cases were included in the ITT labour analysis. In the CBCT‐PCNL group, 56 cases originated from external referrals vs 48 in the standard PCNL group; these were categorised as new consults.

In total, 22 h and 7 min (61%) per patient in the CBCT‐PCNL group and 27 h and 14 min (63%) in the standard PCNL group were attributable to nursing care (Fig. 1A). Among medical specialists, 5 h and 10 min (14%) per patient in the CBCT‐PCNL group and 5 h and 24 min (13%) in the standard PCNL group were attributable to urologists; 1 h and 13 min (3%) and 1 h and 12 min (3%) per patient in the CBCT‐PCNL and standard PCNL groups were attributable to anaesthesiologists, respectively; less than 1.5% of care were attributable to each radiologists and intensivists individually.

Regarding additional unplanned care, the CBCT‐PCNL group required 8 h and 54 min per patient, whereas the standard PCNL group required 16 h and 46 min: an 47% decrease in time with regard to the CBCT‐PCNL group (Fig. 1B,C). Expressed as a proportion of total care, this corresponds to a decrease from 39% to 25%. Nurses provided most unplanned care, with 6 h and 21 min (71%) per patient in the CBCT‐PCNL group and 12 h (72%) in the standard PCNL group. Unplanned care primarily involved extended hospital stays following emergency admissions or re‐interventions (59% CBCT‐PCNL group; 47% standard PCNL group; Fig. 2A,B). ICU staff demand was higher in the standard PCNL group (28% vs 9%), mainly driven by two patients whose significant comorbidities contributed to postoperative respiratory insufficiency and sepsis.

Discussion

This study quantified staff labour demands across the entire care pathway for hybrid PCNL with intraoperative CBCT vs standard PCNL, from preoperative consultation through 18 months postoperatively, revealing a 19% reduction in total labour time (36 h 19 min vs 43 h 4 min per patient). The primary contributor to this reduction was a substantial decrease in unplanned care related to complications, re‐interventions, and SREs in the hybrid CBCT group, despite slightly higher baseline surgical demands.

The higher baseline demands in hybrid CBCT‐PCNL are primarily attributable to the slightly longer operative time, due to both the additional extraction of residual fragments identified on CBCT and the time required to acquire the CBCT images. A minor learning curve effect on procedural duration over time cannot, however, be entirely excluded, as surgeons continuing to use CBCT‐guided extraction may become more efficient at identifying residual fragments.

Previous studies indicate that intraoperative CBCT during PCNL improves stone‐free rates, thereby reducing re‐interventions and SREs [9, 10, 24, 25, 26]. As expected, re‐interventions are associated with additional admissions and an increase in the risk of complications, likely contributing to the elevated staff demands for hospital and ICU care in the standard PCNL group. ICU admissions, in particular, significantly amplify workload due to the low nurse‐to‐patient ratio. Reducing unplanned care related to SREs and complications consequently frees up valuable staff capacity in the emergency department, hospital wards, and operating rooms, allowing these resources to be redirected toward other essential patient care.

Staff demand comparisons comparing different treatment strategies remain scarce across all medical specialties. In urology, a recent study comparing labour input for radical cystectomy vs radiotherapy reported 93.5 h and 29 h of work, respectively, from first consultation to post‐treatment follow‐up [27]. Comparing the procedural complexity and inclusion of 18 months of follow‐up in this study, our labour estimates for PCNL appear reasonable. The lower proportion of nursing care reported by Rutten et al. [27] for radical cystectomy (35%) likely reflects the inclusion of residents in their methodology, as well as the additional surgeons, longer operative times, and more physician‐intensive postoperative care inherent to radical cystectomy.

Our results indicate that increased procedural efficacy reduces the need for unplanned care, which is typically more disruptive and resource‐intensive than planned care. In urinary stone disease, even small residual fragments (≤4 mm) can cause significant morbidity [28, 29], making complete stone clearance critical for both patient outcomes and reduced staff demand. In the context of projected global healthcare staffing shortages [1, 2], labour intensity and staff deployment may therefore influence treatment decisions when long‐term costs are comparable. Indeed, our previously published cost‐comparison study on hybrid PCNL with intraoperative CBCT vs standard PCNL showed that the observed reduction in postprocedural care related to complications and SREs may offset the investment required for a hybrid operating room [30]. These findings highlight the value of incorporating detailed labour profiles into decision‐making, as they can identify where efficiency gains translate into both workforce and potential economic benefits, supporting sustainable care delivery and informed long‐term workforce planning [7, 8].

The framework presented in this article, together with our calculation tool provided in the Supplementary material, is intended to support researchers in urology and other specialties in comparing staff demand. As staffing shortages increasingly constrain the adoption of new procedures and technologies, and because workforce requirements do not always mirror costs or resource use, such analyses are gaining importance [1]. They also reveal which professional groups invest the most time in a care pathway, enabling critical evaluation of task allocation. Future studies can clarify when detailed workforce assessments are essential for decision‐making and when pragmatic approximations suffice, highlighting an area for further methodological development.

This study has several limitations. First, care activity durations were estimated using multiple methods – including literature data, standard times, and bottom‐up and top‐down approaches with expert validation. A uniform approach might have improved accuracy, but consistency is challenging at this stage, and comprehensive bottom‐up measurement is resource‐intensive. Labour inputs also vary across hospitals, regions, and countries. To enhance reproducibility, our calculation model and rationale are provided in the Supplementary material. Physicians in training were excluded to reflect routine care.

Second, by excluding personnel not directly involved in patient care, such as support staff, laboratory, pharmacy, healthcare assistants, IT support, GPs, and preoperative consults, our estimates likely underestimate total labour demands. These groups remain essential, especially amid projected shortages of GPs and support staff [1].

Third, only the 160 randomised patients were included in this workload analysis. The 62 non‐randomised patients were ineligible for CBCT but still required hybrid operating room scheduling. Consequently, only 160 of 222 operated patients (72%) were eligible for CBCT. Although CBCT reduced staff demand by 19% in eligible cases, the reduction at the system level would be ~14% (0.72 × 19%). This distinction between per‐patient efficacy and overall impact should be considered when interpreting the results. In our setting, hybrid CBCT‐PCNL uses the same core personnel as standard PCNL, and longer operative times mainly reflect additional stone extraction. As a result, the system‐level impact may be limited, though it could differ in other clinical settings with different staffing models.

Fourth, limited data on annual PCNL volumes in the Netherlands constrained assessment of the broader national impact of hybrid CBCT‐PCNL. Finally, the 18‐month follow‐up may not capture long‐term effects; differences in labour demands may persist or diminish as recurrences increase, making extrapolation beyond 18 months uncertain.

Despite these limitations, this is the first study comparing staff labour demands between hybrid PCNL with intraoperative CBCT and standard PCNL up to 18 months postoperatively. Hybrid CBCT‐PCNL reduced labour time by 6 h and 45 min (19%) per patient, mainly by reducing unplanned care for re‐interventions and complications. It improves stone clearance, decreases SREs, enhances care efficiency, and reduces unplanned care burden, translating into lower morbidity, less strain on resources, and more sustainable care.

We advocate broader adoption of hybrid PCNL with intraoperative CBCT and encourage further validation of these methodologies to enable staff demand assessments across medical specialties. Amid growing healthcare workforce shortages, such efforts are vital for a viable, efficient, and sustainable healthcare system.
