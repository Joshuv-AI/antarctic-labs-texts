Impact of ambient particulate matter and meteorological factors on COPD risk and its association with blood eosinophilia
Authors: Amja Manullang, Xuan Ngoc Tran, Yueh-Lun Lee, Yuan-Hung Wang, Yuan-Chien Lin, Kang-Yun Lee, Chi-Li Chung, Tzu-Tao Chen, Kuan-Yuan Chen, Chien-Hua Tseng, Chien-Ling Su, Shu-Chuan Ho, Feng-Ming Yang, Sheng-Ming Wu, Kian Fan Chung, Kai-Jen Chuang, Jer-Hwa Chang, Hsiao-Chi Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the association between air pollution and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is well-documented, limited studies investigate the effects of meteorological factors in COPD. The objective of this study is to examine the impact of particulate matter less than < 2.5 μm (PM2.5), temperature and relative humidity (RH) on COPD detection, with a specific focus on the importance of blood eosinophil levels.MethodsThis case-control study was conducted in Taiwan from December 2010 to April 2022, including 937 COPD patients and 833 controls. The average daily exposure and differences in PM2.5, temperature, and RH over 1-day, 7-day and 1-month periods were calculated using the radial basic function interpolation model. The associations between PM2.5, temperature, and RH with COPD were assessed using logistic regression, and non-linear exposure-response associations were further examined using generalized additive mixed models.ResultsWe observed that each 1 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 daily average over the 7-day and 1-month periods were associated with a 1.051-fold and 1.059-fold increase in the odds ratio (OR) of COPD, respectively. The exposure-response curves indicate that individuals with blood eosinophilia (≥ 2%) exhibit consistent increases in the odds of COPD in response to rising PM2.5 levels. The exposure-response relationship between temperature and COPD was also observed in subjects with blood eosinophilia. We observed that a 1-month average PM2.5 was associated with a 1.044-fold increase in the OR of GOLD stages 3–4 among COPD with eosinophil levels ≥ 2%.ConclusionPM2.5 levels were associated with increased odds of COPD detection, with individuals exhibiting blood eosinophilia being especially vulnerable to PM2.5 exposure and more likely to detect severe COPD. Improving air quality for human health protection is important in response to climate change challenges.Keywords: Air pollution, Particulate matter, Temperature, Relative humidity, COPD

Introduction

The contribution of air pollution to respiratory disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) morbidity and mortality has been reported previously [1, 2]. An interquartile range (IQR) increase in particulate matter a diameter of less than of < 2.5 μm (PM2.5) was contributed to a 1.04-fold increase in the risk of COPD among non-smoking women in the US [3]. PM2.5 exposure causes damage to airway cells by triggering apoptosis in airway epithelial cells and initiating inflammatory processes [4]. There was a 2.53-fold increase in the risk of COPD resulting from increased levels of PM2.5 ranging between 35 µg/m3 and 75 µg/m3 [5]. An increase in COPD severity was found to be 1.11-fold higher with each IQR increment in PM2.5 [6]. A Korean study found higher hospitalization for every 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 among individuals aged > 65 years [7]. This evidence suggested that PM2.5 exposure is linked with an increased risk of COPD.

While risk of air pollution-related COPD has been substantially studied, limited research has found that meteorological factors, including temperature and relative humidity (RH) also contribute to COPD. High temperature exposure at a peak level of 29 °C contributed to a 7.6% increase in hospital admissions due to COPD [8]. Both moderate and extreme cold in Brazil increased the risk of COPD-related death by 20–33% and 27–36%, respectively [9]. Regarding to RH, a study conducted in China revealed that daily temperature fluctuations were linked to an increased COPD mortality [10]. An acute exposure to RH of 67% was associated with hospital visits due to COPD, especially in individuals aged over 74 years old [11]. Another study from China found that an elevated percentage of RH is linked to an increased risk of COPD, suggesting that maintaining RH below 70% could reduce COPD symptoms [12].

COPD is a chronic lung inflammatory disease which significantly contributes to airflow obstruction [13]. The role of neutrophils has been recognized to contribute to the inflammatory process in COPD [14]. However, the prevalence of blood eosinophilia was 34.9% among COPD patients compared to 25.8% among those without COPD [15]. About 39% of COPD subjects with blood eosinophil levels ≥ 2% were classified as being in a very severe stage [16]. Elevate eosinophil levels were observed corresponding to particulate matter less than 10 microns (PM10) exposure in both genders, with effects observed within 0–5 days for females and 20–28 days for males [17]. PM2.5 exposure significantly increases blood eosinophilia by inducing cytokines and chemokines [18]. However, it remains unclear whether susceptibility to environmental exposures differs according to an individual’s eosinophil profile. Although the pathological progression of COPD is driven by long-term cumulative exposure to risk factors, short-term environmental stressors can still influence airway inflammation, symptom burden, and lung function [19, 20]. We therefore hypothesize that recent fluctuations in particulate matter (PM) and meteorological conditions may act as proximal triggers that, together with an individual’s eosinophil profile, influence the likelihood of COPD being detected. Assessing short-term environmental exposures prior to diagnosis may thus capture the immediate environmental context associated with COPD diagnosis, rather than reflect their contribution to the chronic development of the disease. Therefore, this study aims to examine the association between short term exposure to PM, temperature, and RH, and COPD diagnosis, with particular interest in whether individuals with blood eosinophilia exhibit heightened vulnerability to these short-term environmental exposures. These findings not only expand the current understanding of air pollution and meteorological impacts on COPD but also offer valuable insights for targeted public health interventions under changing climate conditions.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

The ethics committees of the Taipei Medical University-Joint Institutional Review Board (TMU-JIRB no. N202003075) approved this study. Consent to participate was waived by the Taipei Medical University-Joint Institution Review Board in view of the retrospective nature of the study and all the procedures being performed were part of the routine care.

Study population

This case-control study involved participants who had completed a pulmonary function test determined by spirometry between December 2010 and April 2022. The pre-bronchodilator lung function test was performed using the Vitalograph Spirotac VTM following guidelines from the American Thoracic Society and the European Respiratory Society by healthcare technicians and nurses [21]. The case group consisted of participants diagnosed with COPD from three hospitals in Taipei, defined as a post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio below 70%. GOLD staging was assessed using standardized post-bronchodilator spirometry according to the official GOLD classification. FEV₁ % predicted was used to categorize COPD severity as follows: GOLD 1 (≥ 80%), GOLD 2 (50–79%), GOLD 3 (30–49%), and GOLD 4 (< 30%). For analysis, GOLD 1–2 and GOLD 3–4 were combined to represent mild-moderate and severe-very severe COPD, respectively. All spirometry measurements followed ATS/ERS guidelines [22]. The control group included participants with an FEV1/FVC ratio ≥ 70% from the Tucheng Health Care Cohort (THCC), a community health-focused project in New Taipei City [23]. The study included participants aged 40 to 90 years old. Participants diagnosed with malignancy, bronchiectasis, asthma, and inflammatory condition not related to COPD were excluded. Demographic information such as residence address, age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, medical history, and respiratory symptoms was recorded. Data on white blood cells, including neutrophil, eosinophil, and lymphocyte counts, were collected at the time of the recruitment. Blood eosinophilia was defined as ≥ 2% eosinophil in the total white blood cells, consistent with previous studies [24, 25].

Exposure assessment

Daily temperature and RH information were gathered from weather stations operated by the Taiwan Central Weather Bureau stations, while hourly concentration PM2.5 data were obtained from Taiwan Environmental Protection Administration stations for the years 2010 to 2022. The hourly concentration of PM2.5 was processed to derive daily average. The radial basis function (RBF) interpolation method was applied to estimate daily PM2.5, temperature, and RH levels at each participant’s residential coordinates. The RBF approach is widely recognized as a highly accurate spatial interpolation technique for environmental exposure assessment, as it generates smooth and continuous concentration surfaces from irregularly spaced monitoring data [26]. This approach produced a smoother map and provided a more accurate representation of the measured data when evaluated using cross-validation statistics and plots [27]. In addition, previous work conducted in Taiwan has reported strong validation results for RBF-based PM₂.₅ interpolation, showing high agreement between estimated concentrations and air quality monitoring stations (R² = 0.93, p < 0.001) [28]. The average of daily exposures to PM2.5, temperature, and RH were calculated using the data from the 1-day, 7-day, and 1-month period on the day of recruitment. The average of daily exposure for each period was calculated from t-1 days before the case to the day of the case. For instance, the 7-day average of daily exposure includes data from 6 days before the case to the day of the case. To calculate daily differences, each day’s value was compared to the preceding day’s value. The average of these differences over a t-day period was used to represent the t-day differences. The details of exposure assessment for PM2.5, temperature, and RH has been reported previously [19].

Statistical analyses

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. The 1st and 99th percentiles were used to replace outlier data, following the Winsorization approach [29]. Nominal variables were compared using the Chi-square test, while Student’s t-test was used to compare continuous variables. The association between PM2.5, temperature, and RH in the 1-day, 7-day and 1-month averages of daily exposure and differences and COPD diagnosis was examined using logistic regression analysis. Additionally, logistic regression was employed to examine the associations between 1-day, 7-day, and 1-month daily averages and daily difference in PM2.5 and GOLD stages in COPD patients, stratified by the presence of blood eosinophilia (eosinophils ≥ 2%) and without blood eosinophilia (eosinophils < 2%). The generalized additive mixed model was used to evaluate the exposure–response relationships between PM2.5, temperature, relative humidity, and COPD risk. A four degree of freedom smoothing spline was applied to each exposure variable to flexibly account for potential nonlinear effects [19, 30]. Age, sex, BMI, and smoking status were accounted for in the analysis. SPSS for Windows version 20.0 was used to conduct logistic regression. Analysis of the exposure-response relationship was performed using R software version 4.3.2. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant for two-sided tests.

Results

Characteristics of study subjects

This study involved 1770 subjects, including 937 individuals diagnosed with COPD and 833 controls. The characteristics of all subjects were detailed in Table 1. The COPD group was older and had a higher proportion of male compared to the control group. A lower BMI was observed in the COPD group compared to the control group. The percentage of current and former smokers was higher in the COPD group than in the control group. The control group demonstrated better lung function, as indicated by higher FEV1 and FEV1/FVC ratio. Higher neutrophil levels but lower lymphocytes levels were observed in the COPD group compared to the control group. However, there was no notable difference in mean eosinophil levels between the COPD and the control. PM2.5 in 7-day and 1-month average of daily exposure was higher in the COPD group than control group. Similarly, PM2.5 in 7-day and 1-month average of daily difference was higher in the COPD group than in the control group. RH in 1-day, 7-day, and 1-month daily average was lower in the COPD group than in the control group. Moreover, RH in the 1-day average of daily difference was lower in the COPD compared to the control.

Association of PM2.5, temperature, and RH with COPD detection

Table 2 shows the association between PM2.5, temperature, RH, and the odds of COPD. We found that an increase of 1 µg/m3 in PM2.5 for the 7-day and 1-month averages was significantly associated with a 1.051-fold (95% CI: 1.023–1.079) and 1.059-fold (95% CI: 1.024–1.096) increase in the OR of COPD, respectively. An increase of 1 µg/m3 in PM2.5 average over a period of 7 days was linked to a 1.026-fold (0.999–1.054) increase in the OR of COPD. A 1% increase in RH for the 1-day average was associated with a 0.982-fold decrease in the OR of COPD (95% CI: 0.966–0.999).

Discussion

In the context of increasing air pollutants and climate change, this study investigates the association of air pollutants and meteorological factors with COPD detection. Beyond confirming the adverse effects of PM2.5, the novelty of this study lies in identifying that individuals with blood eosinophilia appear particularly susceptible to PM2.5 exposure and are more likely to be detected with severe COPD.

First, our study found that exposure to higher level of PM2.5 was associated with increased COPD diagnosis. Our findings were similar to a previous report that an increase in daily average PM2.5 concentrations was associated with a higher prevalence in COPD [31]. Each quartile increase in air pollution contributed to a 1.37-fold increased risk of COPD compared to non-exposure [32]. The risk of acute exacerbations increased by 2.43% within 2 days and by 6.65% within 2 weeks for every 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 [20]. The observed association could be attributed to PM2.5 -induced production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequent airway inflammation, which are key mechanisms in the pathogenesis of COPD [33]. Oxidative stress, defined as a state in which endogenous antioxidant defenses are insufficient to counteract ROS, plays a key role in cellular damage [34]. Numerous studies have shown that PM2.5 exposure contributes to this imbalance by enhancing ROS production and reducing antioxidant capacity [35, 36]. Previous research also has revealed that biomarkers of inflammation were increased by brief PM2.5 exposure. Short-term PM2.5 exposure in COPD patients can heighten systemic inflammation by increasing Th1 and Th17 cytokines, leading to elevated circulating levels of IL-2, IL-12, IL-17 A, IFN-γ, MCP-1, and sCD40L [37], and by aggravating airway injury through epithelial remodeling and impaired dendritic cell function [38].

Additionally, our study showed that COPD diagnosis rises when PM2.5 rises and falls when PM2.5 drops. Separating upward and downward trends allows us to detect whether increases and decreases in environmental exposures have different impacts on COPD diagnosis, something that would be hidden if exposure were analyzed as a single overall variable. While the association between PM2.5 and COPD has been discussed above, the potential influence of temperature and relative humidity (RH) fluctuations on COPD also merits consideration. A time series study from Brazil found a significant association between temperature changes and an increased risk of death from COPD [9]. The harmful effects of temperatures are likely due to their contribution to increased respiratory infection risk and reduced lung function of COPD, especially on peak expiratory flow (PEF) [39]. With respect to RH, lower humidity may promote airway drying and bronchoconstriction, impair mucosal defense, and increase vulnerability to respiratory infections, which could contribute to COPD detection [40]. Conversely, higher RH has been shown to alleviate exercise-induced bronchospasm, highlighting the effects of humidity on airway physiology [41].

Next, the exposure-response curves showed that individuals with blood eosinophilia (≥ 2%) exhibit consistent increases in the odds of COPD diagnosis to higher 1-month average PM2.5 exposure. The appearance of peak ORs at relatively modest PM2.5 concentrations likely reflects higher data density and greater statistical stability within commonly observed exposure ranges, rather than biological thresholds or tipping points. At higher concentrations, estimates are less stable due to fewer observations and wider confidence intervals. In addition, the overlap and occasional crossing of curves between eosinophilic and non-eosinophilic subjects likely reflect shared PM2.5-related pathophysiological pathways, overlapping exposure distributions, and smoothing inherent to generalized additive models. These patterns should not be interpreted as evidence of biological thresholds or distinct exposure-response shapes associated with eosinophil status. Previous evidence has shown that exposure to diesel exhaust particles has been shown to induce COPD with an eosinophilic phenotype in animal models [42].

Finally, the study showed that individuals with blood eosinophilia (≥ 2%) exhibit increases in the odds of severe disease (GOLD stages 3–4) in response to higher 1-month average PM2.5 exposure. Previous studies have shown that COPD patients with eosinophilic inflammation was associated with PM exposure, with increased PM2.5 levels associated with a higher risk of acute exacerbations and elevated PM10 levels linked to reduced lung function in this population [43, 44]. This observed association may be interpreted from two complementary, and not mutually exclusive, perspectives. First, individuals with eosinophilia may be inherently more susceptible to the harmful effects of particulate matter. Alternatively, elevated blood eosinophilia may not itself be the cause of increased susceptibility but rather a marker of a specific inflammatory endotype that is particularly responsive to environmental stimuli. Although eosinophils have been implicated in COPD pathophysiology through the release of inflammatory mediators such as eosinophil cationic protein, major basic protein, and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin [45], which contribute to airway inflammation, remodeling, and hyperresponsiveness, key pathological features of COPD [46], it remains unclear whether elevated blood eosinophil levels represent a predisposing COPD phenotype that confers heightened sensitivity to PM or a biological response induced by particulate exposure itself. Regardless of the direction of this association, these findings highlight the critical impact of environmental exposures, particularly PM, on COPD and suggest that blood eosinophil levels may serve as an important biomarker for identifying individuals who require closer monitoring during periods of poor air quality. Importantly, the exposure windows examined in this study primarily capture short-term environmental conditions preceding COPD diagnosis or severity classification, rather than cumulative lifetime exposure. As such, our findings are best interpreted in the context of short-term triggering or amplification of airway inflammation, which may increase the likelihood of disease detection or exacerbate existing pathological processes, rather than indicating a direct role in long-term COPD pathogenesis. Although temperature and humidity also demonstrated exposure-response relationships with COPD, these effects were weaker and appeared only within specific time windows among individuals with eosinophilia, reinforcing PM2.5 as a particularly important environmental exposure in the COPD.

Our study has several limitations. First, although both cases and controls were recruited from metropolitan areas in northern Taiwan (Taipei and New Taipei City), which helps ensure broadly comparable environmental conditions, several important potential confounders were not captured, including socioeconomic factors, occupational exposures, and indoor air pollution sources. Second, due to its observational design, the study cannot establish causality, underscoring the importance of validating these findings in future longitudinal studies. Important clinical variables such as inhaled corticosteroid use, recent exacerbations, and baseline lung function (FEV₁ % predicted), which are strong determinants of COPD severity, were not available in our dataset. The absence of these factors may have influenced the observed associations between environmental exposure and disease severity. Additionally, a single eosinophil measurement at the time of recruitment may not fully reflect an individual’s chronic inflammatory phenotype. Future studies incorporating repeated eosinophil assessments and long-term exposure metrics would enable a more reliable characterization of eosinophilic status and further clarify the role of environmental exposures in COPD pathogenesis.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that PM2.5 and temperature exposure are associated with increased COPD, particularly among individuals with blood eosinophilia who exhibit greater susceptibility to particulate pollution and a higher likelihood of severe disease. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health strategies that focus on improving air quality and addressing climate-related environmental stressors. Strengthening air pollution control measures and incorporating meteorological factors into COPD management may help reduce disease burden.
