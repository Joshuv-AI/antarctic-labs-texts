Variations in alcohol craving and negative mood during a clinical trial of ibudilast for alcohol use disorder
Authors: Lindsay R Meredith, Erica N Grodin, Craig K Enders, Lara A Ray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune medications, including ibudilast, have shown early potential in mitigating alcohol intake in preclinical models and small‐scale trials for alcohol use disorder (AUD). To elucidate clinical utility and clinical mechanisms of these novel treatments, research should carefully assess medication‐related changes in AUD symptomatology, including craving and negative mood, over time.MethodsThis is a secondary analysis of a 12‐week randomized clinical trial of ibudilast for AUD. Participants were 102 individuals (40% female, average age 44 years) seeking treatment for AUD and randomized to ibudilast (50 mg twice‐daily) or matched placebo. Clinical symptom measures of alcohol craving, depression, and anxiety were collected monthly. Growth models compared rates of change in clinical symptomatology between treatment groups and tested whether changes were moderated by sex. A subsample (n = 25) completed a functional magnetic resonance imaging scan with an alcohol cue‐reactivity task at week four to assess a brain‐based craving marker.ResultsAt pretreatment, participants reported moderate craving symptoms and nonclinically elevated negative mood symptoms, on average. Participants taking ibudilast showed significantly steeper reductions in craving during the trial, as compared with placebo (1.32 vs. 0.52 points lower per month; p = 0.035). At treatment endpoint, female participants taking ibudilast reported lower craving than female participants taking placebo (p < 0.001). Greater cue‐elicited brain activation in bilateral insula, bilateral orbitofrontal cortex, and right precuneus (p's < 0.02) was detected among the placebo subsample, compared to ibudilast. Rates of change for depression and anxiety did not differ between medication conditions, nor were they moderated by sex. Depression symptoms decreased during the trial, while anxiety symptoms remained relatively stable.ConclusionsConsistent with prior research, ibudilast reduced neurobiological and self‐report markers of craving, with female participants showing a stronger treatment response by trial endpoint. Despite shared neuroimmune correlates between negative mood and addiction, ibudilast did not alleviate negative mood symptoms beyond placebo effects.Keywords: alcohol use disorder, craving, immune, negative mood, pharmacotherapy

INTRODUCTION

Less than 8% of people with past‐year alcohol use disorder (AUD) received any alcohol treatment, demonstrating a significant care gap (SAMHSA, 2023). Only three medications are FDA‐approved for abstinence or drinking reduction, and the numbers needed to treat are quite high (McPheeters et al., 2023). Moreover, these medications are contraindicated for individuals with commonly co‐occurring health conditions, such as acute hepatitis or opioid use (Fairbanks et al., 2020), and two medications are approved only for patients with established abstinence. Alternative pharmacological treatments, including novel and repurposed agents, have been tested in animal and clinical samples with AUD (Burnette et al., 2022). Among the agents tested are medications that modulate the immune system, with several showing potential by mitigating alcohol intake in rodents (Erickson et al., 2019). A small proportion, including ibudilast, apremilast, and minocycline, have moved forward in the translational pipeline to testing in human laboratory and clinical trials with mixed findings (Mason et al., 2024; Meredith et al., 2021; Ray et al., 2025). Thus, current evidence on the clinical efficacy of immune medications among clinical samples with AUD is lacking.

Interest in exploring immune compounds as treatments for AUD comes from research suggesting that the immune system is involved in the development and maintenance of AUD (Coleman Jr. & Crews, 2018). Sustained heavy alcohol use contributes to altered immune processes and neural functioning, along with an excessive inflammatory response (Adams et al., 2023). Preclinical research shows that sustained alcohol exposure alters immune signaling in the central nervous system through multiple pathways, such as by activating Toll‐like receptors (Alfonso‐Loeches et al., 2010) and modifying astrocytic and microglial expression (Bachtell et al., 2017). Meta‐analytic work indicates that individuals with AUD have consistent elevations in certain peripheral inflammatory markers compared with healthy individuals without AUD (Moura et al., 2022). Alcohol‐related inflammation is suspected to reinforce heavy drinking behavior and worsen salient maintenance factors of AUD, including negative emotionality and craving (Coleman Jr. & Crews, 2018). As clinical data emerge from randomized trials testing immunomodulators for AUD, it will be important not only to determine clinical efficacy but also to thoughtfully test hypothesized treatment mechanisms (Grigsby et al., 2023). This research will help the field better understand how these interventions may work and for which individuals (Meisel et al., 2023). In particular, it will be important to study how treatment efficacy may differ between biological sexes, a traditionally under‐researched area (Kirsch et al., 2024). Sex differences in response to immune treatment are supported by findings in several fields, including alcohol research. Preclinically, female rodents were more sensitive to ethanol‐induced neuroimmune responses (Barton et al., 2017; Pascual et al., 2017) and clinically, females with AUD showed a more pronounced microglia deficit than males with AUD (Zakiniaeiz et al., 2025).

Negative mood states, a common feature of addiction (Lannoy et al., 2021), are likely suggestive of overall greater psychiatric impairment and are consistently linked with poorer treatment outcomes (Cano et al., 2017; Swan et al., 2020). These states are internal experiences marked by unpleasant feelings, such as sadness, anxiety, or frustration, and related bodily sensations, which influence cognitions and behaviors. In the context of alcohol treatment, changes in negative affect are dynamically associated with changes in alcohol intake (Witkiewitz & Villarroel, 2009). Individuals with AUD are more vulnerable to negative mood in part because chronic alcohol exposure sensitizes immune signaling pathways (Coleman Jr. & Crews, 2018; Tynan et al., 2010). Alterations in neuroimmune signaling are hypothesized to be shared by a range of medical and psychiatric conditions such as addiction and depression (Miller & Raison, 2016; Neupane, 2016). Anti‐inflammatory therapies are shown to reduce negative mood in psychiatric disorders such as major depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia (Adzic et al., 2018; Andrade, 2016; Rosenblat et al., 2016). As such, negative mood, an important feature of addiction, may be a potential treatment target of neuroimmune modulation for AUD that improves clinical outcomes (Zhang et al., 2002, 2008).

Another key maintenance factor of AUD is alcohol craving, or a strong desire or urge to drink, which predicts alcohol intake, severity of AUD, and relapse (Martins et al., 2022; Vafaie & Kober, 2022). Craving may also be modulated by proinflammatory signaling in the brain and gut. In preclinical models, administration of anti‐inflammatory compounds is thought to mitigate the rewarding properties of ethanol. In clinical studies, alcohol craving and drinking were associated with multiple peripheral markers of inflammation among predominantly male participants (Heberlein et al., 2014; Leclercq et al., 2012). In pharmacotherapy trials for substance use disorders (SUDs), craving is one of the most commonly tested mechanisms of action. For example, phasic craving or acute/context‐specific craving is often experimentally provoked through behavioral and neuroimaging paradigms, such as alcohol cue exposure (Meredith et al., 2023), while tonic craving is more stable and typically assessed through repeated self‐report measures. An altered neural response to alcohol cues measured via blood oxygen‐level dependent (BOLD) signal during functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a neurobiological hallmark of AUD that has been proposed as a biomarker of addiction (Addiction Cue‐Reactivity Initiative et al., 2024). Further, neural response to alcohol cues in brain regions including the insula, caudate, middle frontal gyrus, and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is sensitive to AUD treatment (Zeng et al., 2021). Yet, a limited number of studies have tested the effects of immune treatments on alcohol craving in clinical samples with AUD.

Ibudilast (MN‐166) is one potential immune treatment for AUD that has been tested in randomized clinical trials. It is a phosphodiesterase (PDE) inhibitor at PDE3, ‐4, ‐10, and ‐11 as well as an allosteric macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) that crosses the blood–brain barrier (Cho et al., 2010). Both PDE4 and MIF are thought to regulate inflammatory responses in microglia and contribute to neuroinflammation (Giampa et al., 2010; Mizuno et al., 2004). It is suspected that ibudilast may reduce neuroinflammation by inhibiting proinflammatory signaling, enhancing anti‐inflammatory signaling, and providing neuroprotection. Our laboratory has conducted three randomized trials with ibudilast for AUD. Most recently, a 12‐week clinical trial of ibudilast was completed and the principal endpoints were focused on reductions in alcohol intake (Ray et al., 2025). Across treatment conditions, participants reported significant drinking reductions during the trial. However, medication adherence was modest and no medication‐specific effects in support of ibudilast's efficacy were detected. Given the novelty of this trial, we were additionally interested in better understanding how immune modulators might impact known maintenance factors of AUD, including craving and negative mood.

Central to the current project, secondary data on clinical measures of negative mood and alcohol craving were collected monthly during the 12‐week trial. Results from our earlier studies of ibudilast demonstrated its potential to reduce alcohol craving and modulate affective states over 1–2 weeks. Specifically, following a stress exposure paradigm, ibudilast promoted a stronger recovery of positive affect (Ray et al., 2017). In a laboratory setting, ibudilast attenuated BOLD signal during an fMRI scan in the bilateral ventral striatum in response to alcohol cues (Grodin et al., 2021). In the natural environment, ibudilast attenuated alcohol‐induced craving but did not alter daily levels of negative affect (Meredith et al., 2022). Taken together, these initial results show that ibudilast modulated multiple facets of phasic craving and transient affective states under experimental conditions. However, little is known about how ibudilast might temper clinical and neurobiological measures of craving and negative mood in AUD over a longer timeframe (i.e., 12‐week trial).

This project is a secondary data analysis in which we tested whether clinical markers of self‐reported alcohol craving and negative mood changed over the course of a 12‐week randomized trial of ibudilast for AUD and whether the rate of change differed between treatment groups. We hypothesized that symptom measure scores for depression, anxiety, and alcohol craving would decrease over the trial and that those randomized to ibudilast would show significantly steeper reductions and lower scores by treatment endpoint. We further tested whether biological sex moderated these changes in clinical symptomatology. Lastly, a small subgroup of participants completed a neuroimaging scan with an alcohol cue‐reactivity task at trial week 4. An exploratory whole‐brain analysis tested the main effect of medication condition on cue‐elicited brain activation to see whether ibudilast attenuated this neurobiological marker of craving.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clinical trial design

The current project is a secondary analysis of a phase 2, 12‐week randomized, double‐blind clinical trial of ibudilast for the treatment of AUD (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03594435) (Ray et al., 2025). The trial enrolled 102 treatment‐seeking participants with current AUD who were randomized to either ibudilast (50 mg twice‐daily) or matched placebo. Participants attended in‐person follow‐up visits (or virtual visits as needed during the COVID‐19 pandemic) at 4‐, 8‐, and 12‐week postrandomization, where they reported on their alcohol and substance use and completed clinical measures of alcohol craving, depression, and anxiety. Telephone visits were conducted at Weeks 2, 6, and 10 to collect interim alcohol use data. The NIAAA‐developed behavioral platform “Take Control” was administered at each in‐person visit (Devine et al., 2016) to deliver evidence‐based information on drinking. At Week 4, a subsample of participants completed a neuroimaging scan to capture neural alcohol cue‐elicited craving.

The study was approved by the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Institutional Review Board and monitored by a Data and Safety Monitoring Board. All participants provided written informed consent after discussing the study procedures and medication with a licensed physician.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meet DSM‐5 diagnostic criteria for current moderate or severe AUD; (2) between the ages of 18 and 65; (3) seeking treatment for AUD; and (4) report drinking at least 14 drinks per week if male or 7 drinks per week if female in the 28 days prior to consent. Exclusion criteria included meeting current DSM‐5 diagnostic criteria for a SUD specific to any psychoactive substance aside from alcohol, nicotine, or cannabis (only mild use disorder allowed), a lifetime diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or psychotic disorder, among others. See Data S1 for full exclusionary criteria, including for neuroimaging.

Screening procedures

The clinical trial was conducted at an outpatient research facility in an academic medical center. Recruitment methods included advertisements inviting individuals who wished to change their drinking to reach out for participation in a treatment study. Interested individuals completed an online/telephone screening interview and, if eligible, were invited for an in‐person intake visit consisting of clinical interviews and measures. To continue with study procedures, participants needed a breath alcohol concentration of 0.00 g/dL and a urine toxicology test negative for all drugs excluding cannabis. Following that, eligible participants were asked to complete an in‐person physical screening consisting of vital signs, laboratory tests, and a physical examination and medical history interview by a study physician.

Randomization and medication

Participants who met all eligibility criteria and attended their in‐person randomization visit were randomly assigned to receive either 50 mg twice‐daily of ibudilast or matched placebo in a 1:1 ratio. The allocation was carried out using a stratified block randomization procedure with biological sex and heavy drinking as the stratification factors. Study medication and matched placebo were supplied by MediciNova, Inc. (La Jolla, CA, USA), and the formulation included 10‐mg delayed‐release Pinatos® capsules. The UCLA Research pharmacy prepared and dispensed study medication in blister packs according to the stratification list. Research staff, providers, and participants remained blind to the randomization condition during the trial. Study physicians closely monitored adverse events. Medication adherence was monitored by study staff using participant self‐report and verified by the pill count method at in‐person visits. Adherence was determined as a participant taking all prescribed medication doses (allowing for 1 missed dose). See Data S1 for details on trial retention and medication stratification, dosing, adherence, and adverse events.

Pretreatment assessments measures

At pretreatment, demographic information and various self‐report measures of substance use and mental health were collected. Severity of AUD was quantified using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) measure (Saunders et al., 1993), which is a well‐validated and widely used alcohol screening instrument for hazardous drinking. The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐5 (SCID‐5; First et al., 2015) was administered to assess inclusionary and exclusionary psychiatric diagnoses. Other exclusionary criteria were assessed by medical clinicians and through urine drug screening, breathalyzer test, concomitant medications form, pregnancy test, and the Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol Scale—Revised (Sullivan et al., 1989).

Alcohol use

Covering the entire study timeframe (i.e., 30 days prior to baseline visit through 12 weeks postrandomization), participants reported retrospectively on their daily alcohol use. Trained research staff administered a modified Timeline FollowBack (TLFB) interview (Sobell et al., 1986) to compute standard drinks based on drink volume, quantity, and type. For primary analyses, the alcohol use outcome, drinks per drinking day (DPDD), served as a covariate and was calculated (1) at pretreatment for the 30 days prior to initial screening and (2) during the trial for each two‐week timeframe preceding the collection of clinical symptom measures.

Clinical measures

At pretreatment and at 4, 8, and 12 weeks postrandomization, participants completed three self‐report measures of interest. The Penn Alcohol Craving Scale (PACS; Flannery et al., 1999) was administered to capture levels of tonic alcohol craving, and the Beck Depression Inventory‐II (BDI‐II; Beck et al., 1996) and Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; Beck et al., 1988) were administered to capture depression and anxiety symptomatology, respectively.

Alcohol craving

The PACS 5‐item self‐report measure assesses the frequency, intensity, and duration of past‐week alcohol craving (Flannery et al., 1999). This measure is shown to have high internal consistency and validity and can be used for longitudinal monitoring of alcohol craving, particularly in treatment contexts. Research suggests PACS scores significantly predict future drinking behavior and relapse (Stohs et al., 2019). For each item (ranging from 0 to 6 points), participants indicated the statement that best described their craving in the past week. Items were summed to create a score from 0 to 30 points. Severity cut‐off scores have not been established, but previous findings from our group suggested a ≥15‐point cut‐off to indicate clinically significant alcohol craving (Hartwell et al., 2019).

Negative mood

The BDI‐II and BAI are commonly used measures assessing the intensity and severity of negative mood symptoms in a variety of clinical populations (Moore et al., 2016). These measures are highly reliable and display good sensitivity and validity (Wang & Gorenstein, 2013). The BDI‐II has 21 items reflecting a range of depression‐related symptoms, thoughts, and attitudes that are summed to create a score ranging from 0 to 63 points. Participants indicated the statement that best described how they felt in the past 2 weeks (ranging from 0 to 3 points). Traditional cutoff scores falling in the mild, moderate, and severe range are ≥14, 20, and 29, respectively, but can vary depending on the clinical sample. Similarly, the BAI has 21 items reflecting a variety of somatic and cognitive symptoms commonly experienced among individuals with anxiety. Items are summed to create a score ranging from 0 to 63 points, and traditional cutoff scores for mild, moderate, and severe are ≥8, 16, and 26, respectively. BAI scores have been positively related to AUDIT scores and alcohol craving (McCaul et al., 2017). Participants reported on how much they were bothered by each symptom in the past week from “not at all” to “severely” (ranging from 0 to 3 points).

Data analytic plan

Descriptive statistics were computed for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentiles were computed for categorical variables. To evaluate whether ibudilast treatment reduced symptoms of craving, depression, and anxiety over the 12‐week clinical trial, linear growth models with random intercepts and random effects of time were carried out. Subsequent growth models examined the moderating effect of sex on medication‐related changes in outcomes over time. Analyses were intention‐to‐treat and included all randomized participants (N = 102). The growth models were fit using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation in the Blimp 3 application (Keller & Enders, 2023). We used MCMC for missing data handling, as the outcomes and DPDD predictor were incomplete, and standard mixed modeling programs discard occasions with missing predictor scores. These analyses invoked a conditionally missing at random (CMAR) mechanism where missingness depends on observed scores (e.g., previous or subsequent measurements, treatment assignment, covariates) but not on the unseen data (e.g., missing craving scores at the time of dropout). Because this assumption cannot be verified without the missing scores, we performed sensitivity analyses to evaluate whether the main craving findings hold under alternative assumptions about the nonresponse process (i.e., missing not at random processes (MNAR)). See Data S1 for this approach and results (Figures S5–S8).

In line with best practice, covariates were centered at the grand mean (CGM; Enders & Tofighi, 2007). Given the longitudinal nature of this data, nested models clustered observations within‐person to preserve suitable Type I error rates (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). Normality assumptions and misspecifications were checked. Several variables, DPDD, BDI‐II, and BAI, were log‐transformed (with an added constant of 1) to minimize skewness and kurtosis and account for a substantial number of zeros.

Exploratory magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) methods and analytic plan

Due to COVID‐19 disruptions to the trial, only a subset of participants (n = 25; ibudilast: n = 15, placebo: n = 10) completed the neuroimaging scan, which assessed medication effects on a neurobiological marker of craving during trial Week 4. Neuroimaging was conducted at the UCLA Center for Cognitive Neuroscience on a 3.0 T Siemens Prisma Scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Inc., Malvern, PA). Participants completed structural (T1‐weighted) and functional scans (T2*‐weighted), including an alcohol‐cue reactivity task (Schacht et al., 2011). For visual cue exposure, participants viewed 24 blocks of alcohol beverages, nonalcohol beverages, blurred images, and a plus sign, presented pseudorandomly. Each block was comprised of five individual pictures of the same type, presented for 4.8 s, totaling 24 s per block. A 6‐s washout period followed each block.

A T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo (MPRAGE) sequence (TR = 2530 ms, TE = 1.74 ms, time to inversion = 1260 ms, flip angle = 7°, voxel size: 1 mm3, FOV = 256 mm2, ~6.2 min) was acquired for co‐registration to the functional data. A T2*‐weighted echo planar imaging (EPI) scan (TR = 2200 ms, TE = 35 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 192 mm, slices = 36, 3.0 mm, ~12 min) was acquired to examine BOLD signal during the alcohol cue reactivity task.

Neuroimaging preprocessing followed conventional procedures implemented through FMRIB Software (FSL v6.0.3, http://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl). This included motion correction through MCLFIRT, high‐pass temporal filtering (100‐s cut‐off), and smoothing with a 5‐mm full‐width, half‐maximum Gaussian kernel. Structural and functional data were skull‐stripped to remove nonbrain tissue. Individual subjects' functional images were registered to their MPRAGE using affine linear transformations and then were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) 152‐brain‐average template through nonlinear registration (Andersson et al., 2007).

For fMRI data, whole‐brain statistical analysis was performed through FSL's FEAT (Woolrich et al., 2001). The primary contrast of interest, alcohol versus nonalcohol beverage cues, was defined in the first‐level models. FLAME 1 + 2 (Woolrich et al., 2004) was used to conduct group‐level analyses, where a one‐way ANOVA evaluated the main effect of medication on alcohol cue‐elicited brain activation. Z‐statistic images were thresholded using a cluster threshold of Z > 2.3 and a corrected‐cluster significance threshold of p = 0.05. Age, sex, and cigarette smoking (yes vs. no) were included as covariates. To show continuity with findings from our laboratory's previous 2‐week trial of ibudilast implementing this paradigm (Grodin et al., 2021), a region of interest (ROI) analysis was conducted for the bilateral ventral striatum (6‐mm‐radius sphere centered at ±12 6 9 in MNI space).

RESULTS

Participant characteristics at pretreatment

Participants (N = 102; ibudilast n = 53, placebo n = 49) enrolled in the 12‐week clinical trial had the following characteristics: average age of 44 years, 40% female, and 51% identifying as White, 24% as Black or African American, and 31% as Latina/o (Table 1). In the 30 days prior to baseline, participants had an average of 23 drinking days and 7.5 DPDD. Regarding clinical symptomatology, 35% and 45% of participants had at least mild depression or anxiety symptomatology, respectively. Average PACS score at pretreatment was 13.8 points, with 41% of participants endorsing clinically significant craving.

Growth model: Craving by medication condition

Results from covariate‐adjusted linear growth model (Table 3) showed that placebo group's mean craving scores decreased by 0.52 points per month during the trial (β1 = −0.52 (0.30) χ 2 = 3.10, p = 0.078), which was nonsignificant. The ibudilast group's monthly change rate was statistically steeper, represented by a significant med × time interaction (β3 = −0.80 (0.38), χ 2 = 4.44, p = 0.035; see Figure 1). Craving scores among ibudilast group decreased by 1.32 points per month (χ 2 = 24.23, p < 0.001). By treatment end, craving scores were significantly lower in ibudilast group compared with placebo (β2 = −3.09 (1.20), χ 2 = 6.73, p = 0.009). All three covariates were significantly associated with craving, such that being female, higher pretreatment AUD severity, and greater bi‐weekly log DPDD were associated with greater craving scores on average. Correlations in Table S2 similarly suggested moderate associations between craving and drinking outcomes during the trial.

Growth model: Depression by medication condition

Results from covariate‐adjusted piecewise growth model showed that log depression scores decreased significantly from pretreatment until 1‐month postrandomization (Table 3), with log‐transformed placebo depression scores decreasing by 0.48 points (β1 = −0.48 (0.15), χ 2 = 10.65, p = 0.001) and ibudilast group by 0.37 points, which was not statistically different (β4 = 0.11 (0.19), χ 2 = 0.32, p = 0.574). During the last 2 months, depression scores continued to decrease but at a slower rate, where placebo group showed more flattening out (β2 = 0.44 (0.18), χ 2 = 6.09, p = 0.014) than ibudilast group, but not at a significantly different rate (β5 = −0.21 (0.24), χ 2 = 0.74, p = 0.389). Medication groups did not have significantly different depression scores at treatment endpoint. No covariates were significant. Follow‐up analyses accounting for medication adherence did not meaningfully change the interpretation of results and adherence was not significantly associated with depression scores (Table S3).

To explore the potential moderating effect of sex on medication‐related changes in depression, we added three‐way interactions to each timeframe: medication × sex × time, along with corresponding lower order two‐way interactions. Three‐way interaction terms were not significant (time1: β11 = 0.66 (0.39), χ 2 = 2.81, p = 0.094; time2: β12 = −0.74 (0.49), χ 2 = 2.31, p = 0.129). DISCUSSION

In this secondary analysis of a 12‐week randomized clinical pharmacotherapy trial enrolling 102 treatment‐seeking individuals with AUD, we explored whether ibudilast treatment had beneficial effects on clinical symptoms of craving, depression, and anxiety. The primary trial outcomes did not support ibudilast's efficacy on alcohol intake beyond placebo, and medication adherence was modest (Ray et al., 2025). As immune treatments for AUD have been tested in a limited number of randomized trials to date, we were interested in probing clinical mechanisms to better understand maintenance factors potentially targeted by this new class of medications. Accordingly, growth models tested whether individuals randomized to ibudilast showed steeper declines in clinical symptoms collected monthly during the trial compared to individuals randomized to placebo. Consistent with craving outcomes from prior randomized trials of ibudilast for AUD, participants in the medication group experienced significantly faster reductions in tonic craving levels, and by treatment endpoint, had significantly lower scores than the placebo group. Medication‐related reductions in craving were further supported by findings from an exploratory analysis of an fMRI‐based visual cue‐reactivity task (subsample n = 25). These results showed greater BOLD activation to alcohol cues in salience and reward circuitry in those treated with placebo compared to those treated with ibudilast. Contrastingly, rates of change for depression and anxiety were similar among ibudilast and placebo groups. Participants in both conditions reported significant reductions in depression levels during the trial, while anxiety symptoms were low and increased slightly before falling. Rates of change for the three clinical outcomes did not differ by sex during the trial. Yet at treatment endpoint, females taking ibudilast showed significantly lower PACS scores than females taking placebo.

As a PDE inhibitor, ibudilast helps normalize the cAMP signaling pathway, which is disrupted with chronic alcohol intake in specific brain regions. Ibudilast may have reduced tonic alcohol craving in this trial by restoring healthy neural transmission and reducing inflammation in relevant regions, such as the nucleus accumbens, amygdala, and hippocampus (Grigsby et al., 2023; Grodin et al., 2022; Wen et al., 2018). Our significant neurobiological craving finding for the small subsample and self‐report craving finding, which remained after accounting for medication adherence, is consistent with a previous set of multimodal findings capturing craving during earlier, shorter clinical trials of ibudilast. Ibudilast, in two prior trials, reduced tonic craving levels over 1 week (Ray et al., 2017), blunted subjective response to alcohol in the natural environment (Meredith et al., 2022), and reduced neural activation to alcohol cue exposure in the ventral striatum, which also predicted subsequent reductions in alcohol intake (Grodin et al., 2021). In this current trial, PACS scores were slightly lower than expected at pretreatment based on other pharmacotherapy trials for AUD (Foa et al., 2013). Despite this, participants enrolled in this trial were a treatment‐seeking group with moderate‐to‐severe AUD reporting heavy, consistent alcohol use, and findings supported associations between lower PACS craving scores and reduced drinking outcomes over 12 weeks. Craving is a known central maintenance factor of AUD and among the most prioritized pharmacological targets for AUD treatment (Marin et al., 2023). Craving reductions during this trial may be clinically meaningful, as the standardized mean difference between treatment groups from the main CMAR model was suggestive of a large effect size, favoring ibudilast. The sensitivity models testing MNAR processes, where attrition or missingness depends on the unseen craving values, consistently supported the overall trends that the ibudilast group had greater craving reductions than placebo. However, the size of these craving effects varied across models highlighting how complex, untestable reasons for participant dropout can influence trial conclusions. Therefore, the positive effect of ibudilast and other neuroimmune modulators on craving should be replicated in future research.

The 12‐week trial's primary drinking outcomes did not support ibudilast's efficacy for AUD, as the treatment group showed similar reductions in drinking measures as the placebo group. Thus, medication‐related reductions in craving were not sufficient to reduce self‐report heavy drinking in this sample beyond the placebo effect. It may be that despite experiencing lower levels of cravings, participants struggled to change their drinking routine, which may be better addressed with combination behavioral treatment (e.g., motivational interviewing, cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness‐based relapse prevention). Alternatively, the strong placebo response may have obscured the relationship between craving reduction and alcohol intake. While this dampens enthusiasm for ibudilast in treating AUD, these findings are not surprising given the complexity of AUD. Null findings may be attributable to a multitude of factors that impact drinking outcomes during treatment, such as social support, response bias, medication adherence, and placebo response as well as a true lack of efficacy for this compound for drinking reduction. The medication formulation necessitated taking five pills, twice daily, likely contributing to lower adherence. Future trials could explore different formulations or select similar immune compounds with lower participant burden. Additional clinical support for neuroimmune modulators comes from a pilot trial of a comparable PDE inhibitor, apremilast, which showed medication‐related reductions in alcohol intake across 11 days, contributing to a large effect size (Grigsby et al., 2023). Taken together, these findings support continued interest in PDE inhibitors for the treatment of AUD and warrant additional trialing in other research laboratories and clinical AUD samples with careful tracking of adherence and bioverification of alcohol intake.

Despite literature supporting shared neuroimmune correlates between negative affectivity and addiction (Miller & Raison, 2016; Neupane, 2016), we did not detect a beneficial effect of ibudilast on depression or anxiety scores during the trial. Ibudilast is thought to alter neurotrophic expression and microglial activation, which are dysregulated in both depression and AUD (Cho et al., 2010; Mizuno et al., 2004). Animal research has shown antidepressant and anxiogenic effects of PDE4 inhibitors similar to ibudilast (Zhang et al., 2002, 2008). However, previous findings from our laboratory have not supported a strong effect of ibudilast treatment on affect. For example, ibudilast did not alter alcohol‐induced changes in positive or negative affect (Meredith et al., 2022), nor did it improve mood on daily reports over 2 weeks (Grodin et al., 2021). Further, results on depression as a moderator of other outcomes in AUD trials of ibudilast have been mixed (Ray et al., 2017), and most recently, those with elevated depression at pretreatment did not respond favorably to treatment in regard to drinking during the 12‐week trial (Ray et al., 2025). It is notable that depression levels for participants enrolled in our laboratory's three trials have been modest. At pretreatment, only 7% of the sample was experiencing a current major depressive episode, and 35% had at least mild depressive symptoms on the BDI‐II. As such, these low negative mood scores at pretreatment likely made it more difficult to detect medication effects on mood.

Positively, participants taking ibudilast had lower depressive and anxiety symptoms on average by trial endpoint, indicating that ibudilast was not iatrogenic to mood. It is interesting that anxiety scores increased slightly from pretreatment to Week 4. This effect could potentially be due to heightened awareness of physiological symptoms after starting a new medication and paying attention to potential side effects. Further, interactions between alcohol intake and mood are complex and influenced by withdrawal and the sedative effects of alcohol. A meta‐analysis of acamprosate trials found that participants were 7.5 times more likely to have depression remit when they were able to achieve continued abstinence (Lejoyeux & Lehert, 2011). In future clinical trials, it may be worthwhile to test dynamic models, such as relations between daily fluctuations in alcohol use and mood.

As an important exploratory aim, we tested whether medication‐related changes in clinical symptoms were moderated by sex. Preclinical and initial clinical research suggests that females may be more sensitive to alcohol's effects on neuroimmune signaling and inflammation (Barton et al., 2017; Pascual et al., 2017; Zakiniaeiz et al., in press), but human research is limited. We hypothesized that females may show a stronger response to anti‐inflammatory treatment. Our results did not find significant differences between male and female change trajectories between treatment groups. Visually, females taking ibudilast tended to have faster reductions in craving, depression, and anxiety symptoms than females taking placebo pills, although differences were modest. At treatment endpoint, female participants randomized to ibudilast were estimated to have PACS craving scores nearly 6 points lower than females randomized to placebo, whereas males showed less distinction between medication groups. Male participants displayed a more robust placebo response across trial outcomes, including reductions in alcohol intake and depression. These results correspond to a sex by medication interaction detected for one of the trial's primary alcohol outcomes, drinks per day, where female participants taking ibudilast had lower scores than placebo group participants, but the opposite was true for males (Ray et al., 2025). Together, these findings show that it will be important to continue probing how treatment efficacy and clinical mechanisms might differ by sex. This trial included around 20 female participants per medication group, so these findings represent an early signal that will need to be replicated and more thoroughly studied. Future research in this area will likely require intentional efforts to recruit female participants with AUD, who are less likely to be treatment‐seeking.

This project should be considered in the context of its strengths and limitations. Notable strengths include the analysis of pertinent clinical data collected during one of the first full‐scale double‐blind randomized trials of a neuroimmune compound for the treatment of AUD. The clinical measures chosen are widely used and validated and capture relevant maintenance factors of heavy alcohol use, including negative mood and craving. These analyses took advantage of the longitudinal nature of the data to test rates of change in symptom severity from pretreatment and explored how different missingness assumptions impacted results. Further, the sample is fairly diverse regarding socioeconomic status and the distribution of race and ethnicity, with half of participants reporting a race other than White and over 30% identifying as Hispanic or Latina/o. However, the proportion of individuals identifying as Asian or Pacific Islander was very low (3%). A previously noted limitation for these analyses is modest symptoms of depression and anxiety at pretreatment, which likely impacted our ability to detect medication effects on negative mood. This could also limit the generalizability of study findings to patients with comorbid AUD and mood disorders. As anticipated, several participants dropped out of the trial, were lost to follow‐up, or did not reliably take the study medication. The trial did not include bioverification of alcohol intake or medication adherence. It will be important to replicate the sex difference finding in similar trials with a larger subsample of female participants. Lastly, due to pandemic‐related disruptions, the sample size for neuroimaging data was much smaller than anticipated, contributing to possible selection bias (e.g., lower AUD severity) and decreased power to detect medication effects. It is also possible that medication effects on cue‐reactivity could be more robust if neuroimaging is completed after a longer duration on study medication.

In summary, this project served as a secondary analysis of a 12‐week randomized, double‐blind clinical trial of ibudilast, a neuroimmune modulator, for the treatment of AUD. We tested whether participants taking ibudilast would show steeper reductions in craving, depression, and anxiety according to clinical symptom measures collected monthly during the trial. Consistent with prior research from our laboratory, ibudilast reduced tonic craving levels significantly more than placebo. Female participants taking ibudilast showed a stronger treatment response for craving by trial endpoint than female participants taking placebo, indicating potential sex differences. Medication‐related reductions in self‐report craving were supported by medication‐related activation differences during a neural cue‐reactivity task among a subsample of participants in this trial. Despite the suspected shared neuroimmune correlates between negative mood and AUD, ibudilast did not alleviate negative mood symptoms of depression or anxiety, which were nonclinically elevated at pretreatment, beyond placebo effects. Future directions include testing dynamic models of alcohol use, mood, and craving during treatment to capture the complex relations and daily fluctuations in these symptoms and intentional recruitment of female participants in future trials of immune treatments for AUD.
