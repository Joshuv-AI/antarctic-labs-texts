Targeting PD-L1-CMTM6 interactions in myeloid cells triggers PD-L1 degradation and enhances cytotoxic T-cell expansion
Authors: Michelle A Hsu, Xin Liu, Whitney Barham, Kevin D Pavelko, Susan M Harrington, Crystal Lin, Jacob B Hirdler, Zhiming Mao, Wenjing Zhang, Emilia R Dellacecca, Dallin S Ashton, Nicholas D Sun, Jessica N Lancaster, Fabrice Lucien, Haidong Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint therapies targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) have been met with limited clinical responses in the treatment of patients of varying cancer types. Current US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved therapies function to prevent PD-1 binding to PD-L1, thereby preventing T-cell suppression. However, these therapies fail to prevent PD-L1 intrinsic signaling and recycling, which can render these treatments ineffective. Moreover, most research primarily focuses on how these antibodies affect tumor cells and their interactions with T cells. Myeloid cells, which also express PD-L1 and influence T-cell responses, are integral to the efficacy of these therapies. However, strategies that can target these cells to boost antitumor responses have not been effective.MethodsOur group has developed a new anti-PD-L1 antibody (H1A), which disrupts PD-L1 recycling and redirects it towards degradation. Due to the loss of available PD-L1 protein, H1A effectively prevents PD-L1 intrinsic signaling. Using in vivo humanized PD-1/PD-L1 mouse tumor models, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of H1A against current FDA-approved PD-L1 blocking antibodies. Using human-derived immune cell in vitro assays, we pinpoint the biological consequences of H1A-induced PD-L1 degradation in myeloid cells.ResultsH1A demonstrated improved tumor control and established immunological memory responses in vivo in the treatment of tumors with moderate immunogenicity that are less responsive to current immunotherapies. This was supported by enhanced activation of myeloid cells (major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-II and CD80) and frequencies of effector T-cell populations found intratumorally. Human myeloid cells treated with H1A were also observed to have increased activation (MHC-II and CD80) and cytokine secretion. Similar to our in vivo findings, human peripheral blood lymphocyte cultures demonstrated increased frequencies of effector T-cell populations and greater tumor cell killing.ConclusionsThis work highlights the importance of targeting PD-L1 recycling and intrinsic signaling, specifically in myeloid cells, to boost antitumor responses. Our studies suggest that H1A can provide a solution to the lack of responses seen with current therapeutics, while also revealing previously unknown intrinsic functions of PD-L1 in myeloid cells.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

While current antibodies that block programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1)/programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) interactions have some clinical success, researchers are still searching for ways to improve patient responses. Limitations in response can be attributed to PD-L1 cell-intrinsic signaling that can occur independent of PD-1 ligation and PD-L1 recycling to the cell surface, which allows PD-L1 to reengage with its extrinsic and intrinsic functions. Beyond cancer cells, host myeloid cells are a large cellular source of PD-L1 and can dictate immune responses. There is a lack of knowledge detailing how PD-L1 functions intrinsically in myeloid cells and a current need to develop new strategies to target myeloid cell PD-L1 to enhance immune responses.

Introduction

Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) immune checkpoint inhibitors are the standard of care for treatment of various cancers.19 Their mechanism of action consists of blocking the interaction of PD-L1 with its cognate receptor, PD-1, thereby inhibiting T-cell responses. Unfortunately, only a minority of patients benefit from these treatments. Besides communicating inhibitory signals through PD-1, there is growing evidence that PD-L1 drives a variety of intracellular signaling cascades,10 11 leading to immunosuppression and tumor progression. In tumor cells, PD-L1 promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by suppressing destruction of EMT-related transcription factors, prevents interferon (IFN)-mediated cytotoxicity through conserved sequence motif signaling, and confers chemoresistance by stabilizing messenger RNAs (mRNAs) of DNA damage repair associated genes.1214 Therefore, it is imperative to develop therapeutic strategies that can target PD-L1’s extrinsic and intrinsic functions.

While PD-L1 function in tumor cells has been extensively described, PD-L1 is also abundant in host myeloid cells,3 15 which include dendritic cells, macrophages and monocytes. Myeloid cells are key facilitators of the immune system, operating as antigen-presenting cells to T cells that dictate their responses. Unfortunately, these cells can also be reprogrammed by tumor cells to promote tumor development and suppress T-cell responses.16 17 Strategies to specifically target myeloid cells have been developed and usually aim to target myeloid specific markers, such as colony stimulating factor 1 receptor, colony stimulating factor 1, and CCR2 (C-C chemokine receptor 2), in hopes of preventing accumulation of immunosuppressive myeloid cells within the tumor site.18 However, clinical data has demonstrated that there are limitations with these strategies as multiple clinical trials have shown little to no clinical benefit as monotherapies or in combination with other immune checkpoint inhibitors.1922 Given that PD-L1 serves as a mechanism to suppress immune responses, it is an attractive protein to target on myeloid cells. As such, many groups have identified host myeloid cell PD-L1 as a key contributor to tumor microenvironment and are essential for PD-L1 blockade responses in multiple preclinical mouse models.2328 On the clinical side, there is documented evidence that myeloid cells, specifically monocytes, can predict patient responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapies,29 30 further highlighting the impact of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis on these cells and can help explain why patients with lower tumor PD-L1 expression can respond well to PD-L1 therapies. Overall, it is clear that targeting PD-L1 expressing myeloid cells is a key factor when considering patient responses to treatment. The accumulation of evidence highlights the importance of targeting PD-L1 in myeloid cells, but there remains a lack of knowledge detailing how intrinsic PD-L1 functions in myeloid cells and how these cells can be efficiently targeted.

Mainly located at the cell surface, PD-L1 is constantly endocytosed and recycled.31 PD-L1 recycling represents a potential mechanism of resistance to PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors by constantly restoring a pool of functional PD-L1 onto the cell surface. Molecular studies of PD-L1 trafficking identified chemokine-like factor-like MARVEL transmembrane domain containing family member 6 (CMTM6) as an important regulator of PD-L1 expression at the cell surface.32 33 CMTM6 binds PD-L1 to induce its recycling and prevent its lysosomal degradation. As such, targeting the interaction between PD-L1 and CMTM6 holds promise to force PD-L1 degradation, thereby preventing its protumor and immunosuppressive activity. Our group has recently developed a novel PD-L1 antibody (H1A) that binds PD-L1 and prevents its association with CMTM6, resulting in lysosomal degradation of PD-L1. In tumor cells, H1A has been shown to restore sensitivity to chemotherapy or radiation therapy by destabilizing PD-L1 mediated protection of mRNAs encoding molecules needed for DNA repair14 and by disrupting PD-L1 promotion of EMT in triple-negative breast cancer.12 In addition, treatment of patient-derived immune cells with H1A led to expansion of effector T cells and superior T-cell cytotoxicity compared with US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved immune checkpoint inhibitors.34 Together, there is ample evidence justifying the therapeutic use of H1A against tumor cells, but the impact of PD-L1 degradation on myeloid cell function remains to be investigated.

In this study, we elucidated the molecular underpinnings and therapeutic potential of H1A-induced PD-L1 degradation in myeloid cells, while also revealing previously unknown intrinsic functions of PD-L1. We report that human and mouse myeloid cells express both PD-L1 and CMTM6 and that disrupting their association using the H1A antibody causes the downregulation of PD-L1. Our laboratory has developed a humanized PD-1/PD-L1 mouse model (huPD-H1) that allows us to test the therapeutic effects of humanized antibody treatments on tumor growth in an immunocompetent host.35 Using this mouse model, we found that huPD-H1 mice with tumors that previously reported limited response to immune checkpoint inhibitors resulted in greater tumor growth inhibition and survival when treated with H1A. Additionally, we found that H1A enhances the immunostimulatory capacity of myeloid cells through increased co-stimulation (CD80) and antigen presentation (major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-II) in both humans and huPD-H1 mice. We also found that H1A caused greater cytokine secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines in human myeloid cells. Furthermore, H1A enhances the expansion of cytotoxic T lymphocytes in both mouse tumor tissues and human peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBMCs). This work highlights the importance of targeting myeloid cell-derived PD-L1 and its association with CMTM6 to initiate innate cell activation, which in turn promotes adaptive immune responses against cancer.

Results

H1A antibody causes greater tumor regression and establishes tumor-specific immunological memory in the treatment of tumors with partial response to immune checkpoint blockade

Our group has generated a humanized antibody, H1A, that binds to human PD-L1 and promotes its degradation by preventing its association with CMTM6 (figure 1A).14 Herein, we aimed to investigate the antitumor efficacy of H1A-mediated PD-L1 degradation compared with atezolizumab (ATZ), an FDA-approved PD-L1 antibody that blocks the interaction of PD-L1 with PD-1. To demonstrate that H1A has a different binding epitope from ATZ and does not block PD-L1’s interaction with PD-1, we evaluated H1A’s ability to prevent PD-L1 from binding PD-1 in a flow cytometry cell-based PD-L1: PD-1 competition binding assay. In this assay, we used the human breast cancer cell line, MDA-MB-231, which is known to have high expression of PD-L1 on the cell surface. To assess PD-1 and PD-L1 binding, we used a soluble PD-1-Fc-biotin to bind to PD-L1 on the cell surface, followed by detection using streptavidin-Allophycocyanin (SA-APC). MDA-MB-231 cells were incubated with PD-1-Fc-Biotin at the same time as H1A or ATZ. The cells were then washed and stained with SA-APC, followed by flow cytometry detection. ATZ efficiently blocked PD-1 binding to PD-L1 on MDA-MB-231 cells in a dose-dependent manner, while H1A was unable to block this interaction, regardless of the concentration (online supplemental figure 1A and B). To further articulate the different binding sites of H1A and ATZ, we performed a competition flow experiment between the two antibodies in which MDA-MB-231 tumor cells were saturated with naked ATZ antibody, followed by staining with H1A conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 (H1A-AF647) and subsequent flow cytometry analysis. The same was done with naked H1A antibody, followed by staining with ATZ conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 (ATZ-AF647). In both cases, saturation with either naked antibody did not diminish the binding of the alternative antibody as demonstrated by the positive AF647 signal (online supplemental figure 1C and D). Together, this data demonstrates the different PD-L1 binding epitopes of H1A and ATZ.

Human and mouse PD-1 share 59.6% amino acid identity while the sequence identity of human and mouse PD-L1 is 77%.36 37 To evaluate the therapeutic effects of antibodies targeting human PD-L1 in vivo, we used our humanized PD-1/PD-L1 (huPD-H1) mouse model. To generate the huPD-H1 mice, homologous recombination was used in embryonic stem cells to replace the murine exons coding for the extracellular domains of PD-1 (exons 2 and 3) or PD-L1 (exons 3 and 4) with the corresponding human exons. To ensure normal downstream signaling of each protein within the cell, the remaining murine intracellular exons of each gene were kept intact. This genetic change was introduced into the background of C57BL/6 mice to generate humanized PD-1 (huPD-1) and humanized PD-L1 (huPD-L1) mouse strains (online supplemental figure 1E and F). Once each strain was established, we then crossbred the huPD-1 mice and huPD-L1 mice to develop our homozygous double knock-in huPD-H1 mice (online supplemental figure 1G). Having previously validated the protein expression of human PD-L1 and PD-1 in the huPD-H1 strain and confirmed that the health and immune cell compartments of these mice were consistent with the C57BL/6 wildtype (WT) counterparts, we used this mouse model to complete the following experiments assessing H1A and other anti-human PD-L1 antibodies.35 To ensure H1A and ATZ can bind to human PD-L1 expressing huPD-H1-derived immune cells, we injected these mice with poly I:C/OVA to induce PD-L1 expression systemically. After 24 hours, the mice received an injection of either H1A-AF647 or ATZ-AF647. To allow proper circulation and binding of the antibodies, blood samples of these mice were collected 1 hour after injection. The blood was then processed into PBMCs, followed by flow staining of immune cell subset markers and subsequent flow cytometry analysis. H1A-AF647 and ATZ-AF647 were bound primarily to myeloid cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and monocytes. Little to no frequencies of CD4/CD8 T cells and natural killer (NK) cells were bound to either antibody (online supplemental figure 1H). In our mouse model, we use syngeneic tumor cell lines in which the endogenous mouse PD-L1 was knocked out and replaced with human PD-L1. This allows engagement of the H1A antibody to both human PD-L1 expressing tumor cells and mouse immune cells. We tested three murine tumor models expressing the human version of PD-L1 and with variable sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade: mouse colorectal carcinoma (MC38-huPD-L1), triple-negative breast cell carcinoma (E0771-huPD-L1), and melanoma (B16F10-huPD-L1). MC38 responds well to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, while B16-F10 is refractory to immune checkpoint inhibitors.35 E0771 is poorly immunogenic but demonstrates partial response to immune checkpoint blockade.38 39 We confirmed that ATZ and H1A bind PD-L1 comparably in the three murine tumor models (online supplemental figure 1I and K). Because H1A can disrupt the association of huPD-L1 with its stabilizing protein, CMTM6, we confirmed that CMTM6 is expressed by humanized murine tumor cell lines and splenocytes from HuPD-H1 mice (online supplemental figure 1L-O). Overall, we have validated the binding of both H1A and ATZ in this mouse model.

To test the antitumor efficacy of H1A and ATZ in vivo, E0771-huPD-L1 cells were inoculated into the right hind flank of huPD-H1 mice. Once tumors were palpable, huPD-H1 mice were sorted into groups and treated with an injection of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), ATZ or H1A every 2 days for a total of five doses (figure 1B). Treatment with ATZ led to significant tumor growth delay compared with the PBS control group (p=0.029) and 43% of animals experienced complete tumor response (figure 1C–F). H1A treatment demonstrated a stronger antitumor efficacy with tumor regression (p<0.001) and complete response in 70% of animals. H1A treatment was associated with prolonged overall survival of mice compared with both PBS and ATZ groups (figure 1G). To determine whether tumor-specific immunological memory would be established in tumor-free mice after treatment with H1A or ATZ, we injected the E0771-huPD-L1 tumor cells subcutaneously on the opposite flank of the mice at least 30 days after they completely rejected the first tumor (figure 1B). Although there were no additional doses of H1A or ATZ antibodies after tumor rechallenge, all pretreated mice rejected the E0771-huPD-L1 tumors (figure 1H). To ensure responses were tumor-specific, B16F10-huPD-L1 tumor cells were also subcutaneously injected into the right underarm. Tumor growth was observed in all animals regardless of the initial treatment received (figure 1I). Our results suggest a tumor-specific immunological memory can be established in mice after both H1A or ATZ treatment, but the mice treated with H1A demonstrated a better tumor control compared with ATZ in the E0771-huPD-L1 tumor model.

Next, we evaluated the therapeutic effects of H1A and ATZ in mice bearing MC38-huPD-L1 or B16F10-huPD-L1 tumor models (online supplemental figure 2A and D). In the MC38-huPD-L1 tumor model, H1A and ATZ demonstrated moderate and comparable tumor growth inhibition (online supplemental figure 2B and C), and similar survival benefit compared with PBS (online supplemental figure 2G). In the B16F10-huPD-L1 model, tumors were resistant to H1A or ATZ treatment showing no sign of tumor growth delay compared with the control group (online supplemental figure 2E and F). Of note, due to the rapid tumor growth, B16F10-huPD-L1 tumor-bearing mice were only able to receive three doses before reaching humane endpoint and all mice enrolled in the study were euthanized by study day 28 (online supplemental figure 2H).

Taken together, our results show that H1A demonstrated better tumor control compared with ATZ in the treatment of tumors with partial response to immune checkpoint blockade, while both H1A and ATZ demonstrated similar therapeutic effects in the treatment of tumors with high immunogenicity, and no effect in those with low immunogenicity.

H1A elicits both innate and adaptive immune responses within tumor tissues

To understand the underpinnings of the improved antitumor response to H1A compared with ATZ in E0771-huPD-L1 tumors, we analyzed the immune landscape of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) across two time points (figure 2A). To examine the innate immune responses, we isolated CD45+ TILs 24 hours after the first dose of antibody injection. From there, we specifically looked at the activation status of myeloid cell populations. We found that H1A treatment resulted in significantly higher frequencies of MHC-IIhigh CD80+ monocytes (defined by TCRβ− CD11b+ Ly6C+ F4/80− cells), compared with ATZ, and trending towards significance, compared with PBS (figure 2B). Additionally, H1A treatment resulted in significantly higher frequencies of MHC-IIhigh CD80+ macrophages (defined by TCRβ− CD11b+ F4/80+ cells), compared with ATZ and PBS (figure 2C). To characterize the adaptive T-cell responses, we isolated CD45+ TILs 72 hours after treatment initiation. Looking specifically at effector phenotype-related T-cell populations, H1A treatment was associated with a significant increase in CD8+ T cells with effector potential (EOMESlow Tbethigh), compared with PBS and ATZ, as Tbet strongly represses Pdcd1 (PD-1 gene) transcription, while EOMES is a weak repressor40 41 (figure 2D). Additionally, H1A treated mice had a significant increase in CD8+ T cells with a full effector phenotype (CD11ahigh CX3CR1+), compared with ATZ, based on CX3CR1’s requirement for CD8+ T cells to reject tumors42 (figure 2E). In addition to effector T cells, we also chose to assess immunosuppressive cell subsets. Interestingly, ATZ-treated mice had higher frequencies of regulatory T cells (Tregs) (defined by TCRβ+ CD4+ FoxP3+ CD25+ cells), compared with H1A and PBS (figure 2F). Together, our results demonstrate H1A can rapidly improve the immunostimulatory function of myeloid cells already present within tumor tissue and can subsequently expand effector T-cell populations for improved tumor control. On the other hand, ATZ resulted in more immunosuppressive Tregs. Ultimately, these differences in the tumor microenvironment provide insight on why we observe improved tumor control in the H1A-treated mice, compared with those treated with ATZ.

Although we observed similar therapeutic efficacy between H1A and ATZ in the treatment of immunogenic MC38-huPD-L1 tumors, we asked whether H1A and ATZ use different mechanisms of action to achieve their therapeutic effects in this model. We isolated CD45+ TILs from mice with MC38-huPD-L1 tumors after three doses of antibody treatment (online supplemental figure 3A). The isolated TILs were then stained and processed for mass cytometry by time-of-flight analysis (CyTOF). Immune cell abundance profiles of isolated TILs were analyzed using dimensionality reduction and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) maps. RphenoGraph clustering algorithm analysis revealed 23 distinct clusters across all treatment groups (online supplemental figure 3B). Density t-SNE plots demonstrate that both H1A and ATZ-treated samples had distinct cluster populations in abundance, compared with PBS-treated samples (online supplemental figure 3C). Among the TILs, CD4 T cells (CD3e+ CD4+), CD8 T cells (CD3e+ CD8+), and B cells (CD19+) were the most dominant immune cell groups, while NK cells (NK1.1+) and myeloid cells (CD3e− CD11b+) were the least abundant subtypes (online supplemental figure 3D). Additionally, we identified lymphocyte populations with markers of cytotoxicity, such as granzyme B (Clusters 2, 18), as well as populations with markers of exhaustion, such as Lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG3), T-cell Immunoglobulin and Mucin Domain 3 (TIM3) and PD-1 (Clusters 5, 11, 21, and 9) (online supplemental figure 3F). Most TIL clusters were found upregulated in both ATZ and H1A groups compared with PBS, which aligns with the similar therapeutic effects observed in tumor-bearing mice (online supplemental figure 3E). Overall, we found that H1A treatment resulted in significantly higher frequencies in activated NK and Natural Killer T (NKT) cell populations, compared with the PBS treatment group. However, we acknowledge that ATZ has a similar effect, although it is not statistically significant (online supplemental figure 3G and H). This observation is in line with the similar tumor inhibition responses in both treatment groups. Cluster 18 was identified as an NKT cell subset (NK1.1high TCRb+ CD3e+ CD28high) with high expression of CD25 and granzyme B. Cluster 15 was identified as an NK cell subset (NK1.1+ TCRb− CD3e− CD38+) with expression of iNOS, a marker associated with NK cell cytotoxicity.43 44 Our results suggest that due to the comparable tumor responses in H1A and ATZ-treated mice, it is likely that both treatments can expand innate-like cytotoxic immune cells within tumor tissues in a similar manner.

Collectively, we found H1A treatment modulates innate and adaptive immune responses across different tumor models, with specific tumor models garnering a stronger effect from H1A.

Without direct interaction with tumor cells, H1A can interact with only host immune cells to induce an antitumor response

Because we previously observed strong antitumor responses and complementary intratumoral immune activation against E0771-huPD-L1 tumors in our huPD-H1 mice, we next investigated whether the reason for these events was due to H1A binding to and interacting with the tumor cells overexpressing human PD-L1 or if H1A was affecting the PD-L1 expressing immune cells in huPD-H1 mice. In our tumor model, there is a possibility that the E0771-huPD-L1 tumor cells were monopolizing the available H1A, leaving little for the host immune cells, specifically myeloid cells. While we previously demonstrated that E0771-huPD-L1 tumors have greater myeloid cell infiltration, it does not necessarily mean that H1A is interacting with these cells. Therefore, we next sought to determine which cells H1A can affect. To address this question, we chose to use the E0771 WT parental cell line, which expresses mouse PD-L1 only, as a tumor model in our huPD-H1 mice. By using this cell line, H1A will only be able to interact with human PD-L1 expressed by huPD-H1 immune cells as H1A, a fully humanized antibody, should not be able to bind to mouse PD-L1 (figure 2G). We first confirmed that H1A cannot bind to E0771 WT cells by flow cytometry, compared with a mouse anti-PD-L1 commercial antibody (figure 2H). ATZ has been reported to cross-react with mouse PD-L1,45 which can cause unprecedented effects in our tumor model. To keep our experiments consistent, we opted to keep ATZ as a treatment group, but we included a different anti-human PD-L1 blocking antibody that similarly does not have Antibody-Dependent Cellular Cytotoxicity (ADCC) properties, durvalumab (DUR). Unlike ATZ, DUR cannot cross-react with mouse PD-L1,46 allowing this as a proper surrogate to test whether a blocking PD-L1 antibody can have similar responses to H1A in this model. Similar to our previous tumor experiments, E0771 WT tumor-bearing mice received a treatment regimen of H1A, ATZ, DUR, or PBS every 2 days for a total of five doses. H1A treatment resulted in greater tumor growth inhibition, compared with ATZ, DUR and PBS groups (figure 2I). Additionally, H1A treatment of E0771 WT tumors had significantly greater probability of survival, compared with the other treatment groups (figure 2J,K). Although the responses observed in the E0771 WT model were not as robust as the E0771-huPD-L1 tumor model, this could likely be due to the increased immunogenicity in the tumor cells when their huPD-L1 has been targeted by H1A, therefore making those tumor cells more susceptible to the host immune response than the WT parental line. Overall, this data provides evidence that H1A does target human PD-L1 expressing immune cells to elicit an antitumor response, further justifying an advantage to the H1A antibody over the other PD-L1 blocking antibodies that did not have any response.

H1A activates human myeloid cells by disrupting the interaction between PD-L1 and CMTM6

Because we observed significantly greater antitumor responses in our huPD-H1 model in response to H1A treatment, we next sought to determine how this antibody would affect human-derived immune cells. We previously showed that H1A disrupts the interaction between PD-L1 and CMTM6 in human tumor cells.14 To determine whether H1A can block the interaction of PD-L1 and CMTM6 in human host immune cells, we first examined the expression of CMTM6 and PD-L1 in PBMCs derived from healthy donor blood samples by flow cytometry. We found that only myeloid cells (CD11c+ or CD14+) co-expressed CMTM6 and PD-L1, while other immune cell populations (T cells, NK cells, and B cells) did not express CMTM6 (figure 3A,B). To determine how H1A affects the PD-L1 expression in these myeloid cells, we treated human PBMCs with H1A, ATZ, or a control IgG1 (Ctrl IgG) in complete media supplemented with interleukin (IL)-2, IL-7, and IL-15. As H1A and ATZ are both humanized IgG1 antibodies, Ctrl IgG was used as a control to ensure that any effects observed from H1A or ATZ were due to their effects on PD-L1 and not due to non-specific binding of Fc receptor to Fc region of the IgG1 antibodies. The media supplemented with IL-2, IL-7 and IL-15 was chosen as this conditioned media has been found to be ideal in promoting expansion of antigen-specific CD8 T cells from healthy donor PBMCs suitable for immunotherapy47 and is used throughout the subsequent experiments. After a 24-hour incubation, the cells were stained with anti-CD14 (for monocytes) and an anti-PD-L1 antibody (clone E1L3N) that is specific for the intracellular domain of PD-L1. We used this antibody to ensure that any effects on PD-L1 were not due to a “masking effect” as ATZ antibody has the same binding epitope of commercial anti-PD-L1 antibodies targeting the extracellular domain of PD-L1. We found that H1A, but not ATZ, significantly reduced total PD-L1 expression in CD14+ human monocytes across multiple healthy donors (figure 3C,D).

To determine how H1A affects monocyte function following H1A treatment, we incubated isolated monocytes from human healthy donor PBMCs with the different antibody treatments for 24 hours. Similar to what we did in our mouse immune phenotyping experiments, we measured markers associated with innate immune activation (MHC-I, MHC-II, CD80, and CD86). H1A treatment significantly increased the relative expression of MHC-II and relative frequencies of CD80+ monocytes compared with ATZ across multiple healthy donors (figure 3E,F; online supplemental figure 4A and B). Interestingly, H1A treatment did not further increase the expression of MHC-I and CD86 expression as both were already highly expressed by human monocytes (online supplemental figure 4A and C). Additionally, among all the three major subsets of human monocytes (classical, intermediate, and non-classical based on their CD14 and CD16 expression)48 (online supplemental figure 4E), we found H1A treatment significantly increased MHC-II expression and frequencies of CD80+ across all subsets of monocytes compared with ATZ (online supplemental figure 4F-K). Since cytokine secretion is a feature of activated monocytes during inflammation, we assessed 13 pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines secreted in the cell culture supernatant of our human monocyte culture with the same treatments as described previously. We found IL-1β, IFN-γ, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) to be significantly elevated in human monocytes treated with H1A compared with ATZ (figure 3G–J). Next, we examined whether H1A affected the transcription of MHC-II, CD80 and the previously identified cytokines in human monocytes after H1A treatment. Our quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis shows that there were no significant increases in RNA expression for CD80, MHC-II, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in monocytes treated with H1A compared with ATZ (online supplemental figure 4L-P). In agreement with our in vivo observations of mouse myeloid cells, we found that H1A treatment promotes the immunostimulatory capacity in human monocytes through post-translational upregulation of antigen presentation and co-stimulation, while also promoting cytokine secretion.

Our data suggests that H1A treatment may regulate the observed immune activation at a protein level. To decipher the underlying mechanism, we performed a proximity labeling analysis by mass spectrometry (PL-MS) to understand the cell surface interactome of H1A-bound and ATZ-bound cells. Through this method, we can identify the proximal protein clusters on each antibody engagement with PD-L1 on the cell surface (figure 4A). Due to the variability of PD-L1 expression and responsiveness to H1A and ATZ antibodies, we used a human monocytic cell line, THP-1, using the same culture conditions to ensure consistency and sufficient amounts of protein needed for the following experiments. THP-1 cells were incubated with H1A or ATZ conjugated to horseradish-peroxidase (H1A-HRP, ATZ-HRP) for 30 min at 4°C, followed by multiple washes. Cells were then incubated for 30 min at 37°C to allow any initial biological effects to occur. To label proximal proteins to any antibody-bound PD-L1, samples were labeled with biotin-tyramide. To validate that cell-surface proteins were captured successfully, samples were assessed by flow cytometry prior to PL-MS. Samples stained with streptavidin conjugated to APC (SA-APC) demonstrated increased binding in samples treated with H1A-HRP+biotin-tyramide and ATZ-HRP+biotin-tyramide, compared with H1A-HRP and ATZ-HRP samples that did not receive biotin-tyramide labeling (figure 4B), validating the proximity labeling using the antibodies conjugated with HRP and biotin labeling. The samples and their corresponding negative controls were then processed for mass spectrometry. For the PL-MS, we repeated the proximity labeling three times. Out of the three replicates, we chose to include data from two replicates that were the most consistent with each other. In our initial quality control (QC) check, a total of 3379 proteins had been identified across all samples. We next filtered out contaminants (keratins and any fetal bovine serum (FBS)-derived proteins) and any proteins with less than two unique peptides, resulting in 3,115 proteins left for analysis. We first confirmed that the total amount of proteins detected and found that each sample across both runs had similar protein abundance and total number of proteins detected (online supplemental figure 5A and B). Additionally, we found a high correlation between replicates and across samples (online supplemental figure 5C). Only proteins that were detected in both replicates were used in the subsequent analysis.

In a PL-MS comparison, we found that 2,003 proteins were commonly found across all sample conditions (figure 4C). Each individual condition shared a small percentage of proteins with another group, while also having unique proteins specific to their treatment. Comparing each treatment with its control condition, H1A-HRP had 67 unique proteins that were significantly upregulated (figure 4D), while ATZ-HRP had 20 that were significantly upregulated (fold change (FC)>2.0, p<0.05) (figure 4E). Comparing proteins specifically found in both H1A-HRP and ATZ-HRP conditions, eight proteins were significantly enriched in the H1A-HRP group (figure 4F). Of those proteins included LYN and PTPRC (known as CD45). LYN is Src family kinase and CD45 is tyrosine phosphatase. Both act as positive and negative regulators of various signaling pathways and effector functions.49 50 Because we have observed increased activation in our human monocyte cultures treated with H1A, it could be possible that these two proteins are working in tandem to affect monocyte signaling to promote activation. Further experiments are needed to explore this theory.

To determine the biological processes associated with the identified proteins unique to each antibody interactome, we ran Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis using the STRING database.51 We input proteins identified in either the H1A interactome (online supplemental table 1) or the ATZ interactome (online supplemental table 2). In our analysis, H1A had 107 out of 134 proteins and ATZ had 53 out of 83 proteins that could be mapped to have documented or predicted protein–protein interactions based on the databases available through STRING (figure 4G, online supplemental figure 5E). Of note, H1A had three proteins that identified as E3 ubiquitin protein ligases, UBE4A, TRIP12 and HECTD1, which could tag PD-L1 for degradation.5254 Our identification of these specific proteins in association with H1A/PD-L1—but not with ATZ/PD-L1—suggests that H1A engagement with PD-L1 may promote its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. This hypothesis aligns with the known role of H1A in facilitating PD-L1 degradation. However, further investigation is warranted to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which H1A influences PD-L1 ubiquitination in association with UBE4A, TRIP12 and HECTD1.

For each interactome, enrichment using GO identified different pathways or localization within the cell that the input proteins would be associated with. Due to the H1A’s interactome having an extensive list of enrichment for biological processes, we highlighted cell migration, vesicle-mediated transport, leukocyte activation, antigen processing and presentation, and phagocytosis (figure 4H). These pathways are in line with our observation of monocyte activation, specifically MHC-II upregulation, following H1A treatment. For the ATZ interactome, the identified pathways were associated with cellular organization, transport, and metabolic processes (online supplemental figure 5F). Next, we also looked at location within the cellular components that each treatment group would likely be found. In agreement with our hypothesis that H1A induces PD-L1 lysosomal degradation, we found that the H1A group was associated with the lysosomal membrane and lysosome (figure 4I). Importantly, this was not seen in the ATZ group (online supplemental figure 5G), further highlighting the distinct effects of H1A. Overall, we found that two important signaling proteins (a kinase and a phosphatase) become upregulated in H1A compared with ATZ, which could potentially be facilitators of the observed immune activation we see in H1A-treated monocytes. Additionally, the PL-MS of H1A treated cells identified ubiquitin ligases that could be directly involved in facilitating the degradation pathway of PD-L1, while also confirming proteins directly associated with monocyte activation and antigen presentation. These results demonstrate that while both antibodies target PD-L1, each antibody has unique cell surface interactome when bound to PD-L1, which can ultimately lead to different biological consequences. More specifically, the presence of proteins unique to H1A-bound PD-L1 suggests disrupting PD-L1’s association with CMTM6 quickly induces reorganization or recruitment of proteins involved in antigen presentation and immune activation pathways, which is in line with our previous observations in both our human and mice data.

H1A expands highly cytotoxic effector cells in human PBMCs

To test the impact of H1A-induced activation of human monocytes in the PBMCs, we measured tumoricidal activity of cytotoxic T lymphocytes in human PBMCs incubated with H1A, ATZ or control IgG (figure 5A). PBMCs from patients with renal cell carcinoma (RCC) were cultured in Cytotoxic T Lymphocyte (CTL) media overnight, followed by incubation with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 antibodies to induce T-cell activation and incubation with control IgG, H1A or ATZ for 48 hours. After the incubation period, the PBMCs were co-cultured with fluorescently labeled tumor cells for 4 hours. Tumor cells that were lysed due to engagement with the PBMCs released the fluorescent dye into the cell culture supernatant, which was collected and quantified. We found the tumor-killing activity significantly increased in PBMCs treated with H1A compared with control IgG and ATZ (figure 5B). From there, we found H1A specifically expanded the frequency of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells with effector phenotype (granzyme Bhigh T cell factor 1 (TCF1)−),55 compared with ATZ or control IgG (figure 5C,D). Our results suggest that H1A treatment may induce the differentiation of effector cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which could originally be T cells in a stem-like state that were activated through the immunostimulatory function of monocytes that are enhanced by H1A treatment.

To further examine whether other immune cell subsets in the PBMC cultures are regulated by monocytes activated by H1A, we performed a more comprehensive profiling of PBMCs after incubation with H1A, ATZ, or control IgG using mass CyTOF using the same previously mentioned culture conditions. We identified 20 distinct clusters across all samples using the RphenoGraph clustering algorithm analysis (figure 5E), in which the density t-SNE plots demonstrated that H1A-treated PBMCs had distinct cluster populations compared with ATZ and control IgG (figure 5F). Based on functional and activation markers, we identified the presence of regulatory T cells (CD4+ CD25high FoxP3+) and effector T cells (granzyme B+, granzyme K+, NKG7+) (figure 5H). Comparing the percentages of each cluster using a heatmap generated by RphenoGraph, we found that the H1A-treated PBMCs had varying frequencies of clusters distinct from ATZ-treated and control IgG-treated PBMCs (figure 5G). We found that H1A treatment significantly expanded three cytotoxic T-cell clusters (C6, C10 and C13) based on their expression of granzyme B/K and NKG7 (figure 5J–L), and one cluster of activated NK cells (C16) (figure 5M). Interestingly, the cluster of regulatory T cells (C14), denoted by expression of CD25/FoxP3 and PD-L1/T-cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domains (TIGIT), was significantly decreased in H1A-treated PBMCs, compared with ATZ-treated or control IgG-treated PBMCs (figure 5N). Together, our results demonstrate H1A’s unique therapeutic potential of indirectly expanding cytotoxic lymphocytes by upregulating the immunostimulatory capacity of myeloid cells.

Discussion

Current antibodies targeting PD-L1 aim to block its engagement with PD-1. However, these therapies are insufficient to induce durable responses in a majority of patients with cancer due to PD-L1’s ability to recycle and its own intrinsic cellular functions observed in tumor cells. This is further complicated by the presence of PD-L1+ myeloid cells, in which PD-L1’s intrinsic functions have not been thoroughly investigated. The goal of our research was to address this gap in knowledge, while also characterizing a novel PD-L1 inhibitor (H1A), which disrupts the interaction of PD-L1 and CMTM6 at the cell surface and induces PD-L1 degradation. In this report, we determined the therapeutic efficacy of our H1A antibody in our huPD-1/huPD-L1 mouse model and human-derived immune cells. We first demonstrated that H1A can suppress tumor growth to a greater degree than ATZ, an FDA-approved antibody that blocks PD-L1 binding to PD-1. Next, we found that H1A activated myeloid cells intratumorally in our huPD-H1 tumor model. On further testing, H1A was observed to induce host immune cell activation to promote antitumor responses without the need to bind to PD-L1-expressing tumor cells. In line with our in vivo data, human myeloid cells in PBMC cultures demonstrated increased activation through MHC-II and CD80 expression. H1A-treated human myeloid cells also demonstrated elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. Finally, we found that H1A increased the cytotoxicity of T cells in human PBMCs through the expansion of effector T cells, a finding that was also reflected in our ex vivo TIL analysis data based on the presence of greater effector T-cell populations. On the other hand, our finding indicates that H1A likely induces expansion of NK and NKT cells indirectly through activation of myeloid cells. These activated myeloid cells—such as dendritic cells and macrophages—produce key cytokines (eg, IFN-α, IFN-β, IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, IL-23, IL-27) that promote NK cell activation. Additionally, NK cells may be stimulated via engagement of NKG2D receptors with ligands like MIC-A/B. For NKT cells, activation occurs through recognition of lipid antigens presented by CD1d molecules on antigen-presenting cells, which could also be influenced by H1A-driven myeloid activation. To that end, future studies are needed to confirm these mechanisms. Collectively, our results revealed PD-L1’s intrinsic capabilities of suppressing myeloid cell activation and that inducing its degradation through H1A improved these functions. Overall, this report supports the therapeutic potential of H1A as a novel PD-L1-targeting agent to induce both innate and adaptive immunity.

The therapeutic potential of H1A was first noticed when this antibody was incubated with human PBMC isolated from patients with RCC, in which we found H1A increased the function of cytotoxic lymphocytes in patients’ PBMCs.34 However, the underlying mechanism of action and potential therapeutic effects of H1A had not been addressed. In this report, we evaluated the therapeutic effects of H1A in vivo in the treatment of tumors growing in our huPD-H1 mice. Additionally, we dissected H1A’s mechanism of action in the context of myeloid cells and their immunostimulatory capacity. A unique therapeutic feature of H1A is that this antibody significantly suppressed the growth of tumors (E0771-huPD-L1) with less immunogenicity that resulted in prolonged survival and a higher percentage of complete responders with an established memory of tumor immunity, compared with ATZ. This unique therapeutic potential of H1A could be due to its ability to activate myeloid cells through upregulation of MHC-II and CD80. Both MHC-II and CD80 are required in antigen presentation and co-stimulation to efficiently activate T-cell responses. Additionally, H1A induced the release of proinflammatory cytokines in human monocytes, a feature that influences T-cell differentiation. Accordingly, H1A treatment expanded cytotoxic T-cell populations in vivo in huPD-H1 mice and in vitro in human PBMC cultures. To our best knowledge, this is the first report about the impact of disrupting PD-L1 and CMTM6 in myeloid cells, which subsequently demonstrates an enhanced immunostimulatory capacity in both human and mouse systems. Because CMTM6 expression is limited to myeloid cells and does not overlap with other lymphocytes, targeting the PD-L1/CMTM6 axis in myeloid cells concurrently with the PD-L1/PD-1 axis in T cells provides an opportunity for therapeutic synergy. Additionally, some tumor cells have been reported to co-express PD-L1 and CMTM6.32 33 This means that H1A can also disrupt this interaction, leading to the downregulation of PD-L1 in tumor cells and diminishment of PD-L1’s role in immune evasion. Thus, targeting PD-L1 and CMTM6 provides a more synergistic approach in cancer immunotherapy through disarmament of the immunoregulatory role of PD-L1 in both myeloid cells and tumor cells.

In our report, our H1A antibody showed superior therapeutic effects in tumor control and survival in the E0771 tumor model, compared with ATZ, while we did not observe these effects in the MC38 and B16F10 models. One possible explanation could be due to the higher infiltration of myeloid cells in the E0771 tumor, which has been characterized by a high monocyte infiltration resulting in accumulation of suppressive tumor-associated macrophages.56 Future studies can compare the immune cell compartment of each tumor type to evaluate any correlation between myeloid cell populations and responsiveness to H1A. The use of H1A as a therapeutic to treat cancer could potentially rely on the presence of PD-L1+ myeloid cells within the tumor.

As the first report detailing the therapeutic potential of an antibody that can disrupt the interaction of PD-L1 and CMTM6 in myeloid cells, our observations and conclusions of H1A are limited to our huPD-H1 tumor models and human monocyte/PBMC cultures that examine H1A’s effect on tumor growth as well as immune cell activation and cytotoxicity. Another limitation in our studies is the use of the THP-1 cell line to examine the H1A-bound PD-L1 surface interactome. We acknowledge that this cell line does not perfectly replicate human monocytes. However, this cell line was used due to the large amount of protein needed for cell-surface capture validation experiments and PL-MS. Additionally, the use of THP-1 cells allowed for more consistent testing because human monocytes tend to have variable expression of PD-L1 and responsiveness to treatment. A future direction would be to validate our findings by performing a smaller scale cell surface capture using the same proximity labeling methods we used in this paper with human monocytes from multiple donors and confirm the accumulation of proteins we identified in the H1A-bound PD-L1 interactome. We acknowledge that more studies are needed to fully dissect the underlying mechanism of action of H1A in the context of PD-L1 cell-intrinsic function in myeloid cells. Based on our data, the disruption of PD-L1 and CMTM6 mediated by H1A seems to induce the reorganization or recruitment of surface proteins involved in antigen presentation, ultimately leading to increased MHC-II expression. Secreted cytokine release may be a consequence of this biological event as MHC-II expression has been previously found to affect cytokine responsiveness in human and mouse immune cells.57 While MHC-II expression can correlate with CD80 expression, there could be other mechanisms at play that are PD-L1 mediated. More specifically, the increased CD80 could be due to loss of PD-L1 on the cell surface as PD-L1 has been demonstrated to have cis-interactions with CD80 on the cell membrane.58 Without PD-L1 present, CD80 has been shown to form homodimers with itself. In the context of our experiments, the degradation of PD-L1 by H1A could lead to more CD80 homodimer formation, therefore leading to higher expression observed. With these considerations, the potential pathway stemming from PD-L1 and CMTM6 should be further dissected in the context of protein signaling factors such as protein phosphorylation. Therefore, our future studies will be directed towards deciphering the signaling mechanism of myeloid cells following H1A treatment.

Another aspect of our report that would require further investigation is the mechanism by which H1A disrupts the interaction between PD-L1 and CMTM6. Although previous studies have not pinpointed the exact interaction regions between CMTM6 and PD-L1, a recent preprint offers a structural prediction. It suggests that the transmembrane domain (TMD) of hPD-L1 likely interacts with the TM2/TM3 regions of bovine CMTM6.59 This interaction is primarily mediated by conserved hydrophobic residues on both proteins, indicating a potentially conserved binding pattern across species. However, how the binding site of H1A on the extracellular domain would affect the TMD of PD-L1 needs further investigation. Our future studies will be directed at investigating this perspective by determining whether H1A may cause conformation changes in PD-L1 in transmembrane space that would release PD-L1 from the binding with CMTM6 in direction to H1A-induced PD-L1 degradation.

Taken together, we demonstrated that by disrupting PD-L1 and CMTM6 in myeloid cells, H1A ultimately led to enhanced activation of myeloid cells through antigen presentation, co-stimulation and cytokine secretion. This highlights H1A’s unique advantage in the treatment of tumors with moderate immunogenicity that are less responsive to current immunotherapy. Thus, our study presents this H1A antibody as a next-generation class of PD-L1 inhibitors for treatment of cancers that are refractory to current cancer immunotherapies.

Materials and methods

Experimental models

H1A antibody

The humanized anti-human PD-L1 antibody H1A (Clone HC4LC1) was produced with a contract at the Fusion Antibodies Public Limited Company (PLC), with a purity of >95% by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), <2endotoxin (EU)/mg of endotoxin.

Animals

HuPD-1/huPD-L1 transgenic mice (huPD-H1 mice) were generated and housed in animal facilities located at Mayo Clinic, Rochester Minnesota, USA, under Dr Haidong Dong’s protocol (A00002530-17R23), which was approved by Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were housed under pathogen-free and temperature-controlled conditions. 6–8 weeks old male and female mice were used in all experimental studies. Currently, our huPD-H1 mice are deposited and available at Jackson Laboratory (C57BL/6-Pdcd1tm1.1(PDCD1)Hdng Cd274tm1.1(CD274)Hdng/J with Strain #:040677 and RRID:IMSR_JAX:040677).

Cell lines

MDA-MB-231 tumor cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (ATCC HTB-26) and were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)+10% FBS+1% penicillin/streptomycin.

MC38-huPD-L1 tumor cells were generated within the Dong Lab as previously described35 and cultured in Rosewell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640+10% FBS+1% penicillin/streptomycin under antibiotic selection with G418 (0.3 mg/mL) (Gibco catalog 10131027).

E0771-huPD-L1 tumor cells were gifted by Dr Kun Ling,12 Mayo Clinic, and were cultured in DMEM+10% FBS+1% penicillin/streptomycin under antibiotic selection with puromycin (1 µg/mL) (InvivoGen catalog ant-pr-1).

B16F10-huPD-L1 tumor cells were generated within the Dong Lab as previously described60 and were cultured in RPMI 1640+10% FBS+1% penicillin/streptomycin under antibiotic selection with G418 (0.8 mg/mL).

E0771 WT tumor cells were obtained from ATCC (ATCC CRL3461) and were cultured in DMEM+10% FBS+1% penicillin/streptomycin.

THP-1 cells were gifted by Dr Leigh Griffiths, Mayo Clinic (ATCC TIB-202) and were cultured in RPMI 1640+10% FBS+1% penicillin/streptomycin unless stated otherwise.

Methods details

Evaluation of preventing PD-1: PD-L1 interactions with PD-L1 antibodies

The abilities of anti-human PD-L1 antibodies: H1A and ATZ (provided by Mayo Clinic Pharmacy) to prevent PD-L1 from binding PD-1 were evaluated using a cell-based blocking/binding assay. To detect PD-L1 and PD-1 binding, MDA-MB-231 cells (known for high human PD-L1 expression) were treated with 10 µg of soluble Biotinylated Recombinant Human PD-1-Fc (PD-1-Fc-Biotin) for 30 min at 4°C. H1A and ATZ were added at various concentrations at the same time as the PD-1-Fc-Biotin to compete for binding of PD-L1. After the incubation, the cells were washed multiple times with PBS. Cells were then treated with streptavidin-APC for 15 min at 4°C, followed by multiple wash steps. Samples were read on the Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer running SpectroFlow software. Subsequent flow analysis was completed using FlowJo.

Fluorescent tagging of H1A and ATZ antibodies

1 mg of each antibody was conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 using Alexa Fluor 647 antibody labeling kit (Invitrogen catalog A20173) per manufacturer’s protocol. These conjugated antibodies are referred to as H1A-AF647 and ATZ-AF647. Both antibodies were diluted in PBS for a final concentration of 1 mg/mL.

Flow cytometry

Cells were first stained with a fixable Live/Dead dye (Zombie UV (BioLegend catalog 423108) or Ghost Dye 510 (Tonbo Reagents catalog 13–0870-T100)) in PBS at a 1:1,000 dilution for 30 min at 4°C in the dark, followed by two washes with fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS+2% FBS+2 mM EDTA). Cells were then incubated with TruStain FcX for mouse (BioLegend catalog 101320) or TruStain FcX for human (BioLegend catalog 422302) to block non-specific binding of Fc receptors in FACS buffer for 5 min at 4°C in the dark, followed by staining of conjugated surface marker antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark in a final volume of 100 µL of FACS buffer. Lists of flow antibodies used in mouse and human experiments are detailed in online supplemental tables 3 and 4. This was followed by two washes with FACS buffer. For cells that only required surface staining, cells were then resuspended in 200 µL of FACS buffer and read on Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer using SpectroFlow software. For cells that required intracellular staining, cells were resuspended and fixed in 200 µL of fixation buffer using eBioscience intracellular fixation and permeabilization buffer set (Invitrogen 88-8824-00) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Intracellular proteins were then stained in 100 µL of 1x eBioscience permeabilization buffer for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Cells were then washed two times and resuspended in 200 µL of FACS buffer. Fluorescence minus one was used as negative controls for gating purposes. Single-color stained UltraComp eBeads Compensation Beads (Invitrogen catalog 01-2222-42) were used for unmixing. Samples were read on the Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer running SpectroFlow software. Subsequent flow analysis was completed using FlowJo.

H1A/ATZ antibody binding validation of huPD-H1 PD-L1+ immune cells

To upregulate PD-L1 expression on immune cells, female huPD-H1 mice were injected intraperitoneally with 0.5 mg of OVA protein (Sigma catalog A5503) and 50 µg of poly I:C (Novus catalog NBP2-25288) resuspended in 200 µL of PBS. 24 hours later, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 µg of H1A-AF647 or ATZ-647 (n=4 mice per antibody treatment). One mouse that received OVA/poly I:C injection did not receive any antibody treatment and was reserved for flow cytometry controls. To allow antibody circulation and binding, blood collection was completed approximately 1 hour following antibody injection via cardiac puncture with an EDTA-coated syringe. PBMCs were then isolated from blood using Lymphoprep with high-speed centrifugation and multiple washing steps with PBS. Red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed using ACK lysis buffer. Cells were resuspended in PBS and counted. 1×106 cells were aliquoted per sample and plated onto a 96-well U-bottom plate for subsequent flow staining.

In vivo huPD-H1 tumor experiments

Because our laboratory is responsible for breeding and maintaining this strain within Mayo Clinic animal facilities, the number of mice used per experiment was determined based on the availability of mice at the given time. To perform statistics, we chose to enroll a number greater than five mice per group per tumor experiment. For studies using MC38-huPD-L1 and B16F10-huPD-L1 tumor cells, approximately equal numbers of male and female mice were used. Because E0771-huPD-L1 and E0771 WT tumor cells are derived from breast cancer, only female mice were used in these studies. For each tumor study, the indicated number of tumor cells per injection (MC38-huPD-L1 = 0.75×106; E0771-huPD-L1/E0771 WT=1.0×106; B16F10-huPD-L1=0.5×106) was suspended in 100 µL of PBS and injected subcutaneously on the right hind flank of huPD-H1 mice. Once tumors were palpable, mice were sorted into groups based on tumor size such that the average tumor volume burden was similar across groups. Tumor measurements of each individual mouse were taken three times per week by calipers. Tumor volume was calculated based on the following formula: tumor volume=0.5×length×width2. Drug treatments included 200 µg each of H1A or ATZ in 200 µL of PBS. 200 µL of PBS was used as a negative vehicle control. Drug treatments were injected intraperitoneally every 2 days, for a total of five doses unless mice reached an endpoint before all doses were completed. In order to minimize potential confounders in all experiments, mice of different treatment groups were co-housed (mixed among the cages) and the order in which the cages received treatments varied each week. Laboratory members performing caliper measurements of mouse tumors were blinded to treatment group. Only laboratory members who did not perform caliper measurements were permitted to know assigned treatment conditions and dose mice accordingly. Mice were euthanized if they reached the designated endpoint (Tumor volume (TV)≥1,500 mm3) or if tumors became ulcerated. Survival was reported using Kaplan-Meier curves. Mice that received any treatment were included in the final analysis, and there were no exclusions.

For rechallenge studies, huPD-H1 mice that received initial E0771-huPD-L1 tumor challenge were required to have no visible tumor after treatment to be considered a complete responder. These mice were then monitored for an additional 30 days to ensure no spontaneous tumor growth. Following this, E0771-huPD-L1 tumor cells were injected subcutaneously into the left hind flank of complete responder mice, while B16F10-huPD-L1 tumor cells were injected subcutaneously near the right underarm. Naïve huPD-H1 mice were used as a negative control. The endpoint for these studies is if the total volume of both tumors is equal to or greater than 1,500 mm3 or if tumors became ulcerated.

HuPD-H1 tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte isolation

Tumors were excised from huPD-H1 mice at designated study day. Tumors were then cut into smaller pieces, followed by automated mechanical and enzymatic digestion using gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters and tumor dissociation kits for mouse (Miltenyi Biotec catalog 130-096-730). Once samples were in single-cell suspensions, TILs were further isolated using EasySep Mouse TIL (CD45) Positive Selection kits (STEMCELL catalog 100–0350), per manufacturer’s protocol. TILs were then counted, and 1×106 cells per sample were plated on a 96-well U-bottom plate for subsequent flow staining and analysis.

Human PBMC preparation and monocyte isolation

PBMCs from patients and healthy donors were isolated from blood by high-speed centrifugation with Lymphoprep (STEMCELL catalog 07861) and SepMate devices (STEMCELL catalog 85450). The remaining RBCs were lysed using ACK lysis buffer, followed by washing with PBS. PBMCs were then counted and plated as described. Any unused PBMCs were resuspended in CryoStor freeze medium and stored in −80°C until future use. Monocytes were isolated from PBMCs using EasySep Human Monocyte Isolation kit (STEMCELL catalog 19359) and subsequently resuspended in RPMI 1640+10% FBS+1% penicillin/streptomycin unless stated otherwise.

Human PBMC/monocyte activation and treatment

Human PBMCs and isolated monocytes were cultured in complete RPMI 1640+10% FBS+1% penicillin/streptomycin supplemented with recombinant human Interleukin 2 (rhIL2) (10 U/mL) (PeproTech catalog 200-02-100UG), rhIL15 (10 ng/mL) (PeproTech catalog 200-15-100UG), and rhIL7 (10 ng/mL) (PeproTech catalog 200-07-50UG). For T-cell activation in PBMC cultures, ImmunoCult Human CD3/CD28 T Cell Activator (STEMCELL catalog 100–0784) was added. For treatment with H1A or ATZ, antibodies were added at the same time of activation at 20 µg/mL. Control Human IgG1 was used as a negative control and used at 20 µg/mL. For PBMC cultures, samples were incubated for 48 hours. For monocyte cultures, samples were incubated for 24 hours.

Multiplex cytokine detection

Monocytes were collected after 24-hour activation as previously described and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min. Cell culture supernatant was collected from monocytes and stored at −20°C until use. 13 pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines were assessed across multiple donors using LEGENDplex multiplex immunoassay (BioLegend catalog 740809), per manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were read on Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer running SpectroFlow software. Analysis was completed using LEGENDplex Data Analysis Software Suite.

RT-qPCR

Monocytes were collected after 24-hour activation as previously described and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min. Supernatant was discarded and cells were resuspended in TRIzol. Samples were processed using RNA Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo Research catalog R1013), per manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop Instrument. Equal amounts of RNA were used for reverse transcription, which was completed with SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Invitrogen catalog 18080–400). The obtained complementary DNA was then used to complete quantitative PCR with PowerUP SYBR Green Master Mix (Invitrogen Catalog A25742) and the listed primers using QuantStudio V.3. All test mRNA was normalized to 18S. Gene and primer sequences are shown in online supplemental table 5.

Proximity labeling of THP-1 cells

0.3 mg of H1A or ATZ were labeled with HRP, using EZ-Link Activated Peroxidase Antibody Labeling Kit (Thermo Scientific catalog 31497), per manufacturer’s protocol. 150×106 THP-1 cells were cultured in previously mentioned CTL media at 5×106 cells per mL for 24 hours at 37°C. Cells were collected, spun down, and resuspended in PBS+2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 10×106 cells per mL. Cells were then incubated with Human TruStain FcX (Fc Receptor Blocking Solution) (BioLegend catalog 422302), per manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were then aliquoted at 30×106 cells per sample and resuspended in a final volume of 10 mL of PBS+2% BSA. Samples were then incubated with 5 µg/mL of H1A-HRP or ATZ-HRP for 30 min at 4°C with constant rotation. Cells were then washed two times with cold PBS and resuspended in 10 mL of PBS. Cells were incubated for 30 min at 37°C. H1A-HRP and ATZ-HRP bound PD-L1, and proximal proteins were labeled with biotin-tyramide (APExBIO catalog A8011) labeling buffer (50 mM Tris-HCL pH7.4, 0.03% H2O2, 80 µg/mL tyramide-biotin) for 1 min at room temperature. 1,000 units of catalase (100 units/mL) (Sigma Aldrich catalog C1345-1G) was added to samples for 5 min on ice. Cells were spun down and resuspended in 10 mL of antibody stripping buffer (50 mM glycine, 150 mM NaCl, 0.9 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2). Cells were spun down and resuspended in PBS. For flow validation, 1×106 cells of each sample were aliquoted, spun down and resuspended in 100 µL of FACS buffer and stained with streptavidin-APC (BioLegend catalog 405207) per manufacturer’s protocol. The remaining cells were then lysed in 400 µL of 8M urea buffer (8M Urea in 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) buffer pH 8), followed by probe sonication. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatants were collected for a bicinchoninic acid protein assay to quantify protein concentration. For streptavidin pull-down experiments, 100 µg of total protein was aliquoted.

Streptavidin pulldown

110 µL of Pierce streptavidin beads (10 mg/mL) (Thermo Scientific catalog 88817) were put in a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The tube was placed into a magnetic stand to collect the beads against the side of the tube. The supernatant was discarded. 1 mL of 20 mM TEAB lysis buffer was added to the tube, which was then inverted several times to mix; the beads were again collected on the magnetic stand and the supernatant discarded. This wash was repeated. The beads were resuspended in 11 µL 20 mM TEAB and mixed well. 20 µL of beads were added to each sample and rotated overnight at 4°C. The following morning, the beads were separated from the solution by a magnetic stand, and the supernatant saved in separate 2 mL tubes. 1 mL of 1M KCl was added to samples and mixed, rotated for 5 min at 4°C, and spun at 3,000×g for 5 min. This was repeated successively with 1 mL 0.1M sodium carbonate, 1 mL 2M urea in 10 mM Tris pH 8.2, and two times with 1 mL 10 mM Tris/0.0002% Z3-16. 25 µL 10 mM Tris/0.0002% Z3-16 with 1 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) was added to each tube, put on a thermomixer at 1,000 rpm at 60°C for 13 min, then cooled on ice for 30 s. 1 µL of 50 mM iodoacetamide (2.5 mM final concentration) was added, and the sample placed on a thermomixer at 1000 rpm for 15 min. 1 µL of Trypsin/LysC (0.05 µg/µL) was added to the samples and digested overnight at 37°C on the thermomixer. After 18 hours, a magnet was used to separate the digest from the beads, and the solution was transferred to a mass spec sample vial. 2 µL of 10% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was used to acidify the sample.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

Extracted peptides were analyzed by nano-electrospray ionization (ESI)-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS) with an Exploris 480 mass spectrometer coupled to a Vanquish Neo nano-LC system (Thermo Scientific; Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The LC system used a gradient with solvent A (2% acetonitrile (ACN), 0.2% formic acid, in water) and solvent B (80% ACN, 10% isopropyl alcohol (IPA), 0.2% formic acid, in water) as follows: 0–80 min, 3–40% B gradient curve 6; 80–90 min, 40–90% B gradient; 90–100 min, 90% B; followed by column equilibration, all at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The mass spectrometer had a resolution of 120,000 at 200 m/z and used data-dependent acquisition, with a full MS1 scan ranging from 350 to 1500 m/z. Dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s. Cycle time was 3 s. All MS/MS spectra were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer 3.0 from Thermo Scientific. Each was set-up to search the current uniprot database, assuming trypsin digestion with up to three miscleavages with a fragment ion tolerance of 0.2 Da and parent ion tolerance of 10 ppm (uniprot is provided in the public domain by the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, Geneva, Switzerland,www.uniprot.org). Oxidation of methionine, biotin-tyramide on tyrosine, and NHS-LC-Biotin on lysine were set as dynamic modifications, and carbamidomethylation of cysteine (an iodoacetamide derivative) was set as a static modification.

LC-MS data analysis

Contaminants, such as keratins and FBS-derived proteins, were filtered out of analysis. Only proteins with at least two spectral peptides were kept for analysis. Protein abundance was transformed in log2 scale and missing value imputation was applied using Perseus. The missing values were replaced by random numbers drawn from a normal distribution of 1.8 SD down shift and with a width of 0.3 of each sample. Sample-wise normalization was applied. A simple median-centering (subtract each sample’s median) aligns all replicate distributions, ensuring downstream statistical comparisons are not biased by outliers. Differential expression protein analysis was conducted with multiple t-testing and two-stage step-up (Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli) with an False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction set at 0.05. Protein–protein interactions and pathway enrichment analysis for each antibody interactome was conducted using the STRING database.

Tumor cell killing assay

786-O (target cells) were labeled with calcein-AM (Invitrogen, catalog C1430) at 5 µmol/L for 30 min in the dark at 37°C. Cells were then washed two times with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and resuspended in RPMI 1640 at 1×105/mL. Human PBMCs (effector cells) and target cells were mixed at a 1:20 target to effector ratio in FBS-free RPMI 1640 and plated into a 96-well plate. The plate was then centrifuged for 1 min at 300×g, followed by incubation at 37°C for 4 hours. As a positive control, 0.1% of saponin was added to wells that only contained target cells to provide a measurement for “maximum calcein release”. Additionally, wells containing target cells without any effector cells were included to measure any spontaneous release of calcein. Wells containing only media were measured to assess any background fluorescence. All conditions were performed in four replicates. After incubation, the plate was centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min. 100 µL of supernatant was then transferred to a new 96-well plate. Fluorescence was then measured at an excitation of 485×20 with an emission filter of 528×25 using BioTek Synergy HTX multimode reader. Any background fluorescence values detected from media-only wells were subtracted from experimental and control wells. To determine the percentage of cytotoxicity, we employed the following formula: cytotoxicity % = 100 × ((experimental condition value – spontaneous release value) ÷ (maximum release value – spontaneous release value)).

Mass cytometry by time-of-flight

Samples were first blocked with Fc block (BD Biosciences) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by staining for cell surface markers in cell staining buffer (Fluidigm catalog NC0501752) at room temperature for 30 min. Intracellular marker staining was performed using Cytofix/Cytoperm Kit (BD Biosciences catalog 554714) per manufacturer’s protocol. List of antibodies used is detailed in online supplemental tables 6 and 7. DNA intercalation was performed using 1:10,000 dilution of 125 µM of Cell-ID Intercalator-Ir (Fluidigm 201192A) with gentle agitation at 4°C for 30 min. Once staining was completed, cells were stored in 1% methanol-free formaldehyde in PBS until data collection. Samples were acquired using Helios mass cytometer (Fluidigm). EQ four-element calibration beads (Fluidigm Cat. No. 201078) were spiked into each sample to allow for signal normalization. Normalization was performed using CyTOF software (Fluidigm). FlowJo software was then used for sample cleanup based on center, time and offset parameters, followed by exclusion of equalization beads. Gating for 191Ir+ and 193Ir+ DNA intercalators was used to discern singlet cells, followed by 195 Pt− to identify live cells. All live singlet events were then exported as a new “.fcs” file. All newly exported fcs files were uploaded into an R-based tool, Cytofkit. Samples uploaded into Cytofkit were then used for RphenoGraph clustering algorithm (t-SNE) and subsequent t-SNE analysis using the markers listed in each respective table. For t-SNE analysis, files were sorted based on treatment: control IgG, H1A or ATZ. Additionally, t-SNE clustering was performed on 2,000 equivalent events from each sample with selection of all parameters.

Statistical analysis

To compare two groups, Student’s t-tests (parametric) and Mann-Whitney tests (non-parametric) were used to generate statistics. To compare three groups, one-way analysis of variance (parametric) and Kruskal-Wallis (non-parametric) tests were used to generate statistics. To perform a parametric test, data sets were evaluated for a Gaussian distribution. If there was not a Gaussian distribution, it was evaluated using a non-parametric test. For probabilities of survival in the mouse experiments, a log-rank test was performed to compare two groups. Results that were considered significant based on p values≤0.05 were displayed on the figures. Graphs and statistics were generated using GraphPad Prism V.10.
