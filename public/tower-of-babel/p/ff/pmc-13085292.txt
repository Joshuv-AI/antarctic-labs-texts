AI-powered surveillance of bronchiolitis in the Nirsevimab era: comparative performance of machine learning, deep learning, and large language models on free-text ED records
Authors: Marianna Costa, Mohd Rashid Khan, Luca Vedovelli, Dario Gregori, Danila Azzolina, Silvia Bressan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBronchiolitis remains a frequent reason for hospitalization in infants during the winter season. Epidemiologic surveillance remains crucial in the era of widespread immunoprophylaxis for the leading viral agent causing bronchiolitis. We investigated the performance of classical machine learning (ML) models, Deep Learning (DL), and a pre-trained large language model (LLM) in classifying bronchiolitis diagnosis from the free-text-diagnosis field of the emergency department electronic health records (EHRs). As a secondary aim, we evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of the actual official administrative ICD-9 encoding for Bronchiolitis diagnosis.Methods28,557 records of infants < 1 year with complete discharge diagnoses fields were retrieved between the years 2007–2018 and manually classified by an expert pediatrician to create the gold standard diagnosis set for training the algorithm. After data pre-processing, classical ML models (Random Forest, Decision Tree, Gradient Boosting Machine, Linear Discriminant Analysis, Support Vector Machine), a Deep Learning (DL) tool, and a pre-trained LLM (GPT-5) were evaluated using balanced accuracy, sensitivity, and F1 scores. The official administrative ICD-9 encoding classification accuracy was compared to the gold standard.ResultsOverall, 1,903 of 28,557 records (6.7%) were classified as bronchiolitis by the gold standard approach. The DL model and GPT-5 outperformed traditional ML models, achieving higher sensitivities (0.97, 95%CI 0.96-1.00, and 0.98, 95% CI 0.98–0.99, respectively), F1 scores (0.96, 95% CI 0.95–0.99, and 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–0.99, respectively), and balanced accuracy (0.98, 95%CI 0.98-1.00, and 0.99, 95% CI 0.99–0.99, respectively). Traditional ML models showed sensitivities between 0.77 and 0.98, F1 scores between 0.86 and 0.96, and balanced accuracies between 0.88 and 0.96. ICD-9 codes showed sensitivity of 85.9% (95% CI 84.27–87.45), and specificity of 98.5% (95% CI 98.36–98.65).ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first study directly comparing an LLM, deep learning, and multiple classical ML models for bronchiolitis surveillance in the post-Nirsevimab era. DL and GPT-5 outperformed traditional ML-based tools in identifying bronchiolitis diagnoses and ICD-9 diagnosis coding. AI-based tools hold significant potential for improving epidemiologic surveillance of bronchiolitis from emergency department EHRs.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01468-6.

Introduction

Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) is the leading cause of bronchiolitis, a lower respiratory tract infection that can be severe, especially in infants younger than 12 months of age. Bronchiolitis is responsible for high yearly hospitalization rates worldwide [1–3]. A recent systematic review has shown that, in industrialized countries, hospital admissions for RSV-associated bronchiolitis are approximately 298,000 (227 000–391 000) per year [4].

A prospective population-based study found that RSV-associated hospitalizations among children under 24 months of age account for 26% of hospitalized pediatric patients with acute respiratory infections [5].

In the past years substantial progress has been made in the development of effective long-lasting pharmacologic prevention strategies for RSV-associated infections [6, 7]. Until recently, prophylaxis could only count on monthly intramuscular injections of the Palivizumab monoclonal antibody, for use in high-risk infants only [8–10]. The implementation of Nirsevimab, a longer-lasting monoclonal antibody providing a passive immunization coverage after a single intramuscular administration, is expected to change the landscape of RSV-associated infections in the near future [11, 12]. It is recommended during the first RSV season for all newborns or infants younger than eight months and children aged eight to nineteen months at increased risk for severe RSV [13].

This prophylaxis, which has been approved in the US and Europe, has been shown to be highly effective in reducing RSV-associated infections in phase three trials, and has been introduced in several industrialized countries, leading to an estimated 80% reduction in hospitalizations [14–16]. Recent clinical study results have confirmed that Nirsevimab immunoprophylaxis reduced by approximately 80% RSV hospitalized and medically attended lower respiratory tract infections [17–19].

In this changing landscape, automatic and accurate surveillance strategies for bronchiolitis-related Emergency Department (ED) visits, which reflect a substantial healthcare burden of RSV-related infections, is going to be particularly valuable in strictly monitoring this evolving panorama [9]. Although the ED electronic health records (EHR) provide a precious monitoring source [20]. accomplishing this goal is hindered by frequently inaccurate diagnosis coding, which results from the time pressure typical of pediatric emergency settings and the highly variable styles used in free-text diagnosis reporting.

Recently, machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL) and large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated substantial potential in accurately classifying clinical notes [21] and diagnoses from unstructured free-text fields in electronic health records. These models offer a promising solution to overcome limitations in traditional coding approaches within pediatric emergency settings [22, 23].

We aimed to compare AI-based methods for automated classification of bronchiolitis diagnoses from pediatric ED free-text records and to evaluate the accuracy of ICD-9 coding, to inform real-time surveillance in the context of widespread RSV immunoprophylaxis.

The second objective is to evaluate the actual diagnostic accuracy of the “Bronchiolitis” diagnosis encoded using the ninth revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9) compared with the description of the free-text diagnosis. We hypothesize that ED EHR ICD-9 coding information, based on coding attribution by ED physicians upon discharge, could be less accurate than administrative coding. This is because the primary focus is on clinical conditions rather than diagnosis coding due to working under time pressure.

Methods

Data selection

This retrospective study was conducted at the Padova University Hospital with a pediatric ED accounting for approximately 25,000 visits per year. Overall, between the years 2007 and 2018 an amount of 221,175 EHRs with completed discharge diagnoses field were elected.

Of these, 28,557 records of infants under one year of age with complete diagnosis data fields were selected (Fig. 1). Data extracted did not include patient-identifiable information. Available data only included patient age (in categories), sex, triage urgency code (emergency, urgent, semi-urgent, non-urgent) at presentation, season of presentation (spring, summer, autumn, winter) free-text diagnoses and corresponding ICD-9 codes and visit outcome in terms of discharge versus hospital admission. For bronchiolitis classification based on administrative ICD-9 discharge codes, the following codes were included: 4661 (Acute Bronchiolitis), 46,611 (Acute RSV Bronchiolitis), 466 (Acute Bronchitis and Bronchiolitis) and 46,619 (Acute Bronchiolitis from other infectious agents). The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Azienda Ospedale-Università of Padova (Hospital Ethics Committee; 0022925) on April 8, 2021.

Data description

Continuous data are reported as medians (first and third quartiles); categorical data are reported as percentages and absolute frequencies. Wilcoxon-type tests were performed for continuous variables; Pearson chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate, were performed for categorical variables.

Gold standard development

An expert pediatric emergency physician manually classified the free-text diagnosis data fields from the selected ED EHRs to identify diagnosis of “Bronchiolitis”. To develop a gold standard for bronchiolitis from the free-text field, we conducted a two-round process. In the first round, we sampled all rows containing the term “bronch” in the free-text field. These samples were carefully reviewed and classified as bronchiolitis “yes” or “no” by an expert pediatrician. In the second round, we selected 5,000 additional rows, which were also reviewed and classified as bronchiolitis “yes” or “no” by the same expert. Both the stages of bronchiolitis classification were supervised by senior expert from PED. Prior to developing the gold standard, the dataset was restricted to children under one year of age. While the diagnosis of bronchiolitis is predominantly based on clinical basis, there is variability in these criteria across different regions. Given the lack of consensus regarding age thresholds (e.g., < 1 year vs. <2 years), we have followed the age definitions outlined in the national clinical practice guidelines for bronchiolitis.

The manual classification was reviewed and verified by a supervising expert. The manually classified bronchiolitis diagnosis from the free-text diagnosis data field was used as the gold standard (Fig. 1).

AI-based free-text classification

Classical machine learning techniques and deep learning

To develop machine learning-based classification tools for bronchiolitis, we first created a training set using the manually classified gold standard described above. The text data underwent pre-processing before training both classical machine learning models and a deep learning (DL) model.

Our study explored multiple classical machine learning techniques (CMLTs) [24] including random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), gradient boosting method (GBM), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and support vector machine (SVM). Additionally, we implemented a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based deep learning model [25] leveraging a sequential CNN architecture combined with trainable word embeddings.

The proposed classification model utilizes sequential 1D CNN begins with a trainable embedding layer that maps input tokens into dense vectors. Feature extraction is performed using a 1D convolutional layer configured with 64 filters and a kernel size of 3 to capture local trigram-level dependencies. To ensure training stability and mitigate overfitting, the convolutional output is processed through Batch Normalization, followed by a Dropout layer with a rate of 0.5. A global max pooling operation is then applied to down-sample the representation, retaining only the most salient features. A secondary dropout layer (rate of 0.3) is applied to the hidden layer to further regularize the network. The final output layer consists of two neurons utilizing sigmoid activation to generate class probabilities. The model is compiled using the adamax optimizer and trained to minimize categorical cross-entropy loss, with performance evaluated via accuracy, precision, and recall metrics.

Data pre-processing

ML tools and DL require pre-processing the free-text diagnosis field. Data were pre-processed by turning free-text into lowercase, removing punctuations, white spaces, and numbers, and keeping only word stems. To reduce noise, standard Italian stopwords were filtered out and porter stemming algorithm to reduce inflected words to their root forms. After cleaning the text corpus, the free-text data were vectorised into a document-term matrix (DTM) using term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) weighting to emphasize discriminative terms. Finally, to mitigate the curse of dimensionality and sparsity, terms appearing in less than 1% of the documents were pruned from the feature space., and for DL model text data, went through the tokenization process. Several classical ML algorithms and DL models were trained to classify bronchiolitis diagnosis.

Machine learning computations were performed using R 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), while deep learning computations were conducted in Python 3.10.13 using the TensorFlow 2.17.0 framework.

LLM model

The pre-trained large language model (LLM)-GPT-5 was also considered for the classification of free-text diagnoses. The LLM tool was directly used on the gold standard dataset without pre-processing.

A subscription-based version was used for ChatGPT (GPT-5). The database with the free text diagnostic field was uploaded in the system on 18/Aug/2025; subsequently, GPT-5 was asked to classify each record as either bronchiolitis or not, using the following prompt: “Please read column A (‘diagnosi’) of the Excel sheet and classify whether each entry indicates bronchiolitis. Report the classification results for each record in column B.”. The Excel sheet uploaded contained exclusively only a free-text field column and was fully anonymized, with no additional patient information attached.

Performance evaluation

The performance of CMLTs, DL models, and GPT-5 was assessed using sensitivity, F1-score, and balanced accuracy, comparing results to a gold-standard dataset. This dataset, also used for training ML models, was sufficiently large for bronchiolitis classification.

Due to severe class imbalance (minority class < 8%), balanced accuracy was used to prevent performance bias toward the majority class. Sensitivity measured the ability to detect positive cases, crucial in clinical settings, while the F1-score balanced precision and recall, assessing overall relevance.

To optimize the possibility that the models could generalize well to new cases and not just memorize the training data, a problem known as overfitting, we tested the classical machine learning models using five repeated 4-fold cross-validation [26]. This technique repeatedly trains and tests the models on different subsets of the data to check for consistent performance. The DL model was trained over 50 epochs, a cycle through the entire dataset, to optimize learning and then evaluated on a separate validation set. For GPT-5, we directly compared its predictions to the expert-reviewed gold-standard diagnoses. We used the same performance metrics for all models to ensure a fair and consistent comparison.

ICD-9 administrative-based classification

To evaluate the accuracy of ICD-9 codes for bronchiolitis compared with free text-diagnosis, we constructed a contingency table analysing the diagnosis of Bronchiolitis and the codes attribution. We calculated sensitivity as the rate of True Positives (TP) among all bronchiolitis patients, and specificity as the proportion of True Negatives (TN) among all bronchiolitis patients. Additionally, we evaluated the positive predictive value (PPV) of the codes by determining the rate of TP among all patients assigned the ICD-9 codes 4661, 46,611, 466, and 46,619, as well as the negative predictive value (NPV), defined as the rate of TN among patients who were not assigned these codes. 95% confidence intervals (CI) were also calculated for accuracy measures.

The results indicate that all models perform reasonably well, with sensitivity values ranging between 0.77 [CI 95% 0.75–0.79] and 0.93 [CI 95% 0.92–0.94], suggesting that most models are reliable in identifying bronchiolitis cases. F1 scores, which range from 0.86 [CI 95% 0.85–0.88] to 0.96 [CI 95% 0.95–0.99], show that these models maintain strong precision-recall balance, avoiding both false positives and false negatives effectively. Balanced accuracy values, between 0.88 [CI 95% 0.87–0.89] and 0.96 [CI 95% 0.95–0.97], suggest that the classifiers generally handle the binary classification task with high reliability across classes despite the presence of a significant amount of imbalance.

The GBM and SVM models achieved the highest scores across all three metrics, indicating their suitability for this classification task. RF and LDA models also performed well, showcasing their robustness for similar classification tasks. Although DT achieved slightly lower sensitivity and balanced accuracy metrics, it still provides an interpretable and straightforward solution that may be valuable in specific contexts. These results reflect that advanced models like GBM and SVM are highly effective for text classification tasks.

The DL model and GPT-5 demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional machine learning approaches, achieving a sensitivity of 0.97 [CI 95% 0.96-1.00], 0.98 [CI 95% 0.98–0.99], an F1-score of 0.96 [CI 95% 0.95–0.99], 0.99 [CI 95% 0.98–0.99], and a balanced accuracy of 0.98 [CI 95% 0.98-1.00], 0.99 [CI 95% 0.99–0.99] respectively (Fig. 2). DL and GPT-5 offer substantial performance improvements, making them highly reliable models for diagnosing bronchiolitis.

Discussion

This study is, to our knowledge, the first to directly compare a large language model, deep learning, and multiple classical machine learning approaches for bronchiolitis surveillance in the context of widespread Nirsevimab use. Our study found that the LLM and DL models tested for classifying bronchiolitis free-text discharge diagnoses were more accurate than the classical machine learning techniques used (RF, GBM, LDA, SVM). The GPT-5 LLM could thus represent a practical and easily implementable tool for verifying the future epidemiological surveillance of bronchiolitis that may be significantly impacted by effective large-scale prophylaxis interventions, such as Nirsevimab. In addition, we showed that the agreement between free text diagnosis classification and administrative ICD-9-based encoding was not optimal, with ICD-9 coding performing more poorly. Consequently, the best approach to enhance epidemiological surveillance of bronchiolitis from real-world ED data appears to be using LLM applied to the free-text diagnosis fields within the electronic medical records, rather than using ICD-9 coding.

The use of ML models to enhance exploitation of unstructured free-text data for proper diagnosis classification has been previously investigated in the adult and pediatric ED setting. Within this context, ML models have shown good accuracy in processing unstructured text data, such as free text data fields within the electronic medical records, and their use was found to be feasible and practicable [21, 22, 24, 27, 28]. The ability of classifying free-text diagnosis through ML-based tools is useful especially in the ED setting where the diagnosis categorization through ICD-9 coding may not be accurate.

Recent literature has assessed the performance of open-source LLM in various information extraction tasks in the emergency medicine field without proceeding with further algorithm training [29–31]. Several studies have consistently found a very high performance of the LLM models assessed.

Similarly, in our study, we found that although the tested CMLTs (RF, GBM, LDA, SVM), DL, and LLM, such as GPT-5, were highly accurate in recognizing bronchiolitis diagnoses in pediatric ED electronic medical records, DL and LLM showed a higher sensitivity and balanced accuracy.

The descriptive analysis of the gold standard definition within the study dataset has revealed that our results are in linealign with recent national and international epidemiological data preceding the wide implementation of Nirsevimab prophylaxis. Specifically, the distribution of patient sex, age group, seasonality of bronchiolitis outbreak and bronchiolitis hospitalization rate [32, 33] mirrors trends observed in these broader datasets. The observed concordance in epidemiological features strengthens confidence in the validity of the applied models. Moreover, because the analysis relied exclusively on free-text data, the generalizability of the model should be maintained despite the despite seasonal fluctuations and shifts in disease incidence that occurred during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

However, the accuracy of ICD-9 coding showed a suboptimal sensitivity in identifying bronchiolitis cases based on the gold standard classification defined by an expert paediatrician using the free-text diagnosis data field. If we consider the total amount of bronchiolitis diagnoses as defined by the gold-standard, just 85.9% had a proper ICD-9 Bronchiolitis codification (4661,46611,466, 46619) with a precision rate of 80.4%. An imprecise ICD-9 diagnosis classification can undermine the analysis of real-world data for surveillance purposes and the subsequent healthcare resource planning [34]. Our results are comparable with previous studies that have focused on ED data [35] and reflect the difficulties of providing accurate and consistent ICD-9 coding of diagnostic information for monitoring purposes in the ED setting.

Indeed, the overcrowded environment, the limited available time for diagnosing and managing patients, and the stress among medical staff may affect the proper ICD-9 coding attribution at discharge from the pediatric EDs, when this information must be provided before the ED discharge letter can be printed out for patients and their families.

Imprecisions on administrative data also concern studies referring to hospitalized patients. A recent cross-sectional study has demonstrated a low-quality recognition of infectious agents leading to pneumonia because of the low sensitivity of the diagnosis codes used, which may limit specific pneumonia prevalence estimation derived from administrative data [36]. With this respect, ED data have intrinsic limitations with regard to identification of the etiologic infectious agents in those settings where point of care viral testing is not available. RSV detection by traditional nasal swabs often requires a prolonged reporting time, and results may not be available during discharge/hospital admission from the ED. Therefore, encoded ICD-9 diagnosis of Acute RSV Bronchiolitis (46611) has been generally low in number in our dataset, as well as in other studies conducted in the ED setting [37].

The suboptimal agreement between ICD-9 and free-text diagnosis supports the need to rely on free-text diagnosis data fields combined with AI-based tools to ensure optimal accuracy in epidemiological surveillance. This study underscores the feasibility and value of leveraging advanced AI tools, particularly deep learning and LLM, for real-time, high-accuracy classification of pediatric ED diagnoses from free-text records. This offers a scalable solution to enhance disease surveillance and inform timely public health responses in the evolving landscape of RSV prophylaxis.

The results of our study should be interpreted considering its limitations. We utilized a database based on ICD-9 discharge diagnosis codes. Although the ICD-10 classification system adopted by many international healthcare institutions and offering enhanced granularity could have potentially facilitated comparison of the automated surveillance performance across a broader coding framework, the requirement for coding in emergency settings remains a challenge common to both ICD-9 and ICD-10. Nevertheless, the ICD-9 system continues to be in widespread national use and remains an appropriate and relevant tool for the objectives of our study. Moreover, our data extraction has occurred in a confined text field that is the discharge diagnosis; we have assumed all the diagnosis of bronchiolitis formulated by clinician are appropriate, not analyzing additional clinical information (medical history, physical examination) contained within the EHR which might have been helpful for a rigorous disease definition. However, using these additional data fields opened privacy-related challenges in terms of potential re-identification of patients, which was not possible for this study. Lastly, although generative AI demonstrated to be a promising approach to clinical surveillance, its integration is hindered by several obstacles, including challenges in interpreting model reasoning, which may undermine scientific reproducibility.

Conclusions

LLM and DL models proved more accurate than the classical machine learning techniques for identifying bronchiolitis diagnoses within the ED electronic medical records’ free-text discharge diagnosis field. The sensitivity of bronchiolitis diagnosis classification based on ICD-9 coding was suboptimal. In evolving RSV prevention strategies, this study demonstrates that DL and LLM can effectively bridge the gap between unstructured clinical free-text encoding and reliable disease surveillance. Our data offers baseline estimates of the hospital burden of disease prior to the introduction of Nirsevimab providing a critical reference point for monitoring the subsequent evolution of epidemic cycles and their inherent characteristics in the context of RSV immunization. In conclusion AI tools offer a practical and scalable approach to support timely monitoring and informed healthcare planning by enabling accurate and automated classification of bronchiolitis diagnoses from free-text ED records.
