The Role of Fatalism in Coping: Disparities in Psychological Distress Among Patients With Indolent Blood Cancers
Authors: Tammarah Sklarz, Jill Schnall Hasler, Carolyn Y. Fang, Zachary A. K. Frosch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with indolent blood cancers managed with observation often experience psychological distress. Racial disparities in blood cancer survival are well documented, but not fully understood. One potential contributor is the mental health burden, as racial minorities experience higher psychosocial stress. This study examined racial disparities in cancer‐related stress and coping strategies in patients undergoing observation for indolent blood cancers. Participants completed measures regarding sociodemographics, health behaviors, cancer‐specific stress, and psychological adjustment to cancer. Patients were categorized as racially minoritized (Asian, Black, or Hispanic) or non‐minoritized (White non‐Hispanic). Multivariable analyses showed the racially minoritized group reported higher responses of fatalism as a coping strategy (adjusted mean difference of 1.8, 95% CI 0.47–3.17, p = 0.01). These findings reflect prior studies linking greater cancer fear and fatalism in racial minorities to reduced follow‐up and delayed treatment, underscoring the importance of understanding these attitudes to address outcome disparities in patients with indolent blood cancers.

Introduction

Patients with indolent blood cancers often lack an indication for treatment at diagnosis. Yet, undergoing observation as the initial management strategy poses a psychological challenge for many patients. They may experience heightened anxiety about possible disease progression due to the absence of active treatment [1] or distress from the uncertainty around the timing of treatment initiation. These emotions can impair patients' ability to cope with their cancer diagnosis. Impaired coping responses may, in turn, predispose patients to psychological distress and mood disorders. This is particularly concerning given observed associations between mental health conditions and decreased cancer‐specific survival [2, 3, 4].

While the potential psychological impact of an observational management strategy is recognized broadly, it is also important to consider how this burden may be experienced unequally across patient populations. In the United States, there are significant racial disparities in survival for patients diagnosed with blood cancers, yet the basis for these differences is incompletely understood [5]. One potential contributor is the differential impact of mental health comorbidities. Racial and ethnic minority individuals in the United States disproportionately experience more baseline psychosocial stress [6]. Moreover, cancer fear and fatalism are often higher in racial and ethnic minority groups, reflecting beliefs that developing cancer is uncontrollable and death is inevitable [7, 8, 9]. These attitudes can hinder engagement in health‐promoting behaviors (e.g., cancer screening [10, 11, 12, 13] or smoking cessation) and may lead to poorer outcomes in racial minority patients.

The objective of this study was to examine if cancer‐related stress reported by patients undergoing active observation for indolent blood cancers differs by race and ethnicity. In addition, we assessed for differences in strategies for coping with their diagnosis.

Methods

This analysis uses data from a prospective observational research study where participants with indolent blood cancers managed by observation completed measures regarding sociodemographic background, health behaviors (smoking status and physical activity level), cancer‐specific stress, and coping with cancer. The methods are described previously [14], and informed consent was obtained in the original study. In brief, participants with indolent blood cancers were enrolled from April 2016 to November 2020. Measures were obtained at study entry, and data for this analysis were used from this initial timepoint only. Enrollment occurred at varying times after diagnosis. Race and ethnicity were self‐reported. Due to limited lower numbers for individual categories, participants were categorized as belonging to a racially minoritized group (patients who are Asian, Black, or Hispanic) or a non‐minoritized group (patients who are White non‐Hispanic).

Cancer‐specific stress was assessed using the Revised Impact of Events Scale (RIES) [15], a well‐validated questionnaire that assesses the degree to which patients experience intrusive thoughts or hyperarousal regarding their cancer. Higher RIES scores denote higher cancer‐specific stress. Cancer coping strategies were assessed using the Mental Adjustment to Cancer (MAC) scale. This well‐validated scale captures the extent to which patients adopt five coping responses (fighting spirit, feeling helpless/hopeless, anxious preoccupation, fatalism, and avoidance) in adjusting to their diagnosis and cancer treatment [16, 17]. MAC responses are rated from 1 to 4 on the Likert scale. Scores range from 4 to 16 for fighting spirit and avoidance, 8 to 32 for helpless/hopeless and anxious preoccupation, and 5 to 20 for fatalism. Higher scores indicate greater endorsement of each coping response. Self‐reported smoking status and physical activity levels were also assessed.

Linear regression models were used to compare scores between patient groups on the RIES and to explore potential differences in cancer coping strategies using the MAC. Normalizing transformations were applied where appropriate. Logistic regression was used to compare smoking status. A proportional odds model was used to compare levels of physical activity. This was quantified as insufficiently active/sedentary, moderately active, and active; greater values on this score correspond to increased risk of being sedentary. Age, gender, marital status, education, and time from diagnosis were adjusted for.

Results

Two hundred thirty‐three participants were enrolled in the study. Sixteen percent of participants were categorized as racially minoritized, and 84% of participants were categorized as non‐minoritized based on self‐reported race and ethnicity (Table 1). Nearly half of the participants had CLL (48%), 22% had follicular lymphoma, 15% had smoldering myeloma, 10% had marginal zone lymphoma, 3% had B cell lymphoma NOS, and 2% had lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma. Compared with the non‐minoritized group, the minoritized group was younger (58 vs. 64 years old, p = 0.002) and less likely to be married/partnered (54.5% vs. 80.7%, p = 0.001); however, there was no difference in gender distribution between the two groups.

In multivariable analyses, there was no difference in levels of cancer‐specific stress between minoritized and non‐minoritized groups (RIES score adjusted mean difference of 0.06, 95% CI: 0.39–0.50, p = 0.8) [Table 2]. Racially minoritized patients scored significantly higher on the fatalism subscale of the MAC compared with the non‐minoritized patient group (adjusted difference of 1.8, 95% CI 0.47–3.17, p = 0.01). No significant difference was observed for any other coping strategy. In comparing rates of smoking status and physical activity, we observed that a significantly higher percentage of patients in the minoritized group were active smokers (adjusted OR 12.2, 95% CI 2.0–72.7, p = 0.01). There was no difference in levels of physical activity between the two groups (adjusted OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.46–2.22, p = 0.99).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore whether cancer‐related stress and coping differ across minoritized and non‐minoritized patients undergoing active observation for indolent blood cancers. Notable findings include higher levels of fatalism and smoking among minoritized patients. These findings suggest differences in coping techniques among racially minoritized patients undergoing surveillance for these diseases, highlighting a potential gap in supportive care during this phase of management. As fatalistic beliefs can hinder uptake of health‐promoting behaviors, ways to address maladaptive coping strategies should be further explored, in addition to whether these beliefs have a significant impact on blood cancer‐specific outcomes.

These findings align with prior studies demonstrating greater cancer fear and fatalism in racial and ethnic minority populations. A large US population‐based survey study found that Latino, Black, and Asian respondents were more fatalistic about cancer preventability than White non‐Latino respondents, even after adjusting for other factors [7]. Fatalistic attitudes—perceiving a cancer diagnosis as uncontrollable and death inevitable—can reduce engagement in seeking cancer prevention, detection, treatment, and survivorship care [8, 9, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Higher fatalism and cancer fear, often linked together in minoritized populations, may be one explanation for the racial disparities observed in cancer screening and delayed symptom presentation [10, 11, 12, 13]. In the context of indolent blood cancers, higher fatalistic beliefs and cancer fear may worsen outcomes by reducing surveillance follow‐up and delaying participation in active treatment when necessary. Moreover, fatalistic beliefs may promote unhealthy behaviors, like smoking, compromising overall health in patients with indolent blood cancers. This is particularly important in this population, in which patients often die from non‐cancer causes. Further steps must be taken, through both educational and psychological support, to address fatalistic beliefs about cancer in racial and ethnic minority patients with indolent blood cancer.

This study is limited by its use of existing observational data. As a secondary, exploratory analysis, it was not powered to draw causal conclusions regarding smoking or physical activity, and findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating. The study design, in which patients with an existing diagnosis of indolent blood cancers were enrolled, precludes determining whether these behaviors arose in response to cancer diagnosis or reflect pre‐existing social, cultural, or coping characteristics, as data prior to diagnosis were not collected. Prior literature supports the relevance of these behaviors to psychosocial outcomes in oncology populations, with physical activity associated with reduced depressive symptoms and distress [23, 24] and current smoking linked to higher psychological distress at diagnosis [25]. These findings provide important context for why smoking and physical activity are meaningful and relevant variables to examine, even though temporality and causality cannot be established in the present analysis.

In conclusion, racial and ethnic minority patients with indolent blood cancers experienced higher levels of fatalism and reported higher levels of smoking. These findings align with the literature in other contexts that cancer fear and fatalism are more prevalent among racial and ethnic minority groups. Such beliefs may affect participation in continued surveillance, promote detrimental coping behaviors, and contribute to poorer outcomes seen in indolent blood cancers.
