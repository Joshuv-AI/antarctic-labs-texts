Frequency and Duration of the Clinical Symptoms of Patients With Mild COVID‐19: A Comparison With Influenza in the 2023–2024 Japanese Influenza Season, an Observational Study
Authors: Takuma Bando, Hideyuki Ikematsu, Naoki Kawai, Tetsunari Maeda, Woon Joo Lee, Shinro Matsuura, Osame Tanaka, Takashi Kawashima, Hareaki Yamamoto, Ietaka Sato, Ken‐ichi Doniwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThis study aimed to compare the clinical symptoms of mild COVID‐19 and influenza during a period when both diseases circulated in the 2023–2024 influenza season in Japan.MethodsOutpatients diagnosed with COVID‐19, influenza A, or influenza B by antigen test kits at 10 medical facilities in Japan were enrolled. Data were collected from a self‐reported diary.ResultsData from 347 COVID‐19, 392 influenza A, and 103 influenza B patients were analyzed. COVID‐19 patients were significantly older than those with influenza A and B (p < 0.001). Sore throat was significantly more prevalent in COVID‐19 patients than in patients with influenza A or B (49.0% vs. 27.3% and 27.2%, p < 0.001). Cough and nasal discharge/congestion were significantly more common in influenza A and B patients than in COVID‐19 patients (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively). Taste and smell dysfunction at Days 5 to 7 was more prevalent in COVID‐19 patients (6.9% and 6.9%, respectively) than in influenza A or B patients (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively). Maximum body temperature was significantly lower in COVID‐19 patients than in influenza A or B patients (38.3°C vs. 38.9°C and 38.9°C, p < 0.001). The frequency of any symptoms over 14 days was significantly higher in COVID‐19 patients, particularly in those aged ≥ 40 years (p < 0.001).ConclusionMild COVID‐19 was associated with longer‐lasting symptoms than influenza, particularly in individuals aged ≥ 40 years. These results suggest a greater disease burden of COVID‐19, even in outpatients with mild disease, compared with influenza.Keywords: COVID‐19, disease burden, influenza, maximum body temperature, outpatients, symptom duration

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic began in Japan in January of 2020, and by December of 2024 a total of 11 waves had been documented [1]. During the early waves, relatively high rates of severe illness were reported [2]. Taste and smell dysfunction were initially considered distinctive features of COVID‐19 [3].

Following the sixth wave of COVID‐19 in early 2022, the predominant viral strain in Japan shifted to the SARS‐CoV‐2 Omicron variant [4]. The proportion of severe diseases such as pneumonia decreased, and upper respiratory symptoms became more prominent [5]. The dominant Omicron sub‐lineages changed sequentially from BA.1, BA.2, and BA.5 to XBB.1.5 and JN.1.

During the Omicron variant period, higher rates of severe illness and hospitalization were reported for COVID‐19 than for influenza. Xie et al. [6] reported a mortality rate for hospitalized COVID‐19 patients of 6% from October 1, 2022, to January 31, 2023, while the mortality rate for hospitalized influenza patients was 3.7%. During the same period, the U.S. CDC [7, 8] reported that the number of COVID‐19 hospitalizations was 2–3 times higher than for influenza. Miyashita et al. [9] reporting on the Omicron period in Japan from January 2020 to April 2023, found that although the fatality rate for COVID‐19 decreased compared to the early stages of the pandemic, the number of cases increased, and the mortality rate, particularly among the elderly, significantly rose. Furthermore, the mortality rate for COVID‐19 among the elderly exceeded that of influenza. However, reports comparing the disease burden of mild COVID‐19 cases, which account for the majority of COVID‐19 patients, to that of influenza are rare.

We reported that additional COVID‐19 vaccination reduced febrile symptoms and partially shortened symptom duration [10]. Antiviral agents have also been reported to shorten the symptom duration of COVID‐19 [11, 12], and anti‐influenza agents have been reported to shorten the symptom duration of influenza [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

During the 2023–2024 Japanese influenza season, influenza A (H3N2), A (H1N1pdm), and B (Victoria) viruses circulated in Japan [19], and the tenth wave of COVID‐19 was driven by the JN.1 variant [20]. Changes in the circulating variants, past infection, vaccinations, and/or administration of antiviral agents can influence the symptoms and disease course of patients with COVID‐19.

Although mild COVID‐19 and influenza are often managed conservatively in outpatient settings, symptom persistence represents an important dimension of disease burden beyond traditional measures of severity. Prolonged symptoms can substantially affect daily activities, work productivity, and quality of life, even in the absence of severe disease. Because COVID‐19 and influenza often co‐circulate in the same time frame and present with overlapping clinical features, comparing the disease burden of mild COVID‐19 with that of influenza provides clinically relevant information for patient counseling, expectation management, and public health messaging.

This study was done to compare the possible differences in disease burden of patients with COVID‐19 and influenza through the use of the clinical characteristics and symptom duration of outpatients with mild COVID‐19 as indicators.

Patients and Methods

Patients

This study was conducted at 10 medical facilities across Japan from October 1, 2023 to May 31, 2024. Patients who were diagnosed with COVID‐19, influenza A, or influenza B by rapid antigen tests were included after giving informed consent. The Hara‐Doi Hospital Ethics Review Committee reviewed and approved this investigation (Approval date, September 19, 2023).

Patients were asked to answer questionnaires about their experience with COVID‐19 and influenza (Figures S1 and S2) that included the following information: age, sex, history of COVID‐19 and influenza vaccination, comorbidities, the date and time when fever ≥ 37.5°C first appeared, body temperature and symptoms at the time of the first visit (cough, dyspnea, sore throat, nasal congestion/discharge, headache, fatigue, myalgia/arthralgia, taste dysfunction, smell dysfunction, and gastrointestinal symptoms), symptoms 5–7 days after diagnosis, and antiviral agent administration. The body temperature was recorded four times daily for 7 days. The date when the fever subsided to < 37.5°C and the date of complete symptom resolution were recorded.

The questionnaires were collected by the attending physicians, who entered the data online [21], and the maximum body temperature during the course of illness, fever duration, and symptom duration were calculated.

Patients were categorized into the age groups ≤ 19, 20–39, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years, consistent with our previous studies evaluating the clinical characteristics and vaccine effectiveness in COVID‐19 patients [10]. These age categories reflect possible differences in vaccination coverage, risk profiles, and clinical presentation across life stages, and facilitate comparability with prior epidemiological studies conducted in Japan.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Categorical variables were compared among groups (sex, age group, and comorbidities) using the χ 2 test. For symptom frequency analyses, binary variables (yes = 1, no = 0) were treated as continuous outcomes to estimate group proportions and 95% confidence intervals using ANOVA. Continuous variables are presented as means or medians, as appropriate.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used as an initial global test to assess overall differences among the three groups (COVID‐19, influenza A, and influenza B). When distributions were markedly skewed or influenced by outliers, non‐parametric tests were applied. Specifically, fever duration showed a skewed distribution with outliers and was therefore compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test in unadjusted analyses; however, multivariable linear regression was used to estimate adjusted mean differences while accounting for potential confounders. To specifically evaluate differences between COVID‐19 and each influenza type, pairwise comparisons were subsequently performed using Welch's t‐test, which does not assume equal variances. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons in the pairwise analyses. Unadjusted group differences were assessed using ANOVA or Welch's t‐test for continuous variables and χ 2 tests for categorical variables.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed using the forced‐entry method, with age group, sex, and comorbidities included a priori as covariates in all models. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated to assess associations between disease group and the presence or absence of individual symptoms. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to estimate partial regression coefficients representing differences from the mean maximum body temperature and symptom duration. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables in the regression models was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), correlation coefficients, and condition indices.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 843 patients were enrolled, and one patient with an unknown influenza subtype was excluded from the analysis; 347 had COVID‐19 (195 female, 152 male), 392 influenza A (215 female, 177 male), and 103 influenza B (51 female, 52 male). Among the 347 patients with COVID‐19, 85 (24.5%) had a past infection.

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. No significant difference by sex was observed among the three groups (p = 0.49). Among the COVID‐19 patients, 40.6% were 40–59 years of age. Few patients were ≤ 19 years of age. In contrast, influenza A was distributed across all age groups, and > 50% of the influenza B patients were ≤ 19 years of age. The age of COVID‐19 patients was higher than for influenza A and B (p < 0.001).

Respiratory disease was the most prevalent disease, and did not differ significantly among the groups (p = 0.40). Diabetes and cerebrovascular disease were significantly more prevalent in COVID‐19 patients than in those with influenza A or B (p = 0.04 and p = 0.03 for diabetes and cerebrovascular disease, respectively).

Among 344 COVID‐19 patients with a record of COVID‐19 vaccination, patients who had received 3–5 doses were most prevalent (56.1%). The proportions of patients vaccinated within the previous 6 months were 11.9% (20–39 years), 28.6% (40–59 years), and 64.2% (≥ 60 years), with an overall rate of 35.9%. Among the 386 influenza A and 103 B patients in the 2023–24 season, 22.0% and 16.5%, respectively, had been vaccinated.

Antiviral agents were administered to only 85 patients with COVID‐19 (24.5%): nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (51.8%), molnupiravir (25.9%), and ensitrelvir (22.4%). In contrast, 380 patients with influenza A (96.9%) received the following antiviral agents: oseltamivir (45.3%), zanamivir (1.32%), laninamivir (16.6%), and baloxavir (36.8%). Among patients with influenza B, 99 (96.1%) received an antiviral agent: oseltamivir (48.5%), zanamivir (4.04%), laninamivir (16.2%), or baloxavir (31.3%).

Although vaccination status and the use of an antiviral agent could potentially influence symptom severity and duration, these variables were not included as covariates in the primary comparative analyses. The analyses were designed to focus on differences in overall symptom profiles and disease burden between clinically diagnosed COVID‐19 and influenza.

Frequency of Symptoms

The frequency of symptoms is shown in Figures 1 and 2 and Table S1. Sore throat was significantly more common in patients with COVID‐19 (49.0%) than in those with influenza A (27.3%) or B (27.2%) (p < 0.001). In contrast, cough (48.4%, 67.9%, and 67.0%, respectively), and nasal congestion/discharge (35.4%, 50.0%, and 44.7%, respectively) were significantly less prevalent in COVID‐19 patients than in influenza A or B patients (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed for dyspnea, fatigue, headache, myalgia/arthralgia, or gastrointestinal symptoms.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusted for age group, sex, and comorbidities revealed that the aOR of cough was significantly higher for influenza A and B than for COVID‐19 (aOR = 2.52 for both, p < 0.001) (Table S2). Influenza A had a significantly lower aOR (0.50) for sore throat (p < 0.001) and a higher aOR (1.85) for nasal symptoms (p < 0.001).

No significant differences in the frequency of taste or smell dysfunction at consultation were observed (Figure 1 and Table S1). On days 5–7, COVID‐19 patients exhibited significantly higher frequencies of taste dysfunction (6.9% vs. 0.5% for influenza A, 0% for influenza B; p < 0.001) and smell dysfunction (6.9% vs. 0%, 0%; p < 0.001) (Figure 2 and Table S1).

Body Temperature, Fever Duration, and Symptom Duration

The mean maximum body temperature, median fever duration, and mean total symptom duration are shown in Figure 3 and Table S3. Mean maximum body temperature was significantly lower in COVID‐19 patients than in influenza A or B patients (38.3°C, 38.9°C, and 38.9°C, respectively; p < 0.001). Median fever duration did not significantly differ among the groups, as assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Mean symptom duration was significantly longer in COVID‐19 patients than in influenza A or B patients (231.7, 158.4, and 150.0 h respectively; p < 0.001).

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that, after adjustment for confounding factors, maximum body temperature was significantly higher in patients with influenza A than in those with COVID‐19 (0.32°C, p = 0.01) (Table S4). In contrast, symptom duration was significantly longer for COVID‐19 than for influenza A or B (63.9 h, p < 0.001; 83.8 h, p < 0.001, respectively) (Table S4).

Multicollinearity Assessment of Variables Used in Multiple Linear and Logistic Regression Analyses

Multicollinearity in the multiple regression models was assessed through the use of condition indices, correlation coefficients among explanatory variables, and variance inflation factor (VIF). The maximum condition index was 1.676, the highest correlation coefficient was 0.37, and all VIF values were < 2.000. These results indicate that multicollinearity was minimal and unlikely to have influenced the analysis.

Discussion

The COVID‐19 epidemic periodically cycled in waves throughout Japan. The tenth wave of COVID‐19 occurred during the 2023–2024 Japanese influenza season. Patients with COVID‐19 were older than those with influenza (Table 1), which is consistent with our previous report on the eighth wave of COVID‐19 in Japan [22]. Overall, 76.5% of the COVID‐19 patients had received three or more doses of vaccine. In contrast, the proportions of patients receiving influenza vaccination were 22.0% for influenza A and 16.5% for influenza B. These results indicate that vaccination alone was insufficient to control the epidemics of COVID‐19 and influenza.

Among the comorbidities reported, respiratory problems were the most common for all three infections. The impact of respiratory problems on the clinical course of COVID‐19 and influenza warrants further study. Diabetes and cerebrovascular disease were more prevalent in COVID‐19 patients than in those with influenza A or B, likely reflecting the older age distribution of COVID‐19 patients.

Clinical symptoms are important for diagnosis in clinical settings. Among the symptoms assessed, significant differences in the frequency of symptoms at diagnosis between COVID‐19 and influenza A and B were observed for sore throat, cough, and nasal symptoms (Figure 1 and Table S1). Overall, the clinical symptoms were similar for COVID‐19 and influenza A and B. These results suggest that differentiating COVID‐19 from influenza based solely on clinical symptoms is quite difficult and that point‐of‐care tests, such as rapid antigen tests, are valuable for accurate diagnosis.

Taste and smell dysfunction are recognized as characteristic symptoms of COVID‐19. In this study, the frequency of these symptoms at consultation was 0.6% and 1.7%, respectively (Figure 1 and Table S1). Interestingly, taste and smell dysfunction were significantly more common among patients with COVID‐19 than among those with influenza 5–7 days post‐onset (Figure 2 and Table S1). These results indicate that these symptoms remain a distinguishing feature of COVID‐19 when present, although their frequency at onset has declined from the years dominated by the Omicron variant BA.1 [23].

The symptoms of COVID‐19 have evolved along with the emergence of the new Omicron subvariants. We previously reported significantly higher maximum body temperature and longer fever duration during the BA.5 wave than during the BA.1 wave [24]. Another study reported that patients with BA.5 had higher odds ratio (OR) than BA.2 for experiencing fever (OR = 2.18) [4]. In the present study, the mean maximum body temperature of patients with COVID‐19 was significantly lower than that of those with influenza A (38.3°C vs. 38.9°C and 38.9°C, respectively, p < 0.001) (Figure 3 and Table S3). We previously reported that older age is associated with a lower maximum body temperature [24]. The older age of the COVID‐19 patients in this study may partly explain their lower maximum body temperature. However, even after adjusting for confounding factors in our multiple linear regression analyses, the maximum body temperature of COVID‐19 patients remained significantly lower than that of influenza A patients and tended to be lower than that of influenza B patients (Table S4), indicating that a lower febrile response is a notable clinical difference between COVID‐19 and influenza. High fever remains a characteristic clinical feature of influenza.

In addition, our previous studies of the sixth to eighth waves and the 11th wave of COVID‐19 in Japan reported that a higher number of COVID‐19 vaccinations was associated with a lower maximum body temperature [10, 24, 25]. It is plausible that the higher number of vaccine doses among patients aged ≥ 60 years partly contributed to their lower maximum body temperature (Table S4).

The comparison of disease burden between COVID‐19 and influenza has primarily focused on severity rates and mortality rates as indicators [6, 7, 8, 9]. However, comparative studies on mild cases, which constitute the majority of both COVID‐19 and influenza cases, are scarce. Symptom duration, in addition to severity, is an important measure of disease burden. During the eighth wave of COVID‐19, dominated by the BA.5 subvariant, COVID‐19 and influenza circulated simultaneously in Japan for the first time. We reported that symptoms lasting > 1 month occurred only in COVID‐19 patients, particularly among patients ≥ 40 years of age [22]. Similarly, symptom duration in the present study was significantly longer for COVID‐19 than influenza, with long‐lasting symptoms > 30 days observed predominantly in COVID‐19 patients (Figure 4). While most influenza A and B symptoms resolved within 1 week, many COVID‐19 patients experienced symptoms persisting > 2 weeks. After adjusting for confounding factors in our multiple linear regression analyses, symptom duration remained significantly longer in COVID‐19 patients than in influenza patients (Table S4). The prolonged symptoms of COVID‐19 compared with influenza suggest a high disease burden from COVID‐19. Because most cases of long COVID arise from mild illness [26], appropriate management during the acute phase of the disease of middle‐aged and older patients with mild COVID‐19 is particularly important. Preventive strategies that reduce viral replication and illness severity, including vaccination, may also help mitigate the overall symptom burden associated with mild COVID‐19.

Our findings align with the ZOE COVID study [27], which showed that COVID‐19 is associated with longer symptom duration than other acute respiratory infections, including influenza, with approximately 13% of cases reporting symptoms lasting ≥ 4 weeks and 2%–3% lasting ≥ 12 weeks, even after initially mild illness. By examining clinically confirmed outpatient cases during the Omicron period in Japan, our study extends these findings and supports prolonged symptom duration as a robust distinguishing feature of COVID‐19 compared with influenza.

From a clinical perspective, distinguishing COVID‐19 from influenza is especially important at this time when both conditions are generally managed with supportive care because clinicians must be aware that mild COVID‐19 is associated with longer‐lasting symptoms than influenza, particularly among middle‐aged and older adults. This prolonged symptom persistence has important implications for patient counseling, follow‐up strategies, and societal burden, underscoring that the disease burden of outpatients with mild COVID‐19 should be evaluated not only by severity or hospitalization rates but also by symptom duration.

Antiviral agents were administered to 24.5% of the COVID‐19 patients compared with 96.9% and 96.1% to those with influenza A and B, respectively. It has been reported that administration of antiviral agents during the Omicron period shortened the duration of COVID‐19 symptoms. Butler et al. [11] demonstrated in a randomized controlled trial that molnupiravir did not reduce the incidence of COVID‐19‐related hospitalization or death among vaccinated high‐risk adults, but it did shorten the time to symptom resolution (estimated benefit 4.2 days; 95% CI, 3.8–4.6). In addition, Yotsuyanagi et al. [12] reported that ensitrelvir shortened the duration of symptoms in patients with mild‐to‐moderate COVID‐19 (24.3 h). Several studies have reported that the anti‐influenza agents, oseltamivir (34.7 h) [13], zanamivir (1 day) [14, 15], peramivir (41.7–43.6 h) [16], laninamivir (equivalent to oseltamivir) [17], and baloxavir marboxil (26.5 h) [18] shorten the duration of symptoms. In this study, the administration rate of antiviral agents to patients with influenza exceeded 90%, whereas it was less than 30% for patients with COVID‐19. These findings suggest that differences in the administration rates of antiviral agents for COVID‐19 and influenza may have influenced the variation in symptom duration among mild cases. However, the reduction in symptom duration by anti‐influenza agents has been reported to be approximately 24 h, and in this study, the symptom duration of COVID‐19 was 63.9–84.8 h longer than that of influenza. Although these differences may be partly explained by variations in antiviral use, it is unlikely to have accounted for the entire disparity. Therefore, further studies will be necessary to evaluate the influence of antiviral use on symptom duration.

In this study, the number of vaccine doses received and the history of COVID‐19 may have influenced the maximum body temperature, acute‐phase symptoms, and symptom duration. Although only a relatively small proportion of COVID‐19 patients received antiviral therapy, its potential impact on their disease course cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

This study, which focused on mild patients managed as outpatients, identified differences in the frequency of the symptoms experienced by COVID‐19 and influenza A and B patients; however, distinguishing COVID‐19 from influenza based on symptoms at the time of a clinic visit is difficult. COVID‐19 was associated with longer‐lasting symptoms than influenza, particularly in individuals aged ≥ 40 years. Even in the case of mild infection, these findings suggest a significantly greater disease burden associated with COVID‐19 compared with influenza.
