Federal Lobbyist Spending to Influence 340B Drug Pricing Program Policy
Authors: Cooper Urban, Caroline Shore, Sunita Desai, Lauren Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study identifies organizations engaged in lobbying related to the 340B Drug Pricing Program, estimates their lobbying expenditures, and characterizes their policy priorities.

Introduction

The 340B Drug Pricing Program, created in 1992, requires pharmaceutical manufacturers to sell outpatient drugs at discounted prices to eligible safety-net organizations. Over time, the program has expanded into a lightly regulated marketplace in which large hospital systems and pharmacies capture substantial financial gains, whereas benefits to patients with low income, federally qualified health centers (FQHCs), and clinic-based safety-net organizations (including Ryan White clinics) remain unclear.1 Outpatient pharmaceutical spending by 340B-covered entities reached $66.3 billion in 2023, a 23.4% increase from 2022.2 This expansion creates a policy arena where financial gains and costs are concentrated among organized stakeholders, with covered entities and contract pharmacies capturing most 340B margins and drug manufacturers bearing losses from discounted sales.

These savings are unevenly distributed across beneficiaries. Hospitals account for 86.6% of 340B drug purchases,2 raising concerns that program growth is driven by large health systems with heterogeneous financial needs. FQHCs and safety-net clinics account for a smaller share of total purchasing but often rely more heavily on 340B revenue and serve higher proportions of patients with low income. This heterogeneity creates divergent incentives, shaping coalitions around which program elements should be protected vs reformed.

Reflecting these pressures, a rebate pilot program was proposed by policymakers in 2025, affecting reimbursement mechanics while leaving eligibility and contract pharmacy arrangements largely untouched,3 which prompted litigation by hospital groups.4 Despite its substantial role in health care financing, political activity surrounding 340B is largely invisible to clinicians and health officials, who encounter its downstream consequences but rarely the policymaking drivers. Greater transparency around 340B-related political activity may help clinicians and administrators understand which stakeholders influence policy and how decisions align with patient needs. We examined federal lobbying disclosures to identify the organizations directly shaping 340B reform debates, compare their lobbying expenditures, and characterize policy priorities.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of federal 340B-related lobbying using OpenSecrets, which compiles quarterly filings under the Lobbying Disclosure Act. We identified all organizations reporting federal lobbying activity in 2024 within OpenSecrets’ hospitals and nursing homes industry and reviewed organizational websites, public materials, and filings to classify organization type. Entities were included if materials indicated that their primary activity was 340B advocacy; organizations with wider policy agendas were not included. Because OpenSecrets does not report membership, stakeholder alignment was identified from websites and advocacy materials. For each identified organization, we extracted lobbying expenditures from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2024, and reviewed advocacy materials summarizing 340B policy priorities and stakeholder membership. This cross-sectional study followed the STROBE reporting guideline. The study did not involve human participants; therefore, we used the NYU Grossman Institutional Review Board’s self-certification tool to determine that the study did not meet criteria for human subjects research and so did not require institutional review board approval or informed consent. Descriptive analyses summarized organizational characteristics, policy priorities, annual lobbying expenditures, and each organization’s proportion of total 340B lobbying expenditure in 2024 (most recent year of data) and 2020 to 2024. Statistical analysis was performed from November to December 2025 using Excel in Microsoft 365 (Microsoft Corp).

Results

This study analyzed 6 340B-focused organizations. Organizational priorities differed as hospital-focused groups emphasized preserving broad eligibility and contract pharmacy savings, whereas manufacturers partnered with FQHCs and safety-net organizations to support narrower eligibility and tighter contract pharmacy regulations (Table 1). Total lobbying expenditures exceeded $2.5 million in 2024 (Table 2). 340B Health, representing hospitals and health systems, accounted for 61.3% of lobbying expenditures from 2020 to 2024 and 51.5% in 2024. In 2024, Ryan White Clinics for 340B Access, 340B Working Table, and ASAP 340B (Alliance to Save America’s 340B Program) each accounted for more than 10% of total expenditure, with newer organizations exhibiting the greatest year-on-year expenditure growth.

Discussion

This analysis of 340B-focused organizations highlights differences in lobbying expenditures and policy priorities. The presence of multiple 340B-specific advocacy organizations is notable, with the emergence of new groups reflecting escalating political engagement. This finding aligns with prior work suggesting that less visible policy domains are often shaped by organized interests rather than broad constituencies.5

Political environments involving concentrated benefits and costs often generate complex issue networks rather than a single dominant coalition.6 340B-focused organizations illustrate this structure through distinct priorities regarding hospital and contract pharmacy eligibility, disease-specific safety-net funding, and government oversight. These divergent priorities fragment potential alliances and prevent 340B advocacy from mapping cleanly onto a manufacturers vs hospitals divide.

These patterns may help explain the struggle to produce foundational 340B reform. Hospital-focused coalitions are often better resourced to mobilize around program features regarding eligibility and contract pharmacy revenue, whereas FQHCs and other safety-net organizations may be unable to sustain comparable lobbying efforts. This asymmetry may favor incremental adjustments, such as the proposed rebate pilot. However, this study had several limitations: it could not assess causal influence on policy outcomes and may not have captured total 340B lobbying expenditure or all organizations engaged in lobbying, particularly larger associations with broader policy agendas.
