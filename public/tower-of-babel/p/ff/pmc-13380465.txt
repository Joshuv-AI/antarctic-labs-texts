Multiple arteriovenous malformations due to liver failure: An unusual etiology
Authors: Kanmani Anbarasan, Elancheralathan Kalyanaraman, Sumathi Bavanandam, Periyakaruppan Alagappan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are rare vascular anomalies, predominantly congenital and intracranial. We report a unique case of multiple extracranial acquired AVM in a 44-year-old man with ethanol-related decompensated chronic liver disease presenting with severe gingival hemorrhage. Angiography showed focal microarteriovenous shunting with early venous drainage in the absence of a mature, compact nidus involving the facial and lingual arterial systems, which was successfully treated with endovascular embolization. In the absence of earlier vascular pathology, an acquired etiology is suspected. Cirrhosis-associated angiogenic and inflammatory dysregulation may contribute to de novo AVM formation, and reported regression following liver transplantation suggests a potentially reversible, hepatic-driven mechanism.Keywords: Multiple arteriovenous malformation, Liver failure, Angiogenesis

Arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are rare vascular anomalies characterized by direct connections between arteries and veins, bypassing the normal capillary bed (incidence ≈1 per 100,000 persons per year). Most AVMs are congenital, whereas de novo AVMs (3%-5%) may develop secondary to trauma, ischemia, radiotherapy, infection, or hormonal changes. Intracranial AVMs account for nearly 90% of all AVMs.

We report a rare case of multiple extracranial AVMs in a patient with ethanol-related decompensated chronic liver disease. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images without disclosure of personal identity.

Case report

A 44-year-old man with a 10-year history of ethanol-related decompensated chronic liver disease, complicated by portal hypertension, portal hypertensive gastropathy, grade II esophageal varices, and coagulopathy, was undergoing evaluation for liver transplantation. He presented with a 3-year history of the left lower alveolar mucosal swelling (Fig 1, A) associated with intermittent bleeding. On examination, an indurated, noncompressible, sessile, 3 × 3 cm erythematous mucosal nodule was noted in relation to teeth 34 to 36, with obliteration of the buccal and lingual vestibules. The lesion had initially been diagnosed as a reactive giant cell granuloma in the setting of chronic local irritation from dental caries, and dental extraction was performed.

A, Swelling in the left alveolar margin before dental extraction. B, Bleeding following dental extraction.

Following extraction, the patient developed persistent pulsatile bleeding from the socket, which remained uncontrolled despite 2 hours of firm local compression and placement of a hemostatic agent (Fig 1, B). In view of underlying coagulopathy, the patient required transfusion of four units of fresh-frozen plasma, after which the bleeding was controlled. After 2 days, he had recurrent episode of bleeding from swelling, which prompted further evaluation, magnetic resonance imaging of the neck reported as ill-defined T2 heterointense lesion with diffusion restriction involving left gingivobuccal sulcus eroding adjacent mandible with postcontrast enhancement, supplied by lingual and facial artery without any flow voids (Figs 2 and 3).

Ill-defined T2 heterointense lesion in the left gingivobuccal sulcus with diffusion restriction and adjacent mandibular erosion.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showing a T2 hyperintense lesion in the left gingivobuccal sulcus with diffusion restriction and adjacent mandibular erosion, showing intense postcontrast enhancement.

In view of recurrent bleeding episodes, angiography was performed, demonstrating focal micro-arteriovenous (AV) shunting1,2 with early venous drainage (Fig 4, A and B) in the absence of a mature compact nidus. These findings suggest an evolving (prenidal)3 (Fig 5) de novo AVM characterized by immature AV shunting. The chronic liver failure-associated hyperdynamic, inflammatory, and proangiogenic state likely contributed to secondary arterialization of the lesion, clinically mimicking a high-flow AVM with pulsatile bleeding.

A, Early-phase angiographic image. B, Delayed-phase image showing micro-arteriovenous (AV) shunts without prominent nidus, with a few prominent venous channels.

Digital subtraction angiography of the left facial artery showing a tuft of capillaries (prenidus) with a prominent draining vein.

Intervention

Endovascular embolization was performed in view of active bleeding and thrombocytopenia. Under local anesthesia, right common femoral arterial access was obtained. Using a 0.035-inch guide wire and a vertebral catheter, the right common carotid artery was cannulated, and digital subtraction angiography was performed. Feeding vessels from the facial artery were selectively catheterized and embolized using Nester coils 3 × 14 mm (Supplementary Video 1, online only). The left common carotid artery was subsequently cannulated, and a feeder from the left facial artery, lingual artery was embolized with a 3 × 14 and 3 × 3 mm Nester coil (Fig 6; Supplementary Video 2, online only), achieving distal flow control and for deep penetration of nidus. Residual blush was treated with cyanoacrylate glue injection (Supplementary Video 3, online only). A cyanoacrylate-lipiodol mixture (1:2) was prepared immediately before injection, and the microcatheter was flushed with 5% dextrose to prevent premature polymerization. This resulted in complete obliteration on final angiography. Coil embolization was used in our case to achieve distal flow control4 and reduce shunt velocity, facilitating deeper penetration of the liquid embolic agent into the micro-AV channels while minimizing reflux and nontarget embolization. The microcatheter was promptly withdrawn to avoid entrapment. Hemostasis was achieved with manual compression after sheath removal.

Postcoil embolization of the right and left facial arteries demonstrating nonopacification of the lesion.

Outcome and follow-up

By postoperative day 2, the alveolar lesion had reduced to approximately 1 × 1 cm, with no further bleeding. Doppler ultrasound study of the access site demonstrated a 2 × 2 cm subcutaneous hematoma without evidence of pseudoaneurysm, which resolved with conservative management. At 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no recurrence of swelling or bleeding.

Additional lesions

On clinical examination with other lesions, including a well-defined, noncompressible, subcutaneous nodular pulsatile lesion measuring 2 × 2 cm over the right temporal scalp and a 2.5 × 2.5 cm lesion involving the right earlobe (Fig 7, A and B). Duplex ultrasound study demonstrated multiple tiny spaces ovoid within the subcutaneous plane, exhibiting high-velocity, low-resistance arterialized flow, suggestive of vascular malformations with a high-flow component. These lesions were asymptomatic and were therefore managed conservatively.

A, Arteriovenous malformation (AVM) in the right temporal region of the scalp. B, AVM involving the right earlobe.

Discussion

Fewer than 20 cases of multiple de novo AVMs5 have been reported in the literature with the most common site are intracranial associated with earlier neurological insults. The development of AVMs in cirrhosis is thought to follow a “two-hit” mechanism6: a latent genetic predisposition (first hit) combined with systemic disturbances caused by hepatic dysfunction (second hit). Cirrhosis creates a proinflammatory and proangiogenic milieu characterized by elevated vascular endothelial growth factor,7 transforming growth factor-β, tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6, and altered matrix metalloproteinase-3 and matrix metalloproteinase-9 activity. Impaired clearance of oestrogen8 and nitric oxide, along with venous hypertension and microthrombosis further promotes endothelial activation and abnormal AV communications. These mechanisms have been linked to systemic AVMs involving the lungs, limbs, and gastrointestinal tract, supporting the concept of a diffuse vascular response in advanced liver disease.

Spontaneous regression of AVMs after liver transplantation has been reported,9, 10, 11 reinforcing the hypothesis that hepatic failure12 may play a direct role in micro-AV shunting/AVM formation, and that restoration of liver function can reverse such vascular abnormalities.

The present case supports this pathophysiological model, as the patient exhibited multiple extracranial AVMs without any previous history of vascular malformations. Although congenital AVMs cannot be completely ruled out in the absence of past imaging, the clinical setting and temporal association strongly favor an acquired origin. Absence of a demonstrable nidus on angiography may result from diffuse micro-AV shunting, high-flow rapid venous transit, thrombosis-related obscuration, or an immature infiltrative prenidal AVM architecture.3

The symptomatic AVM in this patient was difficult to access surgically, and therapy was accomplished through endovascular embolization, resulting in control of hemorrhage. At 9-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of bleeding. The other two lesions that were asymptomatic are stable with no significant increase in size.

Conclusions

Acquired AVMs are extremely rare in patients with advanced liver disease and may arise from angiogenic and inflammatory mechanisms associated with hepatic dysfunction. Early recognition and minimally invasive treatment are effective. Further research is needed to better define the pathophysiology, true prevalence, and potential reversibility of AVMs in cirrhosis, which guide future therapeutic strategies.
