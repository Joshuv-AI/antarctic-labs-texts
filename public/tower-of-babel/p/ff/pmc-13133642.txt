Elevated Plasma IL-18 Levels During Chemotherapy Treatment for Breast Cancer, Exercise Capacity, and Physical Activity
Authors: Moriah P. Bellissimo, Matthew Ambrosio, Adolfo Gabriele Mauro, Eleonora Mezzaroma, Elisa N.D. Palladino, Bonnie Ky, Glenn J. Lesser, Ralph B. D’Agostino, Fadi N. Salloum, W. Gregory Hundley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical Abstract

Inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)–18, are implicated in the development of cardiovascular (CV) diseases for their roles in atherosclerotic plaque destabilization, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, left ventricular (LV) myocardial contractile dysfunction, and fibrosis, all of which increase the risk for advanced stages of heart failure (HF).1 For breast cancer (BC) survivors, CV events after receipt of potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy are emerging as leading causes of morbidity and mortality.2 Formation and activation of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain, leucine-rich repeat, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and the consequent production of IL-18 are proposed to promote cancer therapy–associated cardiotoxicity and HF progression.1 Moreover, findings in preclinical models and a pilot study in adults without cancer demonstrated that aerobic activity inhibits NLRP3 activation.3,4 This has not been evaluated in women receiving potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy, including adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy for BC. We sought to determine if IL-18 levels increase during the first 3 months of BC treatment, whether IL-18 concentrations relate to clinical assessments, and whether an increase in physical activity (PA) is associated with lower IL-18 levels during this time frame.

Women with stages I to III BC enrolled in this multicenter, prospective cohort study conducted through the Wake Forest National Cancer Institute Community Oncology Research Program Research Base under a National Cancer Institute central Institutional Review Board–approved protocol (NCT02791581, WF-97415). Women completed pretreatment testing (ie, baseline), then were reassessed 3 months into treatment. Participants were categorized by treatment groups accordingly: 1) anthracycline-based chemotherapy (anth-bc; ie, doxorubicin) with other chemotherapeutic agents; 2) non-anth-bc (including paclitaxel, cyclophosphamide, capecitabine, and gemcitabine); or 3) receiving aromatase inhibitors (AIs; anastrozole, letrozole, or exemestane) without chemotherapy. LV ejection fraction and stroke volume index (SVI) were measured using cardiac magnetic resonance. Exercise capacity was determined using the 6-minute walk test, and fatigue was self-reported using the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Fatigue survey. Participants self-reported PA levels using the Godin-Shephard Leisure Time PA Questionnaire. Plasma samples were analyzed for IL-18 and IL-1β levels using the Meso Scale Discovery assay, and C-reactive protein concentrations were measured using immunoassay. PA and inflammatory markers were square root transformed for analyses. Changes in clinical assessments were evaluated using Student’s t-tests. Multiple linear regression was used to evaluate change from baseline, and models were adjusted for outcome baseline value, BC treatment, age, body mass index, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and coronary artery disease), low-density lipoprotein level, and high-density lipoprotein level. Changes in IL-18 analyses were additionally adjusted for baseline PA levels.

This study included 181 participants (77.9% White, 16.6% Black) who were 56 ± 11 years of age, with an average body mass index of 29 ± 6 kg/m2. Comorbidities included hypertension (36.3%), diabetes mellitus (11.3%), hyperlipidemia (46.3%), coronary artery disease (1.7%), and smoking history (29.2%). Stage II was the primary (50.3%) BC diagnosis. Most participants received anth-bc (52.5%), 31.4% received non-anth-bc, and 16.0% received AIs. PA declined from 31.0 ± 24.8 to 24.6 ± 24.7 units over 3 months (P = 0.003; mean difference ± SD −6.7 ± 25.9 units). Exercise capacity declined from 462.1 ± 79.6 to 442.6 ± 86.4 m over 3 months (P = 0.014; mean difference ± SE −19.5 ± 8.8 m).

In all participants, IL-18 levels increased from 614.1 ± 28.1 pg/mL at baseline to 872.8 ± 33.3 pg/mL at 3 months (P < 0.001). By treatment group, IL-18 concentrations increased from baseline to 3 months in the anth-bc and non-anth-bc groups (from 599.5 ± 36.9 to 960.4 ± 47.8 pg/mL [P < 0.001] and from 588.4 ± 47.5 to 846.1 ± 58.6 pg/mL [P < 0.001], respectively) but were unchanged in the AI group (from 679.7 ± 76.2 to 665.3 ± 73.7 pg/mL [P > 0.99]). C-reactive protein levels increased nonsignificantly from baseline to 3 months (from 3.21 ± 0.29 to 4.04 ± 0.32 mg/dL; P = 0.055), whereas IL-1β levels were similar at both time points (from 90.1 ± 65.7 to 86.7 ± 54.2 pg/mL; P = 0.75).

A greater increase in IL-18 over 3 months was associated with a greater decline in exercise capacity (β ± SE = −2.1 ± 0.7; P = 0.002) (Figure 1) and worse SVI decline (β ± SE = −0.002 ± 0.001; P = 0.071), though not statistically significantly. Associations Between the Change in IL-18 and Clinical Markers

Analyses are adjusted for baseline interleukin-18 (IL-18) level, age, body mass index, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, breast cancer treatment, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and coronary artery disease).

Accounting for pretreatment IL-18 concentration, increased PA participation from baseline to 3 months was associated with lower 3-month IL-18 levels (β ± SE = −0.68 ± 0.21; P = 0.002). In the fully adjusted model, an increase in PA remained a predictor of lower 3-month IL-18 levels (β ± SE = −0.53 ± 0.24; P = 0.027).

This study reports an increase in IL-18 levels after starting chemotherapy. Preclinical models of anth-bc and non-anth-bc have shown sterile NLRP3-mediated inflammatory activity following chemotherapy administration.1 A greater increase in IL-18 concentration was related to a greater 3-month decline in exercise capacity and trended with worse SVI. The findings also demonstrate an increase in PA associated with lower IL-18 concentration.

Relative to prior studies in HF, pretreatment IL-18 levels were elevated, perhaps because of a role of NLRP3 in tumor progression, higher rates of obesity and comorbidities in this cohort, or higher levels of detection in the assay used.5 Nevertheless, IL-18 levels nearly doubled over the 3-month study, mirroring clinically meaningful increases in IL-18 that would lend these women to higher CV disease risk, but long-term follow-up is needed.6 Others report an increase in IL-18 and negative results for IL-1β.6 This may be attributed to the difficulty in accurately measuring the low-abundance cytokine, with high variability even with increased IL-1β activity.6

Increases in IL-18 levels were associated with clinically meaningful declines in exercise capacity and trended with lower SVI, which are primary symptoms of HF with preserved ejection fraction, the most common form of HF observed in BC survivors.7 Lower IL-18 levels with increasing PA levels during treatment, independent of baseline PA participation, highlight the clinical significance of designing PA programs that are feasible and accessible for cancer survivors during treatment. Exercise may decrease IL-18 levels by reducing ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain) expression, which is required to activate caspase-1 and mediate the cleavage of pro-IL-18 to its active form.1,3 Further work with objectively measured PA could establish the PA dose required to attenuate IL-18 increases during chemotherapy.

Study limitations include the moderate sample size and use of self-reported PA, which hinders inference into the dose of PA needed to reduce IL-18 levels.

In conclusion, IL-18, a cytokine linked to the development of atherosclerosis, HF, and myocardial infarction, was increased after 3 months of potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy for BC. Higher IL-18 concentrations were linked to greater clinical declines but were attenuated with more PA. Future work should determine if increasing PA during BC treatment can mitigate increases in IL-18 and if this is linked to reduced CV disease risk.
