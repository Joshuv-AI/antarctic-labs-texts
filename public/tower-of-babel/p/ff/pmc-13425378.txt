A call for coordinated action on problematic papers in non-coding RNA research
Authors: Ying Lou, Zhengyi Zhou, Zhesi Shen, Menghui Li
Published: 2026

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) have emerged as major regulators of a wide range of biological processes and have attracted considerable attention as promising targets and tools for disease diagnosis and therapy (Nemeth et al., 2024). However, the rapid expansion of this field, driven in part by the vast number of ncRNAs that remain insufficiently characterized, has also created opportunities for the proliferation of potentially fraudulent studies (Byrne et al., 2019). As a result, ncRNA research has become one of the scientific areas most heavily affected by paper mill activity. More than 2 100 ncRNA-related articles have been retracted due to academic misconduct, with the retraction rate in this field reaching 1.41%, approximately 20 times higher than the global retraction rate of 0.068% (Li & Shen, 2024). These figures place ncRNA research among the domains most seriously impacted by academic misconduct (Li & Shen, 2024). The growing accumulation of unreliable papers in this field has inevitably weakened confidence in the broader literature. We identified a significant prevalence of retracted articles and articles receiving critical commentary on PubPeer in the ncRNA field, many of which have already been cited extensively (Figure 1). This pattern carries serious implications for subsequent basic research, translational investigation, and clinical practice. Greater awareness of the scale and consequences of flawed publications in ncRNA research is therefore urgently needed, along with more rigorous scrutiny of published studies.

Analysis of approximately 154000 papers indexed under the Micro & Long Noncoding RNA topic in the InCites database from 2000 to 2023 (accessed 3 January 2025) revealed that 2961 papers had been retracted for various reasons, representing 1.92% of total publications in this field. By comparison, the global retraction rate over the same period was only 0.08%. Moreover, 9108 papers (5.92%) received more than 24300 comments on PubPeer. At the global level, an estimated 105300 articles received comments on PubPeer, primarily in relation to concerns about article content, corresponding to an overall comment rate of approximately 0.27% (Richardson et al., 2025).

Over the past two decades, the ncRNA literature has undergone rapid expansion, with annual publication output increasing from 102 papers in 2000 to 18 492 in 2021, followed by a subsequent decline (Figure 1A). Between 2000 and 2021, the compound annual growth rate of relevant ncRNA publications reached 29.69%, far exceeding the global publication growth rate of 6.54%. During this period of accelerated growth, post-publication concern also intensified. Notably, PubPeer comments increased from 676 in 2019 to 5 142 in 2021 and remained persistently high thereafter, with more than 3 700 comments recorded each year. Retractions also increased sharply over the same period, reaching more than 700 by 2023 (Figure 1B).

When examined by publication year, both comment and retraction patterns closely tracked the expansion of the field. In 2002, only three papers received PubPeer comments and only one paper was retracted; by 2019, these numbers increased to 1 803 and 656, respectively (Figure 1C). Before 2020, the annual proportions of commented and retracted papers increased steadily and reached a peak in 2019, with a comment rate of 11.15% and a retraction rate of 4.06% (Figure 1D). These findings indicate an exceptionally high proportion of problematic publications and stand in sharp contrast to the global retraction rate of 0.1% reported for the same year (Richardson et al., 2025). Because concerns are often raised and retractions are often issued only after a substantial delay, rates for more recent publication years are likely underestimated. Collectively, these findings indicate a progressive increase in the proportion of questionable research output over time and point to publications around 2019 as warranting particularly careful scrutiny. Similar patterns were also observed in analyses of more specific topics, including microRNA in cancer and lncRNA, as well as in string-based searches of titles and abstracts in Web of Science and OpenAlex, further demonstrating qualitative agreement with previously reported findings (Richardson et al., 2025).

Retracted papers in the ncRNA field have already exerted substantial influence across multiple downstream domains, garnering more than 92 600 citations in academic papers, 800 citations in patents, 115 citations in clinical trials, and 30 citations in policy documents (Figure 1E). The impact of papers receiving critical commentary has also been considerable. Notably, commented papers accumulated more than 584 800 citations in academic papers, 6 100 citations in patents, 1 172 citations in clinical trials, and 230 citations in policy documents (Figure 1E). These patterns indicate that problematic publications have not been confined to scholarly literature, but have propagated into translational, regulatory, and clinical contexts. For instance, one retracted paper (PMID: 18589210), published in 2008 and only retracted in 2023, accumulated more than 2 100 citations in academic papers, including more than 40 post-retraction citations in 2024, and was cited more than 170 times in patents. One related patent application (WO2009015357A1) incorporated figures from the study, and the resulting patent family was cited more than 100 times by subsequent patents. The same paper was also cited in 14 clinical trials and three policy documents (indexed in Dimensions). Notably, a policy document (PMID: 27253000) still advocates for using microRNA levels as early detection markers, a suggestion built entirely upon the retracted paper's erroneous conclusions. A second retracted paper (PMID: 32384893), published in 2020 and retracted in 2022, accumulated more than 300 citations in academic papers. Similarly, another article published in 2018 and identified on PubPeer in 2022 following concerns regarding image duplication (PMID: 29478899) was cited 106 times in academic papers, 12 times in patent applications, and four times in clinical trials.

These findings indicate that unreliable knowledge has already propagated widely, extending from basic research to clinical translation and policy development. Studies have shown that articles citing retracted papers face an elevated risk of subsequent retraction (Usman & Balke, 2024). More importantly, incorporation of retracted studies can compromise evidence synthesis and weaken confidence in the resulting conclusions (Liu et al., 2025). Once embedded in citation networks, such distorted evidence can infiltrate clinical decision-making and amplify negative outcomes, with potential consequences for patient health. Additional concerns include circulation of incorrect reagents, routine use of misidentified cell lines, propagation of false gene-disease associations, and continued investment in unproductive hypotheses. Together, these effects make removal of unreliable knowledge from future research and clinical practice exceptionally difficult.

Responsibility for the current situation cannot be reduced to any single group, nor should accountability be framed in national terms. The central purpose of this call is not to assign blame, but to urge coordinated action across the scientific community to address the problematic literature that has already entered the record and to limit further contamination of the knowledge base.

For funding agencies and research institutions, sustained surveillance of identified issues and stronger recognition of the shared responsibility to cleanse the scientific record are both imperative. Effective removal of flawed research is not merely corrective but is fundamental to scientific progress. This need becomes even more urgent in the era of AI4Science, in which unreliable information can be disseminated, amplified, and reused at an unprecedented speed. These agencies should therefore establish policies that encourage voluntary disclosure of previously flawed work, including mechanisms such as time-limited penalty exemptions for self-reporting.

Journals and publishers must also adopt a more proactive role in implementing post-publication review strategies to examine their archival literature systematically. When concerns are raised about a paper, journals should issue a prompt editorial expression of concern to notify readers that an investigation is underway and to provide a clear and visible warning. As indicated by the present findings, image-related problems constitute the most common category of concern (Figure 1F). Post-publication review could therefore begin with AI-assisted screening for suspicious images. A major challenge for the scientific community is the development of affordable AI-based image detection systems that can operate across journals and publishers at scale.

Researchers must also fully recognize the seriousness of this problem. Every cited source should be evaluated carefully, and papers already known to be problematic should not continue to be referenced, thereby limiting further dissemination of unreliable claims. Researchers also carry an ethical responsibility to report flawed papers when identified and to actively raise concerns through post-publication review platforms.

Comprehensive research integrity databases must be developed, together with accessible and user-oriented interfaces that allow both researchers and the public to retrieve relevant information efficiently. In parallel, the characteristics of academic misconduct, particularly patterns associated with paper mills, require more systematic investigation to improve understanding of how such publications are produced and disseminated (Byrne et al., 2024). Integration of signals from anomalous text, citation networks, collaboration structures, public commentary, and related sources could enable construction of candidate publication lists for prioritized scrutiny, thereby substantially reducing the time and effort required for investigation.

Responsibility for mitigating the impact of unreliable information is shared across all stakeholders in the research ecosystem, including its consequences for both the academic community and the public. The problems identified in ncRNA research are not isolated to this field but instead reflect a broader pattern that extends across multiple disciplines (Li & Shen, 2024). Although the degree of severity may differ among fields, no discipline can regard itself as exempt. Broad acknowledgment of the issue and corresponding corrective action are therefore required across all fields of research.
