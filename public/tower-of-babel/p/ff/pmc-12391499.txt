Knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding breast cancer screening and associated factors among female undergraduate students at Bonga University, Ethiopia
Authors: Tadele Emagneneh, Tariku Daniel, Amanuel Tebabal Nega, Delelegn Tsegaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide. Screening aims to detect precancerous lesions and early-stage carcinomas in asymptomatic women, helping to reduce morbidity and mortality. Methods such as breast self-examination (BSE), clinical breast examination (CBE), and mammography play a vital role in early detection. However, in low-resource settings like Ethiopia, awareness and practice of these screening methods remain limited. Female university students represent a key group for prevention, yet little is known about their screening behaviors. This study aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices towards breast cancer screening, along with associated factors, among female regular undergraduate students at Bonga University. A cross-sectional study was conducted at Bonga University from March 11 to 12, 2020, involving 399 female undergraduate students selected through simple random sampling. Participants were invited to complete a structured questionnaire in a private, designated room to ensure confidentiality and reduce peer influence during data collection. Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Bivariate logistic regression was initially performed to examine associations between the dependent and independent variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Statistical significance was determined at a p-value < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval. The study revealed that 211 participants (52.9%) demonstrated inadequate knowledge, 222 (55.6%) exhibited negative attitudes, and 319 (79.9%) reported poor practices regarding breast cancer screening. Factors significantly associated with limited knowledge included the absence of a family history of breast cancer (AOR = 4.5; 95% CI: 2–10), being a first-year student (AOR = 4; 95% CI: 1.3–3.3), rural residency, and being a second-year student (AOR = 2; 95% CI: 1.2–3.3). Negative attitudes were significantly associated with being unmarried (AOR = 7.1; 95% CI: 1.6–32), first-year student status (AOR = 7.5; 95% CI: 2.6–21.5), second-year student status (AOR = 6.5; 95% CI: 1.9–22), and the perception of not being at risk for breast cancer (AOR = 2.6; 95% CI: 1.07–6). Poor screening practices were significantly linked to the absence of a family history of breast cancer (AOR = 5.2; 95% CI: 2.2–12.2), being a first-year student (AOR = 5.5; 95% CI: 2.3–12.7), and belonging to a younger age group (AOR = 8.4; 95% CI: 1.6–42.5). The study revealed that a significant proportion of students at Bonga University had inadequate knowledge, negative attitudes, and poor practices related to breast cancer screening. Key contributing factors included lack of family history, younger age, rural residence, and first-year student. These findings underscore the need for targeted awareness campaigns, particularly among younger and rural students. Collaboration between Bonga University, health professionals, and local media is essential to improve knowledge and promote early detection through breast cancer screening.Keywords: Knowledge, Attitude, Practice, Breast cancer screening, Associated factorsSubject terms: Cancer, Biomarkers, Health care, Oncology

Introduction

Breast cancer is the uncontrolled growth of epithelial cells in the breast and is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women worldwide1. It affects approximately 2.1 million women annually and accounts for the highest number of cancer-related deaths in women. Globally, approximately 627,000 women die from breast cancer each year, accounting for about 15% of all cancer-related deaths among women worldwide2. Although breast cancer was previously more common in developed nations, its incidence is now increasing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) as well3.

Breast cancer screening (BCS) aims to identify precancerous lesions and early-stage cancers in asymptomatic women, thereby reducing morbidity and mortality4. Despite the importance of methods such as breast self-examination (BSE), clinical breast examination (CBE), and mammography, awareness and uptake remain low in low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia. Consequently, most women are diagnosed at advanced stages due to inadequate knowledge and poor screening practices5.

In Ethiopia, breast cancer is the most common cancer among women, accounting for 33% of all cancer cases and 30.2% of adult cancers, as reported by the National Cancer Control Plan6,7. Although the incidence of breast cancer is increasing, especially in low-resource settings like Ethiopia, the majority of cases are detected at advanced stages, leading to poor survival outcomes8. In response, the government has established oncology units in five university hospitals and partnered with NGOs to expand screening services9. While similar efforts in other countries have improved awareness and access, Ethiopia continues to face major challenges, particularly in rural areas where services remain limited6. National data reveal that only 23% of women are knowledgeable about screening methods, 21.4% practice them, and awareness of warning signs and risk factors is as low as 22.1% and 1.4%, respectively6,10,11 .

Several studies conducted in Ethiopia have consistently highlighted gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to breast cancer and its screening12–15. A study in Addis Ababa reported that 36.6% of breast cancer patients had never heard of the disease, and 90.7% had never undergone clinical breast examination or mammography16. At Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, approximately 216 new breast cancer cases are diagnosed annually, with a notable proportion—42.35%—being younger than 39 years and 10.25% under 29 years of age17. Various studies across different regions of Ethiopia have identified key factors significantly associated with breast cancer-related KAP, including age, educational status, family history of breast cancer, income level, marital status, and place of residence18–23. However, most studies in Ethiopia have focused on urban populations and single screening methods, offering limited insight into comprehensive practices. Additionally, important sociodemographic factors, such as parental education, academic year, and residential background, remain underexplored24–26.

Despite the rising burden of breast cancer globally, studies assessing KAP related to BCS among university students in rural or semi-urban settings remain limited. Students in these areas often encounter substantial barriers to accessing health information and screening services due to infrastructural, educational, and socioeconomic constraints. Moreover, previous research has frequently overlooked critical contextual factors—such as parental education, year of study, and place of residence—that may significantly shape students’ awareness, perceptions, and preventive behaviors regarding breast cancer screening26,27. This study aims to address these gaps by assessing KAP and associated factors among female regular undergraduate students at Bonga University, a semi-urban institution in Ethiopia that has not been previously studied in this context. The research is grounded in the Health Belief Model, which underscores the influence of knowledge and perceived susceptibility in motivating health-related behaviors. Accordingly, the study investigates key predictors, including family history of breast cancer, geographic background, and academic year, to identify high-risk groups and barriers to early detection.

Given the increasing incidence of breast cancer in Ethiopia and the crucial role of early diagnosis in improving survival outcomes and reducing healthcare costs, this study provides timely and context-specific evidence to inform targeted awareness campaigns and screening strategies. By focusing on an underserved population, the findings contribute to enhancing preventive healthcare and promoting proactive health-seeking behaviors among young women in Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

This study was conducted at Bonga University, located in the Kafa Zone of the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Regional State, Ethiopia, approximately 449 km southwest of Addis Ababa. Established in 2007 E.C., the university is situated on a single campus and comprises five colleges and 17 departments. It has an enrollment of 4,720 students in the regular program, with 2,217 of them being female. The university’s academic structure includes the College of Social Sciences (offering programs in Geography, History, English, Sociology, Psychology, and Special Needs Education), the College of Business and Economics (offering Accounting, Management, Economics, Hotel and Tourism, and Law), the College of Agriculture (offering programs in Plant Science, Soil and Water Science, Forestry, Animal Science, and Agro-Economics), the College of Natural and Computational Sciences (offering Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry, Biology, and Sport Science), and the College of Engineering (offering Civil Engineering, Mechanical Engineering, and Electrical Engineering). Additionally, the university offers pre-social science and pre-natural science programs, enrolling 2,342 students under the new national curriculum.

The region’s healthcare facilities include one general hospital, one health center, and five government-operated health posts. The private sector includes seven clinics (including five medium-level clinics), two drug distribution stores, and eight drug vendors. The study was conducted over two days, from March 11 to 12, 2020.

Study design

An institution-based cross-sectional study design was used.

Source population

All regular undergraduate female students in Bonga University.

Study population

The study population was comprised of undergraduate female students who were randomly selected from different departments in Bonga University.

Eligibility criteria

All female students who were attending a regular degree program were included, while Female students who were severely ill and unable to communicate at the time of data collection were excluded.

Sampling technique and sampling procedure

The study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique from the source population. Initially, all the colleges within the university were identified by name, followed by a list of departments under each college. Departments were selected proportionally based on the number of departments in each college. Specifically, out of five departments, Biology and Sports were chosen from the College of Natural and Computational Sciences, while Civil Engineering was selected from the College of Engineering, which has only three departments. Similarly, Narm and Agro-economics were chosen from the College of Agriculture, History and Sociology from the College of Social Science, and Accounting and Management from the College of Business and Economics, with a lottery method used to select departments from each college. For first-year students in the Pre-Natural Science program, seven sections were selected by lottery out of 22, while six sections were chosen from the Pre-Social Science program out of 20. The sample size (424) was then allocated to each selected department using probability proportional to size (Fig. 1). Data collection tools and methods

A structured, closed-ended questionnaire was developed based on a review of relevant literature and adapted to suit the study objectives. The questionnaire was prepared in English and organized into five sections. The first section captured socio-demographic characteristics of the participants. The second and third sections assessed respondents’ knowledge of breast cancer and its risk factors, and knowledge of breast cancer screening, respectively. The fourth section evaluated attitudes towards breast cancer screening, while the fifth section focused on participants’ screening practices. Before data collection, a pretest was conducted among 44 students at Mizan-Tepi University’s Mizan campus to evaluate the clarity, relevance, and consistency of the questionnaire items. Based on the pretest findings, minor modifications were made primarily to improve the wording and clarity of items in the knowledge and attitude sections, ensuring better comprehension by respondents. These changes were linguistic and structural in nature and did not affect the scientific content, scope, or objectives of the tool.

Data collection was conducted by six female data collectors, all of whom were trained BSc nurses and midwives. They were supported by three supervisors with bachelor’s degrees in midwifery or nursing. A two-day training was provided for both data collectors and supervisors before the pretest, covering the study objectives, ethical considerations, questionnaire content, and participants’ rights. Supervisors oversaw the overall data collection process and ensured adherence to protocol throughout the study.

Data collection procedure

The principal investigator, in collaboration with department heads and student representatives from the respective departments, coordinated the data collection schedule before its initiation. Once the students were selected randomly, they were assembled in the designated room for data collection. Before beginning, informed consent was obtained from each participant. To maintain confidentiality and prevent information leakage between departments, data collection was carried out simultaneously across the selected departments. Upon completion, the supervisors and principal investigator reviewed the data to ensure its completeness and consistency.

Data quality management

Before the main data collection, a pre-test was conducted on 10% of the total sample size among students at Mizan-Tepi University, Mizan Campus. The purpose of the pre-test was to evaluate the clarity, consistency, and relevance of the questionnaire items. Based on feedback from the participants, the questionnaire was revised by rephrasing unclear questions, removing redundant or irrelevant items, and reorganizing some sections to improve logical flow and comprehensibility. To assess internal consistency, reliability analysis was performed using Cronbach’s alpha. Before the pre-test, the Cronbach’s alpha scores were 0.78 for the knowledge section, 0.90 for attitude, and 0.70 for practice. After incorporating pre-test feedback and making minor revisions, these values improved to 0.82 for knowledge, 0.92 for attitude, and 0.76 for practice, indicating acceptable to excellent internal consistency across the sections.

The questionnaire, which was originally prepared in English, was used without translation as the study participants were proficient in English. The tool was adapted from previously validated questionnaires used in similar studies6,22,25. Face validity was also evaluated by a panel of subject matter experts to ensure the relevance and appropriateness of each item. To maintain data quality, six female data collectors (BSc nurses and midwives) and three supervisors (with bachelor’s degrees in midwifery or nursing) were recruited. They underwent two days of training covering the study objectives, ethical considerations, questionnaire content, and procedures for data collection. Close supervision was maintained throughout the data collection period by the supervisors and the principal investigator to ensure the completeness and consistency of the data. The collected data were initially entered into EpiData Manager version 4.6, then cross-checked for errors and completeness. Cleaned data were exported to SPSS version 22 for further statistical analysis.

Data analysis procedure

Data were processed and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Before analysis, the dataset was carefully reviewed for completeness and consistency. Data cleaning procedures included checking for and addressing missing values and data entry errors. To ensure uniform scoring direction, negatively worded attitude items were reverse-coded so that higher scores consistently reflected more favorable attitudes, improving the internal consistency and interpretability of scale scores for subsequent analyses.

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, measures of central tendency, and graphical summaries, were used to describe the study variables. Attitude scores were computed based on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Multiple-choice and ordinal variables were transformed into dichotomous (binary) variables to facilitate regression analysis. Specifically, knowledge and attitude scores were dichotomized using the mean value as a cut-off point. Participants scoring equal to or above the mean were classified as having “good knowledge” or a “positive attitude,” while those scoring below the mean were considered to have “poor knowledge” or a “negative attitude.” Other continuous or categorical variables, such as income, were similarly categorized using the median or literature-based thresholds to ensure comparability and interpretability.

Bivariate logistic regression analyses were first conducted to explore associations between independent variables and the three outcome variables: knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) related to breast cancer screening. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were selected as candidates for multivariable logistic regression. This relaxed threshold is consistent with epidemiological guidelines, such as those proposed by Hosmer and Lemeshow in Applied Logistic Regression, to avoid excluding potentially important predictors. Multivariable logistic regression was then performed to identify independent predictors of each KAP outcome. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were reported. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model fitness was evaluated using the Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients, which showed statistical significance across all models. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test confirmed an acceptable model fit, with p-values of 0.51 for knowledge, 0.60 for attitude, and 0.80 for practice. Multicollinearity was assessed by examining the standard error of regression coefficients. Elevated standard errors (above ± 2) were observed between knowledge and attitude with practice, indicating potential interdependence among these variables.

Study variables

Dependent variables

Knowledge, Attitude, and Breast Cancer Screening Practice.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors, including age, marital status, place of residence, father’s and mother’s educational levels, and family income. Additional variables include a family history of breast cancer, acquaintance with someone diagnosed with breast cancer, perceived risk of developing breast cancer, and perceived benefits of breast cancer screening. Other factors considered are personal history of breast cancer, academic year, knowledge of signs and symptoms, and awareness of risk factors.

Age grouping and justification

Participants were categorized into three age groups: ≤ 20 years, 21–30 years, and 31–40 years. This classification was informed by commonly used public health research frameworks and previous studies with similar objectives28,29. The grouping reflects key developmental stages and was designed to explore potential differences in knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to breast cancer screening across age cohorts. This approach enabled more meaningful interpretation of age-related behavioral patterns within the study population.

Operational definition

Good knowledge is defined as a knowledge score of greater than or equal to the mean knowledge score25.

Poor knowledge is defined as a knowledge score of less than the mean knowledge score.

Positive attitude It is defined as an attitude score of greater than or equal to the mean attitude score.

Negative attitude an attitude score of less than or equal to the mean attitude score25.

Good practice is the use of any of the screening methods, like that of BSE, CBE, or Mammography, regularly within the recommended time interval for each method; otherwise, poor practice25,30.

Good practice of breast self-examination When breast self-examination is performed, a week after each menses. Otherwise called poor practice30.

Good clinical breast examination is undergoing clinical breast examination by health professionals starting from age 20 and every 3 years thereafter, as well as every year after age 40.

Good practice of mammography undergoing mammography starting from age 40 and every year latter.

Perceived risk and perceived benefit

Perceived risk In this study, perceived risk refers to an individual’s subjective belief about their vulnerability to breast cancer, influenced by factors such as family history, personal lifestyle, and demographic characteristics. It reflects how likely a person feels they are to develop breast cancer. Perceived benefit refers to the individual’s belief in the effectiveness of breast cancer screening (e.g., breast self-examination or clinical breast examination) in reducing the likelihood of developing breast cancer or improving early detection and outcomes. These two concepts are central to the Health Belief Model, which guided our analysis of how students’ beliefs about risk and benefit influenced their attitudes and practices regarding breast cancer screening.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 399 female students from various colleges at Bonga University participated in the study, resulting in a response rate of 94.3%. The majority of participants, 301 (71.0%), were aged 20 years or younger. Most respondents, 246 (61.7%), reported coming from rural areas. In terms of marital status, 359 participants (90.0%) had never been married. Concerning academic level, the majority, 330 (82.7%), were first-year students. Regarding parental education, most mothers, 280 (70.2%), and a substantial proportion of fathers, 153 (38.3%), had no formal education. In terms of religion, the majority of participants, 252 (63.2%), identified as Orthodox Christians (Table 1).

Factors associated with KAP

Fourteen variables were included in the bivariable analysis: age, marital status, residence, academic year, maternal educational level, paternal educational level, family history of breast cancer, acquaintance with someone diagnosed with breast cancer, awareness of breast cancer risk factors, personal history of breast cancer, and perceived benefits. The analysis aimed to identify variables with a p-value less than 0.25 for inclusion in the multivariable analysis.

Factors associated with knowledge of breast cancer screening among participants

In the bivariable logistic regression analysis, perceived susceptibility, knowledge of risk factors, knowledge of signs and symptoms, parental educational levels, family income, personal history of breast cancer, and knowing someone diagnosed with breast cancer were not significantly associated with knowledge of breast cancer screening methods. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25, namely, family history of breast cancer, residence, academic year, marital status, age, and perceived benefit, were included in the multivariable analysis to adjust for potential confounding factors. The multivariable analysis revealed that residence, academic year, and family history of breast cancer were significantly associated with knowledge of breast cancer screening methods, whereas marital status, perceived benefit, and age were not.

Participants without a family history of breast cancer were 4.5 times more likely to have poor knowledge compared to those with a family history (AOR = 4.5; 95% CI: 2.0–10.0). Respondents from rural areas were twice as likely to have poor knowledge compared to their urban counterparts (AOR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.2–3.3). Furthermore, second-year students were 2.9 times more likely to have poor knowledge compared to third-year students (AOR = 2.9; 95% CI: 1.2–13.8), and first-year students were four times more likely to have poor knowledge than third-year students (AOR = 4.0; 95% CI: 1.3–3.3) (Table 6).

Factors associated with attitude towards breast cancer screening among participants

Bivariable and multivariable analyses were performed to identify factors associated with respondents’ attitudes toward breast cancer screening. In the bivariable analysis, variables such as knowledge of risk factors, knowledge of signs and symptoms, age, residence, family income, personal history of breast cancer, knowing someone diagnosed with breast cancer, and family history of breast cancer were not significantly associated with attitudes toward breast cancer screening (p-value > 0.25). The remaining variables, with a p-value less than 0.25, were included in the multivariable analysis to control for potential confounding factors.

The multivariable analysis revealed significant associations between academic year, perceived susceptibility, and marital status with attitudes toward breast cancer screening, while perceived benefit was not significantly associated. Specifically, first-year students were 7.5 times more likely to have a negative attitude toward breast cancer screening compared to third-year students (AOR = 7.5; 95% CI: 2.6–21.5). Second-year students were 6.5 times more likely to hold a negative attitude compared to third-year students (AOR = 6.5; 95% CI: 1.9–22). Additionally, never-married respondents were 7.1 times more likely to have a negative attitude toward breast cancer screening than those who had ever been married (AOR = 7.1; 95% CI: 1.6–32). Furthermore, respondents who perceived themselves as not at risk of developing breast cancer were 2.6 times more likely to have a negative attitude toward breast cancer screening compared to those who considered themselves at risk (AOR = 2.6; 95% CI: 1.07–6) (Table 7).

Discussion

Breast cancer remains a significant public health concern, with early detection playing a crucial role in improving outcomes. This study assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to breast cancer screening among female regular undergraduate students at Bonga University, along with the factors influencing these aspects. Understanding these factors is essential for designing effective awareness programs and interventions to enhance early detection and screening uptake among young women.

Knowledge of respondents towards breast cancer screening

This study found that slightly more than half of the respondents, 211 (52.9%), had poor knowledge of breast cancer screening. This result aligns with findings from studies conducted among undergraduate students at Addis Ababa University in Ethiopia, high school teachers in Nigeria, and students at Klang University in Malaysia, where poor knowledge was reported at 50.1%, 50.7%, and 55%, respectively6,19,31. The poor knowledge of breast cancer screening in this study may be due to limited awareness, lack of structured health education, cultural misconceptions, and restricted access to healthcare services. Similar findings in studies from Addis Ababa University, Nigeria, and Malaysia could be attributed to comparable study populations, methodologies, and the exclusion of health science students, who generally have better knowledge.

Conversely, this finding is lower than results from studies where the majority of respondents demonstrated good knowledge, such as those conducted at Haramaya University (95%), Jimma University (89%), Addis Ababa City (60%), and among female students in Turkey (73.3%)10,20,32,33. The discrepancy with the Turkish study could be due to geographical differences and a more advanced healthcare system, which provides better access to health services and screening information. The variations with studies from Haramaya and Jimma Universities might be explained by the fact that their participants were health science students, who are expected to have greater awareness of breast cancer screening. Similarly, the difference from the Addis Ababa City study could be due to the study participants being health extension workers, who are likely to have better screening knowledge. Furthermore, the current finding is lower than results from studies conducted in Mekelle, Ethiopia (75%), and Palestine (74%), where respondents also demonstrated poor knowledge34,35. The observed variation may be attributed to differences in the operational definitions used in these studies.

Attitude of respondents towards breast cancer screening

The study found that 222 (55.6%) of the participants had a negative attitude toward breast cancer screening. This percentage is lower than a study conducted in Abha City, Saudi Arabia, where 67% of respondents exhibited a negative attitude36. The difference may be attributed to variations in geographical location, educational background—since participants in this study were university students—and sample size, which was relatively smaller than that of the Saudi study. Additionally, this finding is higher than results from studies conducted in Addis Ababa City, among urban health extension workers in Addis Ababa, and in Port Harcourt University and Vietnam, where negative attitudes were reported at 26.6%, 19%, 32.1%, and 38.9%, respectively24,31,33,37. The discrepancy with the Vietnam study may be due to better access to healthcare facilities and information, while the difference with Addis Ababa City could be linked to variations in study populations and sample sizes. The lower percentage among health extension workers is likely because they are health professionals and are expected to have a more positive attitude toward breast cancer screening.

Practice of students towards breast cancer screening

This study found that 319 (80%) of respondents had poor breast cancer screening practices. This result is consistent with studies conducted at Haramaya and Addis Ababa Universities in Ethiopia, Abha City in Saudi Arabia, and Iraq, where poor practice was reported at 19.6%, 17.8%, 23%, and 21%, respectively6,10,36,38. However, the finding is lower compared to studies conducted among urban women and health extension workers in Addis Ababa, as well as in Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, and Lebanon, where better screening practices were reported at 30%, 36.2%, 63.7%, 39.6%, and 79.2%, respectively. The discrepancy may be due to greater access to breast cancer screening information through awareness campaigns in Arab countries and better health education in Nigeria4. The difference with Addis Ababa urban women may be attributed to variations in sample size and age, as respondents in this study were younger. In the case of health extension workers, their higher awareness of breast cancer and screening methods could explain the difference. In contrast, this study showed a higher screening practice than a study conducted in Kersa, Oromia Region, Ethiopia, where 96.4% of rural women had never performed breast self-examination. This difference could be due to variations in educational levels, as participants in the current study were university students39. Additionally, 88.9% of participants reported poor practice regarding clinical breast examination. This finding aligns with studies conducted among female health professionals at public health centers in Addis Ababa and in Abha City, Saudi Arabia, where poor practice was reported at 88.1% and 85.5%, respectively25,36. However, this study found a higher rate of poor clinical breast examination practice compared to studies conducted in Yemen, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, and Vietnam, where non-practice rates were 17.5%, 27.4%, 71%, and 21.9%, respectively22,37,40,41. The discrepancy with Vietnam may be due to better access to healthcare facilities and information on breast cancer risks, while the differences with Saudi Arabia, Lebanon, and Yemen may be linked to national campaigns promoting free or subsidized screening services. Conversely, this study reported a lower rate of poor clinical breast examination practice compared to studies conducted in Kersa, Ethiopia, among rural women (95.4%) and in Nigeria among secondary school teachers (85.5%)23,42. The difference may stem from variations in sample size and educational background, as university students in this study are likely to have greater awareness. Furthermore, an overwhelming majority, 397 (99.5%), had never undergone mammography screening, consistent with findings from a study in Kersa, Ethiopia23. This could be due to limited familiarity with mammography and the younger age of most respondents, making them ineligible for routine mammography screening.

Factors associated with knowledge of breast cancer screening

The findings of this study demonstrated a significant association between students’ knowledge of breast cancer screening and their place of residence. Students from rural areas were twice as likely to have poor knowledge compared to those from urban areas. This disparity may be attributed to better access to health information and services in urban settings. These results align with studies conducted at Jimma University in Ethiopia and mainland China20,21, likely due to similarities in study tools and the educational backgrounds of respondents. Additionally, a family history of breast cancer was significantly associated with knowledge of breast cancer screening. Respondents without a family history of breast cancer were 4.5 times more likely to have poor knowledge than those with a family history. This difference may be because individuals with affected family members are more exposed to information and firsthand experiences regarding the disease. These findings are consistent with studies conducted at Addis Ababa University and in Medina, Saudi Arabia6,43, possibly due to similarities in study tools and study populations. Furthermore, the academic year was found to be a significant factor in knowledge levels. First-year students were four times more likely to have poor knowledge compared to third-year students, while second-year students were 2.9 times more likely to have poor knowledge compared to third-year students. These results are in line with studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and Jimma University20,44, likely due to the use of similar research tools and the comparable educational levels of respondents.

Factors associated with the attitude of breast cancer screening

First-year students were found to be four times more likely to have a negative attitude toward breast cancer screening compared to third-year students. This difference may be due to the increased sharing of life experiences as students’ progress through their academic years. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Saudi Arabia44, where similar inclusion and exclusion criteria and assessment tools were used. In contrast, a study at Addis Ababa University found no association between attitude and academic year6. The discrepancy could be attributed to the inclusion of additional screening tools (e.g., mammography and clinical breast exams) in the current study. The study also revealed a significant association between perceived risk of breast cancer and attitude toward screening. Respondents who did not perceive themselves as at risk of developing breast cancer were eight times more likely to have a negative attitude toward screening compared to those who felt they were at risk. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Turkey, where individuals who did not perceive themselves at risk exhibited negative attitudes toward clinical breast exams and mammography45. Additionally, respondents who were never married were 8.2 times more likely to have a negative attitude toward breast cancer screening compared to those who were ever married. This difference may stem from the support and encouragement married individuals may receive from their partners regarding breast cancer-related issues. However, this finding contrasts with a study conducted in Saudi Arabia, where no significant association was found between marital status and attitude toward screening46. The discrepancy may be due to differences in geographical location, educational status, and cultural factors among study participants47.

Factors associated with the practice of breast cancer screening

This study found significant associations between age, academic year, and family history of breast cancer and the practice of breast cancer screening. Younger students were 8.4 times more likely to not undergo screening than older students. This difference may be attributed to varying life experiences. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in Nigeria, Lebanon, Iran, and Malaysia19,40,42,48, with the similarity to Nigeria possibly due to similar tools used, and the alignment with Lebanon and Malaysia likely due to similarities in the study populations.

Another significant factor associated with breast cancer screening was family history. Respondents without a family history of breast cancer were 5.2 times more likely not to undergo screening compared to those with a family history. This finding is consistent with a study conducted at Addis Ababa University6, likely due to similarities in operational definitions and study populations. The difference in screening practices may be due to those with a family history sharing experiences and knowledge about the disease, its detection methods, and its seriousness.

The academic year was also significantly associated with screening practices. First-year students were 5.4 times more likely not to undergo screening than third-year students. This finding aligns with a study conducted at Jimma University20, likely due to similarities in education levels and inclusion/exclusion criteria. However, it contrasts with a study at Debreberhan University, where no such association was observed. The discrepancy could be due to differences in exclusion criteria, as students residing outside the dormitories were not excluded in this study11.

Based on the findings, clinical breast examination (CBE) and breast self-examination (BSE) are recommended as appropriate initial screening methods for young women. These techniques are simple, cost-effective, and feasible in low-resource settings. While mammography remains the standard for older women, it is not ideal for young girls due to high breast tissue density and limited access, especially in rural areas. Therefore, promoting CBE and BSE is more practical and relevant for early detection in this age group. Recommended strategies include integrating breast cancer education into academic curricula using age-appropriate materials, establishing peer-led mentorship programs, and conducting media and community-based outreach campaigns. In addition, interactive workshops and practical training on breast self-examination (BSE) should be offered in schools and community health centers to enhance early detection skills and confidence among young women.

Limitations of the study

The study was conducted using a cross-sectional design and did not include qualitative research.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study revealed that the majority of female undergraduate students at Bonga University exhibited limited knowledge, negative attitudes, and inadequate practices concerning breast cancer screening. Factors significantly associated with poor knowledge and screening behaviors included rural residence, younger age, absence of a family history of breast cancer, and being in the first year of university. Negative attitudes were more prevalent among unmarried students and those unaware of their risk. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted, evidence-based interventions, particularly for vulnerable subgroups such as rural and younger students. The Ministry of Health should prioritize breast cancer awareness initiatives tailored to rural communities. In parallel, Bonga University, through collaboration with healthcare professionals and media outlets, should implement comprehensive breast health education programs, with a particular focus on first-year students and those with no family history of the disease.

Looking ahead, future research should address the limitations of this study by adopting larger sample sizes, engaging more diverse populations, and employing longitudinal designs to track changes in knowledge, attitudes, and practices over time. Additionally, qualitative studies exploring cultural, social, and behavioral influences on screening practices would provide deeper insights. Investigating the effectiveness of targeted educational interventions will also be critical to developing sustainable and contextually appropriate breast cancer prevention strategies.
